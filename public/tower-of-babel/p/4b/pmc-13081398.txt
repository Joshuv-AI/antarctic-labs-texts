Ocular structural parameters in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a case-control study of drug-naïve and methylphenidate-treated patients
Authors: Neslihan Parmak Yener, Mehmet Erdem Uzun, Hakan Demirci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate ocular structural parameters in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and to investigate potential differences associated with Methylphenidate Hydrochloride (MPH) treatment.MethodsIn this cross-sectional case-control study, 193 children were included and divided into three groups: Group 1 comprised 56 ADHD patients receiving MPH, Group 2 included 55 drug-naïve ADHD patients, and Group 3 consisted of 82 healthy controls. All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmologic examinations, including cycloplegic refraction, intraocular pressure (IOP), corneal topography, specular microscopy, and spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT). Retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, macular ganglion cell complex (GCC), and retinal thickness in parafoveal and perifoveal regions were analyzed.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in refraction, IOP, or corneal parameters among the three groups. OCT measurements showed that superior and inferior RNFL were significantly thinner in MPH-treated ADHD patients compared with controls (p = 0.010 and p < 0.001, respectively). Parafoveal retinal thickness, excluding the nasal quadrant, was increased in drug-naïve ADHD patients (superior p = 0.008, inferior p = 0.015, temporal p = 0.040), whereas perifoveal thickness was reduced in MPH-treated children (superior p = 0.011, inferior p = 0.014, temporal p = 0.004). No significant differences were found in GCC thickness between the groups. No significant correlation was observed between duration of MPH treatment and retinal thickness parameters (all p > 0.05).ConclusionsAnterior segment parameters were similar across groups, but retinal thickness differed between drug-naïve and MPH-treated ADHD patients, possibly related to disease characteristics or treatment effects, with no clear association with treatment duration. Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder in childhood, characterized by inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, with a global prevalence of 5.9–7.1% [1]. Its etiology is multifactorial, involving genetic, neurodevelopmental, environmental factors, and potentially neuroinflammatory mechanisms [2]. Neuroimaging studies have identified structural and functional brain differences in children with ADHD [3].

Catecholamines, including dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin, which play a central role in ADHD pathophysiology, have receptors not only in neuronal tissues but also in ocular structures such as the cornea, uveoscleral and trabecular meshwork, and retina [4, 5]. Therefore, ADHD may influence ocular structural and functional parameters. Previous studies have explored associations between ADHD and visual problems, including refractive errors, accommodative/vergence anomalies, and color vision deficiencies; however, findings regarding structural ocular changes remain inconsistent [6–8].

Pharmacological management of ADHD commonly involves stimulants (e.g., methylphenidate hydrochloride [MPH], amphetamines) or non-stimulants (e.g., atomoxetine, clonidine, guanfacine) [9]. MPH, a central nervous system stimulant, has been reported to potentially affect ocular parameters, including accommodation, pupil size, tear film, and risk of angle-closure glaucoma [10]. Although several studies have assessed ocular parameters in drug-naïve children with ADHD, data on MPH-treated children remain limited and inconclusive [11–17].

Given the widespread use of MPH in pediatric ADHD, understanding its potential effects on ocular structures is clinically important. This study aimed to evaluate ocular structural characteristics in MPH-treated and drug-naïve children with ADHD using Scheimpflug corneal topography, specular microscopy, and spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT).

Materials and methods

Patients and setting

This cross-sectional case-control study included consecutive children diagnosed with ADHD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria [18]. Patients were either drug-naïve or receiving methylphenidate hydrochloride (MPH) treatment. The control group consisted of gender-matched children referred from the Family Medicine Department for routine eye examinations.

The study was conducted between July 2018 and March 2019 at the Departments of Ophthalmology, Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, and Family Medicine, University of Health Sciences, Bursa Yüksek İhtisas Training and Research Hospital. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Date: 13/06/2018; No: 2018/06–14) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all parents prior to enrollment.

For the ADHD group, inclusion criteria were: (1) DSM-IV diagnosis of ADHD, (2) MPH treatment for at least six months or no pharmacological treatment, (3) age 7–18 years, (4) absence of ocular disease, and (5) willingness to participate. Controls were included if they had no psychiatric diagnosis or treatment, were aged 7–18 years, had no ocular disease, and agreed to participate.

Exclusion criteria for all participants included: a history of psychiatric disorders other than ADHD, use of medications other than MPH, neurological or systemic disorders, intellectual disability, and ocular diseases other than refractive errors (including glaucoma, amblyopia, strabismus, congenital cataract, previous ocular surgery or trauma, ocular inflammation, or corneal/retinal disease). Participants unable to maintain focus during ophthalmologic examinations were also excluded.

Psychiatric evaluation

All participants underwent comprehensive psychiatric assessment by a child and adolescent psychiatrist (MEU). ADHD diagnosis was confirmed using DSM-IV criteria and the Turkish version of the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Aged Children Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL) [19]. ADHD patients were divided into MPH-treated (Group 1) and drug-naïve (Group 2) groups. Healthy controls had no psychiatric diagnosis or treatment.

Ophthalmologic examination

All participants underwent a full ophthalmologic examination, including cycloplegic refraction, best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and fundus examination. IOP was measured using a non-contact tonometer (Canon TX-F, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

Anterior segment assessment

Anterior segment parameters were evaluated with a 3D rotating Scheimpflug camera (Sirius, CSO, Florence, Italy), measuring pupil diameter (PD), central corneal thickness (CCT), corneal keratometry (K), anterior chamber depth (ACD), anterior chamber volume (ACV), and anterior chamber angle (ACA). Measurements were obtained in a sitting position under scotopic conditions with non-dilated pupils.

Corneal endothelial parameters, including endothelial cell density (CD), coefficient of variation (CV), and hexagonal cell percentage (Hex%), were assessed using specular microscopy (Konan Medical Inc., Nishinomiya, Japan).

Cycloplegic auto-refraction was performed 30 min after pupil dilation using three drops of 1% cyclopentolate at 5-minute intervals.

Retinal imaging

Retinal imaging was performed using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT, RTVue XR Avanti, Optovue Inc., Fremont, CA). Peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness was measured with a 3.45-mm circular scan centered on the optic disc. Average, superior, inferior, temporal, and nasal RNFL thickness values were recorded.

Macular ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness, comprising the RNFL, ganglion cell layer, and inner plexiform layer, was measured using the device’s GCC protocol. Macular thickness was evaluated using the retinal map protocol based on the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) classification, dividing the macula into nine zones: central 1-mm foveal zone, 3-mm parafoveal zone (temporal, superior, nasal, inferior), and 5-mm perifoveal zone (temporal, superior, nasal, inferior). Foveal, parafoveal, and perifoveal thickness values were recorded.

All ophthalmologic measurements were performed by the same examiner (NPY) between 09:00 and 12:00. Three consecutive measurements were taken from each eye, and the eye with the highest image quality was included. The examiner was blinded to psychiatric evaluation results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data as median (range).

Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Post hoc analyses were conducted with Bonferroni correction (ANOVA) or Dunn test (Kruskal–Wallis). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Correlation between MPH treatment duration and retinal OCT parameters was assessed using Spearman’s test. As this study was designed as a cross-sectional exploratory case–control study, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed. To assess sample size adequacy, a post hoc power analysis was conducted using a three-group ANOVA (α = 0.05, power = 80%), assuming a medium effect size based on previous OCT studies in ADHD. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 193 children were included in the study: 56 children with ADHD receiving MPH treatment, 55 drug-naïve children with ADHD, and 82 healthy controls. The median age was 10 years (range: 8–15) in the MPH-treated ADHD group, 9 years (7–15) in the drug-naïve ADHD group, and 9 years (7–12) in the control group. Male participants comprised 40 children (71%) in the MPH-treated group, 43 children (78%) in the drug-naïve group, and 50 children (61%) in the control group. There were no statistically significant differences among the groups regarding age or sex distribution (p = 0.093 and p = 0.154, respectively). The median duration of MPH treatment was 36 months (range: 6–96 months).

Parafoveal retinal thickness in the inferior, superior, and temporal quadrants was significantly greater in the drug-naïve ADHD group compared with controls (p = 0.015, p = 0.008, and p = 0.040, respectively). In contrast, perifoveal retinal thickness in the inferior, superior, and temporal quadrants was significantly thinner in the MPH-treated ADHD group compared with controls (p = 0.011, p = 0.014, and p = 0.004, respectively). Correlation analysis showed no significant association between duration of MPH treatment and RNFL or perifoveal retinal thickness measurements (all p > 0.05) in the treated ADHD group. Discussion

In the present study, we compared ocular structural parameters in children with ADHD who were either drug-naïve or receiving MPH treatment with healthy controls. Our results showed that refractive parameters, intraocular pressure (IOP), corneal topography, and specular microscopy findings were similar across all groups. However, significant differences were observed in optical coherence tomography (OCT) parameters. Specifically, perifoveal retinal thickness, except for the nasal quadrant, was thinner in children receiving MPH treatment compared with healthy controls, whereas parafoveal thickness, excluding the nasal quadrant, was increased in the drug-naïve ADHD group.

Although visual problems in children with ADHD have been previously investigated, detailed analyses of anterior and posterior segment findings remain limited. Given the widespread use of MPH, it is important to examine possible ocular changes in this population. Our study aimed to evaluate anterior and posterior segment parameters in children with ADHD, both with and without MPH treatment.

Dopamine (DA) plays a role in postnatal refractive development and emmetropization of the eye [20]. Animal studies suggest that MPH can slow myopia progression by increasing extracellular dopamine and norepinephrine, supporting a role for these neurotransmitters in ocular growth regulation [21]. However, the effects of altered catecholaminergic activity on refractive parameters in children with ADHD remain unclear. Previous studies reported similar refractive errors in children with ADHD compared to healthy controls [22], while others observed subtle changes after MPH use [7, 23]. In line with prior reports, refractive measurements in our ADHD groups did not differ significantly from healthy controls.

In our study, neither drug-naïve ADHD patients nor those receiving MPH showed increased intraocular pressure (IOP) or glaucoma. Previous studies have reported similar or higher IOP values in children with ADHD treated with MPH compared with healthy controls [14, 24]. Catecholamines are known to play a role in IOP regulation, and MPH may theoretically increase the risk of angle-closure glaucoma by inducing mydriasis [9, 24, 25]. While Ayyıldız et al. reported similar corneal curvature and anterior chamber depth (ACD) in ADHD patients and controls [11], Larranaga-Fragosono et al. observed a decrease in ACD after 9 months of MPH treatment using cycloplegic measurements [26]. In the present study, ACD measurements were performed without pharmacologic dilation under dimly lit conditions. Since this method does not induce permanent mydriasis, the previously reported ACD reduction may be related to pupil dilation rather than the structural effects of MPH.

Catecholamine receptors in the human cornea contribute to corneal homeostasis [4]. Experimental studies in animals suggest that high-dose MPH may affect corneal epithelial or stromal cells [27]. Clinical studies have reported measurable changes in the corneal endothelium in children with ADHD following MPH treatment, with a six-month prospective observational study demonstrating significant reductions in endothelial cell density and increased corneal thickness, and a case–control study reporting lower endothelial cell counts and morphological alterations in children receiving long-term therapy compared with untreated ADHD patients and healthy controls [16, 17]. In contrast, our study found no significant differences in corneal endothelial measurements between drug-naïve or MPH-treated ADHD patients and healthy controls, suggesting that the effects of MPH on the corneal endothelium may be subtle, variable, or influenced by individual or treatment-related factors, highlighting the need for longer-term, larger-scale studies.

Previous studies on RNFL and GCC thickness in ADHD have shown conflicting results [11, 13–15, 21]. In our study, MPH-treated children exhibited thinner superior and inferior RNFL, while GCC thickness remained similar to controls. These RNFL values remained within the normative database limits, and the clinical significance of these micron-level differences remains uncertain. Parafoveal retinal thickness was increased in drug-naïve patients, whereas perifoveal thickness was reduced in MPH-treated patients. Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant association between treatment duration and RNFL or perifoveal retinal thickness parameters.

ADHD pathophysiology is unclear and has been associated with oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. The parafoveal region contains a high density of ganglion cells and inner nuclear layer neurons, including dopaminergic cells [3, 28]. Yokokura et al. showed that microglial activation increased and DA1R decreased in the same brain region in drug-naive ADHD patients compared to healthy controls [29]. Although microglial activation has been reported in the ADHD brain, similar neuroinflammatory markers in the retina remain largely unexplored and warrant investigation in future studies. MPH increases dopaminergic and noradrenergic activity, which could theoretically exert either protective or adverse effects on retinal cells [9]. Experimental studies suggest that excessive dopamine or MPH exposure may lead to oxidative stress or apoptosis [27, 28, 30, 31]. However, direct evidence in humans is limited. Recent OCT studies have shown outer retinal thinning in ADHD independent of psychostimulant use [32]. A meta-analysis including 433 ADHD patients and 514 healthy children found no significant differences in average RNFL, GCC, or macular thickness overall, although subgroup analyses indicated thinner inferior GCC and greater nasal macular thickness in ADHD patients. MPH appeared to increase RNFL thickness, but differences between treated and untreated ADHD patients were not significant [33]. Our findings relate to regional macular thickness differences rather than global measures, suggesting that ADHD and MPH treatment may be associated with localized retinal changes. However, the lack of a significant association between these differences and treatment duration in our sample does not support a duration-dependent cumulative drug effect. This finding suggests that the observed changes may be related to disease characteristics or other unmeasured factors.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Due to the retrospective design, MPH dosage, administration frequency, and cumulative exposure could not be consistently and comprehensively recorded for all cases. The cross-sectional design and absence of pre-treatment measurements limit causal inference regarding the effects of MPH, and it cannot be excluded that the MPH-treated group had thinner retina before starting medication. Although treatment duration data were available and correlation analyses were performed, duration alone may not fully capture cumulative pharmacologic exposure. The relatively small sample size, along with variability in treatment duration and dosage, may have reduced the ability to detect subtle changes. Sleep patterns, digital screen time, and axial length were not assessed, which may influence retinal measurements and should be considered in future studies. Furthermore, as a single-center study, the generalizability of the findings is limited, underscoring the need for larger, multicenter, longitudinal studies.

Conclusions

Anterior segment parameters were similar between children with ADHD and healthy controls, while macular OCT parameters showed group-specific differences. Retinal thickness patterns differed between drug-naïve and MPH-treated children with ADHD, which may reflect disease characteristics, treatment effects, or other unmeasured factors. However, the lack of association between treatment duration and retinal measurements suggests that these findings do not support a clear duration-dependent effect of MPH within the observed range.

Given the cross-sectional design, causality cannot be established, and baseline structural differences cannot be excluded. Although routine ophthalmologic screening is not indicated solely due to MPH therapy, clinicians should be aware of possible subtle retinal alterations, particularly in children receiving long-term treatment or reporting visual symptoms. Prospective longitudinal studies with baseline and follow-up measurements are necessary to better understand these associations, including potential neuroinflammatory mechanisms in the retina.
