DEX in near Earth orbit in light of Venus Orbiter Dust Experiment
Authors: Jayesh Pabari, Srirag Nambiar, Rashmi Singh, Sonam Jitarwal, Anil Bhardwaj, S. M. K. Praneeth, Bhavik Shah, Pinal Suthar, Shilpa Pandya, Jaimin Rami, Deepak Kumar, V. K. Singh, Rahul Khandekar, Rajesh Kumar Singh, Aanchal Sahu, Hiteshkumr Adalja, Arpit Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Study of IDP has been of interest to researchers for few decades, primarily after missions like Galileo, Ulysses, Cassini, Stardust and observations of comet, Hale-Bopp. The data from experiment like GORID have extended our knowledge about dust surrounding Earth. Similar attempts were made for understanding IDP at planets like Mars and Venus, however, in-situ measurements of IDP are awaited. We have proposed VODEX as well as MODEX for measuring dust around Venus and Mars, respectively and the Dust EXperiment (DEX), a replica of the same, was recently flown in near-Earth orbit at ~ 350 km using POEM of PSLV C-58 (XPoSat) mission. DEX was found working successfully in space and it has also shown presence of IDP in near-Earth orbit during its operation from 1st January to 9th February 2024. Here we report, developmental aspects of DEX, observational details and results. The measured time-resolved IDP flux is from ~ 10−3-10−2 # m−2 s−1 and the flux during entire observational period is 6.46 × 10−3 [2.95 × 10−3, 9.97 × 10−3] # m−2 s−1 from direct detection of IDP in near-Earth environment. The experiment was useful to demonstrate working of dust detector and also, it can provide new measurements of IDP entering Earth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21988-2.

Introduction

Entering Interplanetary Dust Particle (IDP) in a planetary atmosphere undergoes ablation and leaves out metallic ions, leading to formation of meteor layer. Efforts to understand this process began long back1,2 and earlier, the meteor layer was studied using visual, photographic and telescopic observations3–5. Later, the meteor speed and height measurements were made using radars6–9. The meteoric residual particles play an important role in formation of cirrus clouds10. Stratospheric collection of cosmic dust helped in the laboratory analysis11–13. Later decades saw several space based dust detectors for in-situ detection of tiny particles14–17. This theory of meteor layer is further extended to other planetary atmospheres like Mars, Venus, Jupiter, Saturn and Titan18–22. The dust ablation is analysed using optical and radio observations23,24, modelling18,25,26 and laboratory experiments. As far as laboratory experiments are concerned, the hypervelocity particles are ablated at short distance when they interact with atmospheric gas in a chamber27. Also, the material loss from meteorite was studied by Grigat et al.[28] and Dias et al.[29] made use of arc jet to understand the ablation process. The Meteoric Ablation Simulator at University of Leeds was used 30 for measurement of evaporated metal atoms.

The IDP evolves in solar system under the influence of gravitational and non-gravitational forces. Whenever, it is captured by a planet, it enters the atmosphere and experiences ablation. There are some past works to study IDP flux at Earth and other planetary objects in inner solar system, which are summarized below.

IDP at Earth

The theoretical and experimental effort to estimate IDP flux at Earth are described below:

Theoretical Consideration: IDPs originate from various sources like comets or asteroid belt and some earlier works tried to simulate the asteroidal collision and evolution31–35. Different forces affect physical and chemical properties of IDPs over a time period36. The gravitational pull of Sun and planets can alter the trajectories of IDPs in the system37 and smaller particles spiral in due to Poynting-Robertson effect38. Theoretically, N-body integration is used to understand dynamical evolution of dust particles. Marzari and Vanzani39 studied particles’ mean motion resonance with Earth. Later, Cremonese et al.40 recalculated the flux to be 18-21 tons/day for asteroidal dust and 10-13 tons/day for cometary dust, by computing a specific scaling law for impact craters on the Long Duration Exposure Facility and using the realistic impact velocities derived through the numerical integration. Their estimates provide contributions of asteroidal and cometary sources in the dust reaching Earth. Simulation of IDP dynamics within the solar system has led to estimates of ~ 270 tons/day due to the cometary source41 and ~ 13-18 tons/day due to the asteroidal source42. These different estimates of dust coming to Earth could be due to different mass range considered in their approaches. Experimental Work in Space: Grün et al.43 estimated influx of ~ 2.4 tons/day for the particle mass range from 10-21 to 10−1 kg, based on micrometeorite crater analysis on lunar samples and in-situ observations from space based dust detectors like HEOS-2, Pegasus and Pioneer 8 and 9. Laurance and Brownlee44, derived a flux of 1.32 tons/day for mass from 10-16 to 10–10 kg using impacts on a spacecraft. The Munich Dust Counter on-board HITEN satellite was an impact ionization detector with a target area of 10 cm × 10 cm16. MDC measured 67 dust impacts over 140 days, giving an average impact rate of 0.48 impacts/day and an overall flux of the order of 10−4 # m−2 s−1. For particle mass > 10–16 g, the flux near Earth (~ 50000 km) was found an order higher than at farther distance of ~ 250,000 km45. The Long Duration Exposure Facility satellite stayed in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) for few years, undergoing the impacts. By analysing impact craters on the satellite, Love and Brownlee15 estimated particle influx of 55–165 tons/day. A flux of the order of 7 × 10−4 # m−2 s−1 was observed in the geostationary orbit through GORID experiment46. AIM satellite at 550 km altitude provided an average flux of 4.9 × 103 particles/m2 yr for particle radius of 1.5–20 μm17. The Galileo and Ulysses dust detectors separated genuine dust signals using the multi-coincidence measurement. During the Earth flyby, Galileo recorded an impact rate of ~ 10−5 impacts/s47. Ulysses showed much lower impact rate of 0.13 per day (corresponding to flux of 1.5 × 10−5 # m−2s−1) due to different viewing geometry near Earth environment48,49. Experimental Work on Ground and Modelling: The Os isotopes provided IDP flux of 41–123 tons/day50, while analysis of stratospheric dust provided an influx rate of 22–104 tons/day51. Greenland samples showed an upper limit of 17 tons/day for micrometeorite particle accretion in the range from 0.45 to 20 μm52, while Antarctica samples provided influx rate of 14–44 tons/day53. According to estimates, Earth sees a dust particle influx of 5 to 270 tons/day54. Gardner et al.55 estimated a flux of 44–76 tons/day using lidar. Ablation modelling yielded an estimate of 12–44 tons/day[26] and Rudraswami et al.[56] used this influx to estimate the ice accumulation rate. Rojas et al.57 estimated flux of 11–18 tons/day using collection from Antarctica.

IDP at Moon and Mars

Deployed during the Apollo 17 mission, LEAM experiment utilized three sensors to capture dust impacts58. The LDEX instrument on LADEE mission recorded an average impact rates of ~ 1.6 × 10−2 and ~ 1.6 × 10−3 hits/second of particles with radii of a > 300 nm and a > 700 nm, respectively59. The Mars Dust Counter on Nozomi (Planet-B) mission provided IDP measurements in interplanetary space between Earth and Mars, however there were no measurements at Mars60. The LPW instrument on MAVEN mission detected rate of 0.001 to 0.005 hits/second on Mars61.

IDP towards Venus

There are limited measurements of dust particles toward Venus. IKAROS had ALADDIN detected ~ 2800 particles62,63 between Earth and Venus, which was further reported as 1773 particles64. However, the closest approach to Venus was ~ 13 Venus radii (i.e., ~ 78,000 km). Based on ALADDIN observations, Hirai63 has reported the IDP flux near Venus as ~ 10−4−10−5 m⁻² s⁻¹. Some observations were also reported from Helios and Galileo instruments during the flyby14,48,65. The Radio and Plasma Wave (RPW) experiment on Solar Orbiter mission detected dust impacts by measuring voltage generated when a grain collided with the spacecraft, from which, cumulative flux of particles with mass ≳ 10−17 kg at 1 AU was found to be ~ 8 × 10−5 m⁻² s⁻¹ and therefore would be 1.52 × 10−4 m⁻² s⁻¹ at 0.7 AU after appropriate scaling66.

Concluding remarks on IDP flux estimation

Despite past efforts to study IDP near Earth, the research interest continues to understand the long term variability of IDP flux. Also, different approaches have been employed in the modelling and it continues further. Generally, IDP measurements are carried out in mass range of 10−18 to 10−12 kg using an impact ionization detector. Most of the measurements at Earth have provided IDP flux from 10−4 m⁻² s⁻¹ to 10−2 m⁻² s⁻¹, obtained at different times for various particle sizes.

There are no measurements of IDP at Venus and Mars, despite some observations between Earth and Venus or between Earth and Mars. Investigation of meteor layer (V0) on Venus requires observations of IDP from a Venus satellite, since this layer is expected to be created by ablation of entering IDP at Venus. To measure IDP at Venus, the Venus Orbiter Dust EXperiment (VODEX) has been under development for Venus mission and to demonstrate operation of VODEX in space through the detection of IDP, the Dust EXperiemnet (DEX) was flown on PSLV Orbital Experimental Module (POEM) on PS4 (i.e., fourth stage of vehicle) of PSLV C-58 (XPoSat) mission, in the near Earth orbit at ~ 350 km altitude. The POEM on PSLV C-58 is also known as POEM 3, as per the sequence of POEMs flown earlier. After releasing the satellite at 650 km altitudes, the POEM 3 was made operational for the Earth observations for a few months. The POEM 3 had orbit of approximately 90 min in 350 km orbit, having speed of ~ 7.7 km/s. Similar to VODEX, an instrument called Mars Orbiter Dust EXperiment (MODEX) is proposed for future Mars mission, to measure the IDP at Mars67. The present work reports developmental aspects of DEX for POEM 368,69 and the latest results of dust observations in the near Earth orbit.

DEX operating principle and electronics design

The incoming IDP makes an impact with detector target (Fig. 1 (a) and Fig. 2 (a)), creating impact plasma in detector volume. The electron and positive ions are then separated using appropriately biased collectors, placed near the topside. The impact charge is converted to voltage signals using Charge Sensitive Pre-Amplifiers (CSPAs) in Electron Channel (EC), Ion Channel (IC) and Target Channel (TC) in the Front-End Electronics (FEE) card. The analog signals are digitized using Analog-to-Digital Converters (ADCs) and further processed by FPGA (Field Programmable Gate Array) in Processing Electronics (PE) card of DEX. Mechanical design

The mechanical design and structural analysis were carried out to ensure safety and functionality of physical structure in space. The fundamental frequency was found to be 134 Hz, which meets the requirement of > 100 Hz. The maximum stress values at 50 g Quasi Static Loading were found as 67.1 MPa at mesh corner (Z direction), 75.2 MPa at detector vertical PCB centre (Y direction) and 80.3 MPa at detector vertical PCB centre (X direction), with safety margin of 3, 3.58 and 3.29, respectively. Also, vibration test on DEX payload was conducted as per the requirements of PSLV and results were found satisfactory.

Thermal design

The thermal design of DEX for PS4 considered stage (sink) temperature of 60° C. The instrument was anodized and covered using a Multi-Layer Insulation blanket. The results of thermal analysis are shown in Fig. 3, which were found within the acceptable temperature for POEM 3. The power card devices were arranged in such a way that the heat is dissipated evenly to meet the 65° C requirements.

Analog electronics chain

The electrons and ions are collected on collector plates of electron and ion channels, respectively. These charges are converted to voltage signals using CSPA, having an internal JFET. The CSPA has an internal feedback capacitor with value 0.25 pF and a resistor with value 1 G Ω. The achieved gain of the CSPA is found to be ~ 6. The output of CSPA is provided to a buffer for signal conditioning. Further, an adder is used to add signals of two electron channels. Similarly, signals from two ion channels are added by another adder. The estimated SNR of DEX is from 26.9 to 66.9 dB for + 50° C temperature, which is slightly better for − 10° C (details are given in Appendix A). There are total five similar analog chains (Fig. 4) in DEX, viz. two for electron channels, two for ion channels and one for target channel, with appropriate changes based on the signal polarity.

Processing electronics

Three serial, 10 bit ADCs are used to digitize analog signals from three different channels. These ADCs are operated by FPGA at 1.176 MSPS sampling rate and the 32 kByte of volatile SRAM (Static Random Access Memory) is used to process the data onboard POEM 3. Also, two Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs) are used to provide reference voltages to the high voltage DC-DC. A crystal oscillator is used to provide clock to the FPGA and a power on reset circuit is used to reset the FPGA at power on. Further, a transceiver is used to transfer data from DEX to the Data Handling (DH) subsystem of the POEM 3.

Software

Whenever power of DEX is turned on, it immediately starts acquiring data and stores it into an internal SRAM of FPGA for further processing. Since, data processing module requires some minimum time to process the data, the SRAM is divided into two sets internally (Fig. 5). In order to eliminate dead time, the data processing and acquisition are done in parallel, i.e., while data in the first SRAM is being processed, the other SRAM will keep storing the data and vice versa. In data processing module, any event having the highest peak in an interval of 10 s is considered as valid event data. Upon its detection, FPGA generates the data packet containing the start byte, time tag, frame number, parity bytes, science data of all three channels and end byte. The science data is taken for ± 85 µs around the detected highest peak. Once the data packet is ready, it is sent to the satellite data handling subsystem. The gathered data are then transmitted to Earth as per visibility of the ground station.

The output voltage of HV DC-DCs are controlled by DACs by providing voltages in the range of 0–3 V.

Testing using pulse laser

DEX was tested with nanosecond pulse laser setup established at Institute of Plasma Research, Gandhinagar, where a 5 ns, 800 mJ Nd-YAG laser was used to simulate dust particle impact. Though the dynamics of dust impact produced plasma and laser plasma are different, pulse laser has been used to characterise instrument response in terms of charge collection and electronics output62,72–74. A pulsed laser with duration and energy comparable with particle-target interaction times (~ 10 ns) and energies of dust particle can be utilised for this purpose. The detector along with CSPA is placed inside the vacuum chamber, which is maintained at a pressure lower than 10−3 mbar during the experiment, as shown in Fig. 7 (a). The energy of the order of micro-Joules were used for the testing. Figure 7 (b) shows typical response of DEX when shot with a laser pulse of energy lower than 50 µJ. Since the inverting buffers were not used, signal polarity is inverted for Ion channel. The laser energy could not be controlled precisely from one shot to another, which led to observation being recorded at larger vertical scales, leading to discreteness seen in the output. The rise-time of almost all signals was below 80 µs.

Operation and telemetry

DEX took continuous observation in the near Earth environment, whenever it was switched on as per the observation period shown in Fig. 8. As per communication protocol, all payloads on POEM 3 were connected to a single bus. DEX received a prompt every 16 ms from Data Processing Unit (DPU) on POEM 3 and replied with two bytes of data as per the protocol. All data gathered on POEM 3 memory are downlinked during the period of Earth station visibility and subsequently, data were sent to the payload team after separation of individual payload data. The power, visibility and downlink possibility are usually limited in the experimental platform and hence, the actual observation period was quite low compared to mission lifetime (Fig. 8). The DEX data for 152 orbits were made available in total.

DEX data analysis

The received HEX data (Fig. 9 (a), top) were first converted to decimal format and checked for valid (healthy) bytes, from which 73.15% data were found as the valid data. This reduction might have happened due to noise during data transmission or any other unforeseen reason. Next, the valid data were searched to extract the signal after removing headers and footers. Further, the data were filtered using multiple criteria like (i) removal of saturated data, (ii) noisy data removal using mean and standard deviation of signal and signal time series difference and (iii) searching for rise/fall pattern of impact signal to derive final number of genuine impacts. DEX results

A similar version of DEX may be calibrated in near future to differentiate the mass and speed of IDP, however, it was not the objective of DEX to derive the particle mass and speed in the current experiment. The objective of IDP flux estimation does not need DEX calibration, as such and, there is a way to analyse the received data provided by DEX to understand the flux in Earth orbit. Potential applications of in-situ dust measurements

The IDP measurements in Earth/Venusian/Martian environment are useful to understand meteor layer in the lower ionosphere of the planet. In case of a planetary object like moon or asteroid or Mercury, the incoming dust directly reach the surface without ablation (in the absence of atmosphere) and creates the crater as well as the ejecta. The impact and escape study require the measurements of incoming particles78. In addition, there are many applications of the in-situ measurements of dust particles like space environment monitoring and spacecraft hazard assessment.

The measurements made by Pioneer and HEOS have been used to derive the flux models near Earth environment and the same have been used with appropriate scaling to estimate flux at other planets like Mars and Venus18,43. These flux values are then used to estimate the injection rate of metal atoms and ions into the atmosphere by ablation. In absence of in-situ measurement of dust flux at these planets, the scaled flux is useful in understanding the planetary ionosphere. In case of Mars, if in-situ measurements were available, it can help address several unresolved questions like, dust ring around the planet, concentration of noble gas released by meteorites and possible seasonal variation in dust input to the planet79.

Measurements made by passive detectors like Long Duration Exposure Facility has been used in the NASA96 space debris environment model80. The Divine-Staubach model, which is constrained by observations of dust detectors like Pioneer 10–11, Helios, Galileo and Ulysses have been made use of in the MASTER meteoroid model81. Such models are useful in spacecraft hazard assessment, and they are even more crucial for any human rated missions or space stations in low Earth orbit. DEX is an impact ionization type dust detector, which can detect the smaller particles travelling at hyper-velocity, which forms the primary component of IDP flux. Spacecrafts in any orbit are at risk from two components, naturally occurring dust particles and space debris. The naturally occurring dust particles are measured by DEX, however, the debris are generally larger in size, which can be measured after tuning the detection range of the instrument.

Updates for observations in Venusian orbit

The current observations of IDP at Earth using DEX can be useful for future observations at Venus. The importance of dust measurement at Venus with respect to upcoming missions and few updates from DEX to VODEX instrument are described here.

Importance of IDP measurements at Venus

Radio occultation experiment can provide the meteor layer profile in the Venusian ionosphere, which peaks around 110 km20,82. Since, IDP is the source of this layer, the measurement made by VODEX can help in constraining the dust input to Venus as well as in understanding its long term variability. Venus Express Radio Science (VeRa) experiment aboard Venus Express detected the enhancement in electron density in the lower ionosphere, which were expected to be from the dust ablation20. Recently, Tripathi et al.82 analysed the radio occultation data from Akatsuki and showed the presence of this layer under different conditions, indicating meteoric origin as the most likely origin. They note that continuous in-situ measurements are required to further understand the origin of layer.

The upcoming Venus mission by ESA i.e. EnVision, has a Radio Science Experiment (RSE), which can measure the electron density profiles in 40–90 km83,84, which is below the expected meteor layer altitude. The NASA’s Deep Atmosphere Venus Investigation of Noble gases, Chemistry, and Imaging (DAVINCI+) mission could measure neutral metal atoms like Mg and Fe, however, these observations by instruments on-board the descent probe will begin at much lower altitudes, i.e., below 70 km85. Another mission Venus Emissivity, Radio Science, Insar, Topography and Spectroscopy (VERITAS) by NASA is proposed to study the Venusian surface86. In this regard, the IDP measurements at Venus by VODEX will be the first time measurements, which can be useful to study meteor layer in the lower ionosphere. Further, any radio science experiment on future mission providing electron density profile can measure the meteor layer, however, it will need IDP measurements at Venus to explain this layer.

Discussion and summary

Measurement of IDP in the Earth orbit has several key advantages. Since, dust is a building block of solar system, it holds valuable clues about it. The dust observations can be used to validate modelling results of particle evolution at a planet. The measured particle flux at the top of atmosphere directly enters into ablation model for estimation of meteor layer. In case of moon, the entering particle flux on the other side, is useful to understand impact on the surface and subsequent ejecta escaping the moon. For instance, lunar escape process was reported to estimate water ice and regolith escape from lunar surface78. Similarly, Phobos and Deimos were found to be local source of dust in inner solar system in connection with Juno observations91. Interplanetary dust is responsible for zodiacal light in the ecliptic plane and any measurement of IDP could be helpful for its modelling. Also, dust detection on space missions are crucial to understand space environment and its potential impact on spacecraft and human activities in space. Dust observation is crucial for development of effective shielding of spacecraft to safeguard from potential damage. Also, measuring IDP flux in Earth orbit can provide a baseline for future comparison, if the space environments are commercialized in future. Dust measurement in near-Earth environment is useful as a baseline, because any future dust measurements near the moon can be compared to this baseline to assess the impact of human activity on the lunar environment. Updated monitoring of dust flux can reveal how the interplanetary environment evolves over time, including changes in dust density. Impact ionization detectors can provide measurement of IDP in the mass range from 10−18 to 10−12 kg. Such detectors provide immediate measurements, allowing for continuous monitoring of dust flux in space. The IDP flux from most of the past observations at or near Earth is in the range from 10−4 m⁻² s⁻¹ to 10−2 m⁻² s⁻¹, obtained at different times for various particle sizes.

VODEX is under development for Venus mission, which is a metal target, impact ionization dust detector. Looking at the expected future missions to Venus, the IDP measurements at Venus by VODEX will be the first time measurements, which can be useful to explain meteor layer in the lower ionosphere. DEX is a replica of VODEX, flown in the LEO to understand detector operation in space and also, to detect IDP in near-Earth environment. From the DEX development and its data analysis, few updates were identified to be implemented in VODEX.

This work provides developmental aspects of DEX, its operation in space, dust observations, data processing and results. We found the background in space mostly within 80 mV for IC channel and within 60 mV for EC and TC channels through DEX in near Earth orbit. Systematic and logical approach, including thresholding above the background allowed to identify dust impacts from the received data. The IDP hit rate varied within 0.003 #/s within the POEM altitude range from 299 to 341 km from the Earth surface. The time-resolved IDP flux from direct detection in near-Earth environment is found to be approximately between 10−3 to 10−2 # m−2 s−1. Also, the IDP flux observed by DEX during the entire observation time is found to be 6.46 × 10−3 [2.95 × 10−3, 9.97 × 10−3] # m−2 s−1. Further, considering the flux concentrated at smaller particle due to the fact that flux dominates for that range, the DEX measurements are inline with existing dust model for Earth. The work demonstrates working of dust detector in space and also, provide latest measurements of IDP flux at Earth.
