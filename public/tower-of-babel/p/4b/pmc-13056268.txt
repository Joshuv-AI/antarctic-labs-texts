The Roles of Autonomic Arousal and Self‐Reported Stress in Children's Disclosure of a Minor Transgression
Authors: Laura M. Fulton, Joanna Peplak, J. Zoe Klemfuss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccurate disclosures from children are essential in child maltreatment investigations, yet many children are reluctant to disclose adverse experiences. Biologically sensitive children may experience stronger stress responses in morally or socially charged situations, potentially inhibiting disclosure. The present study examined whether stress, measured physiologically via autonomic nervous system (ANS) arousal and subjectively via self‐reported stress, predicted children's disclosure of a transgression. Children (N = 337; ages 4–9 years) participated in a laboratory‐based paradigm, during which two toys broke in their hands, and a confederate asked them to keep it a secret. Acute ANS arousal was indexed by heart rate during the minute following the secrecy instruction relative to baseline. Children also self‐reported their stress and calmness at baseline and immediately post‐transgression. Children were then interviewed using a NICHD‐informed forensic‐style interview protocol. Higher ANS arousal post‐secrecy instruction predicted a lower likelihood of disclosure. In contrast, higher self‐reported calmness post‐transgression predicted a reduced likelihood of disclosure, while self‐reported stress was unrelated. Age was positively associated with disclosure but did not moderate stress−disclosure associations. Findings highlight the value of assessing stress beyond self‐report, with heightened ANS arousal capturing nondisclosure risk that children may strategically downplay or fail to recognize in self‐reports.

Introduction

Eliciting accurate information from children is essential for identifying instances of child maltreatment, especially in cases of child sexual abuse, where disclosures are often the only available evidence [1]. However, children with maltreatment histories are often hesitant to disclose their experiences to adults [2]. One potential factor influencing this reluctance is the stress children experience during maltreatment situations, as stress plays a critical role in how children interpret and respond to situations, shaping not only their internal experiences but also whether they are willing to share them with others [3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

Indeed, some evidence suggests that stress can discourage children from sharing information. For example, Quas et al. [8] found that children aged 3−13 years old who were rated by their parents as experiencing greater fear and upset during and after an invasive medical procedure were less responsive to open‐ended questions compared to those with lower distress ratings, despite demonstrating comparable memory when asked direct questions. Similarly, Sales et al. [9] found that 3‐ to 4‐year‐old children who experienced higher stress during Hurricane Andrew, indexed by more severe damage to their homes, as well as mother‐ and child‐rated stress, provided less information during interviews regarding the event 6 years later. Quas and colleagues [5] suggest that while stress may not impair memory, it may reduce children's willingness to disclose. In other words, children recounting a stressful experience may remember the event accurately but be hesitant to discuss it.

Importantly, these studies examined children's reports of events they would have little reason to hide, such as natural disasters and invasive medical procedures. While these events could be stressful or embarrassing to recount, children likely perceive little risk in disclosing the event. Highlighting the importance of this distinction, a recent study showed that 4‐ to 9‐year‐old children were significantly less likely to disclose playing with toys if those toys were associated with a transgression they were reluctant to disclose [10]. Children engaged in toy play with a confederate. Some of the children were randomly assigned to a condition in which two of the toys were rigged to break in their hands, whereas the remaining children in the control condition received toys that functioned normally. The confederate asked the children in the breakage condition to conceal the toy breakage to avoid potential repercussions. Children in the control condition were significantly more likely to disclose having played with the toys that broke for children in the breakage condition—more than three times more likely than children who initially concealed but later disclosed toy breakage, and five times more likely relative to children who never disclosed that the toys broke. This work suggests that preschool and school‐aged children may be motivated to conceal details related to a transgression. However, no research to date has examined the role of stress on children's disclosures of transgression events, where children may fear implicating themselves or another person in wrongdoing and thus have an incentive to conceal the event. Although such contexts do not model all maltreatment experiences, they capture disclosure‐relevant dynamics, such as secrecy and perceived risk of disclosure, that may be present in some real‐world cases [11].

To understand how stress may impact children's disclosures, it is necessary to consider how individual differences in biological reactivity shape behavioral responses to stressful or morally complex situations. Biological sensitivity to context theory [12, 13] posits that children differ in the extent to which their physiological systems respond to environmental cues. Some children may show relatively muted responses, whereas others are highly susceptible, making them more vulnerable in adverse contexts but also more responsive to supportive ones. In situations involving secrecy or wrongdoing, where emotional, moral, and social pressures are high, these highly sensitive children may experience greater internal conflict. Even subtle cues suggesting that disclosure carries risk can trigger elevated physiological arousal, thereby increasing the perceived cost of disclosure [13, 14].

This heightened physiological reactivity is not inherently maladaptive; in supportive environments, it may foster engagement and learning. However, evidence suggests that children who are more biologically sensitive often rely on withdrawal‐ or avoidance‐based coping strategies when confronted with stress or ambiguity [14, 15]. In the context of a transgression, these children may be more inclined to withhold information, even when they possess an accurate memory of the event. That is, individual differences in autonomic reactivity may shape not only children's internal experiences but also their behavioral decisions about whether or not to disclose.

Further research is needed to explore how stress relates to children's disclosures of mutual transgression events, but studying this relationship in children presents unique challenges. Younger children, in particular, often struggle to recognize and articulate their internal emotional states [9, 16], and those attempting to conceal a transgression may deliberately underreport their stress to avoid further questioning.

To address these limitations, the present study includes heart rate (HR), a physiological marker of stress, which reflects real‐time responses of the autonomic nervous system (ANS) to perceived threat. The ANS includes the sympathetic nervous system, which increases HR during stressful situations (“fight or flight”), and the parasympathetic nervous system, which supports recovery (“rest and digest”) by slowing HR [17]. HR is less susceptible to voluntary control than verbal reports, making it particularly useful when children might engage in strategic concealment.

Understanding the role of stress in disclosure is crucial in the context of child maltreatment investigations, where reluctance to disclose has profound consequences for children's safety and well‐being. By examining both HR and children's subjective stress reports in the context of a transgression, the present study takes a first step toward clarifying how multiple indicators of stress relate to children's willingness to disclose information that they may be motivated to withhold.

The Present Study

The goal of the present study was to address a gap in the literature by examining how stress relates to children's disclosures about a transgression, an event they may be motivated to conceal. Stress was assessed in two ways: first, by measuring children's ANS arousal via HR during the 1‐min period following the secrecy instruction, and second, through children's self‐reported post‐transgression stress, both in relation to baseline.

Building on prior work showing that stress can interfere with children's reports [5, 8, 9] and drawing from the biological sensitivity to context framework [12, 13], we hypothesized that children who showed higher ANS arousal, measured through elevated HR during the 1‐min period following the secrecy instruction (and controlling for baseline HR), would be less likely to disclose the toy breakage during the forensic interview. This prediction was based on the idea that heightened physiological arousal may indicate biological sensitivity, reflecting increased responsiveness to environmental demands and cues. Biological sensitivity may amplify children's perception of emotional or interpersonal risk and increase avoidant‐based responses, such as nondisclosure. Although we also hypothesized that higher self‐reported stress would be associated with a lower likelihood of disclosure, this prediction was treated with caution due to potential limitations in children's ability and willingness to accurately recognize and report their stress in a transgression context [9, 16].

In addition to these primary hypotheses, we expected that child age would be positively associated with disclosure. Prior research has shown that older children are generally more likely to disclose transgressions or wrongdoing [18, 19, 20], potentially due to developmental advances in language, emotion regulation, and moral reasoning [21]. Given developmental changes in emotion regulation, an exploratory analysis examined whether autonomic arousal following the secrecy instruction differed by child age. Exploratory analyses also examined whether age moderated the association between stress (both self‐report and autonomic) and disclosure. Multiple competing developmental mechanisms could plausibly shape these associations. For ANS arousal, two opposing age‐related patterns are theoretically possible. Older children may be more likely than younger children to disclose despite elevated arousal due to greater coping capacity and emotion regulation abilities [22, 23], or less likely to disclose due to more advanced executive functions and theory of mind that support strategic withholding of transgression‐related details following stress [24, 25]. For self‐reported stress, developmental increases in executive functioning and theory of mind may enable older children to strategically manage their verbal responses, including downplaying stress to avoid suspicion. In contrast, younger children's more limited emotional awareness and vocabulary might limit response accuracy and weaken the association with disclosure [9, 16]. Taken together, these developmental processes could operate differently across age to attenuate or mask age‐based moderation effects for both ANS arousal and self‐reported stress. Therefore, no directional hypotheses were specified for these exploratory analyses.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Research participants were recruited from a university database of eligible families, community flyers, and social media platforms. Caregivers were eligible if they were the legal guardian of the participating child, English fluent, and over the age of 18 years old. Child participants were eligible if they were between 4 and 9 years old, English fluent, and did not have any significant developmental, cognitive, or language delays. Of the 371 children who experienced the toy breakage event, 19 were outside of the target age range (10−12), nine were determined post‐session to be ineligible (e.g., developmental delay, non‐English fluency), three did not assent to study procedures, and one became distressed and ended the session. Two additional sessions lacked usable video for disclosure coding and physiological event matching. Therefore, the final analytic sample included 337 children (47% boys, 53% girls), with an equal distribution across age ranges (M = 6.47, SD = 1.71). In terms of ethnicity, 35% of children identified as Hispanic or Latine, 63% did not identify as Hispanic or Latine, and 2% did not report ethnicity. The racial distribution of the child sample was 35% White, 33% Asian, 27% multiracial, 1% Black or African American, 1% American Indian or Alaskan Native, and 1% Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, and 2% did not report their race, reflecting a racial and ethnic distribution similar to that of the surrounding community.

Procedure

The present work is part of a larger study on children's sequencing abilities, memory, and disclosures in maltreatment investigations, and only relevant materials and procedures are described here. All study procedures were approved by the institutional review board at the University of California, Irvine. Caregivers and children attended a 2‐h session at a university laboratory. Caregivers received explanations of study procedures before providing written consent. Children gave assent and were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any point without repercussions. Children wore the Empatica E4 wristband for the duration of the laboratory session, allowing for continuous recording of HR across all tasks, including the resting baseline, talking baseline, broken‐toy paradigm, and forensic interview. Study sessions were audio‐ and video‐recorded for transcription purposes and to generate timestamps linking physiological data to session events.

Resting Baseline

Participants first watched a 3‐min relaxing video featuring sea turtles swimming in the ocean. This segment served as their resting physiological baseline. In adults, resting physiology is typically measured without external stimuli, but children have greater difficulty staying still. To reduce the risk of motion artifacts, researchers frequently use relaxing videos or stories that encourage children to stay calm and physically settled [26]. Children were instructed to remain as still as possible to minimize motion artifacts and told to relax as they watched the video.

Talking Baseline

Following the resting baseline, children were shown developmentally appropriate, neutral images (e.g., pictures of toys and everyday objects) and asked to describe them for 3 min. Because talking can change ANS activity due to the impact of respiration on cardiac functioning [27], the talking baseline task, modified from previous child psychophysiology research [28], provided a comparison baseline measure that accounted for speech‐related changes in ANS activity.

Broken‐Toy Paradigm

After participants underwent both physiological baseline procedures, they completed pre‐transgression self‐reported stress questions. Participants then engaged in the transgression task, modeled after the broken‐toy paradigm [10, 29]. In this paradigm, the primary interviewer pretended to forget some papers and left the room to get them. They asked a different research assistant (the confederate) to keep the child company while they went to fetch the papers. The child and confederate played with five toys that were up on a shelf, during which the second toy (Rubik's cube) and fourth toy (yo‐yo) in the sequence were rigged to break in the child's hands. The confederate feigned surprise and concern when each toy broke. At the end of the toy play, the confederate instructed the child to keep the toy breakage a secret from the primary interviewer to avoid punishment.

Divided Attention Manipulation

As part of a larger study aim, children were randomly assigned to one of two conditions after the transgression task (1 = divided attention condition, and 0 = no divided attention condition). In the divided attention condition, children viewed a brief sequence of images and were instructed to remember them for a later task. Children in the no divided attention condition did not complete this task. This manipulation was designed to address separate research questions regarding attention and memory and was not theoretically relevant to the present study's hypotheses.

On average, across divided attention conditions, 205.64 s (SD = 94.45) elapsed between the end of the secret‐keeping instruction and the beginning of the forensic interview. This interval was longer for children assigned to the divided attention condition (M = 258.37 s, SD = 84.42) than for those in the no divided attention condition (M = 172.56 s, SD = 81.40). Accordingly, the divided attention condition was included as a covariate in all analyses to account for any potential variance (i.e., post‐transgression time‐lag differences) associated with this manipulation.

Forensic‐Style Interview

Following the broken toy paradigm, the interviewer re‐entered the room, administered the divided attention task to half of the sample, asked the post‐transgression self‐reported stress questions, and then questioned the child using a forensic‐style interview. The ground rules and open‐ended free recall phases followed guidelines from the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) Investigative Interview Protocol [30]. The interview protocol was modeled on procedures used in actual child maltreatment forensic interviews but was adapted for a laboratory‐based transgression paradigm. Specifically, although the NICHD protocol permits the use of directive questions when open‐ended recall is exhausted, it does not include a fixed set of structured direct or yes/no questions administered uniformly across interviews. In the present study, the direct and yes/no questions were developed to be standardized across participants and tailored to the toy play event. The interview used in this study did not include a rapport‐building phase.

Interviewers were provided with a semi‐structured interview guide and underwent extensive training on appropriate prompting and interview methods. Interviewer fidelity was monitored throughout data collection through regular peer reviews, in which interviewers reviewed one another's interview videos. Trained graduate students and postdoctoral researchers also regularly reviewed videos of interviews and provided feedback to ensure adherence to the interview protocol.

The interview began with the ground rules section, which included instructions to tell the truth and to say “I don't know” when unsure about something. Then, the interviewer initiated the open‐ended questioning phase by asking an open‐ended prompt (“Tell me everything that happened while I was gone”) and used neutral facilitators (e.g., “What else?” and “Tell me more”) and continued until the child indicated their narrative was complete (e.g., “That's all” or silence after multiple prompts). Next, the interviewer asked 21 direct, closed‐ended questions (e.g., “When you played with the Rubik's cube, what did the [woman/man] in the room do?”) and eight yes/no questions (e.g., “Did anything bad happen to the Rubik's cube?”). See Supplementary Materials, Table S1, for a full list of direct and yes/no questions. Finally, the interviewer closed the interview by asking the child if they had anything else to share (e.g., “Is there anything else you think I should know?”). At the end of the forensic interview, the interviewer explained to the child that even if they broke the toys, they would not get in trouble. A formal debriefing was conducted at the end of all study procedures, with the caregiver present.

Measures

Self‐Reported Stress

Children were asked to self‐report their stress immediately before and immediately after the transgression task. At each time point, children were asked four questions: “How stressed do you feel?”, “How bad do you feel?”, “How calm do you feel?”, and “How good do you feel?”. Each question was rated on a 3‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not very) to 3 (very). When children seemed unsure of what stressed meant, interviewers explained the meaning using synonyms (e.g., worried or anxious) to support comprehension.

Because the original four‐item composite (stress, calm, bad, good) demonstrated low reliability (α = 0.43 at pre‐transgression and α = 0.67 at post‐transgression), we analyzed the items as separate variables instead of aggregating them into a composite “stress” score. As our hypotheses focused on arousal‐related subjective states, we retained only the “stress” and “calm” items. The “good” and “bad” items were added as a more developmentally simple alternative (reflecting the defining attributes of stress conceptions in young children [31]) but were removed because they are conceptually broader and may have captured constructs other than arousal, such as moral evaluation, thereby limiting interpretability. This distinction is consistent with dimensional models of affect, which differentiate arousal (activation vs. deactivation) from valence (unpleasant vs. pleasant) [32]. Importantly, the stress and calm items were not strongly correlated with each other in this sample (r = −0.03 pre‐transgression and r = −0.18 post‐transgression; see Table 1). Accordingly, both variables were entered in the same model to evaluate their unique contributions to children's disclosure.

Autonomic Arousal (HR)

Children's HR was measured using the Empatica E4 wristband, a validated wearable device that uses photoplethysmography to derive HR from blood volume pulse signals [33, 34]. The wristband was worn on the wrist of the child's nondominant hand to reduce motion artifacts. HR data were sampled at a frequency of 1 Hz (one value per second) throughout the study session.

Baseline HR was averaged during two separate 3‐min tasks: a resting baseline (watching a relaxing video) to index children's physiology at rest, and a talking baseline (describing neutral images) to account for speech‐related influences on autonomic functioning. These baselines were analyzed separately as covariates to control for individual and developmental differences in HR.

To capture autonomic arousal following the transgression, HR was averaged during the 1‐min period immediately after the confederate instructed the child to keep the toy breakage a secret. This segment was selected because it captures children's acute ANS arousal in response to being asked to conceal a wrongdoing, an emotionally and morally salient moment distinct from earlier toy play. This approach is consistent with prior work, as minute‐level HR aggregates are common in research with HR wearables to ensure stable HR estimates [35]. Resting baseline HR was unavailable for 11 participants (3.3%), and the 60‐s post‐secrecy HR epoch was unavailable for 26 participants (7.7%) due to technical issues (i.e., signal loss, wristband‐tablet disconnections, and server upload failures).

Disclosure

Children's disclosures of the transgression were coded dichotomously for whether they disclosed toy breakage at any point before debriefing (0 = did not disclose, 1 = did disclose). A disclosure was defined as any explicit verbal acknowledgment that a toy broke (e.g., “The toys broke” or “A piece fell off the Rubik's cube”). Vague references that did not clearly indicate breakage (e.g., “Something bad happened”) were not coded as disclosures. To establish reliability, two trained coders independently reviewed 32% (n = 108) of the transcripts. Agreement for the dichotomous code was perfect (Cohen's k = 1.00).

Analytic Strategy

To test whether physiological and self‐reported stress predicted children's disclosure, we ran logistic regression models with disclosure (0 = no disclosure, 1 = disclosure) as the dependent variable. For the primary logistic regression analyses, missing predictor values were handled using multiple imputation with predictive mean matching, and results were pooled across 20 imputations using Rubin's rules. As a robustness check, all logistic regression models were also estimated using listwise deletion; results were substantively identical and are reported in the Supplementary Materials (see Table S2). All models controlled for the divided attention task (divided attention or no divided attention) and child age; in exploratory analyses, age was also tested as a moderator of stress effects. HR models additionally controlled for resting baseline HR to account for individual differences in resting autonomic functioning. The resting baseline, rather than the talking baseline, was used as a control because it better matched the secret‐keeping HR period, during which children typically spoke very little. Gender and race/ethnicity were also evaluated as potential covariates, but they neither predicted disclosure nor altered the main model results, so they were excluded from the final analyses.

Prior to multiple imputation, we screened HR variables (resting baseline HR and secret‐keeping HR) for outliers using Mahalanobis distance (threshold of p < 0.001) and Cook's distance (D > 4/n). A total of 19 outliers were flagged. We ran our model with and without the outliers but did not find changes in significance, direction, or size of effects; thus, outliers were retained, and all analyses are reported with the full analytic sample (N = 337).

Given that 132 children in the analytic sample had a sibling who also participated (66 sibling pairs), we conducted sensitivity analyses to account for potential nonindependence of observations. We estimated multilevel logistic regression models with child‐level predictors (fixed effects) nested within families (random intercept). The pattern and significance of the results remained unchanged in the nested models; therefore, results from the non‐nested logistic regression models are reported for simplicity.

Results

Preliminary Analyses

Overall, 78.9% of children (n = 266) disclosed the toy breakage. A small proportion (6.5%) disclosed before any interviewer prompts, immediately after the interviewer re‐entered the room. The largest proportion of children disclosed during the open‐ended questioning phase (48.1%). Fewer children disclosed during direct questions (2.7%), yes/no questions (15.4%), and the interview closing (0.3%). An additional 5.9% disclosed only after the interview concluded, when told they would not get in trouble. The remaining 21.1% of children (n = 71) never disclosed the breakage (up until they were formally debriefed at the end of the session).

As an exploratory analysis, we examined whether child age predicted ANS arousal during the secrecy instruction, controlling for resting baseline HR. Results indicated a marginal negative association between age and secret‐keeping HR, B = −0.75, SE = 0.39, 95% CI [−1.52, 0.02], t = −1.92, p = 0.056, such that younger children tended to exhibit higher HR during the secrecy instruction than older children.

ANS Arousal and Disclosure

To examine whether children's ANS arousal was related to disclosure, a logistic regression was used to predict binary disclosure outcomes from children's average HR following the secrecy instruction, controlling for baseline resting HR and child age. As hypothesized, results showed that children who exhibited higher HRs following the secrecy instruction, indicative of higher ANS arousal, were significantly less likely to disclose the toy breakage, B = −0.047, SE = 0.013, p < 0.001; OR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.93, 0.98]. That is, each 1‐BPM increase in HR was associated with 5% lower odds of disclosure. Predicted probabilities show a steady decline as HR increases, with similar trends across ages (see Figure 1).

Self‐Reported Stress and Disclosure

Next, a logistic regression was conducted to examine whether post‐transgression self‐reported stress and calmness predicted disclosure, controlling for age and pre‐transgression self‐reported stress and calmness. Results indicated that post‐transgression self‐reported stress did not significantly predict disclosure likelihood (see Figure 2 for predicted probabilities across ages). However, children who reported greater post‐transgression calmness were significantly less likely to disclose the transgression, B = −0.80, SE = 0.28, p = 0.021; OR = 0.43, 95% CI [0.26, 0.79]. That is, each 1‐point increase in reported calmness (on the 1–3 scale) was associated with a 57% decrease in the odds of disclosure. Predicted probabilities showed a small decrease in disclosure likelihood as post‐transgression self‐reported calmness increased, with similar trends across ages (see Figure 3).

Age, Stress, and Disclosure

As hypothesized, age significantly predicted disclosure. Specifically, older children were more likely to disclose the toy breakage during the forensic interview than younger children (see Table 3). This age effect was observed in both the physiological and self‐reported stress models.

To examine whether the association between stress and disclosure varied developmentally, we tested for age by stress interactions for physiological stress, self‐reported stress, and self‐reported calmness. No significant interactions emerged, suggesting that the effect of stress on disclosure was consistent across the age range represented in the sample.

Discussion

The present study was the first to examine how stress, measured both physiologically through HR and subjectively through self‐report, relates to children's willingness to disclose a transgression. Much of the prior work on stress and memory in children has focused on events that are distressing, but that do not implicate the self or others, such as natural disasters or medical procedures [5, 8, 9]. In contrast, the transgression paradigm used in the present study required children to decide whether to report or conceal an event involving wrongdoing and an explicit secrecy instruction. Although this paradigm lacks the complexity and severity of maltreatment cases, it allows for examination of children's stress and reporting in a transgression context where disclosure may involve perceived interpersonal or personal risk [11].

As hypothesized, and consistent with the biological sensitivity to context framework [12, 13], children who exhibited greater ANS arousal immediately after being asked to conceal the event were significantly less likely to disclose the toy breakage during a subsequent forensic‐style interview. These findings contribute to a growing body of literature showing the role of biological sensitivity in children's social and emotional functioning. Physiological reactivity has been shown to be associated with a range of outcomes, including behavioral inhibition and increased attention to threat [12, 13, 14]. In the current context, increased ANS arousal may reflect an increased perception of interpersonal risk, leading children to avoid disclosure. Importantly, this extends previous work on the links between stress and memory [5, 6, 8, 36, 37] by showing that heightened autonomic arousal predicts reduced reporting in the near‐immediate interview, making a memory‐loss account less likely and a decision not to report more plausible.

Notably, the pattern of results for self‐reported stress diverged from the physiological findings. Specifically, post‐transgression stress ratings did not predict disclosure. On the other hand, post‐transgression calmness ratings did predict disclosure, but in the opposite direction of both our tentative hypothesis and the physiological findings. Specifically, children who reported feeling calmer post‐transgression were less likely to disclose.

One likely explanation for this divergence concerns limitations of self‐report in young children. Young children, particularly those in the preschool years, often struggle with emotional awareness and labeling due to ongoing development of emotional competence and emotion language [38, 39]. This developmental limitation may contribute to underreporting of stress even when physiological indicators, such as elevated HR, suggest otherwise. In the present study, however, age did not moderate the association between self‐reported stress or calmness and disclosure. If limited emotional insight due to developmental factors were the primary explanation for the discrepancy between children's self‐reports and their observed ANS arousal, we would expect this pattern to differ by age, with younger children showing a weaker alignment between their reported and physiological stress. Yet, the absence of a significant interaction suggests that underreporting of stress was not confined to the youngest children in the sample. The discordance between self‐reports and ANS arousal across ages underscores the importance of using multimethod approaches when assessing children's internal states, especially in transgression contexts.

A secondary consideration is that self‐reports in transgression contexts may be influenced by children's motivation to maintain secrecy. In the current study, the secrecy instruction immediately following the toy breakage introduced a clear motivation for children to suppress any indicators of stress that might invite suspicion or further questioning. Their responses may have functioned as a signal that nothing problematic occurred, thereby preserving the secret. Concealment of wrongdoing often involves strategic self‐presentation, and research shows that even young children are somewhat capable of modulating their verbal responses when they anticipate social evaluation or punishment [40, 41, 42]. However, the ability to lie convincingly increases with age, as it requires a more advanced theory of mind and inhibitory controls. Thus, if strategic concealment were the primary driver of self‐report discrepancies, age‐related differences might be expected.

At the same time, the lack of age interaction observed in the present study does not necessarily rule out concealment‐related explanations, because different developmental mechanisms may operate across ages. For older children, more advanced executive functioning and theory of mind may enable strategic management of verbal responses. In contrast, for younger children, their more limited emotional awareness and vocabulary may constrain their ability to accurately report their stress and calmness, resulting in underreporting that is less strategic and more reflective of measurement limitations. Because these processes (strategic response management in older children and limited introspective or verbal capacity in younger children) could both reduce observed associations between self‐reported stress and disclosure, their combined impact may obscure age‐based moderation effects. This reinforces concerns about the suitability of self‐report measures with children in this age range, particularly in transgression contexts where motivation to manage impressions may operate in tandem with limited emotional insight.

The misalignment between stress and calmness is also important to note. Calmness may have functioned as a strategic or protective response, whereas the stress item may have been harder for children to recognize or label accurately. This difference in interpretability may help explain why calmness, but not stress, was related to disclosure behavior.

Implications for Forensic Interviewer Understanding and Psychoeducation

The finding that children with heightened ANS arousal are less likely to disclose has important implications for forensic interview practices. Childhood adversity is associated with physiological dysregulation, which can manifest as either dampened or heightened ANS stress sensitivity [43, 44]. Consequently, children most likely to be referred for maltreatment investigations may also be those most prone to experiencing heightened ANS arousal. This raises concern that, even when interviewers follow neutral interviewing protocols, such as the one used in this study, children with heightened biological sensitivity may perceive disclosure as risky. For these children, a neutral approach may not be sufficient to offset internal stress responses and may instead require additional interviewer support to feel safe enough to share information. This finding is not intended to suggest physiological monitoring in investigative settings. Instead, it informs on the mechanisms underlying internal barriers to disclosure and highlights the potential value of experimentally testing various interviewing strategies aimed at reducing stress, particularly in biologically sensitive children.

Implications for Interviewing Strategies and Future Research

Prior research has shown that interviewer demeanor can moderate the effects of stress on memory and reporting. For example, Quas et al. [36] found that children with high physiological reactivity during a laboratory stressor exhibited better memory accuracy when interviewed by supportive adults but worse accuracy when interviewed by unsupportive adults. Similarly, Quas and Lench [37] reported that HR during memory retrieval was negatively associated with memory performance, but only when interviewer support was low. Together, these findings suggest that supportive interviewing may be particularly important for physiologically reactive children. This pattern aligns with the biological sensitivity to context theory [12], which suggests that biologically sensitive children are more affected by the quality of their environments, raising the possibility that rapport building and supportive interviewing could be especially beneficial in facilitating disclosure among highly reactive children. Future research should extend the current study by experimentally manipulating levels of interviewer support to determine whether enhanced support can mitigate stress experienced during forensic interviews and facilitate disclosure, particularly among children with elevated ANS arousal. Additionally, future research should examine whether children who exhibit heightened physiological reactivity at the time of the transgression also show heightened reactivity during subsequent forensic interviews. Understanding this continuity in stress responses would help clarify whether disclosure barriers reflect enduring biological sensitivity or situational stress tied to the event itself.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although the present study offers novel insight into how physiological and subjective stress responses relate to children's disclosure of a transgression, several limitations should be noted. First, although the broken‐toy paradigm was designed to enhance ecological validity while maintaining experimental control and a known ground truth, it differs in important ways from real‐world maltreatment cases. Breaking a toy is a minor, low‐stakes event, and children in this study were ultimately reassured that they would not get in trouble. In contrast, disclosures of abuse often involve fear, shame, and concerns about serious and lasting consequences such as family disruption or separation. In these higher stress contexts, ANS arousal is likely to be more intense and prolonged than in a minor transgression paradigm. Prolonged or severe stress may shift children's physiological responding [43, 44]. Thus, although the present findings highlight stress‐related barriers to disclosure, the intensity, timing, and mechanisms linking stress and disclosure may differ in more severe and chronic maltreatment contexts. As such, these findings should be interpreted with caution when applied to real‐world forensic settings, where the emotional stakes are higher and the barriers to disclosure are much more complex.

Relatedly, in the present study, only a brief interval elapsed between the secrecy instruction and the start of the forensic interview while the interviewer re‐entered the room, administered the post‐transgression self‐reported stress questions, and transitioned into the interview protocol. In contrast, forensic interviews in real‐world maltreatment investigations often occur after longer delays. ANS responses are typically rapid and operate on relatively short timescales [45]. Consistent with this, children's post‐secrecy HR had, on average, returned to baseline levels by the beginning of the forensic interview.

Furthermore, although delay length was not experimentally manipulated as a primary variable, the divided attention task introduced systematic variation in the post‐transgression time interval, which was statistically controlled for in all models and was not associated with disclosure. Nevertheless, future research should examine how longer delays between transgression and interview alter the pathways linking stress and disclosure, particularly when assessing systems with slower recovery trajectories than the ANS (e.g., the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal [HPA] axis).

Another consideration is that the present study focused on the threshold decision to disclosure rather than informativeness (e.g., the amount, specificity, and accuracy of children's reports). Although this binary outcome maps onto a central barrier in concealment contexts, future work should model the completeness and accuracy of children's narratives, as well as the likelihood of disclosure.

Additionally, although children's self‐reported stress and calmness added important insight, interpreting these findings was challenging. Post‐transgression self‐reported stress did not predict disclosure, and children who did not disclose actually reported more calmness post‐transgression, a pattern that diverged from their heightened ANS arousal. This discrepancy raises concerns about the validity of children's self‐reports in this context. In particular, the self‐reported items were not drawn from a standardized scale normed for very young children. “Stress” may have been too complex for younger children to understand, even with provided clarification [31, 46], and simpler terms such as “good” and “bad” may have been too ambiguous, leading to interpretations unrelated to emotional arousal. It is also possible that children were unaware of their internal states, lacked the verbal sophistication to report them accurately, or intentionally minimized their stress in an effort to conceal the transgression, highlighting the importance of using multimethod approaches to assess stress with children in these contexts [47].

Finally, although HR is a widely used index of ANS arousal, it is only one component of a broader network of physiological functioning. Future work should consider additional physiological indices, such as HR variability and electrodermal activity, which capture parasympathetic and sympathetic activity, respectively. Additionally, future research could examine physiological function outside of the ANS by looking at indices of the HPA axis, for instance, by measuring salivary cortisol. Multimodal physiological assessment may offer a more comprehensive picture of children's stress responses and their associations with disclosure behavior.

Conclusion

The present study contributes to a growing body of research suggesting that children's physiological stress responses are associated with how and whether they communicate negative experiences. This study provides new evidence that children's ANS arousal in response to secrecy demands plays a significant role in shaping their willingness to disclose a transgression, offering insight into how stress may operate as a barrier to disclosure. By assessing both autonomic and self‐reported stress, the results show how different indicators of stress can diverge and relate to disclosure decisions in distinct ways. Although self‐reports added useful information, they did not align with physiological arousal, particularly among children who chose not to disclose, raising several potential limitations to self‐report approaches. This finding underscores the importance of considering physiological measures of stress, in addition to self‐report, in transgression contexts. Importantly, the findings also point to the potential value of supportive interviewing practices for children with heightened autonomic arousal. Future work should explore how supportive interviewing can buffer the effects of stress and promote more complete and accurate reporting, particularly for children who are biologically sensitive.
