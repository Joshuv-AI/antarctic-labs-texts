Depression and general self-efficacy among first-generation college students in China: a longitudinal analysis
Authors: Xinqiao Liu, Ao Shen, Huirui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mental health issues among college students worldwide are becoming increasingly prominent. As a large and distinct subgroup within the student population, first-generation college students warrant particular attention in terms of their mental well-being. Research exploring the relationship between depression and general self-efficacy has faced limitations, as it has relied primarily on cross-sectional data and has rarely focused on this specific cohort. This longitudinal study examines the relationship between depression and general self-efficacy among first-generation college students in China. The findings revealed a significant negative correlation between depression and general self-efficacy. Furthermore, cross-lagged analysis indicated that general self-efficacy significantly predicts subsequent depression levels, whereas the negative predictive effect of depression on general self-efficacy warrants further investigation. These findings underscore the importance of enhancing general self-efficacy in preventing and alleviating depression and mental health issues among first-generation college students. These findings offer new insights for promoting the mental health development of this cohort and informing mental health education initiatives in higher education institutions.

Introduction

First-generation college students (FGCSs) typically refer to those whose biological parents or guardians have never received higher education, or who attended but did not complete college1,2. According to data from the Chinese College Students Survey (CCSS), the proportion of FGCSs in China remained consistently above 70% between 2011 and 2015, fluctuating between 71% and 74%. Among these, FGCSs from rural areas accounted for approximately 69.74%3. According to a report by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the average proportion of FGCSs among OECD member countries is approximately 53%4. The FGCSs have become a large sub-group within the broader student population, particularly in China where they constitute a significant proportion. Consequently, numerous scholars have conducted in-depth research on this demographic. Family capital exerts a significant positive influence on the academic achievement of FGCSs5. Compared to continuing-generation college students (CGCSs), whose parents or guardians have at least a bachelor’s degree, FGCSs are more likely to come from families with lower socioeconomic status and face greater financial pressures6. They are also more inclined to pursue higher education with the goal of helping their families improve their economic circumstances after graduation7. Consequently, they generally exhibit lower intrinsic motivation to learn, receive less campus support, and achieve poorer academic performance8. Their engagement in academic activities tends to be lower9,10, and they are often less likely to pursue further education after completing their undergraduate degrees11. Meanwhile, the minority status of FGCSs within universities and their limited interaction with faculty have significantly negatively impacted their academic achievement12. They tend to engage less frequently with instructors and demonstrate lower initiative in seeking academic support13. Faculty can effectively enhance the academic performance of FGCSs by conveying messages promoting a growth mindset14.

In recent years, with the continuous development of the socio-economic landscape and the intensifying pressures of academic competition and interpersonal relationships, mental health issues among college students have become increasingly prominent15. Approximately one-third of first-year college students have experienced mental health concerns in the past year16, and mental disorders are also highly prevalent17. A meta-analysis found that many college students face depression, with the highest prevalence rates observed in Africa, low- and middle-income countries, and among medical students18. Related studies also indicate that nearly one-quarter of university students from low- and middle-income countries suffer from depression19, with a marked upward trend in depression and anxiety issues experienced upon enrollment20. Depression exerts a negative impact on college students’ academic achievement, serving as a significant predictor of lower GPAs and higher dropout rates21. College students with depression typically exhibit persistent symptoms such as low mood, diminished interest, and loss of pleasure. Maintaining a consistently high level of subjective well-being can effectively reduce the risk of depression among college students22. Cross-lagged studies have also demonstrated that depression not only significantly predicts academic achievement negatively among college students23 but also negatively predicts their satisfaction with university life24. Therefore, as depression stands as one of the most prevalent mental health issues among college students, conducting in-depth research on depression among FGCSs—a distinct and significant segment of this population—holds substantial theoretical value and practical significance.

As a crucial component of social cognitive theory, self-efficacy and depression influence one another25. The concept of self-efficacy was first proposed by Albert Bandura, a psychologist at Stanford University in the United States, during the 1970s. It refers to an individual’s estimation and judgment of his or her own capacity to perform specific behaviors26. The German clinical and health psychologist Ralf Schwarzer27 subsequently proposed the concept of general self-efficacy—a comprehensive sense of confidence that individuals exhibit when confronted with diverse environmental challenges or novel situations—and developed a corresponding measurement scale for this construct. General self-efficacy plays a crucial role in the developmental process of college students, significantly influencing their learning motivation and the formation of critical thinking awareness28. It is positively correlated with life satisfaction29 and has a bidirectional effect on perceived stress30. Furthermore, general self-efficacy significantly influences the academic performance of first-generation college students31 and positively predicts their academic achievement32. Enhancing self-efficacy effectively alleviates depressive symptoms among college students33. College students’ academic self-efficacy exhibits distinct developmental trajectory patterns34. Since stress is negatively correlated with academic self-efficacy35, improving academic self-efficacy also significantly enhances students’ mental health36.

In summary, as a large subgroup within the college student population, the FGCSs face multiple challenges in the higher education environment. These challenges stem not only from material pressures and family responsibilities arising from their household economic backgrounds but also from difficulties integrating into campus life, insufficient access to academic resources, and limited interaction with faculty due to socioeconomic disparities. These factors intertwine, potentially increasing the prevalence of depression among FGCSs and collectively forming complex barriers that hinder their academic progress and personal development.

However, to date, research on the relationship between depression and general self-efficacy among FGCSs remains limited, particularly given the lack of systematic empirical support for the dynamic influence mechanisms between the two. This study employs a cross-lagged model using longitudinal data collected at two time points to explore the association between depressive symptoms and general self-efficacy among first-generation college students in China, as well as the direction of their longitudinal predictive relationship. This research provides robust evidence for universities to develop targeted mental health intervention strategies, effectively improving the mental well-being of FGCSs and enhancing their ability to cope with academic and life challenges. This finding has significant practical implications.

Literature review and research hypotheses

Mental health status of first-generation college students

Mental health issues among college students arise from multiple interrelated factors and are significantly associated with gender, race/ethnicity, religious beliefs, relationship status, and economic circumstances37. Students with specific characteristics—such as minority identity, international student status, or low socioeconomic background—face heightened risks of social isolation and are more prone to mental health problems38. Moreover, compared with domestic students, international students in the United States exhibit higher rates of depressive symptoms39 and relatively higher rates of suicide attempts40. Family SES significantly and positively predicts college students’ mental health41. The lower the self-rated family economic status is, the poorer the mental health level42. Furthermore, the greater the perceived uncertainty stress is, the greater the prevalence of mental disorders among college students43. Frequent alcohol abuse, smoking, or marijuana use also leads to poorer mental health outcomes44. Moreover, psychological stress is significantly associated with suicidal behavior45. In China, college students’ physical fitness is closely linked to their mental health. A certain level of physical activity can directly improve students’ emotional state46, with enhancing their lung capacity and endurance running ability being effective methods for alleviating psychological abnormalities47. In particular, among university students with left-behind experience, the prevalence of mental health issues is significantly greater than that among those without such experience48. Like all college students, those in the FGCSs also experience a wide range of mental health issues, including but not limited to depression, anxiety, perceived stress, alcohol use disorders, and substance use disorders. However, compared with CGCSs, FGCSs perceive a broader range of stress sources49, encompassing unique stressors such as difficult cultural adaptation and a lack of family support. Therefore, they face more severe academic pressures, academic difficulties, and financial hardships50,51. Consequently, FGCSs exhibit more severe depressive symptoms52,53. They also exhibit higher levels of anxiety54, relatively low self-esteem55, poorer self-efficacy and emotional regulation abilities32, and lower rates of mental health service utilization56. Research indicates that during the COVID-19 pandemic, FGCSs faced greater economic pressures than CGCSs57 and exhibited a higher risk of depression58. Following the transition to online remote learning, FGCSs also experienced a significant increase in depressive symptoms and perceived stress, with Latinos and female students showing particularly pronounced increases in stress levels59. However, other scholars contend that no significant differences exist between the FGCSs and CGCSs in self-reported mental health levels, anxiety symptoms, stress, and depression levels54,60.

A sense of belonging is a key factor in sustaining student engagement at school. FGCSs exhibit particularly pronounced daily fluctuations in their sense of belonging61 and report lower levels than CGCSs students do62, making them more susceptible to feelings of alienation within the campus environment. FGCSs experience enhances subjective well-being when individuals are exposed to greater levels of social and interpersonal trust63. Adequate social-emotional support effectively mitigates anxiety symptoms64. Concurrently, reducing perceived stress represents an effective strategy for improving the mental health of individuals with FGCSs65.

The relationship between depression and general self-efficacy

In studies examining depression and general self-efficacy, some scholars have investigated the mediating role of general self-efficacy in the process by which other factors influence depression. A study involving 2,070 Chinese children and adolescents revealed that depression was positively correlated with academic burnout, with self-efficacy partially mediating this relationship66. Another cross-sectional questionnaire survey of 11,031 Chinese residents also demonstrated that self-efficacy plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between personality traits and depression, potentially helping to reduce the risk of depressive episodes67. For college students undergoing depression treatment, the mediating effect of general self-efficacy persists. For example, Zhang et al. reported that family health negatively correlates with depressive symptoms. Self-efficacy moderates both the direct and indirect effects of family health on depressive symptoms68. Li et al. in a survey of 49,717 college students from 106 universities in China, reported that general self-efficacy significantly influenced students’ depression levels and mediated the effect of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) on reducing depression levels69. Qin et al. similarly reported that general self-efficacy mediates the effect of physical exercise on negative emotions (including depression and anxiety) among college students70.

In studies examining the relationship between depression and general self-efficacy, most findings indicate a negative correlation between these two variables. For example, according to a survey by Jalilian et al., 37% of medical students exhibited varying degrees of depressive symptoms, and the severity of depression was significantly negatively correlated with self-efficacy71. Karr and White reported in a study involving 582 college students that those with anxiety or depression tended to have lower academic self-efficacy72. Additionally, research has shown that among college students, those with higher self-efficacy exhibit fewer depressive symptoms, whereas those with lower self-efficacy are more prone to depression73. Concurrently, the presence of anxiety and depressive symptoms can diminish an individual’s self-efficacy74. Therefore, interventions aimed at enhancing self-efficacy through skill training and similar approaches may prove most effective in alleviating depressive symptoms75. However, some scholars hold differing views. Gencoglu et al., after studying 542 university students in Turkey, reported that general self-efficacy does not significantly predict depression, anxiety, or stress76. Using social science students and science students as binary variables, Adhikari et al. reported that for social science students, depression was significantly negatively correlated with general self-efficacy; however, for science students, no significant association was found between depression and general self-efficacy77.

In summary, current research on the relationship between depression and general self-efficacy remains relatively limited, with most studies relying on cross-sectional data to explore the correlations and associations between the two variables. Furthermore, existing research has focused predominantly on the general college student population, with insufficient attention given to a specific group of FGCSs. Therefore, this study conducted a longitudinal tracking investigation targeting FGCSs to more accurately elucidate the complex relationship between depression and general self-efficacy. This approach not only effectively supplements the limitations of existing research but also provides more timely and longitudinal empirical evidence for deepening our understanding of the mental health mechanisms within this distinct group.

The present study

This study aims to examine the longitudinal relationship between depression and general self-efficacy among first-generation college students. According to the relevant literature, factors such as age, gender, ethnicity, personality, and family socioeconomic status may influence depression and general self-efficacy78–80. Therefore, we also validated the robustness of our findings by controlling for these variables. Compared with previous studies, we aim to make the following contributions to enrich the relevant research.

First, this study focuses on a specific group of FGCSs. This cohort typically faces multiple unique challenges during higher education, including academic adjustment, financial pressures, and family expectations. Their mental health status and developmental trajectories of general self-efficacy may differ significantly from those of non-first-generation college students. However, current academic research on this group remains limited, with a scarcity of relevant empirical data. This study provides new empirical support for in-depth exploration of the mental health status of FGCSs.

Second, this study focuses on China’s FGCSs, delving into the unique cultural context and educational environment within China’s higher education system. Unlike their Western counterparts, who tend to pursue independent developmental paths, China’s FGCSs often closely link personal growth with family responsibilities81. Compared with previous research focused primarily on FGCSs within Western cultural contexts, our study provides an empirical foundation for cross-cultural comparisons in understanding the psychological characteristics and general self-efficacy of FGCSs across diverse cultural backgrounds.

Furthermore, this study employs a longitudinal tracking design to empirically examine the developmental trajectories of depression and general self-efficacy among FGCSs, as well as the potential longitudinal relationship between these two variables. Previous research has typically relied on cross-sectional data for correlation analysis, which struggles to reveal the dynamic associations and longitudinal predictive relationship between variables fully.

On the basis of relevant theories and the literature, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1

There is a significant negative correlation between depression and general self-efficacy among first-generation college students.

Hypothesis 2

The level of depression among first-generation college students can negatively predict their general self-efficacy.

Hypothesis 3

The general self-efficacy of first-generation college students can negatively predict their level of depression.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This study conducted a longitudinal tracking survey in two phases among university students enrolled in institutions within the Beijing region of China. The baseline survey in the first phase was defined as Time 1 (abbreviated as T1). A follow-up survey of the same cohort of students one year later was defined as Time 2 (abbreviated as T2). The one-year interval between the two phases effectively yielded two rounds of longitudinal data related to the subjects’ life and psychological conditions.

Regarding dataset selection, we first excluded student samples whose father or mother did not meet the educational attainment criterion of “college degree or higher” on the basis of the definition of first-generation college students. The remaining data subsequently underwent meticulous verification, and student samples with missing data at key measurement scales were further excluded. Ultimately, 1,038 college students meeting the definition of first-generation college students were included in this study. They hail from 15 undergraduate institutions across the Beijing region, encompassing comprehensive universities, science and engineering universities, and humanities and social sciences universities. Their academic backgrounds span various disciplines including engineering, science, literature, and medicine. Among them, 577 were male (55.59%), 461 were female (44.41%), 926 were Han Chinese (89.38%), and 958 (92.29%) came from families with a socioeconomic status at the middle level or below. At T1, the average age of the participants was 21.73 years (SD = 0.909), and at T2, it was 22.73 years (SD = 0.909), which is consistent with the age progression pattern observed in longitudinal studies of the same cohort.

It should be noted that this study complies with the relevant provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin University (Approval No.: TJUE-2022-188; Approval Date: May 4, 2022). Furthermore, informed consent has been obtained from all participants and their legal guardians.

Measures

Depression

This study employed the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-42) to measure participants’ depressive levels82. The DASS-42 is a 42-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess three negative emotional states: depression, anxiety, and stress. It is widely used in the field of psychological problem monitoring. Fourteen items assess participants’ depressive mood states via a 4-point scale ranging from “0” (not applicable at all) to “3” (very applicable or most applicable). The participants’ total scores range from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating more severe depression. The calculations revealed that within the sample data used in this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale was α = 0.904 at T1 and α = 0.915 at T2, indicating high reliability.

General self-efficacy

This study employed the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) to measure participants’ general self-efficacy83. The GSES is a psychometric instrument developed on the basis of Bandura’s social cognitive theory. It comprises 10 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “1” (indicating “strongly agree”) to “4” (indicating “strongly disagree”). The participant’s total score is the sum of all item scores, ranging from 10 to 40 points. A higher score indicates greater general self-efficacy among students. The calculations revealed that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale was α = 0.878 at T1 and α = 0.887 at T2, indicating good reliability.

Control variables

Age, gender, ethnicity, self-reported personality type, and family socioeconomic status were included as control variables. Age was measured in years. Gender was coded as 1 = male, 2 = female. Ethnicity was coded as 1 = Han, 2 = ethnic minority. Self-reported personality type was scored on a scale of 1 to 9, with 1 indicating greater introversion and 9 indicating greater extroversion. Family socioeconomic status was assessed using the original survey item on students’ perceived family socioeconomic background and coded as 1 = upper class, 2 = upper-middle class, 3 = middle class, 4 = lower-middle class, 5 = lower class. These covariates were selected because previous studies have suggested that demographic background, personality-related characteristics, and socioeconomic conditions may be associated with depression and general self-efficacy. In Model 5, these variables were entered as exogenous covariates to control for their potential effects on depression and general self-efficacy.

Data analysis

This study employed Stata 15.0 and Mplus 7.4 for data processing and analysis. First, Stata 15.0 was used to clean the data and conduct descriptive statistics and correlation analyses for depression levels and general self-efficacy. Second, following recommendations in the literature, item parceling was performed using Mplus 7.4. Items from the DASS-42 and GSES were each divided into three groups to reduce measurement error84,85. Subsequently, a cross-lagged model was constructed using Mplus 7.4 to examine model fit and test cross-lagged paths. Finally, control variables were incorporated into the analysis via Mplus 7.4 to ensure their effects on the dependent variables were effectively controlled, thereby obtaining cross-lagged results regarding depression and general self-efficacy among first-generation college students. To enhance measurement comparability across waves, the same instruments, item wording, response options, scoring procedures, and item-parceling strategy were used at T1 and T2. The DASS-42 depression subscale and the GSES also showed satisfactory internal consistency at both waves. Therefore, the cross-lagged analyses were interpreted primarily as prospective longitudinal associations between latent constructs. Because formal longitudinal measurement invariance testing was not conducted, observed mean-level differences across T1 and T2 were interpreted descriptively and cautiously rather than as definitive evidence of latent mean change.

The cross-lagged model is illustrated in Fig. 1. Model 1 is an autoregressive model used to examine the respective stability of depression and general self-efficacy at T1 and T2. Model 2 builds upon the autoregressive path (Model 1) by adding a cross-lagged path from depression at T1 to general self-efficacy at T2, thereby investigating the impact of depression on general self-efficacy. Model 3 builds upon the autoregressive path (Model 1) by adding a cross-lagged path from general self-efficacy at T1 to depression at T2, aiming to examine the influence of general self-efficacy on depression. Model 4 is a bidirectional model incorporating all the previous autoregressive paths and cross-lagged paths to analyze the reciprocal relationship between depression and general self-efficacy. Model 5 builds upon Model 4 by incorporating age, gender, ethnicity, self-reported personality type, and family socioeconomic status as control variables and examining their effects on depression and general self-efficacy at both the T1 and T2 time points.

Additionally, to assess the fit of each model, we employed metrics such as the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), comparative fit index (CFI), and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI)86,87. Generally, a model is considered to fit well if RMSEA < 0.10 (superior fit ≤ 0.06), SRMR < 0.10, CFI > 0.90 (superior fit ≥ 0.95), or TLI > 0.90.

Results

Descriptive and correlation analysis

Table 1 shows the mean scores, standard deviations, and correlation coefficients between depression and general self-efficacy at T1 and T2. According to the scoring descriptions for depression severity in the DASS-42 user manual, total scores of 0–9 fall within the “Normal” range, 10–13 within the “Mild” range, 14–20 within the “Moderate” range, 21–27 within the “Severe” range, and 28 or above within the “Extremely Severe” range82.

The descriptive analysis results indicate that students’ average depression score at T1 was 7.707 (SD = 6.966), whereas at T2, the average score reached 7.724 (SD = 7.203). These findings suggest that first-generation college students’ depression levels remained within the normal range overall at both T1 and T2. However, the depression levels slightly increased as the students progressed through their academic years. In terms of general self-efficacy scores, the mean score at T1 was 29.185 (SD = 5.112), whereas the mean score at T2 was 29.092 (SD = 5.172), both indicating a generally high level. However, the general self-efficacy scores slightly decreased with increasing years of education, potentially reflecting changes in confidence when facing challenges associated with more years of education.

Correlation analysis revealed that in each academic year, the depression levels of first-generation college students in the sample were significantly negatively correlated with general self-efficacy (P < 0.05). Similarly, depression levels across different years were significantly negatively correlated with general self-efficacy (P < 0.05). This further confirms the close association between mental health status and general self-efficacy. Hypothesis 1 is supported.

Model comparison

The fit indices of the models are shown in Table 2. Model 1 indicates that the autoregressive relationship between depression and general self-efficacy exhibits good fit indices (RMSEA = 0.065, SRMR = 0.037, CFI = 0.975, TLI = 0.967), with a significant autoregressive effect, suggesting good stability of the measured variables from T1 to T2. Model 2 examined the predictive role of depression at T1 on general self-efficacy at T2. The results indicated acceptable model fit (RMSEA = 0.065, SRMR = 0.033, CFI = 0.975, TLI = 0.967). Model 3 examined the predictive role of general self-efficacy at T1 on depression at T2. The results indicated good model fit (RMSEA = 0.064, SRMR = 0.024, CFI = 0.976, TLI = 0.968). Model 4 also demonstrated good fit (RMSEA = 0.064, SRMR = 0.022, CFI = 0.976, TLI = 0.968) and can be used to examine the bidirectional relationship between depression and general self-efficacy. Model 5 incorporates additional control variables, including age, gender, ethnicity, self-reported personality type, and family socioeconomic status, in addition to Model 4. The results indicate that the model maintains a robust fit (RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.019, CFI = 0.975, TLI = 0.964), demonstrating that the model remains stable after these demographic variables are added.

Furthermore, comparative analysis reveals that Model 5 has the following fit indices: RMSEA = 0.049 (≤ 0.06) and CFI = 0.975 (≥ 0.95). Although Model 5’s χ² value increased, this was offset by a reduction in χ²/DF due to its expanded degrees of freedom. Considering all other indices collectively, Model 5 demonstrates the optimal fit.

Cross-lagged analysis

The standardized stability coefficients and cross-lagged coefficients for Models 1–5 are shown in Table 3. Overall, in Models 1–5, the autoregressive coefficients β for the variable depression from T1 to T2 were 0.573, 0.585, 0.534, 0.546, and 0.535, respectively. The autoregressive coefficients β for the variable general self-efficacy were 0.676, 0.653, 0.692, 0.673, and 0.636, respectively. All the autoregressive effects were statistically significant (p < 0.05). This finding indicates that across the five models, both depression and general self-efficacy are stable over time.

In Model 2, the cross-lagged coefficient β from depression at T1 to general self-efficacy at T2 was − 0.060, p < 0.05, indicating that depression levels at T1 significantly negatively predict general self-efficacy at T2. However, in Models 4 and 5, this negative predictive relationship was not significant (p > 0.05). In Model 3, the cross-lagged coefficient β for general self-efficacy at T1 predicting depression at T2 was − 0.109, p < 0.05, indicating that general self-efficacy at T1 significantly negatively predicted depression at T2. This negative predictive relationship remained significant in both Model 4 and Model 5 (p < 0.05).

On the basis of the model comparison results from the preceding section, Model 5 demonstrated the best fit. Therefore, we focus on the cross-lagged results of Model 5 (Fig. 2) and adopt these results as the final empirical findings. In Model 5, the cross-lagged coefficient β from depression at T1 to general self-efficacy at T2 was − 0.037. However, this effect is not statistically significant (p > 0.05), indicating that depression levels at T1 do not significantly predict general self-efficacy at T2 in a negative direction. In another path analysis, the cross-lagged coefficient β from general self-efficacy at T1 to depression at T2 was − 0.103, and this effect was significant (p < 0.05). This finding indicates that, after controlling for other variables, general self-efficacy at T1 significantly and negatively predicts depression at T2. These findings further support the dynamic relationship between depression and general self-efficacy.

In summary, after controlling for other variables, a unidirectional predictive relationship exists between general self-efficacy and depression among the FGCSs. Enhancing their general self-efficacy can effectively reduce subsequent depression levels and prevent potential mental health issues. Hypothesis 2 was not validated, whereas Hypothesis 3 was confirmed.

Discussion

Research has indicated that FGCSs often exhibit more severe depressive symptoms52,53 and generally lower self-efficacy32 because of the unique challenges they face. Therefore, employing a cross-lagged model to analyze the relationship between depression and general self-efficacy among FGCSs becomes particularly necessary. These findings provide crucial evidence for developing targeted intervention strategies to effectively prevent and alleviate depression among this population while enhancing their general self-efficacy.

The descriptive analysis results indicate that, first, the depression levels among China’s FGCSs fall within the normal range. However, as academic years progress, their depression levels gradually increase, suggesting that the risk of depression increases for FGCSs as they become more deeply immersed in university life. This finding differs from those of previous studies, which have generally suggested that the prevalence of depression among college students gradually decreases as they advance through their academic years88,89. This may be because during their university years, FGCSs often face multiple challenges—including greater financial pressures, academic adjustment difficulties, and heightened family expectations—than CGCSs do50,51. As they advance through their studies, coursework becomes more demanding, academic competition intensifies, and they may also shoulder increased family responsibilities. The cumulative weight of these pressures gradually triggers and exacerbates depressive symptoms. Second, overall, the self-efficacy of China’s FGCSs is generally positive but shows a declining trend, which is consistent with previous research90. This may stem from the potential disadvantages that FGCSs face in accessing social support and resources, coupled with a lack of effective guidance and assistance from family and social networks. Consequently, they find it more challenging to develop effective coping strategies when confronted with difficulties, which, over time, can negatively impact their general self-efficacy.

The results of the correlation analysis revealed a significant negative relationship between depression and general self-efficacy in China’s FGCSs. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of most previous studies71,73. This implies that when individuals with FGCSs possess greater general self-efficacy, they exhibit relatively lower frequencies of depressive symptoms.

Further analysis of the cross-lagged model revealed that general self-efficacy among China’s FGCSs negatively predicted their depression levels in the subsequent year. This finding demonstrates the prospective protective role of general self-efficacy in the development of depressive symptoms among this cohort. Specifically, in terms of the temporal dimension, higher levels of self-efficacy in the early stages may provide them with greater psychological resilience91,92. This resilience enables them to cope more effectively with subsequent pressures related to academics, finances, and family, thereby reducing the occurrence of depressive symptoms or mitigating their severity93. Previous research has focused primarily on the promotional role of general self-efficacy in enhancing college students’ life satisfaction and academic achievement31,32,94. This study expands the perspective of general self-efficacy research. Moreover, the discovery of this dynamic predictive relationship deepens our understanding of the mechanisms influencing depressive symptoms among individuals with FGCSs. This study also provides crucial empirical evidence for the development of targeted mental health intervention strategies, suggesting that enhancing the general self-efficacy of FGCSs may effectively prevent and alleviate the development of their depressive symptoms. However, depression, as one of the most common mental health issues, is influenced by other variables such as social support, academic pressure, and family environment. Research has shown that students experiencing high levels of academic and family stress are more prone to depression95. College students who have experienced childhood trauma or childhood psychological abuse (CPA) also tend to exhibit higher rates of depression96,97. These variables excluded from the present study likely exhibit complex interactions or chained mediating effects between general self-efficacy and depression. Therefore, future research may consider constructing a more comprehensive multivariate model integrating factors such as academic stress, family environment, and childhood trauma experiences. This approach would systematically examine how these variables interact with general self-efficacy to influence depressive symptoms among FGCSs, thereby revealing their underlying mechanisms more fully.

Furthermore, through validation of model fit, this study failed to effectively confirm the negative predictive effect of FGCSs depression severity on general self-efficacy. However, in Model 2, this negative predictive relationship demonstrated statistical significance. Concurrently, Yang et al. also reported through empirical research that depressive symptoms can predict subsequent levels of self-efficacy98. This suggests that, among the unique cohort of FGCSs, the inverse relationship between depression and general self-efficacy may exhibit certain complexities, potentially moderated or mediated by other latent variables, warranting further investigation.

In summary, the findings of this study confirm that there is a significant negative correlation between depression and general self-efficacy among China’s FGCSs. Furthermore, general self-efficacy significantly predicts depression levels in a negative direction. This implies that enhancing general self-efficacy can alleviate depression among FGCSs, thereby improving their mental health.

Against the backdrop of rapid expansion in higher education, the FGCSs aspires to break the cycle of family poverty99 and achieve comprehensive personal development. Previous research has indicated that individuals who engage in regular physical exercise tend to exhibit higher levels of general self-efficacy100,101. Additionally, a healthy family environment and school setting102, along with increased feelings of belonging, can also increase self-efficacy103,104. Therefore, based on this study and existing research, we recommend constructing a support systems across multiple dimensions to enhance the general self-efficacy of FGCSs. This approach aims to reduce their risk of depression and promote sustainable mental health development. First, at the institutional level, universities should enhance sports facilities and organize physical activities to encourage FGCSs to actively participate in various forms of exercise. As students continually challenge themselves and push their limits through physical activity, they tangibly experience improvements in their capabilities. This sense of accomplishment directly strengthens their general self-efficacy. Second, cultivate a supportive campus environment. Institutions should strive to foster an inclusive and welcoming campus culture, promoting interaction and communication between faculty and students as well as among peers. Establishing counseling centers provides professional psychological support and guidance, promptly addressing challenges encountered during university adaptation and enhancing students’ sense of belonging and security on campus. When students feel the warmth and support of the institution, their general self-efficacy increases, bolstering their confidence to tackle academic and life challenges and thereby reducing depression risk. Furthermore, fostering an understanding, supportive, and encouraging family environment is equally crucial. Reducing negative pressure from families on FGCSs and allowing them to feel warmth and trust can effectively strengthen their psychological resilience, increase their confidence in facing difficulties and challenges, thereby enhancing their general self-efficacy and effectively alleviating depression.

In addition, universities implementing interventions based on social-emotional skills105, combined with AI-assisted screening for depression risk to enable early prevention106, is another effective method to alleviate depression and improve college students’ mental health.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, regarding measurement tools, although the selected scales possess reasonable reliability and validity and are widely recognized, they are all self-report questionnaires. This may be subject to social desirability bias or recall bias among participants, potentially leading to discrepancies between the measured results and actual circumstances. Second, significant differences exist between Chinese and Western cultures. While this study focuses on China’s FGCSs, whether its conclusions can be extended to first-generation college students from diverse cultural backgrounds warrants further exploration. Third, we examined the longitudinal relationship between depression and general self-efficacy among FGCSs using data collected at two time points. We hope that future research will gather longitudinal data across more time points to more comprehensively reveal the relationship between these two variables, thereby enhancing the stability and generalizability of the research conclusions. Fourth, all participants in this study were drawn from universities in the Beijing area. This geographical concentration limits the generalizability of the findings to different higher education settings and FGCSs in other regions of China. Future research should adopt a multi-center, cross-regional sampling design, incorporating samples from universities in central and western regions as well as prefecture-level cities to enhance the external validity of the findings.

Conclusions

This longitudinal study examined the relationship between depression and general self-efficacy among China’s FGCSs, yielding the following core findings. First, the overall depression levels among China’s FGCSs remained within the normal range, though they showed a slight upward trend with increasing academic year. Meanwhile, their general self-efficacy remained generally positive but exhibited a slight decline. This pattern suggests the need to monitor the psychological adaptation process of FGCSs throughout their university years. Particularly as practical challenges such as academic pressure and employment anxiety become more pronounced, their mental health status and general self-efficacy may face new challenges.

Second, there exists a significant negative correlation between depression among Chinese FGCSs and their general self-efficacy. That is, the higher the general self-efficacy of FGCSs, the lower their depression levels tend to be, and vice versa. This indicates a strong mutual association between the two at the static level, corroborating the relational hypothesis proposed by social cognitive theory.

Third, cross-lagged analysis revealed that general self-efficacy among China’s FGCSs significantly predicted their subsequent depression levels, demonstrating the prospective protective role of general self-efficacy in mental health. This suggests that higher general self-efficacy may serve as a buffer and protective factor against the onset and progression of depression. However, the reverse predictive effect of depression on general self-efficacy did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that due to multifaceted influences such as social support and family factors, whether depression further weakens the general self-efficacy of FGCSs requires further validation and clarification through future longitudinal studies with longer time spans and more refined tracking.
