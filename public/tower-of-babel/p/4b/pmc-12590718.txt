Published: 2025

Background

Frailty is defined as a clinical state of decline whereby an older person is more vulnerable to dependency or mortality when exposed to an external stressor [1]. Frailty in older adults is associated with an increased risk of falls, and higher likelihood of all cause hospitalisation and prolonged hospital stay and institutionalisation. Within hospital settings, it is [3], reported that 49% of adult inpatients are being classified as frail. The prognosis for frail older adults is particularly poor when hospitalisation leads to new or additional disability in self-care activities of daily living (ADLs). Although frailty is considered a pre-disability state, the transition to disability is common following acute illness, with studies showing that only 30% of such individuals regain their prior level of ADL function within one year after discharge from the hospital. [4]. Targeted multidisciplinary intervention can stall or reverse this cascade of functional decline.

The 2020 FORTRESS study [5] implemented an embedded individualised multicomponent frailty intervention commencing in hospital where participants received extra sessions of physiotherapy, dietetics, and pharmacy during their admission. The intervention continued in the community in the form of 1–2 home visits plus 4–6 weekly phone calls from a community implementation facilitator in the subsequent 4–6 weeks post hospital discharge to ensure the recommendations from the frailty clinicians were adhered to. Post-recruitment, auditing of electronic medical records of some participants in the control group of the 2020 FORTRESS study showed that frailty assessment in the acute setting occurred in only 17% of cases. Few participants received guideline recommended interventions. Physiotherapy treatment was limited, with only 23% receiving progressive resistance strength training. Nutritional intervention was only provided in 26% of cases, and weight was recorded in only 10% of admissions. Barriers identified during this audit included time constraints, limited staff awareness or confidence in using frailty tools, insufficient allied health resources, and a lack of structured processes for initiating interventions. These findings directly informed the design of FORTRESS Plus, which aims to improve implementation through targeted staff training, designated intervention roles, and structured post-discharge pathways [6].

The hypothesis of FORTRESS Plus is that effective frailty intervention requires a more robust intervention than was offered by the FORTRESS Study to reduce or reverse frailty and reduce hospital readmissions.

Additionally, FORTRESS Plus seeks to address low mood in frail older adults which was not previously addressed in the 2020 FORTRESS study. Older adults with low mood and depressive symptomatology are 2–4 times more likely to develop frailty and 10–15% of older adults are experiencing depressive symptoms [7]. Low mood underpins low energy levels, poor sleep, malnutrition, polypharmacy and reduced physical activity and social isolation which are all contributors to frailty [8]. Frailty screening hence should not only address the physical domains of frailty but also include measures of mood and social frailty so that an individualised, holistic intervention can be established [9]. Providing older adults with supervised progressive resistance and balance exercise as an intervention not only improves physical function but also enhances mental wellbeing, self-esteem, and cognitive function [10]. Exercise as an intervention to address low mood is comparable to traditional psychotherapy or pharmacological interventions and can be initiated in the community in the home setting, enhancing its cost-effectiveness compared to inpatient care [10].

Study design/methods

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the REGIS Northern Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: 2023/ETH00649), as well as Site Specific Governance Approval (ID: 2023/STE01740) and recruitment commenced in October 2023. As of July 2024, 141 participants have been recruited, exceeding the originally planned sample size of 106. This was due to higher-than-anticipated participant availability and ongoing referrals during the recruitment period. The study design and procedures are outlined below.

FORTRESS PLUS is a single arm intervention study with comparison to historical controls. This study protocol is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Intervention Trials (SPIRIT) checklist available as supplementary file. The intervention group from the FORTRESS study will act as a control group for the FORTRESS PLUS study. Comparison between FORTRESS and FORTRESS PLUS outcomes will occur when outcome assessments for FORTRESS Plus conclude 12 months after the start of recruitment.

Initial identification and screening of all eligible participants on participating wards at Hornsby Ku-ring-gai Hospital, Sydney, Australia will be done by the Study Coordinator using the study inclusion criteria. These wards are the cardiology/general medicine ward 2D, the rehabilitation ward 3D and the general medicine/stroke unit 4D. The inclusion criteria include hospitalised older people aged 75 and over who are living in the community with a Mini Mental State Examination score of 22 and above, with an expected life expectancy of at least 12 months (measured by the illness severity scale) and a FRAIL scale score of 3 and above.

This study has received opt out approval from HREC and so all persons across participating wards are eligible unless they choose to opt out. If people elect to opt-out of the study, they will receive usual care but will not receive the frailty specific interventions while in hospital and they will not be followed up in the community once discharged. After the recruitment process, the EQ-5D-5 L health-related quality of life (QOL) questionnaire will be completed with each eligible participant. A Frailty Management Plan will be created by the Study Coordinator for each eligible participant on their Electronic Medical Records to include their FRAIL scale score, the frailty intervention they have received during their hospital stay as well as their continued participation in the community intervention. This document will be sent at the time of discharge along with their discharge summary to the person’s GP. The participant’s nominated carer/family member will also be contacted by the Study Coordinator to inform them of the participant’s involvement. Additionally, the study Pharmacist has provided a document for each participant with their DBI (Drug Burden Index) which is used to assess a person’s total exposure to medications with anticholinergic and sedative properties. The document will also identify whether polypharmacy is occurring, defined by the regular use of 5 or more medications at the same time, and will list any recommendations to the GP regarding review or deprescribing of medications. This will be provided to each participant by the study Pharmacist at the time of discharge.

The participant’s General Practitioner will be informed of their patient’s FRAIL Scale score, and their involvement in FORTRESS Plus Study. Participants will receive up to 7 visits at home by an Exercise Physiologist who will design an individualised exercise program and encourage adherence to any recommendations made by the hospital dietician or/and pharmacist. In addition to the home-based intervention, participants will be recommended to attend appropriate group-based community exercise classes located within a 5 km radius of their residence. These will be identified using the publicly available NSW Active and Healthy website, which lists evidence-based exercise programs tailored to older adults. Where available, frailty-specific or strength and balance-focused classes will be prioritised. The ward specific interventions are outlined in Table 1.

Recruitment and eligibility

We aim to recruit 106 older men and women aged 75 years or more using eligibility criteria based on those applied in the 2020 FORTRESS study, with minor adaptations (a revised MMSE cut-off) to enhance feasibility and participant engagement. To explore feasibility in a younger cohort, we will additionally aim to recruit some participants aged 70–74 years. These individuals will not be included in the main outcome analyses but may provide insight into the acceptability and impact of earlier intervention in pre-frail or frail adults below 75 years. If the potential participant scored 3 or more on the FRAIL Scale, they are classified as frail and included in the study if they met additional eligibility criteria. These include: a MMSE (Mini Mental State Examination) score of 22 or higher, the ability to communicate in English and/or able to cooperate with the program, community residency (they did not reside in an aged care facility), had not been admitted to hospital this admission due to a stroke, and the likelihood that they would survive at least a year post recruitment (based on the modified version of the Illness Severity Rating). The FORTRESS PLUS intervention follows the Asia Pacific Guidelines for Frailty Management [11], and is accepted as best practice, so all participants receive the intervention unless they specifically opted out either verbally or by filling out a written opt out form.

Intervention

Participants admitted during the intervention phase will receive an individualised, multidisciplinary intervention targeting the components of frailty identified during assessment—such as fatigue, weight loss, mobility limitations, polypharmacy, and comorbidities. The intervention is tailored accordingly; for example, participants with fatigue may receive medication review, energy-conserving strategies, and adjusted exercise prescriptions, while those without fatigue but with reduced ambulation may receive more intensive physical reconditioning. Additional file 1 outlines the TIDieR framework describing the FORTRESS Plus intervention.

The FORTRESS Plus intervention is divided into two phases

The Hospital Intervention

After being identified as frail, a pathway of care will be initiated. Participants will receive dietetic review, pharmacy review and a comprehensive geriatric assessment during their acute admission if indicated. All participants receive the pharmacy review to identify possible polypharmacy and those who have been identified will receive further intervention for the pharmacist to deprescribe potentially inappropriate medications. Only those who have been identified at the time of screening as having unintentionally lost 5 kg or 5% of their body weight over the past year receive the dietary intervention. They will receive nutritional education, informing them what their optimal oral intake is. Upon discretion from the dietician this may warrant possible referral for protein supplementation for continuation at home post discharge or requesting diet order during their admission to meet these requirements i.e. a high protein high energy diet. The dietician may also create short term goals with the patient such as “finish all meals” and refer onto a speech pathologist if there are concerns with swallowing or feeding at mealtimes. All participants receive a comprehensive geriatric assessment. Recommendations are then made for the management of frailty and are detailed in the Frailty Management Plan on the Electronic Medical Record. A copy of this is then sent via secure electronic messaging to the participant’s GP along with other relevant discharge information. Malnutrition screening, nutrition education and advice will be provided by the ward dietitian. Participants displaying signs of polypharmacy defined by the regular use of 5 or more medications at the same time will receive a review from the study pharmacist. The study pharmacist will then complete a medication history review and review hospital admission and discharge medications for each participant. The DBI (Drug Burden Index) score is recorded by the study pharmacist and will consult with the Study Geriatrician regarding any adjustments to medications. Vitamin D supplementation (cholecalciferol 1,000 IU daily) will also be recommended to all participants by the study geriatrician, to be continued throughout the intervention period. The FRAIL scale and EQ-5D-5 L (including the VAS) are administered during the hospital admission as part of baseline assessment. These are not repeated at the first home visit to reduce participant burden during the early transition home. Follow-up assessments are conducted at 4 months post-discharge.

Participants who were unexpectedly discharged to an aged care facility rather than returning home are unable to receive the home-based intervention. However, with their consent and willingness, outcome assessments will be conducted at the aged care facility to the extent possible. This approach ensures ongoing data collection and participant follow-up despite the change in discharge destination.

The Community Intervention

After discharge from the hospital, the study Exercise Physiologist will visit participants in their home to deliver supervised progressive resistance exercise training. This will occur once a week over 7 weeks following discharge home with the visits being between 30 min and 1 hr in duration depending on individual needs. The sessions will be delivered weekly in person at the participants home, commencing within one week post discharge. If participants are to be discharged to a private rehabilitation hospital or respite prior to returning home, the intervention will be delayed until they return home. Discharge destination (e.g., rehabilitation, respite care) and time to intervention commencement will be recorded.

The initial visit will consist of a baseline assessment where the short-form Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) test are administered. If low mood is identified by a score of 5 or more on the GDS-15, the Exercise Physiologist will make referral to the Chronic Care Team at Hornsby Hospital for further review. Any recommendations on the frailty management plan by the pharmacist and dietician will be reinforced by the Exercise Physiologist. Motivational interviewing will also occur at the baseline visit to establish whether there is existing social or family support, and whether the participant is engaged in any community activities. Throughout the 7 weeks, the participant will be encouraged to adhere to a tailored exercise program unsupervised (adapted from the OTAGO program), developed by the Exercise Physiologist to assist the participant in achieving self-nominated SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time Bound) goals. An example of a SMART goal created in conjunction with the participants is “By the end of the 7 weeks I would like to strong enough to be able to walk to the letterbox and back on my own without my walking frame”. Unsupervised adherence will be supported through printed instructions of the prescribed exercise and adherence will be tracked through structured exercise logs. Additional measures

Participants will be asked to self-report their current medications at the 12-month outcome assessment. Current medications will be cross-checked through participant’s pharmacists and Home Medicine Review report where possible. Covariables to be considered will include age, sex, degree of frailty at index admission as measured by the FRAIL scale, number of hospitalisations in the preceding 12 months, cognition (MMSE score). A process evaluation will be conducted to describe the adaptation, scale, and spread of FORTRESS Plus multidisciplinary intervention and details will be described in a separate paper. Adherence to the exercise program will be assessed via participant-maintained exercise logs and Exercise Physiologist records of supervised sessions. Nutritional status will be evaluated through dietary recall questionnaires and serial weight measurements at the home visits and outcome assessments at 4 months and at 12 months to assess changes in caloric and protein intake. Medication lists from hospital admission and 12-month follow-up will be compared to evaluate changes in polypharmacy and Drug Burden Index.

Sample size

Sample size calculations indicate that 106 FORTRESS PLUS recruits fulfilling the same eligibility criteria as the FORTRESS study group used for historical controls will be needed to detect a clinically meaningful 0.5 SD difference in the primary outcome with 80% power at a 5% significance level.

Statistical analysis

The FORTRESS study included people aged 75 and older, who had MMSE 24 and over. The sample size for FORTRESS Plus refers to recruitment of people at HKH who have the same inclusion criteria (75 years and older, MMSE 24 and over). Primary analyses will compare these two groups directly using generalised mixed models. Direct comparison of these FORTRESS Plus participants with FORTRESS intervention participants will also be examined using inverse probability weighting that balances key baseline prognostic factors.

Data management

All project data collected from participants and the electronic medical record will be entered in a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) which only the investigators and members of the project team have access to. Data will be checked and cleaned prior to analysis. A data monitoring committee was not established as this trial involves implementing best practice (guideline recommendations). Any serious adverse events related to the intervention will be reported to the ethics committee.

Time frame

Recruitment commenced in October 2023. Follow-up assessment is expected to conclude 12 months after recruitment ends.

Discussion

Frailty has been linked to poorer clinical outcomes for older people and prevalence is likely to increase due to ageing of the population. There is evidence that exercise intervention can effectively reverse frailty and improve mood [9]. Usual care provides low intensity interventions which fail to adequately meet the needs of this population [5]. A strength of the FORTRESS PLUS study is not only the utility of evidence-based guidelines but how they complement a robust and validated screening tool to provide a comprehensive management plan that is readily transferable to clinical practice in both acute and community aged care settings.

Data collected using the EQ-5D-QOL for quality of life and the GDS-15 for mood assessment may provide insight into where intervention is most effective. The supervised exercise sessions delivered by the Exercise Physiologist during weekly home visits are expected to be a key contributor to sustained improvements in physical function and mood. Dietary intervention where there has been weight loss and medication review with the aim of deprescribing where clinically appropriate are also critical frailty interventions. Implementation outcomes from process evaluation will demonstrate the feasibility of integrating an intensive weekly intervention into routine care planning for frail older people. The FORTRESS PLUS study will also report on the involvement of participant’s carers or family member which is hoped to have positive impact on adherence to the intervention.

The FORTRESS PLUS trial has the potential to demonstrate that a more intensive, multidisciplinary approach is optimal for improving the health-related quality of life outcomes of frail older adults. FORTRESS PLUS hopes to demonstrate a benefit in extending rehabilitation services for frail older people beyond hospital by providing a focus on long-term, home-based multidisciplinary interventions that encompass dietary intervention, pharmacological intervention, and exercise. This could contribute to changes in current clinical practice and policy, and more effective delivery of the Asian Pacific Guidelines for the Management of Frailty.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its pragmatic design of a multidisciplinary intervention targeting key drivers of frailty: malnutrition, polypharmacy, and sedentary behaviour. The inclusion of a 12-month follow-up period enables evaluation of both short and longer term outcomes are relevant to health service utilisation and quality of life in older adults.

However, there are also important limitations to consider. First, participants must be able to understand and communicate in English, which may introduce sampling bias and limit the generalisability of findings to culturally and linguistically diverse populations. This criterion was necessary to ensure participants’ ability to engage meaningfully with the intervention components, including the goal setting, education, and communication with clinical staff. Secondly, the MMSE cut-off score of ≥ 22 allows for the inclusion of individuals with mild cognitive impairment, introducing greater variability in cognitive function compared to the original FORTRESS study (which used a cut-off of MMSE ≥ 24/30). This could affect participants’ capacity to engage fully with the intervention and may impact outcome measures. To address this, we will conduct sensitivity analyses using subgroups that meet the original eligibility criteria (age ≥ 75 years and MMSE ≥ 24) to ensure comparability.

Additional potential sources of bias include selection bias due to hospital-based recruitment, performance bias as participants and clinical staff cannot be blinded to group allocation, and attrition bias due to the vulnerability of the frail older adult population over a 12-month period. These limitations will be considered during data analysis and interpretation, with appropriate statistical techniques used to reduce their impact.

Although a formal cost-effectiveness analysis is not included in the current protocol, resource utilisation data (e.g., length of stay, readmission, and service delivery) will be collected where feasible to inform future economic evaluations.
