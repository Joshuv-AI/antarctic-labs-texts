Factors influencing referral among women attending post abortion care at tertiary hospitals in Northern Uganda
Authors: Jimmyy Opee, Gerald Obai, Maria K Wolters, Samuel Obonyo Okot, Simple Ouma, Emmanuel Ochola, Felix Bongomin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReferral is critical in the chain of managing women receiving post-abortion care (PAC). We assessed factors influencing referral among women receiving PAC at two tertiary hospitals in Northern Uganda. An embedded, mixed-methods study design was conducted from July to December 2023. Quantitative data were collected from 152 women of reproductive age (15–49 years) using structured questionnaires, while qualitative data were gathered through 20 in-depth interviews with women and healthcare providers. Logistic regression was applied for quantitative analysis, and thematic content analysis was used for qualitative data. Median age of all participants was 26 years (IQR: 21–32). Referrals accounted for 46 (30.3%). The odds of being referred was significantly higher among rural residence (adjusted Odds Ratio (aOR): 15.5; 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 4.44–53.83; p < 0.001) and those under 20 years of age (aOR: 2.8; 95% CI 1.02–7.56; p = 0.046). Qualitatively, limited PAC services at lower-level facilities, financial barriers to private care, and transport challenges were key drivers for referral. Therefore, strengthening PAC services at lower-level facilities could reduce referrals and improve care access. Major economic reforms are essential to achieving universal health coverage, including PAC services in northern Uganda.Keywords: Referral, Post abortion care, Tertiary hospitals, Northern UgandaSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Globally, about one in every 3 of the 227 million pregnancies each year, ends up in spontaneous abortions, induced abortions or stillbirth 1,2. The incidence of abortions remains high globally with over 48.3 million induced abortions especially in developing countries 3, contributing to global and regional maternal mortality of 7.9% and 9.6% respectively 4,5. Over 75% of the maternal deaths due to abortion and its complications occur in sub-Saharan Africa, where there are laws that may restrict women from receiving comprehensive abortion care 6,7.

Referral is a vital strategy in the chain of managing patients with abortion related complications 8 which supports patients to receive early and appropriate healthcare, reducing morbidity and mortality 9. Patients are either self-referred or referred by qualified healthcare workers from lower to higher level capable health facilities based on clinical judgement 10,11. Incomplete abortion is the commonest complications of first trimester abortions, and despite antibiotics therapy, 1.4% of the affected women develop post-abortion sepsis, and some experience hemorrhage with significant hemodynamic instability requiring blood transfusion 12. Uterine perforation can occur during uterine evacuation or induced abortion, often necessitating hospital interventions. Some women also develop anesthesia-related complications 13. Mitigating these impacts requires appropriate intervention including referral. Referral is one of the modalities in promoting definitive management of patients in health facilities and are necessary because healthcare facilities operate at different levels of care offered 10,11. In Uganda, these levels include health center I, II, III, IV, General hospitals, Regional Referral Hospital and National Referral Hospital each providing healthcare services at certain level as per set standard, hence promoting inter-facility referral systems 14. Level I facilities consist of community-based services mainly delivered by the village health teams (VHTs) serving population of about 1,000 people. Level II facilities provide primary health care limited to outpatient care by nurses servicing approximately 5000 people. Level III facilities provide outpatient services with some inpatient services and is headed by a clinical officer serving about 20,000 people. Level IV facilities provide comprehensive healthcare including outpatient care, inpatient care, surgical intervention including C/section and is headed by a medical officer within a catchment population of 100,000 people. Hospitals (General Hospitals, Regional Referral Hospitals and National Referral Hospitals) are referral centers for lower level facilities providing increasingly complex specialist services at district, regional and national levels 15.

Despite the availability of facilities, many women still die or suffer complications due to delays or failure of referral to healthcare facilities capable of offering post-abortion care (PAC). In Northern Uganda, previous study has shown that about 21.1% of women utilized one or more PAC services 16. The factors associated with referrals included lack of transport means, failure to make decision and lack of money 17–22. Despite the crucial need for a comprehensive PAC, data on factors influencing referral among women attending PAC services are scarce in our low-resource settings. Therefore, we assessed factors influencing referral among women attending post abortion care at two tertiary hospitals in Northern Uganda.

Methods

Study design

This was an embedded mixed-methods study conducted from July to December 2023. This design involved collecting and analysing both quantitative and qualitative data concurrently and comparing or integrating the findings to gain more comprehensive understanding of the research question or phenomenon. The qualitative study used the Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation which are believed to be critical in shaping Behaviour (COM-B) framework of implementation science model 23. COM-B framework was chosen since the drivers for referral are multifactorial and comprehensive approach through a well-grounded study could assess this health-related behaviour 23. This study assessed referral for post-abortion care which can be influenced by many factors including individual, systems and norms that maybe linked to bevor that affect referral.

Study settings

This study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals in Northern Uganda, which are the main referral centers within Gulu and neighboring districts. Gulu Regional Referral Hospital (GRRH) is a public funded hospital while St. Mary’s Hospital-Lacor (SMHL) is a private not for profit faith-based hospital. These are high volume facilities where diverse referral cases from the entire Acholi Sub-region and beyond are sent making them well suited to provide better insight on referral system in northern Uganda. Both hospitals have obstetrics and gynecology departments skilled staff who offer PAC services. In each hospital, more than 10 cases of abortion related conditions are interfaced daily. The data collection period was four months.

Study population

Quantitative study included all women attending PAC at SMHL and GRRH. Participants who received any one or more of the following PAC services were considered, uterine evacuation for incomplete abortion, modern method of contraception, provision of antibiotic therapy, linkage to other sexual reproductive health services, community rehabilitation. Besides, qualitative study included participants who sought PAC and frontline healthcare workers that were purposively selected.

Selection criteria

Quantitative study included all women of reproductive age 15–49 years who sought PAC at SMHL and GRRH and provided written informed consent during the study period and excluded respondents who were too sick to respond to the interview. Qualitative study included all women of reproductive age 15–49 years who sought PAC and the professional healthcare workers of SMHL and GRRH who provided written informed consent during the study period and excluded healthcare professionals not specifically working in PAC.

Sample size estimation

For quantitative study, the sample size was estimated using the Yamane’s formula for finite population. ; Where, = the sample size (the number of participants to be involved in the study), N = the estimated population basing on records of women seeking PAC was about 208 (52 women per month) in both hospitals for a period of four months estimated study period, e = the margin of error (MoE), e = 0.05 based on research conditions. The minimal sample size was 152 including 10% for non-response.

We included 20 respondents for qualitative study; the principle of saturation was used.

Sampling method

The participants were consecutively sampled until the required sample size was reached for quantitative study. Meanwhile for qualitative study, the respondents were purposively selected to ensure variation in term of age and duration of experience.

Quantitative data collection

During the study period, newly admitted patients who attended post abortion care were identified in the morning using registers, screened for eligibility criteria, and then taken to a private room, where consent and pretested structured questionnaire were administered by research assistant to collect dependent and independent variables. Participants were given ample time to reconsider their responses when they require to do so. In addition, those with religious affiliation are considered any one of these strengths; weak, strong, neither strong nor weak adapted from simple grading system based on the UK Office of National Statistics (ONS).

Qualitative data collection

An interview guide based on the COM-B model was used to explore factors influencing their referral, including the reasons for their referral, clinical types of abortion and preferred modern method of contraception (MCM) provided. Interviews were audio recorded and the verbatim was transcribed. Additionally, the interviewer took field notes during the interview. The duration of individual interview was between 15 and 20 min.

Data management and analysis

Data was entered into Microsoft Excel, coded, and exported to STATA Version 17 for cleaning and analysis. The proportion of referral was calculated as the number of women who were referred for PAC divided by total number of participants enrolled and expressed in percentage.

In addition, the factors influencing referral among women attending PAC was analyzed quantitatively at bivariable analysis level, Chi-square and Fischer’s exact tests were used to compare categorical and numerical variables influencing referral for PAC, respectively. Variables with p < 0.2 and those biologically plausible were taken into multivariable logistic regression model. At multivariable analysis level, stepwise, backward, multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to determine factors independently influencing referral for PAC at SMHL and GRRH. Variables with p < 0.05 were reported as factors independently influenced referral for PAC. Qualitative analysis using thematic approach involved coding data using the COM-B model as a framework. The initial codes were reviewed and combined to develop themes, which were then further reviewed and refined. Themes were used to identify commonalities and differences between participants. The findings were reported using “Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research” (COREQ) guidelines 24.

The median age of participants was 26 years (IQR: 21–32), 93 (61.2%) were Protestant, approximately three-quarters of the participants were cohabiting and unemployed. Proportion, reasons for referral, clinical types of abortion and preferred method of contraception

The proportion of referral among women attending PAC at SMHL and GRRH was 30.3% (n = 46) (Fig. 1). Nearly fifty percent of the participants reported that they were referred because their facility lack capacity at low-level to offer PAC, twenty four percent reported lack of equipment at the time they were attending care, nearly twenty percent reported lack of required medical supplies (Fig. 2). A total of 142 (93.4%) women had spontaneous onset of abortion (Table 1). Most women were diagnosed with incomplete abortion, comprising of 92 (60.5%). A similar proportion of cases were diagnosed with invitable and missed abortion, accounting for 23 (15.1%) each. Smaller number of women were diagnosed with complete and septic abortion, at 12 (7.9%) and 2 (1.3%) respectively (Fig. 3). Overall, 177 (77%) participants expressed preference for MCM. The most commonly preferred method was injectables 49 (41.9%), followed by subdermal implants 31 (26.5%). Only a few women opted for condoms and intrauterine devices 11 (9.4%), combined oral contraceptives 8 (6.8%) or natural methods 7 (6%) (Fig. 4).

Factors influencing referral among women attending post abortion care

Results of quantitative data: At multivariable analysis level, the significant factors were age less than 20 years and rural place of residence. Women residing outside rural areas were about 15.5 increase odds of being referred for PAC compared to those living within urban areas (adjusted Odd Ratio (aOR): 15.5; 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 4.44–53.83, p < 0.001). In addition, compared to women between 20 and 34 years of age, those younger than 20 years were about 2.8 increased odds of being referred (aOR: 2.8; 95% CI 1.02–7.56, p = 0.046) (Table 2).

Qualitative findings

The age of respondents ranged from 20 to 45 years, parity varied between 0 to 7 and the number of abortions were between 1 and 2. Four themes emerged as the drivers to referrals namely; (1) lack of expertise of health care professionals, (2) lack of capacity at low-level facilities lacking equipment and medical supplies, (3) financial constraints, and (4) challenges with transport.

Theme 1: Lack of expertise of healthcare professionals: These are often healthcare workers who have not been trained to provide the post abortion care to patients, which means that patients requiring post abortion care need to be referred.

“I did not know that those people could not help me, the healthcare worker there whom we know to even conduct delivery told me that my problem require a doctor who is at Gulu Regional Referral hospital. So, I had to come here” (33F, para 5+2, GRRH)

“I was even told by my husband that we should not waste any more time since people at the health center cannot help with my condition and they brought me directly to hospital” (22F, para 0+1, SMHL)

Theme 2: Lack of capacity at low-level facilities: Low-level facilities lack the capacity to perform procedures and treatments such as uterine evacuation for incomplete abortion or address reproductive health needs.

“You see, for me I come from a place far from here, but we have some health center there which are not able to help us. They can treat simple condition but for this case where I was pregnant and started bleeding, they just told us their health center cannot help with that” (35F, para 7+1, GRRH)

Theme 3: Financial constraints: Since patients and caretakers must be able to pay for services, especially in private healthcare facilities, patients that use private facilities often switch to public or private not for profit facilities when they cannot afford to pay for the required care.

“The reason why I came here was because the doctor from the clinic told me that I need to pay 200,000 Uganda Shillings so that they can work on me. But I did not have that money, so I decided to leave that place” (20F, para 0+1, GRRH)

“Those people in the clinic this time demanded a lot of money, can you imagine that I was expected to pay some good money to be treated from there. I just told them let me go somewhere else. I am here in Lacor we are paying little money with good treatment as well” (33F, para 4+2, SMHL)

Theme 4: Transport challenges: Transport availability and functionality is critical in facilitating referral. Many patients reach facilities late or develop complications, especially when they come from hard to access rural areas, because transportation may be late or unavailable.

“For me the doctors here told me that I took long at home and that is why I am still here. But for me I looked for transport from home and it took me three days to get a boda boda because our home is very far in Amuru. These doctors are still treating me and I am getting improvement although I don’t know when I will go home” (31F, para 4+1, SMHL)

“You know these people come from far and sometime we have to treat the complication which might have occurred. The main reason is that they take long home or on the way because of poor road which is even worse during rainy season” (45F, In-charge, GRRH)

Triangulation

The result from qualitative analysis confirmed and enriched the findings of quantitative analysis. Two significant factors identified in the multivariable, younger age (below 20 years) and rural residency, were validated by the qualitative data. Additionally, the qualitative findings revealed three additional drivers of referral, namely limited expertise among healthcare professionals, financial constraints, and transportation challenges.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed at assessing factors influencing referral among women attending PAC at two tertiary hospitals in Northern Uganda. We found that about 1 in 3 of every case was a referral, mainly consisting of teenagers and rural residents. Lack of expertise of health care professionals, lack of capacity at low-level facilities, financial constraints and challenges with transport were the main reasons for referral from the participants’ perspective.

In this study, about thirty percent of women attending PAC were referred similar to studies conducted in the United States of America 25,26, this could be because they had similar study design and comparable sample sizes. In contrast, a study in South Carolina reported a higher referral proportion 27,28. The disparity is due to difference in study design involving fewer participants. Approximately fifty percent of the participants stated that they were referred for PAC because the facility lacked the necessary abortion care services. Additionally, about twenty-four percent cited lack of equipment at the time of attending care, while nearly twenty percent reported shortage of medical supplies. These findings mirror those of a study conducted in Ethiopia 29. Women residing in rural areas face higher PAC referral rates due to limited resources in rural facilities. Most post abortion care facilities are concentrated within the urban areas, while rural facilities lack trained healthcare workers and essential supplies, necessitating referral for adequate care.

In addition, our study demonstrated that, compared to women between 20 and 34 years of age, those younger than 20 years were about three times increased odds of being referred for PAC at SMHL and GRRH. This is most likely because the younger women are at a higher risk of complication, increasing the demand for PAC 30. Most women were diagnosed with incomplete abortion. Therefore, most women were managed with uterine evacuation in the two healthcare facilities which is similar to a study in Kenya 31. This is because most of the cases of abortion occur in first trimester and majority requiring nanual vacuum aspiration 32. The rate of women preferring to utilize modern contraception was seventy seven percent, higher than the utilization rate reported in Ethiopia 33. Most preferred modern contraception were injectables, followed by subdermal implants, condoms and intrauterine devices, oral contraceptives and natural methods. In contrast, a study in Ethiopia found implants were the most commonly preferred method, followed by injectables, intrauterine devices, oral contraceptives, and condoms 33.

The women attending PAC are demanded to buy medicines and pay for the services especially those who go to private facilities and in most cases the cost of offering PAC services are expensive. Those who cannot afford the services and drugs are referred for care to public healthcare facilities where services are free, or private not-for profit hospitals that charge less 34. This was demonstrated in a study in Uganda and Rwanda 35. Financial constraints was also reported as a factor in South Carolina 27. Besides, women living far from healthcare facilities delay to attend to PAC services because of lack of transport. Transport challenges are reportedly common in low and middle income-countries 36.

Implications of study findings

Our findings suggest that improving healthcare infrastructure and providing financial support, are critical to reducing the referral rates among young and rural women. Robust healthcare infrastructure is particularly important given that over ninety percent of participants experienced spontaneous abortions. Therefore, there is a significant need for accessible and timely PAC services. Enhancing the capacity of healthcare providers in rural areas and increasing awareness about healthcare services could mitigate these challenges, leading to better health outcomes and reduced strain on referral systems. When a referral is needed, smoother transport is essential to ensure timely intervention. Addressing these issues requires a holistic approach, including policy changes, resource allocation, and community-based interventions.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing referral for PAC and offers a nuanced understanding that quantitative data alone might miss. However, while the study was adequately powered, it was conducted at only two hospitals in Northern Uganda over a period of 4 months for data collection. The findings may not be representative of other regions or healthcare settings owing to its short duration of data collection from only two hospitals, limiting the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Reliance on self-reported data introduces potential bias. Participants may not accurately recall details about their healthcare experiences or may provide answers they perceive as socially acceptable.

Conclusion

In this study, approximately one in three cases involved a referral, primarily of teenagers and rural residents, with participants citing limited healthcare expertise, inadequate facility capacity, financial barriers, and transport difficulties as key reasons. There should be improvement in the healthcare facility to provide PAC to reduce the cases of referrals to higher level facilities. The district should increase supplies of medicines and equipment necessary for providing PAC to women in the low-level facilities especially those located in rural areas and conducting routine training of healthcare providers to improve their knowledge and skills in providing services related to PAC. This study further recommends that there should be a multi-center study to assess the referral patterns and modifiable factors related to PAC services (Supplementary Information).
