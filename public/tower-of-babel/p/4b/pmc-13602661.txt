Barriers to the behavioral conversion of dementia screening intentions among high-risk individuals: a qualitative study in an urban Chinese setting
Authors: Yating Wu, Nanxi Fu, Qian Wu, Li Tang, Qiyuan Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs dementia prevention increasingly shifts toward community settings, early screening can help identify individuals who may benefit from timely assessment, referral, and support. Although many individuals at high risk of dementia express willingness to undergo screening, actual participation remains low, indicating an intention–behavior gap. This study explored barriers that prevented individuals with documented screening willingness from completing dementia screening.MethodsThis descriptive phenomenological qualitative study drew on a preliminary survey conducted in two urban communities in Guangzhou, China, from November 16, 2021, to April 2, 2022. Among 439 individuals at high risk of dementia, 272 expressed willingness to undergo dementia screening. At the six-month follow-up, 156 individuals were successfully contacted; 19 had completed screening. Fifteen individuals with documented screening willingness but no subsequent screening behavior were purposively selected for semi-structured interviews between June and December 2022. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method.ResultsThree themes and nine sub-themes were identified. Theme 1, post-intentional hesitation in initiating screening, included low perceived urgency after initial willingness, fear- and stigma-driven postponement, and delayed self-initiation pending trusted confirmation. Theme 2, individual and familial constraints to intention enactment, included limited understanding of dementia screening, economic constraints and competing responsibilities, and insufficient instrumental support for screening attendance. Theme 3, service-level barriers to intention enactment, included fragmented and restricted access to screening services, perceived low credibility of community-based screening, and lack of follow-up or continuity of care. Overall, screening willingness was often postponed or remained unacted upon when screening was perceived as non-urgent, emotionally threatening, difficult to arrange, or insufficiently connected to a trustworthy care pathway.ConclusionAmong individuals at high risk of dementia who were willing to be screened, non-participation was shaped by delayed action, limited screening knowledge, fear and stigma, practical constraints, and service-level barriers. Community-based services should help willing older adults move from intention to action through clear information, emotional reassurance, practical assistance, credible screening procedures, and visible referral and follow-up support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28397-8.

Introduction

Dementia represents a major global public health challenge. Approximately 57 million people were living with dementia worldwide in 2019, and this number is projected to increase to 153 million by 2050 [1]. China has the largest number of people living with dementia, with an estimated 15.07 million cases among adults aged 60 years and older [2], accounting for about one-quarter of global dementia cases [3]. Data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 further indicate that China bears the highest dementia-related disease burden among the Group of Twenty countries, and dementia has become the fifth leading cause of death in the country [4].

In the absence of widely accessible curative or disease-modifying treatments, risk reduction and early identification have become central components of public health strategies for dementia [5]. Recent evidence suggests that up to 45% of dementia cases may be preventable or delayable by addressing modifiable risk factors [1]. Within this prevention-oriented framework, community-based cognitive screening provides an opportunity to identify older adults who may benefit from timely assessment, referral, risk management, and supportive interventions. This is particularly important for individuals at elevated risk of dementia. In the present study, high-risk status refers to older adults without a dementia diagnosis who were identified using the Brief Dementia Screening Indicator developed by Barnes et al. [6]. This indicator uses readily available primary care information, including age, education, stroke, diabetes, low body mass index, difficulty managing money or medications, and depressive symptoms, to identify individuals who may be prioritized for cognitive screening.

In China, dementia screening has increasingly been promoted through primary and community healthcare services. The 2020 national work plan for dementia prevention and treatment recommended that primary healthcare institutions conduct cognitive function assessment as part of older-adult health management services and routine health examinations; it also recommended the AD8 and the brief community dementia screening scale for community-level assessment, with suspected cases referred to higher-level medical institutions [7]. Therefore, community-level dementia screening in this policy context mainly refers to brief cognitive and functional assessments rather than diagnostic imaging or biomarker-based confirmatory testing. More recently, the Dementia Prevention and Treatment Promotion Action for Older Adults (2023–2025) has further highlighted the policy importance of annual cognitive function screening for permanent residents aged 65 years and older in eligible regions, as well as classified interventions for those identified as being at risk [8]. However, the implementation, accessibility, and payment arrangements of screening and subsequent referral services may vary across local settings. Moreover, older adults and their families may perceive screening not merely as a brief assessment, but as the beginning of a longer pathway involving referral, confirmatory diagnosis, specialist consultation, possible imaging examinations, treatment, and follow-up. Concerns about these downstream procedures and potential costs may therefore affect whether individuals act on their screening willingness.

Despite policy promotion and generally positive attitudes toward dementia screening, actual participation remains low. A recent survey of 1,332 urban community-dwelling adults aged 65 years and older in Wuhan, China, found that only 17.4% had undergone dementia screening or cognitive testing in the previous 12 months [9], substantially lower than the national target of 80% cognitive screening coverage among community-dwelling older adults in pilot areas [7]. Our previous study among individuals at high risk of dementia also found that 62% expressed willingness to undergo dementia screening, yet only 12.2% completed screening within six months [10]. This discrepancy between expressed willingness and subsequent screening behavior suggests that improving willingness alone may be insufficient; additional barriers may arise after intention formation and prevent screening intention from becoming actual screening behavior.

Previous studies have mainly examined attitudes toward and acceptance of dementia screening among healthcare professionals [11, 12], caregivers [13], and general older populations [14, 15]. A recent systematic review further summarized factors associated with attitudes toward dementia screening, including dementia knowledge, perceived benefits, perceived susceptibility, fear, stigma, and service accessibility [16]. However, most existing evidence focuses on whether people are willing to be screened. Less is known about why individuals who have been identified as high risk and have documented screening willingness still fail to complete screening. This gap is important because barriers after intention formation may differ from those influencing initial willingness.

Grounded in the intention–behavior gap perspective, this study focused on individuals at high risk of dementia who had documented screening willingness but did not subsequently undergo screening. Using in-depth semi-structured interviews, we aimed to explore barriers that impede the conversion of dementia screening intention into actual behavior. The findings are expected to inform targeted interventions to increase screening participation among high-risk older adults and support the optimization of community-based dementia prevention and screening services.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a descriptive phenomenological qualitative design to explore the barriers that prevented individuals at high risk of dementia from converting documented screening willingness into actual screening behavior. A descriptive phenomenological approach was considered appropriate because the study aimed to understand participants’ subjective perceptions, lived experiences, and contextual interpretations of non-participation in dementia screening.

Data were collected through face-to-face semi-structured interviews and analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method [17]. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [18].

Research team and reflexivity

Each interview was conducted by one of three female master’s students in nursing. The interviewers had received systematic training in qualitative research methods, including semi-structured interviewing, qualitative coding, and the use of NVivo software. The interview process and data analysis were supervised by two researchers experienced in qualitative research.

None of the interviewers had prior personal or professional relationships with the participants before recruitment. Before each interview, participants were informed that the interviewer was a member of the research team and that the study aimed to understand why some individuals who had previously expressed willingness to undergo dementia screening did not subsequently complete screening. Because the interviewers were aware of the preliminary survey findings, reflexive discussions were held during data collection and analysis to reduce the influence of prior assumptions on data interpretation. Differences in coding or interpretation were resolved through group discussion until consensus was reached.

Preliminary survey, screening intention, and screening behavior

The preliminary cross-sectional survey was conducted in two large communities in Guangzhou, China, from November 16, 2021, to April 2, 2022, using convenience sampling. A total of 473 individuals were invited, and 439 individuals at high risk of dementia were ultimately included in the survey [10]. Dementia risk status was assessed using the Brief Dementia Screening Indicator developed by Barnes et al. [6]. This indicator includes seven readily available items: age, less than 12 years of education, history of stroke, diabetes mellitus, body mass index < 18.5 kg/m², need for assistance with managing money or medications, and depressive symptoms. According to the original scoring algorithm, individuals aged 65–79 years with a total score of ≥ 22 points are classified as being at high risk and may be considered for cognitive screening; individuals aged ≥ 80 years are considered appropriate for cognitive screening on the basis of age alone.

Screening intention was assessed using the three-item screening intention section of the Dementia Screening Willingness and Behavior Questionnaire used in the preliminary survey [10]. The items assessed whether participants intended to undergo dementia screening, intended to make an appointment for dementia screening, or would definitely complete dementia screening within the following six months. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Consistent with the operational definition used in the preliminary survey, a response score of ≥ 4 on any of the three items was interpreted as screening willingness.

Six months after questionnaire completion, participants who had expressed screening willingness were contacted by telephone and asked whether they had undergone dementia screening in a professional medical institution or community setting. Screening behavior was determined based on participants’ self-report. Those who answered “yes” were classified as having completed screening behavior, whereas those who answered “no” were considered to have documented screening willingness but no subsequent screening behavior and were eligible for the qualitative interviews.

Study participants

Participants were permanent residents of two large urban communities in Guangzhou, China. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) classified as being at high risk of dementia using the Brief Dementia Screening Indicator [6]; (2) classified as having dementia screening willingness based on the screening intention section of the Dementia Screening Willingness and Behavior Questionnaire [10]; (3) reported no dementia screening behavior during the six-month follow-up; (4) understood the purpose and procedures of the study and voluntarily agreed to participate.

Among candidates meeting the inclusion criteria, individuals were excluded if they were unable to complete the interview because of severe psychiatric symptoms, severe hearing impairment, major communication difficulties, marked physical frailty, or other health conditions.

Eligibility for interview participation was assessed by the researchers during telephone contact and confirmed again before the face-to-face interview. Participants’ ability to communicate effectively and tolerate the interview was evaluated based on their interactions with the researchers. Information on severe psychiatric disorders, hearing impairment, physical frailty, and other relevant health conditions was obtained through participant self-report and, where appropriate, confirmation from family members.

Purposive sampling was used to maximize variation in age, sex, education level, living arrangement, and self-rated health. Self-rated health was assessed by asking participants to rate their overall health as good, fair, or poor. After completing interviews and preliminary analysis for 13 participants, no new codes or themes emerged, suggesting data saturation. To further confirm saturation, two additional eligible participants were interviewed, and no new themes were identified. A total of 15 participants were ultimately included.

Data collection

Interview guide

The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on relevant literature, findings from the preliminary survey, and the domains covered in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)-based Dementia Screening Willingness and Behavior Questionnaire used in that survey [10]. The guide was designed to explore participants’ perceptions of dementia screening, reasons for not undergoing screening despite prior willingness, perceived barriers and facilitators, and support needs. The TPB-informed domains guided the development of broad interview topics, while coding and theme development were conducted inductively. Open-ended questions were used, and neutral probes were applied when clarification or elaboration was needed. The guide was pre-tested with two eligible individuals and refined according to their feedback (Table 1).

Interview guide

Interview procedure

Interviews were conducted between June and December 2022 in either the health education room of the community health service center or the participant’s home, according to participant preference and convenience. A quiet and private environment was ensured during each interview. No non-participants were present during the interviews. No repeat interviews were conducted. No participant withdrew after providing written informed consent.

Each interview was conducted face-to-face and one-on-one by a trained researcher and lasted approximately 45 to 90 min. Before the interview, the researcher explained the study purpose, procedures, data use, voluntary participation, and confidentiality measures. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Non-leading questions and neutral probes were used to avoid suggestion, judgment, or subjective guidance. During the interviews, the researcher took field notes and recorded non-verbal information, such as facial expressions, tone, gestures, and pauses.

Interviews were conducted in Chinese, primarily Mandarin or Cantonese according to participant preference. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Chinese within 24 h after each interview. Initial coding and thematic analysis were conducted using the Chinese transcripts. Representative quotations selected for the manuscript were translated into English using a forward-translation and verification process. Two bilingual researchers independently checked the translated quotations against the original Chinese transcripts to ensure semantic accuracy and cultural appropriateness. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus.

Audio recordings, transcripts, and field notes were stored securely and anonymized using participant codes. Data were used only for the purposes of this study.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Colaizzi’s seven-step method [17]. First, the researchers repeatedly read the verbatim transcripts and field notes to become familiar with the data. Second, significant statements related to dementia screening willingness, non-completion of screening, and perceived barriers were extracted. Third, meanings were formulated from these significant statements while remaining close to participants’ original expressions. Fourth, formulated meanings were organized into theme clusters. Fifth, the research team integrated the theme clusters into an exhaustive description of the phenomenon. Sixth, the fundamental structure of the experience was refined through repeated comparison with the original transcripts. Finally, a summary of the preliminary findings was returned to selected participants for validation.

Initial coding was conducted independently by three researchers. Themes were derived inductively from the interview data rather than predetermined before analysis. The analysis incorporated verbatim transcripts, field notes, and original audio recordings to ensure contextual accuracy. The research team cross-checked the initial coding results and discussed differences until consensus was reached. Final themes and sub-themes were determined through group discussion. NVivo 12.0 software was used to assist data management and thematic organization. The entire analysis process was supervised and methodologically guided by two advisors experienced in qualitative research.

Full transcripts were not returned to participants for correction. However, member checking was performed by a researcher who had not been involved in interviewing or initial coding. Five participants were randomly selected for follow-up contact. Feedback indicated that participants broadly recognized the identified themes, and no substantive objections were raised.

Trustworthiness

Several strategies were used to enhance the trustworthiness of the study. Credibility was supported through the use of non-leading questions, integration of field notes with verbatim transcripts, investigator triangulation during coding, and member checking with five participants. Transferability was enhanced by purposive sampling to include participants with diverse demographic characteristics and by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participants, and data collection procedures. Dependability was strengthened by maintaining an audit trail, including interview records, field notes, coding documents, team discussion records, and theme development processes. Confirmability was supported through independent coding by three researchers, reflexive team discussions, and supervision by two advisors experienced in qualitative research.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University (Approval No.: KY-2022-083). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent after being informed of the study objectives, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures, and data usage. Participant information was anonymized using codes and stored securely.

Fifteen participants were purposively selected from the latter group for qualitative interviews, including 9 males and 6 females aged 65 to 86 years. Themes, sub-themes, and barrier mechanisms

Theme 1: Post-intentional hesitation in initiating screening

Although all participants had documented screening willingness in the preliminary survey, the interviews showed that this willingness did not necessarily lead to self-initiated screening. Some participants postponed action because they did not perceive an immediate need for screening, feared stigma or diagnosis, or waited for confirmation from trusted others or healthcare professionals. This theme captures hesitation and decision-related delay before screening was initiated.

Sub-theme 1: Low perceived urgency after initial willingness

Participants who accepted dementia screening in principle often delayed action because they felt generally healthy, had no obvious symptoms, and could still manage daily activities; therefore, screening was viewed as something that could be delayed rather than acted upon immediately.

“I feel perfectly healthy right now—my memory is sharp, I sometimes help take care of my grandson, play chess, do the grocery shopping. Of course screening has its benefits, but doing it now feels premature. Better to wait until real symptoms show up.” (P1)

“I feel perfectly healthy right now—my memory is sharp, I sometimes help take care of my grandson, play chess, do the grocery shopping. Of course screening has its benefits, but doing it now feels premature. Better to wait until real symptoms show up.” (P1)

Sub-theme 2: Fear- and stigma-driven postponement

Some participants expressed willingness to understand or monitor their cognitive health, but this willingness was accompanied by fear of receiving a dementia-related result. Dementia was associated with incurability, loss of independence, and becoming a burden to family members. Postponing screening helped them manage anticipated psychological distress while keeping the possibility of screening open.

“Oh dear, if I really got dementia, I might not even want to live anymore. I’d become a huge burden on my children! And there’s no cure for this illness. It’s not that I don’t want to know—I actually do. But just thinking about getting tested terrifies me. I may wait a little longer and take things day by day.” (P7)

“Oh dear, if I really got dementia, I might not even want to live anymore. I’d become a huge burden on my children! And there’s no cure for this illness. It’s not that I don’t want to know—I actually do. But just thinking about getting tested terrifies me. I may wait a little longer and take things day by day.” (P7)

Others associated dementia screening with stigma and social labeling.

“Some people say dementia is like punishment for bad deeds. If others knew I got screened, they might think I was mentally unstable. I’ve seen patients who do not recognize anyone and need help with eating or toileting, and people tend to avoid them. I know screening may be useful, but I am afraid of being labeled once I take that step.” (P5)

“Some people say dementia is like punishment for bad deeds. If others knew I got screened, they might think I was mentally unstable. I’ve seen patients who do not recognize anyone and need help with eating or toileting, and people tend to avoid them. I know screening may be useful, but I am afraid of being labeled once I take that step.” (P5)

Sub-theme 3: Delayed self-initiation pending trusted confirmation

Some participants acknowledged the value of screening but did not independently initiate it. They felt uncertain about whether screening was necessary or timely and waited for confirmation or guidance from trusted others or healthcare professionals before taking action. “The idea of screening doesn’t sound bad. I get the point. It’s better to detect it early. But I really don’t know whether I need to do it right away. These things are too specialized. I’d rather let my son decide, or listen to the doctor. If they say I should do it, I’ll go.” (P8)

“The idea of screening doesn’t sound bad. I get the point. It’s better to detect it early. But I really don’t know whether I need to do it right away. These things are too specialized. I’d rather let my son decide, or listen to the doctor. If they say I should do it, I’ll go.” (P8)

Theme 2: Individual and familial constraints to intention enactment

Participants also described individual and family-level constraints that limited the enactment of screening willingness. These barriers included limited understanding of dementia screening, economic constraints and competing responsibilities, and insufficient instrumental support for screening attendance. This theme reflects how screening-related understanding, competing responsibilities, personal resources, and practical support shaped participants’ ability to act on screening intentions.

Sub-theme 1: Limited understanding of dementia screening

Many participants had limited understanding of dementia screening. They were uncertain about the purpose of screening, what it involved, and how it could benefit them. This uncertainty made it difficult for them to take the first step despite previously expressing willingness.

“I don’t really know what this dementia screening involves. Won’t it involve brain scans or something? It sounds like a complicated examination.” (P13)

“I don’t really know what this dementia screening involves. Won’t it involve brain scans or something? It sounds like a complicated examination.” (P13)

Some participants recognized the potential value of screening but remained unclear about its specific purpose and practical meaning.

“I heard about this screening from you. I think it could be helpful, and I want to do it, but I still don’t really understand what it is for or what I can learn from it.” (P12)

“I heard about this screening from you. I think it could be helpful, and I want to do it, but I still don’t really understand what it is for or what I can learn from it.” (P12)

Sub-theme 2: Economic constraints and competing responsibilities

Economic pressure, chronic disease expenses, and caregiving responsibilities made dementia screening difficult to prioritize despite participants’ prior willingness to be screened. Some participants were concerned about the potential costs of diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up after screening.

“It’s not that I don’t want to be screened. My husband already spends a lot on medication. I have diabetes and high blood pressure too, and those drugs aren’t cheap. What if something is found after screening? Treatment would cost even more—that’s too much to think about.” (P6)

“It’s not that I don’t want to be screened. My husband already spends a lot on medication. I have diabetes and high blood pressure too, and those drugs aren’t cheap. What if something is found after screening? Treatment would cost even more—that’s too much to think about.” (P6)

Family caregiving duties also limited participants’ ability to attend screening, even when they were willing to consider it.

“I take my grandson to and from school every day, and on weekends I take him to classes. I barely have any time for myself. Even if I wanted to do the screening, when would I go?” (P4)

“I take my grandson to and from school every day, and on weekends I take him to classes. I barely have any time for myself. Even if I wanted to do the screening, when would I go?” (P4)

Sub-theme 3: Insufficient instrumental support for screening attendance

Some participants needed practical assistance to attend dementia screening. Mobility limitations, difficulty using smartphones, and limited practical support from family members made it difficult for them to act on their screening willingness.

“I really want to do the screening, but my legs aren’t good and I can’t use smartphones. They said I need to scan QR codes or make online appointments—I don’t know how. My husband lives with me, but he doesn’t know how to use smartphones either. My children are all busy with work, and I don’t want to trouble them just for this.” (P14)

“I really want to do the screening, but my legs aren’t good and I can’t use smartphones. They said I need to scan QR codes or make online appointments—I don’t know how. My husband lives with me, but he doesn’t know how to use smartphones either. My children are all busy with work, and I don’t want to trouble them just for this.” (P14)

In addition to mobility and digital barriers, some participants felt anxious about attending screening alone because they lacked accompaniment and communication support during healthcare encounters.

“If someone could go with me and help explain things to the doctor, I might feel more comfortable. But going alone makes me nervous—I’m afraid I won’t understand, or won’t ask the right questions.” (P11)

“If someone could go with me and help explain things to the doctor, I might feel more comfortable. But going alone makes me nervous—I’m afraid I won’t understand, or won’t ask the right questions.” (P11)

Theme 3: Service-level barriers to intention enactment

Participants also identified service-level barriers that limited the enactment of screening willingness. These barriers arose in both community-based screening and referral to higher-level hospitals, reflecting problems in service accessibility, perceived reliability, and continuity of care.

Sub-theme 1: Fragmented and restricted access to screening services

Some participants encountered difficulty accessing dementia screening because of limited local availability, geographic inconvenience, eligibility uncertainty, weak community–hospital linkage, and appointment difficulties.

“Our community does not seem to offer this screening, but I heard from a former colleague that another neighborhood does. It is far away, and I am not sure whether I would be eligible to receive the service there.” (P10)

“Our community does not seem to offer this screening, but I heard from a former colleague that another neighborhood does. It is far away, and I am not sure whether I would be eligible to receive the service there.” (P10)

Another participant described being redirected from the community setting to a higher-level hospital, where appointment difficulties eventually led him to give up.

“I wanted to get checked, but the community doctor said it could not be done there and advised me to go to a large hospital. I tried for several days but could not get an appointment, so I eventually stopped trying.” (P9)

“I wanted to get checked, but the community doctor said it could not be done there and advised me to go to a large hospital. I tried for several days but could not get an appointment, so I eventually stopped trying.” (P9)

Sub-theme 2: Perceived low credibility of community-based screening

Many participants questioned whether community clinics could provide reliable dementia screening. Their concerns focused on the professionalism, standardization, and perceived accuracy of community-based services.

“The community-based elderly health checks sometimes feel rather perfunctory to me. I wonder whether they are just done to meet targets. Is this dementia screening the same?” (P2)

“The community-based elderly health checks sometimes feel rather perfunctory to me. I wonder whether they are just done to meet targets. Is this dementia screening the same?” (P2)

Some participants explicitly contrasted community clinics with higher-level hospitals, questioning whether community-based providers had sufficient expertise to conduct dementia screening properly.

“Those small clinics are okay for a cold or headache, but dementia screening? I’m not sure they can do it properly. I would feel more reassured if it was done by specialists in a large hospital.” (P3)

Discussion

This qualitative study examined why individuals at high risk of dementia who had documented willingness to undergo screening did not complete dementia screening during follow-up. Three interrelated themes were identified: post-intentional hesitation in initiating screening, individual and familial constraints to intention enactment, and service-level barriers to intention enactment. Together, these findings suggest that, even after screening willingness had been expressed, multiple barriers could delay or prevent its translation into completed screening. Screening willingness was often not acted upon, and screening action was postponed when older adults perceived screening as non-urgent, emotionally threatening, practically inconvenient, or insufficiently connected to a trustworthy care pathway. These findings are consistent with prior evidence that knowledge, perceived risks and benefits, stigma, and service accessibility influence dementia screening hesitancy [16]. By focusing on individuals who had already expressed willingness, the present study extends this evidence by showing how such barriers may continue to operate after intention formation.

Post-intentional hesitation: when willingness does not become immediate action

The first theme showed that expressed willingness did not necessarily mean that participants were ready to initiate screening. Some participants accepted dementia screening in principle but did not regard it as necessary at the present time. They described themselves as still able to manage daily activities, care for grandchildren, communicate with others, or maintain usual routines. In this context, screening was not rejected, but was postponed until more obvious symptoms appeared. This pattern suggests that perceived good health and preserved daily functioning may reduce the perceived urgency of screening, even among individuals who have already expressed willingness. For high-risk individuals, this pattern is particularly relevant because cognitive screening is intended to identify those who may benefit from timely assessment and support before more apparent functional decline occurs. In the present study, screening was often viewed as potentially useful in the future but not necessary at present; as a result, some participants’ screening willingness did not translate into action during the follow-up period.

Fear and stigma further contributed to postponement. For some participants, dementia screening was not perceived as a neutral health assessment, but as a possible entry point into a feared diagnosis associated with incurability, dependency, family burden, and social labeling. Previous studies have shown that dementia-related stigma can discourage timely help-seeking and diagnosis [19, 20]. Recent evidence also suggests that although many older adults view cognitive screening positively, they may still worry about distress or stigma if results indicate dementia risk [21]. Similarly, in the present study, participants could recognize the value of early detection yet postpone screening when the anticipated emotional consequences of knowing the result felt overwhelming. Thus, screening willingness in this group should not be interpreted as immediate readiness to act; rather, it may coexist with hesitation, fear, and delayed decision-making.

Some participants also waited for confirmation from trusted others or healthcare professionals before deciding whether to initiate screening. This pattern indicates that intention was sometimes dependent on external confirmation rather than fully self-initiated action. Participants were not opposed to screening, but they lacked sufficient confidence to decide independently when, where, and whether to act. This helps explain why stated willingness may overestimate actual uptake: an older adult may agree that screening is beneficial while still requiring trusted confirmation before taking practical steps.

Individual and familial constraints: when willingness is present but not executable

The second theme showed that willingness was often constrained by limited understanding of dementia screening, economic concerns, competing family responsibilities, and insufficient instrumental support. Many participants were unclear about what dementia screening involved, whether it required brain imaging or specialist examination, and whether costs might arise during screening or after an abnormal result. This uncertainty made screening difficult to plan and prioritize. Economic concerns involved both uncertainty about the possible cost of screening itself and worries about additional expenses if abnormalities were identified, such as further examinations, treatment, or long-term care. Prior evidence on dementia screening hesitancy has similarly identified financial costs as a perceived barrier to screening participation [16]. In the present study, these concerns did not necessarily indicate rejection of screening; rather, they made participants more likely to postpone action despite having expressed willingness.

Family roles also shaped the conversion of intention into behavior. Some participants prioritized caregiving responsibilities over preventive health services. Others were willing to be screened but needed accompaniment or help with transportation, online appointment systems, communication with healthcare providers, or interpretation of medical information. These findings suggest that the intention–behavior gap among older adults may partly reflect a “last-mile” problem: the person may be willing, but the practical conditions required to complete screening are not in place. In this sense, support from family or trusted others is not only emotional encouragement. It also includes concrete assistance with appointment-making, accompaniment, digital access, and communication during healthcare encounters.

Importantly, these individual and familial constraints did not necessarily negate participants’ screening willingness. Instead, they made screening harder to arrange and easier to postpone in everyday life. This distinction is important because strategies that focus only on increasing knowledge or willingness may be insufficient if the practical support needed to complete screening is not also addressed.

Service-level barriers: when the screening pathway is unclear or untrusted

The third theme showed that service-level barriers also limited the translation of willingness into screening behavior. Participants described fragmented access to screening services, uncertainty about eligibility, difficulty making hospital appointments, weak community–hospital linkage, and inconsistent information about where screening could be obtained. These barriers made screening appear difficult to navigate, especially for older adults with limited mobility, digital literacy, or family assistance.

Trust was another important barrier. Some participants doubted whether community health service centers had sufficient expertise, equipment, or standardized procedures to conduct dementia screening reliably. Their concerns did not necessarily mean that they rejected community-based screening; rather, they wanted reassurance that screening results would be professional, meaningful, and appropriately followed up. A qualitative study of general practitioners in China similarly reported barriers to dementia screening and management in primary care, including limited training, insufficient resources, and system-level constraints [22]. The present study complements this provider-side evidence by showing that older adults’ perceptions of community service credibility may also influence whether screening willingness is translated into behavior.

Participants also emphasized that screening should not end with a result alone. They wanted explanation, referral guidance, treatment advice, risk management, or continuing support if screening suggested possible cognitive impairment. Without such a pathway, screening could be perceived as anxiety-producing rather than helpful. Evidence from dementia care research suggests that continuity of care is important for care quality and outcomes [23]. Although this evidence does not directly prove that continuity increases screening uptake, it supports participants’ view that screening is more acceptable when it is embedded in a visible pathway of explanation, referral, and follow-up. Therefore, for individuals who already have screening willingness, the key service issue may not be only whether screening is available, but whether the pathway before and after screening is understandable and trustworthy.

Practice implications

For similar urban community settings, these findings suggest that efforts to improve dementia screening uptake should focus not only on generating willingness, but also on helping older adults act on their existing willingness. First, community health education should provide concrete information about what dementia screening involves, how brief cognitive or functional assessment differs from diagnostic imaging, where screening can be accessed, whether costs may arise, and what steps may follow after an abnormal result. Such information may reduce uncertainty and make screening easier to plan.

Second, screening promotion should address emotional barriers without reinforcing fear or stigma. Cognitive assessment can be framed as part of routine older-adult health management rather than as a label of dementia. Communication from community workers and healthcare providers should acknowledge concerns about diagnosis, family burden, and social labeling, while explaining the potential value of early identification and follow-up support.

Third, practical assistance is needed for older adults who are willing but unable to complete screening independently. Community health service centers could consider simplified appointment procedures, offline registration options, reminder calls, family-inclusive counseling, or accompaniment support for those with mobility limitations or digital difficulties. Finally, community-based screening may be more acceptable when procedures are standardized and when referral and follow-up arrangements are clearly explained in advance. These measures may help transform expressed screening willingness into a feasible health action.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is that it focused specifically on high-risk individuals who had documented willingness to undergo dementia screening but did not complete screening during follow-up. This sampling approach allowed the study to move beyond general attitudes toward dementia screening and examine barriers that occur after intention has already been expressed. The study also used purposive sampling, investigator triangulation, field notes, member checking, and COREQ-informed reporting to enhance qualitative trustworthiness.

Several limitations should be noted. First, participants were recruited from two urban communities in Guangzhou, and the findings may not fully represent older adults in rural areas, other regions, or healthcare settings with different screening arrangements. Second, screening behavior was assessed through self-report at one follow-up point, which limited our ability to observe how willingness, hesitation, family negotiation, and service access may change over time. Third, this study included only high-risk individuals themselves. Although participants frequently referred to family members, caregivers, community providers, and hospital services, these stakeholders were not interviewed directly. Future studies could incorporate the perspectives of family caregivers, community health workers, general practitioners, and referral providers to triangulate the barriers identified here and better understand how interpersonal and service-level factors shape the conversion of screening willingness into screening behavior.

Conclusion

This study explored barriers to dementia screening among individuals at high risk of dementia who had expressed willingness to be screened but did not complete screening during follow-up. Among these willing individuals, non-participation was shaped by delayed action, limited screening knowledge, fear and stigma, practical constraints, and service-level barriers. These findings suggest that community-based services should help willing older adults move from intention to action by providing clear information, emotional reassurance, practical assistance, credible screening procedures, and visible referral and follow-up support.
