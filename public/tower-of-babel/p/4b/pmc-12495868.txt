Taurodontism and related dental anomalies: influence of maternal, prenatal, and postnatal factors in pediatric patients
Authors: Ozemre Mehmet Ozgur, Mustuloğlu Seyma, Korkmaz Aynur, Kocak Topbas Nazan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the association between taurodontism and maternal-child characteristics, as well as its relationship with other dental anomalies including hypodontia, hyperdontia, and pyramidal molars.MethodsPanoramic radiographs were obtained from 815 patients aged 9 to 15 years. A total of 62 children with taurodontism and their mothers agreed to participate. Other dental anomalies and taurodontism types were also recorded. Mothers completed a questionnaire that included the mother and child’s demographic characteristics and potential etiological factors. The collected data were subjected to statistical analysis.ResultsThe most common form of taurodontism was hypotaurodontism, most frequently affecting the right maxillary first molar (43.5%). Heart disease (n = 5) was the most frequently observed systemic condition among children with taurodontism. A statistically significant association was observed between the number of taurodontic teeth and the presence of pyramidal molars (p < 0.05). Regression analysis revealed significant associations (p < 0.05) between the presence of more than two taurodontic teeth and several factors, including maternal smoking during pregnancy, a higher number of siblings, female sex, and consanguineous marriage. These findings may assist in identifying at-risk pediatric populations and underscore the importance of considering these variables in the clinical assessment of dental anomalies.ConclusionsEvaluating taurodontism in children with heart disease or pyramidal molars may contribute to early diagnosis and more effective management. The findings of this study highlight the need for increased clinician awareness and further research with larger sample sizes and healthy control groups to better understand the risk factors associated with taurodontism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06530-0.Keywords: Aetiology, Dental anomalies, Panoramic radiograph, Taurodontism

Background

Taurodontism is a morphological anomaly in which a tooth exhibits an apical displacement of the pulpal base and a vertically enlarged pulp chamber. In this anomaly, the apico-occlusal height of the pulp chamber is increased, the roots are shorter than those of normal teeth, and there is no narrowing at the level of the cemento‑enamel junction [1]. It frequently affects the first and second molars and can occur in any quadrant, unilaterally or bilaterally, in deciduous and permanent dentition [2].

The aetiology of taurodontism formation needs to be clarified. However, an unusual developmental pattern, delayed pulp chamber calcification, odontoblastic deficiency, a change in Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath, or impaired developmental homeostasis are suggested theories regarding the pathogenesis of the anomaly [3]. Taurodontism usually manifests as an isolated anomaly but has also been associated with some genetic and developmental disorders, such as amelogenesis imperfecta, hypophosphatasia, Klinefelter syndrome, ectodermal dysplasia, or Down syndrome [4].

It is well established that some effects and changes in the organism before, during, and after birth can lead to dental anomalies. Maternal diseases, stress, the use of drugs, alcohol and smoking use in the prenatal period; low birth weight, birth hypoxia, premature birth and other birth defects can be considered as risk factors [5–8]. There are many studies in the literature evaluating genetic and environmental factors in the origin of dental anomalies [9–11]. It has been reported that mutations in many gene families such as Distal-less homeobox (DLX3), Msh homeobox 1 (MSX1), paired box 9 (PAX9), fibroblasts growth factor (FGF), ectodysplasin A (EDA), bone morphogenetic proteins (BMP), sonic hedgehog signaling molecule (SHH), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) may play a role in the origin of various different dental anomalies.

In literature, most studies have focused on the prevalence of taurodontism among different populations, anomalies, and syndromes [12–14]. These studies have demonstrated a notable association between taurodontism and various developmental dental anomalies, such as hypodontia, hyperdontia, and the presence of pyramidal molars, suggesting a potential common etiopathogenic pathway [15, 16]. However, the influence of perinatal and prenatal environmental factors, such as maternal health, nutrition, and exposure to toxins, on the development of taurodontism has not been adequately addressed in the literature.

This study aims to address a gap in the current literature by investigating the possible associations between taurodontism and various maternal characteristics, prenatal and perinatal conditions, as well as other dental anomalies in a pediatric population. Prior studies have suggested that environmental and maternal factors may influence dental development, but limited research has examined their relationship with taurodontism specifically. Factors such as maternal health status, prenatal exposures, familial background, and reproductive history may be relevant to the etiology of this anomaly. Identifying such associations could provide insights into underlying mechanisms and support preventive strategies in pediatric dentistry.

Materials and methods

This descriptive study was approved by the Mersin University Institutional Review Board (approval date: 23/02/2022; decision number: 2022/147), and it was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry, Mersin University. The study was limited to patients who visited the pediatric dentistry clinic; therefore, the participants were between 9 and 15 years of age. As this descriptive study was planned for a specific time frame, no formal sample size calculation was performed. Data collection was carried out in two stages: first, taurodontism was diagnosed in panoramic radiographs obtained during routine dental examinations; second, an online questionnaire was administered to the mothers of children diagnosed with taurodontism. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Digital panoramic radiography examination

Firstly, an experienced paediatric dentist and a radiologist jointly analysed 815 digital panoramic images taken between January and September 2023. The evaluations were performed simultaneously on the same computer, and a consensus was reached regarding the presence of hypodontia, hyperdontia, pyramidal molars, and taurodontism. Children with poor image quality, incomplete questionnaire data, or a history of permanent tooth extraction in the mandibular or maxillary first molars, deep caries, endodontic treatment or trauma were excluded from the study. A total of 62 children were included in the study. These participants had at least one taurodontic tooth in the mandibular or maxillary first molars, and data were collected via questionnaires completed by their mothers. (Fig. 1). Molars exhibiting fused, single conical roots were recorded as pyramidal teeth [16]. Based on Stifman and Channel’s description of taurodontism, the crown-body length was determined by measuring the height of the pulp chamber linearly from the lowest point of the roof to the highest point of the floor. Root length was defined as the distance between the floor of the pulp chamber and the apex of the longest root. According to the Shifman and Channel classification, the severity of taurodontism was determined by multiplying the ratio of the values by 100. Ratios of 20–30% were classified as hypotaurodontism, 30–40% as mesotaurodontism, and 40–75% as hypertaurodontism [17]. In addition, hypodontia, defined as the congenital absence of one or more teeth, and hyperdontia, defined as the presence of supernumerary teeth were also recorded [15].

Training calibration

Both observers were trained by an experienced maxillofacial radiologist in the assessment of hypodontia, hyperdontia, pyramidal molars, and taurodontism. Training was conducted using data from 20 children who were not part of the main study. Interobserver calibration was then performed. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa statistic, yielding a value of κ = 0.94, which indicates an almost perfect level of consistency between the two observers.

Questionnaire

In the construction of the questionnaire, a comprehensive and systematic review of the relevant literature was undertaken to identify etiological risk factors associated with dental development during the prenatal and postnatal periods. Based on the findings of this literature review, pertinent variables were integrated into the 30-item questionnaire specifically designed for use within the scope of the present study. The questionnaire was electronically sent to mothers of children via Google Forms, and informed consent was obtained from mothers who agreed to participate in the study by clicking the start button of the questionnaire. The questionnaires were resubmitted two weeks apart for the unanswered portions and reminders were administered three times.

The questionnaire included descriptive information such as the child’s gender, age (years), and educational status of the parents were noted. In terms of mother-child history during and after pregnancy, systemic disease, birth weight (kg), week of birth, birth type, breastfeeding for at least six months, number of siblings, maternal smoking, maternal birth age, folic acid use during pregnancy, and consanguineous marriage were recorded. The English version of questionnaire form is presented in Supplentary Material 1. The questionnaire used in this study was developed based on an extensive literature review and refined through expert consultation with three specialists in pediatric dentistry and dentomaxillofacial radiology. The items were selected for clarity, clinical relevance, and alignment with prior studies addressing prenatal and postnatal factors in dental anomalies. While a statistical validation such as Cronbach’s alpha or exploratory factor analysis was not performed, this process ensured content validity for the descriptive aim of the study. The absence of formal validation and reliability testing is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Statistical analysis

SPSS for Windows 20.0 was used for statistical analysis (IBM Corp., 2012, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics included number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, median, and minimum–maximum values. The chi-square test was used to analyse the differences in the distribution of hypodontia, sex, and taurodontism type. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered. Independent variables associated with the likelihood of having more than two taurodontic teeth were analyzed using binary logistic regression.

Although the study was descriptive in design, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted to determine whether the sample size was adequate to detect significant associations foud in the regression model. Using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), the calculated power was approximately 84% for maternal smoking during pregnancy (OR = 0.019, p = 0.048) and greater than 90% for consanguineous marriage (OR = 25.87, p = 0.010). These results indicate that the sample size (n = 62) was sufficient to support the validity of the statistically significant findings in the multivariate analysis.

Results

A total of 62 children with taurodontism (30 males and 32 females) and their mothers agreed to participate in the study. The mean age of children was 12.34 ± 1.99 years (range: 9–15). While 16.1% (n = 10) of the children had systemic diseases, 30.6% (n = 19) of the mothers had systemic diseases. Heart disease was the most common condition among children (8%, n = 5), whereas familial Mediterranean fever and hypertension were the most frequently reported conditions among mothers (9.6%, n = 6).

A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of various risk factors on the likelihood of having more than two taurodontic teeth, compared to having one or two (Table 5). In the univariate model, no statistically significant associations were observed between the independent variables and the presence of more than two taurodontic teeth (p > 0.05). However, in the multivariate model, female patients were found to have a 0.186-fold increased likelihood of presenting with more than two taurodontic teeth compared to male patients (p = 0.039). Furthermore, a maternal history of consanguineous marriage was associated with a 25.875-fold increased likelihood (p = 0.010). This strong association may indicate a genetic predisposition related to inherited traits, emphasizing the importance of familial background in the risk assessment of dental anomalies. Patients with three or more siblings had a 0.095-fold higher likelihood of having more than two taurodontic teeth compared to those with 0–2 siblings (p = 0.028). In addition, maternal smoking during pregnancy was linked to a 0.019-fold increased likelihood of having more than two taurodontic teeth (p = 0.048). This finding supports the notion that prenatal environmental exposures may influence dental development, highlighting the importance of preventive maternal health counseling. No significant associations were identified for the remaining risk factors (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Taurodontic teeth, which appear to be clinically normal teeth and whose diagnosis is mostly made from radiographs taken for other purposes, are seen mainly in patients with genetic disorders but also in systemically healthy patients. Numerous studies have examined the prevalence of taurodontism and its relationship with other dental anomalies in various sample groups [15, 16, 18]. However, no study has analysed the descriptive characteristics of individuals affected by taurodontism in detail. In the present study, the typical morphological variations and descriptive characteristics of children and their mothers were analysed.

Taurodontism is more common in permanent teeth than deciduous teeth, and its prevalence varies considerably according to the population [19]. The prevalence of taurodontism was reported to be 22.9% in an Iranian adult population [20], 1.4% in children from Saudi Arabia [21], 2.49% in an Indian population [22], 0.61% in a German population [16], 27.19% in a Brazilian population [23], and 5.6% in an Israeli adult population [17]. According to various studies, the prevalence in the Turkish population ranges from 0.26 to 11.2% [12, 24]. In the age group determined in this study, only permanent first molars were evaluated because the second molar had not completed its root development, and the prevalence of taurodontism in first molars was 11.54% out of 641 radiographs examined. In 2016, Elbay et al. [13] reported that the prevalence of taurodontism was 2.22% in their study, in which they evaluated only the first molars of 3642 patients aged 10–14 years in terms of the presence of taurodontism. Variations in the reported prevalence of taurodontic teeth are predominantly influenced by differences in the age distribution of the studied populations, alongside disparities in ethnic background and diagnostic criteria. One of the significant limitations of our study pertains to the age range of the patients included. Since the study was conducted in a pediatric dental clinic, only patients aged 15 years and younger were included, as older individuals are not eligible for treatment in this setting. Consequently, our analysis was confined to the evaluation of permanent first molars for taurodontism. This restriction may have influenced the overall assessment of taurodontism prevalence in our study. Future studies incorporating older age groups, which would allow for the evaluation of second and third molars, are necessary to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the prevalence and distribution of taurodontism.

Consistent with previous studies, hypotaurodontism is the most common type of taurodontism encountered in this study [15, 25]. In addition, in this study, there was a significant relationship between the number of taurodont teeth and the presence of pyramidal molars. However, no relationship was found between the number of taurodont teeth and the presence of hypodontia and hyperdontia. Similarly, Klein et al. [26] also found a significant relationship between the number of pyramidal and taurodontic molars. Teeth with pyramidal molars and taurodontism have a relatively poor periodontal prognosis compared with teeth with healthy roots, and the structural features of the root canal can cause difficulties during pulp treatment. Based on the results of this study, pediatric dentists should be aware of the relationship between pyramidal molars and taurodontism and inform patients of the importance of early preventive measures. However, Gökkaya et al. [15] reported that taurodontism is the most common dental anomaly in patients with hyperdontia and hypodontia. This difference may be due to the small number of children with hypodontia and hyperdontia in this study. The relatively small sample size may also limit the generalizability of the findings and might have compromised the statistical power, potentially affecting the robustness of subgroup and regression analyses. Therefore, future studies with larger and more representative samples are needed to validate and expand upon the present findings.

Taurodontism is characterised by large pulp chambers and short roots. Therefore, endodontic treatment of Taurodontic teeth is complex and difficult [1]. Post placement in these teeth should be avoided. Additionally, these teeth are more sensitive to root resorption during orthodontic treatment. Moreover, difficulties may be encountered during tooth extraction [26]. These challenges make recognising and understanding taurodontic molars important. Therefore, this study defined the characteristics of children with taurodontic teeth and their mothers. Although there was no significant relationship between the determined features and the presence of taurodontic teeth, it can be said that the prevalence of taurodontic teeth is higher in children of mothers with Mediterranean fever and hypertension, and in children with heart disease. Our findings are essential for guiding researchers in identifying groups susceptible to taurodontism.

In our study, regression analysis revealed statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) between the presence of more than two taurodontic teeth and several risk factors, including maternal smoking during pregnancy, a higher number of siblings, female sex, and consanguineous marriage. Among these, the effect of consanguinity appeared to be the most pronounced, while the impact of the other factors was relatively lower. Our study demonstrates that consanguineous marriages increase the likelihood of taurodontism. This finding is consistent with the results of Bagcı et al. [27], Abbas et al. [28], and Rajeswari et al. [29], where consanguinity was strongly associated with dental anomalies, particularly taurodontism. These studies confirm that consanguineous marriages and genetic factors play a significant role in the development of dental anomalies like taurodontism. These findings underscore the importance of genetic counseling and premarital education programs. Moreover, the higher prevalence of taurodontism observed in female children in our study represents another noteworthy finding. This observation suggests that sex-related genetic or hormonal factors may play a role in the development of dental anomalies. In particular, hormones such as estrogen may influence the odontogenesis process, potentially leading to the morphological differences observed in female individuals. However, the exact biological mechanisms underlying this association have not yet been clearly elucidated in the literature. Further research is warranted to better understand the biological and environmental factors contributing to this sex-based variation.

Another important finding of the study was the statistically significant association between maternal smoking during pregnancy and the presence of multiple taurodontic teeth. This relationship is biologically plausible, as smoking is known to interfere with fetal development by reducing uteroplacental blood flow and altering gene expression related to tooth morphogenesis. Nicotine and other toxins may disrupt the activity of dental epithelial cells during critical stages of tooth bud formation, leading to root anomalies such as taurodontism. This aligns with studies linking maternal smoking to a wide range of craniofacial and dental anomalies in children [30, 31]. Therefore, public health policies discouraging maternal smoking remain essential not only for general fetal health but also for reducing specific dental anomalies.

Despite the valuable insights gained from this study, one notable limitation was the lack of a healthy control group. Because the study was retrospective and focused solely on patients with taurodontism, including a control group was not feasible. The lack of a control group limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the relative risk factors associated with taurodontism compared to the general population. Future studies could benefit from the inclusion of a control group to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors contributing to taurodontism and to better assess its prevalence in different population segments.

Finally, a further limitation of this study is the retrospective nature of the 30-item questionnaire administered to the mothers of pediatric patients regarding medication use and illnesses during pregnancy. Since the questions were asked 8 to 15 years after the events in question, many mothers were unable to accurately recall or provide reliable answers to some of the questions. As a result, certain risk factors identified in the questionnaire were excluded from the analysis as the information could not be reliably obtained. Although these questions were part of the initial survey, they could not be assessed in the study due to recall bias. This limitation highlights the challenges of retrospective data collection, and future studies could benefit from prospectively designed questionnaires or more immediate follow-up to reduce the potential for recall bias and improve data reliability.

Conclusion

Identifying vulnerable groups and informing families about taurodontism is important, as taurodontic teeth are often clinically indistinguishable from normal teeth and may present significant treatment challenges. To achieve this, comprehensive studies with larger sample sizes are necessary. The findings of this study offer valuable insights for future research. Since taurodontic teeth are not rare, clinician awareness should be raised to ensure early diagnosis and the implementation of appropriate preventive treatments to minimise complications in at-risk groups.
