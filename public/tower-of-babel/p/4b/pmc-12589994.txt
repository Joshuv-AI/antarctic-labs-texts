Improved properties of high-moisture extruded meat analogs using ultrasound-treated Nannochloropsis gaditana and chickpea protein concentrate
Authors: Lu Lu, Jian Zhu, Yating Sun, Hang Yu, Weibiao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research studied ultrasound treatment for improving the quality of meat analogs made from Nannochloropsis gaditana and chickpea protein concentrate (CP) via high-moisture extrusion (HME). Ultrasound was applied at three intensities (105, 158, and 210 W/cm2) and four durations (5, 10, 20, 30 min) to treat N. gaditana cells. The ultrasound treatment at 158 W/cm2 for 30 min was found to result in the optimal disruption for extrusion without adhesion of cellular debris or intracellular materials to the surface. Extrudates containing ultrasound-treated N. gaditana exhibited more continuous and elongated fibers with better textural properties compared with those with untreated N. gaditana. Specific mechanical energy (SME) was negatively correlated with fibrous structure. Among them, the extrudates with 30% ultrasound-treated N. gaditana (30%-U group) had the highest fibrous degree (2.13 N/N) and the lowest SME (37.70 kJ/kg). Structural analysis showed a higher β-sheet content in the ultrasound-treated samples, contributing to protein network stabilization via enhanced disulfide and hydrogen bonding. Additionally, protein digestibility increased from 68.77-78.32% in raw samples to 75.43–84.37% after the ultrasound treatment. Sensory evaluation results indicated that acceptability of the 30%-U group was closest to commercial meat analogs, although color and flavor remained to be improved. The ultrasound treatment of N. gaditana improved the structure and nutritional value of meat analogs prepared by N. gaditana and CP, which demonstrated its potential as an emerging technology for preparing sustainable microalgae-based protein products.Keywords: Ultrasound, Nannochloropsis gaditana, High-moisture extrusion, Fibrous structure, Protein digestibility

Introduction

Due to the increasing population and shortage of arable land, the global demand for animal meat as a protein source is projected to rise, potentially reaching 470 million tons by 2050 [1]. However, the consumption of animal meat is limited by ethical concerns, environmental impacts (including high energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions), and health risks associated with excessive intake of saturated fats and cholesterol [2]. The development of meat analogs provides an alternative to animal-based meat. Plant-based meat analogs (PBMA), also known as meat substitutes, are innovative products that mimic the texture, flavor, appearance, and nutritional properties of traditional meat using plant proteins [3]. High-moisture extrusion (HME) with a water content exceeding 40% is a common method for PBMA processing [4]. The current protein sources known for high-moisture extruded meat analogs include soybeans, wheat, and peanuts [5]. However, these sources still require extensive arable land for cultivation, and their allergenic potential remains a concern [3].

Microalgae is a reliable new protein source. The annual yield of microalgae is estimated to be 15–30 tons/ ha, which is approximately 10 times higher than the yield of soybeans [6]. Nannochloropsis gaditana is a species of microalgae with a high protein content (> 48%), a complete essential amino acid profile, and a high content of vitamin B and omega-3 fatty acids [7]. Previous studies indicated that it is difficult to generate a desired fibrous structure when directly incorporating microalgae with plant proteins [8,9]. The thick cell walls of microalgae largely restrict the release of intracellular proteins and therefore hinder the protein–protein cross-linking required to form a new protein network. Furthermore, the multilayered cell wall structure impedes the penetration of digestive enzymes, thereby reducing the bioaccessibility of intracellular proteins for human absorption. Therefore, pre-treatment of microalgae prior to extrusion to disrupt the cell wall would be a viable approach to both improve the fibrous structure of microalgae-containing meat analogs and promote the digestibility of protein.

Ultrasound has been proven to be a mild and effective green processing technology for disrupting the cell wall of microalgae, efficiently releasing intracellular components without compromising their biological activity and functionality [10]. During the ultrasound treatment, cavitation, high pressure and temperature would efficiently disrupt the rigid cell wall of microalgae to release cellular compounds [11]. Keris-Sen, Sen, Soydemir and Gurol [12] reported that the cell walls of mixed microalgae cultures dominated by Chlorococcales order was disrupted after the ultrasound treatment (0.4 kWh/L), and the content of proteins and carbohydrates in the culture medium increased. Under the continuous ultrasound treatment at 200 W for 8 min, the extraction rate of water-soluble protein in a 1% Nannochloropsis sp. suspension significantly increased from 0.85% to 1.80% (p < 0.05) [13]. Additionally, ultrasound treatment enhanced the crude protein digestibility of Chlorella vulgaris. An animal study indicated that experimental rats fed by ultrasound-treated C. vulgaris as their sole protein source grew 1.5 times faster than those fed by untreated C. vulgaris without adverse effects on blood or major organs [14,15]. Previous studies normally utilized ultrasound for extracting microalgal proteins; further applications of ultrasound-treated microalgae in extruded meat analogs are largely unexplored.

The use of a single source of protein is detrimental to the formation of fibrous structures in PBMA since the aggregation of same protein molecules results in unstable structures that eventually develop into a layered rather than an anisotropic structure [16]. Accordingly, chickpea protein concentrate (CP) was selected for blending with N. gaditana. Chickpea is widely available and provides a favorable amino acid profile, low allergenicity, and less beany flavor [17]. The excellent water absorption and gelation properties of CP also facilitate the anisotropic fiber formation during extrusion [18]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of ultrasound treatment of N. gaditana on the quality of high-moisture extruded meat analogs prepared from N. gaditana and CP. Firstly, N. gaditana was treated by ultrasound at different parameters, and the optimal ultrasound conditions were selected based on a number of characterization including scanning electron microscopy (SEM), particle size distribution, cell disruption, and protein release. Further impacts of ultrasound treatment on preparing N. gaditana-CP meat analogs were evaluated according to structural properties, protein digestibility and sensory attributes. Results of this study would offer important perspectives on ultrasound treatment in developing microalgae as a potential ingredient in the meat analogs industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

N. gaditana biomass powder and chickpea protein concentrate (CP) containing 48% and 78% protein, respectively, were obtained from Shaanxi Guanchen Biotechnology Co. LTD (Shaanxi province, China). BCA protein kit was purchased from Bi YunTian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chemical reagents, pepsin (EC 3. 4. 23. 1, 1800 U/mg) originated from porcine gastric mucosa, and pancreatin (EC 232. 468. 9, 200 U/mg, based on the enzymatic activity of trypsin) originated from porcine pancreas were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, USA).

Ultrasound processing of N. gaditana

A 5% (w/v) suspension of N. gaditana was selected based on preliminary trials considering proper viscosity of suspension and cavitation efficiency of ultrasound treatment [19]. The N. gaditana suspension was treated by ultrasound (UIP-1000, Hielscher, Germany) at a constant frequency of 20 kHz. The ultrasound condition was modified from Zhang, Grimi, Marchal, Lebovka and Vorobiev [20], where the suspension was treated using a BS4d22 sonotrode at power levels of 400, 600, and 800 W, corresponding to ultrasound intensities of 105, 158, and 210 W/cm2, respectively. Treatment durations were set at 5, 10, 20, and 30 min. A cycle of 10 s on-5 s off was employed, and an ice-water bath was used to prevent overheating. The experiments were conducted at room temperature. The ultrasonic probe was consistently positioned at the center of the 500 mL suspension. Lastly, the samples were freeze-dried, and stored in a sealed container at 4°C for further use.

Scanning electron microscopy analysis

The microstructure of freeze-dried N. gaditana was observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Quanta 250, Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). The N. gaditana powder was fixed on the holder and coated with gold particles. Images were captured at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and magnifications of 5000 × [21].

Particle size distribution

The particle size distribution of the untreated and ultrasound-treated N. gaditana suspensions was measured by a laser diffraction particle size distribution analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). Suspension of 2 mL was dispersed in a sample chamber containing distilled water. The particle refractive index was 1.57, and the solvent refractive index was 1.33. Values smaller than the particle sizes for 10%, 50%, and 90% of the samples were defined as D10, D50, and D90, respectively, and the volume distribution was further calculated [22].

The number of microalgae cells was measured using an automated cell counter (Bio-Rad TC20, USA). A 10 µL of each diluted sample in distilled water was used for analysis. High-moisture extrusion (HME)

The microalgae treated by ultrasound at 158 W/cm2 for 30 min was selected for subsequent HME processing. Before the extrusion, the ultrasound-treated/raw N. gaditana powder was mixed with CP in various ratios (w/w): 0/100 (control group), 10/90, 20/80, 30/70, 40/60, and 50/50. HME trials were conducted using a co-rotating twin-screw extruder (Process 11, Thermo Scientific, Germany). The diameter of the screws was 11 mm and the L/D ratio was 44:1. The extruder contained seven barrels, and a rectangular cooling die (40 × 8 × 4 cm, L × W × H) was located at the end of the extruder. The mixed dry powder was fed at 9.3 g/min at a constant rotating speed of 150 rpm and the moisture level was 52%. The temperature of the seven zones was set at 80°C, 90°C, 115°C, 140°C, 150°C, 150°C, and 150°C, respectively. A circulated water cooler (20°C) was applied for the cooling die. The extrudates were collected after the equipment reached a stable extrusion state. The final products were immediately vacuum-sealed and frozen at −20°C. A fraction of the samples was further freeze-dried for subsequent analysis.

Morphological observation of extrudates

The extrudate was first cut into 2 cm-long strips followed by folding and fixed with sticks, and then freeze dried. The freeze-dried extrudate was fixed on the sample holder using conductive adhesive and gold-coated. The internal structure of the extrudate was observed using SEM (Quanta 250, Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). The accelerating voltage was set to 15 kV and images were taken at 50 × and 10,000 ×.

Texture profile analysis

Fresh extrudates were first cut into 15 × 20 mm (W × L) and then analyzed by using a texture analyzer (Universal TA, Shanghai Tengba Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China). Textural properties and fibrous degree were measured by Chen, Zhang, Zhang, Meng and Wang [24]. Samples were compressed to 50% of original thickness at 1 mm/s for 2 s using a P/20R probe followed by recording their hardness, springiness, and chewiness. For cutting force, samples were cut using a Warner-Bratzler shear blade at a rate of 1 mm/s to a deformation of 75% of their original shape. The cutting force in the vertical direction of the extrusion was referred to as the lengthwise shear force (FL), while the cutting force in the parallel direction was defined as the crosswise shear force (FV). The ratio of FL to FV was considered to be the fibrous degree of the samples. To compare the fibrous degree with real meat, we also selected seven types of meat, namely beef shank, pork chops, lamb leg, pork leg, beef leg, lean meat, and chicken breast. Each meat sample was boiled at 100°C for 10 min as proposed by Samard and Ryu [25] before analysis.

Protein secondary structure

The protein secondary structures of the raw materials and fresh extrudates were evaluated using the ATR mode of a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) [27]. The extrudates were cut into a 1 mm film before measurement. The spectra were recorded in the 4000–400 cm−1 range and averaged 32 scans with a resolution of 4 cm−1. The Peakfit software (ver. 4.12, SeaSolve Software Inc., Framingham, USA) was applied to analyze the amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) in each sample.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectrum

An intrinsic fluorescence spectrophotometer (Shimadzu RF-6000, Japan) was used to assess the polarity of the microenvironment of raw materials and extrudates [28]. Samples of 20 mg were dissolved in 0.1 M PBS (pH = 7.3) and then shaken at 25°C for 1.5 h. The supernatant was extracted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min. The excitation wavelength was set to 280 nm, and the emission wavelength was scanned from 300 to 450 nm. Both excitation and emission slits were 2 nm wide, and the scanning speed was set to 600 nm/min.

Protein molecular interactions

The protein molecular interactions were determined by the protein solubility of samples in different solvents [29]. Eight solvents were selected as follows to break different protein interactions: (1) 0.1 M, pH = 7.3, phosphate buffer solution (PBS); (2) 0.052 M, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) in PBS; (3) 8 M, urea in PBS; (4) 0.1 M, 2-mercaptoethanol (ME) in PBS; (5) 0.052 M, SDS + 8 M, urea in PBS; (6) 0.052 M, SDS + 0.1 M, ME in PBS; (7) 8 M, urea + 0.1 ME, in PBS; (8) 0.052 M, SDS + 8 M, urea + 0.1 M, ME in PBS. An amount of 250 mg freeze-dried powder from either extrudates or raw materials was added with 10 mL of solvent and thoroughly mixed in a shaker for 1 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min and then its supernatant was collected. This extraction process was repeated twice to ensure sufficient protein extraction. The protein solubility in the supernatant was determined using the Kjeldahl method. Protein solubility was calculated based on the ratio of protein content in the supernatant to the protein of the sample.

In vitro protein digestibility

The extrudates were subjected to a three-phase in vitro digestion model. The formulations of simulated salivary fluid (SSF), simulated gastric fluid (SGF), and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) were prepared following the INFOGEST protocol with some modifications [30]. During the oral simulation stage, 250 mg of the raw materials and extrudates were dispersed in 5.975 mL DI water and mixed with 4 mL SSF and 25 µL CaCl2 (0.3 M). The fluid was incubated at 37°C for 2 min. In the gastric digestion stage, 1 mL pepsin (20,000 U/mL) and 9 mL of gastric pre-mix solution (24 mL SGF, 15 µL 0.3 M CaCl2, 1.05 mL 6 M HCl, and 1.935 mL DI water) were incorporated to the mixture. The solution was digested at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 2 h. After the gastric digestion, the sample solution was added to 20 mL intestinal pre-mix solution, which included 15 mL pancreatin (800 U/mL), 7.5 mL bile extract (0.16 M), 25.5 mL SIF, 0.12 mL 0.3 M CaCl2, 60 µL 5 M NaOH, and 11.82 mL DI water. Samples were further incubated at 37°C and 200 rpm for 2 h. After completing the three digestion stages, the sample solutions were denatured to inactivate enzyme activity by boiling for 10 min. Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation of meat analogs was conducted by following the method in Liu, Yi, Ye, Leng, Hossen, Sameen, Dai, Li and Qin [31] with slight modification. Briefly, a total of 20 untrained panelists (10 male and 10 female) voluntarily participated in the sensory evaluation. All the volunteers were familiar with the procedure of sensory evaluation although having no specific training on this type of product. The informed consent was obtained for experimentation with panelists. The samples and commercially available PBMA were uniformly cut into 2 cm × 2 cm × 0.5 cm (W × L × H) pieces and randomly placed in pre-coded paper cups. A 5-point hedonic scale (from 1: not acceptable to 5: very acceptable) was used to assess the fibrous structure, color, chewiness, odour, and overall acceptability of the meat analogs. Drinking water was provided to panelists before tasting each new sample.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicates. The data was described as the mean value ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Duncan’s test as a post-hoc test to determine significant differences (p < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro 2019b software.

Results and discussion

Effects of ultrasound treatment on N. gaditana

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images

Fig. 1 shows the SEM images of raw N. gaditana cells (A) and cells after different ultrasound treatments (B-D). The original N. gaditana cells were near spherical with an average diameter of 2–4 μm. The untreated cells tended to aggregate in clusters, which could be attributed to flocculation and spray-drying during harvesting [20]. Ultrasound treatment induced uniform dispersion of microalgae clusters. However, small clusters and unbroken microalgae cells were still observed under the conditions of low intensity and short duration of ultrasound treatment (e.g., 105, 158, 210 W/cm2, 5 min). Increasing the ultrasound intensity and extending the duration resulted in greater cell disruption. Nevertheless, a small proportion of N. gaditana cells remained undisrupted at 105 W/cm2 for 30 min of ultrasound treatment. When the ultrasound intensity increased to 158 W/cm2, the cell morphology of microalgae was relatively intact at 5 min and showed the increasing disruption at 20 min. At higher energy levels, the cell walls showed some substances adhering to the surface, which could be fragments of individual cells and leaked intracellular materials (such as proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids), and may cause the aggregation. This undesirable aggregation constrains cross-linking and conformational rearrangements between microalgal proteins and CP during subsequent extrusion, thereby hindering the formation of fibrous structures. Moreover, excessively high ultrasound intensities may diminish the expected effect since an overabundance of cavitation bubbles would interfere with the transmission of ultrasonic wave [32].

SEM images of (A) untreated and (B-D) ultrasound-treated N. gaditana cells.

Particle size distribution

The particle size distribution elucidated the extent of microalgae cell disruption caused by ultrasound. Except for the samples uunder 210 W/cm2 ultrasonic treatment for 20 min and 30 min, with increasing ultrasound intensity and duration, the D10, D50, and D90 values of N. gaditana decreased significantly (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1). This phenomenon indicated that the ultrasound treatment promoted the dispersion and rupture of cell aggregates. Fig. 2A shows the changes of structure in N. gaditana before and after 30 min of ultrasound treatment at different intensity levels (105, 158, and 210 W/cm2). The particle size distribution of raw N. gaditana exhibited a unimodal distribution with a peak at 92 µm, which indicated that the untreated microalgae primarily existed in clusters. After 30 min-ultrasound treatment, the cells showed a bimodal distribution. At 105 W/cm2 and 158 W/cm2, the positions of the smaller and larger peaks remained consistent at 3 µm and 26 µm, respectively. The area proportion of the smaller peak was higher under the treatment of 158 W/cm2 for 30 min, suggesting a more thorough dispersion of the clusters compared to the treatment of 105 W/cm2 for the same duration. Notably, the particle size increased significantly under the treatment of 210 W/cm2 for 30 min, with the larger peak shifting from 26 µm to 81 µm (p < 0.05). This phenomenon is mainly attributed to the aggregation or adhesion of broken cells under cavitation leading to re-agglomerate of the cells, which was consistent with the phenomenon observed in Section 3.1.1. These aggregates reduced the stability of microalgal dispersion in N. gaditana-CP mixtures during subsequent extrusion, which may limit the cross-linking between microalgal proteins and CP. Cao, Sun, Shi, Li, Guan, Liu, Huang and Zhang [33] also observed a similar phenomenon.

(A) Volume density, (B) cell disruption, and (C) relative protein release of N. gaditana under different combinations of ultrasound treatment.

Quantification of cell disruption

The impact of ultrasound on cell disruption degree is shown in Fig. 2B. The degree of cell disruption increased from 7.86% to 45.80% with increased ultrasound intensity and extended duration of treatment, which indicated that ultrasound effectively disrupted the cell wall of N. gaditana. With the extension of ultrasound time, the differences in cell disruption between 158 W/cm2 and 210 W/cm2 were not significant (p > 0.05). At 105 W/cm2 for 5 min, the cell disruption degree was 7.86%; it increased to about 2 times higher under 5 min-ultrasound treatment at 210 W/cm2. However, the cell disruption degree only increased by 2.66% and 1.60% at 20 and 30 min, respectively, when the intensity was raised from 158 W/cm2 to 210 W/cm2, without significant difference (p > 0.05). Ultrasound-induced cell disruption relied on the energy generated by the vibration and rupture of pre-existing gas nuclei in water. With an increase in treatment duration, the cavitation bubbles attenuated in the ultrasonic waves. The increased number of cavitation bubbles can absorb and scatter the sound wave, thereby weakening the acoustic field substantially (i.e., acoustic shielding) and reducing energy transfer from the ultrasound transducer to the medium [34]. The high-intensity ultrasound would spread the bubbles and disperse the transmission of ultrasonic waves. The extent of cell disruption did not increase linearly with increased time and intensity. A previous study reported that the effect of ultrasound on cell disruption would weaken with the continuous decrease of cell particle size [35]. Smaller particles were more readily entrained by the oscillatory acoustic field, which would lower slip velocity and surface shear. The increase of debris and aggregates accompanied by the reduction of particle size would also attenuate the effectiveness of cavitation.

Relative protein release

The amount of protein released reflects cell integrity, which can be used as an indicator of cell disruption efficiency. The cross-linking of proteins is one of the main contributors to the formation of a fibrous structure of meat analogs. As shown in Fig. 2C, the protein release in the supernatant closely aligned with the overall trend of cell disruption degree. Ultrasound treatment disrupted cells, facilitating intracellular protein release and enhancing extraction efficiency. When the duration of ultrasound lasted more than 20 min, no significant difference in protein release was observed at 158 W/cm2 or 210 W/cm2 (p > 0.05). The relative protein release of untreated microalgae suspension was 6.80%, and the highest relative protein release was 8.75% under ultrasound treatment. Although the cell disruption degree could exceed 40% during ultrasound processing, only a limited proportion of protein was dissolved in the aqueous phase. The limited dissolution may be attributed to the low water solubility of native microalgae proteins with a high proportion of hydrophobic side chains. Some microalgae proteins are structural components of the cell wall or preferentially bind to other intracellular substances [23,36]. Moreover, prolonged high-intensity ultrasound treatment may cause some protein degradation, potentially reducing the protein content in the supernatant [37]. Therefore, considering the ultrasound effect and energy consumption, 30 min-ultrasound treatment at 158 W/cm2 was selected as the optimal condition for processing N. gaditana cells that were used for subsequent extrusion studies.

Morphological observation of extrudates

To determine the fibrous structure of extrudates, various microscopic and macroscopic observations were conducted, which revealed the changes of structure after extrusion. SEM images were taken at magnifications of 50 × and 10,000 × to observe the microstructure of extrudates and the morphology of microalgae cells in the samples, respectively (Fig. 3). The macrostructure of extrudates was consistent with the observations obtained under low magnification (Supplementary Fig. 1). The CP group did not form fibers but instead, showed a multilayer structure. It could be considered as over-texturization in the extrusion, which was attributed to the strong gelling ability of CP. The inclusion of microalgae N. gaditana in the mixture with CP helped cross-linking by forming a complex multiphase system and the resultant extrudates exhibited a parabolic fiber structure, which confirmed that single-source proteins might be unsuitable for forming anisotropic fibers. The extrudates made from CP and untreated N. gaditana exhibited weak cross-linking. The fibers showed short, discontinuous, and loosely structures as shown in Fig. 3 (B1–F1). When sonicated microalgae were added as raw materials, the fiber structure of the extrudates improved; for example, abundant filamentous fibers appeared in the 30%-U group. Ultrasound treatment promoted more microalgae intracellular protein, enhancing protein–protein interactions and forming more networks. Simultaneously, water-insoluble microalgae components, such as cell wall fragments, provided extra mechanical strength to support the formation of anisotropic fibers due to their strong resistance during flow [38]. Furthermore, N. gaditana had a higher lipid content (10.5%) compared with CP (4.8%). Less than 10% of lipid has been confirmed to help stabilize the fiber structure by strengthening the protein-fat interaction [39]. However, when the microalgae content increased to 50%, the ultrasound and untreated groups were similar with only loose short fibers. This phenomenon indicated that excessive microalgae addition (> 50%) might weaken the fiber structure of the extrudates. The cell disruption rate of N. gaditana was 44.20% under the ultrasound treatment at 158 W/cm2 for 30 min. At the 50% addition level, an excessive amount of undisrupted microalgae was embedded into the protein phase, hindering protein unfolding and rearrangement. Other macromolecular substances in microalgae (e.g., lipids and polysaccharides) may attach to the protein surface and impair protein aggregation. For the control, CP was evenly distributed and thoroughly cross-linked. Apparent microalgae cells were visible in the five untreated groups, whereas microalgae cell walls appeared blurred and indistinct in the ultrasound-treated sample group except for the 50%-U group. Although the ultrasound treatment did not entirely disrupt all microalgae cells, the intact cells underwent stretching, making them more susceptible to rupture under high temperature, pressure, and shear forces during the extrusion process than the untreated samples.

SEM images of meat analog samples made by (A-F) raw or (G-K) ultrasound-treated N. gaditana with addition level from 0 to 50%.

Texture properties of extrudates

The texture properties (hardness, springiness, and chewiness) of the samples are essential indicators for evaluating the quality of meat analogs (Fig. 4). The CP group showed the highest hardness, springiness, and chewiness. The addition of microalgae promoted the formation of microalgae-CP biphasic structure, reduced excessive cross-linking between CP, and made the extruded structure more loose. For samples containing N. gaditana, a moderate inclusion of N. gaditana could enhance the network structure formed between N. gaditana and CP. However, excessive incorporation, particularly at 50% addition level, led to a decline in textural properties. The 50% addition of microalgae impeded the rearrangement of proteins due to the presence of undamaged cell walls and fragments of N. gaditana. This result was consistent with previous studies on microalgae-based meat analogs [40]. Additionally, the increased fat content exerted a lubricating effect that diminished barrel shearing while enhancing protein-lipid interactions, making the samples softer and less chewable. Except for the 50%-U group, the ultrasound-treated groups exhibited higher hardness, springiness, and chewiness than the samples with raw N. gaditana at the same addition levels. Notably, the textural properties began to decline at the 50%-U group, and the 30%-U and 40%-U groups showed no significant differences (p > 0.05). The ultrasound-induced disruption of the microalgal cell walls released more intracellular proteins, which interacted with CP to form more anisotropic, elongated fibrous networks, enhancing structural stability.

(A) Hardness, (B) springiness, (C) chewiness, and (D) fibrous degree of meat analog samples with addition of N. gaditana from 10 to 50%. CP refers to extrudates with only chickpea protein. N refers to extrudates with raw N. gaditana. U refers to extrudates with ultrasound-treated N. gaditana.

Simulating the fibrous structure of real meat is a challenge in producing meat analogs. The fibrous degree of samples was calculated using the ratio of cutting forces in the transverse and longitudinal directions and compared with cooked real meat to assess the product quality (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 2). Although extrudates made solely from CP showed the highest hardness, the high cutting forces in both directions resulted in the lowest fiber ratio. The fibrous degree in groups containing N. gaditana was significantly higher than that of the CP group (p < 0.05). The degree initially increased and then decreased after 30% microalgae addition in both raw and ultrasound-treated samples. The fibrous degree of the ultrasound-treated microalgae samples ranged from 1.65 to 2.13 N/N, which was higher than that of the raw samples. The incorporation of microalgae effectively alleviated the overly dense structure of pure CP extrudates, promoting the fiber formation. However, excessive fat and the inclusion of too much microalgae with intact cell walls may interfere with the cross-linking between microalgae proteins and CP, leading to destabilization of the protein network and reduced fibrousness. Ultrasound treatment exposed more intracellular proteins to contact with CP, which would strengthen the protein–protein cross-linking and stabilize the anisotropic long fibers. Among the selected seven common meat varieties, the boiled beef and pork had similar fibrous degree, ranging from 1.61 to 2.23 N/N, while lamb leg and chicken breast showed higher fibrousness (3.38 N/N and 4.56 N/N, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 2). From the fibrous degree value, the meat analogs made with N. gaditana, especially those treated by ultrasound, closely resembled pork and beef. It again confirmed that the ultrasound-treated N. gaditana could be a promising novel ingredient for producing PBMA.

Specific Mechanical Energy (SME)

SME refers to the mechanical energy per unit mass sample and is a crucial parameter for optimizing and scaling extrusion production. SME is proportional to the melt torque and inversely proportional to the mass flow rate. Torque represents the work required to rotate the screw, indirectly reflecting the flow behavior of material. The mass flow rate mainly reflects the filling condition within the barrel [41]. The trends in torque and SME were similar, while the changes in flow rate were not as pronounced (Supplementary Table 2). The control group had the highest SME (125.86 kJ/kg) and torque (2.28 Nm), as well as the lowest mass flow rate (17.08 g/min). This phenomenon is mainly attributed to that the gelation between CP formed a strong network, increasing the viscosity of the mixture, reducing its flow, and requiring the screw to apply greater shear to move the material forward. The addition of microalgae significantly reduced the SME value and the torque (p < 0.05). For the samples added with either raw or ultrasound-treated N. gaditana, the torque and SME initially decreased with increasing microalgae addition, followed by an increase after the microalgae addition exceeded 30%. SME values and torque in the ultrasound-treated group were always lower than the untreated samples. The lowest SME and torque were observed in the 30%-U group, at 37.70 kJ/kg and 0.84 Nm, respectively. The addition of microalgae resulted in a looser network and captured more water at the flowing phase to reduce the viscosity. The inclusion of lipids decreased the friction and shear between the barrel and screw. Ultrasound treatment allowed more fat to be released into grease. Additionally, the ultrasound treatment made the microalgae size become more uniform to generate a more orderly network and reduced the mechanical resistance caused by microalgae clusters and insoluble large particles. However, excessive microalgae addition may disrupt the stability of the protein network, negatively affecting the flow of the mixture and the shear exerted by the screw. Some previous studies reported a negative correlation between SME and the formation of fibrous structure [29,42], which was consistent with our findings as described in Section 3.3.

Protein secondary structure analysis

FTIR was measured to understand the transformation of protein secondary structure during extrusion (Fig. 5). The amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) is one major band in protein IR analysis, which is highly sensitive to secondary structure composition [43]. It primarily arises from the C=O stretching vibration of the protein backbone with the C-N bond stretching and the N-H bond in-phase bending. According to Chen, Yang, Liu, Qian, Tian, Zhang and Cao [44], the amide I region can be divided into β-sheet (1600–1640 cm−1), random coil (1640–1650 cm−1), α-helix (1650–1660 cm−1), and β-turn (1660–1700 cm−1) to calculate the relative content of secondary structure. The β-structures (including β-sheets and β-turns) are considered to be more ordered, whereas α-helix and random coil are more likely to deconstruct or unfold under changes in the external environment. In the β-structures, β-sheet is more stable and rigid protein configuration, formed by multiple parallel or antiparallel β-strands connected through hydrogen bonds. This arrangement aligns with the highly repetitive sequences required for fiber formation. However, β-turn would alter the orientation of polypeptide chain, leading to reversals in the peptide backbone folding, which may hinder the formation of continuous structures during protein chain stacking [45,46].

N refers to extrudates with raw N. gaditana. U refers to extrudates with ultrasound-treated N. gaditana. Significant differences among samples either before or after HME are indicated by uppercase letters (p < 0.05), while significant differences between raw material and extrudate within the same sample composition are indicated by lowercase letters (p < 0.05).

Both before and after extrusion, the CP sample had the highest β-sheet content. During the extraction and preparation of CP, the protein conformation was changed to a more stable structure. Following extrusion, the CP underwent further tight cross-linking to generate a dense network. The addition ratio of CP/microalgae showed limited impacts on the secondary structure of extrudates with the addition of either ultrasound-treated or raw N. gaditana. The secondary protein structure of all extruded samples was dominated by the β-structures. Specifically, the extrudates from the ultrasound-treated N. gaditana had lower β-turn and higher β-sheet than the untreated group. It suggested that ultrasound facilitated the protein structure transformation of the CP-N. gaditana mixture from β-turn to a more ordered β-sheet structure during the extrusion. Ultrasound disrupted the cell wall of N. gaditana and reduced the particle size, which improved dispersion and provided a more continuous phase for extrusion. Moreover, the shear and cavitation generated by ultrasound treatment partially unfolded proteins and exposed buried hydrophobic residues and sulfhydryl groups. These groups facilitated hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds formation under extrusion conditions, which strengthened the stability of the network. This was consistent with [33] observed. Considering the results of fibrous degree and SEM, the meat analogs containing ultrasound-treated N. gaditana exhibited more elongated fibers than those containing raw N. gaditana. Therefore, the ultrasound-induced transition of protein structure from β-turn to β-sheet was crucial for the formation of cross-linked fiber network in the extrudates.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectrum

Proteins containing aromatic amino acids exhibit fluorescence upon light excitation at 280 nm [46]. The microenvironment of tryptophan residues was characterized by comparing the fluorescence intensity and the maximum emission wavelength (λmax) between the raw material and the extrudates in order to assess the conformation changes of protein in the HME (Fig. 6A and B). In the raw material, samples containing N. gaditana had lower fluorescence intensity and larger λmax than those of CP. The fluorescence intensity positively correlated to the concentration of chromophores in the solution, which indicated a relatively low tryptophan content in the microalgae.

Fluorescence intensity and protein solubility of (A, C) raw material and (B, D) meat analog samples with N. gaditana addition level from 10 to 50%. CP refers to extrudates with only chickpea protein. N refers to extrudates with raw N. gaditana. U refers to extrudates with ultrasound-treated N. gaditana.

The λmax is influenced by the polarity of the solution. A polar environment stabilizes the excited state of fluorophores, which reduces the emitted light energy and causes a red shift of λmax toward longer wavelengths [28]. Hydrophobic amino acids are typically buried within the protein core. Compared with CP, N. gaditana exhibited a looser protein spatial structure making the internal tryptophan more accessible to the polar aqueous solution and therefore, resulted in a larger λmax. This finding aligned with the observation of a more tightly packed β-sheet in CP as described earlier in Section 3.5. After HME, it was observed that the red-shift of λmax and the fluorescence intensity decreased in all the samples. The high temperature and mechanical forces during HME induced protein denaturation and potential degradation. The tertiary structure of proteins further unfolded and rearranged to expose more buried tryptophan residues to the surface. Meanwhile, the interactions between hydrophilic side chains and water molecules also enhanced, which increased the polarity of the solution environment.

Protein molecular interactions

The protein solubility before and after extrusion was determined to assess the impact of ultrasound treatment on protein–protein interactions (Fig. 6C and D). Proteins only in their natural state could be dissolved in PBS. ME is one of the reagents used to cleave disulfide bonds. Urea is a reagent to break hydrogen bonds, and SDS acts to break the hydrophobic interactions [29]. For the raw materials (Fig. 6C), protein solubility in SDS and urea solutions was higher than that in ME solution. In dual-reagent systems, solubility in PMU and PMS was also lower than that in PUS, which was attributed to the limited amount of sulfur-containing amino acids in the raw materials. The hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions collectively promoted the structural stability of raw material proteins, which was consistent with Liu and Hsieh [47]. The solubility of extrudates (Fig. 6D) in the three single solvent systems was similar to that of the corresponding raw materials. In the dual system, the samples had the lowest solubility in PMS before and after the extrusion since hydrogen bonds consistently contributed to the stability of the protein structure. The difference in solubility of the samples in PMUS and PUS increased after the extrusion, while the difference in solubility in PMUS and PMU decreased compared with that of the raw material. These changes of solubility differences suggested that the cross-linking of proteins during the HME process generated new disulfide bonds, and the hydrophobic interactions weakened the contribution to protein arrangement after extrusion. The disulfide bonds may be buried within the protein structure and stabilized by non-covalent bonds, making them difficult to reduce with a single ME solution. The synergistic effect of urea and SDS facilitated the unfolding of protein structure, exposing internal disulfide bonds and increasing their accessibility to ME. In all solvent systems, the solubility of extrudates in the ultrasound-treated group was higher than that of the untreated group at the same N. gaditana addition level. The ultrasound treatment induced greater exposure of hydrophobic cores, enhanced the hydrogen bond interactions between peptide chains, and facilitated the formation of more disulfide bond cross-linking, thereby stabilizing the protein network in the extrudates.

In vitro protein digestibility

Plant-based proteins are generally more difficult to digest compared with animal-based proteins. In vitro digestion was conducted to evaluate the protein bioaccessibility of extrudates (Fig. 7A). The protein digestibility of the raw materials decreased with the addition of microalgae but increased significantly after the ultrasound treatment (p < 0.05). The cell walls of N. gaditana were thick, which composed high content of cellulose and algaenan. Ultrasound treatment disrupted the cell walls and then either released microalgae proteins or made microalgae cellular internals more accessible to digestive enzymes. Consistent with previous studies, the protein digestibility of extrudates was higher than that of the original mixtures [24,48]. For CP, which was highly stabilized before extrusion, the increase in protein digestibility of extrudates was mainly attributed to the degradation and inactivation of antinutritional factors such as phytic acid and tannin due to the high temperature, pressure, and mechanical forces during extrusion [49]. The reduction in interference allowed the digestive enzymes to access protein sites more effectively.

N refers to extrudates with raw N. gaditana. U refers to extrudates with ultrasound-treated N. gaditana. Significant differences among samples either before or after HME are indicated by uppercase letters (p < 0.05), while significant differences between raw material and extrudate within the same sample composition are indicated by lowercase letters (p < 0.05).

The trend of protein digestibility in the extrudates containing microalgae was consistent with that of the raw materials. During extrusion, the cell walls of the microalgae were further disrupted, exposing more intracellular proteins to the external environment, and increasing their accessibility to digestive enzymes. At the same mixing ratio, the protein digestibility of the ultrasound-treated extrudates was higher than that of the untreated group. Ultrasound treatment disrupted the cell walls of N. gaditana, as well as reduced particle size, which improved their dispersion stability within the blends. During extrusion, these effects promoted the formation of uniform networks without large agglomerates, increasing the accessible surface area for digestive enzymes. Therefore, the ultrasound treatment effectively improved the protein digestion of meat analogs containing N. gaditana and enhanced their nutritional values.

Sensory evaluation

To systematically evaluate the impact of ultrasound treatment on product quality, extruded samples with the highest fibrous degree were selected from the ultrasound-treated (30%-U) and untreated N. gaditana (30%-N) groups, along with the control group (CP) and a commercial plant-based meat product (comprising 30% wheat gluten and 70% soy protein) for sensory evaluation (Fig. 7B). The evaluation encompassed five key attributes: odour, fibrous structure, chewiness, color, and overall acceptability. Results showed that the commercial product exhibited better texture but had a lower odour score compared with the other three samples. The ability of wheat gluten to form more disulfide bonds during HME and the excellent gelling properties of soy protein contributed to a stronger protein network [50]. However, the undesirable beany flavor of soybean may reduce consumer acceptance [51]. The CP selected in this study with minimal legume-like odors received a higher score in odour. Nevertheless, the fishy odour of N. gaditana in the microalgae-containing samples led to a lower odour acceptability than the CP group. The 30%-U sample was similar with the commercial product in terms of fibrous structure and chewiness, which confirmed that the ultrasound treatment enabled better cross-linking of microalgae proteins with chickpea protein during HME. Regarding the color, the high content of chlorophyll in N. gaditana resulted in an unpleasant dark and green color. This issue could be resolved through either decolorizing the raw materials, or adding red pigments during extrusion, and/or incorporating seasonings during cooking. Notably, the 30%-U sample exhibited a higher overall acceptability than the 30%-N and CP samples. Further improvements in color and flavor masking are necessary to align with consumer expectations.

Conclusions

This study employed ultrasound to disrupt cell wall of N. gaditana and furthermore, prepared extruded meat analogs by HME using N. gaditana and CP. Results of SEM, particle size, cell disruption, and protein release showed that the ultrasound treatment at 158 W/cm2 for 30 min effectively disrupted the cell walls without excessive adhesion or aggregation. The incorporation of N. gaditana improved the overly dense lamellar structure of the extrudates to create a parabolic fiber structure. The meat analogs prepared by raw N. gaditana were loose, whereas the samples prepared by ultrasound-treated N. gaditana generated continuous and elongated fibers. The results of textural properties demonstrated that the meat analogs prepared by ultrasound-treated N. gaditana had higher hardness, springiness, chewiness than those using raw microalgae at the same addition level. In addition, the ultrasound treatment promoted a structural shift from β-turn to β-sheet structures and meanwhile, improved the protein digestibility. Sensory evaluation revealed that the 30%-U sample was more acceptable than the 30%-N and CP samples, which was closer to the commercial product. However, the chlorophyll content and fishy flavor of N. gaditana may still limit the color and aroma appeal of the final products. Future research should focus on strategies such as pigment removal, deodorization, or targeted seasoning systems to improve the meat-like sensory profiles. The current size of panelists may limit the representation of broader consumer preferences; therefore, larger size of panelists should be employed in future studies that target the acceptability of market-ready products. Overall, this study confirmed that ultrasound treatment is an effective method to improve the fibrous structure and nutritional value of meat analogs containing N. gaditana, making this microalgae species a novel ingredient in extruded meat analogs products.
