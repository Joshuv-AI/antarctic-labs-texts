A GIS and statistical based approach to assessing citizen awareness of the energy transition and climate change across algerian climatic zones
Authors: Kahina Ouazar, Leila Abbas Mansour, Emmanuel Eveno, Khadidja Rahmani, Abderrahim Zemmit, Ahmed A. Arafat, Binalfew Abebe Jemberie, Mohammad Alsharef, Ahmed Althobaiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study analyses how citizens’ perceptions, behaviours, and socio-economic characteristics shape awareness of energy-transition and climate-change issues across Algeria’s main climatic zones. A mixed-methods design was adopted, drawing on a national survey conducted in 2024 (n = 1,204). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) identified three coherent latent constructs—behaviour, perception, and awareness—accounting for 74.9% of total variance. Climatic zoning based on the De Martone aridity index enabled the delineation of three representative climate contexts (arid, semi-arid, and semi-humid), allowing for a territorially sensitive assessment. Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) was then performed separately for each zone to analyse causal pathways among the latent variables. Results reveal that awareness is significantly influenced by both perception and behaviour in all climatic contexts, though the intensity and direction of these relationships vary geographically. Behaviour exerts stronger influence in arid and semi-arid regions, where awareness is more closely tied to concrete daily practices. In contrast, perception has the strongest effect on awareness in the semi-humid zone (β = 0.726, p < 0.001), indicating a more cognitively driven process. Perception and behaviour are consistently interrelated, with the strongest interaction observed in semi-humid areas (β = 0.533, p < 0.001). Socio-economic characteristics moderately influence behaviour in semi-arid regions and negatively affect perception in arid areas, reflecting persistent socio-spatial disparities. Overall, the findings demonstrate that awareness, perception, and behaviour operate as mutually reinforcing dimensions whose relative influence varies across climatic contexts. The study highlights the need for climate-sensitive and territorially differentiated public policies that integrate socio-behavioural drivers to strengthen citizen engagement and enhance the inclusiveness and effectiveness of national energy-transition strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43948-0.

Introduction

Climate change, characterized by long-term changes in temperature, precipitation patterns, and atmospheric variability, represents one of the most pressing global challenges of our time1–3. Since the Industrial Revolution, human activities; particularly fossil fuel combustion and deforestation, have led to a substantial increase in greenhouse gas concentrations, firmly establishing anthropogenic influence as the primary driver of global warming2,4.These changes pose increasing risks to ecosystems and human health worldwide5. Among the multidimensional responses required to mitigate these impacts, the energy transition has gained central importance. It involves shifting from fossil-fuel–based systems to diversified, renewable, and efficient energy models6,7. However, beyond technological substitution, this transition entails deep political, social, and cultural transformations that depend on the involvement of governments, institutions, communities, and citizens8,9.

Citizen participation has therefore emerged as an essential pillar of contemporary energy transitions. While historically confined to the role of passive consumers, citizens now increasingly contribute as active stakeholders through local energy initiatives, prosumer activities, demand-side management practices, and community-based renewable projects10. Empirical evidence from European contexts illustrates how citizen engagement, ranging from consumption reduction to co-production of renewable energy; supports the development of energy democracy and enhances the acceptability of transition policies11. However, this engagement varies across regions and is strongly conditioned by citizens’ perceptions, levels of knowledge, and understanding of energy and climate challenges. Positive perceptions reinforce policy acceptance and behavioural change, whereas negative or ambiguous attitudes can hinder transition progress12. Research has likewise shown that awareness of principles such as a “fair transition” or transparent governance structures significantly shapes public willingness to adopt pro-environmental behaviours13.

A growing body of literature has examined various facets of citizen awareness concerning energy and environmental issues. Studies have addressed drivers of awareness regarding renewable-energy technologies14,15, acceptance of specific technologies such as geothermal16, green hydrogen17, or biogas18, and awareness dynamics within energy communities19 or domestic energy practices20,21. Other research has explored smart-energy systems22, innovative awareness-raising tools such as serious games23,24, and the impact of energy crises on public perception25. Complementary studies have examined pro-environmental behaviours and barriers to their adoption26–28, as well as climate change awareness in terms of knowledge of causes, impacts, risks, and vulnerabilities29,30. More recent and integrated approaches have linked energy transition and climate change awareness by analysing citizens’ understanding of transition processes, willingness to pay, and levels of participation across different contexts31–33. In parallel, several empirical studies emphasize the role of socio-demographic characteristics and individual engagement in shaping climate awareness. Survey-based studies conducted in India and Australia show that information sources, perceived behaviours, personal concern, and socio-demographic profiles significantly influence climate perceptions, environmental responsibility, and the motivation to seek further information30,34,35.

Despite their contributions, existing studies remain fragmented. Few adopt a comprehensive and territorialized approach capable of simultaneously capturing the cognitive (knowledge, perceptions), behavioural (practices, attitudes), and contextual (climatic, socio-economic) dimensions of awareness. Moreover, limited attention has been paid to how climatic heterogeneity may differentially shape citizen engagement with energy-transition and climate-change issues; an important omission for countries with pronounced environmental gradients.

To address this gap, the present study develops an integrative empirical framework combining spatial analysis (GIS), dimensionality reduction (PCA), and causal modelling (SEM) to examine how awareness, perceptions, and behaviours interact across different climatic contexts in Algeria. Citizen awareness is conceptualized not merely as declarative knowledge but as a potential driver of behavioural engagement and a lever for social and environmental change.

Algeria provides an ideal case study due to its marked climatic diversity. From Mediterranean coastal zones to Saharan environments, combined with socio-economic disparities and evolving national transition strategies. This study therefore asks: To what extent are citizens’ awareness, perceptions, and behaviours interrelated in the context of energy transition and climate change, and how do these relationships vary across climatic and territorial settings? By providing a climate-sensitive analysis of the determinants of awareness and engagement, the study contributes to understanding the social foundations of energy transitions and offers a transferable methodological framework applicable in other regions.

Materials and methods

Study area

Algeria (shown in Fig. 1) is the largest country in Africa and presents significant energy-climate challenges arising from its geography and socio-economic conditions. Approximately 86% of the national territory is composed of Saharan desert, while nearly 90% of the population is concentrated in the northern coastal region. The country experiences strong climatic contrasts, with annual precipitation ranging from 400 to 670 mm along the Mediterranean coast to less than 200 mm in the Sahara, making Algeria highly sensitive to drought, water scarcity, and desertification36,37.

Climate projections for 2020–2030 anticipate temperature increases of 0.8–1.1 °C, a 10% decrease in precipitation, and sea-level rise of 5–10 cm, conditions likely to intensify existing vulnerabilities. Despite its abundant solar resources and significant natural-gas reserves, national energy production remains over 98% dependent on fossil fuels38. Rapid demographic expansion and rising energy demand further pressure energy security and export capacity. Although Algeria’s national strategy aims to achieve 27% renewable electricity by 2030, progress has been limited due to regulatory, institutional, and economic constraints.

Given this context, citizens’ awareness and engagement constitute essential components of a successful and socially acceptable energy transition. Algeria’s marked climatic heterogeneity; provides an ideal setting to explore how territorial factors shape awareness and behavioural responses.

Methodology

This study integrates survey data, GIS-based climatic classification, and multivariate statistical modelling to examine the determinants of citizens’ awareness of energy transition and climate change issues. The methodological workflow comprises four main steps:

Survey design and descriptive analysis; 2) Dimensionality reduction using Principal Component Analysis (PCA); 3) Climatic zoning using the De Martone aridity index; 4) Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) to examine relationships among latent constructs across climatic zones.

Questionnaire design and survey implementation

Survey data were collected between May and September 2024 using a mixed-mode approach combining face-to-face interviews and an online self-administered questionnaire. The study followed a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design. The target population consisted of individuals aged 18 years or older with permanent residence in Algeria. Participation was voluntary, and eligibility criteria were identical for both survey modes.

The sampling strategy was non-probabilistic and based on convenience and voluntary participation. For the face-to-face component, respondents were approached in public spaces (such as university campuses, public squares, and transport hubs) across different urban locations, aiming to capture a diversity of socio-demographic profiles. For the online component, the questionnaire was disseminated through academic and professional networks as well as social media platforms, where participants self-selected to take part in the study. As such, the sample cannot be considered statistically representative of the Algerian population.

A total of 2,003 responses were initially collected. After data cleaning procedures, including the removal of incomplete questionnaires and responses exhibiting logical inconsistencies, 1,204 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, resulting in the exclusion of responses with missing values in key variables.

The questionnaire administrated in two languages (French and Arabic), comprised 51 items grouped into four thematic sections (Fig. 3) covering civic awareness related to energy and climate issues, individual perceptions, self-reported behaviours, and socio-demographic characteristics (Detailed in Table S1 in Appendix).

The first section collected socio-demographic and spatial information about the respondents (e.g., age, gender, and commune of permanent residence), using categorical or continuous measures as appropriate.

The second section focused on respondents’ attitudes and self-reported behaviours related to energy use and the energy transition.

The third section examined individual perceptions related to climate change and energy challenges.

The fourth section addressed respondents’ level of awareness and self-assessed knowledge regarding energy transition and climate change issues.

Responses to attitudinal, behavioural, perceptual, and awareness items were measured using five-point Likert-type scales ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), allowing for the quantitative assessment of respondents’ positions across all dimensions.

All responses were numerically coded for analysis.

The questionnaire items were developed based on established instruments and previous empirical studies on energy transition, climate change awareness, and pro-environmental behaviour (e.g.22,39–41, . The items were subsequently adapted to the Algerian context to ensure conceptual relevance and clarity.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study involved a non-interventional, anonymous questionnaire survey among adults (aged 18 years or older) residing in Algeria. In line with Algerian national regulations and institutional practices, formal ethical approval was not required for this type of social science research, and no ethics committee review was available. The study followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained from all participants, and respondents were informed about the study objectives, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw. No personally identifiable data were collected.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to reduce dimensionality and extract latent structures within the awareness, perception, and behavioural variables42,43. Data adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity44. Components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained following Kaiser’s criterion45, and variables with loadings ≥ 0.40 were considered significant contributors46. A Varimax rotation was applied to enhance interpretability of the components47.

Identification of climatic areas

Climatic zones were delineated using the De Martonne aridity index48, a metric widely applied in Mediterranean, semi-arid, and arid environments due to its simplicity and reliability for large-scale bioclimatic classification49,50.

The index integrates two key climatic variables—annual mean temperature (T, °C) and annual total precipitation (P, mm)—according to Eq. Higher DI values indicate wetter conditions, while lower values correspond to semi-arid and arid environments. This makes the index particularly suitable for countries such as Algeria, where strong climatic gradients exist between the northern Mediterranean coast, the central high plateaus, and the Saharan regions.

In this study, DI was computed using reanalysis datasets for the period 2011–2023, combining:

*Annual average temperatures from the ERA5-Land model (0.1° × 0.1° resolution)51, whose global reliability has been demonstrated across diverse climates52,53.

*Annual average precipitation from the CHIRP (Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation) dataset, with a resolution of (0.05° × 0.05)54.

Temperature rasters were resampled to match the precipitation grid before computing DI values across the national territory. Based on the resulting continuous DI surface, three bioclimatic classes were identified, consistent with established De Martonne classifications:

All calculations (DI) and spatial analyses were conducted using ArcGIS Desktop (version 10.4; Esri, Redlands, CA, USA). Climatic zone maps were produced within the same environment, and all cartographic outputs were generated specifically for this study.

Survey respondents were then assigned to one of these three climatic zones according to the location of their province of residence. This classification enabled climate-sensitive analysis of awareness, perception, and behavioural patterns.

Structural Equation Modelling (SEM)

Building upon the PCA results, latent variables representing awareness, perception, and behaviour were defined, and SEM was applied to assess their relationships with each other and with socio-economic characteristics. As in previous studies in the context of public perceptions related to energy39,40. For each climatic zone, measurement models were first specified for each latent construct, and Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to evaluate internal consistency55.

The adequacy of the structural models was evaluated by examining the estimated path coefficients to assess the significance and strength of the relationships between latent and observed variables, together with standard goodness-of-fit indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), as well as residual-based indices, ensuring the reliability and stability of the estimated parameters46,56.

All SEM analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2) using the lavaan package, while exploratory PCA analyses were performed with the psych package. Graphical outputs were generated with ggplot2.

Principal component analysis

Preliminary diagnostic tests confirmed the adequacy of the data for factor analysis, with a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value of 0.75, exceeding the recommended minimum threshold of 0.5057, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was highly significant (χ² = 33,362.97; df = 1326; p < 0.001), indicating that the correlation matrix significantly departs from an identity matrix. This result confirms the presence of sufficient intercorrelations among variables and supports the suitability of the data for principal component analysis58,59.

Three components with eigenvalues > 1 were retained following Kaiser’s criterion (4.459, 2.414, 1.532), explaining 74.9% of total variance, which exceeds commonly accepted thresholds in social science research and confirms the robustness of the factor structure60. This step allows to get 32 variables with loadings > 0.4 (Table 2).

High loadings (0.52–0.88) indicate behavioural coherence centred on resource-saving and low-impact consumption. Variable B7 was excluded due to insufficient contribution.

Structural equation modelling

Using maximum likelihood estimation, model fit for the SEM applied to the three climatic zones was evaluated using standard goodness-of-fit indices (CFI, TLI, and RMSEA), as reported in Table 3. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) values in each zone were slightly below commonly cited cut-off criteria62. Such deviations may be attributed to model complexity, the use of self-reported survey data, and the heterogeneity of the social and climatic contexts considered58,63. By contrast, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) indicated acceptable model fit, remaining below the upper threshold of 0.1264.

Taken together, the joint consideration of absolute and incremental fit indices suggests that the estimated models provide a reasonable representation of the underlying data structure and are appropriate for exploratory and comparative analyses across the studied climatic zones.

The three SEM models reveal statistically significant structural relationships across the climatic zones. The estimated structural relationships, including standardized path coefficients and their statistical significance, are presented in Table 4.

In the arid zone, citizen awareness was significantly influenced by perception (β = 0.135, p < 0.05) and more strongly by behaviour (β = 0.223, p < 0.05). A negative relationship emerged between perception and socioeconomic characteristics (β = − 0.138, p < 0.05), suggesting that economic constraints reduce perceptual engagement with transition policies.

In semi-arid area, awareness was influenced by perception (β = 0.164, p < 0.05) and behaviour (β = 0.191, p < 0.05). A strong interrelation between perception and behaviour (β = 0.389, p = 0.001) indicated mutual reinforcement. Socioeconomic factors moderately influenced behaviour (β = 0.085, p < 0.05).

In semi-humid dry zone, awareness was predominantly influenced by perception (β = 0.726, p < 0.001)—substantially stronger than in other zones—with behaviour contributing significantly (β = 0.104, p < 0.05). A pronounced interaction between perception and behaviour (β = 0.533, p < 0.001) was observed.

Overall, the findings show that citizens’ perceptions of the energy transition and climate change are the main determinants of their level of awareness. Higher awareness strengthens issue perception and, in turn, increases acceptance of national energy transition policies. In addition, a positive relationship is observed between awareness and sustainable behaviour, suggesting that more aware individuals are more likely to adopt practices aligned with sustainable development objectives. This pattern highlights the central role of awareness in fostering responsible energy-related behaviours.

Discussions

Before discussing the substantive findings, it is important to consider methodological factors that may influence the interpretation of the results. The study employed a non-probabilistic, mixed-mode survey combining face-to-face interviews and online questionnaires, with participation based entirely on voluntary response. As a result, younger respondents and individuals with higher education were overrepresented. However, this composition broadly reflects Algeria’s demographic and geographic realities. Although respondents from southern regions are fewer, this is consistent with the fact that these areas account for only around 10% of the national population, while the majority of inhabitants reside in northern regions65. The predominance of younger participants aligns with the national age structure, in which the youth category represents a substantial share of the population65. The relatively high educational level observed among respondents mirrors recent national improvements in literacy and higher education, with an adult literacy rate of 81.4%66 and ertiary enrolment exceeding 66%67.

A similar consistency is observed with respect to income distribution. In this study, approximately 50% of respondents reported a monthly income below 20,000 DZD, while about 22% fell within the 20,000–50,000 DZD range. This pattern corresponds with national statistics indicating that 51.8% of salaried workers earn 25,000 DZD or less per month65. While income data are self-reported and therefore subject to reporting bias, the observed distribution remains consistent with official national statistics, suggesting that the sample reflects broader wage patterns rather than a sampling artefact.

Taken together, these elements suggest that, despite its non-probabilistic and voluntary nature, the sample reasonably approximates the main demographic and socio-economic characteristics of the population most relevant to issues of energy and climate awareness. From a statistical perspective, all sample sizes exceed the commonly recommended minimum threshold of 200 observations for SEM modelling, ensuring adequate power and model stability64,68.

Social and economic disparities

Socioeconomic factors significantly influence individual perceptions and behaviours toward the energy transition in arid and semi-arid regions. The results confirm that age, education, employment status, income, housing type, and family responsibilities shape levels of environmental engagement. Younger generations show a stronger willingness to adopt energy-saving practices and participate in environmental initiatives, in line with international findings. For example, in Jakarta, Gen Z exhibits strong pro-environmental behaviour driven by perceived behavioural control, positioning youth as key facilitators of behavioural change69.

Generational differences also affect responses to transition initiatives: younger cohorts display greater readiness for sustainable practices70, whereas older adults often demonstrate limited involvement in community-based projects and transition programs71. describe this through the concept of energy ageism, referring to institutional mechanisms that limit older adults’ participation in energy processes. However, this pattern is not universal. European initiatives such as “Senior CRE Enthusiasts” illustrate that appropriate inclusive frameworks can foster meaningful participation among seniors72. These results suggest that intergenerational engagement depends not solely on age but also on social context, motivational factors, and opportunities for involvement.

Economic vulnerability also emerges as a major barrier. Low- and middle-income individuals and those experiencing job insecurity display reduced engagement in sustainable behaviours. Higher income enables stronger support for transition initiatives22 and investment in renewable technologies69, while financial constraints limit participation among disadvantaged groups71. Education follows a similar pattern: lower education levels correlate with less favourable transition behaviours, whereas higher education promotes awareness, facilitates the adoption of smart-energy solutions22 and strengthens adherence to sustainable practices73.

Housing type also affects behavioural outcomes. Residents of collective housing show lower propensity for behavioural change and reduced engagement in environmental initiatives. Certain housing configurations are more conducive to energy-saving measures69, and high-performing homes are associated with greater adoption of green technologies74. Additionally, although household heads carry both educational and financial responsibilities, they tend to exhibit limited engagement, suggesting that family obligations may constrain time and resources available for transition-related activities74.

Overall, these findings show that social disparities exert moderate yet significant influence on environmental engagement. Public policies should therefore integrate socioeconomic realities to ensure that transition frameworks promote both environmental sustainability and social equity.

Energy transition perceived by citizens: between personal interest, individual action, and collective commitment

Citizen perception oriented toward individual engagement emerges as a key determinant of awareness. The results shows that engagement in the energy transition is a multifaceted process encompassing personal interest, behavioural adjustments, financial commitment, and collective participation. Citizens’ desire to obtain information about national energy strategy reflects intrinsic motivation rooted in personal values, which significantly impacts energy policies success. Personal norms predict support for energy citizenship75, and intrinsically motivated individuals are more likely to participate actively in transformation initiatives76.

Citizens increasingly adopt eco-responsible behaviours, such as soft mobility, recycling practices, and household waste sorting at individual and community levels, illustrates an emerging transformation of lifestyles consistent with environmental principles. These behaviours demonstrate concrete forms of commitment, in which individual actions represent an essential lever of change.

Behavioural adjustments constitute a core pillar of energy citizenship, fostering energy efficiency and the adoption of renewable technologies77. According to78, energy transition requires strong ethical foundations based on individual behavioural change, emphasising energy sobriety and voluntary reductions in consumption as fundamental elements of ecological citizenship.

The results also indicate that citizens show pronounced willingness to invest financially in energy-related improvements, including photovoltaic panel installation and home energy performance rehabilitation. This tangible commitment signals a transition from passive support to active participation in transforming the energy system. Willingness to pay for renewable electricity reflects heightened environmental awareness and genuine commitment to sustainability goals79, while serving as a measurable indicator of social acceptance80.

Participation in awareness events, training programs, and community associations further reflects a growing readiness for collective engagement. These actions support collaborative learning, strengthen shared responsibility, and contribute to the construction of collective capacity for transition. Citizen-led initiatives foster social innovation, enhance awareness of environmental issues, and mobilise communities around energy concerns81. Participatory dynamics are therefore central in promoting active involvement in energy system transformation82.

Overall, these findings confirm that citizens’ behaviours play a decisive role in shaping their perceptions of energy and climate issues, forming an interdependent relationship between individual action and cognitive framing.

Conditioned citizen awareness: a multidimensional geometry

Citizen awareness emerges as a multidimensional construct shaped by information access, perceived benefits, behavioural experience, and contextual interpretation. Effective dissemination of information regarding national strategies and climate action is essential for fostering informed participation. Accessible and contextualized information, adapted to local realities and incorporating knowledge of sustainable technologies such as geothermal systems; enhances citizen understanding and engagement. Portugal’s experience shows that structured media campaigns and public-information initiatives effectively strengthen trust and support for renewable technologies83, with visible demonstration of tangible benefits further reinforce citizen engagement84.

Awareness also encompasses recognition of excessive energy use in daily environments and understanding of the cause–effect relationships driving climate change. This awareness is essential for promoting responsible behaviours and sustaining support for energy policies. The Netherlands demonstrates how increased environmental impacts awareness fosters proactive energy management and advocacy for sustainable solutions85.

Perceived benefits play a central role in shaping awareness. Recognition of the environmental and economic advantages of renewable energy contributes to more favourable attitudes and engagement. Perceived utility, cost savings, improved environmental quality, and greater energy security; significantly influences engagement behaviours, including information seeking and proactive management of consumption85. International evidence reinforces this: in Kenya, the belief that renewables reduce electricity costs increases public support14, whereas in Germany, acceptance depends on perceived benefits, risks, trust, and fairness86.

The results of this study indicate that awareness is strongly shaped by both behavioural engagement and perceptual framing. Public policies must therefore consider these multidimensional drivers when designing awareness and communication strategies for the energy transition.

Climate disparities and energy perceptions: towards a territorial understanding of awareness

Marked differences across the three climatic regions demonstrate that climatic context moderates awareness formation and shapes behavioural and perceptual pathways. In arid areas, awareness is influenced more strongly by observable behaviours than by perceptions, indicating a pragmatic, experience-driven process in which daily practices serve as key channels of understanding. Environmental constraints, such as heat stress, water scarcity, and energy dependency; make energy issues tangible and immediate. However, socioeconomic limitations restrict access to information and education, resulting in awareness built primarily from lived experience rather than structured cognitive processing.

In semi-arid areas, sustainable behaviours play a central role, moderately shaped by socioeconomic factors but directly influencing both awareness and perceptions. Behavioural engagement modifies how citizens interpret energy issues, suggesting that action precedes awareness by reinforcing understanding. Less severe climatic pressures mean that perception depends more on information, education, and media exposure. Structural factors and limited incentives may hinder the adoption of sustainable practices, producing partial engagement where behaviours alternate between voluntary action and inertia.

In semi-humid dry areas, perceptions serve as key mediators between behaviour and awareness. Strong correlations between behaviours and perceptions indicate that daily practices significantly shape understanding of energy issues. Nonetheless, behaviours often reflect voluntary, value-driven choices rather than responses to environmental constraints. Awareness is therefore formed primarily through mediated processes: education, media, and institutional communication; rather than direct climate experience. Limited visibility of climate impacts reduces urgency-driven behaviours, and awareness reflects constructed cognitive frameworks rather than experiential knowledge.

Conclusion

This study analysed the relationships among citizen awareness, perceptions, and individual behaviours related to the energy transition and climate change across Algeria’s diverse climatic zones, using a mixed-mode survey, GIS-based climatic classification, and multivariate statistical analyses. The results demonstrate that environmental awareness is context-dependent and multidimensional, emerging at the intersection of personal motivations, tangible practices, and collective engagement. Climatic differences shape distinct patterns of awareness formation: in arid zones, awareness is more strongly grounded in concrete behaviours, whereas in semi-humid regions, cognitive perceptions play a more prominent role. Socioeconomic factors further influence these dynamics, affecting behaviours in arid contexts and shaping perceptions in temperate zones. These findings advance understanding of how territorial specificities modulate behavioural and cognitive engagement with energy and climate issues.

The results carry important implications for public policy. An inclusive energy transition requires recognizing how social and economic conditions constrain or facilitate engagement. In arid regions, authorities should prioritize strategies that acknowledge and strengthen residents’ tangible efforts, through grants for energy-efficient equipment, visibility for community initiatives, and recognition mechanisms for eco-responsible actions. Linking energy issues to everyday realities (e.g., the relationship between efficiency and household comfort) may enhance perceived relevance. Training youth leaders and community facilitators as local ambassadors for climate and energy practice would support the long-term dissemination of knowledge.

In semi-humid dry regions, addressing social perceptions requires clear and accessible information on long-term climate trajectories and current energy-use patterns. Integrating climate and energy concepts into school and university curricula can reinforce cognitive understanding. Emphasizing measurable behavioural impacts, such as carbon-footprint visualisations may further strengthen individual awareness. Complementary cultural and narrative tools, including artistic and immersive approaches, can help communicate the complexity and urgency of environmental challenges.

In temperate areas, strategies used in arid and semi-humid zones should be combined to leverage their complementarity. Behavioural engagement (practical levers) and cognitive awareness (cognitive levers) reinforce one another and should be integrated in policy design. Authorities could develop programmes that simultaneously promote eco-responsible practices, via incentives, recognition schemes, and community initiatives, and strengthen awareness through formal and informal educational pathways. Linking theoretical knowledge with community-level experimentation, participatory workshops, and local demonstrations can help citizens understand the impacts of their decisions and identify actionable levers within their everyday environment. Such integrated approaches foster sustained engagement grounded in recognition, empowerment, and citizen ownership.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as the first national-level analysis of these dynamics in Algeria, the findings remain exploratory and cannot be directly benchmarked against comparable national data. Second, the sample, while substantial, was non-probabilistic and based on voluntary participation, with a predominance of younger and highly educated respondents; this may limit the statistical representativeness of the findings, although it reflects key demographic characteristics of the national population. Third, the mixed-mode survey approach, combining face-to-face interviews and online questionnaires, may have introduced mode-specific response biases, as participants’ engagement and reporting could differ across collection methods. Fourth, missing data were handled using listwise deletion, which, while ensuring analytical consistency, could reduce statistical power and potentially influence parameter estimates. Future research should aim to increase sample representativeness, explore alternative or stratified sampling strategies, and examine more granular behavioural factors, such as building energy performance or more mobility choices; to better understand the interplay between daily practices, ecological awareness, and territorial characteristics. Such investigations would deepen knowledge of how climatic diversity and socio-economic heterogeneity shape citizen engagement with the energy transition and climate action.
