Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy caused by Fabry disease: implications for surgical myectomy
Authors: Andrea Frustaci, Valentino Borghetti, Samuele Pentiricci, Romina Verardo, Rossella Scialla, Matteo Antonio Russo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy can be the phenotype of storage disorders as Fabry disease cardiomyopathy. In this instance, its recognition through GLA gene analysis and preventive administration of enzyme replacement therapy may reduce heart failure risk of surgical septal myectomy (SSM). A 59‐year‐old man was referred for SSM as dyspnoea and low threshold muscle fatigue associated to severe left ventricular outflow obstruction (gradient of 100 mmHg) due to both interventricular septal hypertrophy and mitral leaflet systolic anterior motion were not controlled by metoprolol 100 mg bid. Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm and a complete left bundle branch block. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed a preserved left ventricular (LV) contractility (ejection fraction 70%) but failed to reveal reduced T1 mapping and fibrosis of postero‐lateral LV wall suggesting Fabry disease cardiomyopathy. Cardiac catheterization and coronary angiography documented increased LV end‐diastolic pressure but normal coronary arteries. SSM was followed by acute renal and heart failure with left ventricular ejection fraction declining to 35%. Histology of SSM showed regularly arranged severely enlarged cardiomyocytes containing extensive vacuoles that were intensely positive to immunofluorescence with anti‐Gb3 antibodies and appeared at electron microscopy to consist of myelin bodies suggesting the diagnosis of FD. This entity was confirmed by low blood levels of alpha‐galactosidase A (0.8 nmol/mL/h; NV > 1), high values of Lyso‐Gb3 (5.85 nmol/L; NV < 2.3), and the presence of the pathogenic mutation c.644A>G in the exon 5 of GLA gene. This study emphasizes the importance of a genetic screening for FD before SSM be considered for hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy.Keywords: Fabry disease, Obstructive cardiomyopathy, Surgical myectomy

Introduction

Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) is an inherited disease characterized by increased left ventricular wall thickness that is not explained by abnormal loading conditions.

It occurs at a rate of 1:500 in the general adult population. An autosomal dominant pathogenic mutation in sarcomeric protein genes is recognized in about 35–60% of patients but non‐sarcomeric aetiologies contribute significantly. Among the latter, Fabry disease (FD) has been reported in 0.5–2.0% of patients with HOCM.

Surgical septal myectomy (SSM) is the primary treatment option for patients with HOCM drug‐refractory to the control of heart failure. Indeed, accuracy of intervention associated to a mortality rate approaching 0%, makes this indication the most sustainable even in comparison with more conservative solutions as alcohol septal ablation. Morpho‐molecular as well as enzymatic and genetic characterization of myocardial substrate is not always required for SSM. However, its knowledge, particularly for FD cardiomyopathy (FDCM), may influence surgical risk claiming for a pre‐surgical preparation with enzyme replacement therapy.

Case Report

A 59‐year‐old man was referred for HOCM surgical treatment. Previous clinical history was substantially meaningless since 6 months before, when a new onset dyspnoea and low threshold muscle fatigue led him to clinical investigations. Haemato‐chemical exams including blood levels of creatinine and eGFR were normal. Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm and a complete left bundle branch block. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a marked left ventricular hypertrophy with a severe left ventricular outflow obstruction (LVOTO, gradient of 100 mmHg) due to both interventricular septal hypertrophy and mitral leaflet systolic anterior motion. Medical treatment was started with metoprolol 100 mg administration twice a day. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (Figure 1 A,B and Video S1) showed left ventricular ejection fraction 70%, left ventricular end diastolic volume index 67 mL/m2, left ventricular end systolic volume index 20 mL/m2, left ventricular stroke volume index 47 mL/m2, left ventricular cardiac index 2.6 L/min/m2, and left ventricular cardiac mass index 132 g/m2. No reduction of T1 mapping nor elective late gadolinium enhancement in the inferior‐lateral segment of LV suggesting FD cardiomyopathy was observed.

(A, B) CMR showing severe left ventricular hypertrophy with LVOTO (see arrows). (C) LV septal myectomy. (D) Histology of myectomy sample showing regularly arranged cardiomyocytes containing large intracellular vacuoles. (E) Immunofluorescence with anti‐Gb3 ab showing myocyte vacuoles to consist of glycosphyngolipids (green colour) accumulation. (F) Electron microscopy of myectomy tissue showing the vacuoles consisting of myelin bodies suggesting the diagnosis of Fabry disease.

Coronary angiography showed normal coronary artery tree. As beta‐blockers therapy failed to improve clinical symptoms, surgical treatment was suggested in order to combine LVOTO enlargement and mitral valve competence restoration.

Surgery included septal myectomy, which was done beginning 2 mm below the nadir of the right coronary sinus towards the left fibrous trigone in a counter‐clockwise way, going deeply 6–8 mm into the septal muscle and extending it 30 mm down to the left ventricular apex (Figure 1 C ). Despite a relatively contained low heart ischaemia time (60 min), weaning from CPB was prolonged for almost 2 h because of an unexpected left ventricular dysfunction. Intraoperative transoesophageal echocardiogram showed low ejection fraction (35%) and moderate functional mitral valve regurgitation. The patient was extubated in postoperative day 1, but during the second day, he developed an acute kidney injury with a rapid creatinine and Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) increase. A continuous veno‐venous haemodialysis was then started and necessary until the fifth day ICU stay. Recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged at home in the 10th postoperative day. Pre‐discharge echocardiogram showed a LVOTO normalization and systolic anterior motion abolishment.

Histology of myectomy (Figure 1 D,E ) showed regularly arranged severely enlarged cardiomyocytes containing extensive vacuoles (Figure 1 D ) that were intensely positive to immunofluorescence with anti‐Gb3 antibodies (Figure 1 E ) and appeared at electron microscopy to consist of myelin bodies (Figure 1 F ) suggesting the diagnosis of Fabry disease. This entity was confirmed by low blood levels of alpha‐galactosidase A (0.8 nmol/mL/h; NV > 1), high values of Lyso‐Gb3 (5.85 nmol/L; NV < 2.3), and the presence of the pathogenic mutation c.644A>G in the exon 5 of GLA gene. No concomitant mutation of known genes causing a sarcomeric HCM was documented.

Discussion

Fabry disease 1 is a lysosomal storage disorder due to deficiency of the enzyme alpha‐galactosidase A causing accumulation of glycosphingolipids into cardiac cells promoting cardiac wall thickening. Although cardiac contractility can be preserved up to the advanced stage of the disease, cardiomyocytes are mostly occupied by glycolipids, which generate abnormal metabolic phenomena including oxidative stress with cell apoptosis 2 and autoimmune inflammatory reaction. 3 Isolated cardiac involvement by FD (cardiac variant) is a typical manifestation of c.644A>G mutation of GLA gene. However, in its advanced stage, CMR shows a characteristic reduction of T1 mapping and an increase of LGE in the posterior‐lateral LV wall that raises the suspicion of FDCM. These findings failed to be appreciated by CMR. A possible explanation is a concomitant mutation of a sarcomeric gene. 4 This combination is not exceedingly rare as has been observed in 0.4% in a very large cohort of patients with HCM. Indeed, we failed to document a concomitant mutation of genes known to be associated with HCM. Nevertheless, it is recognized that the culprit gene associated with a sarcomeric HCM is not identifiable in around 50% of cases.

Recombinant α‐galactosidase A replacement therapy is able after 20 weeks to clear microvascular endothelial deposits of globotriaosylceramide from the kidneys, the skin, and the heart improving their metabolism and function. 5 Specifically, the production of nitric oxide can be restored normalizing microvascular function. In our report, SSM undertaken in a patient with an unsuspected FDCM was followed by a prolonged phase of renal and heart failure. This complication, although not demonstrated so far, may have been prevented or attenuated by enzyme replacement therapy administration previously to cardiac intervention. In addition, FD diagnosis in a subject with HOCM activates a family screening, which may identify and treat new affected members. This study emphasizes the importance of a genetic screening for FD before SSM be considered for HOCM.
