Association between short-term exposure to ambient ozone concentration and influenza incidence in Kawasaki City, Japan: a time-stratified case-crossover analysis
Authors: Keita Wagatsuma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeasonal influenza continues to pose a significant global public health challenge owing to its considerable disease burden, making it imperative to elucidate its potential driving factors. Although the roles of meteorological factors such as temperature and absolute humidity have been extensively investigated, research quantifying the burden of ambient ozone (O₃) as an environmental determinant of influenza, particularly among vulnerable populations, remains limited. The aims of this study were to assess short-term effects of photochemical oxidants (Ox, parts per billion [ppb]) in a temperate Japanese city, quantify the associated attributable burden, and evaluate variations across subgroups to enhance understanding and identify high-risk populations.MethodsDaily time-series data on influenza cases, photochemical Ox levels, and meteorological factors were compiled for Kawasaki City, Japan, spanning 2015 to 2019. Using time-stratified case-crossover analyses and a distributed lag non-linear model, Ox–influenza associations were evaluated, accounting for long-term trends, seasonality, and day-of-the-week effects, and capturing both non-linear and delayed impacts. The overall attributable fractions (AFs) and those corresponding to low and high Ox levels were subsequently calculated. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted according to influenza subtype, sex, and age group.ResultsData of 163,120 influenza cases were analyzed. Overall, a U-shaped exposure–response curve was observed between Ox concentrations and influenza incidence. The overall minimum morbidity Ox (MMOx) level was 48.4 ppb (94.0%), with cumulative relative risks of 2.06 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.50, 2.82) and 1.47 (95% CI: 0.78, 1.51) observed at low and high Ox levels, respectively. The burden of influenza incidence attributable to non-optimal MMOx level was estimated at 36.6% (95% empirical CI: 17.1, 50.9), with the majority of this burden arising from low Ox concentrations. Furthermore, patients with influenza A exhibited higher risks and AFs than did patients with influenza B, although the differences by sex and age group were not statistically significant.ConclusionsOverall, non-optimal photochemical Ox levels are associated with a substantial burden of influenza morbidity. These findings may inform local public health initiatives by aiding the development of targeted preparedness and prevention strategies to mitigate short-term effects of air pollution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24755-0.

Background

Seasonal influenza, an acute respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses, poses a significant public health challenge [1]. According to the World Health Organization, seasonal epidemics result in approximately one billion infections and 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths annually worldwide, with substantial social and economic burdens [2]. Numerous epidemiological and laboratory studies have demonstrated that factors such as antigenic drift, host susceptibility, environmental conditions (notably temperature and absolute humidity), and social determinants (including human mobility and non-pharmaceutical interventions) contribute to the transmission dynamics of influenza [3–7]. For instance, low temperatures and reduced absolute humidity have been consistently associated with increased viral stability, prolonged airborne viability, and greater host susceptibility due to impaired mucosal defenses [1]. Multilocation studies conducted in Japan, China, and the United States have demonstrated that cold and dry environmental conditions significantly increase influenza incidence, particularly during winter seasons, underscoring the importance of considering their joint effects [8–10]. Furthermore, recent large-scale time-series studies from China have reported positive associations between influenza incidence and exposure to major ambient air pollutants—such as particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm (PM₂.₅), particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤ 10 μm (PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulphur dioxide (SO2), and carbon monoxide (CO)—highlighting the potential contribution of atmospheric environmental stressors to infection risk [11]. Among these pollutants, ambient ozone (O₃)—a secondary pollutant formed through photochemical reactions—has recently garnered particular attention as a potential driver of influenza transmission [11–13]. For instance, a recent time-series study from Hong Kong revealed a negative association between daily ambient O₃ levels and influenza transmissibility, that is, a time-varying effective reproduction number [14]. Meanwhile, studies from mainland China have reported U-shaped non-linear or even positive associations between O₃ and influenza, underscoring potential heterogeneity across climatic and geographic contexts [15]. Despite these findings, few studies have examined the association between ambient O₃ concentrations and influenza incidence within Japan’s temperate climatic context. Furthermore, research quantifying the influenza burden associated with ambient O₃ exposure remains limited.

The aim of this study was to investigate short-term effects of ambient O₃ on the risk of influenza infection in a Japanese city. Moreover, by conducting stratified analyses according to influenza type, sex, and age, this study was aimed at identifying vulnerable subpopulations and quantifying attributable burden by estimating attributable fraction (AF). The findings of this study are expected to provide valuable epidemiological insights that will inform future studies and guide influenza management strategies in the context of ongoing climate change.

Methods

Study site and period

Kawasaki City, located in the northeastern part of Kanagawa Prefecture, adjacent to Tokyo, Japan, is located in the western Pacific at approximately 35°N latitude and 139°E longitude [16]. As the seventh most populous city in Japan, Kawasaki experiences a temperate climate with four distinct seasons characterized by hot, humid summers from June to August and cold, dry winters from December to February.

Data collection

Influenza case data were obtained from the Kawasaki City Infectious Disease Surveillance System, which is administered by the Kawasaki City Institute for Public Health [17]. Under legal mandates, all medical institutions within the city must submit daily reports of influenza cases diagnosed using rapid diagnostic test kits. Epidemiological data regarding the total number of influenza cases recorded between 2015 and 2019 were collected. Additionally, data disaggregated by influenza type (influenza A and B), sex (male and female), and age group (younger [< 60 years] and older [≥ 60 years]) were obtained for subgroup analyses. This age categorization was selected to reflect the heightened vulnerability of older adults and is consistent with commonly used definitions in prior studies, as well as international public health frameworks [18]. However, information on age was not consistently reported across all records in the surveillance dataset, resulting in a proportion of cases (n = 43,551; 26.7%) with missing age data. For ambient O3 concentrations, the daily maximum 8-h moving averages of ambient photochemical air pollution indices—specifically, photochemical oxidant (Ox) concentrations expressed in parts per billion (ppb)—were obtained from the Atmospheric Environmental Regional Observation System provided by the Ministry of the Environment [19]. In Japan, given that O₃ constitutes the principal component of Ox, the Japanese Ministry of the Environment resolved in 1996 to equate O₃ concentrations with Ox concentrations [20]. Ox is defined as the collection of oxidizing substances produced predominantly via photochemical reactions and is measured using the potassium iodide method, a wet chemical technique that responds primarily to O3 (comprising at least 90% of the measured response), with peroxyacetyl nitrate, hydrogen peroxide, and organic hydroperoxides constituting the remainder [21]. Indeed, previous studies have also employed Ox as a surrogate for O₃ [22, 23]. Daily ambient meteorological data, including mean temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), wind speed (m/s), total rainfall (mm), and sunshine duration (h), were obtained from a weather station in central Kawasaki City during the study period [24], through the online data portal (https://www.data.jma.go.jp/gmd/risk/obsdl/index.php). Because all data were anonymized, aggregated, and obtained solely from publicly accessible sources, this study was exempted from ethical review.

Statistical analysis

Before constructing the statistical model, Spearman’s rank-order correlation analysis was conducted to preliminarily examine the relationships between influenza cases and environmental factors. We then applied a time-stratified case-crossover design combined with a generalized linear model using a conditional quasi-Poisson regression model was used to assess the association between photochemical Ox concentrations and influenza incidence in Kawasaki City, while adjusting for relevant covariates [25]. This design inherently controlled for seasonality and long-term trends by matching case and control days within the same year, month, and day of the week, an approach widely adopted in environmental epidemiology [25, 26]. Accordingly, no additional adjustment for time was required, in contrast to conventional time-series regression model. To accommodate both nonlinear and lagged effects, a distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) was employed using a cross-basis function for photochemical Ox concentrations and their associated lags [27]. Specifically, the exposure–response relationship was modelled with a natural cubic B-spline incorporating three internal knots positioned at the 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of Ox distribution. In parallel, the lag–response relationship was modelled using a natural cubic B-spline with three internal knots equally spaced on a logarithmic scale, with the lag period extended to 7 days, consistent with prior DLNM applications [11, 28]. To further mitigate potential confounding factors, the model was adjusted for additional meteorological variables by applying natural cubic B-splines (each with three degrees of freedom [df]) to mean temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, total rainfall, and sunshine duration. The selection of df for these meteorological variables was guided by prior environmental epidemiological studies, which adopted a similar parameterization in assessing the short-term effects of air pollution on influenza incidence [11, 28, 29]. Moreover, a binary indicator for public holidays was incorporated, along with an autoregressive term for the logarithm of the total number of influenza cases (with a one-day lag). Short-term effects were evaluated by estimating the overall lag‐cumulative relative risks (RRs) at the 2.5th (low) and 97.5th (high) percentiles, using the minimum morbidity Ox (MMOx)—defined as the percentile at which morbidity risk is minimized and commonly considered the optimal photochemical Ox level—as the reference.

Subsequently, the RR values derived from the DLNM were used to compute the fraction of influenza cases attributable to non-optimal Ox values (i.e., AF), as described in a previous time-series study [30]. The overall AF was determined as the sum of the contributions from all days, with Ox values deviating from the MMOx level. In addition, the components attributable to low and high Ox concentrations were computed separately by partitioning the associations corresponding to days with Ox values below or above the MMOx level, respectively. The 95% empirical confidence intervals (eCIs) were derived from 5,000 Monte Carlo simulations.

Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying data according to influenza type, sex, and age group [31]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results. Initially, placement of knots in the exposure–response relationship was modified from the 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles to the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. The lag–response relationship was then re-assessed by extending the lag period from 7 to 14 days. Rather than relying on model selection criteria, we followed an empirically informed approach grounded in practices in previous environmental epidemiological studies, thereby ensuring both model flexibility and comparability across analyses [8, 32]. All analyses were conducted with R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with packages “gnm,” “dlnm,” and “attrdl.”

Results

Descriptive analysis

Between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2019, a total of 163,120 influenza cases were reported in Kawasaki City, with the vast majority (73.3%) being attributable to influenza A (Table 1). Sex distribution was nearly balanced (51.4% male vs. 48.6% female); however, a substantial proportion of cases (64.9%) occurred in younger age groups. Over the study period, a daily mean number of 89.4 cases (standard deviation [SD]: 193.0) was recorded. Environmental exposure variables are summarized in Table 2. The daily mean concentration of photochemical Ox was 28.4 ppb (SD: 11.7), with values ranging from 4.3 to 67.2 ppb. Meteorological conditions also exhibited substantial variability: mean temperature averaged 16.8 °C (SD: 7.6; range: 0.7–31.6 °C), relative humidity averaged 68.5% (SD: 15.8; range: 28.0–99.0%), and wind speed was 3.4 m/s (SD: 1.2; range: 1.3–9.0 m/s). Precipitation and sunshine duration showed highly skewed distributions, with median (interquartile range) values of 0.0 mm (0.0–2.0 mm) and 6.2 h (1.1–9.4 h), respectively, reflecting pronounced seasonal trends. Influenza incidence exhibited marked seasonality, peaking in winter (January) and reaching a nadir in summer (June–August) (Fig. 1), whereas the monthly mean concentration of Ox peaked in spring (May) and declined to its lowest level in December. Indeed, a negative linear correlation (Spearman’s ρ = − 0.12, p-value < 0.05) was observed between the total number of influenza cases and Ox levels (Fig. 2).

Association between influenza incidence and Ox concentrations

Figure 3 illustrates the overall lag–cumulative association curves between influenza incidence and photochemical Ox concentrations for the entire population and various subgroups. For the overall population, the exposure–response relationship displayed a nonlinear, U-shaped pattern (Fig. 3A), with a statistically significant increase in influenza risk observed at low Ox levels (2.5th percentile: RR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.50–2.82) compared with the MMOx reference. In contrast, no significant association was observed at high Ox levels (97.5th percentile: RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.78–1.51). Subgroup analyses revealed exposure–response curves that closely mirrored those of the overall population. At low Ox levels, influenza A exhibited a greater risk (RR: 2.44, 95% CI: 1.55–3.83) than influenza B (RR: 1.16, 95% CI: 0.87–1.53) (Fig. 3B, Table S1), while no significant differences were observed at high Ox level. Moreover, the risk curves stratified by sex and age group were broadly comparable, with overlapping confidence intervals (Fig. 3C and D, Table S1).

Influenza incidence attributable to Ox concentrations

Table 3 shows the AF of influenza incidence ascribable to photochemical Ox across the overall population and defined subgroups. The overall AF due to Ox was estimated at 36.6% (95% eCI: 17.1–50.9), with the predominant burden arising from exposure levels at or below the MMOx threshold. This overarching pattern was consistently observed across the subgroups. The overall AF for influenza A was greater at 45.1% (95% eCI: 16.4–63.9) than that of influenza B at 26.3% (95% eCI: 11.8–38.1), indicating a relatively greater burden for influenza A. Gender-specific evaluations yielded an overall AF of 39.8% (95% eCI: 21.1–53.7) among males and 33.7% (95% eCI: 7.77–51.7) among females. Furthermore, age‐stratified analyses indicated an AF of 46.5% (95% eCI: 17.6–65.3) among younger individuals and 42.5% (95% eCI: − 177.0–88.0) among older individuals.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the overall shape of the association remained largely stable, even when adjustments were made to the placement of knots and the lag period, thereby attesting to the robustness of the findings (Figures S1 and S2).

Discussion

This study was conducted using a time-series analysis to quantify the attributable burden of influenza incidence due to photochemical Ox in Kawasaki City, Japan, with assessments stratified by influenza type, sex, and age group. The findings of this study indicate that increasing concentrations of Ox are negatively associated with the risk of being infected with influenza virus. In general, population‐level studies on the relationship between ambient O₃ and influenza have been limited, and the findings reported to date have been inconsistent. Two epidemiological studies—one from Hong Kong and one from Brisbane—have reported a positive association between O₃ and influenza. However, in the Hong Kong study, the relationship between O₃ and influenza was not directly examined; rather, influenza and pneumonia were aggregated into a single outcome that was associated with ambient O₃ [33]. Conversely, the Brisbane study, in which revealed a positive association was observed between O₃ and pediatric influenza, potential temporal confounding was not rigorously controlled for in its time‐series analysis, thereby raising questions regarding the robustness of its findings [34]. Moreover, a recent time‐series analysis using Hong Kong surveillance data up to 2013 revealed an inverse association between ambient O₃ and influenza transmissibility, and a subsequent study based on United States data corroborated the inhibitory effect of O₃ on influenza activity [12, 14]. The present study, which is based on data from Japan, provides further support for these more recent findings. Furthermore, recent investigations have employed DLNMs to explore the interactive effects of air pollutants on influenza and other infectious diseases. For instance, a study conducted in China identified significant synergistic interactions between PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ in modulating influenza risk [35]. Additionally, a modelling study also demonstrated that the combination of a low air quality index and elevated levels of PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ was associated with increased risk of haemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome [36]. Although the underlying mechanisms linking short-term O3 exposure to reduced influenza transmissibility remain incompletely understood, several plausible biological pathways have been proposed. Ambient O₃ has demonstrated virucidal activity in vitro, with studies reporting inactivation of influenza virus within hours of exposure [37]. More plausibly, O₃ may prime antiviral host defences through immunomodulatory effects. Inhalation of O₃ has been shown to enhance pulmonary innate immune responses—particularly among susceptible individuals—potentially via secondary messengers such as interleukin-33 (IL-33) [14]. IL-33 activation has been implicated in both allergic inflammation and protective CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses, suggesting a role in strengthening host immunity against influenza infection [38].

The subgroup analysis in the present study revealed several consistencies and discrepancies relative to those in previous studies. Notably, the association between O3 exposure and influenza incidence appeared to differ by virus subtype, with a stronger association observed for influenza A than for influenza B. This observation is consistent with the finding of an epidemiological study conducted in Sichuan, China, which revealed that influenza A, as opposed to influenza B, was associated with a heightened risk of air pollution exposure [39]. Although a definitive scientific consensus regarding the underlying mechanisms remains elusive, it is plausible that the greater genetic diversity of influenza A viruses—coupled with their propensity for antigenic reassortment and drift, as well as distinct receptor-binding specificities—may account for this divergence [39]. The evolutionary dynamics of influenza A may render it more vulnerable to the adverse effects of air pollutants [40]. By contrast, stratified analyses by sex and age revealed no significant differences in the O3–influenza association, a pattern broadly consistent with findings from multicenter studies in China [41]. Notwithstanding, this study showed a relationship between increased risk of O₃ exposure among individuals aged 0–17 years and influenza infection, presumably owing to the relative immaturity of the immune system in children [41]. Collectively, these findings underscore the complex interplay between O₃ exposure and influenza risk, and suggest that viral subtype—and to a lesser extent, sex and age—may modulate this association. Further research is warranted to clarify the biological pathways underpinning these observations and to determine the extent to which demographic and virological factors modify the health impacts of O₃ exposure.

AF represents the proportion of diseases that can be ascribed to a specific exposure and has significant implications for decision-making in disease management and public health interventions. The findings of this study indicate that 36.6% of influenza cases in Kawasaki City are attributable to photochemical Ox, suggesting that ambient air pollution may constitute a critical environmental stressor with a substantial public health impact. Comparisons with previous studies on the proportion of influenza cases attributable to O3 are challenging because of the limited number of such investigations. However, discernible differences emerge when contrasting the results of this study with those of existing studies from China. For instance, a study conducted in ten cities in southern China—a subtropical region—reported an AF of − 4.46% (95% eCI: −15.58 − 4.32) for influenza cases associated with O₃, albeit with considerable uncertainty [41]. Conversely, a modelling study from Shijiazhuang, China, revealed that the AF of influenza cases due to O3 exposure was 22.56% (95% eCI: 10.11, 32.50), suggesting a substantial burden associated with lower concentrations. This finding is consistent with the results of the present study [42]. The discrepancies in AF estimates between these studies may be attributable to fundamental differences in statistical modelling approaches, variations in the number of regions studied, and heterogeneity in socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. Further epidemiological research is warranted to elucidate the actual burden of ozone-related influenza.

As far as one can ascertain, this is the first epidemiological investigation conducted in Kawasaki, a Japanese city with a temperate climate, to examine the complex association between photochemical Ox concentrations and influenza incidence over five years using detailed daily time-series data that include influenza type, sex, and age group. By employing a robust methodological framework incorporating a time-stratified case-crossover design, long-term trends, seasonal fluctuations, and day-of-the-week effects were effectively controlled for, thereby minimizing potential confounding influences. Moreover, by quantifying the AF of influenza cases due to non-optimal photochemical Ox, this study provides novel insights into the health impact of air pollutants on influenza incidence, thereby contributing to the currently limited evidence in this field. However, some limitations of this study warrant further investigation. First, the ecological nature of the study implies that the results are based on population-level data, which preclude the establishment of causality in individual cases. Second, the study was confined to a single Japanese city in a temperate zone, thereby restricting the generalizability of the findings to other regions or countries. The results of this study warrant further research using data from multiple locations to answer the questions addressed. Third, a substantial proportion of cases (26.7%) were missing information on age, which may have introduced a degree of selection bias in the age-stratified analyses. In particular, the wide eCI observed for the elderly subgroup (≥ 60 years) suggests considerable uncertainty in the AF estimate, likely reflecting the relatively small number of cases in this age group. Fourth, due to data limitations, we were unable to account for key epidemiological factors such as influenza vaccination coverage and viral strain variations. For instance, although vaccination rates in Japan are relatively high—approximately 80% among school-age children and 50% among older adults—residual confounding cannot be ruled out [43]. Further studies incorporating such data are warranted. Although subgroup analyses were restricted to complete cases to minimize potential bias, the possibility of selection bias due to missing data. Finally, while major meteorological factors were incorporated as confounding factors in our models, their short-term effects on influenza incidence were not evaluated. Moreover, other ambient air pollutants—such as PM₂.₅, PM10, NO2, SO2, and CO—were not assessed in this analysis. As the study was designed to examine the association between photochemical Ox and influenza incidence, the roles of these additional environmental exposures remain outside the scope of this study. Future studies should consider a more comprehensive analysis that assesses the independent and combined influences of meteorological and air pollution factors on influenza transmission.

Conclusions

This time-series study revealed that a substantial proportion of influenza cases in Kawasaki City are attributable to non-optimal photochemical Ox concentrations. These findings indicate that public health authorities should re-examine current intervention strategies and incorporate ambient air pollutant considerations into the management of influenza transmission in Japan. While this study focused on photochemical Ox as a proxy for ambient O3, it also highlights the need to consider a wider range of air pollutants that may influence respiratory infection risk. Future studies should adopt integrated approaches that account for multiple pollutants to assess potential interactive effects on influenza and related outcomes. Expanding such analyses across multicounty and multicity datasets would improve generalizability and support the development of evidence-based public health policies targeting pollution-related infectious disease burden.
