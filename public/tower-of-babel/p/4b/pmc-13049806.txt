Construction and comparison of preeclampsia prediction models using logistic regression and machine learning
Authors: Jia Zhang, Hanbing Jia, Tingting Zhang, Huanfang Feng, Xiaoxuan Zhu, Cuijuan Cao, Chunjing Shi, Yingjie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePreeclampsia is a major cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. Early identification of women at high risk is essential for timely prevention and improved outcomes.MethodsIn this study, clinical data as well as ultrasound examination results from 404 pregnant women, among whom 45 suffered and 359 did not suffer from preeclampsia, were retrospectively analyzed. Independent predictors were found via multivariable logistic regression. With these predictors, four classification models involving logistic regression, support vector machine, random forests, and extreme gradient boosting are developed in this paper. The performance of all these four classification models is assessed via metrics such as area under receiver operating characteristic curves, calibration plots, and decision curves.ResultsMaternal age of 35 years or more, assisted reproductive technology, history of eclampsia, history of fetal growth restriction, and two placental blood flow indices - flow index and vascular flow index, were independent predictors. In the internal validations, logistic regression was characterized by the most favorable calibrated method, based on 10-fold out-of-fold prediction, with an intercept of − 0.325, slope of 0.811, and Brier of 0.090, suggesting its suitability as a probability-based predictive tool, while random forest produced the highest AUC of 0.781.ConclusionsLogistic regression appears most suitable for early screening as a probability-based risk tool due to its favorable calibration and interpretability, pending external validation and recalibration. These models quantify statistical associations between routinely collected predictors and preeclampsia in this retrospective cohort; they do not imply causality and require external validation before prospective clinical use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03412-5.

Introduction

Preeclampsia is considered one of the more serious pregnancy-related complications, and its prevalence worldwide is around 1%-8%. Additionally, its prevalence in Chinese women is reportedly around 2%-5% [1–3]. Moreover, preeclampsia not only creates a significant threat to maternal health but also indicates its association with unfavorable perinatal outcomes, such as fetal growth restriction, preterm birth, and perinatal mortality [4, 5]. Thus, it is one of the important subjects studied for predicting preeclampsia.

Traditional preeclampsia prediction relies on clinical evaluation of the risk factor, but most models show low sensitivity and specificity [6, 7]. However, in recent years, with the progress of maternal-fetal medicine, there has been an obvious trend toward the development of predictive models involving multi-dimensional indicators in preeclampsia prediction models [8, 9]. This type of predictive model mainly includes maternal clinical indicators, blood pressure, pulsatility index in the uterine artery, and biochemical indicators such as angiogenic factors (such as the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio) [8, 10, 11]. With regard to the modeling approach, the most often employed method in clinical studies is the logistic method because of its clear meaning and simple operation, but models such as random forests and XGBoost are more effective in dealing with nonlinear and highly dimensional data structures [12].

However, these studies have some limitations as most of the prediction model literature available to date was developed using single-center data and was not externally validated on a large, multicenter data set; hence, their generalizability is unknown [13, 14]. Moreover, few studies have compared prediction models built using different algorithms, and their practical value and cost-effectiveness have not been assessed, which is a significant impediment to translating these good quality prediction models to clinical practice [15–17].

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically construct preeclampsia prediction models based on logistic regression and machine learning, to conduct rigorous comparisons of their predictive performance and clinical value, and to provide a reliable, interpretable, and clinically applicable tool for the standardized early prediction and risk stratification of preeclampsia.

In addition to the widely used first-trimester combined screening algorithms, there is still a need for realistic prediction methods that can be used under diverse and sometimes resource-constrained conditions. Accordingly, the purpose of our investigation was to derive logistic regression and machine learning algorithms using commonly used clinical and ultrasound-based parameters and assess not only the prediction value of the derived prediction schemes but, importantly, their calibration and clinical utility to inform early risk prediction, aiming to facilitate these prediction methods instead of, and not to supersede, full combined screening programs.

Method

Study design, setting, and participants

We carried out a single-center retrospective cohort study in Hebei Maternal and Child Health Hospital. We screened successive pregnant women who delivered between June 2023 and December 2024. We included 404 pregnant women with complete outcome data and available pre-specified predictors in the analysis. Of them, 45 were admitted due to preeclampsia (11.1%), and 359 were not.

This study comprised two methodological components: (i) descriptive comparisons between women who did and did not develop preeclampsia, and (ii) development and internal validation of prediction models using a prespecified predictor set.

Outcome definition

Previously, preeclampsia was described as the onset of hypertension at or after 20 weeks or more of gestation, with systolic values of ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic values of ≥ 90 mmHg combined with proteinuria of ≥ 300 mg protein per 24 h or the presence of end-organ dysfunction, including a platelet count of < 100,000/µL, a rise in liver enzymes more than twice the upper normal level, or a measure of creat [18–22].

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were: maternal age ≥ 20 years and availability of the required clinical and ultrasound-derived predictors.

Exclusion criteria were: major concomitant pregnancy complications, hematologic or immunologic disorders, severe infection or malignancy, and psychiatric disorders that could impair cooperation with examinations.

Timing and definition of predictors

Maternal characteristics, including age group (X3), mode of conception (X1), and history of preeclampsia/eclampsia or fetal growth restriction (X2), were extracted from the electronic medical record at the first antenatal booking visit. Ultrasound-derived parameters—including crown–rump length (CRL) and placental 3D power Doppler indices (FI and VFI)—were obtained during the routine first-trimester scan (11 + 0 to 13 + 6 gestational weeks). Therefore, all predictors were available before 14 weeks’ gestation, well before the clinical diagnosis of preeclampsia (≥ 20 weeks).

Gestational age determination. Gestational age at the first-trimester visit was primarily dated by CRL according to standard first-trimester dating practice; last menstrual period was used as a reference and reconciled with ultrasound dating per institutional protocol when discordant.

Ultrasound examination and Doppler acquisition protocol

All ultrasound procedures were carried out using a GE Voluson E10 ultrasound system, which is fitted with a 1–6 MHz convex transducer and a 3.5-6 MHz abdominal volume transducer. The version of the ultrasound system software was consistent during the data collection period. For the 3D power Doppler ultrasound scan, a standard institutional preset protocol was followed for all the individuals. Some of the parameters were set consistently for better comparability. The individuals were scanned in the supine position with appropriate bladder distension in accordance with routine practice.

CRL measurement. CRL was measured at the first-trimester ultrasound visit (11+ 0 to 13+ 6 weeks) using standardized true mid-sagittal plane criteria.

Uterine artery Doppler. Using the 1–6 MHz convex transducer, the probe was positioned bilaterally at the uterine cervico-isthmic region to identify the uterine arteries. Pulsed-wave Doppler was recorded after obtaining a stable high-resistance waveform. The sample gate was set at 2 mm and the insonation angle was maintained at < 30°. The system automatically calculated the S/D ratio, pulsatility index (PI), and resistance index (RI).

Placental 3D power Doppler indices (VI, FI, VFI). Under 2D guidance, placental location was identified, placental thickness was measured, and the umbilical cord insertion site was documented. Three-dimensional power Doppler was activated and the longest-axis placental plane was selected. The sweep angle was set to 80°. During volume acquisition, the probe was kept stable and participants were instructed to hold their breath to minimize motion artifacts. The acquired volume dataset was stored on the workstation. Placental boundaries were manually traced using VOCAL software to generate a 3D power Doppler histogram, from which vascularization index (VI), flow index (FI), and vascularization–flow index (VFI) were automatically computed. Volume acquisition was repeated three times and averaged for analysis.

Operators and reproducibility. All scans and measurements were performed and interpreted by two obstetric sonographers with > 5 years of experience. Because this was a retrospective study, formal intra- and inter-observer reproducibility statistics (e.g., intraclass correlation coefficients for VI/FI/VFI) were not prospectively collected; this is acknowledged as a limitation and will be addressed in future prospective multicenter studies with standardized imaging protocols and reproducibility testing.

Preeclampsia subtypes

Early-onset preeclampsia was defined as diagnosis before 34 + 0 weeks’ gestation and late-onset preeclampsia as diagnosis at or after 34 + 0 weeks. In this retrospective dataset, gestational age at diagnosis was not consistently available for all cases; therefore, subtype-specific analyses were not performed and this was explicitly noted as a limitation.

Missing data

Of 406 eligible women, 2 were excluded due to missing outcome information, leaving 404 for complete-case analysis. Missingness for the final modeling predictors was minimal; therefore, no imputation was performed.

Statistical analysis and model evaluation

Analyses were performed in R (v4.3.2) using the e1071, caret, randomForest, and xgboost packages (package versions reported in the Supplement). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Normality and variance assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests.

Model development. The prespecified predictor set consisted of conception method (X1), history of preeclampsia/eclampsia or FGR (X2), age group (X3), and placental blood-flow indices (FI and VFI). Using the same fixed predictor set, we developed four models: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine with radial basis function kernel (SVM), random forest (RF), and XGBoost.

Internal validation. The performance of the model was checked using 10-fold cross-validation, wherein the main strategy was to use the out-of-fold (OOF) predicted probabilities. In this strategy, the continuous predictors (FI and VFI) were standardized based on parameters estimated from the training data folds on each of the data folds. The hyperparameter tuning of the machine learning algorithms was run on the training data to avoid data leakage.

Events per variable and overfitting risks. Number of preeclampsia events was recorded as 45, and the final model included five predictors in the logistic regression analysis; hence, the EPV value was found to be nearly 9 (45/5). Although we recognize that EPV: Below 10 may potentially raise overfitting and coefficient stability problems with positive performance bias, internal validation and calculation of performance based on OOF predictions were conducted. We recognize the potential optimism in our study and suggest the usage of shrinkage methods.

Performance metrics. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed using calibration plots and quantitative measures including calibration intercept (ideal 0), calibration slope (ideal 1), and Brier score (lower is better). Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis and clinical impact curves. All tests were two-sided and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bootstrap optimism correction. To further quantify optimism due to the limited number of events, we performed bootstrap internal validation for the final logistic regression model (1,000 resamples) and reported optimism-corrected AUC, calibration intercept/slope, and Brier score (Table S2).

Machine learning specifications and tuning

All models used the same fixed predictor set (X1, X2, X3, FI, VFI). FI and VFI were standardized within the training folds.

SVM. An RBF-kernel SVM was used. The penalty parameter C and kernel width γ were tuned within the training folds. Predicted probabilities were obtained using Platt scaling.

RF. RF models were trained as ensembles of bootstrapped decision trees. Key hyperparameters (e.g., ntree, mtry, and minimum node size) were tuned within training folds.

XGBoost. XGBoost used a binary logistic objective. Tuned parameters included nrounds, max_depth, eta, subsample, and colsample_bytree (and regularization parameters when applicable). The hyperparameter search space is provided in Table S1.

Causal interpretation. As a retrospective observational study, the models quantify associations for risk stratification and do not imply causal effects.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 406 eligible participants, 2 were excluded because of missing data in at least one predictor or the outcome, leaving 404 participants for the complete-case analysis. In the present study, a total of 404 pregnant women were enrolled, of whom 45 (11.1%) developed preeclampsia and 359 (88.9%) did not. As presented in Table 1, women in the preeclampsia group had a significantly higher age compared to the subjects in the nonpreeclampsia group (median age 34.00 vs. 31.00 years, P = 0.004). The proportion of women aged ≥ 35 years was also higher in the preeclampsia group compared to controls (42% vs. 24%, P = 0.011). Utilization of ART was more frequently recorded among women with preeclampsia (71% vs. 45%, P < 0.001). No significant differences were seen in parity, gestational age, or crown-rump length between the two groups (all P > 0.05).

A history of eclampsia or fetal growth restriction (FGR) was more common in the preeclampsia group than in the non-preeclampsia group (6.7% vs. 0.6%, P = 0.011). Biochemical markers, including homocysteine, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and placental growth factor, did not differ significantly between groups (all P > 0.05). Placental volume was significantly lower in the preeclampsia group (44.48 cm³ vs. 53.55 cm³, P = 0.012). In addition, placental blood-flow indices—vascular index (VI), flow index (FI), and vascular flow index (VFI)—were significantly reduced in women with preeclampsia compared with controls (P = 0.004, P < 0.001, and P < 0.001, respectively). The incidences of FGR (22% vs. 3.1%, P < 0.001) and preterm birth (20% vs. 3.9%, P < 0.001) were also significantly higher in the preeclampsia group.

Logistic regression analysis of risk factors for preeclampsia

Categorical variables were coded as shown in Table 2, and variables with statistical significance in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable Logistic regression model. Given the strong correlation between VI and VFI (r = 0.728), VFI was retained in the model to avoid multicollinearity and enhance model stability.

Multivariable analysis identified five independent predictors of preeclampsia (Table 3). A history of eclampsia or fetal growth restriction was associated with markedly higher odds of preeclampsia (OR = 25.280, 95%CI:3.221 to 198.438). However, this estimate was imprecise because the exposure was rare (only 5 women overall: 2 in the non-preeclampsia group and 3 in the preeclampsia group), resulting in a wide confidence interval. Use of ART was also associated with a significantly increased risk (OR = 3.262, 95% CI: 1.577–6.748, P = 0.001). Age ≥ 35 years was an independent risk factor as well (OR = 2.217, 95% CI: 1.101–4.465, P = 0.026), suggesting that older women had about 2.2 times the risk of younger women.

In contrast, placental blood-flow parameters FI (OR = 0.936, 95% CI: 0.893–0.980, P = 0.005) and VFI (OR = 0.780, 95% CI: 0.637–0.955, P = 0.016) were protective factors; higher FI and VFI values were associated with a lower risk of preeclampsia.

Construction of the nomogram and prediction models

The five independent predictors of mode of conception, history of eclampsia/FGR, age group, FI, and VFI were included in a Logistic regression model to create a nomogram for individualized prediction of preeclampsia risk (Fig. 1). Each predictor of preeclampsia was assigned a number of points on the nomogram. Combining all points gave us a total score that corresponds to an estimate of risk for preeclampsia. It was found that of all predictors of preeclampsia risk, history of eclampsia/FGR had the maximum number of points assigned to it, followed by FI.

Based on the same set of predictors, three machine learning models—support vector machine (SVM), random forest, and XGBoost—were additionally developed to allow comparison with the traditional Logistic regression model.

Discriminative performance and calibration of prediction models

Performance of four predictive models, namely Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and XGBoost (XGB), was tested using 10-fold out-of-fold cross-validation with the Area Under the Curve (AUC) metric.

In 10-fold out-of-fold evaluation, Random Forest achieved the highest discrimination in this cohort (AUC = 0.781). XGBoost followed with an AUC of 0.764. Logistic regression showed moderate discrimination (AUC = 0.748) and comparatively better calibration than the machine-learning models. Support vector machine yielded the lowest AUC (0.646), indicating weaker classification performance.In conclusion, Random Forest showed the highest classification ability, while SVM underperformed compared to the other models (Fig. 2).

The Table 4 presents the Intercept, Slope, and Brier score for each predictive model (Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest, and XGBoost) based on 10-fold out-of-fold (OOF) predictions.

The intercept values also show overall prediction bias. For example, Logistic Regression had a negative intercept value of -0.325, indicating systematic underestimation of predicted risk. Meanwhile, the intercept value of XGBoost was found to be slightly positive (0.146), indicating that it slightly overestimates its prediction probabilities.

The slope represents the calibration level of the different models. Therefore, the closer the slope is to 1, the greater the calibrationAmong the models, logistic regression showed relatively favorable calibration (slope = 0.811), while XGBoost slightly overfitted the predictions (slope = 1.075), and random forest exhibited substantial miscalibration (slope = 0.425).

Brier score measures the accuracy of probabilistic predictions. The Logistic Regression model achieved the lowest Brier score (0.090), indicating comparatively better probabilistic accuracy among the models.

In bootstrap internal validation of the final logistic regression model (1,000 resamples), the apparent AUC was 0.781 with an optimism of 0.026, yielding an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.755. The optimism-corrected calibration intercept and slope were − 0.395 and 0.784, respectively (Table S2), consistent with the cross-validated out-of-fold estimates.

Sensitivity and specificity across thresholds

The ROC plots are for sensitivity vs. 1-specificity at various threshold probabilities for the four predictive models: Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest, and XGBoost; each based on 10-fold out-of-fold predictions (Figure 3).

Logistic Regression showed a strong curve fit in sensitivity and specificity values; that is, demonstrating a typical trade-off between sensitivity and specificity across thresholds. The sensitivity values remained higher at lower threshold values but then became lower as the threshold increased. The specificity values increased as the threshold increased.

The SVM-RBF trend was similar to that of Logistic Regression, though with a lower sensitivity and specificity across threshold values, indicating limited discriminative ability and non-preeclampsia using this model.

The Random Forest plot also showed a smoother curve of change from sensitivity to specificity, meaning the model was more balanced. But at higher thresholds, the sensitivity was low, whereas the specificity increased significantly.

Similarly, XGBoost followed the same pattern as the Random Forest model with a smoother curve. In XGBoost, the sensitivity was moderate at lower thresholds, and it decreased substantially as the threshold was increased. The specificity increased along with a decrease in the sensitivity.

These results underscore the balance between sensitivity and specificity while choosing the thresholds. In the models used for the classification, it appears there are different balances of performance. This emphasizes the importance of choosing the appropriate thresholds according to the specific clinical scenario, with sensitivity (true positive) or specificity (true negative) results prioritized based on the preferred outcomes of the predictions of the risk of preeclampsia.

Clinical Impact Curves (CIC)

In the Figure, we can see the Clinical Impact Curves (CIC) of all four models, which include Logistic Regression, SVM (RBF), Random Forest, and XGBoost using 10-fold out-of-fold predictions. As we can see, the number of high-risk patients and true positives per 1000 patients is represented by the blue and red lines, respectively, over different threshold probabilities (Fig. 4).

The use of Logistic Regression showed a considerable decline in the volume of High-Risk Patients as the threshold was increased, and the number of true positives varied accordingly across thresholds. This showed that as the thresholds are lowered, more High-Risk Patients are being identified, but proportionately more True Positives are being identified as well.

SVM (RBF) exhibited a similar pattern, but with a slower decline in the number of high-risk patients and true positives compared to Logistic Regression, suggesting that the SVM model has a less aggressive classification of patients across thresholds.

This was shown by a steep fall of high-risk patients at higher thresholds, with relatively limited changes in true positives across moderate threshold ranges across all threshold values. The above findings show how the Random Forest model is more conservative in identifying high-risk patients.

XGBoost showed a similar trend to Random Forest, with a significant drop in the high-risk patient numbers at higher thresholds, but the true positives remained higher at lower thresholds, indicating that XGBoost is also selective in predicting the risk.

These curves show the trade-off between finding patients who are considered high-risk and true positive results as the threshold increases. These curves thus illustrate the significance of selecting an appropriate threshold in clinical scenarios to optimize the identification of patients and provide accurate diagnostic results. All three models demonstrate different trade-offs; for instance, Logistic Regression focuses on being sensitive to the lower thresholds, whereas Random Forest and XGBoost models are cautious in identifying patients as high-risk patients.

Decision Curve Analysis (DCA)

Figure 5 shows the Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) results for the four predictive models (Logistic Regression, SVM (RBF), Random Forest, and XGBoost) on the basis of out-of-fold predictions using the 10-fold cross-validation scheme. DCA assesses the net benefit of different predictive models over different threshold probabilities, comparing their performance to two extremes: “Treat all” and “Treat none.”

Logistic Regression (red line) and Random Forest (green dashed line) have similar net benefits across threshold probabilities, with a slight advantage over the “Treat none” strategy. The net benefit is reasonably stable across thresholds, suggesting that both models provide a solid net benefit when applied to different probability thresholds.

SVM (RBF) (blue dashed line) shows lower net benefit, particularly higher net benefit levels, meaning the model does not particularly offer much value to what has been achieved in the extreme strategies of treating all or treating none.

The performance of XGBoost, as represented by the blue dotted line, was similar to Logistic Regression and Random Forest in terms of the net benefit values, which exceeded those of the “Treat none” approach at lower values of the threshold probability. But the net benefit of XGBoost declined at high thresholds, making it even less favorable.

The above results demonstrate the usefulness of each model by analyzing how each model balances the benefits found by detecting true positives and the cost incurred by false positives. The Logistic Regression model and Random Forest model performed better than the others by attaining higher net benefits across a large range of threshold values.

Discussion

In this study, we derived four different models to predict preeclampsia, including the use of logistic regression, support vector machines with radial basis kernels, random forest, and XGBoost. Notably, the models presented quantitate associations between predictor variables typically collected during patient care and preeclampsia; they do not demonstrate causality or predict future risk. Additionally, the study drew on 10-fold cross-validation with aggregated predictions to ensure the accuracy of the model.

Several predictors identified in our cohort (advanced maternal age, prior preeclampsia/FGR, and assisted reproductive technology) are consistent with established epidemiological evidence. The main contribution of our study is not the discovery of novel risk factors, but the systematic construction and head-to-head comparison of prediction models under a pragmatic variable set, with transparent reporting of discrimination, calibration, decision-analytic utility, and internal validation. Such locally validated models may facilitate standardized early risk stratification while larger multicenter studies are being established to refine coefficients and explore additional predictors.

With respect to internal validity, the model that performed best in discrimination was random forest (AUC = 0.781). Next was XGBoost (AUC = 0.764), then logistic regression (AUC = 0.748). Conversely, the worst model in discrimination by AUC was svm (0.646). The other concern we wanted to examine after discrimination capability is calibration. The model that performed better in calibration using quantitative metrics was logistic regression (-0.325 intercept; 0.811 slope; Brier score = 0.090). Furthermore, we found that random forest performed poorly in calibration (0.425 slope; 0.101 Brier score). This emphasizes that model selection should take into account the utility of each model.

From an implementation perspective, logistic regression may be preferable as an initial, lightweight decision-support tool because it is transparent, easier to audit, and provided the most favorable calibration in our internal validation. This is consistent with evidence that machine learning approaches do not consistently outperform logistic regression in clinical prediction tasks, particularly when sample size or event counts are limited [23]. Nevertheless, machine learning models may still be useful as complementary tools for refining risk estimates in selected subgroups, provided that external validation and recalibration are performed [15, 20].

Clinical application scenarios (non-causal, implementation-focused)

Scenario 1: Risk stratification within the first trimester to facilitate more intense surveillance. The risk factors (maternal age, conception method, and history, along with blood flow indices) can be assessed within a routine 11–13 + 6 week scan. The results can be used to sift through group A and group B to discuss the intensity of surveillance, particularly for pregnancies within group B, through more intense surveillance or preventive measures based on regional practices.

Scenario 2: Risk-informed follow-up and referral pathways. In tiered maternity-care systems, probability-based stratification could support structured triage. Patients with lower predicted probabilities could continue routine antenatal care, whereas those with higher predicted probabilities could be prioritized for more frequent blood-pressure and laboratory surveillance, repeat ultrasound assessment, or referral to higher-level centers, aiming to allocate specialist resources more efficiently.

Scenario 3: Incorporation into clinical information systems. With its transparency and positive calibration, logistic regression could be embedded in a local electronic medical record or ultrasound reporting system as a decision-support component. If used clinically, predicted probabilities would be matched with prespecified thresholds and standard actions, and periodic recalibration and performance monitoring would be performed to ensure safe general application across populations.

Comparison with previous studies

Scenario 3: Incorporation into clinical information systems. With its transparency and positive calibration, logistic regression could be embedded in a local electronic medical record or ultrasound reporting system as a decision-support component. If used clinically, predicted probabilities would be matched with prespecified thresholds and standard actions, and periodic recalibration and performance monitoring would be performed to ensure safe general application across populations. The use of combined data from the four forms of ultrasound increased the AUC from 0.68 to 0.88 when used alone, as demonstrated in a study. The accuracy of the model was also improved from 77.6% to 84.2%, and AUC from 0.71 to 0.86 in another study when the cardiac and IVC ultrasound images were used collectively. These methodological differences and validation approaches arguably contribute to the heterogeneity between findings, such as “case-mix and base risk,” “gestation age at which the measurements were made,” “selection and standardization of Doppler/3D power Doppler indices,” and “estimation of performance using an independent test set, nested cross-validation, or out-of-fold prediction methodologies.” In our study, although random forest and XGBoost classifiers had modestly higher discriminative performances, logistic regression had better calibration and favorable decision-analytic value, a consideration of immense importance, especially when interpreting risk information and thresholds. However, we want to emphasize that these modeling results reveal only “association” and not “causal” effects.

Interpretation of model choice

Although machine-learning models showed slightly higher discrimination, logistic regression provided more transparent risk estimation and more stable calibration in our data, which may better support risk communication and threshold-based decision support. Importantly, the models describe statistical associations rather than causal effects; any clinical deployment should be framed as decision support and accompanied by ongoing monitoring and recalibration.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the number of preeclampsia events was relatively small (n = 45). With five predictors in the final logistic regression model, the events-per-variable (EPV) was approximately 9, which may increase the risk of overfitting, unstable coefficient estimates (especially for low-prevalence predictors), and overly optimistic performance. Although we used 10-fold cross-validation with pooled out-of-fold predictions and report internally validated performance rather than apparent estimates, residual optimism may remain in small-event settings, and cross-validation alone may not fully prevent overfitting for flexible machine-learning algorithms. In addition, history of preeclampsia/eclampsia or fetal growth restriction was rare (5 women overall), resulting in wide confidence intervals and potential sparse-data bias; this predictor should therefore be interpreted cautiously. To further address optimism, we additionally performed bootstrap internal validation (1,000 resamples) for the final logistic regression model and reported optimism-corrected performance; nevertheless, external validation in larger multicenter cohorts remains necessary before routine implementation.

Second, predictor selection was performed external to the cross-validation procedure, and this may create information leakage and hence increase the model estimates. Although the same predictor selection set was used across all the models to enable comparability across models, future research should seek to adopt nested cross-validation and shrink coefficients using ridge/LASSO methods.

Third, this was a single-center study with internal validation only. Differences in case-mix, ultrasound equipment and acquisition protocols (particularly for 3D power Doppler indices such as FI and VFI), operator training, and clinical pathways may lead to performance drift in other settings. Therefore, external validation in independent multicenter cohorts—followed by recalibration/model updating and prospective workflow evaluation—is necessary before routine clinical deployment.

Our models did not incorporate laboratory parameters. This approach was by design, to make the models as feasible and cost-effective as possible, using readily available clinical and ultrasound-based predictors. Laboratory variables, however, may enhance the biological validity of our models and, possibly, their forecasting potential. Future efforts will take a phased approach, first to externally validate our existing parsimonious model and recalibrate it, then to assess the value of potential laboratory predictors through a set of predetermined methods and decision analytic approaches to assess improvements in calibration, discrimination, and overall net benefit for the added cost.

Finally, generalizability is limited by the single-center design and the modest number of preeclampsia events. Differences in case-mix, measurement protocols, and clinical pathways may lead to performance drift in other settings. Accordingly, external validation with recalibration in larger multicenter cohorts, ideally with standardized ultrasound acquisition protocols and prospective implementation evaluation, is required before routine clinical deployment.
