Speech therapy and parenting for early socio-communicative skills in young children with neurodevelopmental disabilities (SPEAK): study protocol for an Italian multicentre randomised controlled trial
Authors: Chiara Porro, Niccolò Butti, Daniela Sacchi, Roberta Rigamonti, Annalisa Miccoli, Rosario Montirosso, Sandra Strazzer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeurodevelopmental disabilities (NDs) affect early communication, social engagement and parent-child interactions. Children with NDs often struggle with prelinguistic skills, such as maintaining eye contact and using gestures, which are essential for language development. Parenting behaviour plays a key role in fostering these abilities, and early interventions involving parents can enhance socio-communicative and linguistic outcomes. The present multicentred randomised controlled trial evaluates the effectiveness of a parent-involved speech therapy intervention in improving early socio-communicative skills in children with NDs.Methods and analysisSixty children with NDs aged 6 months to 5 years will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group (IG, n=30), receiving a parent-involved speech therapy intervention focused on socio-communicative skills, or the control group (CG, n=30), receiving care-as-usual speech therapy. Participants will be recruited from eight rehabilitation centres in Lombardy, Italy. Both interventions will last 8 weeks, with weekly 45 min sessions led by trained speech therapists. Primary outcomes include early communication and intersubjective skills and parenting behaviour, assessed at baseline (T0) and post-intervention (T2—2 months from enrolment). Socio-cognitive skills and socio-emotional development will be considered as secondary outcomes. Feasibility and potential confounders (eg, parental stress) will be measured and controlled between groups.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received formal approval by the deputed ethical committee for all eight participant centres (Comitato Etico Lombardia 2 – Protocol n. R1916/24 – L2-109). Results of the main trial and of the exploratory aims will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and international conferences.Trial registration numberNCT06666777.Keywords: Developmental neurology & neurodisability, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Disabled Persons, Parents, Social Cognition

Introduction

Early prelinguistic communicative behaviours, including vocalisations, eye contact, smiling, following sounds and gestures, lay the foundation for linguistic and social development in the first months and years of life.1 2 From birth, infants use these non-intentional communicative signals to establish emotional and interactive connections with the surrounding environment and specifically to coordinate their behaviour with caregivers. This shared dyadic coordination, which has long been described in infant research as primary intersubjectivity, is crucial to promote a basic form of interpersonal understanding.3 4 Subsequently, in the second half of the first year of life, the emergence of joint attention episodes is considered the main hallmark of secondary intersubjectivity, which includes initial forms of intentional communication.5 6 At this stage, infants become aware of their ability to direct others' attention and begin using more targeted gestures, such as pointing to an object, to communicate purposefully.7 Despite variations in terminology and conceptual frameworks, it is well established that contingent parenting behaviours play a crucial role in social, communicative and language development.8 9 Timely and appropriate parental responses, such as recognising and responding to an infant’s vocalisations or gestures, reinforce and attribute meaning to these early communicative signals, thereby strengthening intentional communication and social understanding.

Every year, millions of children worldwide are diagnosed with neurodevelopmental disabilities (NDs), a definition that encompasses a wide range of disorders affecting the development of the nervous system, genetic syndromes, congenital brain malformations and perinatal brain damage such as infantile cerebral palsy.10 NDs usually impact multiple developmental domains, including motor, attentional and emotional functioning.11 Due to these impairments, many children with NDs face significant challenges in early socio-communicative skills, such as gaze orientation, maintaining eye contact, appropriate smiling, vocalisation and gestures, which in turn hinder social and language development. For instance, speech disorders were found as prevalent among individuals with complex NDs.12 In many genetic syndromes, language is often absent or composed of few words,13 14 and even when verbal language is present, these children frequently struggle with pragmatics and socio-cognitive features of communication.15 16 In children with cerebral palsy, early communication abilities have been found to mediate the relationship between motor impairments and later social functioning, highlighting the importance of early screening and interventions that target early socio-communicative skills.17

The presence of NDs not only directly affects linguistic and social development but also influences the quality of parent-child interactions, thereby impacting the emergence of primary and secondary intersubjectivity.18 Early communication difficulties and poor emotional regulation disrupt everyday parent-infant exchanges, as a child’s unclear or unpredictable signals can hinder parental contingent and appropriate responses, creating a vicious cycle that limits developmental learning opportunities.4 19 Additionally, life-threatening complications and the need for complex medical care can further strain parental psychological well-being, with cascade effects on their caregiving skills.20 21 In this light, involving parents in rehabilitative interventions may help them cope with these difficulties, provide suitable stimulations for their children and ultimately support the child’s social and communicative development.22 23

Despite their variability in methods and outcomes, a growing body of research has demonstrated the effectiveness of parent-implemented communication interventions.24 25 However, most of these interventions consist of parent training sessions focused on linguistic development while neglecting pre-linguistic socio-communicative skills that find their foundation in early parent-child interactions.26 Parent-implemented programmes have been shown to enhance parents’ use of strategies that support children’s language development, but findings on their effects on positive parenting behaviour during parent-child interactions remain inconsistent and require further investigation.27,29 Moreover, these interventions often target specific populations, such as children with specific language impairments,30 late-talkers,31 preterm infants31 32 and those with autism spectrum disorder.33 34 Less is known about the effectiveness of early interventions that actively involve parents and specifically target socio-communicative and intersubjective skills in children with NDs.

One of the main challenges is the limited availability of tools for assessing these skills in children with NDs. One of the most widely used tools for assessing early communication and language development is the MacArthur-Bates Communicative Development Inventory (CDI).35 Although the CDI is considered a reliable outcome measure for early interventions,29 36 it was designed to focus on typical language development, with less consideration of primary and secondary intersubjectivity. Moreover, this and the other few available tools rely on parent reports or use structured procedures,5 37 offering limited flexibility and adaptability to rehabilitative settings and the complex needs of individuals with NDs.38 For instance, children with NDs may present severe motor impairments that hinder the use of typical communicative gestures. Rather, they often show idiosyncratic behaviours that, through the repetition of contingent responses to these actions, may become communicative acts.39 To address these issues, Strazzer et al. developed the Observation of Prelinguistic Intersubjective and Socio-Communicative Skills checklist (OPISCoS), an observational tool designed for use in rehabilitative settings. Beyond assessment, the OPISCoS can serve as a clinical guide, helping speech therapists support parents in recognising and responding to early communicative signals from their children. A structured implementation of the OPISCoS could improve social and communication outcomes of children with NDs, while also fostering developmental parenting behaviours.40 41 The current multicentre, randomised controlled trial aims to test these hypotheses by comparing a short-term speech therapy intervention focused on early socio-communicative skills and involving parents with a standard, care-as-usual speech therapy intervention in children with NDs aged 6 months to 5 years.

Aims

Primary aim and hypothesis

Using a pre/post study design, the primary aim is to test the feasibility and efficacy of the speech therapy and parenting for early socio-communicative skills (SPEAK) intervention in improving social and communication outcomes. We hypothesise that children assigned to the intervention group (IG) will show greater improvements in early socio-communicative skills assessed by the OPISCoS and the CDI, compared with those in the control group (CG), who will receive standard speech therapy (care as usual).

Secondary aim and hypothesis

A secondary objective of the study will evaluate whether the SPEAK intervention, which will actively involve parents, will lead to improvements in how parents interact with their children. Key parenting domains, considered as pivotal for supporting child development, will be assessed before and after the interventions. We hypothesise that parents assigned to the IG will demonstrate more positive parenting behaviours after the intervention compared with those in the CG. Moreover, we expect that only in the IG will changes in parenting behaviour be associated with improvements in child socio-communicative skills.

Exploratory aims

Additional exploratory aims of this study include (a) verifying whether the SPEAK intervention may also result in improvements in children’s socio-cognitive skills and socio-emotional development; (b) investigating associations between parenting behaviour and other outcome measures at T0 across groups; and (c) assessing the psychometric properties of the OPISCoS tool, including convergent and discriminant validity, through comparisons with standardised tests that evaluate communication and social development.

Methods

Study design

The study employs a multicentre, randomised, parallel-arm, active-controlled trial design. Participants will be assigned to either the IG, which receives a speech therapy intervention focused on early socio-communicative skills and actively involves parents, or the CG, which receives standard speech therapy (care as usual) without additional parent-directed components. Randomisation will follow a stratified permuted block randomisation procedure.42 The study adopts a prospective superiority design, as greater improvements in both primary and secondary outcome measures are expected in the IG compared with the CG. This independent trial is sponsored by IRCCS Eugenio Medea, which is the scientific institute of the ‘Associazione La Nostra Famiglia’. The sponsor is responsible for all aspects of trial implementation in accordance with ICH E6 Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study is supported by institutional research funds allocated annually by the Italian Ministry of Health (Ricerca Corrente). The Ministry has no role in the study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation of data or manuscript preparation.

Study population

Sixty children with NDs and their parents will be consecutively recruited at each participating facility according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Children will be eligible for the study if they meet the following criteria: (1) age between 6 months (corrected for prematurity) and 5 years; (2) developmental delay and/or socio-communicative difficulties documented by clinical assessments or standardised developmental scales (eg, Griffiths Developmental Scales - III); (3) mental age of at least 6 months; and (4) a diagnosis of ND, including genetic syndromes affecting neurodevelopment, cerebral palsy or congenital, non-progressive neurological disorders even if not yet attributed to a specific syndromic framework. The presence of severe hearing (ie, deafness) and visual (ie, blindness) impairments will be considered as exclusion criteria, as well as a diagnosis of ND due to neurodegenerative diseases, outcomes of brain tumours or traumatic brain injury. It is worth noting that both the experimental and control interventions are delivered alongside routine clinical care, allowing children to continue receiving all necessary support. For this reason, previous or concurrent participation in other rehabilitative treatments (eg, psychomotor therapy, psychological support for parents) will not be considered as an exclusion criterion. Nonetheless, this and other clinical and socio-demographic information (eg, family income) will be collected through medical records or specific questionnaires and considered as potential confounding variables. While this approach may introduce potential bias in the observed effect sizes, it ensures ethical appropriateness and ecological validity by reflecting the typical context of neurorehabilitation services. To minimise variability within the sample, parents must also meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) be older than 18 years; (2) have good proficiency in Italian, even if it is not their native language; and (3) not manifest psychiatric conditions or intellectual disability. Since the study involves both assessment and intervention with parents, the same parent (mother or father) must attend all sessions. Only children with an available parent meeting these criteria will be eligible and randomly assigned to either study group.

Patients and public involvement

Patients and their parents have not been involved in the design of this study. However, patient associations will be involved in dissemination efforts and outreach activities (see Ethics and dissemination section below).

Procedures

Enrolment and allocation

Participants will be recruited from eight rehabilitation centres located in Lombardy, a middle-high income region of northern Italy. Specifically, the following centres of the network ‘Associazione La Nostra Famiglia’ will be involved in the study: Bosisio Parini (LC), Lecco (LC), Ponte Lambro (CO), Como (CO), Carate Brianza (MB), Cislago (VA), Vedano Olona (VA) and Sesto San Giovanni (MI). These centres follow similar clinical populations and adopt a multiprofessional, family-centred care approach to rehabilitation.43 At each facility, the responsible child neuropsychiatrist will identify eligible patients and preliminarily verify parental willingness to participate. Identified participants will then be assigned to either the IG (n=30) or CG (n=30) group according to a stratified permuted block randomisation procedure. Stratification factors are represented by age and the most recently available developmental quotient (DQ) assessed through standardised scales (eg, Griffiths-III, Bayley-3 scales). Specifically, infants and toddlers (age range: 6–35 months) will be considered separately from preschoolers (36–59 months), as well as children with severe/moderate developmental delay (DQ<55 or non-assessable) who will be assigned to different blocks than those with mild/borderline developmental delay (DQ≥55). Within each block, participants will be randomly assigned to one of the two study arms following a predetermined permuted sequence. Each block aims to enrol 16 participants to achieve and overcome the target sample size. The stratified blocks and permutation sequences are reported in table 1.

It is worth noting that only the research staff will have access to the allocation sequences and will be responsible for assigning each enrolled participant to either the IG or CG. Allocation is concealed through the use of sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes. Clinicians, therapists and administrative staff involved in participant enrolment at the eight rehabilitation centres will remain blinded to upcoming allocations and will only become aware of group assignment once the intervention begins. In addition, all individual data are pseudonymised, with assigned codes linking participant information to group allocation (please see the Data management, monitoring and protection section for further details). This procedure prevents identification of the sequence and minimises risks of selection or allocation bias. Finally, as recruitment is conducted in parallel across eight centres, the likelihood of anyone deducing the allocation order is further reduced.

Assessment sessions

Assessments of primary and secondary outcome measures will be conducted before (T0) and after (T2) the interventions. In detail, a speech therapist will evaluate early socio-communicative skills by means of the OPISCoS (see the Outcomes section for more details). This assessment will be approximately conducted over two sessions in the same or in two consecutive weeks, although the number and duration of sessions may be adjusted to meet each participant’s features (eg, attention and collaboration). The same speech therapist will also conduct the interventions. In a single session lasting about 1 hour, a research-assistant psychologist will administer the Early Socio-Cognitive Battery (ESB; see Outcomes section for more details) and the parent-report questionnaires evaluating language and socio-emotional development. Moreover, ten minutes of free-play interaction between child and parent will be videorecorded and coded off-line through the Parenting Interactions with Children: Checklist of Observations Linked to Outcomes (PICCOLO). Only at T0 will socio-demographic and clinical information be collected by medical chart review and parent-report questionnaires. Also, parent-report questionnaires will be used to assess parental emotional state and child’s emotional-behavioural functioning.

Intervention focused on socio-communicative skills and involving parents (IG arm)

The SPEAK intervention will consist of eight 45 min sessions conducted over a 2-month period (weekly frequency). Each session will actively involve the same parent (either the mother or father). Based on each child’s profile, assessed through the OPISCoS tool, targeted stimulations will be provided to enhance parent-child interaction. Parents will receive guidance on observing and supporting their child’s intersubjective and socio-communicative skills in daily activities. These stimulations will take place in various settings, such as free play, snack time or semi-structured activities. For instance, therapists may use toy sets to stimulate and elicit dyadic and triadic attention (eg, sounding toys, bubbles, balls), or promote lexical (eg, small world toys, picture books) and syntactical (eg, daily-use objects like toy cutlery) comprehension. Topics and contents addressed during the SPEAK intervention

Rather than following a standardised sequence, settings and activities will be individualised according to each participant’s strengths and weaknesses identified through the OPISCoS. For example, for younger children or those with more severe impairments, primary intervention targets may include maintaining dyadic attention, expressing simple communicative acts such as smiling and facial mimicry, and acquiring first comprehension skills. In such cases, face-to-face interactions and daily routine activities with parents (eg, snack time) may be prioritised. Parents in the IG arm will receive a bespoke designed booklet containing examples of activities to support socio-communicative development at home. Care-as-usual speech therapy intervention (CG arm)

Participants assigned to the CG arm will receive a standard speech therapy intervention of the same duration as the experimental intervention (8 weekly 45-min sessions). Based on the child’s linguistic and communicative abilities, traditional speech therapy targets (eg, speech sounds and vocabulary) will be addressed. Early socio-communicative skills will not be the primary focus, although they may be indirectly stimulated through standard activities (eg, play-based tasks stimulating dyadic or triadic attention). As typically occurs in standard rehabilitation settings, at the end of each session, parents may receive general guidance on supporting therapy targets at home; however, sessions will not involve parent-child interactions unless strictly necessary.

Training and intervention monitoring

Speech therapists from each participating facility attended a 3-h training session on the administration and rehabilitative use of the OPISCoS in October 2024. A second 2 hour training, focused on study procedures and intervention protocols, was delivered in January 2025. Once the interventions begin, monthly meetings will be held between speech therapists at the rehabilitation centres and the research team to ensure consistency in procedures and to address any challenges related to recruitment, assessment or intervention delivery. Moreover, for both the IG and CG, speech therapists will keep an intervention diary to document session topics, therapy targets and any other relevant observations.

Outcomes

Primary outcome measures

Socio-communicative development and parenting behaviour will be considered as primary outcomes. They will be assessed before the interventions (T0) and at the conclusion of the interventions (T2), which will approximately occur 2 months after the first session.

The primary outcome measures have been previously validated and are routinely used in clinical and research settings with children with NDs. The OPISCoS is the only tool not yet formally validated. The assessment of its psychometric properties represents, indeed, an exploratory aim of the present study.

Early socio-communicative and intersubjective skills

The OPISCoS form will be administered by speech therapists to assess pre-linguistic intersubjective and socio-communicative skills.40 Compared with the published version, the form has been modified to exclude items from the vocal production domain, as these can be thoroughly assessed using other standardised tools. The adopted version consists of 31 items, divided into two domains: pragmatics and communication, evaluating interaction and communicative acts, communicative intentionality, attention and socio-conversational skills; and decoding, including first signals of comprehension and recognising. Each item is rated on a 3-point Likert scale: 0=absent, 1=partially present, 2=present. Scores from the two domains are summed to a total score, which serves as the primary outcome measure, with higher scores indicating better early socio-communicative skills. It is important to note that the OPISCoS was originally designed for children aged 0–36 months. However, it was not designed as a standardised assessment providing age-equivalent levels of competence; rather, it is intended to guide rehabilitation and monitor improvements over time.40 Therefore, it can also be applied to older children with NDs, who may continue to show difficulties in one or more domains.

Communication and language development

The CDI short form45 will be completed by parents to obtain an index of child communication and language development. This tool captures both expressive and receptive language skills by asking parents to report the words their child understands and produces, as well as the child’s use of gestures and other communicative behaviours. There are three different versions of the CDI-SF: one for infants from 6 to 18 months, one for toddlers from 18 to 30 months and one for children older than 30 months. Higher scores on the inventory indicate better communication and language developmental outcomes. The CDI has shown good validity and reliability for many different languages and cultures,46 including Italian.47 48

Parenting behaviour

A ten-minute parent-child interaction will be recorded at T0 and T2 and coded off-line by trained research assistants using the PICCOLO observational checklist.49 50 The PICCOLO assesses developmentally supportive parental behaviour across four domains: affection, responsiveness, encouragement, and teaching. Domain scores are aggregated into a total score, with higher scores indicating more positive parenting behaviours. The PICCOLO was designed to be used with parents of children with NDs19 51 and was found to be a reliable outcome measure of short-term parenting support interventions.44 52 The Italian adaptation of the PICCOLO has been shown to be a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating parent–child interaction.50

Secondary outcome measures

Socio-cognitive and socio-emotional development will be considered as secondary outcome measures, which will be assessed at T0 and T2. All secondary outcome measures have been previously used and validated in clinical populations with NDs, including Italian settings. Assessors are trained and supervised, and any difficulties encountered during administration will be documented to ensure consistency and reliability of the data.

Early socio-cognitive skills

The ESB will be administered to assess socio-cognitive skills, specifically social responsivity, joint attention and symbolic comprehension.53 Raw scores will be converted into standardised scores according to normative tables, with higher scores indicating better socio-cognitive skills. The ESB can be administered to children older than 23 months. The ESB has been recently translated and validated in an Italian context.54

Socio-emotional development

Socio-emotional development will be evaluated through the parent-report questionnaire of the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development—Third Edition.55 Raw scores will be converted into standardised scores according to normative tables, with higher scores indicating better socio-emotional development outcomes. The Bayley Scales represent a widely used gold-standard assessment of developmental functioning in the first years of life, particularly in at-risk populations.56

Feasibility outcome measures

Feasibility will be assessed by tracking the number of dropouts, namely the number of participants who fail to complete the eight-session intervention, and the number of sessions attended per child. The intervention diary kept by speech therapists will document any participation or compliance issues. Sessions missed for justified reasons (eg, illness) will be rescheduled within a week whenever possible.

Acceptability and perceived efficacy measures

At T2, parents from both the IG and CG will complete a short questionnaire to provide subjective evaluations of the intervention’s acceptability and perceived efficacy. The questionnaire, adapted from previous intervention studies,57 58 will assess recommendation level and satisfaction, as well as parents’ perception of their child’s required effort and the difficulties encountered in initiating and maintaining the intervention.

Other pre-specified measures

With the exception of the form collecting socio-demographic and clinical information, all pre-specified measures are validated and reliable tools that are routinely applied in clinical practice and research within the participating facilities.

Socio-demographic data and clinical information

At T0, socio-demographic information (eg, parental age, presence of siblings, familial socio-economic status) will be collected by means of a bespoke questionnaire. Clinical data will be collected by research assistants through chart review of medical records. These may include diagnosis, comorbidities, participation in other rehabilitative interventions and results of previous developmental and psychopathological assessments.

Child behaviour

At T0, the parent-report Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) 1½−559 60 will be administered to assess the presence of emotional-behavioural problems (eg, anxiety and sleep problems) in children older than 17 months. Raw scores will be converted into T-scores according to normative tables, with higher scores indicating greater problems.

Parental emotional state

At T0, the designated parent attending all sessions will compile standardised questionnaires assessing their emotional state. Specifically, the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI),61 the parent-report State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-Y),62 and the Parenting Stress Index (PSI)63 64 will measure, respectively, depressive symptoms, state and trait anxiety, and parenting stress. Higher scores on all measures indicate greater distress. Blinding

The psychologists administering the ESB battery and the questionnaires will be blind to group assignment. Additionally, parent-child interactions will be coded using the PICCOLO by research assistants who will be blind to the study’s aims and procedures. Due to the nature of the assessments and interventions, the therapists administering the OPISCoS cannot be blind. However, approximately 15% of the OPISCoS assessments will be videorecorded and coded off-line by the speech therapists who designed this tool, who will be blind to group allocation. This process will ensure consistency in its administration across therapists from different rehabilitation centres and help identify any potential biases. The informed consent form (online supplemental material 2) reports that one of the interventions foresees parental involvement, whereas the other does not. As a result, parents will be aware of the treatment allocation. For this reason, unblinding procedures are not foreseen within the study protocol, as no circumstances are expected in which unblinding would be required or applicable.

Statistics

Sample size estimation

Despite the great variability of caregiver-implemented interventions24 and their predominant focus on language development,66 previous studies have documented large effect sizes for short-term interventions on both child development30 and parental outcomes.44 Based on this evidence, we estimated the target sample size assuming a large effect size (η²p=0.14), a statistical power analysis of 80% and a mixed-model ANOVA as the primary outcome analysis, with group as a between-subject factor and time (T0, T2) as a within-subject variable. This way, a total of 54 participants was estimated. Considering a dropout risk of around 10%, the study aims to enrol 60 participants assigned either to the IG or CG arms. This sample size is consistent with and even larger than the sample sizes reported in a recent review of parent-implemented communication intervention.24 It is worth noting that in the stratified randomisation procedure, we considered 16 participants per block to balance the number of participants per group within each block.

Plan of data analysis

Data entry will be performed by research assistants who will remain blind to group allocation. To ensure the accuracy and quality of the data, the principal investigator (PI) will oversee the process, cross-checking the entries and validating the data as needed. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise the baseline characteristics of the participants. Continuous variables will be presented as means with SD or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on the distribution of the data. Categorical variables will be described using frequencies and percentages. To assess differences in baseline characteristics between the IG and CG arms, appropriate statistical tests will be employed. Independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests will be used for continuous variables, depending on their distribution, while χ2 tests will be applied to categorical variables. Any significant baseline differences between groups, such as demographic or clinical variables, will be controlled for in the analyses using appropriate statistical methods like ANCOVA models.

Changes in primary outcome measures from baseline (T0) to post-intervention (T2) will be analysed using mixed-model ANOVAs. These models will include group (IG vs CG) as the between-subject variable, time (T0 and T2) as the within-subject variable. For each primary outcome measure, the change between T0 and T2 (intervention effect delta) will be calculated. To test the hypothesis that changes in early socio-communicative skills may result from improvements in parenting behaviours, moderation models will be performed inserting the delta of the OPISCoS and CDI scores as dependent variables, the delta of the PICCOLO total score as the predictor and the group as moderator. Specifically, we expect that the intervention effect on parenting behaviour will be significantly associated with the intervention effect on early socio-communicative skills only in the IG.

The same analysis plan will be applied to secondary outcome measures. However, given that socio-cognitive and socio-emotional skills are not specifically targeted by the SPEAK intervention and that the ESB will be administered only to participants older than 23 months, these analyses will be considered as exploratory. For the second exploratory aim, Pearson’s bivariate correlations will be used to examine associations between parental behaviour, child social and communication development, child behaviour and parental emotional state. These exploratory analyses will be conducted across groups, considering only baseline evaluation results (T0). Pearson’s bivariate correlations will also be used to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the OPISCoS in comparisons with other measures of social and communication development (ie, CDI-SF, ESB and Bayley-III). The false discovery rate procedure by Benjamini and Hochberg will be used to account for multiple comparison bias.67

Missing data will be addressed using a modified intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, meaning that all participants who have completed both baseline and final assessments will be included in the analyses, regardless of whether they attended all of the intervention sessions. Due to the relatively small sample size and limited number of time points, no imputation of missing data will be performed. However, sensitivity analyses may be conducted to assess the impact of missing data on the study’s results.

Data management, monitoring and protection

All data, particularly personal and health-related information, will be handled in compliance with the EU Regulation 2016/679 (GDPR) and the Italian Legislative Decree 10 August 2018, No. 101. The data will be pseudonymised and stored in a secure Data Management System (RedCap), which is already in use at IRCCS Medea. This system will ensure the confidentiality and integrity of the data by implementing user-level access restrictions, real-time data validation and encryption techniques. Data will be entered in digital format through a dedicated electronic Case Report Form (eCRF), with only authorised personnel having access to the data.

Personal information will be stored separately from identifying details, ensuring that participants cannot be identified without additional information, which will be securely stored. The data will be stored for 25 years after the study’s completion in a protected, password-secured database. Participants will have the right to enquire about the stored data and, on request, receive information on the results of the study that pertain to them.

The privacy and security of data will be safeguarded throughout the study. Only authorised researchers and staff will have access to the data, and they will sign confidentiality agreements to ensure no information is disclosed to third parties. A data monitoring committee is not foreseen for this study. However, collected data may also be accessed by regulatory authorities to verify compliance with data protection requirements during the conduct of the study.

Ethics and dissemination

Publication and dissemination plan

Results of this study will be submitted to peer-reviewed, international journals and will be presented at international and national scientific conferences within the fields of developmental psychology and paediatric rehabilitation. Additionally, activities will be promoted to create support guidelines aimed at specific groups, such as parents' associations, to better support parenting and promote social-communication development in children with NDs.

Generally accepted criteria related to the actual and documented involvement of researchers in data planning, collection, analysis and/or drafting will be used to determine authorship. The use of professional writers is not planned.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee Lombardia 2 in Milan, Italy, on the 24th of July 2024 (protocol number L2 - 109). This approval was valid for all eight participating centres. Study procedures will be conducted in accordance with the approved protocol and the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study follows the SPIRIT guidelines for non-pharmacological interventions (see online supplemental material for the SPIRIT checklist). Before starting any procedure, informed consent will be obtained from all legal guardians of children participating in the study (see online supplemental material 2 for the participant consent form). Any modifications deemed necessary during the course of the study will be promptly reported to the Ethics Committee that approved the current protocol. If required, a formal amendment will be submitted for review and approval before implementation.

No risks are anticipated for the study population. The assessments and procedures implemented represent an integration of activities that are already routinely carried out in the clinical practice of the participating rehabilitation centres. Nevertheless, any unexpected events that occur during the study will be documented in the intervention diary and promptly reported to the research staff, who will in turn notify the Ethics Committee.
