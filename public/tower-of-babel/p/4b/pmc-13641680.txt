Genetic and genomic insights across the steatotic liver disease spectrum
Authors: Jake P. Mann, Karn Wijarnpreecha, Vitchapong Prasitsumrit, Eric Trépo, Elizabeth K. Speliotes, Vincent L. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic liver disease is a rapidly growing cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and this increase is largely driven by steatotic liver disease (SLD) comprising metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), and MASLD and increased alcohol intake (MetALD). Hepatic steatosis and fibrosis are highly heritable and genetic variation has also been associated with the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and hepatic decompensation. This review summarizes the state of the art of genetics and genomics in SLD, with particular emphasis on potential clinical utility. Variants in/near genes including PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and HSD17B13 have been associated with cirrhosis and HCC in both MASLD and ALD, while others such as those in GCKR, TRIB1, and GPAM have been consistently associated with greater risk of hepatic steatosis in MASLD with much weaker effects if any on cirrhosis and HCC. While data on MetALD genetics are limited, we believe that the genetic risk factors for liver steatosis, cirrhosis, and HCC will be similar in MetALD compared to MASLD and ALD based on the largely shared genetic risk factors for MASLD and ALD. Finally, we discuss future directions in SLD genetics, notably genetically-defined disease subtypes, gene-environment interactions and their implications for therapy, and the utility of genetics in clinical risk stratification. Genetic studies have provided insight into important disease processes in SLD and may have an upcoming role in clinical care.

INTRODUCTION

Steatotic liver disease (SLD)—predominantly comprising metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), MASLD and increased alcohol intake (MetALD), and alcohol-related liver disease (ALD)—is the greatest challenge facing gastro-hepatologists worldwide. SLD is becoming increasingly common in parallel with the high prevalence of obesity, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and harmful alcohol use [1-4]. SLD is rapidly becoming the leading indication for liver transplantation and the predominant driver of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in high-income countries, even in the absence of cirrhosis [5-7]. Yet, the majority of individuals with SLD will not experience major adverse liver events [8]. The development of SLD is strongly shaped by inherited genetic variation, and there is substantial overlap in the genetic drivers across the spectrum of SLD. Genetics and genomics therefore offer important opportunities to understand disease pathogenesis, stratify patients into risk subgroups, and identify novel therapeutic targets. Here, we review the evidence from genetic studies of MASLD, ALD, and MetALD and the implications of these findings.

METABOLIC DYSFUNCTION-ASSOCIATED STEATOTIC LIVER DISEASE

Common germline polymorphisms account for up to 30% of the observed inter-individual variability in liver fat content in MASLD [9,10]. This observation has been replicated across multiple genome-wide association studies (GWAS), twin [10] and family studies [11,12]. The effect is greatest at the most severe end of the disease spectrum; having a first-degree relative with advanced MASLD fibrosis confers an 11.5 adjusted odds ratio for advanced fibrosis [11]. This is distinct from monogenic disorders that are associated with severe phenotypes of MASLD (e.g., congenital lipodystrophies, severe insulin resistance syndromes) [13].

Foundational genome wide association studies

Large GWAS have now identified dozens of independent genes or loci that are reproducibly associated with MASLD risk [14-16]. Genome-wide studies leveraging imaging, histology, and electronic health record–derived phenotypes have substantially refined understanding of the genetic architecture underlying MASLD. Early unbiased investigations, most notably from the Dallas Heart Study, identified a missense variant in PNPLA3 (p.Ile148Met) that was strongly associated with increased hepatic triglyceride content [17]. There are substantial differences in allele frequency across ancestral groups (i.e., >40% in those of Hispanic ancestry, compared to <10% in those of African ancestry), which mirrors observed ancestral differences in MASLD prevalence and severity [18], though there are many other factors that influence disparities [19]. PNPLA3 encodes a lipid droplet–associated protein with a central role in hepatic lipid metabolism. Specifically, PNPLA3 interacts with ABHD5 to regulate triglyceride lipolysis by ATGL, which is a main hepatic lipase [20-22]. This mechanism appears to have a differential effect on long, polyunsaturated triglycerides compared to shorter, unsaturated triglycerides, which shifts the hepatocyte lipidome towards a more lipotoxic phenotype [23,24]. PNPLA3 has also emerged as a key genetic determinant of steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC [25-27]. In addition, notable geographic and ethnic differences have been observed in the distribution of the PNPLA3 rs738409 variant between Asian and Caucasian populations. PNPLA3 rs738409 (G allele) is markedly more frequent in East Asian populations, with reported minor allele frequencies ranging from 35% to 45%, compared to approximately 20–25% in European ancestries [28]. This may partially explain the relatively high prevalence of MASLD in Asia despite less severe metabolic conditions, commonly known as lean MASLD or lean patients with MASLD.

Lean patients with MASLD (body mass index [BMI]<23 kg/m2) are common in Asians, and previous studies have demonstrated the role of PNPLA3 genetic polymorphism has a greater impact on hepatic steatosis among lean individuals with MASLD than in overweight or obese group [29]. In addition, another study from Taiwan showed that genetic variants in PNPLA3 and SAMM50 were linked with lean individuals with MASLD, and were replicated in the All of Us dataset, further supporting the important role of genetic susceptibility in the pathogenesis of lean MASLD [30]. Overall, the major genetic risk factors for MASLD have been consistently validated across Asian cohorts [31-34]. Large-scale GWAS conducted in the Korean populations have identified several key MASLD-associated variants previously identified in Caucasian populations, including PNPLA3, APOB, GCKR, and APOC1. These findings suggest that the overall genetic architecture of MASLD in Asian populations largely mirrors the core susceptibility loci identified globally, underscoring their importance as potential targets for genetic risk stratification.

Subsequent large-scale meta-analyses of population-based cohorts identified additional MASLD-associated loci, including GCKR and TM6SF2, which have been replicated across diverse ancestral populations and diagnostic modalities including histology and imaging [14,35,36]. TM6SF2 has been robustly associated with hard disease outcomes including HCC [27,37] while GCKR variant associations with HCC and cirrhosis have been mixed [25,27]. In contrast, variants in PPP1R3B, initially associated with reduced liver attenuation on imaging [9], were subsequently shown to influence hepatic glycogen storage [38] rather than triglyceride accumulation, underscoring the importance of careful phenotypic interpretation when using indirect measurements of SLD.

The establishment of large population biobanks has enabled further gains in statistical power and locus discovery. Exome-wide association studies identified variants in HSD17B13 that were associated with protection from liver disease, including reduced risk of steatohepatitis and fibrosis, despite in some contexts not being linked to changes in hepatic steatosis [39-41]. These protective effects were consistently replicated across histology-based cohorts, candidate gene studies, and multi-ancestry GWAS, supporting a role for HSD17B13 in modifying disease severity and progression rather than steatosis per se [42-44]. HSD17B13 is a lipid droplet binding protein that may shift the intrahepatic lipid profile [39,45], though it is unclear precisely how this variant has such pervasively protective effects against advanced liver disease. Some possibilities include possibly affecting retinol metabolism [46] but this mechanism is debated.

Imaging-based GWAS using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-PDFF (and derived liver fat from raw abdominal MRI data [47,48]) have identified additional loci, including APOE [49]. Phenome-wide association analyses on APOE revealed certain alleles associated with increased hepatic steatosis and confer favorable cardiometabolic traits while protecting against Alzheimer’s disease, whilst others influenced only the presence of Alzheimer’s disease [50]. Studies relying on diagnostic codes alone identified PNPLA3, TM6SF2, GCKR, MTARC1, TRIB1, FTO, and APOE [51,52]. Some associations, in particular FTO, may reflect effects on MASLD correlated traits such as abdominal adiposity rather than hepatic steatosis itself, or be a reflection of indirect effects (i.e., genetically encoded obesity [53] subsequently associated with SLD). Though, aside from FTO (for obesity) and GCKR (for type 2 diabetes), the relative lack of overlap between GWAS-significant loci for body mass index, type 2 diabetes, and SLD [36,54,55] is notable, which indicates that the genetic predictors of liver fat are distinct from additional environmental drivers of advanced SLD [56], as discussed further below.

Whole-exome sequencing and gene-based burden testing have captured the effects of rarer variants. This approach has demonstrated predicted loss-of-function variants in CIDEB conferring protection against steatosis, steatohepatitis, and fibrosis [57]. CIDEB is another fundamental hepatic lipid droplet-associated protein, and its loss seems to protect against MASLD through altered lipolysis with reduced insulin resistance [58,59].

Somatic mutations

CIDEB is one of several genes that accumulate somatic mutations in SLD. Deep sequencing from distinct locations within the same liver can identify recurrent somatic mutations within clonal populations [60-62]. These mutations give clones protection under MASLD promoting conditions through a variety of gene- and variant-specific mechanisms. For example, mutations on Serine-22 of FOXO1 lead to persistent nuclear localization and increased gluconeogenesis, which appears to be beneficial in advanced MASLD. It is unclear to what extent this mutation contributes to these individuals’ systemic insulin resistance or whether it is a consequence of altered hepatic insulin signaling. Similarly, mutations in the DNA-binding domain of TBX3 (predicted to cause protein destabilization) cause increased expression of genes that promote VLDL secretion from hepatocytes, thereby protecting them from elevated lipid load [63]; in other genes (e.g., CIDEB and GPAM) there are multiple different clones present within each patient.

In some cases, the mechanism of protection from somatic mutations is consistent with the germline function of the variant. For example, loss of function mutation in CIDEB causes smaller lipid droplets, which is associated with increased lipolysis by adipose triglyceride lipase (encoded by PNPLA2) and resulting upregulation of fatty acid oxidation pathways (i.e., PPARA) [59]. However, for other genes work to date has shown counterintuitive mechanisms. Biallelic germline loss of function variants in PKD1 leads to autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease type 1, but variants near exon 15 are enriched in cirrhotic livers [61]. In mouse models of MASLD, these variants protect against steatohepatitis through increased mTOR activation, prevention of glucose intolerance, and increased β-oxidation of fat, but it is unclear precisely how this happens [61].

Somatic mutation analysis is a relatively recent field of liver genetics, and further affected genes are likely to be identified, though this technique generally requires carefully collected liver samples (snap-frozen cubes), costly deep sequencing, and substantial bioinformatic expertise to perform [64]. While it initially appeared that only a minority of somatically mutated genes were also implicated in MASLD through germline variation [65], larger sample sizes with greater coverage are demonstrating greater overlap [57]. This principle could be applied in reverse by performing targeted somatic mutation sequencing in genes that have been previously implicated in MASLD GWAS. This appears to work in murine studies [66] where a MASLD-inducing diet selected for loss-of-function clones in GPAM, MBOAT7, PKD1, and TBX3 [63,67,68], Germline mutations and convergent somatic mutations are conceptually linked in that they both provide evidence of evolutionary benefit (at least at the cellular level) for these mutations, so this overlap in germline and somatically mutated genes provides intriguing biologic insights.

Integrative analyses

Integrative analyses combining imaging-derived phenotypes with diagnostic codes have further increased discovery power [69]. These studies, as well as those taking a disease agnostic approach [25,26,47,70,71], have demonstrated that common variants in HFE, SERPINA1, and ADH1B are primarily associated with hepatic fibrosis with an additional bystander effect on hepatic lipid content [25,26]. Analyses across SLD have also helped to identify causal genes, most notably MBOAT7, which was initially identified in an ALD GWAS [72] and subsequently replicated in MASLD [67,73]. MBOAT7 is a further example of a gene that perturbs the hepatic lipid profile towards a more lipotoxic context, as well as having modest effects on total lipid accumulation [24,66,74,75].

Large meta-analyses integrating imaging data, electronic health records, and multi-ethnic cohorts have now defined a core set of reproducible SLD-associated loci (Table 1). Collectively, these implicate pathways governing hepatic lipid input and export, glucose metabolism, adiposity and fat distribution, insulin resistance, and mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum function. Studies using alanine aminotransferase (ALT) as a proxy phenotype have enabled even larger sample sizes [14,76] but show limited overlap with steatosis-specific loci, suggesting that ALT reflects broader mechanisms of hepatic injury beyond fat accumulation alone. Similarly, genetically informed case definitions based on clinical risk scores have identified additional loci (e.g., MAST3, an abdominal obesity gene) [77], though these approaches have captured genes related to obesity [78] and metabolic comorbidity as they have not been replicated in other histologically characterized cohorts [35].

In contrast to steatosis, the genetic determinants of histologically defined steatohepatitis and fibrosis remain less well characterized, largely due to limited cohort sizes and reduced statistical power. Some early analyses that identified suggestive signals associated with disease activity, fibrosis, and lobular inflammation have not been replicated [79]. Subsequent studies confirmed associations between PNPLA3 and histological features of steatohepatitis and fibrosis [35,51,80]. Overall, almost all variants that have been independently replicated to associate with histological MASLD severity (steatohepatitis and/or fibrosis) also affect hepatic lipid content, though the reverse is not necessarily true (Table 1). The only exceptions are those described above (e.g., HFE, SERPINA1, ADH1B) that exacerbate the severity of existing MASLD. It is interesting to observe that, even though inflammation is critical to the development of liver fibrosis, inflammatory pathways have not been consistently associated with liver fibrosis/cirrhosis in genetic association studies [25]. Another limitation is that there are to our knowledge no GWAS specifically for MASLD-associated HCC.

To overcome the constraints of biopsy-based studies, imaging-derived biomarkers have increasingly been used as surrogate phenotypes. GWAS of corrected T1 time (cT1), an MRI-based measure of hepatic fibroinflammation [81], identified associations at PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and genes involved in metal transport (SLC39A8) [49]. However, some of these signals likely reflect the sensitivity of MRI to non-fibrotic processes, such as hepatic manganese accumulation [82]. Nevertheless, as liver biopsy becomes less common in routine practice, the continued expansion of imaging-based cohorts represents a pragmatic strategy for advancing understanding of the genetics of steatohepatitis and fibrosis. Such studies will also be required to facilitate larger GWAS in children [83].

ALCOHOL-RELATED LIVER DISEASE

The global prevalence of harmful alcohol use continues to rise, with ALD accounting for approximately 25% of global cirrhosis deaths and up to 20% of HCC cases [84]. Although excessive alcohol consumption is the primary driver of ALD, only a subset of individuals progress from non-cirrhotic stages ranging from steatosis and steatohepatitis to advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC, underscoring that other factors such as genetic variation are critical modulators of hepatic susceptibility [85]. Growing evidence highlights a critical role for genetic and genomic factors in modulating disease risk, severity, and progression across the ALD spectrum [86,87]. Thus, understanding the genetic and genomic architecture underlying ALD pathogenesis is essential to explain heterogeneity in clinical outcomes, refine risk stratification for cirrhosis and HCC, and ultimately inform precision-based prevention and therapeutic strategies.

We highlight one key difference in the literature on ALD vs. MASLD genetics. In MASLD, most studies have focused on the early phenotype of hepatic steatosis and only subsequently assessed the impact of steatosis-associated variants on later phenotypes of cirrhosis and its complications. In contrast, most ALD GWAS have investigated advanced stage disease, specifically the presence versus absence of the definitive endpoints of cirrhosis or HCC among individuals with heavy alcohol consumption [71,72,88,89].

FOUNDATIONAL GENETIC DRIVERS OF CIRRHOSIS IN ALD

Large candidate-gene and GWAS have consistently demonstrated that common risk variants—most notably in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, MBOAT7, and HSD17B13—substantially modify susceptibility to advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis in heavy drinkers, despite comparable levels of alcohol exposure [72,85,88]. In 2011, Stickel et al. [90] evaluated the impact of PNPLA3 rs738409 in ALD population from German cohorts, including 1,043 heavy alcohol users with or without liver injury and 376 at-risk drinkers from a population-based cohort. They demonstrated that PNPLA3 rs738409 G allele was more frequent in alcohol-associated cirrhosis and lowest in drinkers with steatosis with normal liver enzyme levels (35% vs. 17.7%) [90]. The population attributable risk of cirrhosis among heavy alcohol users of allele PNPLA3 rs738409(G) was estimated at 26.6% [90]. Subsequently, a landmark 2015 two-stage GWAS by Buch et al. [72] evaluated genetic susceptibility to alcohol-associated cirrhosis by comparing heavy alcohol users with cirrhosis to alcohol-misusing controls without liver disease across multiple European cohorts. The discovery phase included 712 cases and 1,426 controls, followed by replication in German and Belgian cohorts, identifying genome-wide significant associations at PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and MBOAT7 loci [72]. Key differences between these studies and earlier MASLD-focused GWAS included that these studies used individuals with excess alcohol intake without liver disease (or without advanced liver disease) as controls, thereby isolating genetic determinants of disease progression rather than alcohol consumption itself. Similar to MASLD, the findings showed that dysregulated lipid turnover is a shared pathogenic mechanism linking alcohol-associated cirrhosis and metabolic liver disease. In 2020, Innes et al. [91] conducted a large GWAS study to identify genetic modifiers of susceptibility to alcohol-associated cirrhosis by comparing individuals with alcohol-related cirrhosis to heavy drinkers without liver disease. In addition to confirming previously established risk loci such as PNPLA3, the study expanded this landscape by identifying two novel genome-wide significant associations: a missense variant in MARC1 (rs2642438), in which the minor A allele conferred protection against alcohol-related cirrhosis, and an intronic variant near HNRNPUL1 (rs15052), where the minor C allele increased cirrhosis risk [91]. Notably, the protective MARC1 variant was also associated with lower liver fat and reduced hepatic injury markers, suggesting a functional role in mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative stress pathways and has been associated with MASLD [91].

More recently, Schwantes-An et al. [88] developed and validated a novel 20-SNP polygenic risk score PRSALC for alcohol-associated cirrhosis using a multi-stage, data-driven approach that meta-analyzed GWAS data and validated findings in two independent cohorts (UK Biobank and Indiana Biobank Liver cohort) totaling over 20,000 participants. The study found that individuals in the top decile of the PRSALC carried a nearly three- to four-fold higher risk of cirrhosis compared to those in the bottom decile, even after adjusting for clinical variables such as age, sex, body mass index, and diabetes [88]. Crucially, the PRSALC provided incremental predictive accuracy over standard clinical risk factors and demonstrated significant utility in identifying “fast progressors” who developed severe liver dysfunction despite reporting lower behavioral alcohol intake. This research expands upon previous 3-SNP genetic scores (which included PNPLA3, HSD17B13, and TM6SF2) [92] by identifying additional contributors like KLHL8 and PDE7B, while further underscoring the shared genetic architecture of liver injury by showing the PRS also effectively stratifies risk for cirrhosis [88]. It should be noted that previous studies consistently reported genetic polymorphisms of ALDH1B and ALDH2 which are common in East Asian populations (40–50% carry the low activity of ALDH2) that causes toxic acetaldehyde and increase risk of ALD despite lower overall alcohol consumption [93,94].

GENETIC MODIFIERS OF PROGRESSION TO HCC IN ALD

Several studies have also identified variants that specifically predispose alcohol-associated cirrhotic patients to HCC. A 2014 individual participant meta-analysis of 2,503 patients identified associations between PNPLA3 rs738409-G and ALD-associated HCC [95]. Shortly after, another multicenter case-control design from liver centers across Europe (Switzerland, United Kingdom, Germany) that included patients with alcohol-associated cirrhosis with and without HCC investigated effects of PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 genotypes on HCC risk [89]. The investigators demonstrated that the PNPLA3 rs738409 G allele and the TM6SF2 rs58542926 T allele were independently associated with about two-fold increased risk of HCC, even after adjustment for age, sex, diabetes, and body mass index, and accounting for nearly 50% of the combined population-attributable fraction for HCC [89]. Importantly, by using controls with ALD cirrhosis, this study showed that these variants conferred HCC risk beyond their established effects on fibrosis/cirrhosis, suggesting a more direct role in hepatocarcinogenesis. The findings of this study established a genetic basis for interindividual variability in HCC risk among heavy drinkers and supported the concept that PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 contribute not only to disease progression but also to cancer susceptibility, laying the groundwork for genetically informed HCC risk stratification in ALD. Two other studies confirmed the HSD17B13 rs72613567:TA splice variant as protective from HCC among individuals with ALD cirrhosis [96,97].

In 2022, Trépo et al. [98] published a GWAS specifically focused on alcohol-related HCC, utilizing a two-stage case-control design that included over 4,000 European patients. The meta-analysis identified a novel susceptibility locus on chromosome 1 spanning the WNT3A-WNT9A genes (rs708113) and confirmed the genome-wide significance of previously reported risk variants in TM6SF2 and PNPLA3 [98]. A major breakthrough of this research compared to earlier candidate gene studies was the discovery that the WNT3A-WNT9A variant modulates HCC risk independently of liver fibrosis severity, suggesting a distinct biological role in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway during alcohol-mediated hepatocarcinogenesis [98]. Somatic activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, predominantly through gain-of-function CTNNB1 mutations, represents one of the most frequent oncogenic events in HCC [99]—occurring in approximately 28% of cases and particularly enriched in alcohol-related tumors [100]. Further study by Buch et al. [72] utilizing large multicentre two-stage GWAS investigated host genetic determinants of HCC specifically among patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis by comparing 1,214 cirrhotic patients with HCC to 1,866 cirrhotic controls without HCC across European cohorts, with additional validation in individuals with alcohol misuse but no liver disease [71]. The study confirmed previously established associations between PNPLA3 (rs738409) and TM6SF2 (rs58542926) variants and nearly two-fold increased HCC risk and identified a novel genome- wide significant association at the TERT locus, where the rs2242652 A allele conferred a substantial protective effect against HCC development independent of age, sex, obesity, and diabetes. Importantly, the TERT variant was not associated with the risk of developing cirrhosis itself, indicating a specific role in hepatocarcinogenesis rather than fibrosis progression [71]. These associations align with the extensive literature on the importance of somatic mutations in TERT promoter and Wnt/β-catenin pathways seen in HCC; notably, there are no GWAS to our knowledge associating germline TERT or WNT3A-WNT9A variants to MASLD-associated HCC though somatic mutations in both have been observed in MASLD-associated HCC [101,102].

Compared with most of the MASLD literature, the ALD genetics field has largely been more consistent about using as controls individuals with excess alcohol intake but without cirrhosis or HCC. This may reduce statistical power to identify ALD-HCC cases, for instance if alcohol intake status is unknown or underreported. However, it allows for measurement of more direct effects of genetic variants on fibrosis (for the cirrhosis outcome) or hepatocarcinogenesis (for HCC outcomes) compared to studies that use the general population as controls, where effects of genetic variants on cirrhosis/HCC may be mediated by their effects on steatosis and fibrosis.

SHARED GENETIC MECHANISM ACROSS THE SLD SPECTRUM: IMPLICATIONS FOR METALD

The introduction of MetALD into the SLD nomenclature acknowledges the clinical reality where metabolic dysfunction and alcohol consumption synergistically drive liver injury [103]. Most studies found that MASLD and ALD share largely similar genetic variants, which lead to liver cirrhosis. Schwantes-An et al. [88] showed that PRSALC for ALD cirrhosis can also stratify risk of MASLD-related cirrhosis in the same cohort, which suggests shared genetic etiologies between MASLD and ALD [85,104,105]. In addition, a large-scale 2024 multi-ancestry meta-analysis of 12 cohorts, including 18,265 cirrhosis cases and nearly 1.8 million controls by Ghouse et al. [25] identified and validated 14 genome-wide significant risk associations for cirrhosis, demonstrating that many risk variants are located near genes involved in hepatic lipid metabolism. A key highlight of the study is the extensive overlap in genetic susceptibility between ALD and MASLD. The authors found that major risk loci, including those in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and MBOAT7, exert directionally concordant effects on cirrhosis risk regardless of the underlying etiology [25]. This integrative approach underscores that the biological threshold for advanced liver fibrosis is determined by shared genetic factors that modulate the hepatic response to both metabolic and toxic environmental triggers.

Genetic research specifically dedicated to the MetALD category is in its early stages and we are not aware of GWAS specifically for MetALD. However, current evidence detailed in the prior paragraph suggests MetALD is likely governed by a genetic architecture shared with MASLD and ALD [106]. Major fibrosis and HCC risk loci, including in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, HSD17B13, and MBOAT7, have been confirmed to exert directionally concordant effects on the risk of cirrhosis and HCC across the entire SLD spectrum [88,106,107]. Additionally, the vast majority of patients in prior ALD GWAS had cardiometabolic risk factors, and some of them may have met criteria for MetALD depending on their specific alcohol consumption.

Characterizing the precise genomic landscape of MetALD remains methodologically challenging due to the dynamic nature of alcohol consumption, which often fluctuates significantly over time. Most existing genetic data rely on cross-sectional assessments of alcohol intake, which are highly susceptible to self-reporting bias and can lead to the frequent misclassification of individuals with alcohol use disorder as pure MASLD cases [108]. To improve the precision of genetic risk stratification in MetALD, research increasingly emphasizes the integration of longitudinal drinking histories and objective biomarkers like phosphatidylethanol (Peth) to delineate active versus historical consumption [103,109]. Furthermore, while large-scale “all-cause” cirrhosis studies identify shared genetic markers, they may obscure variants that interact uniquely with the specific moderate-to-heavy drinking thresholds that define the MetALD category [26].

Future research using dedicated longitudinal MetALD cohorts, with alcohol exposure validated by Peth level as suggested from recent studies, together with multi-omics integration, is essential to understand how the polygenic background modulates the transition from simple steatosis to advanced cirrhosis in this heterogeneous population and to confirm the hypothesis of shared genetic polymorphism between MetALD and MASLD/ALD [110,111].

Future directions in SLD genetics

Molecular subtyping

Not all genetic variants associated with SLD work by the same mechanisms. One recent GWAS of MASLD identified 17 variants associated with MASLD and identified several categories of variants with distinct effects on other traits [36]. Some including those in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, APOE, MBOAT7, and MARC1 were also associated with cirrhosis, while most other variants were not. There were also different patterns of effects on lipids, with some including those in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and APOE associated with reduced atherogenic lipids and myocardial infarction, while others including those in GCKR, TRIB1, MARC1, and TOR1B were associated with increased low-density lipoprotein and myocardial infarction. While most variants increased risk of diabetes, the MASLD-promoting GCKR and TRIB1 variants were associated with decreased diabetes risk [36]. Another study that identified 27 novel SLD-associated risk variants categorized the variants based on effects on plasma triglycerides as a proxy for hepatic retention of lipids [114]; that is, variants that reduced plasma triglycerides presumably led to retention of these lipids inside the liver, while those that increased plasma triglycerides presumably had primarily extrahepatic effects (e.g., adipocyte lipid mobilization or de novo lipogenesis) and secondarily hepatic effects. They found that while both variants that reduced plasma lipids vs. increased plasma lipids were associated with higher risk of cirrhosis and HCC, these effects were more pronounced in the reduced plasma lipid (hepatic lipid retention) group. In contrast, variants associated with increased plasma triglycerides were associated with higher risk of cardiovascular complications while the opposite was seen in variants associated with decreased plasma triglycerides [16]. A third GWAS categorized variants as increasing de novo lipogenesis (mostly corresponding to the increased plasma triglyceride group) vs. reducing hepatic fat export (corresponding to the decreased plasma triglyceride group) and found similar associations in which the de novo lipogenesis variants tended to increase cardiovascular disease while the reduced hepatic fat export group reduced cardiovascular risk but had stronger effects on advanced liver disease [15].

The findings that not all genetic variants drive liver disease by the same mechanism imply that different patients may have different underlying pathophysiology. For example, one recent study on subtypes of MASLD based on homeostatic model of insulin resistance scores vs. overall genetic risk identified “metabolic” contributions to MASLD dominated by substrate surplus largely related to systemic insulin resistance and excess caloric intake, vs. “genetic” contributions related to liver-specific mitochondrial redox state (Fig. 1) [112]. Notably, the genetic variants included in this study were those that primarily drive hepatic steatosis by reducing lipid export from the liver and which also increase liver fibrosis/cirrhosis [37], so that the metabolic vs. genetic subtypes of MASLD may reflect the biology of a subset of genetic risk variants rather than global genetic contribution. Still, this concept raises the possibility that determining which mechanisms are dominant in which patients—whether based on genotyping, clinical phenotypes (e.g., diabetes status, lipid levels), or high-throughput methods such as metabolomics and proteomics—may help guide optimal therapy. There is limited and mostly retrospective or post-hoc evidence that PNPLA3 genotype may influence response to therapy [113-115]. While some of the associations may simply reflect severity of disease (i.e., those with more advanced baseline disease are more likely to respond to therapy), mechanism may also play some role. For instance, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids may be less likely to result in hepatic steatosis reduction in patients carrying PNPLA3 risk variants [116-118]. PNPLA3 variant carriers typically have increased hepatic polyunsaturated fatty acids levels at baseline because one of the functions of wild-type PNPLA3 is to mobilize polyunsaturated fatty acids so they can leave the liver [119], and thus it is unlikely that PUFA supplementation would improve liver disease in these individuals. It could potentially be more beneficial in patients with MASLD driven by different disease biology however.

Gene-environment interactions

Genetics increase risk of disease but this effect is exacerbated by non-genetic environmental factors; a hypothetical example is shown in Figure 2A. In one study of individuals without overt diabetes, the prevalence of MASLD increased based on PNPLA3 genotype in individuals at the lowest quartile of insulin level (23% in CC vs. 39% in GG) but increased even more drastically in those with the highest quartile of insulin (54% vs. 94% in CC vs. GG) [120]. Indeed, insulin resistance appeared to mediate interactions between MASLD, PNPLA3 genotype, and other environmental factors including body mass index and triglyceride levels [120]. These findings highlight the central role of insulin resistance in driving PNPLA3-associated liver disease and imply that therapeutics aimed at reducing insulin resistance may attenuate genetic risk for hepatic steatosis. Indeed, later studies have found that diabetes interacts with PNPLA3 genotype to multiplicatively increase risk of not just hepatic steatosis, but also major adverse liver outcomes (Fig. 2B) [121]. Moreover, several genetic variants may also influence adipose tissue by modulating insulin signaling and fat distribution which promotes MASLD progression [122-125]. One study reported the interaction between the FTO (fat mass and obesity-associated) rs9939609 genotype and a high-calorie diet in adults with overweight [126]. In addition, a recent study from Mongolia found that the FTO rs9939609 AT/AA genotype, which promotes hepatic lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, was linked to MASLD [123]. Furthermore, genetic variants in INSR (insulin receptor) gene and a key downstream signaling molecule such as IRS1 (insulin receptor substrate 1) have been strongly associated with impairment in insulin’s ability to suppress lipolysis in adipose tissue [124]. This impairment results in dysregulated lipolysis and altered fat distribution, effectively lowering the threshold at which environmental factors, including high-calorie diet, trigger the progression from simple steatosis to MASH [125].

Other studies have found interactions between lifestyle factors and genetics to drive disease risk. In one study of MRI-quantified liver fat content in UK Biobank, liver fat content and iron-cT1 (a marker of hepatic fibrosis and inflammation) were increased with genetic risk (PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 risk variants and a polygenic risk score) and specific dietary patterns (Mediterranean-style diet, increased fruit/vegetable/legume intake, increased fish intake, and low red/processed meat intake) [127]. The dietary and genetic factors also interacted to promote disease risk. For example, higher fruit/vegetable/legume intake was associated with a –0.35% reduction in liver fat content in PNPLA3-rs738409-CC, but a –1.12% reduction in liver fat content in rs738409-GG carriers, implying that dietary effects could have as much as three-fold increased effect in those with high genetic risk and implying that these individuals may derive even greater benefit from lifestyle changes than those at lower genetic risk [127]. Gene-diet interactions have also been associated with risk of hard endpoints. In one study in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey III, PNPLA3 genotype also consistently interacted with modest alcohol intake, smoking, increased saturated fat intake, and increased cholesterol intake to increase risk of liver-related mortality [128]. Finally, of particular relevance to ALD and MetALD are the well-documented gene-environment interactions between alcohol consumption and liver-related phenotypes. One study from the NHANES dataset found that even light alcohol use (≤7/14 drinks/week in women/men) was associated with increased liver-related mortality and that this effect was greater in PNPLA3 risk allele carriers [128]. Alcohol use can interact with both cardiometabolic risk factors and genetics. Another study using UK Biobank found that obesity and excess alcohol use (≥25/36 drinks/week in women/men) synergistically interacted with PNPLA3 genotype [129]. For example, the hazard ratio for liver-related death with excess alcohol use in those without obesity was 2.93 vs. 6.67 in PNPLA3-rs738409-CC vs. -GG; HR with obesity without excess alcohol use was 1.73 vs. 6.85; and HR with excess alcohol use and obesity was 4.85 vs. 21.82 [129]. These findings imply that efforts at reducing alcohol intake, achieving weight loss, and improving diet may be even more impactful in individuals at higher baseline risk due to their genetics. As a practical matter, knowing baseline genetic risk may be a motivating factor to help patients at increased risk adhere to lifestyle recommendations [130].

Clinical risk stratification

To facilitate incorporation of genetics into clinical care, it will be critical to show that including genetics provides incremental value beyond standard of care, i.e., non-invasive tests (NITs) and/or liver biopsy in the context of risk stratification (Fig. 3). There have been conflicting results in this space. One study of 197,509 participants in UK Biobank with SLD risk factors (increased waist circumference, increased BMI, excess alcohol intake, or type 2 diabetes) assessed prognostic accuracy of non-proprietary blood-based NITs, with or without a cirrhosis PRS, to predict 10-year risk of liver-related events [131]. The best NITs including those most widely used in clinical practice—APRI, NFS, and FIB4—had area under the receiver operating characteristic curve values of approximately 0.8, and addition of the PRS did not increase prognostic accuracy [131]. In another UK Biobank study, a PRS including PNPLA3, TM6SF2, GCKR, and MBOAT7 genotypes was not associated with cirrhosis and HCC among patients with low NFS, FIB4, or APRI [132]. However, in patients with intermediate/high NIT values, or with other risk factors including diabetes or obesity, the PRS was associated with risk of cirrhosis and HCC [132]. Another study assessed the prognostic value of genetics in individuals with ALT elevations and likely MASLD in two independent cohorts, Michigan Genomics Initiative and UK Biobank [133]. In low FIB4 participants, there was again little association between a cirrhosis PRS or PNPLA3 genotype and incident cirrhosis; but in intermediate/high FIB4 participants, both the PRS and PNPLA3 genotypes were significantly associated with incident disease. Further, participants with intermediate FIB4 who had both high-risk genetics (PNPLA3-rs738409-GG homozygosity) and clinical risk factors (diabetes) had risk for incident cirrhosis similar to that of those with high FIB4 [133], so that they were “bumped up” a risk category. Another study assessing fibrosis stage using magnetic resonance elastography or VCTE found that patients with diabetes and low FIB4 but elevated genetic risk had a prevalence of advanced fibrosis comparable to that of the overall intermediate FIB4 population with diabetes [134]. Finally, a study of almost 100,000 Million Veterans Program participants assessed FIB4 slope (change in FIB4 values divided by time elapsed, among those with serial measurements) as a surrogate measure of disease progression, and found that the PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 risk loci associated with MASLD were associated with greater FIB4 slope [135]. Together, these studies imply that even if genetics do not improve prognostic area under the curve—which is intrinsically a continuous measure—when used in conjunction with clinically-utilized thresholds for FIB4 and other NITs, genetics may provide additional prognostic information, especially when combined with cardiometabolic risk factors.

Genetics has also been shown to carry prognostic significance over histology. In 2,075 patients with biopsy-confirmed MASLD from the MASH Clinical Research Network, PNPLA3-rs738409-G allele was associated with increased risk of LRE especially in conjunction with diabetes status [121]. A separate study of 1,178 patients in Japan with biopsy-confirmed MASLD found a significant association between LRE and PNPLA3 genotype as well as between LRE and a PRS including PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and HSD17B13 genotypes [136]. Notably, both studies found that the absolute increase in LRE due to PNPLA3 risk alleles was greater in participants with advanced fibrosis [121,136], similar to the NIT-based studies that also found that genetics showed stronger associations in those with high FIB4 scores. Together, these findings imply that genetic risk (either PNPLA3 genotype or polygenic risk scores) combined with cardiometabolic risk factors (especially diabetes) may provide prognostic information beyond NITs and histology and result in actionable risk stratification (Fig. 3). However, data in patients undergoing elastography (which is largely replacing biopsy for risk stratification) are still lacking and are crucial for wider expansion to clinical care.

There has been interest in using genetics to stratify risk of HCC in at-risk populations. However, despite the strong associations between several risk alleles and HCC compared to controls with cirrhosis, studies have yielded mixed results. One European study in ALD and hepatitis C-related cirrhosis found that polygenic risk scores including PNPLA3, TM6SF2, HSD17B13, APOE, and MBOAT7 variants were associated with risk of HCC in patients with cirrhosis [137]. However, the incremental benefit of incorporating genetics on top of the aMAP score was minimal. Another US study showed that incorporating PNPLA3 genotype improved prediction of one-year but not two-year HCC risk in patients with cirrhosis [138].

Therapeutic implications

Drugs targeting genes and variants implicated in SLD are in development [139]. ARO-PNPLA3 is a conjugated small interfering RNA targeted at PNPLA3-rs738409G and resulted in >70% reduction of mutant PNPLA3 expression and >20% relative reduction in liver fat content in phase 1 trials [140]. AZD2693 is an anti-sense oligonucleotide also targeted against PNPLA3-rs738409-G which also potentially decreased expression and liver fat reduction in phase 1 trials [141]. However, in a phase 2b randomized controlled trial, AZD2693 did not show efficacy over placebo in reduction in steatohepatitis or fibrosis regression [142]. Rapirosirin (formerly HSD-ALN) is a small interfering RNA agent which reduces HSD17B13 expression; a phase 2 clinical trial comparing rapirosirin to placebo is underway (NCT05519475) [143]. A phase 2 trial for an antisense oligonucleotide inhibiting DGAT2 (ION224) also demonstrated efficacy in steatohepatitis reduction without worsening of fibrosis [144].

Genetics may also influence response to non-targeted therapy, and this has been best studied for the PNPLA3-rs738409-G genotype. However, studies on influence of PNPLA3 genotype on treatment response have yielded conflicting results for glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and no association with resmetirom response [113,145-147]. All studies on differential response to pharmacotherapy to date have been post-hoc, and prospective analyses are required to address this topic.

CONCLUSIONS

Genetic studies have provided insight into important disease processes in SLD. Variants associated with MASLD and ALD overlap almost completely for non-HCC phenotypes, with the caveat that data for MASLD-related HCC are minimal. The findings suggest that mechanisms underlying fibrosis and carcinogenesis may overlap. Future prospective studies may demonstrate utility of genetics including PNPLA3-rs738409-GG genotype and polygenic risk scores (if appropriate ancestry-specific calibration can be demonstrated) in routine clinical care. So far this concept has been best established in intermediate-high risk MASLD (by FIB4 and/or biopsy) and strong cardiometabolic risk factors (especially type 2 diabetes) for prediction of MALO; studies based on elastography-based disease staging remain largely lacking. Gene therapy targeted against PNPLA3 variants and HSD17B13 is in development but the PNPLA3 variant-targeting AZD2693 phase 2b clinical trial was negative for histologic outcomes and histologic efficacy for other agents has not been demonstrated.
