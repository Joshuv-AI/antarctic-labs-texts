Protocol for analyzing protein interactomes under starvation in fission yeast using in vivo cross-linking
Authors: Livia Pérez-Hidalgo, Celia Gálvez-Merchán, Javier Encinar del Dedo, Sergio Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIdentifying a protein’s interactome can be challenging when studying low-abundance proteins, labile or transient interactions, or cells under starvation conditions. Here, we present a protocol to purify proteins and their interacting partners in the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe. We use formaldehyde to cross-link proteins in vivo, thereby stabilizing protein complexes prior to immunoprecipitation. We then describe immunoprecipitation procedures and approaches for interpreting mass spectrometry datasets. Finally, this protocol enables the validation of specific protein interactions by western blotting.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Gálvez-Merchán et al.1 and Encinar del Dedo et al.2Subject areas: Cell Biology, Molecular Biology, Protein Biochemistry, Proteomics

Before you begin

Cells continuously adapt to changes in their nutritional environment. When nutrients become limiting, the cell cycle arrests in G1 phase and cells enter quiescence, a reversible state of non-division. This transition requires repression of the transcriptional program that promotes the G1/S transition. In fission yeast, transcription at the G1/S transition is controlled by the MBF (MluI cell cycle box-binding factor) complex, composed of Cdc10, Res1 and Res2.3,4,5 We recently showed that, under nitrogen starvation conditions, Whi5 physically interacts with the MBF complex and represses MBF-dependent genes, thereby promoting G1 arrest and entry into quiescence.1

This protocol describes the immunoprecipitation of Cdc10 from nitrogen-starved cells followed by interactome analysis by mass spectrometry. Using this approach, we identified Whi5 as a Cdc10 interactor, together with other components of the MBF complex. Reciprocal analysis of the Whi5 interactome also detected MBF components. In addition, proteins of Clr6-I histone deacetylase (HDAC) complex were identified as interactors of both Cdc10 and Whi5. Mass spectrometry analysis showed that the interaction between Cdc10 and Clr6-I is impaired in the absence of Whi5, supporting a role for Whi5 in mediating the association between the MBF and Clr6-I complexes (Figure 1).1

Model of MBF-dependent transcriptional regulation by Whi5 and the Clr6-I complex under nitrogen starvation

The interaction network derived from mass spectrometry analysis of Cdc10-L-YFP shows that, under nitrogen starvation, Cdc10 physically interacts with components of the MBF complex, the transcriptional repressor Whi5, and components of the Clr6-I histone deacetylase complex. The interaction between Cdc10 and the Clr6-I complex is disrupted in the absence of Whi5, suggesting that Whi5 facilitates this interaction.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) is one of the most widely used approaches for studying protein-protein interactions and can be coupled with mass spectrometry (IP-MS) or western blot analysis (co-IP). However, low-abundance and unstable proteins, as well as transient interactions, are often difficult to detect because weak interactors may dissociate during sample preparation and washing steps.

In vivo crosslinking is an effective strategy to stabilize protein complexes before cell lysis. Formaldehyde is particularly useful because it rapidly penetrates cells due to its small size and forms reversible covalent cross-links between proteins and between proteins and nucleic acids. At low concentrations (1%) and short incubation times (10 min), formaldehyde primarily cross-links molecules that are in close proximity. In addition, covalent stabilization allows more stringent washing conditions, reducing non-specific interactions and improving the recovery of physiologically relevant protein complexes. In addition, it is inexpensive and commonly found in most laboratories. These properties make formaldehyde a widely used reagent for the analysis of protein interactions in yeast,6,7 plants,8 and mammalian cells.9

Innovation

The protocol described here was adapted from the protocol described by Fang et al. (2014)7 for in vivo cross-linking and immunoprecipitation in budding yeast. Although protocols for purifying and analyzing protein interactomes after in vivo cross-linking have been published,8,10,11,12 this protocol is optimized for the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe under starvation conditions, and it has been successfully used to characterize the interactomes of transcription factors1 and protein phosphatases2 and to validate protein-protein interactions by Western blot analysis.2,13 Additionally, with modifications, this protocol can be implemented for use in other organisms.

In this protocol, the inclusion of a formaldehyde cross-linking step enhances the capture of weak or transient interactions and improves the detection of interactions with low-abundance proteins, which are commonly associated with regulatory processes. This protocol has three features that improve efficiency. First, the Freezer/Mill cryogenic grinder breaks cells in liquid nitrogen, thereby minimizing protein degradation. Second, nanobodies provide high specificity and low background. Third, the use of low protein-binding tubes reduces sample loss, which is crucial for proteins with DNA- or lipid-binding domains that are prone to non-specific surface adsorption.

Together, these improvements allow a reduction in the volume of the starting material required. Furthermore, this approach facilitates the analysis of samples obtained under challenging growth conditions, such as nutrient starvation, where protein synthesis is inhibited and protein degradation (by autophagy, proteasome, etc.) is increased.

Sequence coverage: the percentage of the protein sequence covered by identified peptides. Higher coverage increases confidence in protein identification.

Number of unique peptides: peptides that map to a single protein and are used for its identification.

Intensity: represents the raw signal strength of all detected peptide peaks for a given protein. As larger proteins generate more peptides and therefore higher intensity, this metric is not suitable for comparing two different proteins within the same sample.

iBAQ (Intensity-Based Absolute Quantification)18: calculated by dividing the total peptide intensity of a protein by the number of theoretically observable peptides. This normalized value allows comparison of protein abundance within the same sample. For example, if two proteins have the same intensity, but different sizes, the smaller protein will have a higher iBAQ value.

The target protein should have high coverage, intensity, and iBAQ values. Thresholds for defining interacting proteins should be established on a case-by-case basis.19 Detection of interactors previously reported in the literature among the detected proteins supports the validity of the results.

Contaminants resulting from non-specific binding to beads, antibodies, or epitope tags should be discarded. As mentioned previously, inclusion of at least a negative control (e.g., an untagged strain) is essential to identify proteins that bind non-specifically. Additionally, to rule out contaminants arising from non-specific binding to the epitope tag, it would be advisable to include an unrelated protein carrying the same tag as a negative control. Compare the list of proteins in the target IP with the proteins in the negative control IP. True interactors should not appear in the negative control list or should be significantly enriched in the target IP compared to the negative control.

Certain proteins are commonly detected as background in IPs, including ribosomal proteins, cytoskeletal proteins (e.g., actin or tubulin), chaperones, and elongation factors. Comparison with interactomes obtained from unrelated proteins using the same protocol and the same tag can help distinguish true interactors from contaminants. Experienced laboratories often compile lists of recurrent contaminants observed across multiple purifications.20 Additionally, public databases such as CRAPome compile the results of negative control datasets from numerous experiments conducted by different laboratories using various tags and organisms, mostly in human cells and S. cerevisiae, that can be used to assess the likelihood of non-specific interactions.21

Limitations

This protocol has been validated using GFP- and Myc-specific nano-traps (Chromotek, Cat#gtma-100 and Cat#yta-10), as well as anti-HA beads (Sigma, Cat#A2095). Commercial beads coupled to antibodies recognizing other tags should also work. If tagging is not possible but specific antibodies against the protein of interest are available, the antibodies can be coupled to commercial beads. This protocol can be used in these cases, although additional optimization and validation steps may be required.

Mass spectrometry is highly sensitive, allowing the detection of proteins present in very low amounts in a sample. The inclusion of appropriate controls in each experiment is critical to minimize false positives. To prevent non-physiological localizations and interactions, avoid overexpression of the target protein. Results from each experiment should be carefully analyzed to establish appropriate thresholds for identifying significant hits. Increasing the number of biological replicates can further improve discrimination between true and false positives. Newly identified interactors should be validated using alternative approaches, such as IP followed by Western blot (Co-IP), reverse Co-IP, or microscopy-based techniques. These complementary methods can also help distinguish between direct and indirect interactions, which cannot be resolved by mass spectrometry alone.

Formaldehyde-induced modifications may prevent the generation and identification of certain peptides during mass spectrometry analysis. However, cross-linking stabilizes specific protein-protein interactions and enables more stringent washing conditions that effectively remove non-specific interactors arising during lysis, when molecules from different cellular compartments come into contact. Together, these advantages ultimately improve the quality and specificity of the result. The limitations of mass spectrometry analysis should be discussed with the proteomics facility staff before performing the experiment. In some cases, alternative protease digestions should be performed. In addition, selecting an appropriate data acquisition method may improve results. Data-independent acquisition (DIA), for example, has been shown to be superior to data-dependent acquisition (DDA) when analyzing complex samples and may improve detection of low-abundance interactors.22,23

Troubleshooting

Before starting the purification protocol, confirm that the tagged protein is functional and detectable under denaturing conditions. Monitoring each step of the protocol by Western blot analysis in a control experiment may be helpful.

Problem 1

Tagging affects the functionality or stability of the protein.

Depending on the protein of interest, different assays can be used to assess the functionality of the tagged protein. For example, the localization pattern of the tagged protein can be compared with that of the untagged protein. Alternatively, if the phenotype associated with the mutant is known, a functional assay can be performed. For instance, if gene deletion causes a defect in cell cycle progression, the tagged strain can be analyzed to determine whether it behaves similarly to the wild type, the deletion mutant, or exhibits an intermediate phenotype. An intermediate phenotype may indicate that the tagged protein is only partially functional, or it is not expressed at wild-type levels. In other cases, complementation assays can be used to determine whether the tagged protein rescues the mutant phenotype. If the protein is part of a complex, interactions between the tagged protein and its known partners can also be evaluated.

Potential solutions

•Change the position of the tag. Although C-terminal tagging is often more straightforward, some proteins are only functional when tagged at the N-terminus.
