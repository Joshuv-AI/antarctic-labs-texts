Brain structure in the cingulate cortex and locus coeruleus in late life is associated with engagement in complex mental activities across the life span
Authors: Jennifer L. Crawford, Hsiang-Yu Chen, Alex A. Adornato, Johanna Matulonis, Jacob M. Hooker, Heidi I.L. Jacobs, Anne S. Berry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is great interest in characterizing the activities or lifestyle factors that are important for successful aging; nevertheless, rigorous investigations using multimodal neuroimaging measures in conjunction with validated measures of activities are underrepresented in the literature. To address this gap, we assessed whether engagement in complex mental activities across early, middle, and late life, is associated with metrics of brain health in a sample of cognitively unimpaired older adults (N = 76). Complex mental activities were measured using a validated questionnaire indexing lifetime engagement in various pursuits (e.g., occupational complexity, stimulating activities). Indices of late life brain health were quantified using simultaneous PET-MRI, which included estimates of gray matter volume, cortical thickness, and tau burden. The integrity of the locus coeruleus (LC) was also assessed in a subsample of participants (N = 60) using MRI. We found that overall higher levels of complex mental activities were associated with larger estimates of gray matter volume and cortical thickness in the cingulate cortex and higher integrity of the LC. There were no associations between complex mental activities and tau burden. Exploratory analyses suggest that the associations between complex mental activities and late life brain health are life stage specific. Greater engagement in complex mental activities in early life was associated with cingulate cortex volumes and thickness, whereas greater levels of complex mental activities in late life were associated with the integrity of the LC. Together, these findings suggest that a mentally stimulating life may help to confer benefits towards brain health in late life.

Introduction

Many studies have investigated the relationships between different facets of an engaged life (e.g., performing mentally stimulating activities, engaging in physical exercise) and late life cognitive and brain health. From this work, the cingulate cortex has been identified as an important marker of brain health in older adults. Namely, the cingulate cortex plays a central role in attention, cognitive control, and motivation (Crawford et al., 2024) — processes that are integral to engaging in cognitively, socially, and physically enriching activities. Specifically, prior work has shown that gray matter volume in the anterior cingulate cortex in older adults was positively associated with greater engagement in complex mental activities (Turpin et al., 2025). There is also evidence that cortical thickness in the cingulate cortex is positively associated with participation in enriching activities in older adults (Bae et al., 2020). Collectively, these lines of evidence contribute to a growing body of work which has demonstrated the centrality of the cingulate cortex as a reliable marker of cognitive and brain health in older adults. Specifically, indices of both cingulate structure and function have been associated with successful cognitive and brain aging, independent of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology (Arenaza-Urquijo et al., 2019; Pezzoli et al., 2023). Moreover, other work has shown that cingulate cortex structure is associated with superior cognitive performance in older age, or “super aging” (Gefen et al., 2015). Together, these findings suggest that the cingulate cortices may be important loci for understanding the neural mechanisms associated with the potential benefits of engaging in enriching behaviors, particularly relevant to older adult populations.

In parallel, the locus coeruleus (LC) has also emerged as a target of investigations studying the associations between complex activities and cognitive and brain aging. The LC undergoes substantial changes with age and is one of the earliest sites of abnormal tau pathology in the brain (Mather and Harley, 2016; Weinshenker, 2018). Interestingly, the preservation of the LC-norepinephrine system in older age is thought to be supported, in part, by engagement in various enriching behaviors (e.g., mentally stimulating activities) (Mather, 2021; Robertson, 2013). Emerging evidence provides support for this notion, demonstrating a positive relationship between LC function and greater levels of participation in complex mental activities in a sample of cognitively unimpaired older adults (Ciampa et al., 2022). Nevertheless, there has been very little work to date that has quantified the associations between the structural integrity of the LC, a metric sensitive to late life cognitive and brain health (Dahl et al., 2022; Jacobs et al., 2023), with engagement in various complex mental activities across the life span.

Investigations aimed at understanding the linkages between late life brain health and activity participation have also focused on markers of pathological aging. Prior work has found that participation in enriching daily life behaviors (e.g., mental activities) is associated with reduced incidence of developing AD (Wilson et al., 2007). Nevertheless, understanding the relationships between pathological markers of brain health and engagement in enriching activities has proven to be complex. To date, there is not clear evidence pertaining to the relationships between histopathological, or in vivo imaging, markers of pathology and complex mental activities. For example, although some work has observed that post-mortem neuropathological markers were associated with complex mental activities (Li et al., 2021), other work has failed to observe this association (Wilson et al., 2007). These inconsistencies are mirrored across studies using in vivo imaging measures of amyloid-β pathology. Although some work has shown that greater levels of engagement in mentally demanding activities is associated with lower levels of amyloid-β pathology (Landau et al., 2012; Wirth et al., 2014), other studies have failed to observe relationships between measures of these activities and amyloid-β biomarkers (Turpin et al., 2025; Vemuri et al., 2012). Moreover, despite recent developments in the assessment of tau pathology in vivo (Schöll et al., 2016), there are no investigations to date that have assessed the extent to which complex mental activities are related to tau burden. This represents a significant gap in our understanding pertaining to the potential neurobiological mechanisms that a lifetime of engagement in various enriching behaviors could have towards tau pathology in older adults.

Moreover, it has been increasingly recognized that when assessing enriching behaviors, and their associations with brain health in late life, the specific life periods in which individuals engage in these activities holds great importance. Modifiable behaviors that promote greater intellectual engagement at discrete life stages (e.g., early life, midlife, late life) are differentially associated with the risk of developing dementia (Livingston et al., 2024). Empirical evidence supports the utility of examining activities across multiple life stages, demonstrating that both early and late life activities are independently associated with late life cognitive ability (Frank et al., 2023). Multiple studies have also found that metrics of brain health are associated with activity engagement in particular life stages. For example, late life gray matter volume has shown unique associations with activity participation in midlife (Chan et al., 2018; Turpin et al., 2025). Other work has revealed that although mean levels of engagement in mentally demanding activities and amyloid-β pathology were related to each other, these associations were strongest for activities in early- and mid-life, in particular (Landau et al., 2012). Such findings highlight the value of understanding the life stage-specific mechanisms that may promote successful brain aging in older adults through participation in a variety of complex mental activities.

Adopting a life span approach to studying modifiable, enriching activities and their associations with markers of brain health in late life also provides the opportunity to map these empirical data onto theoretical models of brain aging and resilience. Models of resilience have been used to explain the ways in which the brain maintains its function in both healthy and pathological aging (Stern et al., 2023). Indeed, researchers have already begun to suggest that resilience to age- and pathology-related declines may be conferred across the entire life span (Arenaza-Urquijo and Vemuri, 2020). As such, studies that assess engagement in complex mental activities across different life stages can help to refine our understanding of specific resilience mechanisms. For example, a recent study measured engagement in complex mental activities across early, middle, and late life and found that higher levels of complex activities in midlife were with associated with greater gray matter volume, whereas higher levels of complex activities in late life were associated with higher perfusion (Turpin et al., 2025). In kind, the authors attribute the effects of midlife engagement as a potential brain maintenance mechanism that guards against structural declines; in contrast they posit that the relationship between late life activities and brain perfusion may reflect a more resilience/reserve-like mechanism (Turpin et al., 2025). Although cross-sectional studies do not provide the means to rigorously test these potential mechanisms, they do have utility in providing the baseline of descriptive analyses needed to design future studies needed to sharpen our theoretical understanding.

To begin to address some of these gaps in the literature, the current study investigated the extent to which brain structure and tau burden measured in late life were associated with engagement in a variety of complex mental activities across the life span. To accomplish these aims, we asked participants to complete a multimodal neuroimaging protocol (structural MRI, tau PET) and a previously validated measure of complex mental activities across the life span (Valenzuela and Sachdev, 2007). We hypothesized that engaging in complex mental activities across the life span would be associated with lower levels of tau burden in the medial temporal lobe (i.e., entorhinal cortex, hippocampus) and that greater levels of engagement in complex mental activities would be positively associated with estimates of brain structure (gray matter volume, cortical thickness, LC integrity) measured in late life.

Methods

Participants

Participants were adults ages sixty and older recruited from the greater Boston metropolitan area through newspaper advertisements, flyers, direct mail, and research participant registries. Inclusion criteria for participation included English language proficiency, no lifetime history of neurological disorders, and a score ≥ 26 on the Mini Mental Status Exam (see Table 1 for descriptive information). Additional exclusionary criteria consisted of the following conditions: history of claustrophobia, metallic objects or implanted devices in the body deemed non-compatible with MRI, pacemakers or other implanted cardiac devices, physician-diagnosed heart disease, heart murmur, arrhythmia, or routine exposure to radiation. The sample consisted of 76 participants (42 females, 34 males; Mage= 69.4; 6 Asian, 7 Black or African American, 62 White, 1 more than one race; 1 Hispanic or Latinx; Table 1). A subset of these participants (N = 60) had MRI scans indexing the integrity of the LC; there were no demographic differences between this sub-sample and the full sample, ps > 0.721 (see Supplemental Material). These data were collected from January 2020 through November 2025. All experimental procedures were approved by the Brandeis University Institutional Review Board and the Partners Human Research Committee of Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) prior to data collection. Participants provided informed consent, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and were compensated for all study procedures.

Behavioral assessments

To examine the relationships between complex mental activities and indices of late life brain health, we used the combination of self-report measures and multimodal neuroimaging. A self-report questionnaire was used to measure complex mental activities across the life span. This construct was assessed using the Lifetime of Experiences Questionnaire (LEQ)(Valenzuela and Sachdev, 2007). A general descriptive summary of the LEQ data can be found in the Supplemental Material. Specifically, the LEQ assesses complex mental activities across three distinct time periods: early life (ages 13–30), midlife (ages 30–64) and late life (ages 65 +). For each life stage, the scale quantifies activity engagement with a “non-specific” score, which reflects frequency of engagement across a wide array of activities, including learning a new language, physical activity, travel, and artistic pursuits. Moreover, for each life stage, there is a “specific” score, which reflects educational attainment (early life), managerial and occupation history (midlife) and current social and intellectual activity (late life). The sum of all “specific” and “non-specific” scores yields the LEQ total score, which was used in our planned analyses. Sub-scores were used in exploratory analyses to examine the life stage-specific contributions towards late life brain health variables (e.g., gray matter volume, LC integrity, tau pathology).

Neuroimaging acquisition and processing

MRI acquisition and processing: gray matter volume and thickness

Scanning was performed on a Siemens 3 T Biograph mMR, capable of simultaneous PET-MRI acquisition. A T1-weighed MPRAGE scan was acquired for each participant (TR = 2530 ms, TE =1.69 ms, Flip angle = 7 °, 192 slices, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, FOV = 256 × 256 mm). Results included in this manuscript come from preprocessing performed using fMRIPrep 22.0.2 (Esteban et al., 2019) (RRID:SCR_016216), which is based on Nipype 1.8.5 (Gorgolewski et al., 2017) (RRID:SCR_002502). The following paragraph describes the key stages of fMRIPrep preprocessing used for the structural MRI processing.

The T1-weighted (T1w) image was corrected for intensity non-uniformity (INU) with N4BiasFieldCorrection (Tustison et al., 2010), distributed with ANTs 2.3.3 (Avants et al., 2008) (RRID:SCR_004757), and was used as T1w-reference throughout the workflow. The T1w-reference was then skull-stripped with a Nipype implementation of the antsBrainExtraction.sh workflow (from ANTs), using OASIS30ANTs as target template. Brain tissue segmentation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white-matter (WM) and gray-matter (GM) was performed on the brain-extracted T1w using fast (FSL 6.0.5.1, RRID:SCR_002823 (Zhang et al., 2001)). Brain surfaces were reconstructed using `recon-all` (FreeSurfer 7.2.0, RRID:SCR_001847, @fs_reconall), and the brain mask estimated previously was refined with a custom variation of the method to reconcile ANTs-derived and FreeSurfer-derived segmentations of the cortical gray matter of Mindboggle (RRID:SCR_002438, @mindboggle). Volume-based spatial normalization to one standard space (MNI152NLin2009cAsym) was performed through nonlinear registration with antsRegistration (ANTs 2.3.3), using brain-extracted versions of both T1w reference and the T1w template. The following template was selected for spatial normalization: ICBM 152 Nonlinear Asymmetrical template version 2009c [(Fonov et al., 2009) RRID: SCR_008796; TemplateFlow ID: MNI152NLin2009cAsym].

Regions-of-interest (ROIs) were obtained from the Desikan–Killiany atlas included as the default cortical parcellation within FreeSurfer (Fischl, 2012). Using the available anatomical delineations in the Desikan–Killiany atlas, ROIs were selected to approximate brain regions identified in prior work examining the relationships between brain structure and metrics of successful aging, including lifestyle factors (Gefen et al., 2015; Pezzoli et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2025). These included regions in the cingulate cortex: posterior cingulate [posterior midcingulate (pMCC], caudal anterior cingulate [anterior midcingulate (aMCC)], and rostral anterior cingulate [pregenual anterior cingulate (prACC)]. Gray matter volumes were adjusted for total intracranial volume using a covariance approach (Buckner et al., 2004) and averaged across hemispheres as no a priori effects of hemisphere were expected. Cortical thickness estimates included were averaged across hemispheres for each ROI.

MRI acquisition and processing: locus coeruleus integrity

To visualize the LC, a 2D T1-weighted turbo-spin-echo (TSE) sequence with additional magnetization transfer (MT) contrast was applied (voxel size = 0.5 × 0.5 × 3 mm, TR = 743 ms, TE = 15 ms, flip angle = 180°, six online averages). Each participant completed two LC-sensitive scans with the TSE-MT sequence. Signal intensities in the LC were extracted from each participant’s TSE-MT image using a previously published binarized LC mask (Dahl et al., 2022). Brainstem image registration proceeded in two main steps, consistent with prior work (Chen et al., 2023). First, each participant’s brainstem images were aligned to their native whole-brain images (Step 1). After the coregistration, the two LC-sensitive scans were then averaged for subsequent analysis (see Fig. 1A). Second, the whole-brain images were co-registered to MNI 0.5 mm linear space, in which the LC mask was defined, to obtain the transformation matrices (Step 2). Both registration steps used the “antsRegistration” function from ANTs 2.1.0. Step 1 involved linear registration (rigid followed by affine); Step 2 incorporated additional non-linear registration (SyN) to improve alignment with the MNI template. The transformation matrices obtained from Step 2 were then applied, in reverse, to warp the LC mask from MNI space back into each participant’s native brainstem image space using nearest-neighbor interpolation (Fig. 1B). A pontine reference mask consisting of two bilateral 4 × 4 mm regions was created for each participant (Fig. 1B). The dorsal boundary of this reference region was defined by shifting 8 mm ventrally from the dorsal edge of the LC region into the pons (Dahl et al., 2022). These masks were then used to extract signal intensities. To allow normalization and between-participant comparisons, the LC-MRI contrast ratio was calculated on each slice along the rostrocaudal axis of the LC using the following formula: LCratio=meanSLC-meanSRefmeanSRef MeanSLC denotes the mean signal intensity of the left or right LC and meanSRef indicates the mean signal intensity of the pontine reference region in the corresponding slice. In other words, the LC signal intensity was standardized using nearby tissue that does not contain neuromelanin (i.e., pontine reference region). To obtain more stable intensity estimates for subsequent correlation analyses, LC contrast ratios were averaged across hemispheres and slices within each participant.

Tau PET acquisition and processing

For the [18F]MK—6240 PET scan, participants received approximately 5 mCi of tracer (5.28 ± 0.37) and were scanned 90–110 min post-injection (4 × 5-minute frames) during the same scanning session in which the structural MRI data (cingulate cortical thickness and volume) were acquired. Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) images for MK—6240 were created by normalizing mean tracer uptake to the inferior cerebellar gray matter reference region (Harrison et al., 2023). Geometric transfer matrix partial volume correction (PVC) on FreeSurfer-derived ROIs was applied to account for partial volume effects (Rousset et al., 1998). The entorhinal cortex and hippocampal ROIs, defined by the Desikan-Killiany atlas, were specifically chosen to for the present study because these regions are some of the earliest sites of tau pathology in AD (Braak et al., 2011). Unlike first generation tau-PET tracers, MK—6240 shows limited off-target binding in nearby choroid plexus, ensuring an accurate estimation of hippocampal tau burden (Betthauser et al., 2019).

Analysis

The relationships between assessments of complex mental activities across the life span and late life brain structure and tau burden were quantified using linear models. We included age and sex as covariates in the linear models; age was entered as a continuous numeric variable and sex was entered as a dummy-coded variable (female = 0, male = 1). All LEQ variables (i.e., complex activities) were standardized prior to being entered into linear models. Years of education was not included as a covariate because it is a component of the LEQ; this is a common approach when modeling relationships between neuroimaging variables and the LEQ (Turpin et al., 2025). When the total score reflecting engagement in complex mental activities across the life span (i.e., LEQ total score) was identified as a significant predictor, we conducted additional exploratory analyses that examined the specific contribution of life stage scores towards late life brain health. For the exploratory analyses, we entered each of the life stage scores as an independent variable in a linear model describing a metric of late life brain health. To better parse the potential life stage-specific associations with late life brain health, we ran additional analyses that assessed whether the life stage scores differed significantly from each other in each model. In all the models described in this manuscript, the effects are reported as the estimate from the model with 95% confidence intervals and the corresponding test statistic and p-value and were not corrected for multiple comparisons. Model diagnostics, including a summary of variance inflation factors across all models, are described in the Supplemental Materials to ensure all models are robust to concerns of multicollinearity. The Supplemental Materials also include Bayesian tests of the Probability of Direction to provide a complementary lens from which to view the results by quantifying the probability that the observed effect is positive or negative (i.e., an index similar to a frequentist p-value). Data analysis was conducted in R version 4.3.1(Team, 2023) using the ‘easystats’ ecosystem (Lüdecke et al., 2020).

Results

Brain structure in the cingulate cortex is associated with engagement in complex mental activities

To better understand the potential associations between engagement in complex mental activities across the life span and brain health measured in late life, we sought to assess the extent to which the LEQ is related to metrics of brain structure in the cingulate cortex, a region consistently identified in studies of successful aging and engagement in lifestyle behaviors (Arenaza-Urquijo et al., 2019; Gefen et al., 2015; Pezzoli et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2025). Notably, we found that the life time engagement in complex mental activities as indexed by the LEQ total score, was positively associated with ICV-adjusted estimates of gray matter volume in the aMCC, B = 64.08 [13.31, 114.85], t = 2.52, p = 0.014 (Fig. 2A), and was observed at trend-level in the pMCC, B = 66.12 [−4.33, 136.58], t = 1.87, p = 0.065 (Fig. 2B). We did not observe associations between the LEQ total score and gray matter volume in the prACC, p > 0.181. These findings suggest that greater levels of engagement in complex mental activities across different life stages are associated with larger volumes in the cingulate cortex measured in late life. Follow-up exploratory analyses revealed that distinct life stage-specific behaviors may help to explain the positive association between the LEQ total score and gray matter volume in these regions. Namely, higher early life scores, a measure indexing both educational attainment and participation in a wide array of activities including learning a new language, physical activity, travel, and artistic pursuits from the ages of 13–30, were positively associated with gray matter volume in the pMCC, B = 72.89 [14.95, 130.84], t = 2.51, p = 0.014 and showed a trend-level effect in the aMCC, B = 72.50 [−10.06, 155.05], t = 1.75, p = 0.084 (Table 2). Neither midlife nor late life scores were associated with gray matter volume in the pMCC or aMCC, ps > 0.118 (Table 2). To further probe the specific associations between early life activities and gray matter volume in the pMCC and aMCC, we tested if the early, middle, and late life predictors significantly differed from each other in each model. In both the aMCC, p = 0.009, and the pMCC, p = 0.0654, we found that the early and late life scores tended to differ from each other; there were not reliable differences observed between the early and midlife scores in either region, ps > 0.658.

Consistent with the volumetric analyses described above, analyses examining the associations between complex mental activities and cortical thickness revealed positive associations with early life activities. Specifically, higher levels of complex mental activities across the life span (i.e., LEQ total score) were associated with greater cortical thickness in the aMCC, B = 0.07 [0.03, 0.10], t = 3.73, p < 0.001 (Fig. 2C) and the pMCC, B = 0.06 [0.03, 0.09], t = 4.05, p < 0.001 (Fig. 2D). There was not reliable evidence of the relationship between the LEQ total score and cortical thickness in the prACC, p > 0.110. Interestingly, when examining the contribution of the life stage scores on the cortical thickness estimates, early life stage participation in various lifestyle behaviors once again emerged as a significant predictor of brain structure in the cingulate cortex (BpMCC = 0.04 [0.00, 0.09], t = 2.05, p = 0.044; BaMCC = 0.05 [0.01, 0.09], t = 2.81, p = 0.006; Table 3). Midlife and late life scores were not associated with cortical thickness in either the pMCC or aMCC, ps > 0.124 (Table 3). Once again, we examined if the early, middle, and late life predictors significantly differed from each other in each model. We did not find clear evidence that these life stage scores differed from each other in either model, ps > 0.151, which suggests that the specific association between early life complex mental activities and late life cortical thickness in the cingulate cortex should be viewed more tentatively.

Locus coeruleus integrity is positively associated with engagement in complex mental activities

We next sought to test if the integrity of the LC was associated with engagement in complex mental activities across the life span. We found that higher LEQ total scores, reflecting greater levels of engagement in varied lifestyle behaviors across early, middle, and late life, were associated with greater LC integrity, quantified using LC-sensitive MRI sequences, B = 0.01 [0.00, 0.02], t = 3.13, p = 0.003 (Fig. 3). Interestingly, exploratory analyses examining the life stage specific scores revealed that late life activities, a metric comprised of engagement in late life social and intellectual activities and participation in a wide array of activities including learning a new language, physical activity, travel, and artistic pursuits, were associated the integrity of the LC, B = 0.01 [0.00, 0.02], t = 2.26, p = 0.028 (Table 4). Neither early life nor midlife scores were associated with LC integrity measured in late life, ps > 0.194 (Table 4). To test the robustness of this selective association between late life activities and late life LC integrity, we formally tested if the early, middle, and late life predictors significantly differed from each other in this model. We did not find evidence that the life stage predictors significantly differed from each other, ps > 0.527, which renders the specificity of the association between late life activities and LC integrity as suggestive, rather than definitive.

Tau pathology is not related to complex mental activities across the life span

Given the hypothesized importance of enriching activities in supporting brain health in older age and its potential in promoting resistance to pathology, we sought to examine the relationships between engagement in complex mental activities and tau pathology. More specifically, we examined the associations between sites of early tau pathology—entorhinal cortex and hippocampus—and the LEQ total score. We did not observe significant effects of lifetime engagement in complex activities, as measured via the LEQ total score, with tau pathology in either the entorhinal cortex, B = 0.05 [ 0.11, 0.22], t = 0.64, p = 0.525, or hippocampus, B = 0.03 [−0.02, 0.07], t = 1.25, p = 0.214. Put another way, we did not find reliable evidence that participation in complex mental activities across the life span is associated with estimates of tau pathology in cognitively normal older adults.

Discussion

An interest in supporting brain health in older adults has spurred research that examines the impact of enriching behaviors, such as engaging in complex mental activities, on measures of brain structure and function in late life. The overarching aim of this study was to expand upon this work by examining 1) whether engaging in various complex mental activities across early, middle, and late life is associated with gray matter volume and cortical thickness in the cingulate cortex, 2) if higher levels of engagement in complex mental activities was positively associated with the integrity of the LC, and 3) the extent to which greater engagement in complex mental activities across the life span is associated with burden in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus. We found clear and definitive support for two of our a priori hypotheses. Namely, we found that higher levels of engagement in complex mental activities across the life span is positively associated with structural brain indices in both the cingulate cortex and LC measured in late life. In contrast, we did not observe any reliable associations between engagement in complex mental activities and tau burden. Together, these results contribute to a growing narrative on the importance of both the cingulate cortex and LC towards brain health in older adults. Both the structure and function of the cingulate cortex and LC have been linked to better cognitive outcomes in older adults and indices of pathologic burden (Arenaza-Urquijo et al., 2019; Bueichekú et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023; Ciampa et al., 2022; Harrison et al., 2012; Pezzoli et al., 2023). The cingulate cortex has also been identified as a critical hub for cognitive effort-based decision-making, which also may help to explain its relationship in the current study with because participants must weigh the costs and benefits of engaging in these repeatedly in their daily lives (Crawford et al., 2024).

A notable aspect of the current study is that we found suggestive evidence that the effects of complex mental activities on late life brain structure in the cingulate cortex and LC were associated with distinct life periods. For the cingulate cortex, complex mental activities in early life, but not midlife or late life, were associated with both gray matter volume and cortical thickness. Although these findings should be viewed tentatively because early and late life scores did not significantly differ from each other across all models, they are consistent with many studies that demonstrate that early life education is associated with better brain health in older age (Ourry et al., 2024). Further, the results also bear a striking resemblance to the work describing “super agers”, which has identified patterns of successful brain and cognitive aging in the same cingulate ROIs (pMCC and aMCC) (Gefen et al., 2015). Intriguingly, in this previous work, postmortem stereologic quantification revealed that “super agers” had a higher density of Von Economo neurons in these cingulate ROIs relative to age-matched controls (Gefen et al., 2015). Although the functional significance of Von Economo neurons is not entirely known, it is hypothesized that they play a role in the selective vulnerability to neurodegenerative processes; the density of Von Economo neurons is lower in people diagnosed with AD relative to healthy controls (Gefen et al., 2018). Directly relevant to the findings of the current study, individual differences in the density of Von Economo neurons are thought to be conferred at birth and during post-natal stages of early cortical development (Gefen et al., 2018), which overlap with the early life period captured by the LEQ.

In contrast, we observed that the integrity of the LC was associated with engagement in complex mental activities in late life. Although additional work is needed to demonstrate a more definitive contribution of late life activities on LC integrity, the present data suggest a pattern that is consistent with theoretical work, which suggests that engaging in stimulating behaviors (e.g., social engagement, mental activities, novelty) has a neuroprotective effect through the downstream effects of the release of norepinephrine (Robertson, 2013). Indeed, recent empirical work supports this notion, revealing that greater levels of pathology in the LC were associated with lower levels of social function, which in turn, impacted cognition (Zide et al., 2024). The late life lifestyle items on the LEQ include measures of both social and intellectual activity, lending additional evidence to support the idea that these facets of late life lifestyles have the strongest relevance to LC structure and function. When viewed together, these findings suggest that engagement in complex mental activities at different life stages may have dissociable impacts on the neural mechanisms supporting brain health in older adults.

We did not find reliable evidence that engagement in complex mental activities across the life span is associated with estimates of tau burden. In other words, participation in various activities (e.g., mentally stimulating activities, learning a new language, physical activity) across the life span was not related to levels of tau pathology in either the entorhinal cortex or hippocampus. Although this result may be somewhat surprising, the literature is mixed when describing the associations between markers of pathology and complex mental activities (Ourry et al., 2024). Moreover, there is emerging evidence that successful cognitive and brain aging may be achieved by different, independent pathways; one pathway is associated with tau burden with effects on memory ability and hippocampal atrophy, whereas the other pathway is characterized by tau-independent structural preservation of the cingulate cortices, benefitting working memory processes (Pezzoli et al., 2025). As such, it is possible that participation in complex mental activities across the life span has a stronger influence on the latter pathway, rather than showing direct associations with tau burden. Indeed, this mechanistic framework is consistent with our findings, in which we found robust associations between complex mental activities and both gray matter volume and thickness in the cingulate cortex.

When viewed together, the present results help to shape our understanding of the potential neurobiological mechanisms that are related to a lifetime of engagement in complex mental activities. Namely, we found clear evidence that indices of brain structure in both the cingulate cortex and LC are reliably associated with enriching experiences across the life span. Although speculative, the mapping of specific life stage scores to cingulate and LC structure invites the possibility that early and late life activities, in particular, may play complementary roles in supporting brain health in late life. Consistent with prior work (Turpin et al., 2025), we observed associations between engagement in complex mental activities in early life periods with the volume and thickness of the cingulate cortices. This suggests that enriching activities and educational attainment may play a role in promoting brain maintenance in the cingulate cortex. In contrast, the association between late life activities and LC integrity is suggestive of a reserve-like mechanism. Alternatively, the relationship between late life activities and late life brain structure in the LC is also consistent with the ‘use it or lose it’ principle that has often been ascribed to engagement in mentally demanding activities (Bielak, 2010; Bielak and Gow, 2023) and LC function (Robertson, 2013). Nevertheless, it is also important to note that although we hypothesized that engagement in complex mental activities would be negatively associated with tau burden, consistent with a resistance-like mechanism, the relatively young and healthy nature of our sample may have precluded our ability to detect these effects. Future, longitudinal, work is needed to fully adjudicate these possibilities.

Although the current study was notable for its assessment of life span engagement in complex mental activities in conjunction with multimodal neuroimaging, future work will be well served to expand the types of neurobiological variables used understand the relationships between enriching activities and experiences and brain health in older adults. Chiefly, the present study did not include measures of amyloid-β pathology; this precludes our ability to understand the relationships between LEQ variables and AD. Lower LEQ scores are also observed in patients with Parkinson’s disease (Ophey et al., 2024); accordingly, studies that employ measures of amyloid-β, tau, and alpha synuclein will be best suited to parse general pathological versus disease-specific patterns. Relatedly, the present work provided some of the first empirical evidence for the role of the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine system and engagement in complex mental activities across the life span. Nevertheless, the current study is limited in its scope as it did not assess other neuromodulator systems. This is important because dopamine and acetylcholine both play in effort-based decision-making about mentally demanding actions (Crawford and Braver, 2026; Silvestrini et al., 2023). Monoaminergic and cholinergic systems also have strong relevance to normative and pathological aging (Berry and Harrison, 2023; Crawford and Berry, 2024). As such, it is imperative that future work can more deeply characterize the relationships between engagement in complex mental activities across the life span with multiple neuromodulator systems.

Beyond expanding the scope of neuroimaging assessments related to engagement in complex mental activities across the life span, it will be imperative for future studies to employ different kinds of methodological approaches to enhance the robustness and generalizability of this work. For example, future studies could use Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) to assess the extent to which metrics of brain health are associated with lifestyle behaviors measured directly in daily life contexts. Indeed, EMA holds promise for these types of investigations in that it helps to reduce retrospective response biases that are known to co-occur with age (Neubauer et al., 2020), of which retrospective self-report measures, like the LEQ used in the current study, are most susceptible. Repeated daily life assessments also provide the means to link cognitive and neural processes that cannot be gleaned from single assessments of lifestyle factors alone (Crawford et al., 2022; Crawford and Berry, 2024). Further, the sample in the present study was comprised of mainly white, highly educated participants; this limits the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, we do not have granular socioeconomic information for our participants, but this will be a critical factor to consider in future studies particularly because many of the activities included in the LEQ (e.g., world travel, time afforded to learn new skills and languages) may not be easily accessible to people with different socioeconomic backgrounds. Development of richer assessments of complex mental activities would greatly benefit the field. For example, a limitation of the LEQ is that it does not capture management roles within the family, household and community. Further, we did not have the ability to test how other individual differences (e.g., personality, health) are associated with LEQ scores, which will be important for future work to include. Moreover, our sensitivity to detect effects between metrics of brain health and complex mental activities could be improved with longitudinal assessments, in contrast to the cross-sectional assessments used in the present study. Prior work has found that higher levels of engagement in positive mentally stimulating activities are associated with less cognitive decline and less gray matter atrophy over the span of several years (Suo et al., 2012; Valenzuela et al., 2008). However, limited longitudinal evidence exists using multimodal neuroimaging to examine its linkages with participation in complex activities across the life span. As such, future work should aim to assess a diverse pool of participants using the combination of multimodal neuroimaging measures across time to better elucidate the linkages between these constructs.

Despite its limitations, the present study provides some of the first data leveraging multimodal neuroimaging measures indexing brain structure and tau burden to examine their relationships with lifetime engagement in complex mental activities We found that overall greater levels of engagement in lifestyle behaviors were associated with larger estimates of gray matter volume and cortical thickness in the cingulate cortex and greater LC integrity. In contrast, we did not observe associations between lifestyle behaviors and tau burden. Further, our findings provided suggestive evidence that early life lifestyle behaviors are more tightly linked with gray matter volume and thickness in the cingulate cortex, whereas late life lifestyle behaviors are more closely associated with the integrity of the LC. When viewed together, the results contribute to a growing body of work which suggests that activity participation at different life stages may have complementary benefits on brain health measured in late life (Turpin et al., 2025). As such, the present study helps to build on prior work and underscores a further need to continue to expand the methodological approaches used to study aging and complex mental activities to better understand their potential role in both healthy and pathological aging.
