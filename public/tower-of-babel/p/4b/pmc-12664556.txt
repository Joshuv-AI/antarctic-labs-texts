TNFR2 signaling shapes the sex-specific remyelinating properties of microglia after experimental stroke
Authors: Stefano Raffaele, Francesca Carolina Mannella, Estrid Thougaard, Pernille Vinther Nielsen, Morten Blickfeldt-Eckhardt, Eva Tolosa, Justine Münsterberg, Tim Magnus, Jens D. Mikkelsen, Mathias Gelderblom, Helle Hvilsted Nielsen, Bettina Hjelm Clausen, Roberta Brambilla, Marta Fumagalli, Kate Lykke Lambertsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microglial tumor necrosis factor receptor 2 (TNFR2) has emerged as a critical modulator of neuroinflammation and repair following ischemic stroke. Here, we investigated the sex-specific role of TNFR2 in regulating microglial responses during the acute and subacute phases after stroke. Using a conditional knockout model, we found that TNFR2 ablation in microglia exerted opposite effects on morphology, reactivity, and phagocytic capacity in female compared to male mice at day 5 post-stroke. In females, TNFR2 deletion impaired microglial reactivity and myelin debris clearance, reduced oligodendrocyte maturation, and worsened motor and cognitive recovery. In contrast, TNFR2 ablation in males produced milder and occasionally opposing effects, without affecting functional outcomes. Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid from stroke patients revealed a positive association between TNFR2 and microglial phagocytosis markers (TREM2, Galectin-3), particularly in females, which may be useful to track microglial pro-regenerative state during rehabilitation and therapy. These findings identify TNFR2 as a sex-dependent regulator of microglial function and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for enhancing post-stroke recovery, especially in women.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke, caused by an interruption of cerebral blood flow, remains a major cause of death and long-term disability worldwide [1]. Despite its high prevalence, current therapeutic options are limited by narrow time windows and a significant risk of hemorrhagic complications, underscoring the urgent need for novel strategies aimed at promoting neurorepair and functional recovery [2]. One major pathological consequence of ischemic injury is the loss of oligodendrocytes, the myelin-producing glial cells essential for axonal conduction and neuronal support. This loss results in myelination deficits that strongly correlate with poor neurological outcomes in stroke patients [3,4]. Therefore, promoting remyelination represents a promising therapeutic avenue to mitigate post-stroke deficits.

Compelling evidence from rodent models and analysis of human post-mortem tissue indicates that oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), which primarily reside in a quiescent state in the adult brain, rapidly respond to stroke-induced demyelination in an attempt to replenish lost oligodendrocytes. However, their spontaneous differentiation capacity is limited, leading to the accumulation of immature OPCs at lesion borders [5,6]. Impaired OPC maturation is thought to be driven by the post-stroke inflammatory microenvironment, shaped predominantly by brain-resident microglia [7]. Although microglia initially support myelin integrity, they undergo a temporal shift toward a dysfunctional phenotype characterized by heightened and dysregulated inflammation and reduced regenerative support, ultimately hindering OPC-mediated remyelination [8,9]. Understanding the molecular cues that promote the beneficial properties of microglia in the early post-stroke phase is critical to developing strategies that preserve myelin and foster endogenous repair.

A central modulator among these cues is the pleiotropic immunomodulatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor (TNF) [[10], [11], [12]]. TNF exists in two biologically active forms: soluble TNF (solTNF), which primarily signals through TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) and is associated with neurotoxic effects [13,14], and transmembrane TNF (tmTNF), which preferentially engages TNF receptor 2 (TNFR2) and exerts neuroprotective and regenerative functions [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. In cultured microglia, TNFR2 activation enhances phagocytosis, stimulates the release of trophic factors, and suppresses pro-inflammatory signaling [21,22]. Conversely, conditional deletion of TNFR2 in microglia exacerbates inflammation and myelin damage in models of autoimmune demyelination [17]. Our recent findings revealed that microglia-specific ablation of TNFR2 leads to sex-dependent alterations in microglial reactivity after ischemic stroke, reducing microglial density in females while increasing it in males, with associated changes in lesion size, cytokine levels, and leukocyte infiltration [18]. However, the extent to which TNFR2 signaling in microglia shapes their regenerative phenotype and consequently influences remyelination and functional outcomes following stroke, in a sex-specific manner, remains to be determined.

In this study, we used Cx3cr1CreER:Tnfrsf1bfl/fl mice to conditionally delete TNFR2 in microglia [17] and investigate its role in post-stroke myelin repair. We examined how TNFR2 signaling differentially affects microglial morphology, reactive state, and myelin debris clearance in female and male mice following ischemia. We then evaluated the impact of these sex-specific microglial responses on oligodendrocyte differentiation and disease-associated inflammatory state, and their consequences for motor and cognitive recovery. Finally, we analyzed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma samples from stroke patients and healthy donors to explore potential sex-dependent associations between TNFR2 levels and biomarkers of microglial reactivity, which may enable clinicians and healthcare personnel to track the neuroinflammatory response during rehabilitation and pharmacological therapy.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental procedures

Mice

In this study, 12–17 weeks old male and female conditional knockout mice with specific microglial TNFR2 ablation (Cx3cr1CreER:Tnfrsf1bfl/fl, hereafter referred to as Tnfrsf1bcKo) and control littermates (Tnfrsf1bfl/fl) expressing normal TNFR2 levels, have been used [17,18]. Breeding mice were transferred from the University of Miami, Miller School of Medicine (Miami, FL) to the University of Southern Denmark, Institute of Molecular Biology (Odense, DK), where a colony was established. All animal procedures adhered to Danish Veterinary and Food Administration guidelines (J. No. 2013–15-2934–00924 and J. No. 2019–15-0201–01620). Mice were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle, regulated temperature, and humidity, with unrestricted access to food and water. All experimental procedures complied with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [23], and efforts were made to minimize any potential pain or distress. Genotyping was conducted on DNA samples obtained from ear biopsies following established protocols, as previously described [17]. To induce recombination, male and female adult Tnfrsf1bcKo and Tnfrsf1bfl/fl mice received intraperitoneal injections of tamoxifen (1 ​mg/day, MP Biomedicals LLC, Roskilde, Denmark) dissolved in sunflower oil (Sigma-Aldrich, Søborg, Denmark) over five consecutive days. After this treatment, a 28-day waiting period allowed for tamoxifen clearance and the turnover of circulating TNFR2-expressing leukocytes. Validation of TNFR2 deletion in microglia of both female and male mice, with preserved expression in circulating monocytes, has been previously performed by qPCR and mesoscale assays [17,18].

Permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO)

Permanent occlusion of the left middle cerebral artery (pMCAO) was conducted according to the previously established protocol [18,24]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized using Hypnorm (fentanyl citrate 0.315 ​mg/ml and fluanisone 10 ​mg/ml, VetaPharma Ltd, Leeds, UK), Midazolam (5 ​mg/ml, Hameln Pharma, Hameln, Germany), and distilled water in a 1:1:2 ratio. A vertical incision was made in the skin between the ear and the orbit, followed by a dissection of the temporal muscle. A 1 ​mm cranial opening was created with a drill to expose the left MCA, which was then permanently occluded by coagulation using bipolar forceps. Following surgery, mice were sutured, given saline injections to prevent dehydration, and placed in a 28 ​°C recovery cabinet for 24 ​h. Buprenorphine hydrochloride (0.001 mg/20 ​g, Temgesic) was administered at 8-h intervals, beginning at the time of surgery, for effective pain management.

Behavioral tests

Following pMCAO, motor and cognitive functions were evaluated in both Tnfrsf1bcKo and Tnfrsf1bfl/fl mice through rotarod and Y-maze tests, respectively, by experimenters blinded to the genotype. Mice were given a minimum of one rest day between tests, and the specific intervals between trials for each assessment are detailed below. Baseline behavioral assessment has been performed, showing no differences between groups in both female and male mice prior to pMCAO [18].

Rotarod

The rotarod test was assessed on days 3 and 5 post-pMCAO to evaluate motor coordination, using an accelerating rotarod apparatus. In our experimental protocol, the rod rotation gradually increased in speed from 4 to 40 ​rpm over 5 ​min, and the time for the mouse to fall off was recorded [25]. Before testing, the animals underwent a one-week training period. Mice were tested in four trials with a 20-min interval. The study included 25 Tnfrsf1bfl/fl (14 males and 11 females) and 19 Tnfrsf1bcKo (12 males and 7 females).

Y-maze

The Y-maze test was performed on day 14 post-pMCAO to assess spatial working memory. Each mouse was placed at the center of a Y-shaped maze, and the entries in each arm were recorded over a 10-min session. An entry was considered valid when all four paws were inside an arm. The first two entries were excluded to eliminate bias from the mouse’s initial orientation in the maze. For each animal, the total number of arm entries (activity) and percentage of spontaneous alternation were calculated as previously described [9]. An alternation was defined as consecutive entries into all three arms in sequence. The study included 19 Tnfrsf1bfl/fl mice (8 males and 11 females) and 18 Tnfrsf1bcKo mice (8 males and 10 females).

Autoradiography

To collect fresh-frozen brain tissue, mice at 5 and 14 days post-pMCAO were deeply anesthetized using a combination of pentobarbital (200 ​mg/ml) and lidocaine (20 ​mg/ml). Afterward, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation and brains were dissected and snap-frozen with CO2 snow, then stored at −80 ​°C. Brains were sectioned into 30 ​μm-thick coronal slices, and parallel series were mounted on gelatin-coated slides. For the autoradiography analysis of translocator 18 ​kDa protein (TSPO) binding, coronal sections were obtained from two regions of each brain: one slide per mouse contained at least three sections at the striatal level (Bregma 0.74 ​mm), while the other had sections at the hippocampal level (Bregma −1.70 ​mm). Sections were excluded from further analysis if no lesion was visible, the lesion was very small, lacked high-intensity infarct areas, or if the section was damaged or unclear. TSPO binding was evaluated at 5 and 14 days post-pMCAO. At 5 days post-pMCAO, a total of 25 mice were analyzed: 11 Tnfrsf1bfl/fl mice (7 females and 4 males) and 14 Tnfrsf1bcKo mice (7 females and 7 males). At 14 days post-pMCAO, 22 mice were examined: 11 Tnfrsf1bfl/fl mice (7 females and 4 males) and 11 Tnfrsf1bcKo mice (7 females and 4 males). [3H]PBR28 (Tritec AG, Teufen, Switzerland) was used as the TSPO radioligand (specific activity of 78 ​Ci/mmol). Slides were first rinsed twice in pre-incubation buffer (50 ​mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, containing 0.5 ​% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in distilled water) for 10 ​min at room temperature. They were then incubated for 60 ​min in 800 ​μl of 50 ​mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 5 ​mM MgCl2, 140 ​mM NaCl, 2 ​mM EGTA, 0.5 ​% BSA, and 3 ​nM [3H]PBR28 per slide, while rotating at room temperature. After incubation, the slides were rinsed three times for 5 ​min each in 4 ​°C pre-incubation buffer, followed by a brief 10-s dip in 4 ​°C deionized water. Slides were air-dried in a fume hood for 45 ​min and then exposed overnight to paraformaldehyde (PFA). After PFA exposure, they were dried for an additional 30 ​min in a desiccator. The radioligand-bound brain sections were placed on a Fuji imaging plate (BAS-IP TR 2040, FujiFilm) alongside tritium standard slides (American Radiolabeled Chemicals, #ART-123) for 24 ​h at 4 ​°C. Autoradiography images were quantified after grayscale and area scanning using Image J software (NIH, Rockville, MD). For gray values, the data were standard-converted into values of fmol/mg tissue equivalent (TE). We were interested in measuring the intensity over a given threshold. The identification of the region of interest (ROI) and the quantification of the gray values were performed by an individual not aware of the genetic background of the mice and the coding of the slides [22]. First, we set the threshold that identified a lesion in all brains. For each animal, one level was chosen with the highest intensity of labeling of the identified high-intensity area.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on brain tissues from Tnfrsf1bcKo mice and Tnfrsf1bfl/fl littermates, euthanized at day 5 and day 14 post-pMCAO. A total of 5–6 mice per experimental group were used at each time point. Mice were deeply anesthetized using pentobarbital (200 ​mg/ml) combined with lidocaine (20 ​mg/ml) and then perfused transcardially with 0.01 ​M PBS, followed by perfusion with 4 ​% PFA in 0.01 ​M PBS. Dissected brains were post-fixed for 1 ​h in 4 ​% PFA and cryoprotected by immersion in a 30 ​% sucrose solution in 0.01 ​M PBS at 4 ​°C. Next, 20 ​μm-thick coronal sections were obtained using a cryostat and incubated overnight at 4 ​°C in 0.01 ​M PBS containing 5 ​% goat serum (Agilent, Glostrup, Denmark) or 5 ​% donkey serum (Euroclone, Pero, Italy) and 0.3 ​% Triton X-100 (Merck, Milan, Italy). Image acquisition and quantitative analyses

Images of the peri-infarct region (0–500 ​μm from the boundary of the ischemic lesion) were acquired at 20X magnification using a confocal microscope (6-μm z-stack merged from 2-μm intervals; A1R, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and a fluorescence microscope (BX53 equipped with a DP73 camera, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Image analysis was performed by an investigator blinded to the genotypes using Fiji/ImageJ software on three slices per mouse, spanning from −1.00 to 0.00 ​mm relative to bregma, as described previously [9], [18]. Cells positive for Iba1, Iba1 and Trem2, Iba1 and Gal-3, NG2, GPR17, GSTπ, Olig2 and Serpina3n, and CD11b and FMy were manually counted using the “cell counter” tool and normalized to the total area analyzed to calculate cell density. For the evaluation of myelin debris clearance, three distinct subtypes of CD11b+FMy+ cells were analyzed: “touching” cells, characterized by small inclusions within their terminal branches; “hungry” cells, identified by large inclusions within the cell body; and “foamy” cells, which are completely engulfed with myelin. For morphological analysis of Iba1-positive cells, the “particle analysis” and “skeleton” tools in Fiji/ImageJ were utilized. The Iba1 signal was converted to binary images, and a minimum particle size of 75 ​μm was applied to exclude incomplete cells and debris. Quantitative parameters, including cell size, branch count, and junctions per Iba1+ cell, were measured [26]. To evaluate demyelination after ischemic injury, densitometric analysis was conducted on brain sections stained for MBP. Images were analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ: the red fluorescent signal was converted to binary images, the “threshold” plugin was applied, and the signal was quantified. The region of interest (ROI) was defined, and the percentage of the area positive for MBP staining was measured.

Human CSF and plasma collection

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from ischemic stroke cases were obtained at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf, Germany. All procedures were approved by the local ethics committee at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf (PV5340), and informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives. Lumbar puncture was performed within the first 3 days after stroke as part of the regular diagnostic procedures [18]. For comparison, CSF samples from healthy donors were collected after at least 2 ​h of fasting at Odense University Hospital, Denmark. Ethical approval was granted by the local research ethics committee (The Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark: J. no. S-20160173), and informed written consent was obtained from all participants. Pain-free individuals with no neurological, infectious, malignant, psychiatric, or immunological diseases or substance abuse were included [27]. After extraction, CSF was centrifuged, and the cell-free supernatants were collected, aliquoted, and frozen at −80 ​°C until use. Clinical data of CSF donors are included in Supplementary Table 1.

Venous blood samples were obtained from ischemic stroke patients at admission, as well as from healthy volunteer controls, at Odense University Hospital (OUH), Denmark, as described [[27], [28], [29]]. Briefly, blood was collected in 4-mL vacutainers and EDTA tubes, followed by centrifugation at 2000 ​g for 10 ​min. Plasma samples were then aliquoted and stored at −80 ​°C until analysis. Ethical approval was granted by the local research ethics committee (The Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark: J. No. S-20160152G, J. No. S-20160037, J. No. S-20080042, and J. No. S-20160173), and the study was reported to the Data Protection Agency. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants, and clinical data of blood donors are included in Supplementary Table 2.

Electrochemiluminescence and ELISA assays

Electrochemiluminescence assays were used to quantify the protein levels of TNFR2 and Galectin-3 (Gal-3) in human CSF and plasma samples. TNFR2 levels were originally quantified using the Human TNFRII Ultra-Sensitive Kit (Mesoscale Discovery), and data included in our previous work [18,[27], [28], [29]] have been reanalyzed here to explore sex differences and correlations with phagocytosis markers. Gal-3 levels were analyzed using the R-PLEX Human Galectin-3 Assay (Mesoscale Discovery). Measurement was performed on a SECTOR Imager 6000 Plate Reader (Mesoscale Discovery). Samples with coefficient of variation (CV) values ​> ​25 ​% in individual analyses were excluded. The lower limit of detection (LLOD) was a calculated concentration-based on a signal 2.5 standard deviations (SD) above the blank (zero) calibrator.

A commercial ELISA kit was used for sTREM2 detection (Cat. No. EH464RB; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 10 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ​± ​standard error of the mean (SEM). The Gaussian distribution of values within each experimental group was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test for sample sizes less than 8 and the D’Agostino-Pearson test for larger sample sizes (n ​> ​8). For comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data, an unpaired Student’s t-test was applied, whereas nonparametric data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Correlation analysis has been performed using the Spearman test. Outliers were identified and excluded using the Robust Regression and Outlier Removal (ROUT) test (Q ​= ​1) in GraphPad Prism. Statistical significance was defined as p ​< ​0.05. Specific details regarding the experimental groups and the statistical tests used are provided in the figure legends.

Results

Ablation of TNFR2 has opposite sex-specific effects on microglial morphology and TSPO binding in the early phase after experimental stroke

In our previous work, we observed opposite effects of TNFR2 ablation on microglial density in the peri-lesion area of female and male mice at day 5 post-pMCAO, suggesting that microglia reactivity in the early phase after stroke may be regulated by TNFR2 signaling in a sex-specific manner [18]. To verify this hypothesis, we performed a detailed morphometric analysis of Iba1+ microglia in the peri-infarct area of female and male mice at day 5 post-pMCAO (Fig. 1A–J), providing valuable hints on the reactive state of these cells [30]. In female Tnfrsf1bcKo mice compared to controls, we observed a significant reduction in the density of Iba1+ cells, which displayed a significantly smaller cell size and a lower number of branches and junctions (Fig. 1A–E). Conversely, TNFR2 ablation in males led to a significant increase in the density of Iba1+ cells, which were bigger in size but did not show differences in their branching compared to controls (Fig. 1F–J). To further explore the effects of TNFR2 ablation on microglial responses during the early stage after stroke, we performed ex vivo autoradiography studies using a [3H]PBR28 radioligand to TSPO, a mitochondrial protein reflecting microglial reactivity [31,32]. The autoradiograms revealed a strong binding in the choroid plexus, in the meninges and in the ventricular epithelium, whereas the level of binding in the neuronal tissue was low. A strong and intense binding was observed in all animals with ischemic lesions of the cerebral cortex (Fig. 1K–M). Interestingly, microglial TNFR2 ablation in females resulted in a significant reduction of TSPO binding intensity within the lesion compared to control at day 5 post-pMCAo, while no differences were found in males (Fig. 1L–N).

Ablation of TNFR2 dampens microglial phagocytic state in females but not in males at the early phase after stroke

A key process by which microglia regulate remyelination is the clearance of dead cells and debris from the lesion site [8,33]. To explore the impact of TNFR2 ablation on microglial phagocytic state in the peri-lesion area at day 5 post-pMCAO, we evaluated the co-localization of specific phagocytosis regulators in Iba1+ cells [34]: TREM2, an innate immune receptor involved in microglial lipid recognition, uptake, and degradation [[35], [36], [37], [38]]; Gal-3, a β-galactoside-binding lectin that regulates cytoskeleton dynamics during phagocytosis [[39], [40], [41]]; CD16/32 (also known as FcγRIII and FcγRII, respectively), two low-affinity Fc gamma receptors regulating microglial inflammatory signaling and antibody-mediated internalization of opsonized material [42]. Interestingly, in female Tnfrsf1bcKo mice compared to controls, we observed a dramatic reduction in microglial cells expressing TREM2 and Gal-3 at day 5 post-pMCAO, while no changes were observed in CD16/32-expressing cells (Fig. 2A–D). Conversely, microglia expressing TREM2 and Gal-3 were not affected by the loss of TNFR2 in males, which instead caused a significant reduction of CD16/32 expression (Fig. 2E–H). Ablation of TNFR2 limits myelin debris uptake by microglia in females only early after stroke

The presence of myelin debris represents a strong inhibitory signal limiting OPC recruitment at ischemic lesions and subsequent remyelination. Damaged myelin clearance by microglia is therefore essential to set the stage for efficient myelin repair by OPCs [34]. This process is mainly composed by three consecutive steps: (i) sensing of myelin debris by microglial cells that display a “touching” phenotype, with debris clearly visible only at branch endpoints; (ii) internalization of myelin debris by “hungry” microglia, with debris detectable inside branches and in developing phagosomes; (iii) degradation of myelin debris by “foamy” microglia, characterized by the presence of mature phagosomes and absence of debris in cell branches [43]. Here, we used CD11b and fluoromyelin (FMy) immunofluorescence to evaluate the impact of TNFR2 ablation on microglial clearance of myelin debris at day 5 post-pMCAO. We then quantified the internalization of FMy+ particles inside CD11b+ cells in the peri-lesion area by confocal microscopy. In TNFR2-ablated female mice compared to controls, we observed a significant decrease in the percentage of CD11b+ cells engulfing FMy+ myelin debris and a concomitant increase in non-phagocytic CD11b+ cells without internalized myelin material (Fig. 3A–C). We then focused on the different steps of myelin internalization by microglia, observing that TNFR2 deletion induced a trend toward a decrease in the percentage of “touching” microglia and a significant decrease in the fraction of “hungry” microglia, whereas “foamy” microglia showed a trend toward increase, albeit not significant (Fig. 3D–F). Conversely, in male mice, loss of microglial TNFR2 had no significant effects on the overall percentage of microglia engulfing myelin debris and the corresponding fraction of non-phagocytic cells (Fig. 3G–I). When analyzing the different stages of myelin internalization in male mice, we observed limited effects compared to females, with TNFR2 deletion producing a significant reduction in the percentage of “foamy” cells compared to controls, without affecting the fractions of “touching” and “hungry” microglia (Fig. 3J–L). Ablation of TNFR2 does not affect microglia reactivity at the late phase after stroke

Microglial beneficial states observed at early stages after stroke are progressively lost during disease progression, giving rise to dystrophic microglia that become prevalent during later phases and are believed to hamper efficient myelin repair [9,44]. We thus evaluated the impact of TNFR2 ablation on microglia reactivity in female and male mice at day 14 post-pMCAO (Supplementary Fig. 1). In females, we observed no effects of TNFR2 ablation on the densities of Iba1+ and reactive Iba1+CD68+ cells, nor in the size, circularity, and branching of Iba1+ cells compared to control (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Conversely, in TNFR2-deficient male mice, we found a significant decrease in Iba1+CD68+ cell density compared to controls, whereas Iba1+ cell density and morphological parameters were not affected (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Autoradiography assays revealed no differences in TSPO binding in either female or male microglial TNFR2-deficient mice compared to their respective sex-matched controls (Supplementary Fig. 1C and D). Finally, quantification of FMy+ myelin debris uptake by CD11b+ cells revealed no effects of TNFR2 ablation on myelin phagocytosis by microglia in both female and male mice at day 14 post-pMCAO (Supplementary Fig. 1E and F). Hence, these results indicate that TNFR2 signaling is not significantly implicated in regulating microglial reactive state in later stage after stroke.

Ablation of microglial TNFR2 inhibits oligodendrocyte maturation and fosters disease-associated inflammatory oligodendrocyte state in females but not males at the late phase after stroke

Conversely, ablation of microglial TNFR2 in males did not result in any changes in oligodendrocyte maturation stage, myelin integrity, or immune-inflammatory state (Fig. 4G–L). Ablation of microglial TNFR2 worsens functional recovery after stroke in females only

The effects of microglial TNFR2 ablation on motor and cognitive recovery after stroke were assessed by rotarod and Y-maze tests, respectively. Discussion

This study identifies microglial TNFR2 signaling as a key regulator of post-stroke remyelination and functional recovery, with a striking sex-specific profile. Using conditional knockout models, we demonstrate that loss of TNFR2 in microglia impairs debris clearance, disrupts oligodendrocyte differentiation and remyelination, and hinders long-term behavioral recovery in female, but not male, mice after focal cerebral ischemia. These findings highlight a previously unrecognized, sexually dimorphic role for TNFR2 in orchestrating the reparative immune responses necessary for central nervous system (CNS) repair following stroke.

We observed that female microglia lacking TNFR2 fail to adequately adopt a phagocytic phenotype in the subacute phase post-stroke. This is supported by reduced expression of TREM2 and Gal-3, two molecules essential for lipid sensing and phagocytosis [35,40], and confirmed by impaired uptake of myelin debris. These data are in line with previous work, showing downregulation of phagocytosis genes, including Trem2, in Tnfrsf1bcKo mice subjected to experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) compared to controls [17]. Accordingly, TNFR2 deficient primary microglia displayed Trem2 downregulation and phagocytosis deficits, whereas TNFR2 activation with the agonistic compound NewSTAR2 significantly enhanced microglial phagocytic capacity in vitro [17,22]. Microglial TNFR2 may therefore represent a promising target to enhance phagocytosis at early stages after stroke in females, setting the stage for subsequent tissue remodeling.

Microglial reactive states dynamically change over time after stroke, shifting from early beneficial properties toward dysfunctional traits that are prevalent at late stages and contribute to hindering recovery [8,9]. Interestingly, here we found that while TNFR2 ablation had a significant impact on microglial reactivity at an early stage after stroke, it had no effects at later stages. This time-dependent pattern fully overlaps with gene expression data, showing a peak of TNFR2 levels at day 5 post-pMCAO, followed by progressive downregulation at later time points [48]. Moreover, recently published pseudotime trajectory analysis of single-cell RNAseq data suggested that homeostatic microglia can progress toward microglial states identified either at early or late stages after stroke. However, this approach was not able to predict a direct trajectory from early to late microglial states after stroke [44]. On this basis, we hypothesize that TNFR2 signaling may be crucial to drive pro-regenerative microglial functions at an early stage after stroke in females, while its relevance may be minor or less important at later stages. Since even transitory changes in microglial reactivity may be relevant for long-term outcome, these data aid in defining the best therapeutic window for TNFR2-targeted interventions.

A critical consequence of disrupted microglial phagocytosis is the failure to effectively clear myelin debris, which are known to inhibit remyelination [34]. In female TNFR2-deficient mice, impaired myelin clearance at an early stage after stroke was reflected by subsequent remyelination defects, as indicated by a higher density of immature OPCs coupled with a reduction of mature oligodendrocytes and myelin integrity. Our data agree with a recent work performed using mouse models of demyelination, showing that microglial TNFR2 ablation impairs myelin repair, whereas deletion of pro-inflammatory TNFR1 in microglia worsens myelin damage and oligodendrocyte injury [49]. Additionally, the upregulation of Serpina3n in oligodendrocyte lineage cells of microglial TNFR2-deficient mice may reflect a switch from a myelinating phenotype toward a maladaptive, immune-inflammatory state that further impairs tissue repair and sustains neurodegeneration [47]. At the behavioral level, histological and molecular deficits translated into impaired motor and cognitive recovery in TNFR2-deficient female mice, which displayed persistent deficits in fine motor coordination and spatial working memory, suggestive of disrupted white matter repair. Hence, these data suggest that TNFR2-expressing microglia support remyelination and functional recovery not simply by modulating inflammation, but by directly shaping the lesion environment that instructs OPC fate.

Compared to females, TNFR2 deletion in males had overall milder or no effects on microglial reactive state after stroke, which ultimately did not compromise microglial phagocytosis and myelin uptake. Accordingly, we did not observe any change in oligodendrocyte lineage cells and behavioral readouts after microglial TNFR2 ablation in males. These data reinforce the link between impaired myelin clearance, myelination defects, and worse functional outcome that we reported in female TNFR2-deficient mice. Moreover, they suggest that pro-regenerative TNFR2 signaling may be less relevant for microglial states in males, or that compensatory pathways may buffer against the loss of TNFR2 in the male microglial milieu. This is consistent with current literature indicating sexually divergent transcriptional and epigenetic microglial programs across development and disease, indicating a prevalent pro-inflammatory state in male microglia compared to their female counterpart [[50], [51], [52]]. The sex-specificity of our findings reinforces the need to systematically account for sex as a biological variable in neuroimmunology. Given the comparable levels of microglial TNFR2 expression between females and males after stroke [18], the molecular basis for this dimorphism remains to be fully elucidated. Nevertheless, recent findings further support the possibility of sex-specific roles of glial TNFR2. Astrocyte-specific TNFR2 deletion was found to disrupt hippocampal synaptic function and plasticity, with male mice showing more severe molecular and functional deficits compared to females, suggesting that TNFR2 signaling in astrocytes is regulated differently in males and females [53]. Combined with our data on microglial TNFR2, this highlights a broader sex-dependent regulation of glial TNFR2 signaling. Interestingly, we observed higher TSPO binding levels in females compared to males, consistent with earlier findings from our group [53]. While the design of our analysis, where male and female samples were processed on separate plates, prevents us from drawing firm conclusions on sex differences in TSPO binding, our results align with independent reports that also describe higher TSPO levels in females [54,55]. Since TSPO expression has been associated with the phagocytic phenotype of microglia [56], these observations may point to sex-specific differences in microglial reactive state in the post-stroke brain, in agreement with our histological analyses. These differences may be driven by epigenetic mechanisms, possibly modulated by sex hormones, sex chromosome-linked gene expression, or differences in microglial developmental trajectories affecting TNFR2 expression or downstream pathways [52,57,58]. Accordingly, our previous findings, demonstrating a sex-dependent regulation of Akt phosphorylation in microglial TNFR2-deficient mice [18], suggest that divergent TNFR2 signaling pathway dynamics may underlie the opposite microglial responses we observed after stroke. Future studies using transcriptomic and epigenomic profiling may further unravel these mechanisms.

These findings have significant translational implications, highlighting TNFR2 as a potential therapeutic target to enhance post-stroke repair, particularly in women. Since current stroke therapies remain neuroprotective in nature, with few strategies aimed at promoting repair and remyelination, enhancing TNFR2 signaling in microglia could represent a novel approach to foster tissue regeneration and functional recovery. Our human data lend support to the translational relevance of these mechanisms, as elevated TNFR2 levels in the CSF, but not in plasma, of female stroke patients and their correlation with Gal-3 and TREM2 suggest that this pathway is active in the human CNS and may serve as a biomarker for reparative immune responses. However, although our clinical biomarker findings are suggestive, they are correlational in nature; future longitudinal studies linking TNFR2 dynamics in the CSF to imaging and recovery metrics are warranted.

In summary, our study reveals a critical and sex-specific role for microglial TNFR2 in coordinating myelin repair and functional recovery after stroke. These findings expand the reparative landscape of TNF signaling and open new avenues for targeted, immune-based therapies tailored by sex.
