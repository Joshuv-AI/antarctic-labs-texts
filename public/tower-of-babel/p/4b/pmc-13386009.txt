Histological evaluation of clinically defined atrial cardiomyopathy stages and their association with atrial fibrillation burden under continuous monitoring for a 2.5-year follow-up
Authors: Joris Winters, Michal Kawczynski, Martijn Gilbers, Aaron Isaacs, Stef Zeemering, Zarina Habibi, Michiel Rienstra, Isabelle van Gelder, Bart Maesen, Elham Bidar, Sander Verheule, Ulrich Schotten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsAtrial cardiomyopathy (AtCM) is associated with atrial fibrillation (AF). While histological studies describe underlying mechanisms of AtCM, these insights remain disconnected from clinical AtCM. This study determines the histological basis of non-invasive AtCM markers and assess their prognostic value in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.Methods and resultsLeft and right (LA/RA) atrial tissue samples were obtained in patients undergoing cardiac surgery (n = 136) in the RACE V Tissue Bank study. Pre-operative rhythm history, digital ECG, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and biomarker levels were used to group patients in AtCM stages: AF: history of paroxysmal, persistent or permanent AF, Severe (LAVi>50 mL/m2 or LAEF<35%), Moderate (LAVi 34–50 mL/m2, or LAEF 35–50% AND NT-proBNP >250 pg/mL), Mild (no AF, LAVi≤34 mL/m2 AND P-terminal Force V1 > 5 mV*ms OR P wave duration > 120 ms), and no AtCM. Tissue was stained (WGA/CD31/Vimentin) to quantify fibrosis, fibroblast density, myocyte diameter and vascularization. Post-operative rhythm was monitored continuously (2.5 years) using implantable loop recorders. Patients with severe AtCM had extended endomysial fibrosis (LA: +0.82µm, pFDR,LA < 0.001; RA: +0.64µm, pFDR,RA = 0.010) and larger left atrial cardiomyocytes (LA: +0.92µm, pFDR,LA = 0.026). Moderate AtCM patients showed LA myocyte hypertrophy (LA: +1.35µm, pFDR,LA = 0.007). Mild AtCM patients had similar histological features to patients without AtCM. Moderate (β = 3.56, pFDR = 0.045) and severe (β = 3.74, pFDR = 0.034) AtCM patients had a higher AF burden late after cardiac surgery.ConclusionClinical AtCM markers reflect pro-fibrotic and pro-hypertrophic remodelling in the moderate to severe AtCM stages and are associated with a higher AF burden late after surgery.

Introduction

Many cardiovascular pathologies functionally, mechanically, or structurally impair heart function, including the occurrence of supraventricular arrhythmia (e.g. atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter), heart failure, and valvular dysfunction. Impaired function of the atria associates with the development of atrial fibrillation (AF) and increased stroke risk.1

Timely and non-invasive detection of atrial cardiomyopathy (AtCM) is therefore highly desirable.2–4 The term atrial cardiomyopathy (AtCM) is an umbrella term encompassing all changes in the atrial myocardium, including contractile, electrical, macroscopic (i.e. atrial dilation, hypertrophy, valvular dysfunction) and microscopic (i.e. fibrosis, cellular hypertrophy, amyloidosis, vascular remodelling, fat and immune cell infiltration) structural properties of the atria.5 Recently, a non-invasive, clinical classification systems for AtCM was suggested,6,7 based on serum biomarkers, atrial geometry, functional features and electrophysiological changes reflected in the ECG.6 AtCM stages progress from mild (subclinical, detected by electrophysiological changes on the ECG), to moderate (clinically manifested, detected by atrial mechanical dysfunction and/or elevated NT-proBNP/ANP), to severe (atrial failure, detected by impaired atrial contractility or atrial enlargement). Largely disconnected from this process, histological features of diseased atria informing on its mechanisms were previously described.6,8–15 The aim of this study was to bridge this gap by assessing associations between clinical stages of AtCM criteria and histological features in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, thus integrating two previously separate research fields. In addition, the associations between the different stages of AtCM with post-operative AF burden were investigated.

Methods

Study population and tissue sampling

Human left and right atrial appendage samples (LA: n = 121, RA: n = 120) were collected from 136 patients enrolled in the prospective Race V Tissue Bank Study (clinicaltrials.gov ID NCT03124576) at the Maastricht University Medical Centre (MUMC+).16 Patients underwent open-chest or thoracoscopic surgery: coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), and/or mitral valve (MVS), or aortic valve surgery (AVS). The primary analyses focused on patients without a history of AF at baseline, to investigate the relationship between atrial substrate and incident AF development during follow-up. Patients with a history of AF were analyzed as a separate group. These patients underwent concomitant surgical ablation according to standard clinical practice and the expertise of the treating physician. Patients with prior surgical ablation for cardiac arrhythmias, or with severely limited life expectancy (<2.5 years) were excluded from the study.

Prior to surgery, patients underwent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), standard 12-lead 10s electrocardiogram (ECG) measurement (MuseTM, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, USA) and blood was drawn to measure a panel of circulating biomarkers (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland).17 Post-operative rhythm was continuously monitored for 2.5 years using implantable loop recorders (Reveal link, Medronic, Milwaukee, USA) implanted during surgery. All analyses complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki governing the use of human tissue samples and were approved by the local medical ethical committee. Data will be made available upon reasonable request and within legal limits.

TTE

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was performed within 6 months prior to surgery (EpiQ, Philips Healthcare, Netherlands). Systolic and diastolic left ventricular volumes were estimated using the biplane Simpson method. Atrial volumes (LAVi, RAVi) were measured in 4-chamber and 2-chamber views using the biplane arc-length method, indexed for body surface area. Atrial total ejection fraction (LATEF/RATEF) and atrial booster-pump function (LAAEF/RAAEF) were manually calculated in 4-chamber and 2-chamber recordings retrospectively, and valvular function was assessed.

ECG

Electrocardiographic parameters were determined from digital 12-lead 10s ECGs recorded at admission. Routine ECG parameters included PR-interval, P-wave duration (PwD), PQ-time, QRS-duration, QT and RR-interval (MuseTM, GE Medical systems, USA). Additionally, P-terminal force in V1 (PTFV1) and P-wave morphology, amplitude and spatio-temporal variability were assessed on signal-averaged P-waves using custom-made MATLAB algorithms, as previously described.18

Non-invasive classification of AtCM

Data obtained from non-invasive measurements were used to classify patients into AtCM stages (Table 1), as suggested in the recent consensus article by Goette et al.6 Given the existing evidence for structural remodelling in AF, patients with a history of AF were included as a separate stage group.

Clinical and histological features of patients by AtCM stage

Pre-operative patient characteristics, transthoracic echo (TTE) data, ECG, medication use and blood biomarkers were compared by AtCM stage. Variables are presented as means ± standard deviation (continuous variables) or as absolute values and proportions (%, categorical variables). Normality of traits was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilks test and visual inspection of histograms and QQ-plots. Normal traits were compared using ANOVA and TukeyHSD posthoc testing (continuous data) or chi2 test (categorical data). Non-continuous data were tested using the non-parametric Kruskall-Wallis and Wilcox Rank-sum test (continuous). For these explorative univariable analyses, a standard significance threshold of α = 0.05 was considered, without multiple testing adjustment.

Histological assessment of atrial specimen

Left and/or right atrial appendage tissue samples were collected from 136 patients (LA: n = 121, RA, n = 120) and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cryosections (6 μm) were stained with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated Wheat Germ agglutinin (WGA 1/200, 120 min, Thermo Fisher W11262), mouse anti-CD31 (1/50, o/n, Abcam ab9498) and mouse monoclonal anti-vimentin (1/150, o/n, Abcam ab92547), as described previously.19 Prior to staining, antigens were fixed in aceton and blocking solution (2 m/V% fraction V BSA + 0.3M glycine) was applied to block non-specific binding sites. For each sample, at least 3 non-overlapping randomly chosen areas were photographed at 400× magnification (Leica DM4B microscope with MC170HD camera).

Images were processed and analyzed automatically using the JavaCyte algorithm described previously.19 Total extracellular matrix fraction (total ECM) was calculated as a fraction of WGA-positive pixels. Distance between each pair of neighbouring cardiomyocytes was determined on WGA images. Per sample, median inter-cardiomyocyte distance was used as a measure of endomysial fibrosis. Fibroblast/cardiomyocyte ratios were determined on vimentin-positive images. Cardiomyocyte size was determined by measuring the median minimal Feret diameter per sample. For each sample, vascularization was assessed by measuring capillary/cardiomyocyte ratio and capillary size (median minimal Feret diameter).

Monitoring post-operative rhythm

A Reveal LinQTM (Medtronic Minneapolis, MN, USA) loop recorder was inserted peri-operatively at the left parasternal area. Post-operative rhythm was continuously monitored for 2.5 years using a remote CareLink® monitoring system. All post-operative AF (POAF) episodes of >2 min were recorded.16

Only verified episodes captured on an ECG recorded by the implantable loop recorder (ILR) and confirmed by the investigators or treating cardiologist were included. Early-POAF was defined as any ILR-detected AF-episode within the first 30 days following surgery. AF episodes occurring beyond 30 days were considered late AF during follow-up after surgery, reflecting a transition from transient post-operative triggers to sustained arrhythmogenic substrate. We opted for a 30-day cut-off based on prior results of the RACE V registry, where episodes recorded after the first post-operative month were significantly more predictive for long-term AF.20 In agreement with recent ESC consensus statements, AF burden was defined as the percentage of time spent in AF during the early (first 30 days) and late (beyond 30 days) monitoring period.21 AF episodes detected during follow-up were managed according to standard clinical care. No protocol-driven rhythm control strategy was imposed as part of this study, and treatment decisions, including the use of antiarrhythmic drugs, electrical cardioversion, or catheter ablation, were made at the discretion of the treating physician. Importantly, the primary analyses focused on patients without a history of AF at baseline, and AF episodes detected during follow-up therefore represent incident AF rather than recurrence of a pre-existing rhythm disorder.

Statistical analyses

Variables are presented as means ± standard deviation (continuous variables) or as absolute values and proportions (%, categorical variables). Normality of traits was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilks test and visual inspection of histograms and QQ-plots. Clinincal and histological differences per AtCM stage where first tested univariably using ANOVA with TukeyHSD posthoc test (normal, continuous), Kruskal-Wallis with Wilcox posthoc test (non-parametric, continuous), or chi2 test (categorical). Univariable analyses were done using a standard significance threshold (α = 0.05). Given the explorative nature, no multiple testing adjustment was done.

Next, based on univariate results, histological differences between AtCM stages were tested using multiple regression models adjusted for age, sex and surgery indication. We did not adjust for heart failure in our regression models, as heart failure is a major upstream determinant of atrial cardiomyopathy and shares defining features with its staging criteria, including NT-proBNP levels and atrial functional parameters. Adjusting for heart failure would risk overadjustment and underestimation of the total association between clinical AtCM stages and histological remodelling or AF burden. To account for repetitive testing, FDR-adjusted P-values were calculated from linear models and post-hoc contrasts using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Spearman partial correlation tests were performed to establish individual correlations between non-invasive markers (TTE/ECG/biomarkers) and histological traits, adjusting for age, sex and surgery indication.

Left-right histological differences were assessed in LAA/RAA pairs (n = 91) using paired t-testing. ANOVA was used to assess between group differences between LA and RA endomysial fibrosis and myocyte diameter.

To assess the predictive value of AtCM stage for the occurrence of AF after surgery, survival analyses were performed using Cox-proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex and surgery indication. Data were presented in Kaplan-Meier curves, FDR-adjusted P-values are reported using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. A generalized linear model (GLM) was used to assess the relationship between AtCM stages and atrial arrhythmia burden (log-transformed), adjusted for age, sex and surgery indication. All analyses and plots were generated using the statistical environment R (version 4.2.2).

In many proposed staging systems for atrial cardiomyopathy, including the widely cited framework by Goette et al.6 pre-existing AF is incorporated as criterium for AtCM severity. Nonetheless, AF itself is associates with well-described structural remodelling of the atria.8 Moreover, individuals with pre-existing AF frequently underwent concomitant ablation during surgery and inherently carry a higher risk of AF recurrence postoperatively, not equivalent to new-onset AF after surgery. For these reasons, to obtain a clearer understanding of how AtCM severity relates to atrial tissue remodelling and late AF during follow-up in patients without prevalent AF, individuals with pre-existing AF were analyzed as a separate AtCM category rather than within the staged AtCM cohort.

Exploratory (α = 0.05) univariable analyses across the 136 cardiac surgery patients mainly revealed significant clinical differences between patients with/without AF history (Table 1). Patients with AF were older (P = 0.041), more often underwent mitral valve surgery (P = 0.008) and more often received digitalis (P = 0.002), anti-coagulants (P < 0.001) and less often anti-platelets (P < 0.001). Use of other pre-operative medication such as β-blockers, ACE inhibitors and ARBs did not differ significantly among AtCM stages. Multiple circulating markers were elevated in AF patients: NT-proBNP (P = 0.001), proBNP2 (P < 0.001), ANG2 (P < 0.001), BMP10 (P < 0.001).

By definition, patients with higher AtCM stages exhibited greater left and right atrial chamber enlargement with worsening function. Accordingly, global differences were seen in LAVI (P < 0.001), RAVI (P < 0.001) and LATEF (P < 0.001). No differences in the left ventricle were observed across AtCM stages. Patients without AtCM had less heart failure (P < 0.001).

Individual correlations between non-invasive markers of AtCM and atrial histology

To better understand the relative contribution of individual AtCM markers to the observed histological changes, the individual correlations between left/right atrial endomysial fibrosis or myocyte diameter and TTE, ECG and circulating biomarkers were explored (Figure 3). Endomysial fibrosis was positively associated with LAVi (rLA = 0.30, pFDR,LA = 0.015), NT-proBNP (rLA = 0.34, pFDR,LA = 0.005, rRA = 0.28, pFDR,RA = 0.015) and inversely with LAEF (rLA = −0.3, pFDR,LA = 0.011). Left atrial myocyte diameter was positively associated with LAVi (rLA = 0.39, pFDR,LA = 0.013), NT-proBNP (rRA = 0.36, pFDR,RA = 0.013) and P-wave duration (rLA = 0.34, pFDR,LA = 0.0.015), whereas right atrial myocyte diameter was not associated with any of the investigated markers.

LA vs. RA histological features in different AtCM stages

Left-right differences in histological features were quantified in patients were paired appendage biopsies were available (n = 91, Supplementary material online, Table S1, Figure 4). Across the full cohort, RA exhibited significantly higher endomysial fibrosis than LA (Δ = 1.01 ± 0.91 µm, pFDR<0.001). When stratified by AtCM stage, RA endomysial fibrosis remained significantly greater than LA endomysial fibrosis across all AtCM stages, including No AtCM (Δ = 1.01 ± 1.01 µm, pFDR = 0.005), mild AtCM (Δ = 1.18 ± 1.00 µm, pFDR = 0.015), moderate AtCM (Δ = 0.75 ± 0.86 µm, pFDR = 0.023), severe AtCM (Δ = 1.17 ± 0.80 µm, pFDR<0.001), and AF (Δ = 0.95 ± 0.94 µm, pFDR<0.001). ANOVA on paired differences confirmed that the magnitude of the LA–RA fibrosis gap did not differ across AtCM stages, indicating a conserved right-dominant fibrotic phenotype independent of remodelling severity.

Moreover, we observed stage-dependent myocyte hypertrophy. Patients with mild AtCM had large RA myocytes (Δ = 0.81 ± 0.70 µm, pFDR = 0.038), while patients with moderate AtCM (Δ = −1.16 ± 1.28 µm, pFDR = 0.038) or AF (Δ = −0.87 ± 1.12 µm, pFDR = 0.001) had larger LA myocytes. Interestingly, while patients with severe AtCM showed hypertrophy of left atrial myocytes, no differences between LA vs. RA myocyte size were observed. ANOVA confirmed that the LA–RA difference in diameter varies significantly across AtCM stages, unlike fibrosis. This suggests that myocyte hypertrophy exhibits remodelling-stage specificity, with divergent LA–RA behaviour.

New-onset AF late after cardiac surgery

Raw, univariate AF data early and late after surgery are provided in Supplementary material online, Table S2, stratified by AtCM stage. After a 30-day blanking period, new onset AF late after surgery was detected in 26 patients (32.10%), while AF recurrence occurred in 27 AF patients (49.10%). Patients with AF-history had a high recurrence of AF early (HR = 3.75 ± 0.43, pFDR = 0.012) and late (HR = 5.03 ± 0.51, pFDR = 0.010, Figure 5A,B) after surgery, adjusting for age, sex and surgery indication. In patients without AF history, incidence of early or late new-onset AF was similar in all AtCM groups (Figure 5A,B). However, in patients that develop new-onset AF late after surgery, late AF burden differed markedly across AtCM stages (Figure 5C). Discussion

This manuscript bridges the gap between clinical definitions and criteria for AtCM stages6 and histological assessment of structural alterations in the atria. Application of a clinical staging system integrating TTE, ECG and serum biomarkers on the RACE V Tissue Bank Study database highlighted histological differences between clinically defined AtCM stages. Patients with AF or severe AtCM showed more left and right atrial endomysial fibrosis than patients with no/mild or moderate AtCM. Patients with moderate/severe AtCM or AF history had larger left atrial myocytes. Importantly, these histological differences were already observed in patients who do not (yet) have AF. Our study is the first to show which structural alterations are associated with clinically defined AtCM, to what extent and at which stage. Additionally, we show for the first time that AtCM associates with higher late post-operative AF burden.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings align with prior studies that emphasized the interplay between atrial structural remodelling and AF pathogenesis. The dominant consensus is that progression from self-terminating AF to persistent AF requires additional structural changes such as fibrosis or myocyte hypertrophy.9,22,23 In PREDICT-AF, an observational cohort study designed to uncover early markers of AF in high-risk patients undergoing cardiac surgery, Van den Berg et al.10 showed that extracellular matrix remodelling, including collagen turnover, increased expression of pro-fibrotic genes and larger epicardial fibrotic areas, occurred in patients who later developed incident AF or POAF. Due to the use of holter/ECG for follow-up, AF-burden could unfortunately not be assessed. These data carefully suggested that structural remodelling may not merely be a consequence of arrhythmia but part of its substrate. Another study contradicted the central role of fibrosis and myocyte hypertrophy in the pro-arrhythmic substrate,11 stating that the degree of overall, endo- or perimysial fibrosis does not increase with AF persistence, nor does it correlate strongly with electrophysiological abnormalities including low voltage areas or conduction block. However, this study may have been limited by a relatively small sample size (SR: n = 8, parAF: n = 8, PersAF: n = 13) and the use of picrosirius red for the visualization of fibrotic area as well as peri- and endomysial fibrosis. Together, these findings highlight the potential impact of spatial distribution of fibrotic patterns or gradients in cell hypertrophy across the atria. These studies and ours support the emerging view that structural remodelling is not merely a late byproduct of AF but can precede and promote its development. Moreover, our data show that fibrotic remodelling is uniformly more pronounced in the RA, whereas myocyte hypertrophy displays a heterogeneous response that depends on the degree of atrial structural remodelling.

Mechanisms underlying the association between histological changes in the atria and markers for AtCM

Linking structural alterations, such as atrial fibrosis and myocyte hypertrophy, to the recently established clinical definition and staging of AtCM offers valuable insights into the arrhythmogenic nature of this condition. Our data provide a plausible mechanistic explanation for why AtCM is associated with incident AF, heart failure, and stroke. Beyond serving as a common consequence of underlying cardiovascular conditions, the structural and functional alterations observed in the atria may themselves promote a pro-arrhythmic substrate, impair left ventricular filling and pump function, and induce atrial remodelling that increases the propensity for thrombus formation.24 These findings support the concept of AtCM as an active contributor to adverse cardiovascular outcomes rather than merely a secondary phenomenon.

Previous studies focused on the histological characterization of the human atrial myocardium in patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery and the association of histological features with clinical traits and comorbidities.8,13,17 Distinct types of structural remodelling occur in the atria, with fibrotic remodelling occurring more in women and patients with persistent AF or heart failure, while hypertrophic remodelling was more common in men.8 Both the extent and the spatial organization of fibrosis critically affect conduction patterns in AF. Particularly endomysial fibrosis correlates consistently with persistent AF history8 and with the degree of conduction block during AF.13 Larger, patchy areas of replacement fibrosis contribute to zigzag conduction in infarction border zones, or contribute to macro-re-entrant fibrillation waves anchoring around a non-conducting fibrotic core.25 In contrast, endomysial fibrosis contributes to epi-endocardial dissociation12,14,26 and to lateral uncoupling of neighbouring cardiomyocytes,25 further delaying transverse propagation.27–29 The anisotropic conduction favours micro re-entry, contributing to initiation and stabilization of AF.14,30 Uncoupled islands of cardiomyocytes may facilitate propagation of afterdepolarizations and contributes to ectopic firing,25 aiding the transition from hierarchical single-circuit re-entry towards more chaotic multiple-wavelet re-entry.31 Hypertrophic myocytes prolong lateral intracellular conduction paths and disorganize gap junctions required for efficient transverse propagation.32

Association of AtCM markers with histological features and AF-related outcome

AF episodes occurring beyond the early post-operative period are unlikely to be driven by transient surgical factors alone. Instead, they likely reflect an underlying arrhythmogenic substrate that becomes clinically manifest after surgery. Continuous monitoring in our cohort supports this distinction, as AF burden during long-term follow-up was associated with structural remodelling (AtCM), linking clinical AF expression to the underlying atrial substrate.

During moderate AtCM, the atrial myocardium adapts to ongoing stressors, resulting in myocyte hypertrophy. Patients who progress to severe AtCM show both myocyte hypertrophy and high levels of endomysial fibrosis. Both NTproBNP and the echo marker LAVi were individually associated with myocyte hypertrophy and endomysial fibrosis and P-wave duration was associated with myocyte hypertrophy. In line with these observations, P-wave terminal force in lead V1 (PTFV1 > 5000 µV·ms)33 and prolonged P-wave duration (>120 ms) indicative of partial or complete (negative or biphasic P-waves in II, III or aVF) intra-atrial block, associated with stroke risk, independent from rhythm history.34 AtCM35,36 and higher risk of AF-recurrence35–37 were previously reported in patients with prolonged P-wave duration, possibly due to the presence of low voltage areas modifying P-wave duration and morphology.38–41

Furthermore, as shown by our study, even moderate stages of AtCM are associated with a higher AF burden late after surgery. Importantly, AF burden was quantified as the percentage of time spent in AF during continuous monitoring, which is considered the most robust approach to capture AF burden, as detection strongly depends on monitoring intensity and duration. Therefore, expressing AF burden relative to total monitored time avoids the bias inherent to intermittent detection and aligns with current expert recommendations.42

Notably, at this stage, substantial fibrotic remodelling and cellular hypertrophy are already present in left atria, confirming that structural and electrical derangements precede the onset of clinically manifest AF. Interestingly, right atrial associations were in general weaker, presumably due to the smaller impact of pathological stimuli on right atrial tissue structure.

Our data challenge the traditional view that AF detected after surgery is predominantly transient, and instead suggest that AF occurring beyond the early post-operative phase reflects clinically relevant atrial disease linked to underlying atrial remodelling.

AtCM begets AF and AF begets AtCM

Importantly, our findings indicate the presence of endomysial fibrosis and myocyte hypertrophy before the occurrence of AF in patients with AtCM. Although a causal relation between these histological alterations and the occurrence of AF cannot be inferred from our observational study, the mechanistic studies mentioned above together with our observation that AtCM is associated with late post-operative AF burden support the hypothesis that AtCM plays an important role in AF severity. Vice versa, patients with AF in the history showed, regardless of their AtCM stage, histological changes comparable to patients with severe AtCM in SR suggesting that AF may actually drive the occurrence or progression of AtCM. In that sense, our study supports the view that AtCM begets AF and AF begets AtCM. These insights emphasize that AtCM is not merely a marker of disease severity or a byproduct of AF, but actively shapes the arrhythmogenic substrate, underscoring the importance of early intervention to prevent or reverse structural changes. By characterizing the atrial substrate early, before AF develops, our study highlights a potential window for upstream intervention even before AF develops.

Limitations

First, although histological analysis offers unique insights, tissue availability was limited to patients undergoing cardiac surgery, potentially limiting the translation of our findings to other populations with AtCM. Secondly, data collection for this study did not include measurements of late gadolinium enhanced cardiac MRI (LGE-MRI), which is increasingly used for non-invasive assessment of atrial fibrosis. However, the thin atrial wall relative to LGE-MRI resolution, and a lack of standardized LGE detection and quantification43 limit robust quantification of atrial fibrosis. Additionally, it is unclear how well LGE reflects the thin fibrotic septae between myocytes present in endomysial fibrosis, which mostly affect conduction properties.13 A direct comparison of histology-based fibrosis with LGE findings in the context of AtCM-staging would be very useful but falls outside the scope of this manuscript. Third, flow velocity measurements, such as left atrial appendage ejection velocity, were not available, restricting evaluation of atrial contractile function and thrombotic risk. Fourth, we cannot exclude that rhythm control interventions during follow-up influenced AF burden. However, incidence of new-onset AF was similar across AtCM stages and no differences in baseline medication were observed, making major confounding by treatment unlikely. Finally, the sample size within this study was relatively limited for the mild and moderate AtCM group, potentially impacting statistical power for observations in these groups.

Conclusion

This study provides histological evidence of atrial structural remodelling in progressive stages of clinically defined AtCM in patients with and without a history of AF, identified using non-invasive ECG, TTE, and serum markers. Integrating these modalities, as suggested by Goette et al.1 allowed the identification of distinct remodelling patterns, including cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and endomysial fibrosis, in patients with moderate or severe AtCM. While no association was observed with new-onset AF late during continuous monitoring for a 2.5 year follow-up, patients with more advanced AtCM showed higher relative AF burden. These findings highlight that AF occurring beyond the early post-operative phase reflects underlying atrial disease and that clinically defined AtCM corresponds to measurable structural remodelling of the atria, underscoring the importance of early AtCM detection, providing a foundation for risk stratification strategies beyond AF presence alone.

Clinical impact statement

This study supports the potential of multi-modal, non-invasive AtCM staging to identify atrial structural remodelling in patients without established AF. Our findings help close the gap between histopathology and clinical cardiology, offering a framework to better understand how AtCM, well before the clinical presentation of AF, develops and contributes to AF severity. This strengthens the rationale for earlier detection, staging, and treatment of AtCM as a distinct and modifiable disease entity.
