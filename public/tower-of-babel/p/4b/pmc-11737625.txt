Successful heart transplantation after 4 years of bridging with HeartWare HVAD for left ventricular noncompaction in an 11-year-old boy with prohibitively high pulmonary vascular resistance
Authors: Komarakshi R. Balakrishnan, Sureshkumaran Kandasami, Suresh Rao Kemundel Genny, Rajesh Kumar Ramaswamy, Ratnagiri Ravikumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnd-stage heart failure due to left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC) poses unique challenges for ventricular assist device implantation, like inflow cannula obstruction due to trabeculations. We report a case of an 11-year-old boy with LVNC who underwent successful HeartWare implantation as a bridge to transplant for high pulmonary vascular resistance and had successful heart transplantation after 4 years of HeartWare support.

INTRODUCTION

There are limited options available for children requiring long-term left ventricular assist devices (LVAD),[1] especially with left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC), which poses unique challenges like inflow cannula obstruction due to trabeculations.[2]

CASE REPORT

An 11-year-old boy (weight 22 kg, height 134 cm, and body surface area [BSA] 0.9 m2) was airlifted on an 8-h flight in INTERMACS 2. He was intubated, lapsing in and out of consciousness, with a history of multiple cardiac arrests. His N-terminal brain natriuretic peptide (NT Pro BNP) level was elevated (23,530 pg/mL). Echocardiography showed LVNC, severe mitral regurgitation, hugely dilated left atrium, severe LV systolic dysfunction with ejection fraction of 20%, normal right ventricular function, and severe tricuspid regurgitation. The LV cavity was extremely small, with a very short distance from the apex to the mitral valve [Videos 1 and 2] Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computerized tomography scan chest showed the maximum ratio of noncompacted to compacted myocardium to be 3.2, suggesting LVNC. A right heart study ruled out a heart transplant as an option due to prohibitively high pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) [Table 1]. He became delirious and was evaluated by a neurologist. An MRI of the brain showed mild diffuse cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, and the electroencephalogram was within normal limits. The autoimmune encephalitis panel was negative. He was managed with antiepileptics and antipsychotics.

With limited options available, a VAD was offered despite the small size of the child after a virtual fit done in our laboratory confirmed the feasibility of using the HVAD HeartWare (HeartWare Inc, Miami Lakes, FL, USA), and this has been reported.[3] The VAD insertion was uncomplicated at surgery, and the left pleura had to be opened to accommodate the pump. Transesophageal echocardiography showed an optimal LVAD position and an adequately decompressed LV and left atrium with the interatrial and interventricular septum in the midline. The inflow cannula, inserted through the LV apex, was at the tip of the mitral valve [Videos 3 and 4]. At 2200 RPM and flow of 1.9 L/min, with the use of intravenous sildenafil and inhaled nitric oxide, the hemodynamics were satisfactory, with a central venous pressure of 12 mmHg, mean pulmonary artery pressure of 40 mmHg, mean arterial pressure of 60 mmHg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) of 16 mmHg. Postoperative management was challenging because of the severe pulmonary hypertension and recurrent pulmonary edema despite achieving adequate flow for his BSA. He needed supranormal flows of 3.6 L/min at 2700 RPM to achieve physiological PCWP, in addition to pulmonary vasodilators (nitric oxide, sildenafil, and ambrisentan) and angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) (sacubitril valsartan). After 10 days of ventilatory support, the child made an uneventful recovery. After 4 years of support, a hemodynamic study showed a dramatic drop in PVR [Table 2], and he underwent a successful heart transplant. The explanted heart showed the inflow cannula to be at the tip of the mitral valve [Figure 1], and there was a layered thrombus along the nonsintered part of the cannula [Figures 2 and 3].

DISCUSSION

This case is interesting for several reasons. LVNC is a rare congenital cardiomyopathy characterized by prominent ventricular trabeculations and deep intertrabecular recesses or sinusoids in communication with the ventricular cavity, involving the apical portion of the LV predominantly.[4] The prevalence is higher in children, who generally present with advanced heart failure or arrhythmias,[56] and LVAD is recommended as a bridge to transplantation,[7] This is challenging because of the limited options available, particularly in this case, given the small size of the child and the very small LV cavity. The other interesting finding was the drop in PVR after a period of decompression with an LVAD. In this case, we achieved this by positioning the inflow cannula at the tip of the mitral valve through the LV apex. The presence of a layered thrombus at the nonsintered part of the inflow cannula is interesting and important from the perspective of inflow cannula design.

CONCLUSION

Heart transplantation in high PVR recipients leads to postoperative right ventricular failure. LVAD as a bridge to heart transplantation helps in reducing the PVR. Mechanical circulatory devices in the pediatric population and the use of LVAD in LVNC pose distinct challenges. The use of LVAD in this boy helped us decrease the PVR and made him an ideal recipient for heart transplantation.
