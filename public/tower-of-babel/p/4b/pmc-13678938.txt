Latent profiles of teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors among clinical nurse preceptors and factors associated with profile membership: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yiman Hou, Li Zhang, Ming Jin, Yunying Li, Yuan Wang, Tong Cheng, Xiaoya Li, Zhiqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical nurse preceptors play a central role in supporting nursing students and newly employed nurses during clinical practice. Teaching self-efficacy may be related to preceptors’ clinical teaching behaviors, yet little is known about how these psychological beliefs and observable behaviors co-occur within level-based profiles. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors among clinical nurse preceptors and examine factors associated with profile membership.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from February to April 2026 at Guangyuan Central Hospital, China. Of 400 invited clinical nurse preceptors, 358 provided valid questionnaires, yielding an effective response rate of 89.5%. Participants completed a sociodemographic and occupational information questionnaire, the Chinese version of the Clinical Nurse Educator Skill Acquisition Assessment (CNESAA), and the Clinical Teaching Behavior Inventory (CTBI-23). Latent profile analysis was performed using the mean item scores of five teaching self-efficacy dimensions and six clinical teaching behavior dimensions as manifest indicators. Model selection considered fit indices, entropy, likelihood ratio tests, profile size, posterior classification probabilities, parsimony, and interpretability. Differences in participant characteristics across profiles were examined using χ² tests, and factors associated with assigned profile membership were analyzed using exploratory multinomial logistic regression.ResultsThe mean item scores for teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors were 3.70 ± 0.54 and 3.65 ± 0.53, respectively, indicating moderate levels slightly above the scale midpoint. A three-profile solution was retained as the more parsimonious and substantively interpretable model, although the four-profile solution showed better statistical fit and remained a plausible competing model. The retained solution comprised a low-level profile (n = 67, 18.7%), a moderate-level profile (n = 169, 47.2%), and a high-level profile (n = 122, 34.1%). Average posterior classification probabilities were high (0.978, 0.969, and 0.961 for the low-, moderate-, and high-level profiles after reordering), supporting classification accuracy. Compared with the low-level profile, older age, established employment, higher professional title, longer nursing and preceptorship experience, and more frequent participation in clinical teaching training were associated with membership in higher-level profiles.ConclusionsClinical nurse preceptors showed moderate levels of teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors, slightly above the scale midpoint. The three profiles mainly represented ordered, level-based differences rather than qualitatively distinct patterns. These findings may inform stratified preceptor development, but should be interpreted cautiously because of the cross-sectional, single-center design and potential classification uncertainty.Keywords: Clinical nurse preceptor, Teaching self-efficacy, Clinical teaching behaviors, Latent profile analysis, Profile membership, Nursing education

Background

Clinical practice education is a core component of nursing workforce development. It helps nursing students and newly employed nurses integrate theoretical knowledge, enhance clinical skills, and develop a professional identity. High-quality clinical teaching promotes learners’ clinical competence, professional identity, clinical adaptation, and career development [1, 2]. At the health-system level, the World Health Organization has emphasized that investment in nursing education, employment, and leadership is essential for strengthening the nursing workforce and improving health system capacity [3]. Clinical nursing preceptors bridge classroom-based theoretical instruction and clinical practice. They are responsible for knowledge transmission, skills guidance, role modeling, feedback and evaluation, and emotional support, and they are key supporters of learners’ professional growth [4–8]. Clinical teaching behavior refers to the activities and behavioral approaches adopted by preceptors during teaching, including feedback and evaluation, communication guidance, caring support, use of learning strategies, and creation of a positive learning atmosphere. Positive clinical teaching behaviors are associated with learners’ clinical learning experiences, satisfaction, clinical adaptation, and sense of professional belonging; insufficient support and feedback or inappropriate teaching approaches may undermine learning confidence and affect practice performance [1, 2, 9–13]. Feedback and evaluation help learners identify gaps in competence, correct behaviors, and engage in reflective learning [14–17]. Because preceptors must simultaneously undertake nursing service and teaching responsibilities, their teaching behaviors may be influenced by workload, fragmented teaching time, feedback competence, and organizational support, resulting in individual differences [18–21].

Teaching self-efficacy refers to preceptors’ beliefs in their ability to complete clinical teaching tasks and promote learner development. It originates from Bandura’s social cognitive theory, which posits that self-efficacy influences behavioral choices, goal setting, effort, persistence, and coping with difficulties [22, 23]. In clinical teaching, preceptors with higher teaching self-efficacy are more likely to actively engage in teaching and adopt positive behaviors such as constructive feedback, situated guidance, reflective facilitation, and learning support. Previous studies have shown that clinical teacher self-efficacy is associated with teacher performance, professional engagement, learner development, and educational quality, and that preceptor training, teaching experience, and teaching support are related to improvements in teaching competence and clinical teaching behaviors [24–28]. Therefore, teaching self-efficacy may be an important psychological factor for understanding differences in preceptors’ teaching engagement and teaching behaviors.

Most existing studies have adopted variable-centered approaches, focusing on the overall levels and associated factors of teaching self-efficacy, teaching competence, or clinical teaching behaviors [18, 19, 25, 26], while paying insufficient attention to individual differences under multidimensional combinations of characteristics. Variable-centered research can reveal average associations between variables but may obscure within-group heterogeneity. Preceptors are not a homogeneous population, and their combinations of teaching beliefs and teaching behaviors may differ. Analyses based solely on average levels are insufficient for identifying groups with different levels of support needs and may limit stratified training and precision support. Latent profile analysis is a person-centered approach that identifies latent profiles within a population based on multiple continuous observed indicators and is suitable for revealing multidimensional patterns of characteristics [29, 30]. Nursing education research has identified distinct latent profiles in clinical mentors’ mentoring competence and clinical nursing teachers’ effective teaching behaviors, with self-efficacy and years of teaching experience associated with class membership [31, 32]. However, most existing studies have focused on a single domain and have rarely incorporated both teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors. Including both psychological teaching resources and overt teaching behaviors in the same framework may help identify heterogeneity in overall teaching performance.

In hospital-based nursing teaching management in China, preceptors undertake clinical nursing, student preceptorship, training of newly employed nurses, and quality management. Their teaching performance may be associated with age, professional title, years of nursing experience, years of preceptorship experience, employment type, teaching training, and qualification review systems. Identifying the characteristics of different latent profiles and their associated factors can provide evidence for managers to implement stratified support. This study incorporated teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors into the same analytical framework, used latent profile analysis to identify latent profiles among clinical nurse preceptors, and analyzed the relationships between sociodemographic and occupational factors and profile membership, thereby providing a reference for stratified training, targeted support, and optimization of teaching management.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from February to April 2026 at Guangyuan Central Hospital. The hospital was selected because it has an established nursing preceptorship management system, including pre-service training, qualification review, and routine teaching coordination. Clinical nurse preceptors who undertook clinical teaching responsibilities were recruited using convenience sampling. During the survey period, 400 clinical nurse preceptors were invited to participate, and 358 provided valid questionnaires, yielding an effective response rate of 89.5%. The study was coordinated by the hospital nursing department. All participants volunteered to participate; the nursing department was responsible only for organization and coordination and did not participate in questionnaire completion, data analysis, or evaluation of individual results.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: holding a valid registered nurse license; being engaged in clinical nursing work and undertaking clinical preceptorship responsibilities; providing informed consent and voluntarily participating in the study; and being able to independently understand and complete the questionnaire. Clinical nurse preceptors in the study hospital are generally selected according to registered nurse qualification, clinical experience, department recommendation, completion of pre-service teaching training, and regular qualification review. The exclusion criteria were as follows: not engaged in clinical preceptorship during the survey period because of further training, leave, or external study; or being unable to complete the questionnaire independently for other reasons. After checks for completeness and logical consistency, 358 valid participants were included in the final analysis.

Instruments

Data were collected using a sociodemographic and occupational information questionnaire, the Chinese version of the Clinical Nurse Educator Skill Acquisition Assessment (CNESAA), and the Clinical Teaching Behavior Inventory.

Sociodemographic and occupational information questionnaire

The sociodemographic and occupational information questionnaire was developed by the researchers based on the study objectives and relevant literature. It included sex, age, educational level, marital status, employment type, professional title, position, years of nursing experience, years of preceptorship experience, frequency of clinical teaching training participation, pre-service training, regular qualification review, and full-time preceptor status. It was used to collect participants’ demographic and occupational characteristics.

Teaching self-efficacy

The Chinese version of the Clinical Nurse Educator Skill Acquisition Assessment (CNESAA), translated and culturally adapted by Sun et al. [33], was used to assess participants’ teaching self-efficacy in clinical preceptorship. The scale comprises five dimensions: facilitating student learning, integrating theory and practice, participating in academic activities, fulfilling preceptorship responsibilities, and focusing on personal professional development, with 24 items in total. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher teaching self-efficacy. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for the five dimensions ranged from 0.704 to 0.889, and Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.944.

Clinical teaching behavior Inventory

The Clinical Teaching Behavior Inventory (CTBI-23) developed by Lee-Hsieh et al. [34] was used to assess clinical nurse preceptors’ teaching behaviors. The inventory contains 23 items across six dimensions: providing feedback and evaluation, guiding interprofessional communication, showing care and support, applying appropriate learning strategies, teaching and role modeling, and creating a learning atmosphere. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scoring method, with higher scores indicating more positive clinical teaching behaviors. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for the six dimensions ranged from 0.722 to 0.843, and Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.938.

Data collection and quality control

Before the survey, the researchers developed standardized instructions and explained the study purpose, procedures, anonymous nature of data collection, data use, and questionnaire completion requirements to eligible clinical nurse preceptors. Of 400 invited preceptors, 358 provided valid questionnaires. After providing informed consent, participants independently completed the questionnaires according to their actual circumstances. During the survey, the researchers avoided leading language to reduce interviewer bias.

After questionnaire collection, completeness and logical consistency were checked. Questionnaires with incomplete responses, obvious logical contradictions, or clear invalid response patterns were excluded. After data entry, consistency checks were performed for variables such as age, years of nursing experience, years of preceptorship experience, professional title, and position, and missing, duplicate, and abnormal data were rechecked. All data were processed using unified codes; the statistical database did not contain identifiable information such as names, staff identification numbers, or contact details.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangyuan Central Hospital (approval no. 2026-022). Before the survey, the researchers explained the study purpose, procedures, questionnaire completion method, data use, privacy protection measures, and voluntary nature of participation to participants. Participants could withdraw before questionnaire submission, and withdrawal would not affect their work, training, or assessment. Questionnaires were completed anonymously, and results are presented only in aggregate form without disclosing identifiable personal information.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3 were used for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations. Harman’s single-factor test was used only as a preliminary screening approach for common method bias [35], and Cronbach’s α coefficients were used to evaluate the internal consistency of the scales.

Latent profile analysis was conducted using the mean item scores of the five dimensions of teaching self-efficacy and the six dimensions of clinical teaching behaviors as manifest indicators. This study did not aim to test a causal pathway from teaching self-efficacy to clinical teaching behaviors; rather, it aimed to describe how teaching-related psychological beliefs and observable teaching behaviors co-occur among clinical nurse preceptors. Models with one to five profiles were fitted sequentially. Model selection was based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMRT), bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), profile proportions, average posterior classification probabilities, parsimony, and theoretical interpretability. Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicate better model fit; entropy values closer to 1 indicate higher classification accuracy; and P < 0.05 for the LMRT and BLRT indicates that the k-profile model fits better than the k − 1 profile model.

The sample size was considered acceptable for LPA because the total sample exceeded 300 and the smallest retained profile included 67 participants, accounting for 18.7% of the sample. However, the relatively small size of the low-level profile and sparse categories in some occupational variables may limit the stability of multinomial regression estimates; therefore, the covariate analysis was treated as exploratory.

After determining the retained latent profile model, participants’ most likely profile membership was used as the grouping variable. The χ² test was used to compare sociodemographic and occupational characteristics across profiles. Given the relatively small size of the low-level profile and sparse categories in some occupational variables, the exploratory multinomial logistic regression model focused on theoretically relevant and relatively stable covariates, including age group, employment type, professional title, years of nursing experience, years of preceptorship experience, and clinical teaching training participation. To preserve model parsimony, the ordinal covariates were entered as ascending integer scores: age group (1 = < 30 years, 2 = 30–34 years, 3 = 35–39 years, 4 = 40–45 years, and 5 = > 45 years), professional title (1 = junior, 2 = intermediate, and 3 = associate senior or above), years of nursing experience (1 = < 5 years, 2 = 5–10 years, 3 = 11–15 years, and 4 = > 15 years), years of preceptorship experience (1 = < 5 years, 2 = 5–10 years, and 3 = > 10 years), and clinical teaching training participation (1 = never, 2 = occasionally, and 3 = frequently). Employment type was coded as 0 = non-established or contract-based employment and 1 = established post. Accordingly, for each ordinal covariate, the OR represents the change in the odds of profile membership associated with a one-category increase, assuming a linear trend across the ordered categories. The low-level profile was used as the reference category. Because this classify-analyze approach did not fully account for classification uncertainty, the regression findings were interpreted as exploratory. All statistical tests were two-sided, with α = 0.05.

Results

Participants’ sociodemographic and occupational characteristics

Of 400 invited clinical nurse preceptors, 358 provided valid questionnaires and were included in this study, yielding an effective response rate of 89.5%. Of these, 336 (93.9%) were women. The main age groups were 30–34 years and 35–39 years, comprising 121 (33.8%) and 97 (27.1%) participants, respectively. Most participants had a bachelor’s degree (n = 309, 86.3%) and were married (n = 309, 86.3%).

In terms of occupational characteristics, 243 participants (67.9%) had non-established or contract-based employment; 201 (56.1%) held an intermediate professional title; and 253 (70.7%) had no managerial position. The largest groups by years of nursing experience were 11–15 years and > 15 years, comprising 115 (32.1%) and 111 (31.0%) participants, respectively. The distributions of participants with < 5 years, 5–10 years, and > 10 years of preceptorship experience were relatively balanced. A total of 187 participants (52.2%) frequently participated in clinical teaching training; 345 (96.4%) had received pre-service training; 334 (93.3%) underwent regular qualification review; and 297 (83.0%) were not full-time preceptors. Common method bias test and scale reliability

Harman’s single-factor test was used only as a preliminary screening approach for common method bias [35]. Unrotated exploratory factor analysis extracted 11 principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first principal component explained 32.4% of the variance, which was below the commonly used threshold of 40%. No obvious phenomenon of a single dominant factor was observed; however, same-source measurement bias could not be completely excluded.

Reliability analysis showed that Cronbach’s α coefficients for the five dimensions of the CNESAA ranged from 0.704 to 0.889, and Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.944. Cronbach’s α coefficients for the six dimensions of the Clinical Teaching Behavior Inventory ranged from 0.722 to 0.843, and Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.938. The internal consistency of all dimensions and total scales reached acceptable levels. Reliability results are shown in Table 2.

Scores for teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors among clinical nurse preceptors

The total teaching self-efficacy score was 88.86 ± 12.87, with a mean item score of 3.70 ± 0.54, indicating a moderate level slightly above the scale midpoint. Among the dimensions, fulfilling preceptorship responsibilities had the highest mean item score (3.91 ± 0.63), whereas participating in academic activities had the lowest mean item score (3.60 ± 0.64). These results suggest that participants reported relatively stronger self-efficacy in fulfilling preceptorship responsibilities, whereas self-efficacy in participating in academic activities could be further strengthened.

The total clinical teaching behaviors score was 83.85 ± 12.10, with a mean item score of 3.65 ± 0.53, indicating a moderate level slightly above the scale midpoint. Among the dimensions, showing care and support had the highest mean item score (3.80 ± 0.67), whereas providing feedback and evaluation had the lowest mean item score (3.45 ± 0.65). These findings suggest that clinical nurse preceptors reported relatively higher levels of emotional support and teacher-learner interaction, whereas feedback and evaluation behaviors require further improvement. Latent profile model fit and model selection

Using the five dimensions of teaching self-efficacy and the six dimensions of clinical teaching behaviors, this study fitted one- to five-profile models. The three-profile solution was retained as the preferred substantive model and comprised low-level, moderate-level, and high-level profiles. These profiles primarily reflected ordered, level-based differences in overall teaching-related psychological resources and observable teaching behaviors.

The three-profile model had an AIC of 5293.219, BIC of 5471.723, aBIC of 5325.789, entropy of 0.931, and statistically significant LMRT and BLRT results, with profile proportions of 0.187, 0.341, and 0.472 in the original class order. After substantive reordering, these corresponded to the low-, high-, and moderate-level profiles, respectively. Model-fit indices did not uniformly favor the three-profile solution. Compared with the three-profile model, the four-profile model had a lower BIC (5385.444 vs. 5471.723; ΔBIC = -86.279), and the LMRT comparing the four-profile model with the three-profile model was statistically significant (P = 0.003), indicating improved statistical fit for the four-profile solution. The four-profile model also had the lowest BIC among the one- to five-profile models examined. However, its entropy was lower than that of the three-profile model (0.900 vs. 0.931), and the additional profile, which accounted for 12.8% of the sample, mainly represented a further subdivision along the same overall level gradient rather than a clearly distinct configuration across the 11 indicators. By contrast, the three-profile model retained adequately sized profiles, showed high average posterior classification probabilities of 0.978, 0.969, and 0.961, and yielded a more parsimonious and practically interpretable low-, moderate-, and high-level structure. The five-profile model did not provide further support for increasing the number of profiles because its BIC increased to 5403.190, entropy decreased to 0.855, and the LMRT was not statistically significant (P = 0.168). Therefore, although the four-profile model showed better statistical fit and remained a plausible competing solution, the three-profile model was retained as the preferred substantive model on the basis of classification quality, profile size, parsimony, theoretical interpretability, and practical utility. Naming and characteristics of latent profiles

Based on the score patterns of the 11 dimension mean item scores in the three-profile model, the profiles were descriptively named. The labels “low-level,” “moderate-level,” and “high-level” indicate relative positions within this sample. They should not be interpreted as qualitatively distinct educational phenotypes or longitudinal developmental stages. The low-level profile included 67 participants (18.7%) and had relatively low scores across all dimensions, suggesting substantial room for improvement in both teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors.

The moderate-level profile included 169 participants (47.2%) and had moderate scores across all dimensions. Participants in this profile showed moderate levels of teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors, with further improvement needed in feedback and evaluation, professional development, and teaching support. The high-level profile included 122 participants (34.1%) and had the highest scores across all dimensions. Participants in this profile reported relatively high levels of both teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors.

The profile plot showed that the three curves were broadly parallel overall. Differences among latent profiles were mainly reflected in ordered overall level gradients rather than obvious dimension-specific imbalance. Profile-specific indicator means are shown in Table 6. Comparison of sociodemographic and occupational characteristics across latent profiles

The χ² test showed statistically significant differences across latent profiles in age group, employment type, professional title, position, years of nursing experience, years of preceptorship experience, frequency of clinical teaching training participation, regular qualification review, and full-time preceptor status. In the low-level profile, higher proportions of participants were younger than 35 years, had non-established or contract-based employment, held junior professional titles, had no managerial position, had shorter nursing and preceptorship experience, and participated only occasionally in clinical teaching training.

In the high-level profile, higher proportions of participants held an established post, had intermediate or associate senior and above professional titles, served as team leaders or head nurses, had more than 15 years of nursing experience, had more than 10 years of preceptorship experience, frequently participated in clinical teaching training, underwent regular qualification review, and were full-time preceptors. Sex, educational level, marital status, and pre-service training did not differ significantly across the three profiles. Discussion

Teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors were moderate, but feedback and evaluation competence require strengthening

This study showed that the mean item score for teaching self-efficacy among clinical nurse preceptors was 3.70 ± 0.54, and the mean item score for clinical teaching behaviors was 3.65 ± 0.53. Both were slightly above the theoretical midpoint of the scales, suggesting that participants had a moderate foundation in teaching beliefs and clinical teaching behaviors. This finding may be related to the high coverage of pre-service training, clinical teaching training, and regular qualification review in the study sample, although this explanation requires further confirmation.

Dimension-level analysis showed that fulfilling preceptorship responsibilities had the highest score in teaching self-efficacy, whereas participating in academic activities had the lowest score. In clinical teaching behaviors, showing care and support had the highest score, whereas providing feedback and evaluation had the lowest score. These results suggest that clinical nurse preceptors attach importance to responsibility fulfillment, emotional support, and teacher–learner interaction, but their competence in educational research participation, academic development, formative assessment, and constructive feedback still needs to be strengthened.

These differences may be related to the competence requirements of different teaching behaviors. Showing care and support is an emotional form of support that is relatively easy to express during routine preceptorship interactions. By contrast, feedback and evaluation require preceptors to continuously observe learners’ performance, identify gaps in competence, provide suggestions for improvement, and follow up on behavioral changes. These processes place higher demands on pedagogical judgment, communication, and reflective facilitation. Under the influence of heavy clinical nursing workload, fragmented teaching time, and complex preceptorship contexts, preceptors may find it easier to provide general guidance and emotional support than to deliver timely, specific, individualized, and actionable feedback [16, 17]. Previous studies have shown that feedback helps learners identify deficiencies, correct behaviors, and engage in reflective learning and is an important component of improving clinical teaching quality [14–17]. Therefore, future clinical teaching training should extend beyond institutional regulations and preceptorship process training to include feedback and evaluation, teaching reflection, clinical reasoning facilitation, and educational research competence, and these elements should be incorporated into preceptorship responsibilities, teaching quality evaluation, and continuous improvement requirements [17, 36, 37].

The three latent profiles reflected overall gradients in teaching beliefs and teaching behaviors

Using the five dimensions of teaching self-efficacy and the six dimensions of clinical teaching behaviors, this study retained a three-profile model as the preferred substantive solution, comprising low-level, moderate-level, and high-level profiles and accounting for 18.7%, 47.2%, and 34.1% of participants, respectively. Although the four-profile model had a lower BIC and a statistically significant LMRT, its additional profile primarily represented a further subdivision along the same overall level gradient, and its entropy was lower than that of the three-profile model. The retained three-profile solution therefore provided a more parsimonious and practically interpretable representation of the data, rather than the statistically best-fitting solution. This finding indicates that clinical nurse preceptors showed identifiable level-based heterogeneity in teaching beliefs and teaching behaviors.

The profile plot showed that the three curves were broadly parallel, and differences between profiles mainly reflected overall level gradients across dimensions rather than a single prominent strength or deficit. Therefore, the profiles should be interpreted as ordered, level-based profiles within this sample rather than qualitatively distinct educational phenotypes. Nevertheless, LPA remains useful because it allowed the simultaneous consideration of multiple dimensions of teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors without imposing arbitrary cut-off points on a single composite score.

This finding is consistent with social cognitive theory. Bandura proposed that self-efficacy is related to behavioral choices, effort, sustained engagement, and coping with difficulties [22, 23]. In clinical teaching contexts, preceptors with higher teaching self-efficacy may be more likely to report positive teaching behaviors, such as providing feedback, guiding communication, supporting learners, and creating a positive learning atmosphere. Bourne et al. [24] noted that clinical teacher self-efficacy is an important psychological resource in teaching practice. In addition, previous studies have suggested that preceptor training, accumulated teaching experience, and teaching support are associated with teaching competence and clinical teaching behaviors [25–28]. Therefore, the level-based profiles identified in this study may reflect the co-occurrence of psychological teaching beliefs and observable teaching behaviors.

Because this study used a cross-sectional design, the causal direction between teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors cannot be determined. Higher teaching self-efficacy may be associated with more positive teaching behaviors, whereas successful preceptorship experiences, positive student feedback, and organizational recognition may also be related to higher teaching self-efficacy. Longitudinal studies are needed to further explore the dynamic relationship between these two constructs.

The low-level profile may warrant priority attention in stratified support

In this study, the low-level profile accounted for 18.7% of participants. Preceptors in this profile had low scores across all dimensions of teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors, with particularly substantial room for improvement in facilitating student learning, participating in academic activities, providing feedback and evaluation, and applying appropriate learning strategies. The distribution of sociodemographic and occupational characteristics showed that this profile included higher proportions of younger preceptors, those with junior professional titles, shorter nursing and preceptorship experience, non-established or contract-based employment, and insufficient clinical teaching training participation, suggesting that this group may warrant priority attention in clinical teaching workforce development.

The low-level profile should not be interpreted simply as indicating unsuitability for preceptorship. Rather, it represents a group with relatively lower teaching-related psychological resources and reported teaching behaviors within the present sample. For younger, less-experienced, or novice preceptors, clinical experience, teaching judgment, ability to identify learners’ problems, and feedback and evaluation skills may still be developing. Without successful preceptorship experiences, opportunities for role modeling, and organizational teaching support, these preceptors may have lower teaching confidence and fewer positive teaching behaviors.

According to self-efficacy theory, mastery experiences are an important source of self-efficacy [22]. Therefore, support for preceptors in the low-level profile should not be limited to broad requirements to improve teaching quality but may focus on helping them obtain successful teaching experiences that are perceptible, cumulative, and transferable. Nursing managers can support novice preceptors through entry training, mentorship, demonstration-based preceptorship, observation of teaching ward rounds, structured feedback templates, and progressive teaching tasks. These measures may help create a supportive teaching environment and provide opportunities for preceptors to accumulate teaching experience.

Clinical teaching training participation was associated with higher-level profile membership

This study found that the frequency of clinical teaching training participation was associated with latent profile membership. Compared with the low-level profile, preceptors who participated more frequently in clinical teaching training were more likely to be classified into the moderate-level or high-level profile, and the association was stronger for the high-level profile. However, because of the cross-sectional design, this association should not be interpreted causally. Reverse causation is possible: preceptors with stronger teaching self-efficacy or more positive teaching behaviors may be more willing or more likely to participate in teaching training.

Previous evidence is consistent with the present findings. Wu et al. [25] showed that a blended clinical teaching training program helped improve teaching competence among registered nurse preceptors. Hong and Yoon [26] found that nurses who had received preceptor training performed better in clinical teaching behaviors. Kim and Shin [19] reported that clinical teaching behaviors were associated with perceptions of patient safety management, clinical reasoning competence, critical reflection competence, workload, and scheduling fit. These findings suggest that clinical teaching training should shift from general institutional training to competence-oriented training. On the basis of clarifying preceptorship responsibilities and teaching processes, training should strengthen clinical reasoning facilitation, constructive feedback, reflection competence, psychological support for students, interprofessional communication, and patient safety education [28, 36–38].

Based on the present results, hospitals could consider establishing a stratified and progressive clinical teaching training system [36, 37]. For the low-level profile, training may focus on basic teaching skills, preceptorship role identity, feedback and evaluation competence, and teaching confidence. For the moderate-level profile, individualized teaching plans, case-based discussions, clinical reasoning facilitation, teaching reflection, and peer review may help refine teaching behaviors. For the high-level profile, training may emphasize educational leadership, teaching innovation, academic development, and mentoring of novice preceptors. These implications should be interpreted as practice-oriented suggestions rather than evidence of causal effects.

Career development and organizational factors were associated with latent profile membership

This study found that age, professional title, years of nursing experience, years of preceptorship experience, and employment type were associated with latent profile membership among clinical nurse preceptors. Older preceptors, those with higher professional titles, and those with longer preceptorship experience were more likely to be classified into the moderate-level or high-level profile; those with longer nursing experience were more likely to be classified into the high-level profile. These factors may reflect accumulated clinical experience, teaching exposure, role familiarity, and institutional support.

As years of nursing and preceptorship experience increase, clinical nurse preceptors may encounter a wider range of clinical situations, learner issues, communication challenges, and teaching tasks, and may develop a more stable understanding of the preceptor role and more effective strategies for managing teaching challenges through repeated preceptorship practice [8, 27, 31]. According to self-efficacy theory, previous mastery experiences are an important source of self-efficacy [22, 23]. Therefore, accumulated clinical and teaching experiences may be related to teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors. However, because the study was cross-sectional, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a developmental trajectory.

Employment type was also an important factor associated with latent profile membership. This study showed that preceptors with an established post were more likely to be classified into the moderate-level and high-level profiles, especially in the comparison between the high-level and low-level profiles. This finding may be related to job stability, organizational belonging, training opportunities, and career development pathways. Preceptors with an established post may have relatively stable career expectations, greater access to training resources, stronger organizational belonging, and clearer role expectations. In contrast, preceptors with non-established or contract-based employment may face uncertainty in career development expectations, teaching incentives, training opportunities, and organizational support, which may be associated with teaching engagement and the development of teaching self-efficacy [20, 21]. Previous nursing management research has shown that structural and psychological empowerment are related to nurses’ work stress and job satisfaction [39]. Therefore, nursing teaching management should not attribute lower profile membership among non-established or contract-based preceptors simply to inadequate ability; instead, it should consider training resources, career development opportunities, and organizational support.

Age, professional title, years of nursing experience, and years of preceptorship experience may be interrelated and may all reflect career development stage; employment type may also be intertwined with training opportunities, job stability, and organizational support. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that any single factor has a causal effect on teaching self-efficacy or clinical teaching behaviors. Rather, they indicate associations between career development stage, organizational support factors, and latent profile membership. Future studies could include objective assessments of teaching competence, student feedback, organizational support, workload, departmental teaching climate, and preceptor–learner matching models to further explain differences in teaching performance among preceptors at different career stages and with different employment types. Hospitals could also incorporate clinical teaching training frequency, years of preceptorship experience, student feedback, teaching quality evaluation, peer observation, and career development support into preceptor workforce development systems [13, 36, 37], with particular attention to preceptors with fewer years of experience, lower professional titles, non-established or contract-based employment, and insufficient training participation. Ongoing support and targeted professional development may help support the quality of clinical nursing education.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding relationships among teaching self-efficacy, clinical teaching behaviors, and associated factors. Reverse causation may exist, particularly for clinical teaching training participation. Second, convenience sampling was used, and participants were recruited from a single hospital; therefore, sample representativeness was limited. The identified profile structure may be influenced by the hospital’s teaching culture, preceptor selection system, organizational support, workload distribution, and regional nursing education context. The findings should therefore be generalized to other hospitals or regions with caution. Third, data were primarily collected using self-report questionnaires, which may be affected by recall bias and social desirability bias. Harman’s single-factor test was used only as a preliminary screening approach, and common method bias could not be completely ruled out. Fourth, several potentially important educational and organizational variables, including department affiliation, organizational support, workload, teaching climate, mentoring support, educational preparation, learner characteristics, burnout, teaching time, student feedback, and department-level teaching resources, were not measured. Residual confounding may therefore remain.

In addition, model-selection uncertainty should be acknowledged. Although the three-profile solution was retained because of its classification quality, profile size, parsimony, and substantive interpretability, the four-profile solution had a lower BIC and a statistically significant LMRT and was therefore a statistically better-fitting competing solution. Replication in larger and more diverse samples is needed to determine whether the three- or four-profile structure is more stable. After determining the retained latent profiles, this study used participants’ most likely profile membership as the grouping variable for the analysis of associated factors. Although the three-profile model had a high entropy value and high average posterior classification probabilities, suggesting good classification accuracy, the influence of classification uncertainty on the results cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, findings regarding associated factors should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies may use the three-step approach, such as R3STEP or BCH, to further account for classification uncertainty [40, 41]. Multi-center longitudinal studies with larger samples are also needed to examine whether the level-based profiles remain stable over time and whether targeted preceptor development strategies are associated with subsequent changes in teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors.

Conclusion

This study found that mean item scores for teaching self-efficacy and clinical teaching behaviors among clinical nurse preceptors were both slightly above the theoretical midpoint of the scales, indicating moderate overall levels; however, there remains room for improvement in areas such as participation in academic activities, feedback, and evaluation. A three-profile solution comprising low-level, moderate-level, and high-level profiles was retained as the more parsimonious and substantively interpretable representation of the observed ordered, level-based heterogeneity, although the four-profile solution showed better statistical fit and remained a plausible competing model. Older age, established employment, higher professional title, longer nursing and preceptorship experience, and more frequent participation in clinical teaching training were associated with membership in higher-level profiles. These findings may inform stratified preceptor development, but should be interpreted cautiously because of the cross-sectional, single-center design, potential residual confounding, model-selection uncertainty, and classification uncertainty.
