TIGIT-targeted IL-12 fusion protein engages NK and CD8+ T cells for potent tumor immunotherapy
Authors: Maoxing Tang, Yingying Huang, Jiacheng Bi, Dandan Meng, Xiaodong Zheng, Hui Peng, Rui Sun, Hongdi Ma, Zhigang Tian, Haoyu Sun, Xiaohu Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe limitation of wild-type interleukin-12 (IL-12) in its clinical application lies in its systemic activation, which results in severe toxicities. Here, we develop a fusion protein named αTIGIT-IL12 (T-12), which fuses the 13G6 (αTIGIT) antibody scFv fragment in tandem with IL-12. T-12 can selectively localize to the tumor site and concurrently target intratumoral natural killer (NK) and CD8+ T cells in vivo. T-12 demonstrated exceptional efficacy in reducing tumor burden across multiple tumor models in mice, dependent on NK and CD8+ T cells. T-12 preferentially activates tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T cells over their peripheral counterparts, in contrast to wild-type IL-12. Compared with wild-type IL-12, T-12 exhibits greater safety upon systemic administration while treating tumor-bearing models, and the maximal tolerance dosage was elevated by up to about 100-fold. T-12 exhibits potent therapeutic efficacy in checkpoint-insensitive tumor models and metastatic tumor models. These findings underscore the potential of the T-12 fusion protein as a strategy in immunotherapy.Keywords: immunocytokine, cancer immunotherapy, TIGIT, IL-12, NK cells, CD8+T cells

Introduction

During tumorigenesis and progression, natural killer (NK) cells and CD8+ T cells, two key cytotoxic components of the immune system, mediate the elimination of malignant cells. While T cell-based immunotherapies have achieved remarkable clinical success,1 most notably through PD-1 blockade that primarily reinvigorates cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), the main effectors of adaptive immunity,2,3 the contribution of innate immunity is gaining increasing attention, particularly NK cells.4,5,6 For example, the checkpoint receptor NKG2A has been identified as a functional inhibitory molecule on NK cells, and its blockade has emerged as a viable immunotherapeutic approach.7 Contemporary immunotherapies typically target T cells or NK cell subsets through receptor-ligand interactions; however, CTLs and NK cells can also cooperate to orchestrate antitumor immune responses within the tumor microenvironment (TME).8 These insights underscore the therapeutic potential of co-stimulating both the innate and adaptive immune systems.

Cytokines, renowned for their ability to enhance lymphocyte effector functions, have been evaluated in numerous clinical trials. However, high-dose cytokine therapy is limited by the risk of cytokine storms and systemic toxicity.9,10 To overcome these challenges, modified cytokines or combination strategies are being pursued to enhance safety and efficacy.11,12,13 Immunocytokines—fusion proteins comprising a cytokine linked to a targeting antibody—offer a promising platform for targeted cytokine delivery, benefiting from the specificity of the antibody and a relatively simple molecular framework. Moreover, cis-presentation of cytokines through antibody binding can significantly boost their bioactivity.14 Currently, immunocytokines in clinical development fall into four categories based on antibody specificity: tumor surface antigens, angiogenesis markers, necrotic regions, and immune checkpoints.15 For example, NHS-IL12, which fuses interleukin-12 (IL-12) to the NHS76 antibody targeting DNA complexes in necrotic tumor areas, exhibits a prolonged half-life and enhanced tumor localization compared to IL-12 alone.16,17 Another example is PD1-IL2v, a fusion of a low-affinity IL-2 variant and an anti-PD-1 antibody, designed to convert IL-2 into a safer and more potent therapeutic via checkpoint-mediated cis-presentation.18 Despite these advances, no precedent exists for immunocytokines that simultaneously activate both NK and CD8+ T cells through a shared targeting strategy.

IL-12, a heterodimeric cytokine composed of IL-12A (p35) and IL-12B (p40) subunits, plays a central role in enhancing the activation and cytotoxicity of NK and T cells, while also promoting IFN-γ production.19,20 Because of its potent proinflammatory and immunostimulatory properties, IL-12 has long been considered a candidate for immunotherapy. However, its clinical application has been hampered by systemic toxicity arising from widespread immune activation.21 Thus, strategies that localize IL-12 activity and limit systemic exposure are critical for harnessing its therapeutic potential.

TIGIT, an inhibitory receptor expressed on both NK and T cells, has been implicated in functional exhaustion in the TME.22,23,24,25 High levels of TIGIT are commonly observed on these cell types in tumors, and its blockade can reinvigorate both NK and T cell responses, promoting tumor-specific immunity in an NK cell-dependent manner.26 Leveraging the dual role of anti-TIGIT antibodies—to both localize and block this inhibitory pathway—offers a compelling strategy for delivering IL-12 specifically to intratumoral NK and CD8+ T cells.

Here, we report the design, physicochemical properties, efficacy, and toxicity evaluation of a fusion protein, T-12, consisting of IL-12 linked to a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) of an anti-TIGIT antibody. We show that T-12 preferentially accumulates in tumors, selectively targets intratumoral NK and CD8+ T cells, and exhibits superior therapeutic efficacy compared to IL-12, with minimal toxicity. Notably, T-12 effectively treated immunologically “cold” tumor subtypes unresponsive to PD-1 blockade and demonstrated activity against tumor metastases. These findings suggest that fusion of IL-12 with an anti-TIGIT antibody represents a promising approach to boost antitumor immunity through localized activation of both the innate and adaptive arms of the immune system.

Results

Design and functional validation of T-12, an IL-12/αTIGIT fusion protein

The goal of our study was to develop an immunocytokine to selectively stimulate intratumoral NK and CD8+ T cells. IL-12 is an obviously suitable cytokine for this goal, as it is known to potently stimulate both NK and CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and IFN-γ production. For the “immuno” component, we focused on TIGIT, as previous studies have reported that TIGIT, as an immunosuppressive receptor, is expressed on NK and CD8+ T cells.27 Providing empirical support for this with our experimental system, flow cytometry-based profiling of checkpoint receptor levels in tumor-infiltrating immune cells from tumor-bearing mouse models showed, as expected, that TIGIT was highly expressed on both NK and CD8+ T cells within tumors. For other checkpoint receptors, PD-1 expression was elevated only in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, while TIM-3 and LAG-3 were expressed at low levels in tumor immune cells (Figure S1).

We subsequently designed an immunocytokine (T-12) by fusing IL-12 to a tandem scFv diabody derived from the anti-TIGIT antibody clone 13G6 (Figure 1A; Figures S2A–S2D). Before any application testing, we conducted surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments, which confirmed that the diabody retained high affinity for its epitope on TIGIT (Figure 1B). To evaluate the storage stability of T-12, we conducted SDS-PAGE to assess whether the T-12 protein was degraded and detected IFN-γ secretion to assess the activity of the IL-12 component. We conducted the experiment on the first and seventh days, respectively, and the data demonstrated that T-12 has good storage capacity under different temperature conditions (Figures S2E–S2G). To evaluate TIGIT-blocking capacity, we generated a 293T-mTIGIT cell line overexpressing murine TIGIT (Figures S2H–S2K). Incubation of these cells with increasing concentrations of T-12 or 13G6, followed by CD155-hFc, showed that T-12 effectively blocked the CD155-TIGIT interaction, matching the performance of 13G6 (Figure 1C). We also conducted ex vivo stimulation of murine splenocytes and found that exposure to T-12 induced IFN-γ production by NK cells at levels equivalent to exposure to unmodified control IL-12 (Figure 1D). Collectively, these results confirmed that T-12 preserved the bioactivity of IL-12 and the TIGIT-binding and -blocking ability of the parental antibody.

T-12 preferentially binds tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T cells

Our design was based on the assumption that the αTIGIT component will drive T-12 accumulation in tumors. We first labeled T-12 and IL-12 with fluorescent tags (Figures S2E–S2H). MC38 tumor-bearing mice were then randomized into two groups and administered fluorescently labeled T-12 or IL-12 via intraperitoneal injection. At 12 h post-injection, whole-body imaging was performed to assess the biodistribution (Figure 1E). T-12, but not IL-12, showed clear and specific accumulation at the tumor site, supporting enhanced tumor-targeting capacity (Figure 1F). Quantitatively, there was a 65.8-fold enrichment of T-12 in tumors compared to surrounding tissues, while IL-12 exhibited only a modest 2.6-fold increase in tumor versus peripheral organ accumulation (Figure 1G). We also harvested major organs (heart, lung, spleen, and liver) and tumors from treated animals and performed ex vivo fluorescence quantification. In agreement with in vivo imaging, T-12 accumulated significantly more in tumors than in other tissues, whereas IL-12 was more broadly distributed. Using the photon flux of the spleen as the default “1,” calculation of tumor-to-normal tissue fluorescence ratios confirmed the selective retention of T-12 within tumors and reduced off-target localization (Figures 1H–1J). These data suggest that T-12 preferentially accumulates in tumors. We performed in vivo and ex vivo imaging over multiple time points, finding that 12 h was the most appropriate time point (Figures S3A–S3C). To further investigate the specific immune cells T-12 acted on, we administered fluorescently labeled T-12 to MC38 tumor-bearing mice in vivo and harvested tumors and spleens 12 h later (Figure 1K). Flow cytometry analysis revealed preferential binding of T-12 to intratumoral NK and CD8+ T cells, with lower levels on CD4+ T cells and myeloid cells (Figure 1L). In contrast, T-12 binding to splenic immune cells was uniformly low across all subsets, with no significant differences, confirming its tumor-specific engagement (Figure 1M). We performed ex vivo T-12 distribution studies on tumor-infiltrating immune cells across multiple time points, finding that 12 h was the most appropriate time point (Figure S3D). We also performed ex vivo binding assays using isolated immune cells from tumors and spleens. Cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled T-12 or IL-12 (control) (Figure 1N). Consistent with the in vivo results, T-12 bound more strongly to tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T cells than to CD4+ T cells or myeloid cells (Figures 1O and 1P). In particular, T-12 was preferentially targeted to PD1+TIM3+ terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells rather than PD1−TIM3− progenitor-exhausted CD8+ T cells (Figure S3E). In the spleen, T-12 exhibited uniformly low binding across all subsets (Figure 1P). In contrast, IL-12 showed no selectivity, binding evenly to all immune cell populations in both tumor and spleen (Figures 1Q and 1R). To determine whether this selectivity is mediated by the αTIGIT antibody component of T-12, we pretreated MC38 tumor-bearing mice with the 13G6 monoclonal antibody (αTIGIT), followed by incubation with fluorescently labeled T-12 (Figure 1S). This pretreatment abolished the preferential binding of T-12 to tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T cells (Figures 1T and 1U), confirming that T-12’s targeting specificity is mediated by the αTIGIT antibody fragment.

Thus, T-12 works as designed and readily accumulates in tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T cells known to exhibit high TIGIT expression.

T-12 exhibits superior antitumor efficacy compared to a combination of αTIGIT antibody and IL-12

We next evaluated the antitumor efficacy of T-12 in vivo. Using the MC38 colorectal cancer model, we tested multiple doses of T-12 (0.01–0.5 mg/kg) delivered via three administration routes: intraperitoneal, intravenous, and subcutaneous injection. T-12 treatment suppressed tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner, with 0.25 mg/kg achieving substantial tumor growth inhibition across all administration routes (Figure 2A). Ultimately, 0.25 mg/kg delivered intraperitoneally was selected as the optimal regimen for subsequent experiments. To compare T-12 with existing therapies, we treated MC38 colorectal cancer- and Hep1-6 liver cancer-bearing mice with T-12, 13G6 (αTIGIT antibody), IL-12, or a combination of 13G6 and IL-12. T-12 showed superior antitumor activity compared with the IL-12 or αTIGIT antibody (13G6) alone and the combination under the condition of equal molar amounts. T-12 could also prolong the survival period of mice among all groups in both MC38 and Hep1-6 tumor models (Figure 2B; Figures S4A–S4C). This proof-of-concept study with two models of different cancer types shows that T-12, as designed, confers a superior antitumor benefit to the parent antibody and free cytokine.

We also investigated whether NK and CD8+ T cells are essential for its antitumor effect. Using the MC38 model, we performed antibody-mediated depletion of NK or CD8+ T cells during T-12 treatment (Figures 2C and 2F). Depletion of either NK or CD8+ T cells markedly reduced the therapeutic effect of T-12 (Figures 2D and 2G; Figures S4D and S4E). NK or CD8+ T cell depletion significantly reduced the survival benefit conferred by T-12 (Figures 2E and 2H). These results demonstrate that both NK and CD8+ T cells are essential for the therapeutic effect of T-12.

We also evaluated T-12 efficacy in additional models. In the MCA205 fibrosarcoma model, which is characterized by weak immunogenicity, T-12 effectively controlled tumor growth (Figure 2I; Figure S4F). Digestive system cancers have a high clinical incidence and poor immunotherapy response rates,28,29 and we tested T-12 in murine models of liver (H22) and colorectal (CT26) cancers. T-12 exhibited robust antitumor activity in both models (Figures 2J and 2K; Figures S4G and S4H). Notably, even when administered to mice bearing advanced MC38 tumors (200 mm3), T-12 treatment continued to shrink tumor burden over the course of three intraperitoneal injections (Figure 2L; Figure S4I). Meanwhile, we established a murine tumor model by inoculating primary liver cancer cells and initiated T-12 or IL-12 + 13G6 treatment once tumors reached approximately 50–100 mm3. Tumor volumes were measured every 2 days to assess tumor-control efficacy (Figure S4J). T-12 demonstrated markedly superior antitumor activity compared with the combination of αTIGIT antibody (13G6) and IL-12. We also established MC38 cancer models to evaluate the antitumor efficacy of T-12 compared with αcontrol-IL12 (Figure S4K). T-12 showed superior antitumor efficacy compared with αcontrol-IL12. Together, these results highlight the potent and consistent antitumor efficacy of T-12 across multiple tumor types.

T-12 promotes the activation and proliferation of intratumoral NK cells

In light of the therapeutic benefit, and having implicated NK cells in that benefit, we subsequently profiled NK cells from T-12-treated MC-38 tumor-bearing mice, aiming to characterize how this localized exposure to IL-12 and TIGIT blockade changes the NK cell population in tumors. We analyzed the phenotype and functional status of tumor-infiltrating NK cells in MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Flow cytometry revealed that IL-12 preferentially activated splenic NK cells—elevating IFN-γ and TNF-α expression. T-12 did not alter the IFN-γ levels on splenic NK cells but did selectively activate intratumoral NK cells (Figure 3A). Moreover, T-12 significantly promoted the proliferation of intratumoral NK cells, as indicated by elevated Ki-67 expression, whereas IL-12 failed to induce comparable proliferation (Figure 3B; Figure S5A). Then, we performed an ex vivo cytotoxicity assay using CD155-coated target cells co-incubated with NK cells and either T-12 or IL-12, followed by 7-AAD-based cytotoxicity detection. Effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 1:1, 2.5:1, and 5:1 were tested in triplicate. Target-cell lysis was quantified by flow cytometry. The in vitro killing assay showed consistently higher cytotoxicity with T-12 compared with IL-12 (Figure S5E).

We also performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on CD45+ immune cells isolated from tumors of IL-12- or T-12-treated mice. Dimensionality reduction using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) revealed distinct clustering of lymphoid and myeloid populations (Figure 3C), with notable differences in the immune landscape between the two treatment groups (Figure 3D). T-12 treatment resulted in an increased proportion of NK cells and other immune cells (i.e., macrophages, DCs, and CD8+ T cells) compared with IL-12 (Figure 3E). Subclustering identified four NK cell subsets: cytotoxic (NK1), chemotactic (NK2), exhausted (NK3), and immature (NK4) populations (Figures 3F–3H). T-12 treatment expanded cytotoxic (NK1) and chemotactic (NK2) NK cell subsets (Figure 3I). A pseudotime analysis showed that these subsets were preferentially enriched at enhanced effector functional late differentiation stages in T-12-treated tumors (Figure 3J). Compared with the IL-12 group, T-12 improved the expression of effector, activation, differentiation, and proliferation-related markers of intratumoral NK cells (Figure 3K). In addition, cell-cell communication analysis revealed that T-12 treatment enhanced functional interactions between NK cells and DCs within the tumor (Figure 3L). These data demonstrate that T-12 not only promotes the activation and proliferation of intratumoral NK cells but also facilitates their integration into immune regulatory networks supporting antitumor immunity.

T-12 induces a memory-like signature in intratumoral CD8+ T cells

In light of the therapeutic benefit, and having also implicated CD8+ cells in that benefit, we also profiled the changes of CD8+ cell population in tumors from T-12-treated MC-38 tumor-bearing mice. We assessed cytokine expression and proliferation after treatment. Flow cytometry revealed that T-12 significantly increased the expression of IFN-γ and TNF-α by tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells compared with IL-12 treatment (Figure 4A). In addition, T-12 markedly enhanced CD8+ T cell proliferation within tumors, as indicated by elevated Ki-67 expression, a feature not observed with IL-12 (Figure 4B; Figure S5B). To quantify antigen-specific T cells in MC38-OVA tumors, MC38-OVA-bearing mice were treated with PBS or 5 μg T-12 twice weekly, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were isolated after the final treatment. OT-1 T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, revealing a clear increase in tumor-infiltrating antigen-specific T cells following T-12 treatment (Figures S5F and S5G). Additional experiments in MC38-bearing mice were performed to assess CD8+ T cell exhaustion by evaluating surface co-inhibitory receptors and effector molecules. C57BL/6 mice (n = 5/group) were inoculated subcutaneously with MC38 cells and treated with T-12, IL-12, or PBS. Tumors were harvested after three treatments and analyzed by flow cytometry. T-12 significantly reduced the proportion of PD1+TIM3+CD8+ T cells while increasing the proportion of PD1−TIM3−CD8+ T cells, whereas IL-12 did not produce these effects (Figures S5H and S5I). These findings indicate that T-12 effectively alleviates CD8+ T cell exhaustion.

We performed scRNA-seq analysis focusing on intratumoral CD8+ T cells. Transcriptomic profiling identified eight distinct subsets (Figures 4C–4E). T-12 treatment led to a significant expansion of the early effector-like and progenitor-like CD8+ T cell populations and a concurrent reduction in naive-like and central memory-like CD8+ T cells (Figure 4F), suggesting that T-12 promotes the differentiation of CD8+ T cells toward an effector-memory phenotype.

At the same time, our scRNA-seq data showed that T-12 treatment increased the abundance of DCs and NK cells in the TME compared to IL-12 (Figure 3E). Using CellChat, we detected an enhancement of ligand-receptor interactions between innate immune DCs, NK cells, and adaptive immune CD8+ cells following T-12 treatment (Figures 3L, 4G, and 4H). We further performed an in vivo treatment experiment in MC38-bearing mice to evaluate DC responses after three T-12 injections. Flow cytometric analysis showed an increased proportion of DCs (CD45+CD11b+CD11c+MHCⅡ+F4-80−)30 following T-12 treatment (Figure S5C). In parallel, we assessed splenic memory T cells and Treg populations after T-12 or IL-12 treatment. Both treatments led to down-regulation of splenic memory T cells and up-regulation of Treg cells; however, no differences were observed between the IL-12 and T-12 groups (Figure S5D). These interactions suggest that T-12-driven innate immune activation promotes antigen presentation and costimulatory signals contributing to effector-memory CD8+ T cell differentiation.

Together, these results demonstrate that T-12 not only activates CD8+ T cells but also reprograms them toward a memory-like, highly proliferative state.

T-12 has enhanced in vivo tolerability over IL-12

We next performed a tolerated dose evaluation of T-12 in vivo. MC38 tumor-bearing mice were treated with equimolar doses of T-12 or IL-12 via intraperitoneal injection (three times), and body weight was monitored as an indicator of systemic toxicity (Figure 5A). While IL-12 led to dose-dependent weight loss at doses ≥0.25 mg/kg, T-12 treatment did not cause detectable body weight loss even at doses up to 2 mg/kg (Figure 5B). Mice also received repeated injections—up to six doses—over a 2-month period (Figure 5C). The minimum dose of IL-12 (0.0625 mg/kg) caused significant weight loss and increased mortality, whereas equivalent molar doses of T-12 were well tolerated without adverse effects. Quantification of maximum tolerated dose (MTD) revealed that T-12 exhibited an approximately 100-fold higher MTD compared to IL-12 based on the survival data (Figure 5D).

Long-term survival studies support these findings. IL-12-treated mice exhibited significantly reduced survival, with all animals in the control, 0.5 mg/kg, and 0.25 mg/kg IL-12 groups succumbing within the 2-month treatment period (Figures 5E and 5F). In contrast, T-12 treatment substantially improved survival, with 100% of mice surviving in the 1 mg/kg and 2 mg/kg T-12 groups and 62.5% survival even at 4 mg/kg (Figures 5G and 5H).

We evaluated the safety of three administration routes in MC38-bearing mice (Figure S6A). Body-weight monitoring under a consistent dosing regimen (0.25 mg/kg, three injections) revealed that T-12 did not induce weight loss regardless of the route of administration (Figure S6B). Serum collected from T-12- or IL-12-treated mice demonstrated substantially higher MCP-1 and IFN-γ levels after IL-12 treatment compared with T-12 (Figure S6C). Additional inflammatory mediators, including IL-23p19, IL-4, G-CSF, IL-17, IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10, and IL-1β, were also assessed. Although none of these cytokines showed significant differences between the IL-12 and T-12 groups, their overall serum levels remained low. The results revealed that T-12 exhibited an approximately 100-fold higher MTD compared with IL-12 based on the cytokine data.

Together, these data demonstrate that T-12 maintains potent antitumor efficacy while dramatically reducing systemic toxicity compared with IL-12.

T-12 effectively controls αPD-1-resistant tumors

To expand the therapeutic potential of T-12, we tested its efficacy in immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-resistant tumor models. Based on prior studies demonstrating that microsatellite instability-low (MSI-L)/proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) tumors and immunologically cold tumors exhibit poor responses to αPD-1 therapy, we established subcutaneous models of B16 melanoma, E0771 breast cancer, and Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) (Figures 6A–6D and 6G). T-12 treatment induced robust antitumor activity across all three models, achieving complete tumor regression to undetectable levels. In contrast, αPD-1 therapy showed limited or no efficacy (Figures 6B–6E and 6H; Figures S7A–S7C). Upon completion of treatment, tumors were harvested, weighed, and photographed. Quantitative analysis confirmed that T-12 significantly outperformed αPD-1 in reducing tumor burden in all models tested (Figures 6C–6F and 6I). We next evaluated the combination of T-12 and αPD-1 in the B16 tumor model. C57BL/6 mice (n = 6/group) were subcutaneously inoculated with B16 cells and treated intraperitoneally with T-12 (5 μg), αPD-1 (150 μg), or both, every 3 days for a total of three doses. Tumor volumes were measured every 2 days. The combination of T-12 and αPD-1 produced synergistic antitumor activity and significantly enhanced tumor growth inhibition compared with either monotherapy (Figures S7D and S7E). These results demonstrate that T-12 effectively controls tumors that are resistant to PD-1 blockade, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic option to overcome ICB resistance.

T-12 inhibits metastatic tumor progression

Given the clinical importance of metastasis in driving cancer-related mortality, we next evaluated whether T-12 could suppress metastatic tumor growth. To this end, we established a pulmonary metastasis model by intravenously injecting MC38 cells into mice (Figure 7A). Fourteen days post-injection, T-12 treatment resulted in a markedly stronger antitumor response compared to IL-12 or 13G6 antibody, effectively suppressing metastatic lesions (Figures 7B and 7C). Moreover, T-12 treatment significantly prolonged overall survival in this model.

To further examine the generalizability of this effect, we established a pulmonary metastasis model using B16 melanoma, an immunologically cold tumor (Figure 7E). Mice were treated three times with T-12, IL-12, or 13G6, and lung tissues were collected for analysis. T-12 again showed superior efficacy in reducing metastatic burden compared to control treatments, both grossly and histologically (Figures 7F, 7G, and 7H). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining confirmed a substantial reduction in tumor foci in lungs from T-12-treated mice.

Together, these data demonstrate that T-12 is capable of suppressing both primary and metastatic tumor growth, highlighting its broad therapeutic potential.

Discussion

In this study, we designed and validated a fusion immunocytokine, T-12, comprising IL-12 linked to an αTIGIT antibody fragment. T-12 preserved full cytokine and antibody functionality, efficiently localized to tumors, and preferentially targeted intratumoral NK and CD8+ T cells. It displayed superior antitumor activity across multiple tumor models, including PD-1-resistant tumors, and significantly reduced metastases. Mechanistically, T-12 enhanced intratumoral NK cell activation and proliferation, promoted effector differentiation of CD8+ T cells, and strengthened innate-adaptive immune crosstalk. Importantly, T-12 exhibited a substantially improved safety profile, with an approximately 100-fold higher tolerated dose compared with IL-12.

The robust therapeutic efficacy of T-12 across multiple tumor settings highlights the importance of simultaneously activating NK and CD8+ T cells. In the immunosuppressive TME, both cell types frequently undergo functional exhaustion—marked by inhibitory receptor up-regulation and reduced proliferative capacity.31,32 NK and CD8+ T cells are critical for productive antitumor immunity, especially in the context of ICB. Notably, NK cell depletion diminishes αPD-1 efficacy by impairing NK-mediated recruitment of stimulatory DCs, which are essential for optimal CD8+ T cell activation and durable therapeutic responses.33 Clinically, combining pembrolizumab with adoptive NK cell therapy has significantly prolonged survival in PD-L1+ advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients.34 Besides, NK cells possess a unique capacity to limit metastatic seeding and outgrowth, positioning NK-centered strategies as promising antimetastatic interventions.35 By concomitantly activating NK and CD8+ T cells, T-12 effectively bridges innate and adaptive immunity to generate a coordinated and potent antitumor response.

The PD-1/PD-L1 axis constitutes a central immunosuppressive pathway across cancers, and its blockade has transformed modern oncology. Nonetheless, resistance to PD-1 monotherapy is common, particularly in immunologically “cold” tumors.36 Remarkably, T-12 retained potent antitumor activity even in PD-1-nonresponsive tumors, suggesting that its mechanism—driven by NK cell engagement and DC-CD8+ T cell crosstalk—can bypass the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and overcome ICB resistance.

Conventional immunocytokines are mainly designed for targeting tumor-associated antigens, such as NHS-IL12, which fuses IL-12 to the NHS76 IgG antibody that recognizes DNA/histone complexes in necrotic tumor regions.16 While NHS-IL12 delivers IL-12 to tumors and reduces systemic toxicity, its therapeutic efficacy is limited due to its restriction to necrotic areas. In contrast, our designed immunocytokine, T-12, fuses an αTIGIT scFv fragment with IL-12. The anti-TIGIT component not only directs T-12 to tumor-infiltrating immune cells with elevated TIGIT expression but also blocks TIGIT signaling, thereby reversing immune cell exhaustion. Unlike NHS-IL12, which primarily localizes to necrotic tumor regions, T-12 accumulates broadly within the TME and activates tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T cells in a targeted manner.

Key challenges in translating bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) from discovery to large-scale production include controlling heavy/light-chain mispairing, mitigating self-association, and balancing yield versus quality during scale-up. Fusion formats incorporating cytokines (immunocytokines) are particularly prone to aggregation and conformational heterogeneity. Industrial strategies to minimize mispairing include Fc heterodimerization (“knobs-into-holes”), CrossMab domain swapping to enforce correct light-chain pairing, and DuoBody-style controlled Fab-arm exchange. T-12 was designed as IL-12 linked to an anti-TIGIT antibody in a tandem bivalent scFv architecture fused to the cytokine. Compared with conventional IgG-like formats, T-12 has lower molecular weight, higher production yield, and >90% purity. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis, validated with positive and negative controls, demonstrate that T-12 is predominantly monomeric, exhibits favorable peak shape and hydrodynamic size distribution, and shows good reproducibility, supporting its developability and scale-up potential.

In conclusion, our study underscores the essential roles of NK and CD8+ T cells in tumor control and establishes T-12 as a next-generation immunocytokine that couples TIGIT blockade with localized IL-12 delivery. T-12 elicits potent antitumor activity, markedly reduces systemic toxicity, and effectively activates intratumoral NK and CD8+ T cells, thereby overcoming key limitations of conventional monoclonal antibody therapies. Through the synergistic combination of TIGIT inhibition and targeted IL-12 signaling, T-12 represents a promising and versatile immunotherapeutic candidate warranting further translational development.

We found that T-12 treatment increased the proportions of early effector-like and progenitor-like CD8+ T cell subsets in the TME and also led to an increase in the terminal-like T cell subset. This phenomenon may be associated with the bifunctional character of T-12. T-12 is composed of an αTIGIT-blocking antibody and the cytokine IL-12. TIGIT blockade could alleviate terminal exhaustion of CD8+ T cells, and IL-12 could activate CD8+ T cells and enhance the expression of effector molecules and cytotoxicity. Consequently, it is readily interpretable that, compared with IL-12, T-12 treatment results in an elevated proportion of “early effector-like” and “progenitor-like” cell populations, while concurrently reducing the ratios of “short-lived effector” and “naive-like” cells as observed. Terminal-like T cells are commonly defined by the co-expression of PD-1 and TIM-3 surface biomarkers. Although this double-positive population was generally considered terminal-like T cells, previous studies have reported that these cells could still keep strong cytotoxic activity and secrete higher levels of effector cytokines. Consistently, our data showed that T-12 was able to bind to tumor-infiltrating PD1+TIM3+ T cells and significantly elevate the expression of cytokines in this subset. These findings suggested that PD1+TIM3+ terminal-like T cells may represent an important effector population contributing to the powerful antitumor efficacy of T-12.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. Tumor metastasis models are essential for investigating the dissemination of primary tumor cells to distant organs, elucidating metastatic mechanisms, and evaluating therapeutic strategies. Tail vein injection is technically simple and well suited for modeling lung metastasis; however, it does not fully recapitulate the biological complexity of spontaneous metastasis. Orthotopic models more closely mimic natural metastatic progression and the native TME, but they require longer establishment periods and are prone to variability in tumorigenicity. In this work, we established several widely used metastasis models and monitored metastatic progression using in vivo fluorescence and bioluminescence imaging, which provide valuable reference data. Although tumor metastasis is inherently complex, our model systems and therapeutic assessments offer a feasible and informative approach for evaluating the potential efficacy of immunotherapeutic strategies.

IL-12 shows strong therapeutic effects, but its clinical development is confronted with significant obstacles due to its toxicity. The fusion of cytokines with antibodies has represented an important method for modification, and T-12 has already shown improved therapeutic efficacy with reduced toxicity as a result of this approach. The present study focuses exclusively on exploring murine-format T-12 in mouse tumor models, thereby providing support for the development and mechanistic characterization of its human-format surrogate. For the human-format counterpart, it is necessary to explore different methods for modification, estimate the optimal structure, and verify the activity.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal studies

Male BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Shanghai Experimental Animal Center (Shanghai, China). IFN-γKatushka reporter mice were bred and maintained in-house. All mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Mice were inoculated with tumor cells suspended in 100 μL PBS as follows: 2 × 105 MC38, 2 × 105 MCA205, 1 × 106 LLC, 5 × 105 B16-F10, 2 × 105 E0771, 1 × 107 Hep1-6, 2 × 106 H22, 1 × 106 CT26, or 2 × 106 primary cancer cells. Treatments were administered as described in the figure legend. Tumor volume was calculated by length × width × width/2. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with 12/12 dark/light cycle, 20–26°C, and 30–70% humidity. Animal care and experiments were carried out under institutional protocol and guidelines. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTCACUC26080123038) and conducted following the National Guidelines for Animal Usage in Research (China). As approved by the Animal Ethics Committee, the maximum permitted tumor volume is 1500mm3. Experiments are terminated immediately once the tumor vvolume exceeds this limit. Mice were euthanized by exposure to carbon dioxide.

The fibrosarcoma cell line MCA205 was obtained from Prof. Yuting Ma (Suzhou Institute of Systems Medicine, China). Method details

Protein production and purification

αTIGIT-IL12, αControl-IL12, and mCD155-Fc were produced and purified in Biointron lnc (Taizhou, China) using the CHO-K1 expression system. The amino acid sequence of anti-mouse TIGIT scFv was derived from 13G6 generated in our laboratory. The amino acid sequence of anti-mouse PD-1 scFv (clone: RMP1-14) was from Biointron lnc. αControl-IL12 was composed of anti-human LUNX scFv and mouse IL-12. Mouse CD155 extracellular domain was linked to human IgG1 Fc to generate mCD155-Fc fusion protein.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR assays were performed using a Biacore 8K high-throughput molecular interaction system (GE Healthcare, UK). Mouse TIGIT-Fc fusion protein was immobilized onto a CM5 sensor chip at 25°C according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The 13G6 antibody or T-12 fusion protein was injected over the chip at increasing concentrations in running buffer, with a constant flow rate of 30 μL/min. Sensor chips were regenerated with 50 mM NaOH between cycles. Specific binding affinities were calculated using a reference blank channel. All raw sensorgram data were analyzed using Biacore 8K Evaluation Software (v1.1, GE Healthcare).

Ex vivo stimulation assay

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from spleens of wild-type or IFN-γKatushka reporter C57BL/6 mice. Cells were cultured overnight in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C in the presence of either 13G6 antibody, IL-12, or T-12. After stimulation, cells were stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against CD45 (30-F11), CD3 (145-2C11), CD4 (RM4-5), CD8α (5H10-1), NK1.1 (PK136), and CD11b (M1/70) (BD or BioLegend). Dead cells were excluded using Fixable Viability Stain 780 (BD). Data acquisition was performed on FACS Celesta (BD), FACS Fortessa (BD), or CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter) flow cytometers, and analyzed using FlowJo (Tree Star) software.

Ex vivo cytotoxicity assay

CD155-Fc was prepared and dispensed into a 96-well U-bottom plate, then incubated at 4°C overnight. After rinse with PBS, macs-sorted murine splenic NK cells and IL-12/T-12 were added per well. The mixed system was incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. RMA-s cell lines were labeled with CellTrace Violet (CTV) (Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA), at 37°C for 15 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Labeled target cells were added to 96-well round-bottom plates along with effector cells (NK cells) for 4 h in complete RPMI at an effector:target ratio of 1:1, 2.5:1, and 5:1, with three technical replicates. After 4 h, cells were harvested, and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) (BD, San Diego, CA, USA) was added before analyses. Samples were mixed thoroughly and analyzed by flow cytometry.

In vivo tumor efficacy model

For subcutaneous tumor models, mice were inoculated with tumor cells suspended in 100 μL PBS as follows: 2 × 105 MC38, 2 × 105 MCA205, 1 × 106 LLC, 5 × 105 B16-F10, 2 × 105 E0771, 1 × 107 Hep1-6, 2 × 106 H22, 1 × 106 CT26, or 2 × 106 primary cancer cells. For lung metastasis models, 2 × 105 luciferase-expressing MC38 cells were injected intravenously via the tail vein. Mice were randomized into treatment groups when tumors reached approximately 50–100 mm3 (small tumors) or 200 mm3 (large tumors). T-12 was administered at doses ranging from 0.01 to 0.5 mg/kg via intraperitoneal, intravenous, or subcutaneous injection. For efficacy studies, a dose of 0.25 mg/kg T-12 was administered intraperitoneally. Control groups received equivalent molar doses of IL-12, 13G6 antibody, or their combination. For PD-1 blockade studies, αPD-1 antibody was administered intraperitoneally at 7.5 mg/kg. Tumor volumes were measured every other day and calculated using the formula: length × length × width/2. Mice were euthanized when tumor volumes exceeded 1,500 mm3.

Isolation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL)

Tumors were excised, minced, and enzymatically digested in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL DNase I and 1 mg/mL collagenase IV. Samples were incubated for 1 h at 37°C with agitation (220 rpm). Digested tissues were passed through 70-μm cell strainers to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were then subjected to density gradient centrifugation using a discontinuous Percoll gradient to enrich for lymphocytes. Isolated TILs were washed and resuspended for downstream applications.

Biodistribution studies

MC38 tumor–bearing mice were used for in vivo and ex vivo biodistribution studies. For in vivo biodistribution, 20 μg of fluorescently labeled T-12 or IL-12 was administered intraperitoneally on day 10 after tumor inoculation. After 12 h, spleens and tumors were harvested, and single-cell suspensions were prepared for analysis. For ex vivo biodistribution assays, freshly isolated splenocytes and tumor-infiltrating cells were incubated directly with fluorescently labeled fusion proteins for 30 min. In blocking experiments, cells were pre-incubated with 13G6 antibody for 30 min prior to fusion protein treatment. The distribution of T-12 and IL-12 was analyzed by flow cytometry.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

T-12, IL-12, and 13G6 were labeled with either biotin or Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647) using standard protein labeling kits. MC38 tumor–bearing mice received intraperitoneal injections of fluorescently labeled proteins on day 10 post-tumor inoculation and were imaged 12 h later. For metastasis models, mice bearing MC38-luciferase tumors were injected intraperitoneally with D-luciferin (GoldBio) on day 7 after tumor inoculation and imaged 15 min later. All mice were anesthetized using a Compact Small Animal Anesthesia Machine (R500, RWD) prior to imaging. Fluorescence and bioluminescence signals were acquired using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer). Tumors were excised and imaged ex vivo 12 h after treatment.

Toxicity and tolerability studies

To assess treatment-related toxicity, tumor-bearing mice were treated with various doses of T-12 or IL-12. Body weight was recorded every other day, and the initial body weight prior to treatment was used as the baseline. The percentage change in body weight was calculated for each time point. In addition to weight monitoring, overall survival was recorded, and survival curves were generated. Besides, Peripheral blood was collected for serum inflammatory biomarker analysis. Serum was collected from treated MC38 tumor bearing mice at day 10 post last treatment, measured using a commercial assay kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol.

In vivo cell depletion

To deplete specific immune cell populations, mice received intraperitoneal injections of depleting antibodies. NK cells were depleted using anti-NK1.1 monoclonal antibody (PK136, purified from hybridoma supernatant), and CD8+ T cells were depleted using anti-CD8β monoclonal antibody (Lyt 3.2, BioXCell). Depleting antibodies were administered one day before tumor inoculation and subsequently once per week throughout the experimental period.

Flow cytometry staining

For surface staining, cells were first blocked with rat serum to reduce non-specific binding, followed by staining with fluorescently conjugated antibodies targeting specific surface markers, including: anti-CD45 (30-F11), anti-NK1.1 (PK136), anti-CD3ε (145-2C11), anti-CD4 (RM4-5), anti-CD8α (5H10-1), anti-CD11b (M1/70), anti-CD226 (10E5), anti-CD96 (3.3), anti-CD44(IM7), anti-CD25(PC61), anti-NKG2D (CX5), anti-NKG2A (20d5), anti-PD-1 (29F.1A12), anti-TIGIT (GIGD7), anti-LAG3 (C9B7W), and anti-TIM3 (RMT3-23) (BD or BioLegend). Fixable Viability Stain 780 (BD) was used to exclude dead cells.

For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated for 4 h with a cell activation cocktail containing brefeldin A (BioLegend). After surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (BD) and then stained with antibodies against IFN-γ (XMG1.2), TNF-α (MP6-TX22), perforin (S16009A), granzyme B (16G6), Ki-67 (16A8), and TRAIL (N2B2) (BD or BioLegend).

Samples were analyzed using FACS Celesta (BD), FACS Fortessa (BD), or CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter) flow cytometers. Data analysis was performed with FlowJo (Tree Star) software.

Staining with hematoxylin and eosin

Tissues of the lung were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections of 5 μm thickness were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, which was carried out by Servicebio (Wuhan, China).

Single-cell RNA sequencing and bioinformatics

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed using the Chromium Single Cell 5′ Library and Gel Bead Kit (10x Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Single-cell suspensions were loaded onto Chromium Single Cell Chips with barcoded gel beads, aiming for ∼10,000 captured cells per sample. Captured mRNA was barcoded during complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis and processed into single-cell libraries for high-throughput sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina).

All samples were processed concurrently to minimize batch effects. Sequencing data were analyzed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (10x Genomics) for demultiplexing, read alignment, and unique molecular identifier (UMI) quantification. Subsequent analyses, including quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, unsupervised clustering, and differential gene expression analysis, were performed using the Seurat R package.

Quantification and statistical analyses

Statistical analyses and graphical presentations were performed using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software), Origin (OriginLab 2019) and R package Seruat (v5.1.0). All experiment details were labeled exact value n and used appropriate tests as indicated in the figure legends. The value n was represented the number of mice. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Significance levels are indicated as follows: p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), p < 0.005 (∗∗∗), and p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).
