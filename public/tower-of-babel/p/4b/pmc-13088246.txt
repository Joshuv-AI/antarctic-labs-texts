Vitamin C Alleviates BPA Embryotoxicity by Attenuating Oxidative Stress and DNA Damage-Mediated Apoptosis via Modulation of the ATM/Chk2/p53 Pathway
Authors: Fei Ren, Zhen Lu, Xueping Cheng, Shitong Hao, Lingli Chen, Zhihong Yin, Dongfang Hu, Hongmei Ning, Yaming Ge, Junping He, Shanshan Shen, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bisphenol A (BPA), an environmental contaminant that migrates from food packaging, poses significant risks to embryonic development. This study investigated the protective effects and mechanisms of vitamin C (l-ascorbic acid) against BPA-induced embryotoxicity using Xenopus laevis embryos and P19 stem cells under a simultaneous exposure design. A multiassay approach was applied, including developmental bioassays, histological analysis, single-cell gel electrophoresis, and molecular profiling. The results showed that vitamin C significantly alleviated BPA-induced mortality, malformations, growth retardation, neurodevelopmental impairments, and notochord damage. Mechanistically, vitamin C restored the expression of antioxidant genes (gpx4, sod, ppard, and cat.2), reduced reactive oxygen species generation and oxidative DNA damage, and suppressed apoptosis via modulation of the ATM/Chk2/p53 signaling pathway. These findings demonstrate that vitamin C mitigates BPA-induced embryotoxicity by limiting oxidative stress and DNA damage-mediated apoptosis under coexposure conditions, supporting further evaluation of antioxidant-based protection across different exposure regimens and in combination with other antioxidants.

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a synthetic compound widely used in the manufacture of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins (e.g., food containers, water bottles, and food can linings) and is known to disrupt normal embryonic development and exhibit embryotoxicity. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that over 90% of the population has detectable BPA in body fluids (urine and plasma), and BPA levels are positively associated with the risk of preterm birth and miscarriage. An increasing number of studies show that prenatal BPA exposure causes adverse embryonic effects, including hormone disruption, impaired organogenesis, and an increased incidence of developmental abnormalities. , BPA embryotoxicity is multifactorial and is mediated by oxidative stress, characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and suppression of the antioxidant system, DNA damage, including strand breaks and oxidative base damage, as well as disruption of estrogenic signaling. These processes collectively lead to apoptotic cell death and developmental disorders.

The adverse effects of BPA are not restricted to its early development. Zhou et al. reported that BPA promotes prostate cancer progression by reprogramming mitochondrial energy metabolism through targeting the transcription factor estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), thereby enhancing cancer cell glycolysis via the glycolytic enzyme PFKFB4. This finding highlights the long-term molecular dysregulation associated with BPA exposure and further supports the need to investigate its toxicity during critical embryonic stages to reduce life-long health risks. Toxicity assessments have shown that BPA and its alternatives reduce the hatching rate and frequency of spontaneous movement and induce yolk sac edema, pericardial edema, and spinal deformation by increasing superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and apoptotic cell death in larvae. Xenopus laevis (X. laevis) is a well-established model species in embryotoxicology, and its embryos are widely used to systematically assess chemical developmental toxicity by quantifying endpoints such as lethality, teratogenicity, and growth inhibition potential. Our previous study demonstrated that BPA induces embryotoxicity in X. laevis, reflected by increased embryo mortality and malformation rates, cardiac edema, abdominal swelling, facial deformities, and shortening and bending of the anterior–posterior (A–P) axis through induction of DNA damage and apoptosis.

Oxidative stress is a universal mediator of reproductive and developmental toxicity induced by nonchemical and chemical stressors. As reported by Gao et al., heat stress triggers excessive ROS production in germ cells, which subsequently causes DNA damage, germ cell apoptosis, and abnormal sperm morphology. Similarly, BPA exposure induces ROS accumulation, leading to DNA damage (a major genotoxic effect) and ultimately triggering apoptosis in zebrafish embryos. Several studies have also shown that BPA reduces the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), decreases glutathione (GSH) levels, and increases ROS generation, followed by DNA damage and apoptosis. , Antioxidant enzymes constitute the cellular defense system that eliminates free radicals and protects cells from ROS-induced genetic damage.

Given the central role of oxidative stress in BPA-induced embryotoxicity, agents capable of scavenging excess ROS represent a plausible strategy to mitigate its adverse effects. Vitamin C (l-ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble antioxidant and free radical scavenger present in the cytosol and extracellular fluid. It acts as a cofactor for enzymes that regenerate other antioxidants and can directly neutralize free radicals, thereby preventing oxidative stress and DNA damage. Zhang et al. demonstrated that vitamin C protected early mouse embryos from juglone-induced developmental arrest by eliminating excessive ROS, which reduced DNA damage and embryonic cell apoptosis. Chen et al. supplemented vitamin C in culture media to alleviate oxidative stress and DNA damage and to rescue abnormal embryo development induced by excessive ionomycin stimulation. Collectively, these studies indicate that vitamin C can protect embryonic development from chemically induced oxidative stress. However, the effectiveness and underlying mechanisms of vitamin C in alleviating BPA-induced embryotoxicity remain unclear. In particular, whether vitamin C can rescue BPA-induced developmental abnormalities in vertebrate embryos and which molecular pathways mediate this protection have not been systematically investigated.

We hypothesize that vitamin C alleviates BPA-induced embryotoxicity by restoring embryonic antioxidant capacity, attenuating oxidative stress, and suppressing subsequent DNA damage-mediated apoptosis via the ATM/Chk2/p53 pathway. To test this hypothesis, the present study employed the X. laevis embryo model and P19 stem cells (P19SCs) to (1) evaluate the protective effects of vitamin C on BPA-induced mortality, malformation, growth retardation, and behavioral deficits; (2) assess its role in restoring antioxidant capacity and reducing ROS levels, DNA damage, and apoptosis; and (3) elucidate the contribution of the ATM/Chk2/p53 signaling pathway to this protective mechanism.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

BPA (≥99% purity), vitamin C, and MS-222 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). l-Cysteine, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), paraformaldehyde (PFA), a hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining kit, proteinase K, penicillin–streptomycin, RIPA lysis buffer, and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). A terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) kit was purchased from Boster (Wuhan, China), and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was obtained from ZSGB-BIO (Beijing, China). The TRIpure Reagent and a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit were supplied by Bioteke (Wuxi, China). EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix were purchased from TransGen Biotech (Beijing, China). 2× SYBR Premix UrTaqII was purchased from Nobelab Biotech (Beijing, China). Alpha minimum essential medium (α-MEM) was obtained from Hyclone (Logan, UT, USA). Newborn calf serum (NCS) was from Biological Industries (Kibbutz Beit-Haemek, Israel), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) was from Gibco (South America). A ROS assay kit was obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). An Annexin V-EGFP/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit was purchased from KeyGEN BioTECH (Nanjing, China). Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.2 μm) were from Merck Millipore (Tullagreen, Ireland). Primary antibodies were obtained from the following sources: anti-γH2AX (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-ATM (ABclonal, Wuhan, China), anti-phospho-Chk2Thr68 (HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), anti-Chk2 (Affinity Biosciences, Melbourne, Australia), anti-p53 (Wanleibio, Shenyang, China), and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat antirabbit and goat antimouse secondary antibodies were obtained from ZSGB-BIO (Beijing, China).

Animal Husbandry and Embryo Collection

Adult X. laevis were maintained in a recirculating aquatic husbandry system (ESEN Water-Purification Tech, China) with charcoal-filtered water at 20 °C under a 12 h: 12 h light: dark photoperiod. For breeding, pairs were injected into the dorsal lymph sac with human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG; 700 and 400 IU/male). After 16 h, eggs were collected by gentle abdominal expression and transferred into 0.1× Marc’s modified ringer’s (MMR) solution. Males were anesthetized with MS-222 and euthanized for testis collection. Testes were minced in 0.1× MMR to prepare sperm suspensions, which were immediately mixed with eggs for in vitro fertilization. Fertilized eggs were dejellied in 2% (w/v) l-cysteine (pH 8.0) and rinsed gently with 0.1 x MMR. Healthy embryos at the two-cell stage were selected and maintained at 22 ± 0.5 °C for subsequent experiments. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Henan Institute of Science and Technology (LLSC2024035).

Waterborne Exposure

Two-cell stage embryos were randomly assigned to 60 mm glass dishes containing 15 mL of 0.1× MMR with BPA (0, 1, or 10 μM) in the presence or absence of vitamin C (50 μM; optimized in preliminary experiments; Figure S1). BPA stock solutions were prepared in DMSO, and vitamin C stock solutions were prepared in sterile water. The final DMSO concentration was 0.01% (v/v) in all groups. Exposure media were renewed daily. Each dish contained 20 embryos, and three independent replicate dishes were used per treatment.

Development and Behavioral Assessments

Embryos were examined at regular intervals by using a stereomicroscope (SZX7, Olympus, Japan). Mortality and malformation rates were recorded daily. Malformations at 96 h postexposure (hpe) were evaluated based on the score of malformation (SOM) and the profile of phenotypes as described previously.

For morphometric analysis, embryos at 96 hpe were fixed with 4% PFA overnight. The full length, head length, and eye diameter were quantified using ImageView 3.7 software (Jingtong Instrument Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) according to predefined landmarks (Figure S2).

Behavior was assessed by touch-response tests at 48, 60, and 72 hpe and by autonomous swimming at 96 hpe. Touch responses were elicited by gently contacting the dorsal tail and head regions with a plastic straw. For autonomous swimming, locomotor trajectories, distance traveled, swimming speed, parallel index, and turning tendency were recorded and analyzed by using Smart Video Tracking Software (Smart 3.0, Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). Parallel index (−1 to 1) reflects the consistency of movement direction, with values closer to 1 indicating straighter trajectories and values closer to −1 indicating more frequent or larger direction changes (greater tortuosity). Absolute turning tendency (0 to 1) quantifies the intensity of turning regardless of the left or right direction, with larger values indicating stronger turning and more tortuous paths.

Notochord Histology

To evaluate notochord development, embryos at 96 hpe were fixed in 4% PFA overnight, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections (6 μm) were prepared using a rotary microtome (RM2235, Leica, Germany). Transverse and longitudinal sections from the mid A–P axis were subjected to H&E staining and TUNEL assay. A semiquantitative histological damage scoring system based on the lumpers ordinal method with a 0–4 scale (see Table S1 for detailed scoring criteria) was established to evaluate somite and notochord lesions.

Single-Cell Gel Electrophoresis

DNA strand breaks were quantified using alkaline single-cell gel electrophoresis (SCGE) as described previously. Briefly, single-cell suspensions were mixed with 0.5% low-melting-point agarose (LMA) and layered onto slides precoated with 0.5% normal-melting-point agarose (NMA). After gel solidification, a cell-free LMA layer was applied. Slides were lysed overnight at 4 °C in cold lysis buffer (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM Na2EDTA, 10 mM Tris, 1% sodium sarcosinate, pH 10, with 1% Triton X–100, and 10% DMSO) and washed three times with PBS. DNA was unwound for 30 min in an alkaline electrophoresis buffer (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM Na2EDTA, pH 13), followed by electrophoresis at 25 V and 300 mA for 20 min. Slides were neutralized with 0.4 M Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) for 15 min and stained with a red fluorescent nucleic acid dye. Comets were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse 50i, Nikon, Japan). For each group, 100 randomly selected cells were analyzed with CASP software (version 1.2.3, Comet Assay Software Program, CASPLab). DNA damage indices included tail length, percentage of tail DNA, tail moment, and olive tail moment.

TUNEL Assay

Apoptotic cells in embryonic tissue sections were detected using the TUNEL kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sections were quenched with 3% H2O2 for 10 min at room temperature, digested with 0.5% proteinase K (in Tris-buffered saline, TBS) for 3 min at 37 °C, and washed three times with TBS. The sections were incubated with labeling solution at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by sealing solution at room temperature for 30 min, and diluted biotinylated antidigoxigenin antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. The signal was developed with DAB for 15 min and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Cell Culture and Treatments

P19SCs, which are characterized by pluripotency similar to that of embryonic stem cells and commonly used as an in vitro model of early embryogenesis, were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 7.5% NCS, 2.5% FBS, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. P19SCs were seeded into plates and allowed to attach for 24 h, followed by exposure to BPA (50 or 100 μM) and/or vitamin C (15 μM) for ROS determination and gene/protein expression analyses. These BPA concentrations were selected based on our prior dose–response characterization in P19SCs, which showed that 50 and 100 μM BPA maintained approximately 80–90% cell viability with preserved morphology, thereby providing sublethal and reproducible conditions for mechanistic analysis. DMSO was used as the vehicle at 0.01% (v/v) in all groups. Media were refreshed every 24 h.

Real-Time Cellular Analysis

Cell viability and proliferation were monitored using an xCELLigence real-time cell analysis (RTCA) system (ACEA Biosciences, USA). P19SCs were seeded into 16-well E-plates at 5 × 103 cells/well in 150 μL of α-MEM containing the indicated concentrations of BPA and/or vitamin C. The cell index (CI), which represents the electronic impedance changes caused by adherent cells, was automatically calculated and normalized with RTCA software.

Intracellular ROS Measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the ROS assay kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. After exposure, cells were incubated with 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. DCF fluorescence was measured using a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti, Nikon, Japan) and a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO, Tecan, Austria) at Ex/Em = 488/525 nm.

Annexin V/PI Staining and Flow Cytometry

Cellular apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-EGFP/PI apoptosis detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer containing 5 μL of Annexin V-EGFP and 5 μL of PI. After incubation for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The percentages of Annexin V-positive and PI-positive cells were calculated.

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIpure Reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed prior to reverse transcription. The cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix and stored at −20 °C. The real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using 2× SYBR Premix UrTaqII and gene-specific primers (Table S2). Target genes included antioxidant-related genes (gpx4, sod, ppard, and cat.2) and apoptosis/DNA damage response genes (bcl-2, bax, caspase 3, puma, atm, chk2, and p53). Amplification was conducted on a QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA): 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 20 s; and 72 °C for 20 s. Relative expression was calculated using the 2 –ΔΔCt method and normalized to the housekeeping gene eukaryotic elongation factor (ef1α) for X. laevis and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) for P19SCs.

Western Blot

Total proteins were extracted in RIPA buffer and quantified using the BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to the PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against γH2AX (1:1000), cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000), ATM (1:1000), Chk2 (1:1000), phospho-Chk2T68 (1:2000), P53 (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:5000). After being washed, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized with an Amersham Imager 600 instrument (Cytiva, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). For all endpoints except semiquantitative scores, differences among treatment groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Semiquantitative scores were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Vitamin C Alleviated the Embryonic Developmental Toxicity of BPA in X. laevis Embryos

Following BPA exposure, mortality and malformation rates increased in dose- and time-dependent manner. Vitamin C significantly alleviated the increased mortality (p values ranged from 0.012 to <0.001 compared with the 10 μM BPA group during 48–96 h exposure, and p = 0.009 compared with the 1 μM BPA group at 96 hpe) and malformation rates (p values ranged from 0.029 to 0.002 compared with the 10 μM BPA group during 48–96 h exposure, and p = 0.019 compared with the 1 μM BPA group at 96 hpe; Figure A,B). Embryos exposed to 1 μM and 10 μM BPA exhibited significantly retarded development, including a shortened A–P axis (p = 0.004 and <0.001, respectively), reduced head length (p < 0.001), and smaller eye diameter (p = 0.002 and <0.001, respectively). Co-treatment with vitamin C significantly mitigated these BPA-induced growth defects, including shortening of the A–P axis (p = 0.006 and <0.001, respectively), head length (p = 0.009 and 0.001, respectively), and eye diameter (p = 0.027 and 0.001, respectively; Figure C–E).

(C–E) Vitamin C mitigated BPA-induced growth retardation, as reflected by increase in full length (C), head length (D), and mean eye diameter (E) at 96 hpe. (F) Vitamin C significantly lowered the overall malformation incidence and mean SOM in BPA-exposed embryos. (G,H) Vitamin C decreased SOM and improved the distribution of individual malformation phenotypes at 96 hpe. Phenotypes included bent tail (bt), abdominal edema (ae), short tail axis (sta), bent notochord (bn), edema in proctodaeum (ep), and edema in heart (eh). (I) H&E staining indicated that vitamin C ameliorated BPA-induced histopathological alterations, including indistinct somite boundaries (black arrow, longitudinal section), loose or thinned somites (black arrowheads, transverse section), shrunken notochord vacuoles (red arrow), and a smaller neural tube (red arrowhead). The SOM and phenotype profiling showed that the percentage of deformed embryos and the average SOM were significantly increased after 96 h of BPA exposure (p < 0.001; Figure F). Vitamin C markedly reduced the teratogenic effects of BPA, particularly a bent tail, abdominal edema, shortened tail axis, and bent notochord (Figure G,H).

Neurodevelopment was assessed by touch response at 48, 60, and 72 hpe and by autonomous swimming analysis at 96 hpe. Embryos exposed to BPA for 48–72 h showed a significantly reduced response rate to gentle tactile stimuli (p values ranged from 0.003 to 0.001), which was significantly restored by vitamin C cotreatment (p values ranged from 0.027 to 0.001; Figure S3A). Autonomous swimming analysis revealed that BPA exposure disrupted normal swimming trajectories (Figure S3B) and significantly decreased the parallel index (p = 0.013 and <0.001, respectively) while increasing the absolute turning tendency (p < 0.001; Figure S3D,E). Swimming performance was also impaired at 96 hpe, as indicated by decreased maximum speed, mean speed, and travel distance (p < 0.001; Figure S3C). Co-treatment with vitamin C for 96 h restored swimming ability, yielding more regular trajectories and recovering the parallel index (p = 0.048 and 0.042, respectively) and absolute turning tendency (p = 0.008 and 0.002, respectively). Moreover, mean speed (p = 0.012 and 0.029, respectively) and travel distance (both p = 0.001) were significantly improved compared with the BPA-treated groups (Figure S3B–E).

H&E staining of the middle region of the A–P axis showed that BPA caused damage to somites and the notochord. The affected embryos exhibited blurred somite boundaries, loose and thinner somites, and shrunken vacuolar cells with reduced vacuolar size within the notochord, as reflected by elevated composite scores of somite and notochord injuries. With vitamin C supplementation, embryos exhibited dense and well-defined somites, enlarged vacuolar cells, and significantly decreased composite scores of somite injury (p = 0.004 and 0.026, respectively) and notochord injury (p = 0.004 and 0.002, respectively) compared to those of the BPA-treated groups, indicating that vitamin C mitigated BPA-induced tissue damage to these structures (Figure I–K).

Vitamin C Alleviated the Decline in Antioxidant Capacity and DNA Damage Induced by BPA in X. laevis Embryos

To investigate the effects of BPA and vitamin C on the antioxidant capacity, mRNA expression levels of gpx4, sod, ppard, and cat.2 were determined. As shown in Figure A, expression of these genes was significantly downregulated by BPA exposure (p values ranged from 0.048 to <0.001, except for gpx4 expression in the 1 μM BPA group, p = 0.316). Vitamin C supplementation significantly attenuated these changes (p values ranged from 0.04 to 0.001, except for gpx4 expression compared with the 1 μM BPA group, p = 0.074), indicating restoration of antioxidant capacity in X. laevis embryos.

Exact p values for pairwise comparisons are provided in Supporting Information, Table S3.

SCGE was used to assess DNA damage. The comet tail, containing fragmented DNA, reflects the extent of DNA lesions in the individual cells. As shown in Figure B,C, all comet parameters measured, including tail length, percentage of tail DNA, tail moment, and olive tail moment, were significantly increased in BPA-exposed embryos (p < 0.001). These increases were significantly reduced by vitamin C cotreatment (p values ranged from 0.007 to <0.001, except for olive tail moment compared with the 1 μM BPA group, p = 0.062).

Vitamin C Alleviated BPA-Induced Apoptosis in X. laevis Embryos

TUNEL staining showed that embryos exposed to 10 μM BPA exhibited a significantly larger TUNEL-positive area along the A–P axis compared with the control group (p = 0.006). This TUNEL-positive area was significantly reduced in the vitamin C cotreated group compared with the BPA group (p = 0.004; Figure A). RT-qPCR analysis showed that mRNA expression levels of bax, caspase 3, and the bax/bcl-2 ratio (2.7-fold and 4.4-fold increases in the 1 μM and 10 μM BPA groups, respectively) were significantly elevated in BPA-exposed embryos (p values ranged from 0.039 to <0.001), whereas bcl-2 expression was significantly decreased compared with the control group (p < 0.001). Vitamin C significantly reduced bax and caspase 3 expression and the bax/bcl-2 ratio (2.1-fold and 2.4-fold changes compared with the 1 μM and 10 μM BPA groups, respectively; p values ranged from 0.024 to 0.001) and significantly increased bcl-2 expression (p = 0.023 and <0.001 compared with the 1 μM and 10 μM BPA groups, respectively; Figure B). In parallel, mRNA expression levels of the DNA damage response genes atm, chk2, and p53 were also reduced by vitamin C cotreatment (Figure C). Collectively, these results indicate that vitamin C suppressed BPA-induced apoptotic responses in X. laevis embryos.

Vitamin C Ameliorated Oxidative Stress and DNA Damage Induced by BPA in P19SCs

To assess the capacity of vitamin C to reduce oxidative stress, intracellular ROS levels in P19SCs were measured and are indicated by green fluorescence. BPA treatment significantly increased ROS levels in P19SCs (p < 0.001). Co-treatment with vitamin C markedly reduced this increase compared with the BPA-only groups (p < 0.001 and p = 0.007, respectively), indicating that vitamin C effectively mitigated BPA-induced ROS generation in P19SCs (Figure A).

(B,C) Vitamin C attenuated BPA-induced DNA damage, as shown by representative comet images (B) and reduced comet indices (C). n = 100. (D) Vitamin C suppressed BPA-induced accumulation of γH2AX protein. N = 3. ***p < 0.001 vs control; †††p < 0.001 vs 50 μM BPA; ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001 vs 100 μM BPA. Exact p values for pairwise comparisons are provided in Supporting Information, Table S4.

The effect of vitamin C on DNA damage was further evaluated by SCGE and by the analysis of the protein expression of the DNA damage marker phospho-H2AX (γH2AX) in P19SCs. As shown in Figure B,C, vitamin C significantly reduced BPA-induced increases in comet assay indices (p values ranged from 0.002 to <0.001), with the exception of olive tail moment and tail moment in comparison with the 50 μM BPA group (p = 0.067 and 0.082, respectively). Consistently, γH2AX protein levels in the cotreated groups were significantly lower than those in the BPA-treated groups (p < 0.001; Figure D), indicating that vitamin C alleviated BPA-induced DNA damage in P19SCs.

Vitamin C Attenuated Apoptosis by Blocking BPA-Induced Activation of the ATM/Chk2/p53 Pathway in P19SCs

To evaluate the effect of vitamin C on apoptosis in P19SCs, RTCA and flow cytometry were performed. RTCA showed that cell indices in the cotreated groups were significantly higher than those in the BPA-treated groups (p < 0.001), indicating that vitamin C restored the viability of BPA-treated P19SCs (Figure A). Flow cytometric analysis showed that vitamin C effectively decreased the proportion of apoptotic cells, which was elevated after BPA exposure (Figure B). The expression of the apoptosis marker cleaved caspase-3 was investigated by Western blot, and vitamin C significantly attenuated the BPA-induced upregulation of cleaved caspase-3 (p = 0.001 and 0.012, respectively; Figure E). In addition, vitamin C significantly suppressed BPA-induced increases in the mRNA expression of Puma (p = 0.002 and 0.005, respectively) and reduced the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, showing 2.3-fold and 3.6-fold decreases compared with the 50 μM and 100 μM BPA groups, respectively (p < 0.001; Figure C). These results indicate that vitamin C blocked the BPA-induced activation of mitochondrial apoptosis-related gene transcription and apoptosis in P19SCs.

To clarify the mechanism underlying the anti-apoptotic effect of vitamin C, the p53-mediated apoptotic pathway in P19SCs was examined. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses showed that BPA treatment significantly increased the expression of ATM, Chk2, phospho-Chk2T68, and p53 at both the mRNA level (p values ranged from 0.001 to <0.001) and the protein level (p values ranged from 0.033 to <0.001), indicating activation of the ATM/Chk2/p53 pathway. In contrast, cotreatment with vitamin C significantly suppressed BPA-induced upregulation of this signaling cascade (p values ranged from 0.041 to <0.001; Figure D,E), indicating that attenuation of BPA-induced apoptosis by vitamin C is associated with suppression of the ATM/Chk2/p53 signaling axis.

Discussion

Dietary exposure is the primary route of BPA contact as BPA can migrate from food packaging and water bottles. According to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), average BPA concentrations exceed 30 μg/kg in seven packaged food categories, including grain and grain-based products; legumes, nuts, and oilseeds; meat and meat products; fish and other seafood; herbs, spices, and condiments; composite foods; and snacks, desserts, and other foods. In unpackaged foods, reported levels are 9.4 μg/kg for meat and meat products and 7.4 μg/kg for fish and other seafood. EFSA lowered the tolerable daily intake (TDI) from 50 to 4 μg/kg bw/day in 2015 and further established a TDI of 0.2 μg/kg bw/day in a 2023 re-evaluation of public health risks. In that assessment, EFSA reported that BPA dietary exposure across all age groups exceeds the new TDI by two to 3 orders of magnitude, raising substantial health concerns. Reproductive and developmental toxicity of BPA has been widely reported across species and cell types, whereas studies addressing potential protective interventions remain limited. One proposed mechanism is BPA-induced oxidative stress, characterized by suppression of the antioxidant system and increased ROS production. Owing to its established antioxidant activity, vitamin C is widely applied in the food industry as an additive to inhibit oxidation, preserve color, flavor, and nutrients, and extend shelf life in products such as juices, canned foods, and oils. Jahan et al. reported that vitamin C alleviated oxidative stress, DNA damage, and reproductive impairment caused by mercury exposure in male rats. In the present study, vitamin C mitigated BPA-induced embryonic malformations and reversed the downregulation of oxidative stress-related genes, including gpx4, sod, ppard, and cat.2, in X. laevis embryos. These genes encode key antioxidant enzymes, such as GPX4, SOD, and catalase, which are essential for scavenging excess ROS. Restoration of their expression enhanced the cellular antioxidant capacity, which was further supported by reduced ROS production in P19SCs and attenuation of BPA-induced oxidative DNA damage.

Once ROS is generated, it induces DNA strand breaks and base modifications, such as 8-oxoguanine, leading to oxidative DNA lesions that affect cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Brieño-Enríquez et al. and Hu et al. showed that BPA upregulates genes associated with double-strand break (DSB) formation and promotes the appearance of the DSB marker γH2AX foci in human fetal oocytes and embryonic cells, respectively. Consistent with these observations, our results show that BPA suppresses antioxidant gene expression, elevates ROS levels, and increases the level of γH2AX expression in both X. laevisembryos and P19SCs. These molecular alterations represent mechanistic endpoints and potential early biomarkers of embryotoxic stress. This interpretation aligns with growing interest in early detection of subclinical injury. Yao et al. reported that routinely measured peripheral immune markers during early pregnancy could serve as accessible indicators of preeclampsia risk. Population-based studies in children conducted by Zhou et al. and Rocha et al. identified a significant positive association between BPA exposure and the oxidative DNA damage biomarker 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) levels, supporting the relevance of oxidative stress markers in realistic exposure settings. Therefore, the oxidative stress-related markers identified in this study, including γH2AX and altered antioxidant gene expression, which precede overt morphological abnormalities, may be suitable for integration into biomarker-based strategies for predicting developmental risk. Importantly, oxidative DNA damage can be reduced by enhancing the antioxidant capacity to suppress ROS generation. Abdallah et al. supplemented BPA alternatives with N-acetylcysteine, which decreased oxidative DNA lesions, mitigated delayed meiotic initiation, and reduced oocyte aneuploidy. In the present study, vitamin C increased antioxidant-related gene expression, limited ROS accumulation, and alleviated BPA-induced oxidative DNA damage, supporting the antioxidant-based protective strategy as a feasible translational approach that aligns with early detection and intervention strategies.

A primary cellular response to severe DNA damage is apoptosis mediated by activation of the p53 pathway. Following DSBs, ATM activates Chk2, leading to p53 stabilization and induction of pro-apoptotic genes such as bax and puma. This canonical DNA damage response has been implicated in BPA-induced genotoxicity. A comprehensive review by Ďurovcová et al., summarized evidence showing that BPA exposure increases Chk2 and p53 expression in multiple cell models and proposed that BPA-induced oxidative stress triggers DNA damage and subsequent activation of the ATM/Chk2/p53 pathway. In this study, BPA activated the ATM/Chk2/p53 pathway by increasing ATM, Chk2, and p53 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels and by stimulating the transcription of the p53 target genes bax and puma. These changes ultimately promoted apoptosis in X. laevis embryos and P19SCs. Vitamin C reduced ATM, Chk2, and p53 expression at both transcriptional and translational levels, indicating that reduction of embryonic cell apoptosis is associated with the suppression of ATM/Chk2/p53 pathway activation. Similar findings have been reported for other antioxidants. Song et al. showed that melatonin alleviated zearalenone-induced ovarian and granulosa cell damage by reducing oxidative stress and modulating the ATM-Chk2-p53 pathway. Li et al. reported that supplementation with glutathione or cysteine reduced ROS levels and apoptosis in intracytoplasmic sperm injection-derived porcine embryos, thereby improving cleavage and blastocyst formation rates. It should be noted that although ATM acts as a major kinase in response to DSBs, BPA-induced embryotoxicity is unlikely to be mediated by a single pathway. A previous study reported the involvement of the ATR/Chk1/p53 axis in BPA-induced cytotoxicity in BEAS-2B cells. In addition, bulk RNA-sequencing analyses revealed that BPA exposure induced excessive oxidative stress and concurrently activated endoplasmic reticulum stress/unfolded protein response signaling, intrinsic apoptosis, and ferroptosis, collectively contributing to blastoid developmental failure.

Collectively, numerous studies indicate that BPA exposure at environmentally relevant or low-dose chronic levels can elicit key toxicological responses, including ROS generation, DNA damage, and apoptosis. For example, Biswas et al. reported that BPA exposure at environmentally relevant concentrations (1–100 μg/L) induced oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptotic signaling in zebrafish ovaries. In addition, chronic exposure to BPA at doses defined as eco-relevant by the authors (0.5–50 mg/kg/day) resulted in oxidative stress and apoptosis in mouse liver. Moreover, population-based studies further reported positive associations between urinary BPA levels and increased sperm DSBs in men. Hou et al. demonstrated that the activation of the ATM pathway in response to DNA damage induced by 2 μg/mL deoxynivalenol was mitigated by pretreatment with vitamin C or vitamin E, supporting the role of antioxidants in modulating DNA damage–ATM signaling. Together, these findings suggest that the oxidative stress–DNA damage–apoptosis axis represents a conserved toxicological framework across species and exposure scenarios. In this context, the toxic effects are observed in X. laevis embryos and P19SCs in this study can contribute to our understanding of the mechanisms underlying BPA-induced embryotoxicity.

Apoptosis-inducing stimuli generate cellular stress, including ROS accumulation and DNA damage, which increases apoptosis and reduces embryonic quality, leading to developmental delay, malformations, and death. Embryos show high sensitivity to environmental stressors, particularly in vulnerable structures such as the notochord and neural tissues. This tissue-specific susceptibility to stress has also been described in immune cells. Kang et al. demonstrated that uterine tissue-resident CD4+ and CD8+ T cells display distinct functional characteristics to balance local immune defense and fetal tolerance, indicating that different tissues and cell types possess specialized stress-response mechanisms. Similarly, the notochord, which is essential for embryonic axis formation and neurodevelopment, shows heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress and apoptosis, which may explain its preferential damage following BPA exposure in the present study.

Beyond clarifying the protective mechanism of vitamin C against BPA-induced embryotoxicity, these findings highlight the importance of reproductive safety evaluation, including the assessment of environmental pollutant exposure and protective strategies, within modern reproductive medicine and public health frameworks. Systematic evaluation of environmental exposures and protective approaches is essential for the formation of public health policy and clinical practice. This principle is illustrated by Yang et al., who showed in a prospective cohort study that rigorous safety assessment of medical interventions is necessary to guide clinical decision-making without compromising reproductive results. In this context, this study contributes to safety evaluation by elucidating the developmental toxicity mechanism of BPA and by validating the protective efficacy of vitamin C. These data address an important gap in the evaluation of environmental contaminant-associated embryotoxicity. It should be noted that, in the present study, vitamin C was administered simultaneously with BPA, reflecting antioxidant protection observed under a coexposure design, which differs from both dietary preloading and postexposure scenarios. Consistent with this interpretation, previous studies have reported mitigation of oxidative stress-related end points and tissue injury in vitamin C and BPA cotreatment models. Fewer reports have shown that pre-exposure to vitamin C can attenuate BPA-associated oxidative stress and tissue damage, and vitamin C preincubation reduced oxidative DNA damage in cells exposed to BPA-related dental methacrylates. In contrast, direct evidence supporting vitamin C post-treatment, specifically in BPA-induced toxicity models, is currently limited. Therefore, whether vitamin C remains effective when administered after the initial BPA exposure window was not tested here and remains to be determined. Evidence from other oxidative injury models indicates that vitamin C administered after an insult can still reduce ROS accumulation, oxidative DNA damage, and apoptosis. , Together, these observations suggest a mechanistic basis for postexposure antioxidant protection.

Although our data support a protective role of vitamin C in protecting against BPA-induced embryotoxicity, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the BPA concentrations used in mechanistic assays with P19SCs (50–100 μM) are substantially higher than typical environmental exposure levels and were selected to elicit clear core molecular responses rather than to model real-world exposure. Second, the study relied on X. laevis embryos and murine P19SCs, which may not fully recapitulate mammalian in vivo metabolism and placental transfer. Third, the coexposure design evaluated concurrent vitamin C protection only, leaving the efficacy of pre-exposure loading and post-exposure intervention unresolved. Fourth, mechanistic conclusions are based on pathway activation patterns and lack direct causal validation by pathway-specific inhibitors or genetic loss-of-function approaches. Future studies should (1) test environmentally relevant, low-dose chronic exposure paradigms in mammalian in vivo developmental models that capture maternal–fetal transfer and metabolism; (2) determine the effective intervention window by comparing vitamin C pretreatment, cotreatment, and post-treatment paradigms; and (3) establish mechanistic causality by using ATM/Chk2/p53 pathway-specific inhibitors or gene knockdown/knockout strategies. These steps will clarify exposure–response relationships and refine antioxidant-based protective strategies against developmental toxicants.

In summary, this study demonstrates that vitamin C alleviates BPA-induced embryotoxicity by restoring antioxidant gene expression, reducing oxidative DNA damage, and inhibiting apoptosis via the modulation of the ATM/Chk2/p53 pathway (Figure ). These findings support an antioxidant-based protective strategy against a common environmental contaminant and emphasize the role of antioxidant defense in reproductive and developmental safety. Although the Xenopus model provides a valuable vertebrate system for mechanistic analysis of developmental toxicity, further studies in mammalian models using environmentally relevant exposure designs across diverse exposure windows are required to translate these protective effects to human-relevant physiology. Future work should also examine potential synergistic effects between vitamin C and other antioxidants to develop more effective strategies against complex environmental exposures, including bisphenol analogues.

Proposed mechanism by which vitamin C counteracts BPA-induced embryotoxicity. BPA exposure induces oxidative stress and DNA damage, which is accompanied by activation of the ATM/Chk2/p53 signaling cascade and subsequent apoptosis, ultimately resulting in developmental malformations. Vitamin C acts by scavenging ROS, restoring antioxidant defenses, limiting DNA damage, and thereby dampening pathway activation and apoptosis.
