A mediastinal eosinophilic adenoma misdiagnosed as an esophageal stromal tumor: A case report
Authors: Siyu Chen, Jusi Wang, Ning Ao, Shouyin Di, Hui Wu, Taiqian Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEosinophilic adenoma, a benign tumor characterized by eosinophilic cell proliferation, typically arises in endocrine organs such as the thyroid or parathyroid glands. Ectopic parathyroid eosinophilic adenoma is rare and may mimic other mediastinal pathologies, leading to diagnostic challenges.Case presentationWe present a case of a 54-year-old woman with a one-year history of dysphagia. Initial imaging and endoscopic studies suggested an esophageal stromal tumor. Thoracoscopic resection revealed a posterior mediastinal mass, later confirmed as an ectopic parathyroid eosinophilic adenoma through histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis.ConclusionsThis case underscores the importance of integrating imaging, laboratory tests, and histopathology for accurate diagnosis of mediastinal masses. Minimally invasive thoracoscopic surgery proved effective for resection, highlighting the need for tailored therapeutic approaches. In order to avoid such misdiagnosis, clinicians should make a definite pathological diagnosis before surgery as much as possible.Keywords: Eosinophilic adenoma, Ectopic parathyroid gland, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Gastroscopy

Background

Esophageal stromal tumors, originating from smooth muscle or stromal tissue, commonly present with dysphagia or chest pain [1]. Eosinophilic adenoma is extremely rare in the posterior mediastinum,constituting 1–2% of all parathyroid adenomas [2]. Because the main structures in the posterior septum are the heart, great vessels, trachea and esophagus. Eosinophilic adenomas are epithelial tumors, especially of glandular epithelial origin, whereas mature, functional glandular epithelial tissue, absent or extremely rare in normal mediastinum, is restricted to ectopic tissue [3]. Though rare in the mediastinum, may mimic such lesions. Parathyroid-derived eosinophilic adenomas can induce hypercalcemia, necessitating surgical intervention [4]. This report details a misdiagnosed case, emphasizing the diagnostic pitfalls and management strategies.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old woman presented with progressive dysphagia for one year. Her medical history included well-controlled allergic asthma.

Endoscopic ultrasound identified a hypoechoic submucosal lesion (7.8 × 10.4 mm) arising from the muscularis propria at a distance of 20–26 cm from the incisor was observed, the mucosa of the posterior wall of the esophagus was elevated, and the surface mucosa was smooth. The ultrasonic examination showed that the submucosal lesion was of uneven hypoechogenicity, originating from the muscularis propria, with the local serosa not clearly visible. Based on the above findings, the initial diagnosis was considered to be an esophageal stromal tumor (Fig. 1). Its intact mucosa and origin from the muscularis propria might be mistaken for features of stromal tumors.

Endoscopic ultrasound image of the patient. A stromal tumor is possible (arrow)

Chest CT revealed a 64 × 29 × 18 mm posterior mediastinal mass compressing the esophagus, located on the posterior wall of the esophagus and the anterior edge of the vertebral body, The surrounding space was clear. The size, location and shape on CT were consistent with the results of EUS. After enhanced scanning, the mass was mainly enhanced at the edge, with obvious enhancement in the arterial phase, a slightly reduced enhancement degree in the delayed phase, non-enhanced areas inside, a smooth outer edge, and the esophagus was compressed and narrowed (Fig. 2). Its well-defined margin and arterial phase enhancement might be mistaken for features of a stromal tumor.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest. A stromal tumor is possible (arrow)

One abnormality on the preoperative laboratory testing was notable: serum calcium was mildly elevated, which was a possible clue toward parathyroid origin (2.7 mmol/L; normal: 2.08–2.6 mmol/L).

The patient underwent right-sided single-port thoracoscopic resection. Intraoperatively, a 6 × 3 × 2 cm well-circumscribed mass was identified adjacent to the spine; the mass could be seen beside the spine in the posterior mediastinum, with a relatively clear boundary and an obvious space from the esophageal muscular layer, distinct from the esophageal wall (Fig. 3A). Complete excision was achieved with minimal blood loss (20 mL). Histopathology demonstrated nests of eosinophilic cells with positive parathyroid hormone (PTH) immunostaining (Fig. 4A, B), confirming an ectopic parathyroid eosinophilic adenoma. Postoperative calcium normalized (2.18 mmol/L), and the patient was discharged on day 3.

Discussion

Eosinophilic adenoma is a relatively rare endocrine tumor, and ectopic parathyroid eosinophilic adenoma is even rarer [5]. On CT, eosinophilic adenoma usually presents as a well—defined mass, mostly with low—density or low—signal characteristics, and obvious enhancement can be seen after enhanced scanning [6]. In MRI examinations, the signal intensity of the tumor varies depending on its tissue components. It usually shows a low signal on T1—weighted imaging and a high signal on T2—weighted imagingTwo cases of Wolffian tumor with novel magnetic resonance imaging findings reflecting characteristic pathology [7]. During ultrasound examination, the tumor mostly presents as a homogeneous hypoechoic area with a clear boundary, and blood flow signals may be present inside, which can be used to evaluate the blood supply of the tumor. Ultrasound can also assist in evaluating the size of the tumor and its impact on surrounding structures [8]. 99mTc—MIBI scintigraphy is widely used in locating hyperfunctioning parathyroid glands, with high sensitivity and specificity [9, 10]. In addition, laboratory tests, such as blood calcium, PTH, and thyroid function tests, are also important links in the diagnostic process, which help to rule out other endocrine diseases and confirm the presence of parathyroid adenomas [11]. The importance of combining laboratory tests, including measurements of parathyroid hormone and calcium, with imaging techniques for a more accurate differential diagnosis of parathyroid disorders is emphasized. In this case, the preoperative CT report of the patient showed that the mass was mainly enhanced at the edge after enhanced scanning, with obvious enhancement in the arterial phase, a slightly reduced enhancement degree in the delayed phase, non—enhanced areas inside, a smooth outer edge, and the esophagus was compressed and narrowed. Considering the results of the endoscopic ultrasound in the external hospital, an esophageal stromal tumor was strongly suspected before the operation. However, under the background of medical insurance cost control, further examinations such as MRI and parathyroid hormone tests were not carried out, which was an important factor leading to misdiagnosis. However, even under cost constraints, when the lesion is close to the esophagus or thyroid gland, it should be recommended that basic endocrine testing be performed preferentially.

Key points for the differential diagnosis of suspected mediastinal Eosinophilic adenoma include the following. First, ectopic thyroid-derived oncocytic adenoma (Hurthle cell type) with an immunophenotype of TTF-1 +/TG +/PAX8 + and follicular histology must be ruled out [12]. Second, thymomas, especially type B subtype, should be strictly differentiated as they may contain eosinophilic cells with lymphocytic infiltration and express CD5 +/CD117 +/CKpan + [12]. Third, metastatic eosinophilic renal tumors, such as low-grade oncocytic tumors or ESC RCC, characterized by mutations in GPNMB + and TSC/MTOR pathways, should be suspected [13]. Fourth, salivary gland-type carcinoma, like primary clear cell lung carcinoma with EWSR1 translocation and S100 +/p63 +, should be excluded [14]. Additionally, non-epithelial lesions such as inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (ALK +), paraganglioma (CgA +/Syn +), and Castleman’s disease require differentiation [3, 15, 16]. The final diagnosis should integrate morphology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular detection to avoid misdiagnosis.

The main treatment for this disease is the surgical resection of parathyroid eosinophilic adenoma. Open surgery or minimally invasive surgery can be selected according to the specific situation of the tumor [5]. In this case, the patient initially visited the general surgery department. At that time, it was considered an esophageal lesion, and endoscopic resection was recommended. Endoscopic surgery has the advantages of minimal trauma and rapid recovery and is suitable for the resection of intramural esophageal tumors. However, in this case, the lesion originated from the parathyroid gland and was located outside the esophagus. If the esophageal lumen approach was used, it would be necessary to incise the mucosa and the entire muscular layer of the esophagus to reach the lesion. Even if the resection was complete, currently, metal clips are mostly used to close the incision under endoscopy [17]. This may not be effective for this patient, and under the negative pressure of the thoracic cavity, it would also increase the risks of esophageal diverticulum and thoracic infection. Finally, the patient in this case underwent thoracoscopic surgery, and the operation was successful with a good postoperative recovery. This minimally invasive surgical method can not only effectively and completely resect the mass but also reduce postoperative complications. After the operation, the patient needs to be followed up regularly to monitor calcium levels and parathyroid function to ensure the durability of the surgical effect [18].

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that in clinical practice, when facing mediastinal masses, it is necessary to attach importance to the combined application of imaging, endoscopic, and laboratory examinations, perform pathological diagnosis in a timely manner, and select an appropriate treatment plan according to the specific situation of the patient. Improving early detection tools and developing guidelines for the evaluation of mediastinal lesions will change the management of mediastinal lesions. Future research can focus on improving the early screening and diagnostic techniques for mediastinal masses to enhance the clinical treatment effect and better serve patients.
