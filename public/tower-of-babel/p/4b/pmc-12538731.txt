Healthy lifestyle partly mediates the association between self-rated health and risk of overall and cause-specific mortality
Authors: Ya Miao, Bin Zhao, Yi Yang, Kai Yu, Linda M Liao, Stephanie J Weinstein, Fang Fang, Ben Lu, Demetrius Albanes, Jiaqi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-rated health status is a subjective but important indicator of an individual’s perception of overall health. However, it remains unclear whether lifestyle may mediate or modify the association of self-rated health status with overall and cause-specific mortality.MethodsThis prospective cohort analysis included 401,410 US adults from the National Institutes of Health-AARP Diet and Health Study. Self-rated health was categorized as “very good to excellent,” “good,” or “poor to fair.” A healthy lifestyle was defined by a normal body mass index, never smoking, moderate alcohol consumption, adequate physical activity, and a higher diet quality score. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for overall and cause-specific mortality were estimated using multivariable-adjusted Cox models.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 23.6 years, we ascertained 181,776 deaths. Compared with individuals reporting “very good to excellent” health, those reporting “poor to fair” health had an elevated risk of overall mortality (HR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.87–1.93) as well as mortality from cardiovascular disease (CVD), heart disease, stroke, cancer, respiratory disease, diabetes, infection, and injuries and accidents (risk increment ranged from 24% to 361%). Mediation analysis showed that 5.1% to 33.6% of the observed associations were mediated by lifestyle. Self-rated health was more strongly associated with overall mortality than traditional risk factors except smoking. Compared with individuals reporting “very good to excellent” health who adhered to a healthier lifestyle, those reporting “poor to fair” health with a less healthy lifestyle experienced a significantly elevated risk of overall mortality and mortality from CVD, heart disease, cancer, and respiratory disease (relative excess risk due to interaction > 0).ConclusionsPoor self-rated health was significantly associated with higher risk of overall and cause-specific mortality, and these associations were partially mediated by lifestyle. A positive additive interaction between self-rated health and lifestyle was noted for overall mortality and for mortality from CVD, heart disease, cancer, and respiratory disease. These findings underscore the clinical importance of self-rated health and suggest that lifestyle modification may improve health and longevity, particularly among individuals with low or moderate self-rated health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04399-y.Keywords: Self-rated health, Healthy lifestyle, Mortality, Mediation analysis, Additive interaction

Background

Self-rated health is a subjective and important indicator that reflects an individual’s perception of their overall health, encompassing biological, mental, and social dimensions that may be less apparent to an outside observer [1, 2]. It reliably measures both a person’s health status and the burden of potential illnesses as it may capture subclinical biological and physiological changes [3]. Self-rated health status, queried through simple, one-item question, has been found to be a strong and independent predictor of morbidity and mortality, including overall mortality and mortality due to cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer [4–7]. However, additional evidence is needed regarding the long-term impact of self-rated health on mortality, as well as the potential mediators for such link.

Multiple studies have shown that modifiable lifestyle factors, including smoking, diet, alcohol use, body weight, and physical activity, can influence risk of mortality and the occurrence of chronic diseases [8–10]. In the Nurses’ Health Study and the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study including more than 110,000 participants, a healthy lifestyle (i.e., never smoking, body mass index [BMI] of 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, moderate to vigorous physical activity, moderate alcohol intake, and a higher diet quality score) was associated with an increased overall life expectancy and life expectancy free of major chronic diseases, including CVD, cancer, and type 2 diabetes [8]. However, it remains unclear whether lifestyle mediates the association between self-rated health and mortality or whether there is an interaction between self-rated health and lifestyle in modulating the risk of mortality.

To this end, we conducted analyses in the National Institutes of Health (NIH)-AARP Diet and Health Study including more than 400,000 participants who were followed for 24 years. We aimed to comprehensively examine the association of self-rated health with the risk of overall and cause-specific mortality. Additionally, we sought to explore the importance of self-rated health, relative to traditional risk factors, in predicting mortality. Furthermore, we evaluated whether lifestyle could mediate or modify the relation between self-rated health and mortality.

Methods

Study population

Details of the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study have been documented previously [11]. Briefly, a total of 617,119 participants aged 50 to 71 years were enrolled from six states (California, Florida, Louisiana, New Jersey, North Carolina, and Pennsylvania) and two metropolitan areas (Atlanta and Detroit) of the USA between 1995 and 1996. All participants completed and returned comprehensive questionnaires examining diet and other lifestyle factors, and returning the study questionnaires was regarded as an indication of informed consent for study participation, which is in line with the Common Rule requirements. The study was approved by the Special Studies Institutional Review Board of the US National Cancer Institute, NIH.

Following quality assessment of the questionnaire data, the baseline cohort consisted of 566,398 participants. Based on the exclusion criteria (Additional file 1: Text S1), the final analytic cohort was comprised of 401,410 participants, including 228,803 men and 172,607 women.

Exposure assessment

Self-rated health status was assessed through a question at baseline: “Would you say your health in general is: 1) Excellent, 2) Very good, 3) Good, 4) Fair, or 5) Poor.” In line with previous studies [12, 13], we classified self-rated health status as “high” (very good to excellent), “medium” (good), or “low” (poor to fair). We generated a healthy lifestyle score incorporating information on cigarette smoking, physical activity, BMI, alcohol consumption, and diet. This score was calculated as relatively simple overall measure of lifestyle which helps to address issues of multicollinearity in an analysis including all individual lifestyle factors. This method has been validated and used successfully in previous studies [8, 14]. Information on lifestyle factors was collected through baseline questionnaires including the National Cancer Institute Diet History Questionnaire (DHQ) with 124 dietary items. We calculated a Healthy Eating Index (HEI)−2015 score including 13 dietary components that assessed overall diet quality (Additional file 1: Table S1) [11]. The study participants were assigned a score of 1 for each of the following low-risk lifestyle factors: normal BMI (18.5– < 25 kg/m2), never smoking, moderate alcohol consumption (5 g ethanol/day to 30 g/day for men and 5 to 15 g/day for women), adequate physical activity (≥ 3 times per week), and a higher HEI-2015 score (upper 40% percentile). The sum of these scores was defined as a healthy lifestyle score, ranging from 0 to 5, with a higher score indicating a better/healthier lifestyle. The healthy lifestyle score was used as an indicator of overall lifestyle.

Outcome assessment

Vital status was identified through linkage with the Social Security Administration Death Master File (including data from national death register), which has been shown to exhibit an accuracy of 95% for ascertainment of mortality [15]. Causes of death were identified via linkage with the National Death Index Plus [16], using the 9th and 10th revisions of the International Classification of Diseases codes (Additional file 1: Table S2). In the analyses of both overall and cause-specific mortality, study participants were followed from the date when the baseline questionnaires were completed (during 1995–1996) to the date of death or the end of follow-up (December 31, 2019), whichever came first.

Assessment of covariates

Information on covariates was collected via baseline questionnaires, including age at baseline (continuous variable), sex (men or women), race or ethnic group (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, or other race/ethnicity), educational level (less than high school, high school graduate, post-high school training or some college, or college graduate or above), marital status (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), vitamin supplement use (yes or no), and history of cancer among first-degree relatives (yes or no). Participants with missing information on a covariate were assigned a missing indicator for categorical variables and the mean value of the entire cohort for continuous variables.

Statistical analysis

Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank test were used to estimate cumulative incidence of mortality for study participants with different self-rated health status and healthy lifestyle score. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as estimates of the associations between self-rated health status and overall and cause-specific mortality, with time since start of follow-up as the underlying time metric. In the analyses of cause-specific mortality, follow-up was censored at the time of mortality from other causes. The proportional hazards assumption was tested by including a cross-product term of exposure variables and follow-up time in the regression model. In the analysis of overall mortality, a quantitative interaction was observed between self-rated health status and time (i.e., the estimate of HR was in the same direction but the magnitude of the estimate diminished over time), although no qualitative interaction was noted (i.e., the estimate of HR changed in direction over time). Because of this, although we presented results of the main analysis without stratifying by follow-up time, we did report the HR during different periods of follow-up as a subgroup analysis. We also plotted Kaplan–Meier curves to assess the cumulative incidence of overall mortality across various levels of categorical covariates. Generally, there was no evidence of crossed curves during follow-up period.

In the multivariable-adjusted model 1, we adjusted for age at entry, sex, race or ethnic group, educational level, marital status, history of diabetes, and vitamin supplement use. Vitamin supplement use has been shown to be associated with both the risk of mortality and self-reported overall health [17–19]. As a result, vitamin supplement use might serve as a potential confounding factor for the association between self-reported health and mortality; we thus adjusted for vitamin supplement use in the multivariable-adjusted models. In the analysis of cancer mortality, model 1 was further adjusted for history of cancer among first-degree relatives. P values for trend were obtained from models where self-rated health was used as a continuous variable. To assess the potential mediating role of lifestyle in the association between self-rated health and mortality, we additionally adjusted for the healthy lifestyle score in model 2. We also conducted a mediation analysis to quantify to which extent the association between self-rated health and mortality was mediated through the healthy lifestyle score. We applied the “difference method” to calculate the mediation proportion and its 95% CI by comparing estimates from models with and without adjustment for the healthy lifestyle score, using the %MEDIATE macro in the SAS 9.4 software [20]. Further, to evaluate the synergistic effect of self-rated health and healthy lifestyle, we first categorized the participants into nine groups according to self-rated health (low, medium, or high) and the healthy lifestyle score (0–1, 2, or ≥ 3). We then assessed the additive interaction between self-rated health and healthy lifestyle score based on the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), as well as their corresponding 95% CIs [21]. On the additive scale, RERI of 0 or AP of 0 indicates no interaction, RERI > 0 or AP > 0 indicates a positive interaction, whereas RERI < 0 or AP < 0 indicates a negative interaction. To evaluate the importance of self-rated health in predicting overall and cause-specific mortality, we calculated the adjusted R2 values from the Cox models to estimate the relative importance of self-rated health, in comparison with other important risk factors for mortality, including smoking status, BMI, diabetes, diet, physical activity, alcohol consumption, race or ethnicity, education, and marital status [22, 23].

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the findings. 1) To test for potential effect modifiers of the study associations, we conducted stratified analyses by age at baseline (< 60, 60– < 65, or ≥ 65 years), sex (men or women), diabetes (yes or no), BMI (< 25, 25– < 30, or ≥ 30 kg/m2), vitamin supplement use (yes or no), and time of follow-up (0– < 5, 5– < 15, or ≥ 15 years) in the analyses of overall and CVD mortality. Likelihood-ratio tests were used to test the statistical significance of the interactions between self-rated health and these variables by comparing Cox models with or without the cross-product terms of self-rated health and these variables. 2) In model 2, instead of adjusting for healthy lifestyle score as a proxy for overall lifestyle, we adjusted for individual lifestyle factors, including BMI (< 18.5, 18.5– < 25, 25- < 30, ≥ 30 kg/m2, or missing), smoking status (never, former smoking with ≤ 20 cigarettes a day, former smoking with > 20 cigarettes a day, current smoking with ≤ 20 cigarettes a day, current smoking with > 20 cigarettes a day, or missing), moderate alcohol consumption (yes or no), physical activity (never/rarely, 1–3 times/month, 1–2 times/week, 3–4 times/week, ≥ 5 times/week, or missing), and HEI-2015 score (quintile categories) simultaneously. 3) To diminish potential influence of diabetes, together with their treatments and comorbidities, on the studied associations, we excluded participants with a reported history of diabetes at study entry. 4) To minimize bias from reverse causality, we excluded the first 2 or 5 years of follow-up. 5) We additionally adjusted for quadratic terms of age in the Cox models. 6) To assess potential influence of missing data, we excluded participants with missing information on lifestyle factors or covariates from the analysis. To further address this concern, we used multiple imputation to assign values of different lifestyle factors and covariates among participants with missing data on such. 7) To evaluate the potential influence of competing risk due to mortality from other causes, cause-specific hazard models were used in the analyses of cause-specific mortality. 8) As the calculation of the healthy lifestyle score assumes equal importance of the individual lifestyle factors, we performed another sensitivity analysis to examine the soundness of results being based on this assumption. We calculated a weighted healthy lifestyle score, weighting each lifestyle factor by the beta-coefficient of its association with mortality in the regression model.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and R software, version 4.4.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing). All reported P values are two-sided with the type I error rate of 0.05. To control for multiple comparisons, a 2-sided P value of 0.005 was applied to indicate statistically significant threshold for the primary analysis (the association between self-rated health and mortality) and subgroup analyses, based on earlier studies [24].

During a median follow-up of 23.6 years, there were 181,776 deaths (45.3% of the study participants; 111,694 men and 70,082 women), including 57,436 from CVD (44,739 from heart disease and 10,714 from stroke), 47,260 from cancer, 12,554 from respiratory disease, 4710 from diabetes, 7267 from infection, and 6206 from injuries and accidents (Additional file 1: Table S4). The Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated that participants in the “poor to fair” self-rated health group had statistically significantly increased cumulative incidence for overall and cause-specific mortality compared with those in the “very good to excellent” group (log-rank test P < 0.0001, Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Additionally, participants with the lowest healthy lifestyle score (score = 0) experienced significantly increased cumulative incidence of overall and cause-specific mortality compared with participants with a higher lifestyle score (log-rank test P < 0.0001, Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

When compared with participants reporting “very good to excellent” health, we observed increased risk of mortality among those reporting “good” or “poor to fair” health after adjustment for age, sex, race or ethnic group, educational level, marital status, diabetes, and vitamin supplement use (Table 2). After further adjustment for healthy lifestyle score, there was still a higher risk of mortality among participants reporting “poor to fair” health, compared to those reporting “very good to excellent” health. The HR for overall mortality was 1.90 (95% CI: 1.87, 1.93). The HR was 1.93 (95% CI: 1.88, 1.99) for CVD mortality, 2.01 (95% CI: 1.95, 2.08) for mortality due to heart disease, 1.63 (95% CI: 1.52, 1.75) for mortality due to stroke, 1.24 (95% CI: 1.20, 1.29) for mortality due to cancer, 4.61 (95% CI: 4.39, 4.85) for mortality due to respiratory disease, 2.19 (95% CI: 2.01, 2.39) for mortality due to diabetes, 2.53 (95% CI: 2.35, 2.72) for mortality due to infection, and 1.74 (95% CI: 1.59, 1.90) for mortality due to injuries and accidents (all P for trend < 0.0001). The HRs were slightly attenuated but remained statistically significant when adjusting for individual lifestyle factors (i.e., BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and HEI-2015 score) instead of the healthy lifestyle score (Additional file 1: Table S5). The mediation analyses showed that, when comparing “poor to fair” health with “very good to excellent” health, 12.3% (95% CI: 11.7%, 12.9%), 11.7% (95% CI: 10.8%, 12.7%), 12.3% (95% CI: 11.3%, 13.3%), 8.3% (95% CI: 6.1%, 11.4%), 33.6% (95% CI: 29.7%, 37.7%), 12.4% (95% CI: 11.7%, 13.3%), 13.2% (95% CI: 10.9%, 15.9%), 8.6% (95% CI: 7.0%, 10.4%), and 5.1% (95% CI: 2.8%, 9.2%) of the associations for overall mortality and mortality due to CVD, heart disease, stroke, cancer, respiratory disease, diabetes, infection, and injuries and accidents were mediated through the healthy lifestyle score, respectively (all P values ≤ 0.0004) (Table 2). The mediation proportions were slightly larger when comparing “good” health with “very good to excellent” health (all P values ≤ 0.0017).

Results from stratified analyses are shown in Fig. 1 and Additional file 1: Table S6. The association between self-rated health status and overall mortality was generally similar regardless of sex or vitamin supplement use. In contrast, when comparing “poor to fair” with “very good to excellent” self-reported health status, we found a stronger association among participants at younger age (< 60 years, HR = 2.06 [95% CI: 2.00, 2.13], P for interaction < 0.0001), those without diabetes (HR = 1.96 [95% CI: 1.92, 1.99], P for interaction < 0.0001), those with a lower BMI (< 25 kg/m2, HR = 2.12 [95% CI: 2.06, 2.18], P for interaction < 0.0001), and during the first 5 years of follow-up (< 5 years, HR = 3.31 [95% CI: 3.15, 3.48], P for interaction < 0.0001). The association between self-rated health status and CVD mortality did not vary by sex, diabetes, or vitamin supplement use. Conversely, when comparing “poor to fair” with “very good to excellent” health, a stronger association was seen for those at younger age (< 60 years, HR = 2.09 [95% CI: 1.97, 2.21], P for interaction < 0.0001), those with a lower BMI (< 25 kg/m2, HR = 1.98 [95% CI: 1.87, 2.10], P for interaction < 0.0001), and during the first 5 years of follow-up (< 5 years, HR = 3.07 [95% CI: 2.80, 3.37], P for interaction < 0.0001).

These findings did not materially change after excluding participants with a history of diabetes (“poor to fair” versus “very good to excellent” self-rated health, HR = 1.96 [95% CI: 1.92, 1.99] for overall mortality), excluding the first 2 or 5 years of follow-up (HR = 1.86 [95% CI: 1.83, 1.89] or 1.79 [95% CI: 1.76, 1.82]), additionally adjusting for quadratic terms of age (HR = 1.90 [95% CI: 1.87, 1.93]), excluding participants with missing data on lifestyle factors or covariates (HR = 1.93 [95% CI: 1.90, 1.96]), using multiple imputation for lifestyle factors and covariates (HR = 1.91 [95% CI: 1.88, 1.94]), or after replacing the original unweighted healthy lifestyle score with a weighted score (HR = 1.91 [95% CI: 1.88, 1.94]) (Additional file 1: Table S7). Additionally, our results did not alter after using cause-specific hazard models for the analyses of cause-specific mortality (“poor to fair” versus “very good to excellent” self-rated health, HR = 1.93 [95% CI: 1.88, 1.99] for CVD mortality). The proportion of mediation by healthy lifestyle score in the association between self-rated health and overall mortality also remained similar to main analysis.

When evaluating the relative importance of self-rated health in predicting overall and cause-specific mortality, we found that self-rated health status was the 2nd most important predictor for overall mortality, following smoking status (Fig. 2). Additionally, self-rated health status was among the top three predictors for cause-specific mortality.

Discussion

In this analysis of a large prospective cohort in the USA, individuals reporting “poor to fair” self-rated health experienced significantly increased risks of overall and cause-specific mortality, and 5.1% to 33.6% of the elevated risks were mediated through lifestyles. We found that self-rated health was the second most important predictor of overall mortality, following smoking status but above other traditional risk factors for mortality. Of note, significant additive interactions were noted between self-rated health and healthy lifestyle score for overall mortality, and mortality from CVD, heart disease, cancer, and respiratory disease. The greatest risk of overall and cause-specific mortality was observed among individuals with both poor-to-fair self-rated health and unhealthy lifestyle (i.e., healthy lifestyle score = 0 or 1), among whom a nearly tripled risk of overall mortality was noted compared with individuals reporting very good-to-excellent health and a healthier lifestyle (i.e., healthy lifestyle score = 3 or above).

Poor self-rated health is a strong and independent risk factor for overall mortality and CVD outcomes in different populations [2, 25]. In a study of the UK Biobank including approximately 500,000 participants, self-rated health status was a strong factor in predicting 5-year mortality in both sexes [25]. The present study confirms and extends this by adding data on cause-specific mortality, demonstrating a strong association for respiratory disease mortality but a weak association for cancer mortality. Only a few studies have evaluated the association for non-CVD or non-cancer mortality, yielding data consistent with the present study [26, 27]. In the National Health Interview Surveys (NHIS) Study of 689,710 US adults, a stronger association was found between self-rated health and risk of mortality from diabetes and respiratory diseases compared to mortality from heart disease, stroke, or cancer [26]. A strong association was also observed for respiratory disease mortality in the Neurological Disorders in Central Spain (NEDICES) Study including approximately 5000 participants [27].

We also compared the relative importance of self-rated health in predicting mortality, relative to several traditional risk factors for mortality. Our finding demonstrated that self-rated health ranked higher in predicting overall mortality, compared to other established risk factors, including diabetes, BMI, marital status, physical activity, diet, race or ethnicity, alcohol consumption, and education. Self-rated health likely represents an individual’s integrated self-assessment of their overall health, including physical/biological, psychological/behavioral, and possibly also social dimensions, some of which may be inconspicuous and more challenging to evaluate [1, 3]. Poorer self-rated health has been related to elevated inflammatory biomarkers, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP) [28, 29]. Additionally, self-rated health can be partially attributed to the genetic propensity of common pathologies and metabolic traits, including BMI, type 2 diabetes, blood pressure, coronary artery disease, large vessel disease stroke, and depressive disorder [30], supporting the utility of this single-item question in evaluating health outcomes.

The present study revealed stronger associations between self-rated health and overall and CVD mortality among younger participants (< 60 years), those with a lower BMI, and during the first 5 years after reporting of self-rated health. Findings of the attenuated associations among older participants are consistent with previous studies [31, 32]. For example, a prospective study of 42,645 individuals in Spain showed that the association of suboptimal self-rated health with increased mortality was stronger among younger, compared to older individuals (15–44 years: HR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.59, 5.18; 45–64 years: HR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.45, 2.39; and ≥ 65 years: HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.36, 1.68) [31]. It has been postulated that the attenuation in the association between self-rated health and mortality in older individuals could be explained by more disease-related or life-threatening health events in older populations over time, leading to considerable fluctuations in health status, which may dilute the predictive value of self-rated health for subsequent health outcomes [33].

Few studies have examined the effect of self-rated health and adherence to a healthy lifestyle on mortality risk. In the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) Study of 30,222 participants aged 80 years or older, leisure activity and regular physical activity were found to partially explain the association between self-rated health and mortality [34]. In support of this, our study shows that 6.7–41.2% and 5.1–33.6% of the increased mortality among individuals reporting “good” and “poor to fair” self-rated health might be mediated through lifestyle factors. The slightly greater mediation proportion noted in the “good” health group, compared with the “poor to fair” group, might indicate that lifestyle might be a particularly important modifiable risk factor for mortality among individuals with relatively good health. If this finding is validated in future studies of independent study populations, it might help design targeted interventions for individuals with different health statuses. We further observed significant additive interactions between self-rated health and healthy lifestyle score for mortality risk. Our findings underscore the critical roles of lifestyle interventions as preventative strategies, particularly for individuals with a lower self-rated health.

Major strengths of our study include the large sample size and number of death events, the prospective design, and a long follow-up period, rendering substantial statistical power to perform analysis on overall as well as cause-specific mortality and examine effect modification by healthy lifestyle score. Limitations should also be considered. First, self-rated health status and lifestyle factors were queried only once at baseline. Given potential changes during follow-up, there might be some misclassification in these measurements. Second, despite the long observational period and large sample size, the number of deaths due to diabetes, infection, or injuries and accidents was relatively low; interpretations of these findings should be cautious. Third, residual confounding cannot be ruled out even though we adjusted for a wide range of confounding factors. Fourth, our study participants were primarily non-Hispanic white, which limits generalizability of our findings to other racial/ethnic groups. Fifth, although the calculation of the healthy lifestyle score has been validated and is widely employed in large-scale cohort studies [8, 14], it assumes equal importance of different lifestyle factors. The similar results obtained when using the weighted healthy lifestyle score demonstrate the soundness of our results based on this assumption. Sixth, as self-rated health and lifestyle score were both assessed at baseline, it is difficult to determine the temporal order between these events. Although we hypothesized a causal framework including self-rated health as the exposure, healthy lifestyle score as the mediator, and mortality as the outcome, the uncertainty around the temporal directions between exposure and mediator calls for caution in strong causal interpretation of the results from the mediation analysis. Regardless, we further applied an alternative mediation analysis, namely the causal mediation analysis under the counterfactual framework using the PROC CAUSALMED in the SAS 9.4 software [35, 36], as a sensitivity analysis which showed largely similar results as those observed in the main analysis (data not shown). However, given the fact that the cross-world independence assumption is likely violated in our data, as in many observational studies, there is also validity concern in the results of this analysis [37]. Finally, of note, the HRs derived from the Cox models need to be interpreted as weighted averages of all HRs over the follow-up period [38], and we did observe stronger positive associations between self-reported health and mortality risk in the early period follow-up compared with the later period of follow-up as expected [7].

Conclusions

In summary, poor-to-fair self-rated health was associated with higher risk of overall and cause-specific mortality, and the risk increase was partially mediated through lifestyle factors. Self-rated health was a more potent predictor for overall mortality than many traditional risk factors (apart from smoking status). A positive additive interaction was noted between self-rated health and healthy lifestyle score for overall mortality as well as mortality due to CVD, heart disease, cancer, and respiratory disease. These findings provide evidence regarding the potential significance of self-rated health in clinical practice, and further suggest that lifestyle modification may promote health and longevity, for individuals with a low or moderate self-rated health status.
