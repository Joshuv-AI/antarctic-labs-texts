Cocaine induced astrocytic activation was associated with its vasoconstricting effects independent of its neuronal effects in the prefrontal cortex
Authors: Yanzuo Liu, Kevin Clare, Yukta Jetalpuria, Yingtian Pan, Nora D Volkow, Congwu Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAstrocytes bridge neuronal and cerebrovascular systems, regulating neurotransmitter dynamics and modulating neurovascular coupling (NVC). Cocaine disrupts neuronal, astrocytic, and cerebrovascular system function, which in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), could interfere with executive function. Though astrocytes are known to influence neurotoxic and addictive effects of cocaine through their regulations of local neurotransmitter environments, their role in cocaine-induced vasoconstriction and dysregulated NVC remains unclear. To investigate astrocytic regulation of vascular response to cocaine, we expressed the genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP6f in either neurons or astrocytes alongside concurrent expression of astrocytic DREADD(Gq) in the mouse PFC. Hemodynamic changes and cellular Ca2+ responses to an acute cocaine challenge (1 mg/kg, i.v.) with and without DREADD(Gq) activation were recorded through a cranial window using a custom multi-wavelength imaging platform. We found that when naïve animals were exposed to acute cocaine, intracellular Ca2+ increased in both astrocytes (Ca2+A) and neurons (Ca2+N), cerebral blood vessels vasoconstricted, and oxygenated hemoglobin in tissue (HbO2) decreased. Activation of astrocytes by DREADD(Gq) exposure also caused long-lasting increases in Ca2+A and vasoconstriction. Consequently, when cocaine was administered after astrocytic activation, there was further Ca2+A elevation and vasoconstriction but limited in magnitude, suggesting a ceiling effect. Meanwhile, cocaine induced increases in Ca2+N were unaffected. Correlation analysis revealed a strong negative association between Ca2+A and cerebrovascular diameters, while Ca2+N was negatively correlated with HbO2 and this correlation remained unchanged by astrocytic activation. These findings support astrocytes’ involvement in regulating vascular tone including cocaine’s vasoconstricting effects and its disruption of neurovascular coupling. Introduction

Cocaine is a highly addictive drug, and its repeated use disrupts cerebrovascular, neuronal and astrocytic function1,2. At rest, the brain is estimated to consumes 20% of the body’s energy, with increasing demands during cognitive tasks or exposure to stimulant drugs, such as cocaine3. To meet these nutrient and metabolic demands, cerebral blood flow (CBF) dynamically adjusts to ensure adequate oxygen and nutrient delivery to active neurons through neurovascular coupling (NVC)4,5. Astrocytes provide a cellular link between neuronal circuitry and blood vessels6,7, with their end feet enveloping 98% of the cerebrovasculature, releasing vasoactive compounds to trigger vasodilation or vasoconstriction8–10. Astrocytes also regulate the intrasynaptic space by buffering electrolytes11,12, recycling neurotransmitters, and functioning as a syncytium to communicate with the surrounding neurons13,14. Astrocytes’ bidirectional communication with both neurons and the vasculature uniquely positions them as key mediators of NVC15.

Astrocytes’ involvement in drug seeking behavior16 and in NVC17 suggests that they may play a role in cocaine’s effects on cerebral blood vessels18,19. However, the investigation of astrocyte’s role in brain function has been challenged by the difficulties in recording their activity20 as they have a more stable membrane potential than neurons and do not generate action potentials21, making traditional electrophysiological methods less effective for studying their dynamics22. Since astrocyte activation involves fluctuations in intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, the detection of astrocytic Ca2+ (Ca2+A) signaling can be used as a correlate for their activity23,24. Genetically encoded Ca2+ fluorescence indicators (GECIs), such as GCaMP6f, selectively expressed in astrocytes of transgenic mice (GFAP-Cre), have enabled researchers to advance prior constraints and image cortical astrocytic activity25–27.

To further characterize astrocytes contribution to cocaine-induced alterations of NVC, we utilized chemogenetics receptors (DREADDs -Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs) to modulate astrocytes activation28,29. GFAP-DREADD (Gq) was used to selectively stimulate astrocytes through G-coupled protein receptors (GPCRs)30, which increase Ca2+ through phospholipase C activation and the release of intracellular Ca2+ stores31,32. Previous studies examining astrocytic inhibition (via DREADD(Gi)) demonstrated that reducing astrocytic activity led to vasodilation (up to 9%) and attenuated cocaine-induced vasoconstriction33. However, the role of astrocytic activation on cocaine’s induced disruption of NVC is still unclear. Here, we hypothesized that activation of astrocytes would impair cocaine induced vasoconstriction without affecting its effects on neuronal activities, thus leading to neurovascular uncoupling. To test this, we utilized our multi-modality optical image technique and genetically encoded calcium indicators (GCaMP6f) along with astrocytic activation via DREADD(Gq) to investigate the effects of astrocytic activation on cocaine induced cerebrovascular changes and astrocytic and neuronal activities in vivo. We studied the PFC as this brain region is critical for executive function and its disruption is implicated in the impairments of self-regulation and compulsive drug consumption in patients with cocaine use disorder (CocUD)34. We found that cocaine induced both neuronal and astrocytic Ca2+ elevations together with vasoconstriction and reduced tissue hemodynamics. Astrocytic Ca2+A stimulation with DREADD(Gq) resulted in long-lasting vasoconstriction, such that when cocaine was administered after their activation further vasoconstriction was limited, suggesting a ceiling effect whereas cocaine induced increases in Ca2+N were unaffected. Temporal correlation analysis revealed a strong negative association between Ca2+A and cerebrovascular diameters, highlighting astrocyte’s involvement in regulating vascular tone and in cocaine’s disruption of NVC.

Methods

Animals and experimental design

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Stony Brook University and conducted according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Transgenic GFAP-Cre (Jackson Laboratory, https://www.jax.org/strain/024098) and C57BL/6 wild type (WT) mice (Jackson Laboratory) were used for this study when they reached ~ 10 weeks of age. These experimental mice were divided into three groups: an astrocytic group, a neuronal group and a control group (summarized in Table 1). Physiological conditions of the animals (e.g., body temperature, respiration rate) were continuously monitored and recorded (Small Animal Monitoring and Gating system, model 1025 L, SA Instruments Inc.). Specifically, the body temperature was maintained as 36–37.5 °C by a customized heating pad (with voltage-controlled electric circles) and the respiration was maintained at 40–80 breaths/minute using isoflurane mixed with oxygen gas (1–2% isoflurane in 100% O2 at ~ 1.5 L/min) to ensure similar physiological status for all mice. All experiments were performed according to the relevant guidelines, regulations, and ARRIVE guidelines.

Dual viral injection into mouse cortex

To detect astrocyte or neuron-specific Ca2+ fluorescence response to an acute cocaine challenge with or without astrocytic activation (GFAP-DREADD (Gq)) by clozapine, we used the genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP6f via viral delivery into the prefrontal cortex (PFC). Specifically, a mixture of two viruses (0.5 µl/each virus) AAV5.CAG.Flex.GCaMP6f.WPRE.SV40 (AV-5-PV2816, Addgene) with AAV5.GFAP. hM3D(Gq).mCherry (50479-AAV5, Addgene) were slowly infused at 0.1 µl/min into transgenic mice (GFAP-Cre, Fig. 1ci) to express GCaMP6f in astrocytes in the right PFC [A/P: +2; M/L: 0.75; D/V: −0.5 mm]. To express GCaMP6f into neurons, 0.5 µl AAV5.Syn.GCaMP6f.WPRE.SV40 (AV-5-PV2822, Addgene) mix with 0.5ul AAV5.GFAP. hM3D(Gq). mCherry, were infused into the same brain region of wild type mice (C57BL/6, Fig. 1cii). During viral injections, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane mixed with pure oxygen and their heads mounted on a stereotaxic frame and their physiology monitored. After completion of procedures, the mice were monitored daily for three days to ensure they were healthy.

Cranial window implantation

To image brain function in the PFC, an optically clear cranial window (~ 2 × 2 mm2) was created on the skull over the viral injection spot. The mouse skull surrounding the viral injection spot was carefully thinned with a dental drill and then removed to expose the brain. Saline was applied to the brain tissue, and the open cortex was immediately covered with a sterile coverslip (4 × 3 mm2) and sealed with biocompatible glue. Dental cement was spread around the edges of the coverslip to further secure its attachment. After window implantation, a catheter was placed in a tail vein for drug administration during imaging. Then the animal was positioned on a custom head mount and moved to the imaging stage for in-vivo image acquisition. All surgical procedures were performed under isoflurane anesthesia.

In-vivo MIP imaging

A custom multimodality imaging platform (MIP, Fig. 1a) developed in our lab25,35–37 was used for simultaneous imaging of astrocytic/neuronal calcium fluorescence (ΔCa2+A, ΔCa2+N, respectively), along with oxygenated hemoglobin (Δ[HbO2]) from the mouse PFC in response to cocaine without or with DREADD (Gq) activation. As shown in Fig. 1, light beams at three wavelengths (488 nm, 568 nm and 630 nm) were delivered to the PFC with a time-sharing mode (10ms exposure time per channel). For GCaMP6f-Ca2+ fluorescence imaging, a blue LED at λex = 488 nm was used for excitation and the fluorescence images are shown in (Fig. 1bi). The DREADD(Gq) expression image (Fig. 1bii) was captured with λex = 550 nm, where the emission filter EM2 (≥ 570 nm) was used. Images of the merged Ca2+ and DREADD(Gq) signals are shown in Fig. 1biii. The reflected images captured from λ1 = 568 nm, and λ2 = 630 nm light channels were used to distinguish arteries and veins and create tissue oxygenation hemoglobin map (Fig. 1biv). These images were detected with a sCMOS camera (synchronized with the illumination paradigm). To assess cocaine-induced dynamic changes in astrocytic and neuronal Ca2+ fluorescence, brain hemodynamics and astrocyte’s involvement in cocaine induced vasoconstriction, animals underwent three imaging sessions per experiment (Fig. 1d): d1: 1 st cocaine injection (1 mg/kg, i.v. 70 min); d2: clozapine pretreatment (0.1 mg/kg, i.p. 40 min)/saline pretreatment for control group; d3: 2nd cocaine injection 40 min following clozapine pre-treatment (1 mg/kg, i.v. 70 min).

Image and data processing

For each experiment, three data sets were obtained from each mouse cortex: (1) [Ca2+] dependent fluorescence intensity changes from astrocytes (ΔCa2+A) or neurons (ΔCa2+N); (2) changes in vessel diameters (Δ[Φ]); (3) changes in oxygenated-hemoglobin tissue (Δ[HbO2]). These data sets were collected without and with DREADD (Gq) activation by clozapine in response to acute cocaine challenge. Data across animals was averaged, basal and integrated reactivity response to cocaine were quantified. Besides that, to assess the impact of astrocyte activation on cocaine effects, the data was also normalized to the basal activity prior to the first cocaine injection (10 min preceding first cocaine injection).

GCaMP6f-Ca2+ fluorescence was selected within GCaMP6f expressing cortical areas devoid of visible blood vessels, five regions of interest (ROIs shown as yellow circle in Fig. 3a, b for astrocyte and Fig. 6a, b for neuron) selected for each animal. The astrocytic or neuronal ΔCa2+ changes were quantified as relative changes of fluorescence signal over their baseline, i.e., Δ[Ca2+]=[F(t)-F(baseline)/F(baseline)]x100%.

To assess cocaine-induced vasoconstriction and the involvement of astrocytic activation in vasoconstriction, the vessel diameters were quantified using a custom Matlab program. About 6–10 ROIs were selected from blood vessels of different sizes to monitor their diameter changes from baseline to 60 min after cocaine administration. Vessel diameter measures were performed on images acquired at λ1 = 568 nm using a Gaussian process method published by Asl et al.33,38. Specifically, x and y coordinates of a seed point placed at the center of a vessel of the selected ROI. The centerline and direction of the vessel are then computed using the Radon transform, and trained MATLAB to start tracking from the input seed point to the next 10 points (ϕi, i = 1–10, within ~ 60 μm) lying in the center of the blood vessel. The diameter ϕi at each point is then estimated using a separate Gaussian process. The mean volume of ϕi (i = 1–10) was calculated as the vessel diameters at the location selected ROIs. To track the time course of vessel diameter changes, a while-loop was added to read from the first image to the last one (e.g., m = 4200 frames or based on our imaging time for each experiment above), which allowed us to continually track each frame and its change was quantified over its baseline (before cocaine) as a function of time following cocaine injection.

To identify whether the spatial distribution of cocaine-induced ΔCa2+A or ΔCa2+N changes was affected by astrocytic activation we developed a custom (Python-based) image analysis program to quantify the astrocytic and neuronal Ca2+ responses before and after DREADD(Gq) activation. The flowchart of the process is illustrated in Figure S1. Briefly, ratio maps were computed as peak divided by their baseline to normalize fluorescence intensity changes across the entire field of view. A fixed threshold (no-response area as cutoff) was then applied to binarize each map, and the fraction of pixels above the threshold was calculated to represent the responsive area.

Statistics

Results are reported as mean ± standard error (SEM). Comparisons between the two groups (e.g., before vs. after) were analyzed using Student’s T-tests and comparisons between the four groups with One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to determine correlation coefficients (R values). Two-way repeated measure ANOVA with time and treatment factors was used to identify periods showing statistically significant differences between groups (e.g., with or without Gq activation). All statistical tests were performed using SigmaStat software (Version 12.5; Systat Software Inc.), with P < 0.05 considered significant. The sample sizes for each experiment are summarized in Table 1.

Results

To investigate whether astrocytic activation could alter the cellular and vascular response to cocaine, we recorded Ca2+A/Ca2+N fluorescence, vascular diameters, and hemodynamic changes in response to acute cocaine with and without DREADD(Gq) activation. The magnitude of the response was calculated by integrating the time course of parameter change until its recovery. For comparison purposes the second cocaine response was normalized to the baseline activity prior to the first cocaine injection. To assess the sensitivity of the hemodynamic and cellular signals to our intervention of astrocytic activation we compared ROIs in regions within or without DREADD (Gq) expression (Supplemental figure S2,3,4). All the results presented below are based on ROIs selected within the DREADD expression region.

Activation of DREADD(Gq) elevates astrocytic Ca2+A and induces long-lasting vasoconstriction with a minimal influence on neuronal or hemodynamic changes

DREADDs is a chemogenic approach that enables subtype selective activation (Gq) or silencing (Gi) of cellular signaling (e.g., astrocytes) via clozapine activation39. We used DREADD(Gq) to determine the impact of astrocytic activation on cellular and vascular activity in the mouse PFC. Figure 2 shows time-course responses of cellular and vascular activities acquired before and after clozapine injection (0.1 mg/kg, i.p.) at t = 0 min followed over 30 min. Figure 2a is a spatial ratio map post clozapine (t=25 min) normalized to its baseline (t=−5 min), revealing increases in astrocytic Ca2+A (Fig. 2aii) localized within the Gq expression region (Fig. 2ai) along with vasoconstriction (Fig. 2aiii). Figure 2c summarizes the mean changes in Ca2+A as a function of time post clozapine administration across animals (n = 6). DREADD(Gq) activation resulted in Ca2+A elevation compared to baseline (1.325 ± 0.5689% from t = 11–30 min, P* < 0.05). Figure 2d shows mean changes in vascular diameters, revealing that DREADD(Gq) induced long-lasting vasoconstriction, with a decrease of (−3.541 ± 0.349%) over its baseline (−0.2221 ± 0.9649%). We also evaluated whether astrocytic activation would affect neuronal and hemodynamic activity. Figure 2bii shows a representative ratio image of ΔCa2+N fluorescence at t = 25 min after DREADD(Gq) activation over the baseline (t = − 5 min), exhibiting no significant Ca2+N changes (−1.232 ± 1.535%) after DREADD(Gq) activation (Fig. 2e). Meanwhile, the time course of hemodynamic ΔHbO2 in response to astrocyte activation is shown in Fig. 2f, revealing ΔHbO2 remained stable throughout the DREADD (Gq) activation, (−1.668 ± 0.5158%) at t = 25 post clozapine. Taken together, these results indicated that excitation of astrocytes via clozapine activation of DREADDs (Gq) did not influence baseline neuronal activity (Ca2+N) nor ΔHbO2, but induced elevation of astrocytes Ca2+A and vasoconstriction.

Activation of astrocytes increases basal Ca2+A limiting further Ca2+A increases from cocaine

Cocaine triggered significant changes in Ca2+A that differed with or without astrocytic activation. Figure 3 shows Ca2+ dependent fluorescence changes in response to a cocaine challenge in astrocytes without (Coc) and with (Gq + Coc) astrocytic activation. The ratio images of cocaine-induced ΔCa2+A-Coc and ΔCa2+A-Gq + Coc at baseline (t=0 min), 5 min, and 40 min after injection are illustrated in Fig. 3b (upper and lower panels, respectively). Figure 3a is used to confirm the ROIs were selected within the DREADD (Gq) expression area. As shown in supplemental Figure S2, activation of DREADD (Gq) increased astrocytic Ca²⁺ activity only in the expressing region. Figure 3c shows the time courses of cocaine induced Ca2+A changes (n = 6) without (orange line) and with (blue line) astrocytic activation. Without astrocytic activation cocaine increased ΔCa2+A (2.072 ± 0.6823%) from t = 0 to t = 4 min (P*<0.05) returning to baseline at ~ 34 min; while following DREADD(Gq) activation, there was no further increase in ΔCa2+A (n = 6). Post-hoc analysis of a two-way repeated measure ANOVA showed differences between the two-time courses from baseline (t = 0) to 2 min post-cocaine (F (70,280) = 12.718, P*<0.001, Fig. 3c) indicating that after DREADD(Gq) activation further cocaine induced Ca2+A increases were curtailed. The mean integrated response of ΔCa2+A increase with cocaine was significantly smaller after Gq activation (Coc vs. Gq + Coc: 0.82 ± 0.07%/min vs. 0.04 ± 0.04%/min, P*=0.032, Fig. 3e) presumably reflecting a ceiling effect as ΔCa2+A was already increased by the Gq activation. Meanwhile, when normalizing to the baseline prior to the first cocaine injection (1st -Coc at t = 0; Fig. 3d), the ΔCa2+A of the second cocaine was higher than the 1 st -Coc from the beginning until the end of experiment. This likely reflected the fact that the 2nd -Coc was given when astrocytes were still activated resulting in a significantly higher Ca2+A baseline than prior to the 1st cocaine injection (1st vs. 2nd : 0.1704 ± 0.0580% vs.1.311 ± 0.0306%, Fig. 3f, P*< 0.001). We interpret this to suggest that ΔCa2+A increase associated with DREADD(Gq) activation created an astrocytic “ceiling effect” curtailing the magnitude of further ΔCa2+A in response to cocaine. As shown in Fig. 3g, the spatial distribution of astrocytic Ca2+A responses before and after DREADD(Gq) activation normalized to the baseline that preceded each cocaine injection indicated that the fraction of activated pixels was significantly decreased (before vs. after: 10.61 ± 3.426% vs. 3.422 ± 1.320%, P*=0.046, Fig. 3h) after DREADD(Gq) activation.

Activation of astrocytes decreases vascular diameters limiting further vasoconstriction by cocaine

Both astrocytic activation and acute cocaine decreased vascular diameters (Φ). However, when cocaine was given after astrocytic activation, the cerebral vessels were already constricted, thus further responses were limited. Figure 4a-b shows representative vascular images obtained from the multi-wavelength platform and their corresponding diameter maps without (Fig. 4a) and with (Fig. 4b) astrocytic activation at t = 0 and at t=5 min after cocaine administration. ROIs are selected within the DREADD(Gq) expression area, as shown in Supplemental S3. Without DREADD(Gq) activation, acute cocaine induced a persistent decrease in vascular diameter (ΔΦ-Coc, −5.464 ± 1.80%, t = 4–13 min P*<0.05) (orange line in Fig. 4e). Whereas, with astrocytic activation, cocaine’s effect was significantly reduced compared to its own baseline (ΔΦ-Gq + Coc, blue line in Fig. 4e). Comparison between mean time course of ΔΦ-Coc and ΔΦ-Gq+ Coc with the two-way repeated measure ANOVA showed a significant difference from t = 3 to 29 min post cocaine [F(70, 350) = 1.745; P*<0.001]. The mean integrated changes of vessel constriction were also decreased (from − 5.47 ± 1.30%/min to −1.25 ± 1.02%/min) when cocaine was given after Gq activation (Fig. 4g, P*= 0.037). We separated blood vessels into three groups based on their diameters: large vessels Φ > 60 μm, medium vessels Φ = 40–60 μm and small vessels Φ < 40 μm. Without DREADD(Gq) activation, acute cocaine induced a significant reduction in all three vessel sizes (Fig. 4c, P*< 0.05), especially in microvessels, which decreased to (−5.146 ± 1.119%). With DREADD(Gq) activation, all types of vessels had an attenuated response (Fig. 4d) compared to preceding each cocaine injection. This likely reflected a ceiling effect due to the pre-existing strong vasoconstriction from astrocytic activation, as baselines prior to 1st -Coc (−0.537 ± 0.6218%) and 2nd -Coc (−5.375 ± 0.5613%), differed significantly (Fig. 4h, P*<0.001). Indeed, after normalizing ΔΦ from cocaine to the baseline prior to the first cocaine administration (Fig. 4f), the analyses showed that after Gq activation, the vessels were already significantly constricted (−6.029 ± 1.466%) blue line in Fig. 4f prior to the second cocaine challenge and remained markedly vasoconstricted after the second cocaine injection to an even greater extent observed from cocaine without Gq activation. These vascular effects are not attributable to clozapine injection as previous studies showed it has no impact on vascular diameters19,33.

Discussion

In this study, astrocytes were selectively activated by chemogenetic manipulation with simultaneous recording of cerebral hemodynamics and cellular activity in response to acute cocaine. We found that without astrocytic activation, cocaine induced an increase in both neuronal and astrocytic ΔCa2+ along with vasoconstriction and a decrease in hemodynamics. However, when astrocytes were activated by DREADD(Gq), which by itself enhanced ΔCa2+A and triggered vasoconstriction, curtailed further increases in ΔCa2+A and vasoconstriction by cocaine whereas its ΔCa2+N increases were unaffected. The correlation between astrocytes and vasoconstriction was disrupted by DREADD(Gq) while the correlation between astrocytes and neurons with Δ[HbO2] was not affected.

These findings further support that cocaine disrupts NVC and that astrocyte plays an important role in regulating cocaine-induced changes in vascular tone. Specifically, that cocaine, which triggered neuronal activation alongside vasoconstriction interfered with the typical increases that would be expected from neuronal activation, resulting in neurovascular uncoupling. Thus, our findings suggest that cocaine’s activation of astrocytes may trigger vasoconstriction40,41 and disrupt normal NVC. However, further investigation might need to distinguish these effects from cocaine’s direct effects on the cerebrovascular mural cells. Also, cocaine’s blockade of monoamine reuptake can potentiates sympathetic vasoconstriction and restricts blood flow even during heightened neuronal activity7.

DREADDs modulate astrocytes

Astrocytes are increasingly recognized for their multifaceted roles in brain function16,42. However, the ability to study how neurons and glial cells communicate has been limited by the few tools available for targeted manipulation2,43. In this respect chemogenetic tools (DREADDs) have allowed cell selective and spatiotemporal precise modulation of glial cells32,44. Excitatory hM3Dq DREADDs stimulate astrocytes through phospholipase C (PLC) and inositol trisphosphate (IP3) leading to intracellular Ca2+ release31. Inhibitory DREADD(Gi) suppress astrocytes by reducing cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling, which regulates neurotransmitter release, synaptic plasticity, and neuroinflammation45,46. Recently, the effect of DREADD(Gq) in astrocytic Ca2+ signaling was assessed for the first time in awake mice47, documenting long-lasting increases. Here we observed a similar response in anesthetized mice which showed that DREADD(Gq), resulted in a ~ 1.4% Ca2+A increase (Fig. 2c).

Manipulation of astrocytes with DREADDs has raised concern that this artificial manipulation may create conditions that do not reflect natural astrocytic activity. DREADDs themselves are modified G-coupled protein receptors and utilize the same endogenous signaling cascades activated in normal function. Importantly, the amplitude and temporal dynamics of DREADD(Gq)-evoked calcium responses have been shown to be comparable to those from endogenous Gq-GPCR activation31,47–49.

Astrocytes activated by DREADD(Gq) do not affect neuronal response to cocaine

Astrocytes communicate with neurons modulating neuronal activity through neurotransmitter uptake and gliotransmitter release50,51. Upon activation via DREADD(Gq), astrocytes can release glutamate that can then stimulate NMDA and AMPA receptors on neurons increasing their excitability48,52. We previously demonstrated that the partial NMDA antagonist memantine attenuated cocaine-induced intracellular neuronal Ca²⁺ increases, significantly reducing both ΔCa²⁺ dynamics and hemodynamic changes in the rat PFC53. Interestingly, in the current study, Gq activation did not alter the neuronal response to cocaine. There remains controversy as to whether activation of astrocytes through DREADD(Gq) impacts neuronal function. In the hippocampus, some studies have documented that neither the loss of nor the activation of astrocytic Gq protein receptors in the CA1 region leads to changes in excitation of pyramidal neurons54,55. Conversely, others have reported that activation of astrocytes through Gq leads to an increase in excitatory potential or elevated neuronal activity of single cells observed by electrophysiology recording or 2-photon imaging56,57. For our study the neuronal ΔCa2+ increases from cocaine were robust (~ 6%) and persisted over 40 min similar to results reported by other studies58,59. Therefore, if a small change in neuronal activity (~ 1%) occurred when activating DREADD(Gq) (Fig. 2e), these changes may be hidden by the larger change from cocaine. Additionally, the studies to date reporting on neuronal activity focus on single cells using electrophysiology or 2-photon microscopy60–62. These single cell observations may not generate a signal that is large enough to resolve at the tissue level with our methodology.

Cocaine induced vasoconstriction is distinct from cortical spreading depression

Given the large neuronal calcium shift induced by cocaine, one possibility is that this reflects cortical spreading depression (CSD). However, the vascular and neuronal responses we observed are distinct from those reported with CSD. Our group has examined cocaine’s effects on cortical field potentials using electroencephalography in the rat cortex63. Unlike CSD, which is characterized by a propagating wave of intense depolarization followed by prolonged suppression of evoked potentials lasting up to 30 min64, spikes in local field potentials (LFPs) in association with forepaw stimulation were observed even after cocaine administration (1 mg/kg i.v.), consistent with preserved neuronal responsiveness rather than post-SD depression. Also, optical imaging studies of CSD with GCaMP6f show a propagating wavefront of calcium activation across cortical tissue65. In contrast, our experiments revealed a simultaneous, spatially uniform activation across the 2 × 2 mm2 field followed by a slow, spatially uniform decay66,67. This pattern is inconsistent with the spatiotemporal hallmark of CSD. Finally, CSD typically produces a biphasic vascular response, with initial transient hyperemia followed by prolonged vasoconstriction64. While we observed sustained vasoconstriction after cocaine, we did not detect the preceding hyperemia in prior66 nor current experiments. Together, these findings suggest a different mechanism for cocaine-induced vasoconstriction than CSD.

Activated astrocytes by DREADD(Gq) constrict blood vessels

Astrocyte activation modulates vasoconstriction and NVC through the release of vasoactive substances59. Our group has documented the pivotal role astrocytes play in controlling vascular tone in response to different stimuli25. Specifically, during electrical forepaw stimulation, we observed a gradual increase in astrocytic Ca²⁺ levels that peaked at approximately 4.7% and that was strongly correlated (R = 0.66) with the vasoconstriction that followed the hyperemic response, suggesting a role of astrocytes in resetting vascular tone post-vasodilation68. In our current study, the activation of astrocytes via DREADD(Gq) led to an increase in astrocytic Ca²⁺ of 1.4% that was temporally associated with an average vasoconstriction of 3.5% (Fig. 2). Astrocytes regulate vascular tone through the release of substances that act on vascular smooth muscle cells, such as phospholipase A2 (PLA₂), arachidonic acid (AA), and the intermediate 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE)69–71. Notably, 20-HETE is a potential vasoconstrictor72 that reduces blood vessel diameter and works through Ca2+channels such as the L-type voltage gated Ca2+ channels on surrounding smooth muscle-cells73. Consistent with this mechanism, we previously demonstrated that the L-type Ca2+ channel blocker, nifedipine, attenuated vasoconstriction in rodents following cocaine administration66,72.

Comparison of DREADD(Gq) and DREADD(Gi) effect on cocaine response

Several studies have used chemogenetic receptors (DREADDs) to manipulate cocaine-induced effects in mice74,75. Recent publications19,33 in our lab have focused on the inhibition of astrocytes using DREADD(Gi) and its impact on NVC and on cocaine-induced vasoconstriction. Inhibition of astrocytic activity led to a drop in astrocytic ΔCa2+ of ~ −3% and up to a ~ 9% increase in vascular diameters. When astrocytes were inhibited, cocaine-induced astrocytic ΔCa2+ rise and vasoconstriction were significantly attenuated while ΔCBFv changes were eliminated. Conversely, here we show that during astrocytic activation by DREADD(Gq), there was an increase in astrocytic ΔCa2+ of 1.4% and vasoconstriction of 3.5% (Fig. 2). Though, DREADD (Gq), attenuated the astrocytic ΔCa2+ and vasoconstriction ΔΦ in response to cocaine this likely reflected a ceiling effect since astrocytes were already activated, and vessels were constricted when second cocaine was given. The control experiment with saline injection also indicates that the prolonged vasoconstriction was not due to remaining effects from the first cocaine injection but instead the result of astrocytic activation.

Clozapine instead of CNO was used to active DREADDs, because a recent study showed that clozapine (the clozapine-N-oxide metabolite) rather than CNO itself stimulates the DREADDS receptor39. The clozapine dose (0.1 mg/kg) was within the smaller range used in chemogenetic studies76. We previously studied the effects of clozapine by itself in control experiments (no DREADD expression) and observed that clozapine did not affect neuronal activities nor CBF33. Thus, neither neuronal activity nor baseline hemodynamics were affected by clozapine, and cocaine-induced vascular responses remained intact, suggesting minimal off-target effects of clozapine itself.

Astrocytic Ca2+ and neuronal Ca2+ drive separate components of hemodynamics and are disrupted by DREADD(Gq) activation

Astrocytes contribute to cocaine’s vasoconstrictive effects in cerebral blood vessels14,77. We had previously reported a long lasting ΔCa2+A increase with acute cocaine that was associated with cocaine-induced vasoconstriction19,33. Our current findings are consistent with our previous work. However, this association was disrupted, following activation of astrocytes via DREADD(Gq), likely due to a sustained elevation in astrocytic calcium; suggesting that cocaine was unable to further increase ΔCa2+A under these elevated conditions. The mechanism underlying cocaine-induced increases in Ca2+A is unclear but it could reflect both its dopaminergic and noradrenergic effects as stimulation of dopamine D1 and D2 receptors or of alpha-1 adrenergic receptors in astrocytes increases intracellular Ca2+ signaling78,79.

Meanwhile, cocaine induced increases in ΔCa2+N and ΔCa2+A were both inversely correlated with tissue oxygenation, the correlation was significantly stronger for ΔCa2+N indicating that cocaine’s effects on neuronal activation have a greater influence than astrocytic activation in affecting tissue oxygenation and metabolism58,80,81. Though, astrocytic activation did not affect cocaine induced changes in ΔCa2+N but attenuated Δ[HbO2], this could reflect the more localized effects of DREADD(Gq) activation than that of the area where the hemodynamic responses were measured82,83. This study extends our previous findings by demonstrating that activation of astrocytes disrupts NVC, triggers vasoconstriction and decreases tissue oxygenation but does not impact ΔCa2+N.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insight into the role of astrocytes in cocaine-induced vasoconstriction, several limitations should be considered. First, our experiments focused only on acute cocaine exposure, which is crucial for understanding its cerebrovascular effects but future studies with chronic administration are necessary to unveil its role in the behavioral disruptions observed in animal models of cocaine addiction. Further studies are also required to investigate potential confounds in our design. In particular, two cocaine injections were administered with a two-hour interval between them to minimize lingering effects from the first cocaine injection. Though control experiments confirmed that this duration was sufficient for the vasculature to recover from the initial cocaine injection (Fig. 5), future studies should evaluate the potential cumulative effects of multiple exposures. Anesthetics may present a confounder as they can attenuate the sensitivity of neurons67,84 and astrocytes to cocaine. In this study, the breathing rate was 40–60 breaths/min consistent with previous reports of mice under isoflurane anesthesia85,86, which is lower than the > 100 breaths/min typical in awake animals. Though isoflurane was always administered in a gas mixture with oxygen, we acknowledge that isoflurane anesthesia can attenuate canonical NVC responses87. Nonetheless since animals with and without DREADD(Gq) activation were imaged under the same anesthetic conditions the effect of isoflurane cannot account for the observed difference in astrocytic modulation of cocaine induced vasoconstriction.

The goal of our study was to characterize the role that astrocytes have in regulating the vascular and neuronal response to cocaine including neurovascular uncoupling. For this reason, we prioritized calcium imaging using GCaMP6f, a genetically encoded calcium indicator that we have extensively validated with our multimodal optical platform. Cocaine induces robust and reproducible increases in neuronal and astrocytic Ca²⁺, which we can record simultaneously with vascular responses across the wide cortical region33,66. Moreover, we have previously shown that this approach is sensitive enough to resolve both single-cell activity and single-stimulus responses, making it a versatile tool for studying NVC68. We are aware that the observed calcium changes represent the integrated influence of multiple neurotransmitters, including glutamate. Indeed, in prior studies we showed that selective blockade of NMDA receptors attenuated cocaine-induced calcium influx53, confirming the contribution of glutamate to the recorded Ca²⁺ dynamics. We also recognize that cocaine triggers inflammatory cascades, including microglial activation, astrocytic reactivity, and cytokine release. However, these processes typically evolve on a slower timescale emerging over hours to days after exposure88,89. Because our study was designed to examine rapid neuronal and vascular responses occurring within minutes of cocaine administration, we did not include inflammatory markers.

Conclusion

In summary, using our unique imaging modality with specific Ca2+ indicator, we were able to simultaneously assess neuronal/astrocytic Ca2+ dependent fluorescence, vascular diameter, and hemodynamic changes in response to acute cocaine in the cortex of mice invivo. DREADD(Gq) activation by itself increased astrocytic Ca2+ and induced long-lasting vasoconstriction and it attenuated the magnitude of further cocaine induced vasoconstriction under these conditions suggesting a ceiling effect both for Ca2+ and for degree of vasoconstriction. Combined with our previous findings using inhibitory DREADD(Gi), these results establish astrocytic Ca²⁺ signaling as a key modulator of cerebrovascular responses to cocaine. Importantly, our findings suggest astrocytes as a potential therapeutic target for cocaine induced cerebrovascular pathology.
