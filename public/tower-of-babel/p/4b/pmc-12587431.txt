Tracheal Structural Changes in Tracheostomy‐Dependent Children With Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia
Authors: Alexander Szymczak, Ben Hirsch, Ashley Young, Rachel D Yoon, Taher Valika, Jonathan Ida, Saied Ghadersohi, Inbal Hazkani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients with severe Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) are prone to upper airway complications due to their need for prolonged intubation, early tracheostomy placement, and chronic high‐pressure ventilation. This study examines structural changes over time in tracheostomy‐dependent BPD patients compared to those without BPD.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of children who underwent tracheostomy at an age less than 6 months at a tertiary‐care children's hospital between January 2012 and December 2022.ResultsA total of 82 patients underwent tracheostomy at an age of less than 6 months, of which 42 (51.2%) had BPD. Patients with BPD were older at the time of tracheostomy (4.44 vs. 3.36 months, p = 0.012) and had a younger gestational age (26.5 vs. 37.0 weeks, p < 0.001). Additionally, patients with BPD had higher rates of mechanical ventilation (42/42 vs. 24/40, p = 0.0018) and a greater number of days intubated prior to tracheostomy (92 vs. 21, p < 0.001). The median follow‐up period for all patients was 28.8 months [IQR = 36.8]. BPD infants were more likely to develop subglottic stenosis (30/42 vs. 16/40, p = 0.0082), glottic edema (32/42 vs. 19/40, p = 0.0074), require laryngotracheal reconstruction (16/42 vs. 5/40, p = 0.0095) and get decannulated (21/42 vs. 8/40, p = 0.0045) compared to infants without BPD.ConclusionsTracheostomy‐dependent infants with BPD had higher rates of glottic edema, subglottic stenosis, and laryngotracheal reconstruction but were more likely to be decannulated. These findings suggest that, despite increased airway pathology, tracheostomy is a safe and effective intervention in this population, with outcomes comparable to other pediatric cohorts.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: airway complications, BPD, decannulation, infants, subglottic stenosis, tracheostomy

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD) is a chronic lung disease that affects premature infants [1, 2]. BPD arises from a combination of lung immaturity, oxygen toxicity, mechanical ventilation, and inflammation, all of which impair alveolar and pulmonary vascular development [1, 3, 4]. Although advances in neonatal intensive care have improved survival rates for extremely premature infants, BPD remains a significant source of morbidity due to the prolonged respiratory support these infants often require [2, 5, 6]. For infants with severe BPD, who cannot be weaned from respiratory support in the near term, tracheostomy is often considered to provide a safe, long‐term ventilatory solution [7, 8, 9]. Tracheostomy carries known morbidities including bleeding, infection, granulation tissue formation, and subglottic stenosis [10, 11, 12]. These risks are particularly relevant in infants with severe BPD, who require more intensive ventilatory strategies, including higher tidal volumes (10–15 mL/kg), prolonged inspiratory times (0.5–1.0 s), lower respiratory rates (12–20 breaths/min), and significantly elevated levels of positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP; 7–12 cm H2O) [13, 14, 15]. Chronic exposure to these elevated airway pressures—alongside longer durations of intubation, earlier tracheostomy placement, and a chronic need for an inflated tracheostomy cuff—may increase the likelihood of tracheostomy complications in this population [8, 11]. In addition, the weight of the ventilator circuit connecting the tracheostomy tube to the ventilator may exert significant and asymmetric traction on the tracheostomy tube, potentially leading to off‐midline positioning and weakening of the lateral tracheal wall (Figure 1).

To date, there is a paucity of literature relating to the specific airway complications in tracheostomy‐dependent infants with BPD. Understanding this relationship is essential for informing surgical decision‐making and optimizing long‐term airway outcomes. Thus, the objective of this study was to compare the airway findings and outcomes of tracheostomy‐dependent children with and without BPD.

Methods

Study Population and Data Sources

A retrospective chart review was conducted at a tertiary pediatric care hospital, with data being extracted from the electronic medical record (EHR) of patients who underwent tracheostomy between January 2012 and December 2022. Patients were included if they underwent tracheostomy at an age less than 6 months and had a minimum follow‐up of 6 months. Patients who underwent tracheostomy at a different hospital were excluded. Institutional review board approval was obtained from the Ann and Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago.

Airway Findings

Direct Laryngoscopy and bronchoscopies (DLBs) were performed according to our institution's standardized protocol [16]. Following tracheostomy tube placement, patients undergo scheduled surveillance DLBs to assess airway patency and ensure continued safety. The initial DLB is typically performed 1 month after the tracheostomy procedure, with subsequent evaluations every 3 to 6 months until the child reaches 2 years of age, when growth‐related airway changes begin to slow. From ages 2 to 5, DLBs are performed approximately every 6 months, and then annually thereafter. Each DLB began with an assessment of the stoma and a direct laryngoscopy, followed by the use of a 0‐degree telescope to visualize the larynx, subglottis, and trachea. The tracheostomy tube was retracted as needed to optimize visualization of the stoma site. After each procedure, the surgeon documented findings in a detailed operative report. These reports were reviewed to identify the timing and nature of any interventions performed.

Additional DLBs may be prompted by concerns raised during clinic visits or admission, including granulation tissue formation at the stoma, tracheal bleeding, the need for tracheostomy tube upsizing, or evaluation for decannulation readiness.

Study Variables

Patient charts were reviewed for demographic data (sex, ethnicity, and race) and previous medical information (age at tracheostomy, gestational age at time of birth, days intubated prior to tracheostomy, hypotonia, BPD, and structural/cardiac defects). “Days intubated” was calculated as the total number of days a patient was intubated prior to undergoing tracheostomy. A patient was considered to experience hypotonia if a diagnosis of hypotonia or neuromuscular disease was included in their EHR. BPD and grade of severity were defined according to the 2001 National Institute of Health (NIH) criteria [17]. Structural cardiac defects were included if they had a significant impact on cardiopulmonary function such as atrioventricular septal defects, patent foramen ovale, tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of great vessels or valvular disease that requires surgical interventions. Additional outcome information such as tracheostomy decannulation, laryngotracheal reconstruction (LTR), and death during follow‐up, was collected. Tracheal findings were derived from operative reports and included subglottic stenosis (SGS), tracheomegaly, suprastomal collapse, local edema, the occurrence of granulation tissue, cartilage exposure prior to tracheostomy, vocal cord mobility, and mechanical ventilation status. A patient was considered to require mechanical ventilation regardless of whether the ventilation was intermittent or constant. Ventilation time was calculated as the time from tracheostomy to the DLB at which mechanical ventilation dependence was no longer required. DLBs were reviewed up to the 10th DLB or until death, LTR, or decannulation.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic and medical information was summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR) or as count and percentage when appropriate. Categorical variables were evaluated through Chi‐Square or Fisher's exact test when appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using non‐parametric tests. A log‐rank test was used to calculate the difference in ventilator liberation between subgroups. Logistic regression analysis was utilized to identify variables associated with SGS in an adjusted model. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio, Version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 82 patients met inclusion criteria, with 42 (51.2%) of patients having a diagnosis of severe BPD. The mean age at the time of tracheostomy for all patients was 4.37 months (SD = 1.50) and the mean gestational age at birth was 32.0 weeks (SD = 5.98). A majority of the patients were male (n = 45, 54.9%), white (n = 30, 36.6%), and non‐Hispanic (n = 61, 74.4%). The median follow‐up period for all patients was 28.8 months [IQR = 36.8], with no significant difference in the length of follow‐up between patients with and without BPD (33.3 months vs. 26.3 months, p = 0.75). A majority of patients underwent mechanical ventilation during the study period (n = 66, 80.4%), with 28 (42.4%) of these patients being weaned off ventilation during the study period. Among ventilator‐dependent patients, the median time to wean off the ventilator was 28.4 [IQR = 29.6] months. There was no statistically significant difference in the median time to ventilator liberation between patients with and without BPD (p = 0.51) (Figure 3). All patients with BPD underwent tracheostomy due to prolonged mechanical ventilation requirements (n = 42, 100%); however accompanying indications within the cohort featured pulmonary hypertension (n = 8), subglottic stenosis (n = 5), and diaphragmatic hernia (n = 2). Additional diagnoses that were mentioned as affecting the decision to proceed with tracheostomy included intraventricular hemorrhage (n = 4), hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, and genetic syndrome (n = 1). Indications for tracheostomy for patients without BPD included upper airway obstruction in patients with genetic syndrome (n = 13), neuromuscular disease (n = 10), congenital cardiac defect (n = 5), pulmonary hypertension (n = 3), vocal cord paralysis (n = 3), congenital diaphragmatic hernia (n = 2), subglottic stenosis (n = 2), and intraventricular hemorrhage (n = 2).

Patients with BPD were significantly older at the time of tracheostomy (4.44 vs. 3.36 months, p = 0.012), had a younger median gestational age at birth (26.5 vs. 37.0 weeks, p < 0.001), and were more likely to have a diagnosis of hypotonia (47.6% vs. 17.5%, p = 0.0037). Patients with BPD were intubated for a significantly longer period of time prior to tracheostomy (median time 92 vs. 21 days, p < 0.001) and were significantly more likely to undergo mechanical ventilation during the study period (100% vs. 60.0%, p < 0.001). Additionally, patients with BPD were more likely to have a diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension than those without BPD (75.2% vs. 40.0%, p < 0.001). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of tracheostomy‐dependent children, we compared clinical characteristics, airway findings, and outcomes in patients with and without BPD. Our findings underscore both the unique vulnerabilities and favorable outcomes associated with severe BPD in the tracheostomy population.

Patients with BPD were born at significantly lower gestational ages, were more likely to present with pulmonary hypertension, and were intubated for a longer period prior to tracheostomy. The older postnatal age at tracheostomy in this population likely reflects the clinical decision‐making process to delay surgery in hopes of weaning from ventilation.

This study highlights distinct differences in airway pathology between tracheostomy‐dependent infants with and without BPD. Patients with BPD in our cohort had significantly higher odds of presenting with glottic edema and SGS during follow‐up. While prolonged intubation, high‐pressure ventilation, and chronic airway irritation are known contributors, the increased vulnerability of the premature airway likely amplifies risk. Other factors, such as increased laryngeal reactivity or off‐midline ventilation tube positioning, may also contribute to the condition. However, multivariate analysis did not identify any single variable as a significant predictor of subglottic stenosis, highlighting the multifactorial and complex nature of airway injury in this population. Larger studies with more granular data are needed to better define these relationships.

Despite these differences, rates of other structural airway findings, including tracheomalacia, tracheomegaly, suprastomal collapse, and vocal cord paresis, did not differ significantly between groups despite differences in ventilation requirements. This may reflect the protective role of standardized tracheostomy care and ventilator management protocols in preventing additional airway trauma. The comparable rates of suprastomal collapse suggest that mechanical factors related to tube positioning may play a greater role than underlying pulmonary disease. Our findings align with multicenter registry data showing that, among ventilator‐dependent infants with BPD, the presence of tracheobronchomalacia does not significantly impact ventilator requirements, timing of liberation from ventilation, or the likelihood of decannulation [18]. Despite their increased incidence of proximal airway pathology, infants with BPD were more likely to be decannulated than non‐BPD patients. This finding is particularly interesting given the higher rates of SGS and glottic edema in BPD patients, and may reflect the difference in underlying indications for tracheostomy. In BPD, tracheostomy is typically placed for respiratory support in the setting of pulmonary immaturity—a condition that may improve over time with lung growth and development. In contrast, non‐BPD patients in our cohort had complex anatomical, neurologic, or syndromic comorbidities that likely did not improve with time and thus made decannulation within the study period less feasible. Consistent with this, prior work in a large BPD cohort found that the presence of a chronic neurologic condition was the only factor significantly associated with prolonged time to decannulation, particularly beyond 3 years of age, whereas other comorbidities, race, ethnicity, and provider volume were not significant predictors [19]. Prior studies have similarly shown that premature infants with BPD have higher rates of ventilator liberation and decannulation than term infants with other tracheostomy indications, with median decannulation ages ranging from ~32 to ~49 months, though timing and practice patterns vary across centers [20, 21, 22].

Importantly, although BPD patients were more frequently decannulated, they also had significantly higher rates of LTR, suggesting a greater burden of airway remodeling and structural compromise requiring surgical correction prior to decannulation, as is evidenced by higher rates of subglottic stenosis [23]. This is consistent with prior work in a cohort of preterm infants with severe BPD undergoing tracheostomy, in which nearly half of decannulated patients required airway reconstruction before decannulation [8]. However, it should be noted that patients with BPD may intrinsically be better candidates for LTR as they are more likely to be good candidates for decannulation when compared to other indications for tracheostomy. The mortality rate among patients with severe BPD in our study was 11.9%, which is lower than previously reported rates of 15%–21% [24, 25]. This discrepancy is likely due to differences in follow‐up duration and the fact that mortality in our cohort was measured only after tracheostomy [26].

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

Our findings emphasize the importance of early and targeted surveillance for infraglottic injury in BPD patients, particularly given the strong association between BPD and SGS. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion and consider timely endoscopic evaluation in this population, especially in the setting of weaning difficulties. Furthermore, the high rate of successful decannulation supports a proactive approach to decannulation planning in BPD patients, with multidisciplinary involvement to optimize timing and assess airway readiness.

Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings in larger, multicenter cohorts and to explore potential modifiable risk factors such as timing of tracheostomy, ventilator settings, or circuit configuration—to inform prevention strategies in BPD patients. Additionally, implementing standardized protocols for the timing and documentation of DLBs would enhance the consistency of airway assessments and facilitate more meaningful comparisons across institutions. These efforts could ultimately enhance clinical decision‐making and support more informed counseling for families of tracheostomy‐dependent infants with BPD.

Study Limitations

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design limits the ability to establish causal relationships and introduces the potential for selection and documentation biases. Second, the sample size may limit the statistical power to detect differences in airway findings. Third, all structural airway abnormalities were identified through DLBs performed per institutional routine. As these procedures were not standardized in timing or frequency across all patients, it is possible that certain findings were under‐ or over‐represented depending on the clinical indications for each DLB. Fourth, our ability to assess the impact of neurologic comorbidities on outcomes was limited, as chart documentation did not reliably capture the presence or severity of significant brain injury (e.g., clinical implications of severe intraventricular hemorrhage or hypoxic–ischemic injury), which may have a greater influence on decannulation rates and long‐term mechanical ventilation needs than the broader and less specific diagnosis of hypotonia. Additionally, surgeon interpretation and documentation may have introduced subjective variability in the reported airway pathology. Furthermore, although the median follow‐up in our cohort was 28.8 months, not all patients had sufficiently long surveillance to capture late‐onset airway sequelae such as suprastomal collapse, tracheal A‐frame deformity, decannulation, granulation tissue, and other findings which often develop 12–24 months after tracheostomy, potentially leading to underestimation of their true incidence. However, existing literature does suggest that airway anomalies tend to manifest in earlier periods following tracheostomy [16]. Conversely, the shorter follow‐up period may have led to the underestimation of tracheostomy outcomes such as decannulation. Other variables such as tracheomalacia may have additionally been underreported as study variables were collected from DLBs rather than dynamic evaluations. Finally, ventilation settings differ significantly for BPD patients across institutions and between one surveillance DLB and another, further limiting the generalizability of these findings [27].

Nevertheless, our study contributes new data on airway pathology and outcomes in tracheostomy‐dependent infants with BPD, highlighting findings such as higher rates of SGS while also demonstrating the potential for successful decannulation in this high‐risk group.

Conclusion

In this study, tracheostomy‐dependent infants with BPD exhibited higher rates of glottic edema, subglottic stenosis, and a greater need for laryngotracheal reconstruction compared to non‐BPD patients. Despite this, BPD patients had higher odds of successful decannulation, reflecting their airway disease and clinical trajectory. These findings highlight the complex airway challenges in BPD patients while underscoring their potential for decannulation with appropriate intervention.
