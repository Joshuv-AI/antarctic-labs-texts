Aberrant glycosylation in hematologic malignancies: mechanisms, immune evasion, and therapeutic targeting
Authors: Xiao Lu, Zhouying Song, Chunhui Wang, Bing Pan, Ziqi Xu, Zilong Wang, Hequn Wang, Xiaobo Wang, Bo Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hematologic malignancies are a group of malignant diseases originating from hematopoietic stem cells or the lymphatic system, mainly including leukemia, lymphoma, myeloma and so on. These diseases are characterized by their high heterogeneity, rapid disease progression and poor prognosis. Glycosylation is one of the most common post-translational modifications. In recent years, it has been found that abnormal glycosylation plays an important role in the genesis, development and treatment of hematologic malignancies. Aberrant glycosylation has been demonstrated to exert a significant influence on the progression of disease, impacting various biological processes including tumor cell signaling, the tumor microenvironment, cellular recognition, and immune evasion. This review synthesizes recent advances in how dysregulated glycosylation drives the biology of leukemia, lymphoma, myeloma, and other types of malignancies. Additionally, it discusses the potential value of glycosylation products that can be used as biomarkers for disease diagnosis and prognosis.

Introduction

Glycosylation, a ubiquitous post-translational modification, plays a pivotal role in regulating protein stability, cellular communication, and immune responses [1]. This enzymatic process involves the glycans transferred to specific amino acid residues in proteins by glycosyltransferases, which shapes their functional diversity [2]. Most glycans are assembled and initially matured in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum, and some of them require further trimming through the Golgi [3]. Mature glycoproteins are then distributed to different locations such as the cell membrane or secreted outside the cell.

Glycosylation encompasses a variety of types, each with distinct roles and mechanisms (Fig. 1) [4]. N-glycosylation, the predominant form, involves the transfer of an oligosaccharide chain containing N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) to the asparagine residue of proteins, a process catalyzed by glycosyltransferases [5]. This modification is essential for protein folding and quality control, particularly within the endoplasmic reticulum, where it facilitates the proper folding of nascent glycoproteins through interactions with the glycoprotein chaperone system [6]. O-glycosylation, which involves the attachment of sugar moieties to serine or threonine residues, occurs predominantly in the cytoplasm and nucleus [7]. This modification plays a crucial role in regulating protein stability and function, particularly in processes such as cell adhesion and immune evasion [4]. O-glycosylation includes two primary types: O-GalNAc and O-linked β-D-N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) [8]. The initiation of O-GalNAc glycosylation is catalyzed by N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) transferase, and it is expressed in a variety of tumor types and plays a role in cell-cell interaction, and cell-matrix interactions [9]. On the other hand, O-GlcNAc glycosylation is regulated by O-GlcNAc transferase and O-GlcNAcase, influencing protein modification and being implicated in the progression and invasiveness of tumors [10]. C-glycosylation, a relatively rare form of glycosylation, involves the covalent attachment of mannose to the tryptophan residue via a C-C bond. This modification is typically observed in specific protein classes, such as certain serine proteases [11]. Glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored glycosylation is pivotal for the proper localization and functional activity of cell surface proteins. Additionally, glycosylation modifications such as sialylation, which involves adding sialic acid to the glycan chain terminus, fucosylation, which introduces fucose residues, and sulfation, characterized by the addition of sulfate groups, significantly influence cellular processes by altering glycan structure and properties [13]. These modifications are not only fundamental to various cellular functions but also play pivotal roles in disease pathogenesis, disease classification, immune evasion, drug resistance, making them critical targets for therapeutic intervention [14–16].

In hematologic malignancies, aberrant glycosylation is increasingly recognized as hallmarks of specific blood diseases, which could drive signal transduction, tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. In this review, the role of aberrant glycosylation in hematologic malignancies can be summarized into three functional dimensions. First, aberrant glycosylation acts as a pathogenic driver by rewiring cell signaling and differentiation. In B-cell malignancies, the acquisition of atypical N-glycosylation motifs within the B-cell receptor (BCR) variable region, such as high-mannose glycans in follicular lymphoma (FL), creates a pathological interface with microenvironmental lectins [17]. This interaction sustains antigen-independent signaling (via SYK/PI3K/AKT pathways), promoting tumor survival [18, 19]. Second, glycosylation creates physical barriers to therapy. Hyper-glycosylation or specific sialylation patterns can form a “glycan shield” that sterically hinders the binding of therapeutic antibodies (e.g., Polatuzumab vedotin targeting CD79b) or prevents drug internalization, leading to primary or acquired resistance [20]. Third, aberrant glycans orchestrate immune evasion and microenvironment crosstalk. A prominent strategy involves the “sialylation-Siglec” axis, where tumor cells hypersialylate surface proteins (e.g., PSGL-1 in myeloma) to engage inhibitory Siglec receptors on NK cells and macrophages, thereby dampening anti-tumor immunity [21]. Concurrently, specific glycans like sLeX facilitate the homing of tumor cells to protective niches in the bone marrow, fostering resistance [22].

To be brief, aberrant glycosylation has emerged as a critical contributor to pathogenesis, with distinct glycan signatures influencing disease heterogeneity, microenvironment interactions, dysregulated differentiation and clinical outcomes in hematologic malignancies [4].

B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma

B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL), which comprises 70–85% of all NHL cases, spans biologic extremes: while some subtypes follow an indolent course, others erupt as highly aggressive malignancies derived from transformed B lymphocytes [23]. It is notable for its significant heterogeneity and rapid progression, which pose substantial challenges to effective treatment [24]. A key pathological feature is the aberrant N-glycosylation of the BCR, which plays a critical role in the pathogenesis, progression and drug resistance of these diseases, especially in the subtypes of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and FL. In contrast, mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), although classified within B-NHL, rarely exhibits the acquired N-glycosylation sites like FL and DLBCL, indicating that its pathogenesis does not rely on the BCR glycosylation [25].

To understand how glycosylation affects BCR function, it is essential to first examine its basic structure (Fig. 2a). The BCR is a membrane protein expressed on the surface of B cells which plays a vital role in antigen recognition. It is composed of surface Ig and Igα/Igβ (CD79A/CD79B), and is considered as one of the most significant molecules on the surface of B cells [26]. The variable region of the BCR, composed of heavy and light chain variable regions (collectively called the complementary determining region, CDR), is responsible for recognizing specific epitopes of antigens, and the constant region of the BCR is responsible for transmitting signals to the interior of the cell [27]. The normal process of antigen recognition and binding triggers the polymerization of the BCR, leading to the activation of immunoreceptor tyrosine-activated motifs (ITAMs) within the cytoplasmic region of the Ig α/Igβ cell. These ITAMs can recruit downstream signaling molecules, thereby initiating signal transduction [28]. BCR signaling involves a network of kinases and phosphatases, and the transmission pathways can be classified into two types: chronically activated BCR and tonic BCR. Chronically activated BCR is an antigen-dependent process that primarily utilizes the NF-κB/MAPK/ERK pathway, among others. In contrast, tonic BCR is antigen-independent and maintains B-cell survival through the PI3K/AKT pathway [29, 30].

However, normal B cells trigger Ig gene rearrangement, somatic hypermutation, and chromosomal translocation through BCR signaling. Aberrant BCR activation, via antigen-dependent or -independent pathways, induces malignant transformation of B cells. In certain B-NHL subtypes like ABC-DLBCL and MCL, persistent BCR signaling is closely linked to tumor cell survival and proliferation [31].

In FL and certain DLBCL subtypes, BCR glycosylation triggers persistent, low-level signaling by engaging specific microenvironmental lectins (Fig. 2d). This sustained signaling has been demonstrated to promote tumor cell survival and proliferation. The inhibition of glycosylation-related enzymes has been shown to significantly reduce BCR signaling. Consequently, the targeting of glycosylation emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy [32].

Follicular lymphoma

FL is an indolent B-cell lymphoma, characterized by the genetic hallmark of t(14;18) translocation, with up-regulating expression of the BCL-2 oncoprotein [33]. Meanwhile, during the somatic hypermutation, the N-glycosylation sites of the vast majority of FL are introduced in their immunoglobulin variable region genes, resulting in the acquisition of new glycans in the Fab fragment of the BCR on the cell surface [34, 35]. Crystallographic analysis of lymphoma-derived Fabs has now revealed the specific position of the N-glycosylation site, which is in CDR1 at position N38 [36]. The N-glycosylation of BCRs in FL cells is characterized by bearing high mannose glycans [37]. Another feature of these glycans is usually restricted to immature glycoproteins in the endoplasmic reticulum, since most surface glycoproteins, including the constant region of normal Ig, are matured by the addition of further glycan chains during the transport of Ig through the Golgi. This indicates that they are not fully matured in the Golgi, most likely due to their inaccessibility to the appropriate Golgi enzymes [17]. Further, most of these sites are embedded in the CDR loop in the heavy chain thus preventing further maturation [38]. This may be due to CDR specific spatial obstruction, where further processing of the glycan chain is prevented, resulting in exposure of the mannose Ig to the microenvironment. The Glycosylation feature of the BCR in classical FL cells has been extensively proved, and its indispensable role suggests a critical requirement for lymphomagenesis [39, 40].

Among the different subgroups of FL, the frequency of N-glycosylation sites was significantly higher in t(14;18)-positive FL than in t(14;18)-negative FL [41]. It has been demonstrated that N-glycosylation sites exhibit stability during the process of FL evolution, thus indicating that FL tumor cells are dependent on them for their survival [34]. Both N-glycosylation sites positive and negative tumor subclones may be selected and expanded during tumor evolution. Expression of BCR pathway and inflammatory response-related genes was higher in positive cells, whereas tumor cells without N-glycosylation sites were more active in energy metabolism-related pathways. N-glycosylation sites negative tumor cells may overcome the selective advantage of N-glycosylation sites positive cells in FL by up-regulating metabolic pathways [34]. Concurrently, another study observed that N-glycosylation sites positive FL cells exhibited increased expression of genes associated with the dark zone and reduced expression of genes associated with the light zone in comparison to N-glycosylation sites negative FL cells. Besides, the dark zone is the site in the germinal center where B cells proliferate in large numbers and undergo somatic high-frequency mutations, where the BCR undergoes somatic hypermutation and competes for the help of T cells in the light zone to complement their continued proliferation in the dark zone. This finding suggests that the BCR facilitates the polarization of FL cells towards the dark zone phenotype through N-linked glycosylation, including increased activation-induced cytidine deaminase expression, increased somatic hypermutation rates and subsequent enhanced subcloned evolution [33]. This could provide clues for further elucidation of FL cell glycosylation drivers. Acquisition of the N-glycosylation site in the surface Ig (SIg) variable region of the gene is not unique to FL, and it is also detected in other germinal center-associated lymphomas, particularly in a subset of DLBCL.

These sites are rare in normal B cells, suggesting that they are involved in tumor pathogenesis. In FL, these high mannose glycans were found to severely impair BCR specificity and affinity to the initial cognate antigen [37].

On the other hand, the aberrant N-glycosylation with high mannose glycan of the BCR leads to its interaction with lectins such as Mannose Receptor and the dendritic cell-specific intercellular adhesion molecule-3-grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN) [17], which is expressed by dendritic cells and macrophages, and in turn activates multiple intracellular signaling pathways that are critical for FL cell survival and proliferation. DC-SIGN, also known as CD209, expressed by dendritic cells and macrophages, plays an initiating role in the early stages of diseases such as infection, inflammation and tumors. It is involved in the progression of FL by interacting with the high mannose in surface Ig of BCR. In FL, mannose Ig has a specific binding affinity for the carboxylate recognition structural domain of DC-SIGN. This binding interaction is unique to FL cells and does not occur with normal B cells or other blood cells. It triggers relatively reduced but sustained BCR responses which are antigen-independent, but fails to induce endocytosis in comparison with anti-Ig, further activating a variety of intracellular signaling pathways essential for FL cell survival and proliferation, and contributing to FL progression [18].

In an in vitro experiment, primary FL BCRs stimulated with DC-SIGN resulted in phosphorylation of the signal pathways extracellular protein kinase (ERK1/2) and protein kinase B (AKT), and the response was even more pronounced in magnetic bead-conjugated DC-SIGN [19]. The responses were markedly inhibited by the inhibitors of the BCR-associated proximal kinases, SYK and BTK. Furthermore, the investigation revealed that it causes the phosphorylation of phospholipase Cγ 2, which plays a pivotal role in the regulation of intracellular calcium and cell signal, a critical component of BCR signaling. In addition, the basal expression of the downstream BCR-related target gene MYC was very low, but MYC expression increased dramatically after stimulation with magnetic bead-conjugated DC-SIGN. Control experiments using normal B cells showed that this phenomenon was not observed upon DC-SIGN stimulation [19].

There is a controversy over the glycosylation of the Ig isoforms that can bind lectins and trigger FL activation. Amin et al. have shown that DC-SIGN activation occurs in IgM+ and IgG+ FL, whereas only IgM+ FL cells are significantly responsive to lectin. Some IgM+ FL samples showed BCRs with high mannose glycans are able to efficiently bind DC-SIGN [18]. Nevertheless, lectin-glycosylated BCR interactions may still play a role in the pathogenesis of IgG+ FL, although they appear to be unnecessary by enhancing autoantigen-specific activation. What’s more, FL B cells activated a stronger BCR signaling network than IgG+ FL B cells and normal GC B cells [18, 40, 42]. The potential causes of this heterogeneity are not clear but may be found in both BCR expression levels and phosphatase activity, and related to differences in the self-reactivity of IgG+ FL and IgM+ FL.

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

DLBCL is the most prevalent form of aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphoma, characterized by rapidly growing tumor cells that usually originate from B lymphocytes [1]. DLBCL is classified into three categories based on cell-of-origin, namely germinal center B-cell-like (GCB) subtype, activated B-cell-like (ABC) subtype and other subtypes. ABC tumors are dependent on self-antigen engagement of the BCR, which initiates chronic active signaling and activation of NF-κB and PI3 kinases. BCR signaling in GCB tumors, on the other hand, is antigen-independent and predominantly activates PI3 kinases rather than NF-κB signaling [43, 44]. Genomic studies can further illustrate the genetic alterations in DLBCL and facilitate the classification of genetic subtypes, including MCD (based on the co-occurring L265P and CD79B mutations in MYD88), BN2 (based on the BCL6 fusion and NOTCH2 mutation), N1 (based on the NOTCH1 mutation) and EZB (based on the EZH2 mutation and BCL2 fusion) and so on [44]. The BCR glycosylation status in DLBCL is strongly associated with disease subtype and progression, prognosis and drug resistance.

Disease subdivisions

The aberrant N-glycosylation of the BCR is able to identify DLBCLs with distinct origin and behavior. The EZB subtype is characterized by the presence of EZH2 mutations and BCL2 gene rearrangements. These genetic alterations are commonly associated with the GCB subtype of DLBCL and are associated with a better prognosis, which may indicate a shared N-glycosylation features with FL, because they may have a common origin. In GCB-DLBCL, N-glycosylation sites are obtained in approximately 60% of the cases, and 85% of them are in the CDRs of immunoglobulins, particularly prevalent in the EZB genetic subtype (74%). Through mass spectrometry and X-ray crystallography, the functional N-glycosylation sites N38 (HCDR1), N55 (HCDR2), and N107 (HCDR3) are further identified. This contrasts with ABC-DLBCL, which rarely possesses loci in the CDR and does not appear to acquire glycans structures [38].

In a similar manner like FL, glycosylation BCR specifically recognizes and binds to DC-SIGN in the tumor microenvironment in EZB-DLBCL. This binding triggers a low-level antigen-independent and persistent signal [18, 19, 37]. This process has been observed to result in the phosphorylation of signaling molecules, including SYK, ERK and AKT. These phosphorylation events have been found to activate downstream signaling pathways, which promote the activation of ERK and AKT. This has been proved to have a pro-survival and proliferation-promoting effect on lymphoma cells. In DLBCL patients without the MYC translocation, DLBCL carrying the BCR N-glycosylation site showed significantly faster clinical progression. This suggests that BCR glycosylation and the signaling that glycosylation triggers may accelerate disease progression in DLBCL [38].

Targeted glycosylation

Oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) is a multi-subunit complex located in the endoplasmic reticulum, which catalyzes the transfer of a high-mannose glycan onto secretory proteins [45]. Mammals express two distinct OST complexes that act in a cotranslational (OST-A) or posttranslocational (OST-B) manner, and OST-B has a significantly higher affinity for glycosylated sequences than OST-A [46]. OST-B is responsible for adding N-glycosylation modifications to the BCR complex (such as IgM, CD79A, and CD79B).

These glycosylation modifications maintain the aggregated state of the BCR on the cell membrane, thereby promoting proximal BCR signaling and activation of the PI3 kinase pathway in BCR-dependent ABC and GCB DLBCL cells [43]. The inhibition via the OST inhibitor NGI-1, particularly its catalytic subunit STT3B, has been shown to reduce the expression of IRF4 by >70%, a direct transcriptional target of the NF-κB signal pathway and a potent marker of BCR signaling in ABC-DLBCL(Fig. 2b) [43]. This reduction was achieved by decreasing the glycosylation of the BCR. BCR deglycosylation reduced the phosphorylation of ITAM tyrosines of CD79A and CD79B, as well as the phosphorylation of BCR proximal kinases, such as the Src family kinases and SYK. BCR deglycosylation also affected the PI3-kinase-dependent phosphorylation of AKT, which inhibited downstream NF-κB activation.

Furthermore, a reduction in BCR glycosylation exerts the following significant effects: 1) mutations in specific glycans in the BCR subunits IgM and CD79B have been shown to impair their ability to maintain ABC DLBCL cell survival; 2) mutations in IgM glycans have been demonstrated to bind the BCR to the negative regulator CD22; 3) the formation of My-T-BCR super complexes is impaired in cells expressing IgM glycosidic mutant isoforms, thereby attenuating BCR-dependent NF-κB signal. The N46 glycoside of IgM has been previously identified as being essential for BCRs formed with alternative light chain λ5 [47]. However, the role of IgM N46Q mutant isoforms in ABC cells is distinct, as they do not impair cell surface BCR expression [43]. By directly interfering with proximal BCR signal, OST-B inactivation has been shown to be effective in models of ABC and GCB-DLBCL, thus supporting the development of selective OST-B inhibitors for the treatment of these aggressive cancers. The OST inhibitor NGI-1 has been observed to synergistically interact with the BTK inhibitor ibrutinib and the PI3 kinase inhibitor copanlisib, resulting in the killing of models of DLBCL in vitro and the inhibition of the growth of transplanted tumors in vivo. In addition to DLBCL, PI kinases are constitutively activated in lymphomas of other germinal center origins, including FL and Burkitt’s lymphoma [48, 49], suggesting that selective OST-B inhibitors may have a broad spectrum of activity against B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas.

Drug resistance of polatuzumab vedotin (Pola-V)

In January 2024, the NCCN B-cell lymphoma guidelines (2024.V1) upgraded the Pola-R-CHP regimen to Class I evidence for first-line treatment of DLBCL, which means a paradigm shift from the 20-year R-CHOP standard [50, 51]. Pola-V is the first antibody-drug conjugate to target CD79B, which has been demonstrated to exhibit high levels of anti-tumor activity in B-cell lymphoma [52–54]. Pola-V combines a monoclonal antibody with the cytotoxic drug monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), which is capable of precisely targeting the CD79B protein on the surface of cancerous B cells [55, 56]. CD79B is expressed on the surface of B cells through the formation of a disulfide bonded heterodimeric complex with Igα. It is widely expressed in a variety of malignant B cell types, which gives Pola-V a more precise targeting effect and renders it an important therapeutic option for a wide range of B cell tumor patients [55]. While Pola-V offers significant clinical benefits by delivering a cytotoxic payload directly to B cells, recent studies indicate that its efficacy is heavily influenced by post-translational modifications of its target antigen.

To be specific, aberrant N-glycosylation of CD79B, particularly α2,6 sialylation, sterically hinders the Pola-V epitope and is emerging as a primary mechanism of primary or acquired resistance to Pola-V in DLBCL (Fig. 2c). Sialylation refers to the enzymatic addition of sialic acid (N-acetylneuraminic acid, Neu5Ac) to terminal glycans of glycoproteins or glycolipids via α-2,3, α-2,6, or α-2,8 linkages, catalyzed by sialyltransferases (STs) using cytidine monophosphate-Neu5Ac (CMP-Neu5Ac) as a donor [57]. ST3GAL (β-galactoside α-2,3-sialyltransferase), a group core enzyme in sialylation, catalyzes α-2,3-sialylation to synthesize functional selectin ligands (e.g., sialyl Lewis X, sLeX). Sialic acids, positioned at glycan termini, regulate cell recognition, signaling, and intercellular interactions [58, 59].

Researchers used the CRISPR-Cas9 screening method to successfully identify two functional categories of genes associated with N-linked glycosylation. The first category includes genes that encode subunits of the oligosaccharide transferase (OST) complex, such as STT3B, MAGT1, OST4, RPN1, DDOST, RPN2, DAD1, and TMEM258. The second category includes glycosylation enzymes, glycoprotein transporters, and glycosyltransferases that modify the Golgi apparatus (ST6GAL1, SLC35A1, SLC35A2, B4GALT1, B3GNT2, GALE, GNE, NANS, and CMAS) [60]. ST6GAL1, one of the highest-scoring genes in the screening, is responsible for adding α2,6-linked sialic acid to the N-glycan chains of cell surface and secreted glycoproteins. It is also closely associated with various human cancers. Tumor-specific ST6GAL1 promotes tumor cell invasion and resistance to cellular stress and chemotherapy/radiotherapy. In vitro experiments demonstrated that CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of ST6GAL1 significantly increased the sensitivity of DLBCL cells to Pola-V. Specifically, ABC (TMD8, RIVA) and GCB (FL-318, SUDHL4) cell lines with ST6GAL1 knocked out exhibited a significant decrease in viability under Pola-V treatment, ranging from 50% to 70%. Additionally, in NSG mouse models, RIVA and FL-318 cell lines with ST6GAL1 gene knockout exhibited significantly enhanced sensitivity to Pola-V, with tumor volumes reduced by approximately 75% and 60%, respectively, compared to the control group.

CRISPR-Cas9 screening, saturation mutagenesis and glycoproteomic mapping have pinpointed specific asparagine residues (CD79B N127/N128) whose glycan occupancy creates a “shield” that reduces antibody binding and downstream MMAE delivery. Pharmacological or enzymatic deglycosylation reverses this shield. A study found through CRISPR-Cas9 screening that inactivation of the STT3B subunit of the OST-B complex enhances sensitivity to Pola-V. Further experiments showed that the small molecule OST-B inhibitor NGI-1 (targeting STT3B) enhances the cytotoxicity of Pola-V in vitro and significantly improves the antitumor effect of Pola in lymphoma mouse models (RIVA and FL-318), respectively 60% and 50% compared to using Pola-V alone [20]. Combinatorial studies show sub-micromolar NGI-1 concentrations synergistically enhance Pola-V cytotoxicity (combination index <0.6), translating into improved tumor control and prolonged survival in vivo.

An orthogonal strategy exploits recombinant sialidase conjugated to B-cell–directed antibodies (e.g., anti-CD20-sialidase) to selectively prune terminal sialic acid from cancer cells, thereby may unmask the CD79B epitope and augmenting Pola-V efficacy while simultaneously boosting endogenous anti-tumor immunity via desialylated glycoproteins. Phase I trials of antibody-sialidase conjugates (NCT05259696) have reported acceptable safety, providing a clinically viable path to circumvent glycosylation-mediated resistance [58]. Pola-V toxicity is also subject to regulation by KLHL6, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that is recurrently inactivated in germinal center-derived lymphomas. The present study elucidates the mechanism by which KLHL6 targets the degradation of CD79B in normal and malignant germinal center B cells, thereby determining the expression of the surface BCR complex. KLHL6 promotes the degradation of CD79B by ubiquitinating specific lysine residues of CD79B (e.g., K219), which reduces the level of CD79B on the cell surface. This regulatory effect impacts the expression of the BCR on the cell surface, which in turn affects the sensitivity of cells to Pola-V [61]. In KLHL6-knockout SU-DHL-6 xenografts, Pola-V improved median survival from 28 to 54 days [20]. These functions of KLHL6 suggest that it may be a potential target for DLBCL therapy. The restoration of KLHL6 function or the mimicking of its action may enhance the efficacy of Pola-V, particularly in the GCB subtype of DLBCL.

Collectively, these data establish aberrant glycosylation as a tractable determinant of Pola-V sensitivity and position glyco-engineering—either systemic (NGI-1) or targeted (sialidase conjugates)—as a rational adjunct to Pola-R-CHP that may extend its benefit to molecularly defined DLBCL subsets currently at high risk of relapse.

Multiple myeloma

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a malignant hematologic disorder originating from clonal proliferation of plasma cells in the bone marrow, characterized by osteolytic lesions, anemia, renal impairment, and poor prognosis [62, 63]. The hallmark of MM is excessive production of monoclonal immunoglobulin (M protein), which plays a critical role in diagnosis and disease monitoring. Recent studies highlight that aberrant glycosylation patterns of M protein, particularly sialylation, a specific form of N-glycosylation, are closely associated with disease progression [64]. Specifically, myeloma cells remodel their cell surface glycans through hypersialylation, forming a “glycan shield”. This mechanism persists throughout the disease course: elevated sialylation levels emerge as early as the monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) stage, peak during active MM progression, and correlate closely with poor prognosis. Hypersialylation acts as a pivotal hub linking immune evasion, microenvironmental dependence, and osteolytic lesions by: - Inhibiting NK cell cytotoxicity via PSGL-1/Siglec axis regulation - masking CD38 antigen to diminish monoclonal antibody efficacy - Promoting ST3GAL6-mediated myeloid cell homing and CAM-DR resistance - Driving IgG-Fc hypersialylation to exacerbate bone destruction.

Immune evasion

MM cells exploit glycosylation-dependent mechanisms to evade immune surveillance, with sialylation playing a central role (Fig. 3). The P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) on MM cells acts as a primary ligand for sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectins (Siglecs) [65]. Enhanced sialylation of PSGL-1 via ST6GAL1 facilitates binding to Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 on natural killer (NK) cells, activating the SHP-1/SHP-2 phosphatase pathway to suppress NK cytotoxicity [66, 67]. Clinically, Siglec-7 expression is elevated in bone marrow-derived NK cells from MM patients, and Siglec-7 knockout significantly enhances NK-mediated tumor killing, which increases NK-mediated lysis of MM cells by 40–60% in vitro and reduces tumor burden by 50% in NSG mice. This immune evasion mechanism intensifies during progression from MGUS to active MM and correlates with poor prognosis. Additionally, hypersialylation sterically masks surface antigens (e.g., CD38), reducing the efficacy of targeted antibodies like daratumumab (Dara), and desialylation restored Dara-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), increasing MM cell (H929 and JJN3) lysis from 20% to 65% [21].Fig. 3Immune evasion mechanisms in Multiple Myeloma via hypersialylation.MM cells evade immunity by hyper-sialylating PSGL-1 (ST6GAL1-mediated), which engages Siglec-7/9 on NK cells and recruits SHP-1/2 phosphatases to shut down cytotoxicity. MM cell homing and colonization

Altered sialylation modification has been demonstrated to directly affect the adhesion and migration capacity of MM cells. In vitro experiments showed that knockdown of ST3GAL6 by shRNA technology significantly reduced the level of α-2,3-sialic acid on the surface of MM cells, resulting in a significant decrease in their adhesion to bone marrow stromal cells, fibronectin and endothelial cells. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that ST3GAL6 knockdown MM cells exhibited a significantly diminished capacity for homing and colonization in mouse bone marrow. Using in vivo confocal microscopy, the study observed that ST3GAL6-knockdown MM cells in SCID-Beige mice exhibited significantly impaired homing: homing ability decreased by approximately 75% within 18 hours, colonization ability decreased by >90% after 4 weeks, tumor burden was reduced by 87%, and median survival was extended from 30 days to >50 days [22].

Furthermore, the study found that high expression of ST3GAL6 was closely associated with MM cell homing to the bone marrow [21]. The sialic acid transferase ST3GAL6 catalyses the generation of sLeX, which promotes MM cell adhesion, trans-endothelial migration and bone marrow colonisation by binding to E-selectin in bone marrow endothelial cells and homing to the protective bone marrow microenvironment [22]. Bone marrow stromal cells (BMSC) have been shown to mediate tumor cell rolling and adhesion at bone marrow microvessels through cell adhesion-mediated drug resistance, which in turn facilitates their entry into the bone marrow microenvironment to evade killing by chemotherapeutic agents, leading to enhanced drug resistance and protection of MM cells from bortezomib killing [65].

Glycosylation and MM bone destruction

In addition to the aforementioned mechanisms, the glycosylation profile of M protein in MM is closely associated with bone disease in MM patients. Studies reveal that the Fc region of M protein (primarily IgG) in MM patients with bone disease exhibits significant hyposialylation and hypogalactosylation [68]. This glycosylation deficiency promotes the formation of immunoglobulin aggregates, which bind to FcγRII receptors on osteoclast precursors, directly inducing osteoclast differentiation and exacerbating bone loss. Experiments confirm that IgG isolated from the serum of bone disease patients induces osteoclastogenesis, whereas IgG from healthy individuals or non- bone disease patients does not. Gene expression analysis shows downregulation of sialyltransferase ST6GAL1 and galactosyltransferase B4GALT1 in plasma cells from BD patients, leading to terminal sialic acid and galactose deficiency in IgG-Fc glycans. Notably, IgG hyposialylation worsens with bone disease progression in advanced-stage patients, and hypogalactosylated patients exhibit significantly elevated serum levels of the bone resorption marker CTX-1, indicating a direct correlation between glycosylation deficiency and bone destruction. In animal models, supplementation with the sialic acid precursor N-acetylmannosamine (ManNAc) increases IgG sialylation, significantly reducing osteolytic lesions and tumor burden, providing a rationale for glycosylation-targeted therapies [62].

Targeted Sialylation therapy

Given the critical role of sialylation in MM, targeting the sialylation pathway has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy [69, 70]. The multifunctionality of ST3GAL6 makes it a dual target: inhibiting ST3GAL6 can both block tumor migration and reverse immune suppression [21, 71]. ST3GAL6 is also closely associated with MM progression and patient prognosis. In MM patients, the expression level of ST3GAL6 gradually increases with disease stage (from MGUS to relapsed MM), peaking in secondary plasma cell leukemia [22]. In the MRC IX clinical trial, patients with high ST3GAL6 expression exhibited significantly shorter overall survival, with a median OS of 36 months compared with 48 months for those with low expression (P = 0.041). Multivariate analysis identified high ST3GAL6 expression as an independent poor prognostic factor (HR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.01-2.13; P = 0.04), particularly in patients who did not receive high-dose therapy or thalidomide [22]. By inhibiting ST3GAL6 activity using neuraminidase (NEURA) or 3Fax-Neu5Ac, the removal of sialic acid from the surface of MM cells significantly reduces the expression of Siglec-7/Siglec-9 ligands, thereby relieving the inhibition of NK cells. Experiments have shown that desialylated MM cells are 2–3 times more efficiently killed by NK cells, with a marked increase in NK cell degranulation (CD107a expression) and cytokine (IFN-γ, TNF-α) secretion [21]. Desialylation also exposes CD38 expression on the surface of MM cells, significantly enhancing the ADCC effect when combined with Dara. For MM cells with low CD38 expression, combining all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) to induce CD38 expression with desialylation can further amplify therapeutic efficacy [72]. Specifically, ATRA alone increased CD38 expression levels by 1.9- to 4.4-fold in MM cell lines such as RPMI8226, UM9, and XG1, even at a low concentration of 10 nM. This upregulation of CD38 expression resulted in a significant enhancement of Dara-mediated ADCC and complement-dependent cytotoxicity. For instance, in primary MM cells from patients, ATRA treatment improved dara-mediated complement-dependent cytotoxicity from 16.1% to 43.9% (P < 0.0001) and ADCC from 25.1% to 39.5% (P = 0.0315). Additionally, in a humanized mouse model, ATRA significantly enhanced the anti-MM effect of Dara, reducing tumor burden and improving overall survival [72]. To address cell adhesion-mediated drug resistance, the sialyltransferase inhibitor 3Fax-Neu5Ac inhibits integrin α4 maturation, blocking the interaction between MM cells and the bone marrow microenvironment. This restricts tumor cell homing, forcing MM cells to remain in the circulatory system and thereby enhancing sensitivity to drugs such as bortezomib [70].

Leukemia

Leukemia is a group of malignant clonal diseases of hematopoietic stem cells, characterized by the abnormal proliferation of leukemic cells in the bone marrow and other hematopoietic tissues, infiltration of other organs and tissues, suppression of normal hematopoietic function, and potential spread to other organs. In leukemia, aberrant glycosylation plays a key role in tumor initiation, progression, and immune evasion [73]. For example, abnormal expression of certain glycosyltransferases leads to changes in the glycan structures on the surface of leukemic cells, affecting their recognition and interaction with immune cells, thereby promoting immune evasion. Additionally, glycosylation is closely related to drug resistance, cell adhesion, and signal transduction in leukemic cells. In recent years, with the development of glycomics technology, research on the glycosylation characteristics of leukemia has gradually deepened, revealing the potential value of glycosylation in disease stratification, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic target discovery [4]. For example, leukemia-specific glycan features (such as α2,6-sialylation and core fucosylation) not only serve as novel diagnostic biomarkers but also provide a theoretical basis for the development of glycosylation-targeted therapies (such as OGT inhibitors and glycoengineered CAR-T cells) [74, 75].

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML)

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly heterogeneous hematologic malignancy in terms of genetics and phenotype, with a stagnant survival rate over the years [76]. AML is characterized by the presence of immature leukemic cells in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and extramedullary tissues, which suppress normal hematopoietic function through rapid growth and malignant signaling [77]. Glycomic changes have been reported in AML, involving major categories such as N-glycans, O-glycans, glycosaminoglycans, and glycosphingolipids (GSLs) [78]. Researchers performed glycomics analysis on 21 AML cell lines and found that different FAB subtypes of AML cell lines have unique glycosylation profiles, revealing a strong association between FAB classification and glycosylation features. This suggests that the glycomic profile of AML cells may reflect their differentiation status and FAB classification and impact drug resistance and prognosis in AML. The M5 and M6 subtypes are the most representative [79] (Table 1). The N-glycosylation of AML cell lines is highly heterogeneous, with oligomannose-type glycans being dominant (average 54.4%), but in the M5 subtype, paucimannose and sialylated complex-type glycans are significantly enriched. The M5 subtype is characterized by high expression of (sialyl) Lewis x/a ((s) Lex/a) antigens, with (s) Lex/a accounting for 4.5% of N-glycans (significantly higher than 0.3% in M6) and 12.3% of O-glycans. This phenotype is mainly driven by the synergistic upregulation of glycosyltransferases FUT7 (responsible for fucosylation) and ST3GAL4 (catalyzing α-2,3-sialylation) [80]. The impact of this glycosylation profile on the M5 subtype is mainly reflected in the following aspects: (1) (s) Lex/a binds to E-selectin in the bone marrow vascular niche, activating the PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathways to promote leukemic cell survival and drug resistance [81]; (2) Immune evasion: Sialylation (especially α-2,3 type) enhances interaction with Siglec receptors (such as Siglec-7/9), similar to the mechanism of MM Immune evasion mentioned above, inhibiting the cytotoxic function of natural killer (NK) cells and macrophages, protecting leukemia cells from innate immune clearance provides a microenvironmental support for the progression of AML cells and chemotherapy resistance [82]; (3) Cell differentiation block: High (s) Lex/a expression is associated with downregulation of monocytic differentiation markers (such as CD14) and may maintain an undifferentiated state through the Notch signaling pathway [83, 84]. From a therapeutic perspective, glycosylation intervention strategies targeting (s) Lex/a may provide new avenues for treating the M5 subtype. For example, glycomimetic drugs like GMI-1271 (uproleselan), which block the binding of (s) Lex/a to E-selectin, have shown potential to improve chemotherapy outcomes [85]. The latest clinical trial results show that in a phase I trial (NCT 04964505), uproleselan combined with azacitidine and venetoclax regimen achieved a CR/CRi rate of 38%, and among responders, 64% attained MRD negativity [86]. These data support uproleselan with azacitidine and venetoclax as an active and feasible backbone, further phase II evaluation is in progress. In another phase II study (NCT04848974), patients with relapsed/refractory (third-line) acute myeloid leukemia received uproleselan combined with cladribine low-dose cytarabine [87]. After a median follow-up of 4.2 months, the composite response rate (CR+CRi+MLFS) was 38%. Among 12 MRD-evaluable responders, 42% (5/12) achieved MRD negativity. Median leukemia-free survival was 2.9 months and median overall survival 4.2 months; estimated 1-year post-transplant survival was 65% [88]. Uproleselan added to cladribine and low-dose cytarabine showed clinically meaningful activity and an acceptable safety profile in this ultra-poor-prognosis population; randomized trials are ongoing [89].

The M6 subtype is characterized by extensive sialylation, particularly α-2,6 and α-2,8 sialylation modifications [79]. Its N-glycans have 40% α-2,6 sialylation (higher than 26.6% in M5), and O-glycans have 5.4% α-2,8 sialylation. Glycosyltransferases ST8SIA6 (α-2,8 sialylation) and ST6GALNAC4 (α-2,6 sialylation) are significantly upregulated, regulated by transcription factors GATA1/2 and RUNX1. Additionally, M6 cells are enriched with biantennary sialyl-T antigen (H1N1S2) and Neu5Gc-modified GSLs, while (s) Lex/a expression is extremely low. The impact of glycosylation on the M6 subtype also involves multiple aspects: (1) Immunosuppressive microenvironment: α-2,8 sialylated glycosphingolipids (GSLs) (such as GD3) bind to Siglec-15 to inhibit CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T-cell activity and promote immune tolerance [90]. Meanwhile, high sialylation reduces the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC), weakening antigen presentation; (2) Erythroid differentiation abnormality: Sialylation interferes with the function of key erythroid differentiation proteins (such as GATA1 target genes), leading to erythrocyte maturation blockage. Animal models have shown that inhibiting ST8SIA6 can partially restore erythroid differentiation; (3) Metabolic adaptation: α-2,6 sialylation stabilizes the membrane localization of glucose transporters (such as GLUT1), enhancing glycolytic efficiency to support the survival of rapidly proliferating erythroblasts [91]. Additionally, the high sialylation feature may also serve as a potential biomarker for predicting patient prognosis and treatment response. Transcriptional regulatory network analysis indicates that the SPI1/CEBPA and GATA/RUNX1 axes dominate the glycosylation phenotypes of M5 and M6, respectively, providing new ideas for targeted therapy [79]. In other leukemia subtypes, such as chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), unique glycosylation patterns have also been observed. These patterns may be related to the origin, differentiation stage, and genetic abnormalities of leukemic cells and play important roles in disease initiation, progression, and drug resistance. Recent studies have shown that ST3GAL4 adds sialic acid to the LacNAc core structure to generate Sialyl-LacNAc, a ligand for Siglec-9. Binding of Siglec-9 to these glycans activates the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM), thereby inhibiting the cytotoxic effect of immune cells on AML cells and enhancing immune evasion, indicating the significant role of sialylation in AML immune evasion [73].

Myelodysplastic syndromes/acute myeloid leukemia (MDS/AML)

Beyond de novo AML, glycosylation alterations also play crucial roles in leukemogenesis from preleukemic conditions like MDS. MDS is a clonal disorder of hematopoietic stem cells, characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis, peripheral blood cytopenias, and a propensity to transform into acute leukemia [92]. Approximately 30% of cases transform into acute leukemia, especially high-risk MDS patients. Once progressed to AML, it is generally difficult to cure and has a poor prognosis. This type of disease is called secondary acute myeloid leukemia (MDS/AML). Aberrant glycosylation in MDS and AML patients has certain specificity and thus holds promise as a diagnostic biomarker. Using surface plasmon resonance imaging biosensors and lectin microarray technology in combination with glycomics, it has been found that the levels of glycoproteins in the plasma of MDS and AML patients that bind to lectins are significantly higher than those in healthy controls [93]. This helps distinguish MDS/AML patients from healthy controls and can even stratify MDS patients of different risk levels. In the study, compared with healthy controls, the plasma samples of MDS and AML patients showed more significant interaction with AAL lectin (Aleuria aurantia), especially low-risk and intermediate-risk MDS patients. AAL lectin can recognize and bind to glycan structures containing core fucose and Lewis antigens, indicating that AAL lectin can identify the abnormal fucosylation of glycoproteins in the plasma of MDS patients. ECL lectin (Erythrina cristagalli) has high affinity for galactosylated N-glycans, particularly the Gal-GlcNAc structure. It has been found that the plasma samples of MDS and AML patients also show more significant interaction with ECL lectin, indicating that ECL lectin can detect the abnormal galactosylation of N-glycans in the plasma of MDS patients [93]. Additionally, glycosylation biomarkers can be used in combination with other traditional biomarkers to improve diagnostic accuracy.

In MDS/AML, aberrant glycosylation may also alter the interactions between leukemic cells and bone marrow stromal cells, immune cells, etc. It has been found that MDS/AML clonal cells change the glycosylation levels of stromal cells to generate a self-promoting microenvironment. Specifically, malignant clonal cells transfer miR-188-5p to bone marrow stromal cells via exosomes, a process that inhibits the expression of the MGAT3 gene. MGAT3 (β1,4-mannosyl-glycoprotein 4-β-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase) is a glycosyltransferase responsible for adding biantennary N-GlcNAc structures to melanoma cell adhesion molecule at asparagine 56. The inhibition of MGAT3 reduces the biantennary GlcNAc levels on bone marrow stromal cells, leading to enhanced melanoma cell adhesion molecule expression. After the reduction of biantennary GlcNAc on the surface of stromal cells, melanoma cell adhesion molecule binds more strongly to CD13 on myeloid cells, activating the ERK signaling pathway and thus promoting myeloid cell growth [94].

Conclusion

Aberrant glycosylation has emerged as a fundamental regulator of pathogenesis across hematologic malignancies, driving tumor survival, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. This review synthesizes compelling evidence that glycan modifications are not mere bystanders but active participants in shaping the biological and clinical landscapes of leukemia, lymphoma, and myeloma. Critically, the conservation of glycosylation-driven mechanisms—such as BCR hyperactivity in lymphomas, sialylation-mediated immunosuppression in myeloma, and glycan-dependent bone marrow homing—across diverse malignancies underscores its role as a unifying hallmark of hematologic malignancies.

Glycosylation as a pathogenic driver

In B-cell malignancies, the acquisition of atypical N-glycosylation motifs within the BCR variable region (e.g., high-mannose glycans in FL and EZB-DLBCL) creates a pathologic interface with microenvironmental lectins. DC-SIGN binding to mannosylated BCRs sustains antigen-independent, low-level signaling via SYK/BTK-PI3K/AKT/ERK cascades, promoting lymphoma survival. This mechanism is distinct from chronic BCR activation in ABC-DLBCL, highlighting subtype-specific glycan dependencies. Notably, the evolutionary stability of these glycosylation sites in FL suggests they are non-redundant for lymphomagenesis, though metabolic adaptations may permit clonal escape. Targeting OST-B, the enzyme governing BCR N-glycosylation, disrupts proximal signaling and synergizes with BTK/PI3K inhibitors, revealing a promising therapeutic axis for aggressive lymphomas.

Immune evasion and microenvironment crosstalk

Sialylation dysregulation exemplifies how glycosylation subverts immune surveillance. In MM, hypersialylation of PSGL-1 engages Siglec-7/9 on NK cells, inhibiting cytotoxicity via SHP-1/2 phosphatases. Concurrently, ST3GAL6-generated sLeX facilitates E-selectin–dependent bone marrow homing and cell adhesion-mediated drug resistance, sequestering tumor cells in protective niches. Similarly, AML exploits α2,3-sialylation ((s)Lex/a) to bind E-selectin and Siglecs, fostering vascular niche–mediated chemoresistance and immune evasion. These parallels across diseases emphasize that glycan-lectin interactions are a conserved immunosuppressive strategy. Therapeutic desialylation (e.g., neuraminidase or 3Fax-Neu5Ac) restores NK-mediated killing and sensitizes tumors to antibodies (e.g., daratumumab), supporting combinatorial approaches to overcome microenvironmental protection.

Glycosylation in therapy resistance and development

The impact of glycosylation extends to targeted therapies. In DLBCL, CD79B sialylation impedes Pola-V binding, while KLHL6-mediated CD79B ubiquitination inversely regulates ADC sensitivity. For MM, Fc hyposialylation of M protein promotes immunoglobulin aggregation, activating osteoclasts via FcγRII and exacerbating bone disease—a phenotype reversible with sialic acid precursors. These findings position glycosylation enzymes (e.g., ST6GAL1, ST3GAL6) and glycan editors (e.g., KLHL6) as biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

In hematologic malignancies, both CD22 and CD33 are heavily glycosylated Siglec receptors: the α2,6-hyper-sialylated, highly branched plus polysialic acid glycoform of CD22 on B-ALL cells accelerate inotuzumab ozogamicin internalization and toxin release, whereas the hypersialylated CD33 glyco-mask on AML cells occludes antibody epitopes and slows endocytosis, constituting a key mechanism of gemtuzumab ozogamicin resistance. Pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of ST3Gal4/ST6Gal1, supplementation with sialic acid precursors, or site-specific glycan-engineered conjugation can restore sensitivity and reduce off-target toxicity, providing a precise glyco-biological foundation for subsequent therapies.

Strategies targeting glycosylation in clinical trials.

With deepening research into glycosylation mechanisms, vaccines targeting tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens (TACAs), bispecific antibodies, and glycosylation-modified immune checkpoint inhibitors are progressively entering clinical exploration phases. These developments offer new strategies and directions for precision treatment of hematologic malignancies. In the future, glycosylation-targeted therapies are expected to become an integral component of comprehensive treatment for hematologic cancers.

Aberrant glycosylation has emerged as a core driver of pathogenesis, immune evasion, and drug resistance across hematologic malignancies. By rewiring BCR signaling in lymphomas, masking tumor antigens in myeloma, and orchestrating bone marrow homing in leukemia, glycan modifications actively shape disease biology rather than serving as passive by-products. These insights reposition glycosylation enzymes (e.g., ST6GAL1, ST3GAL6, OST-B) and their products (e.g., sLeX, high-mannose BCR glycans) as actionable biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

In conclusion, precision glyco-oncology will integrate multi-omics datasets to stratify patients by tumor-specific glycan signatures and guide combination regimens that pair glycosylation inhibitors (NGI-1, sialidases) with immunotherapies or ADCs to dismantle protective “glyco-shields.” Continued clinical validation of agents such as uproleselan, antibody–enzyme conjugates, and CRISPR-engineered glyco-modulations is expected to transform the treatment landscape, turning glycan-mediated resistance mechanisms into vulnerabilities for patients with leukemia, lymphoma, and myeloma.
