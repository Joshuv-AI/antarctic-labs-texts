Higher Short‐ and Long‐Term Fluent Reading Abilities Following an Executive Functions‐Based Reading Intervention Are Moderated by Executive Functions Improvement in Children With Reading Difficulties
Authors: T. Horowitz‐Kraus, N. Taran, J. Fotang, K. Rosch, R. Farah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrevious studies have demonstrated how an executive functions‐based reading training was related to improved reading and executive function abilities as well as changes in neural circuits underlying these abilities in children with reading disabilities and in typical readers. The executive components of this programme include visual attention, initiation, shifting/inhibition, speed of processing and working memory. Whether and which executive functions moderate these reading and cognitive effects in the short and long term remain elusive. This study aimed at assessing the short‐term (immediately after) and long‐term (3 months post‐training) outcomes of a previously described computerised executive function‐based reading training. Executive functions, speed of processing and visual attention were assessed pre/post‐training, and reading abilities were assessed pre‐, post‐ and long post‐training in 41 children aged 8–12 (18 typical readers, 23 children with reading disabilities). The intervention improved reading fluency and word decoding in both reading groups, and the performance gain remained significant 3 months after the intervention. Moreover, improvements in speed of processing, inhibition and initiation post‐training were found to be significant moderators of long‐term reading improvement. Results suggest that individuals showing greater improvement in executive functions following reading training with an imposed time constraint show the greatest improvement in long‐term reading gains.

Introduction

Altered Executive Functions Abilities in Children With Reading Difficulty

Reading difficulty (RD) is characterised by slow and inaccurate reading that is not attributed to a lack of environmental exposure, intellectual disability or another neurological or psychiatric disorder (IDA 2011). Several theories were raised regarding the source of RD, whereas the leading one is the phonological deficit theory (Snowling 1995), with recent studies pointing at an additional difficulty in executive functions (EF) (Farah et al. 2021). EF, an umbrella term for several basic (attention, inhibition, working memory, speed of processing, etc.) and higher‐order cognitive abilities (planning, action monitoring), significantly contribute to reading proficiency (Haft et al. 2019; Nouwens et al. 2021). Neuroimaging studies supported these findings, consistently demonstrating alterations in the involvement of neural circuits supporting EF in children with RD while reading single words (Horowitz‐Kraus and Holland 2015), sentences (Meri et al. 2020), fluent reading (Freedman et al. 2020; Taran et al. 2022), stories listening (Horowitz‐Kraus et al. 2016; Twait et al. 2018), verb generation (Twait and Horowitz‐Kraus 2019) as well as during specific EF tasks measuring switching/inhibition, such as the Stroop task (Levinson et al. 2018) and Wisconsin task (Horowitz‐Kraus 2012). Over the years, several interventions aimed to improve reading skills in children with RD by focusing on phonological processing (Richardson and Lyytinen 2014), with some recently developed interventions designed to improve reading via visual attention‐based interventions (Peters et al. 2019). The theoretical justification for using an EF training or components of EF as part of reading interventions stems from the extension of the Simple View of Reading model (Hoover and Gough 1990). Originally, this model highlighted the contribution of word decoding and language processing to intact reading and reading comprehension abilities. However, recent studies suggested that this model should also include reading fluency (Adlof et al. 2006) as well as EF (Cutting et al. 2015). More specifically, using a structural equation model, it was suggested that working memory and cognitive flexibility contribute significantly to reading development and comprehension and hence these components should be considered as having a role in theoretical reading models, especially in children with RD (Spencer et al. 2020). This study, among others (Cutting et al. 2015; Haft et al. 2019; Nurul Anis et al. 2018), supports the benefit of training basic cognitive functions underlying RD, in addition to phonological processing.

Flexibility of EF and Reading Abilities

Several previous studies suggested training subcomponents of EF as part of reading‐related interventions for various reasons. The literature focusing on working memory suggested that this component is related to the phonological loop—on which reading comprehension relies—and hence training working memory will enhance reading proficiency (Loosli et al. 2012; Swanson 1999). These findings were confirmed in adults with RD (Horowitz‐Kraus and Breznitz 2009; Nevo and Breznitz 2014). Others who focused on visual attention suggested that this is a critical component of letter decoding during reading and, therefore, specific visual training will result in better decoding skills (Caldani et al. 2020; Ebrahimi et al. 2019; Peters et al. 2019). However, training reading by implementing specific EF components within the reading training is different. In such training, the user is still exposed to written materials but training is done by manipulating the presentation of the written material (Breznitz and Share 1992). One such manipulation is referred to as the ‘Reading acceleration phenomenon’, where written materials are presented while the letters are being deleted from the screen (or replaced with asterisks) at an increasing rate. When using this manipulation, the reader is instructed to silently read sentences shown on the computer screen with the letters being deleted from the screen from left to right (in English) based on the individual's reading rate. This manipulation is thought to reinforce faster processing of letters which lowers the bottleneck in working memory of slow, non‐automatic letter decoding, pushing towards word recognition rather than decoding and increasing the number of words read orthographically in the mental lexicon, thereby improving fluency (Korinth and Nagler 2021). This, in turn, encourages children to holistically recognise words (or parts of words, like lexemes or infixes) and, by that, expand their mental lexicon (Breznitz and Share 1992). The need to holistically recognise words and perform quick searches in the lexicon forces the reader to inhibit competing lexicon entries. While enhancing the deletion speed during training, the speed of processing is being trained. Furthermore, as the programme determines the pace, it inhibits the reader from regressing or fixating on specific locations within the word/sentence for too long.

We have repeatedly shown how training while using this EF‐based reading training improves reading abilities in Hebrew‐speaking (Horowitz‐Kraus and Breznitz 2014) and English‐speaking children with RD (Horowitz‐Kraus, DiFrancesco, et al. 2015; Horowitz‐Kraus and Holland 2015; Horowitz‐Kraus, Toro‐Serey, et al. 2015), as well as specifically improving EF (Horowitz‐Kraus 2015). This phenomenon was recently reviewed in several orthographies and the conclusion was that it is a viable method to improve reading comprehension in various orthographies and reader groups (Korinth and Nagler 2021). However, whether the beneficial effect of this training on reading remains long‐term and whether the changes in EF moderate it is unknown, information which would provide a mechanistic explanation for the training‐related reading improvement. Here, we aimed to explore the short‐ and long‐term benefits of the EF‐based reading intervention as well as the moderating effect of EF involved in this training, with a specific emphasis on the trained executive components (attention, working memory, inhibition, initiation and speed of processing). We hypothesized that children with RD and typical readers (TR, i.e., controls) training with the EF‐based reading intervention would show short‐term reading and EF improvements as well as a long‐term reading improvement. We expected the reading improvement to be moderated by improvements in inhibition, initiation, visual attention, working memory and speed of processing—the EF components that are trained in the programme.

Methods

Participants

A total of 41 children (22 males, 19 females), ages 8–12, participated in the study. In this sample, 18 participants had a reading disability (RD; six females, mean age = 9.89 ± 0.912) and 23 were typically developing children considered to be TR (13 females, mean age = 9.82 ± 0.889). Based on a previous study conducted using a similar study design (Horowitz‐Kraus and Holland 2015), employing 18 participants per group, a statistical power ranging between 91% was achieved (Cohen's d = 1.03, smaller effect size) and 98% (Cohen's d = 2.19, largest effect size).

Participants in the RD and TR groups did not significantly differ in age (t(36.22) = 0.222, p > 0.05). None of the children had any developmental or neurological condition. All participants demonstrated an average to above average nonverbal IQ (measured by the Test of Nonverbal Abilities, 4th edition (Brown et al. 2010) and did not display clinically elevated symptoms of inattention based on parent‐ and self‐report on the Conners Short Form (Conners 2006)).

Study Procedure

First, parents participated in a phone screening and were excluded in case of a history of neurodevelopmental or psychiatric disorders (other than learning difficulties) and contraindication for MRI, as well as screening of ADHD symptoms. Then, they were enrolled in the study for the baseline testing to determine eligibility for the RD or TR group. Children in the RD group were all diagnosed in the past with a reading disorder. Their RD was verified using several reading tests (listed below; see Table 1). To be qualified for inclusion in the RD group, children had to score below the 25th percentile in at least two reading measures from the list. Typical readers had to demonstrate intact reading abilities in all tests (one standard deviation within average and above). Then, they were enrolled in the computerised EF‐based reading intervention training. Children and parents signed written assents and consents, respectively. The study was approved by the Cincinnati Children's Hospital and Medical Center Institutional Review Board. Participants were compensated for their time and travel with gift cards of overall $75.

Training With the EF‐Based Reading Intervention

The intervention lasted 4 weeks, five sessions a week, 20–25 min each session. Training sessions were completed on the child's personal computer at their home and were monitored remotely by the research team based on information logged within the programme. The EF‐based intervention (previously reported in Cecil et al. 2021) consisted of two phases: the first phase is the assessment phase, where the child's initial reading pace is determined (msec/letter). In the second phase (i.e., training period), the child is exposed to sentences and is required to read them in a time‐imposed manner: the letters of the text are deleted from the screen starting from the first letter. A reading comprehension question follows each sentence. Upon five correctly answered reading comprehension questions, the deletion rate of the letters increases by 5%. Following training, children completed another behavioural test to evaluate putative improvements in their reading and EF using different versions of the tests, when available (T2). Three months after the intervention, a follow‐up session was conducted (T3), in which reading abilities were assessed.

Behavioural Measures

Reading Abilities

Word and non‐word reading accuracy, as well as fluency measures, were collected: (1) timed word and non‐word reading were evaluated using the Test of Word Reading Efficiency (TOWRE) Sight Words Efficiency (SWE) and Pseudoword Decoding Efficiency (PDE) subtests, respectively (Torgesen et al. 1999); (2) untimed word and non‐word reading were evaluated using the Woodcock‐Johnson Letter‐Word and Word Attack subtests, respectively (Schrank et al. 2014); (3) contextual reading fluency was assessed using the Test of Silent Reading Efficiency and Comprehension (TOSREC) (Wagner et al. 2010). All tests were collected during Test 1 (i.e., T1, pre‐intervention) and Test 2 (i.e., T2, post‐intervention). The TOWRE and TOSREC measures were also collected at Test 3 (i.e., T3, long post‐intervention).

Executive Functions

Several components of EF were assessed with performance‐based and questionnaire measures including the following: (1) Switching/inhibition was assessed using the Colour‐Word subtest of the Delis‐Kaplan Executive Functions System (DKEFS) (Delis et al. 2011); (2) Speed of processing was assessed in T1 and T2 using the Coding subtest of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children—4th edition or WISC‐V (Wechsler 2003); (3) Working memory was assessed with the Digit Span subtest of the WISC‐V (Wechsler 2003); Parent‐rated problems with (4) initiation (Initiate subscale T‐score) and (5) inhibition (Inhibit subscale T‐score) were also obtained using the Behaviour Rating Inventory of Executive Function, Parent Report (BRIEF‐PR) (Gioia 1996); and (6) Visual attention was measured using the Sky Search subtest of the TEA‐Ch (Manly et al. 2001). All tests were collected at T1 and T2, but not at T3. Age‐adjusted standardised scores were used. Higher values on the BRIEF questionnaire reflect greater executive dysfunction.

Data Analysis

Independent sample t‐tests were conducted to determine baseline (i.e., T1) differences in reading and EF between the RD and TR groups. Results

Baseline Measures (Test 1—Differences Between RD and TR Groups)

Group comparisons on baseline (T1) measures of reading and EF are summarised in Table 1. Typical readers significantly outperformed children with RD in both contextual reading speed and comprehension as well as in word/non‐word reading level.

Moreover, TR also showed significantly higher EF abilities than children with RD in two different EF tests: DKEFS switching/inhibition and WISC Digit Span working memory. Typical readers also demonstrated higher visual attention skills (TEA‐Ch Sky Search) than children with RD.

Short‐Term Training Effect (T1–T2)

Overall, children with RD showed poorer reading comprehension abilities compared to the TR, as indicated by a main effect of Group, [F(1, 39) = 69.6, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.022]. Across groups, there was also a main effect of test, such that participants displayed significantly higher scores after the EF‐based intervention on measures of word reading [TOWRE SWE: F(1, 39) = 20.156, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.025], non‐word reading [TOWRE PDE: F(1, 39) = 12.43, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.31], and reading fluency and comprehension [TOSREC index: F(1, 38) = 7.8, p < 0.05, ŋ 2 = 0.16]. No Group × Time interactions were found for any reading measures.

Regarding EF measures, children with RD performed more poorly on several measures, resulting in a significant main effect of Group for working memory (WISC Digit Span: F(1, 39) = 39.2, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.501), initiation (BRIEF‐PR Initiation: F(1, 39) = 5.3, p < 0.05, ŋ 2 = 0.0037) and speed of processing (WISC Coding: F(1, 39) = 13.2, p < 0.01, ŋ 2 = 0.253). Increased EF abilities were observed following training across groups, as indicated with a main effect of Test on measures of switching/inhibition (DKEFS Colour‐Word: F(1, 39) = 8.6, p < 0.01, ŋ 2 = 0.0413) and visual attention (TEA‐Ch Sky Search: F(1, 39) = 11.97, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.3043). Furthermore, a Group × Time interaction was revealed for parent‐rated problems with inhibition (BRIEF inhibition: F(1, 19) = 6.1, p < 0.05, ŋ 2 = 0.3166) and initiation (BRIEF initiation: F(1, 19) = 5.3, p < 0.05, ŋ 2 = 0.2767). Examination of post hoc tests revealed significantly improved inhibition in the TR group, but not the RD group, whereas the RD group showed an increase in difficulties in initiation with no change in parent‐rated initiation problems in the TR group.

Post hoc paired t‐test analyses between T1 and T2 for the reading and EF measures revealed that both groups improved their performance in word reading (TOWRE SWE word reading for TR = 3.049, p < 0.01, and RD t‐test = 3.812, p < 0.01). However, different gains were observed in additional reading domains and EF: TRs improved in their performance in non‐timed word reading (Woodcock‐Johnson Letter‐Word t = 2.408, p < 0.05), reading fluency and comprehension (TOSREC index t = 2.586, p < 0.05), switching/inhibition (DKEFS colour‐word t = 2.485, p < 0.05), inhibition (BRIEF‐PR inhibition t = 5.704, p < 0.05) and visual attention (TEA‐Ch Sky Search t = 3.137, p < 0.01). Children with RD improved in non‐word reading (TOWRE PDE t = 2.741, p < 0.05), and initiation (BRIEF parental report of initiation t = 3.25, p < 0.05). See Table 2 for these results.

Long‐Term Effect of Training (T1–T3)

Reading abilities: we found overall poorer reading abilities in children with RD as detected in a main effect of Group for word reading (TOWRE SWE Group F(1, 39) = 67.109, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.0231), non‐word reading (TOWRE PDE Group F(1, 39) = 92.531, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.0233) and comprehension and fluency abilities (TOSREC index Group F(1, 39) = 67.423, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.0225). Moreover, a main effect of Test was found for word reading (TOWRE SWE F(1, 39) = 9.875, p < 0.01, ŋ 2 = 0.2507), non‐word reading (TOWRE PDE F(1, 39) = 21.195, p < 0.001, ŋ 2 = 0.5429) and reading fluency and comprehension (TOSREC index F(1, 38) = 8.144, p < 0.01, ŋ 2 = 0.2142). The results suggest sustained improvement in reading abilities from T1 to T3 (i.e., baseline to long post). A significant Group × Test interaction (TOWRE PDE: F(1, 39) = 10.412, ŋ 2 = 0.2667, p < 0.01) was observed for non‐word reading, such that the RD group showed significant long‐term reading gains, whereas the TR group did not. Moderation Analyses

To determine if the change in EF following intervention moderates the short‐ and long‐term effect of the intervention programme on reading abilities, a moderation analysis was conducted in the entire cohort (across RD and TR groups). Results demonstrated that the training‐related improvements in inhibition (ΔR 2 = 0.845, ΔF(3, 16) = 29.18, p < 0.05, b = 0.055, t(13) = 2.24, p < 0.05), initiation (ΔR 2 = 0.849, ΔF(3, 16) = 30.00, p < 0.001, b = 0.105, t(13) = 2.46, p < 0.05) and speed of processing (ΔR 2 = 0.7805, ΔF(3, 16) = 42.66, p < 0.05, b = 0.976, t(13) = 0.062, p < 0.05) between T1 and T2 were found to be significant moderators for the long‐term reading fluency gains (T1–T3). Visual attention and working memory did not show significant moderation effects; see Figure 2.

Discussion

The goal of the current study was to determine the short‐ and long‐term effects of EF‐based reading training on reading and EF measures in children with RD versus TR. We also aimed to determine if the change in EF has a moderating role on the long‐term reading improvement in these readers, such that those children showing greater EF improvement also show greater long‐term gains in reading. The results of the study confirm our hypothesis that reading and EF abilities do improve following training in both children with RD and TR, not only short term (reading: in the word/non‐words level and also fluency/comprehension, EF: switching/inhibition and visual attention) but also long term (words and non‐word reading and reading fluency and comprehension). Also, and in line with our second hypothesis, greater fluency and comprehension abilities 3 months post‐training were moderated by a greater change in EF (performance‐based speed of processing and parent‐rated problems with inhibition and initiation) across RD and TR groups. The present results suggest that an EF‐based reading intervention is related to improved decoding skills and reading fluency comprehension immediately after intervention and 3 months after the intervention moderated by several key EF that are embedded within the training programme.

EF and Reading Improvements: The Moderating Role

The extended SVR model suggested that fluency and EF are essential components to achieving higher comprehension levels (Cutting et al. 2015; Spencer et al. 2020) and extending the original model (Hoover and Gough 1990). This suggestion was supported by the ‘double deficit theory’ coined by Martha Denckla in the 70's (Denckla and Rudel 1976), pointing at the importance of speed, switching and naming in reading. These cognitive components were related to reading abilities in children with RD, strengthening the claim that these are important components in the reading process (Norton et al. 2014; Vukovic and Siegel 2006). Our results support this reporting of reduced EF components in children with RD and add the importance of modifying these cognitive components in order to achieve long‐term reading improvement. Here we show that the manipulation of deleting the letters from the screen in a speeded manner demonstrated both short and long‐term outcomes, especially for those with RD, whose starting point was lower in all measures (as was observed in the baseline results). It was previously suggested that one possible explanation was that as this training involves EF components, it might help to sync neural circuits/networks supporting sensorimotor, sensory and language abilities participating in the reading process (Cecil et al. 2021). This synchronisation may lower the neuronal noise in the sensory system, that was previously suggested as a possible cause of reading problems (Hancock et al. 2017). A faster fluency reading comprehension is achievable by reducing the noise, per the extended SVR model.

The fact that improvements only in inhibition, initiation and speed of processing showed a moderating effect for long‐term gain is interesting, as the current programme also trains working memory and visual attention. Despite previous reports of enhanced divided attention, visual attention, speed of processing and switching/inhibition following training using this EF‐based reading training manipulation (Horowitz‐Kraus and Holland 2015), the results of the current study suggest that for reading comprehension and fluency, changes in these EFs specifically (inhibition, initiation and speed of processing) are important. The ability to process information quickly and efficiently (also referred to as ‘speed of processing’) has a long legacy as being an important part of reading proficiency and as the cognitive mechanism synching auditory and visual attention systems and by supporting reading fluency (Breznitz 2006). In a way, the double deficit hypothesis and the suggestion of the Rapid Automatized Naming (Denckla and Rudel 1976) as a test that differentiates children with RD from TR rely on the speed component and the ability to inhibit the responses, another important moderator that was pointed at in the current study. Inhibition has been previously associated with reading and reading comprehension (Booth et al. 2014; Borella et al. 2010). Lower inhibition is related to poor comprehension in children ages 10–11 years (Borella et al. 2010) and to word reading skills in those with RD (Booth et al. 2014). Another model linked inhibition, speed of processing and initiating eye movements (saccades) as important contributors to the reading process (Findlay and Walker 1999). This model suggested that the decision of when to initiate a saccade and when to inhibit it is determined by a resolution of several conflicts arising during the reading process. Hence, when there is an allocation of attention to specific visual targets, it affects the inhibition and initiation levels. Due to the specific manipulation of this intervention, it might be that the ability to inhibit saccadic regressions (i.e., gazing backward to the beginning of the sentence) or even forward saccadic movements to other parts of the text—which are findings previously reported in those with RD (Rayner et al. 2003)—is trained and transferred to other domains requiring inhibition even outside the programme. A future eye‐movement study should verify the points above.

Interestingly, in an exploration to examine if short‐term changes in EF are related to short‐term reading improvement, we found significant positive correlations between short‐term gain in word reading fluency (T2 vs. T1 TOWRE, SWE, scaled score) and short‐term EF visual attention gain (T2 vs. T1 measured by the Sky Search scaled score, TEA‐Ch battery) were observed r = 0.323, p < 0.05 and working memory gain r = 0.409, p < 0.01 (T2 vs. T1 measured using the Digit Span WISC, scaled score). Moreover, significant positive correlations were found between short‐term gain in contextual reading fluency (T2 vs. T1 TOSREC, index score) and short‐term EF speed of processing (Coding) gain r = 0.286, p < 0.05. However, the moderation analyses examining whether TOWRE and TOSREC at T1 predicted T2 moderated by the gains in these EF measures were not significant. We assume that despite the significant correlations between short‐term improvements in word and contextual reading fluency and short‐term gains in subcomponents of EF, the moderation relations between reading fluency and EF gains may be dependent on the baseline EF measures. We believe these differences are attributed to lower variability in EF measures observed in children with dyslexia and assume that a significant moderation short term would be observed in a trained group of children with a specific EF deficit, such as children with ADHD and reading difficulties. A follow‐up study should examine this point in depth.

Taken together, the results point at the importance of stimulating EF as part of a reading intervention or even as an add‐on component as suggested previously (Chuntonov and Breznitz 2015; Nevo and Breznitz 2014). Warming up these EF skills during or/and before the actual training has been shown to have a positive effect on pre‐readers (Nevo and Breznitz 2014), in reading children and in adults (Chuntonov and Breznitz 2015), strengthening the importance of EF in reading remediation. The authors suggested that ‘preparing’ the brain to read by using short games targeting sub‐EF results in better reading achievements than training only on reading (Chuntonov and Breznitz 2015).

EF As Part of the Reading Network

Numerous studies in the past decade consistently demonstrate that different EF have an important role in proficient reading and reading remediation: inhibition (Booth et al. 2014; Borella et al. 2010), visual attention (Bosse and Valdois 2009), working memory (Gathercole et al. 2006), speed of processing (Breznitz 2006), initiation (Yang and McConkie 2001) and switching (Colé et al. 2014). However, classical reading models such as the parallel distributed processing model (Seidenberg and McClelland 1989), the dual‐route model (Coltheart 2006) as well as the neurobiological models for reading (Dehaene 2009; Shaywitz and Shaywitz 2003) have yet to include this component and mainly focus on the linguistic/auditory component, visual processing, semantics and articulation. One of the reasons for this complexity is the several sub‐abilities included under EF and the challenge of separating them into different brain regions or networks (for an attempt to separate the networks and match them to the different EF, see Levinson et al. 2018). However, the importance and gain of including these components and neural circuits to update the ‘reading network’ is of high relevance and importance as it may enable pointing at a secondary or primary cause for RD which will guide interventions, or even be evaluated early in life before reading is officially acquired (see Anderson 2002; Anderson and Reidy 2012 for the development of the subcomponents of EF).

Reading is a complex and phylogenetically recent cognitive ability and thus is based on and relies on different sub‐abilities. For this reason, individuals with RD suffer not only from reading impairments but usually also report EF and speed of processing difficulties (Horowitz‐Kraus and Holland 2015). This is the main concrete rationale for the use of EF as a mechanism for reading improvement. Here, we demonstrated how an EF‐based reading programme enhanced reading abilities in both children with and without RD. This intervention is designed to address speed of processing, inhibition, working memory and cognitive flexibility, all of these abilities underlying fluent reading. This study shows for the first time that the reading improvement following a reading intervention based on reading with an imposed time constraint is directly related to the effect of the intervention on speed of processing, inhibition and initiation abilities. Furthermore, our results suggest the benefit of exploring different options to treat RD by addressing EF and not directly reading. Future studies should consider including this type of manipulation in programmes aiming to enhance reading in individuals with RD.

Study's Limitations

The findings of this study should be taken into account with its limitations. First, EF was not evaluated at T3, which limited our ability to examine the relations between the remaining change in EF at T2–T3 and reading proficiency. Secondly, a more extensive EF battery that also includes a Rapid Automatized Naming test could have related our findings specifically to the double deficit hypothesis, as suggested earlier in the discussion. Third, the lack of a control group (either passive control or active group undergoing an intervention, the goal of which would not be an improvement in reading) prevents from experimentally confirming the positive outcome of the intervention. However, this research question was addressed before; testing the effectiveness of the EF‐based intervention was not the main goal of the present study.

Conclusions

The results of the current study support both behavioural and neurobiological studies that repeatedly point to the important role of EF in reading and reading remediation. Additional studies should expand the findings and explore the possibility that an EF warm‐up before the EF‐based reading intervention may increase its effectiveness in improving reading abilities in children with dyslexia in Hebrew (Chuntonov and Breznitz 2015) and English, and to define the level of EF moderation in such a training in comparison to the design of the current study. Expanding the reading network to also include EF components may also direct researchers' and clinicians' attention to include the subdomains of EF in reading evaluations, and even before reading is acquired, in children at risk for RD.
