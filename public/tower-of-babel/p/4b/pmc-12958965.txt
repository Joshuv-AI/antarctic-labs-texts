Eosinophilic esophagitis in Colombian children: relationship between food allergens, symptoms and endoscopic findings
Authors: Michelle Higuera Carrillo, Ailim Margarita Carias Domínguez, Natali Gonzalez Rozo, José Fernando Vera Chamorro, Juan Pablo Riveros López, Catalina Ortiz-Piedrahita, Carlos Augusto Cuadros Mendoza, Stephania Peña Hernández, Natalia Velez-Tirado, César Augusto Moreno Serrano, Otto Gerardo Calderón-Guerrero, Fernando Alonso Medina Monroy, Adriana Prada Rey, Juanita Higuera, Monica Maria Contreras Ramirez, Carlos Marcelo Timossi, Pablo Vasquez-Hoyos, Jhon Camacho-Cruz, Wilson Daza-Carreño
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is an immune-mediated disease characterised by oesophageal eosinophilic infiltration, leading to chronic inflammation and tissue remodelling.ObjectivesTo describe the clinical, epidemiological and endoscopic characteristics of children with EoE in Colombia, and explore associations between symptoms, allergen sensitisation and endoscopic findings.MethodsObservational, multicentre retrospective study with an analytical component, conducted on paediatric patients diagnosed with EoE across 10 tertiary referral centres in Colombia between 2015 and 2023. All cases had histological confirmation with ≥15 eosinophils per high-power field. Data were extracted from standardised medical records by trained investigators.ResultsA total of 143 cases were included, with a male predominance (66.4%) and median age at diagnosis of 92 months (IQR 56–144). Relevant early exposures included prematurity (11.2%), low birth weight (10.5%), caesarean delivery (50.3%), infant formula use (62.2%) and antibiotics in infancy (27.9%). Family history of atopy was present in 54.6%, and EoE in 4.9%. Food specific IgE was present, particularly to cow’s milk (56.6%) and egg (38.1%). Most frequent symptoms included abdominal pain (67.1%), nausea (60.1%) and vomiting (46.2%). Endoscopic findings included longitudinal furrows (70.9%) and white exudates (67.4%). Statistically significant associations were found between dysphagia and oesophageal rings (p=0.003), vomiting and white exudates (p=0.018), abdominal pain and longitudinal furrows (p=0.025) and regurgitation and oedema (p=0.031).ConclusionsPaediatric EoE in Colombia shows symptom and endoscopic heterogeneity. Identifying potential allergen triggers may contribute to understanding symptoms and endoscopic patterns in EoE.Keywords: Gastroenterology

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

As a descriptive study, our findings do not support changes in clinical management but rather offer preliminary observations that may inform future research. The patterns identified between presenting symptoms and endoscopic abnormalities highlight potential clinical correlations that warrant confirmation in prospective studies with adequate diagnostic performance analyses. Similarly, the high frequency of food-specific IgE sensitisation observed in this cohort underscores the need for further investigation into the role of allergy testing in paediatric EoE, particularly regarding its predictive value for treatment response beyond empiric elimination diets. Future studies should evaluate whether these symptom–endoscopy relationships and sensitisation profiles can ultimately contribute to more refined diagnostic algorithms or personalised therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Primary eosinophilic gastrointestinal diseases are disorders characterised by eosinophilic inflammation in the absence of known causes of eosinophilia, affecting various segments of the gastrointestinal tract. The global rise in eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) has significantly impacted both patients and healthcare systems.1 The diagnosis of EoE is based on the presence of symptoms related to oesophageal dysfunction, a biopsy demonstrating more than 15 eosinophils per high-power field (eos/hpf) and the exclusion of secondary causes of oesophageal eosinophilia.2 In North America, the prevalence of EoE in adults is estimated at 28.9 per 100 000 individuals, compared with 26.9 per 100 000 in the paediatric population. Incidence rates vary geographically, with an annual incidence of 5.4 per 100 000 in North America and 1.7 per 100 000 in Europe, as reported by Arias et al.3 Pathophysiological factors associated with EoE include genetic predisposition, gut dysbiosis, food allergies and environmental triggers. Although sensitisation to food allergens is frequent in patients with EoE, its clinical relevance in symptom generation remains uncertain. Despite increasing recognition of the disease, its epidemiological profile and, particularly, the relationship between food allergen sensitisation and clinical manifestations remain largely unknown in Colombian children. Therefore, this study aimed to describe the clinical, endoscopic and allergic sensitisation characteristics of paediatric patients diagnosed with EoE in Colombia and to explore the relationship between specific food allergen sensitisation, symptoms and endoscopic findings.

Methods

Study design and participant selection

This was a multicentre, retrospective, observational study with an exploratory analytical component. It included paediatric patients (<18 years) diagnosed with EoE at their initial evaluation across 10 paediatric gastroenterology referral centres in Colombia. The study was carried out across multiple specialised paediatric gastroenterology referral centres in Colombia, covering different regions of the country to ensure a diverse and representative sample of cases. Patients younger than 18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of EoE between August 2015 and May 2023 were included. Eligibility criteria required (1) symptoms related to oesophageal dysfunction, (2) histological confirmation of ≥15 eos/hpf in at least one oesophageal biopsy site and (3) the exclusion of other systemic or local causes of oesophageal eosinophilia. Patients with alternative causes of EoE, such as eosinophilic gastroenteritis with oesophageal involvement, Crohn’s disease, vasculitis, oesophageal infections, inflammatory bowel disease or immunodeficiencies, were excluded. Participants were identified retrospectively through consecutive case-finding at each centre during the study period, using a convenience sampling approach that included all patients diagnosed with EoE within that timeframe. Data collection was conducted by investigators and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), an electronic data capture software licensed by the Latin American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition. Allergy sensitisation tests followed standardised protocols for serum specific IgE (sIgE), skin prick tests and patch tests. Positivity was defined according to international recommendations and manufacturer’s cut-offs for each test. Endoscopic findings were documented by experienced paediatric gastroenterologists and confirmed through histopathological reports. This study was designed as a retrospective cross-sectional analysis based on medical records, rather than as a cohort recruited at the time of diagnosis. Information regarding whether patients were in remission or presented with active eosinophilic inflammation at the time of testing was not consistently available. This constraint represents an inherent limitation of the study and is now explicitly acknowledged in the manuscript.

Variables

Multiple variables were included in the registry, covering demographic information, perinatal history, early environmental exposures and comorbidities. Perinatal variables included birth weight, mode of delivery, duration of breastfeeding, use of infant formulas and prematurity; these were obtained from parental report or medical records, with prematurity defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation. Early environmental exposures, such as tobacco exposure during pregnancy or early childhood and household pet contact, were recorded according to caregiver report and referred to any time prior to the diagnosis of EoE. Family history of EoE and neonatal hospitalisation were also included. Clinical characteristics prior to diagnosis were assessed, along with food allergen sensitisation evaluated through specific serum IgE tests, skin prick tests and patch tests. All participating centres used the same reference laboratory, ensuring uniform analytical methods and reporting standards. For this study, only sensitisation tests performed within a 3-month window before or after the date of diagnosis were included. Sensitisation to a specific food allergen was defined as a serum sIgE concentration ≥0.35 kUA/L (Class 1 or higher on the Radioallergosorbent Test -RAST- scale), while values <0.35 kUA/L (Class 0) were considered negative. Skin prick testing was performed using standardised allergen extracts with potency expressed in biological equivalent potency, applied with a 1 mm single-use lancet. Readings were obtained exactly 15 min after application, and the weal diameter was recorded in millimetres; a reaction was classified as positive when the weal measured ≥3 mm compared with the negative control. Patch testing was conducted using occluded Finn Chambers loaded with fresh food allergens and applied for 48 hours. Readings were performed at both 48 and 72 hours, and reactions were classified as positive when erythema and palpable infiltration were present, or negative when no significant cutaneous changes were observed. Patch test interpretation followed internationally accepted criteria for delayed hypersensitivity reaction. Allergens were categorised into cow’s milk proteins (cow’s milk protein, casein, lactalbumin, lactoglobulin), egg proteins (egg yolk, egg white, ovalbumin, ovomucoid), cereals and grains (wheat, corn, oats), legumes and nuts (soy, peanut, almond, walnut) and marine proteins (tuna, shrimp, salmon). Legumes (soy, peanut) and tree nuts (almond, walnut) were analysed as a single category because these foods share structurally related storage proteins that may result in overlapping sensitisation patterns, and because the number of patients sensitised to individual tree nuts was too small to allow reliable independent analysis.

Dependent variables included clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, food impaction, heartburn, regurgitation and chest pain, along with symptom duration. Endoscopic findings, including oedema, longitudinal furrows, fixed rings, strictures and crepe-paper mucosa appearance, were evaluated using the Eosinophilic Esophagitis Endoscopic Reference Score (EREFS), which assesses oedema, rings, exudates, furrows and strictures. Histological findings included eosinophil count per high-power field in any oesophageal segment, along with gastric and duodenal biopsies if available. For the definition of EoE, we used the threshold of >15 eos/hpf in any oesophageal segment. To ensure consistency with widely accepted criteria, we excluded patients who had eosinophilia exclusively in the distal oesophagus without involvement of the mid or proximal segments. Thus, all included cases had at least one biopsy from the mid or proximal oesophagus showing ≥15 eos/hpf, and patients with isolated distal eosinophilia were not considered. This approach aligns with current recommendations and is explicitly reported to clarify the inclusion criteria.

Sample size and statistical analysis

The study included a small analytical component aimed at exploring whether sensitisation to specific food allergens was related to variations in clinical symptoms or endoscopic findings. Based on this exploratory objective, the sample size was estimated using a conservative hypothetical scenario, given the absence of reliable local prevalence data. We assumed a symptom prevalence of 50% (the value that maximises variance) and a sensitisation prevalence of approximately 30%, consistent with published paediatric EoE cohorts. Under these assumptions, detecting a 50% relative difference (RD) between sensitised and non-sensitised patients with 80% power and a two-sided α of 0.05 required roughly 120 patients. Because EoE is uncommon, all eligible cases during the study period were included. Descriptive analyses included frequency and proportion calculations for categorical variables, and medians with IQRs for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Associations between allergen sensitisation and dependent variables were explored using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. To ensure valid statistical testing, symptoms with extremely low frequency (fewer than 5–6 cases) were not included in the analytical comparisons because such sparse cell counts make χ² or Fisher’s exact tests unstable and prone to spurious significance. Importantly, all patients remained included in the full cohort description and in the summary of overall symptom frequencies. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata V.18.5, with a p value <0.05 considered statistically significant. Because the analytical comparisons were exploratory and hypothesis-generating, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Given the rarity of EoE and the heterogeneity of sensitisation and symptom profiles, such adjustments could increase the risk of false-negative findings. Instead, effect sizes and the consistency of patterns across analyses were considered alongside p values, and statistically significant results were interpreted with appropriate caution.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was not incorporated into the design, conduct, analysis, reporting or dissemination of this study. This was a multicentre, retrospective observational study based exclusively on anonymised clinical records of paediatric patients with EoE. The research question and outcome measures were defined by the investigators based on clinical relevance and existing knowledge gaps, without direct input from patients or caregivers. No prospective recruitment, intervention or patient contact occurred; therefore, the burden of the intervention was not assessed by patients. Study results will not be disseminated directly to participants due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data. As no patients or patient advisers were involved, none were included in the contributorship statement or acknowledgements.The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee under approval code SDM-042–22, which served as the principal ethics board (Full details are reported in the Ethics approval section).

Results

Demographics and diagnosis

A total of 143 cases from 10 centres in Bogotá, Medellín, Bucaramanga, Cúcuta and Cali were included, with an average of 14 cases per centre (range: 1–44). Male patients predominated (66.4%), and the majority were of Caucasian race (69.2%). Age at diagnosis varied widely, with a median of 92 months (IQR 56–144), with school-age children being the most frequently affected group (table 1). Regarding perinatal factors, 11.2% of the patients were premature, 10.5% had low birth weight and the most common mode of delivery was caesarean section (50.3%). Neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) hospitalisation was recorded in 16.8% of cases. Among early exposure factors, the median age for introducing complementary feeding was 6 months (IQR 5–6), although 5% started before 5 months. Additionally, 62.2% received infant formulas in their 1st year of life, and 27.9% had early exposure to antibiotics. Environmental factors revealed that 25.9% had frequent contact with animals, 11.9% shared their bedroom with others and 2.1% were exposed to tobacco during childhood or gestation. Regarding family history, 54.6% had a history of atopy and 4.9% reported a relative with EoE. As for comorbidities, oesophageal atresia was the only reported condition, occurring in 2.1%. Data on some perinatal and early exposure variables were missing for a small proportion of patients, as detailed in table 1.

Allergen testing

Allergic reactions mediated by IgE against food allergens were evaluated in 115 patients (80.4%) (table 2). Cow’s milk showed the highest frequency of serum test positivity (56.6%, 64/113), followed by casein (37.2%, 42/113) and egg white (38.1%, 43/113). In skin prick tests, the most frequently positive allergens were wheat (23.8%, 10/42) and egg white (23.8%, 10/42), whereas in patch tests, the highest reactivity was observed for egg white (18.2%, 4/22) and ovomucoid (18.2%, 4/22). Blood eosinophilia was measured in 54 patients, with a median of 374 /mm³ (IQR 80–800). Most patients (74.5%, 85/115) excluded at least one food following allergen testing results, while 25.5% did not implement any exclusions. Among those who modified their diet, 17.7% excluded one food, 22.7% excluded two and 19.2% excluded four or more. The number of patients undergoing each type of allergen testing varied due to the convenience-based sampling and clinical decisions at each participating centre. As a result, denominators differ across allergens and test modalities, and percentages should be interpreted accordingly (table 2). Symptoms and initial endoscopic findings

Most patients experienced symptoms for over 6 months before diagnosis (59.4%), while 18.9% had symptoms lasting between 3 and 6 months, 16.1% between 1 and 3 months and 4.2% for less than a month. The most common symptoms included abdominal pain (67.1%), nausea (60.1%), vomiting (46.2%), regurgitation (42.7%) and heartburn (37.1%). Other reported symptoms included feeding difficulties (32.2%), dysphagia (32.2%), pharyngeal irritation (23.1%), retrosternal pain (16.1%), cough with feeding (16.1%), weight loss (25.2%), oesophageal impaction (2.8%) and haematemesis (2.8%). Initial endoscopies revealed findings consistent with EoE in most cases. The most frequent findings included longitudinal furrows (70.9%), white exudates (67.4%), oedema (50.4%) and fixed rings (16.3%). Strictures and crepe-paper mucosa were less common (4.3% and 14.9%, respectively). Histological analysis of oesophageal biopsies showed that 71.4% of patients had between 15 and 50 eos/hpf in the distal third of the oesophagus, 21.1% had between 51 and 100 eos/hpf and 7.5% had more than 100 eos/hpf. In the mid/proximal third, 76.5% of patients had between 15 and 50 eos/hpf, 18.0% had between 51 and 100 eos/hpf and 5.5% had more than 100 eos/hpf. Gastric and duodenal eosinophilia were identified in 3.6% and 13.5% of cases, respectively. Endoscopic dilation was performed in four patients (2.8%).

Association between allergens and symptoms

An exploratory analysis of the relationship between allergen sensitisation and gastrointestinal symptoms, grouping categories with a low number of cases, identified significant associations (figure 1). While table 3 summarises the main symptoms by allergen group, the full distribution of all symptoms is presented in online supplemental table 1. Regurgitation was more frequent in patients sensitised to cow’s milk proteins (59%) compared with non-sensitised individuals (24%; p=0.0001). Retrosternal pain was less frequent in patients sensitised to egg proteins (4%) compared with non-sensitised individuals (23%; p=0.007). Regarding nausea, non-sensitised patients with cereals and grains had a higher frequency (72%) than sensitised individuals (37%; p=0.0002).

Other symptoms showed relevant clinical RDs between groups but were not statistically significant. Heartburn was more frequent in patients sensitised to cow’s milk proteins (44% vs 26%, RD 41%, p=0.054) and egg proteins (44% vs 31%, RD 30%, p=0.174). Feeding difficulties had an RD of 36% in patients sensitised to egg proteins (42% vs 27%, p=0.104). However, the statistical power to detect these differences ranged from 0.35 to 0.58, indicating that the sample size was insufficient to confirm these associations.

Association between allergens and endoscopic findings

Significant associations between allergen sensitisation and endoscopic findings were also identified (table 4, figure 2). Exudates were more frequent in patients sensitised to cow’s milk proteins (89% vs 49%) and cereals and grains (82% vs 68%). Longitudinal furrows were more common in patients sensitised to cow’s milk proteins (91% vs 56%) and cereals and grains (90% vs 69%). Likewise, fixed rings were more prevalent in patients sensitised to legumes or nuts (30% vs 12%) and cereals and grains (28% vs 11%), while crepe-paper mucosa was more frequent in cereal and grain sensitisation (13% vs 1%).

Instituted treatment

A total of 95.0% of patients (134/141) received proton pump inhibitors (PPI) as the first-line treatment. The most common dosing regimen was two times per day in 86.9% of cases (113/130), with a median dose of 1.68 mg/kg/day (IQR 1.0–2.0). The duration of PPI treatment was 8 weeks in 58.2% of patients (82/141) and 12 weeks in 34.8% (49/141). Esomeprazole was the most frequently used PPI in 89.4% of cases (126/141), followed by omeprazole in 0.7% (1/141), lansoprazole in 2.1% (3/141) and pantoprazole in 1.4% (2/141). Topically swallowed corticosteroids were documented in 16.3% of patients (23/141), with viscous oral budesonide being the most used in 69.6% of cases (16/23), while swallowed liquid fluticasone was used in 21.7% (5/23). The doses of topical corticosteroids ranged from 0.5 mg to 2 mg/day, with a predominant treatment duration of 8–12 weeks in 85.7% (18/21). Systemic corticosteroids were used in 2.8% of patients (4/141), including prednisolone in 75.0% of cases (3/4) and methylprednisolone in 25.0% (1/4), with a predominant duration of 1–4 weeks. Dupilumab was used in only one case (0.7%, 1/141), and there was no documented use of montelukast or antihistamines in this group. Regarding the treatment of concomitant atopy, therapies were reported in 31.8% (14/44) of patients with atopic conditions. Antihistamines were the most frequently used in this subgroup, followed by salbutamol and inhaled beclomethasone, both used in 13.6% of cases (6/44 each). Dietary management was implemented in 41.8% of patients (59/141). Among them, 40.7% (24/59) required the use of elemental formulas, while targeted food elimination was applied in 94.9% (56/59). Among those who received targeted elimination, 37.5% (21/56) excluded two allergens, 23.2% (13/56) excluded four allergens and 14.3% (8/56) excluded up to eight allergens.

Discussion

This multicentre study describes the clinical, endoscopic and allergen sensitisation characteristics of paediatric patients with EoE in Colombia. Our results show a predominance of male patients and a wide age range at diagnosis, most frequently during school age. Abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting were the most common symptoms, while longitudinal furrows and white exudates were the most frequent endoscopic findings. Sensitisation to food allergens, particularly cow’s milk proteins and egg proteins, was frequent. Although some associations with symptoms and endoscopic patterns were observed, many did not reach statistical significance. These findings describe patterns of allergen sensitisation in relation to the clinical features of EoE; however, determining their diagnostic or therapeutic relevance will require studies with different designs, particularly prospective cohorts.

A study conducted in Latin America by Pierre R et al described the epidemiological characteristics and therapeutic strategies used in children with EoE across 10 countries. Their findings were comparable to those reported globally. Our study builds on that work by focusing specifically on Colombian children and adding detailed data on allergen sensitisation. The predominance of male patients observed is consistent with studies such as that by Lucendo et al, who reported a male-to-female ratio of 3–4:1.2 Likewise, the age at diagnosis, most frequently during school age, aligns with previous paediatric series. Data on perinatal and early-life exposures were collected, including prematurity, low birth weight, caesarean delivery, early introduction of complementary feeding, use of infant formulas and early antibiotic exposure. Given the lack of population-level comparators, these results are presented descriptively and should be interpreted as observational data. Because the study collected perinatal and early-life exposures in patients of different ages, these variables should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive early-life risk factors in older children. Their relevance remains hypothesis-generating and should be confirmed in prospective cohorts. Atopy was frequently reported among patients, whereas fewer than 10% had a family history of EoE. This observation is consistent with existing literature describing EoE as frequently occurring in the context of atopic conditions such as asthma, rhinitis and eczema4; for example, one study noted that 30–50% of paediatric patients with EoE had associated asthma, compared with 10% in healthy controls.5 Beyond atopy, although these conditions were not observed in our sample, previous reports have also linked EoE to connective tissue disorders, including Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, joint hypermobility syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome, as well as to Crohn’s disease and coeliac disease, with the latter showing an estimated EoE prevalence of 1–4%.6 7

The available evidence shows that several perinatal and postnatal factors are consistently associated with an increased risk of EoE, and the findings from our cohort support these observations. In our population, 27.9% had early exposure to antibiotics, which aligns with prior reports indicating that antibiotic use in infancy represents one of the strongest associations described in four out of five paediatric studies.8 Similarly, the literature has documented that both maternal and infant exposure to antibiotics and acid suppressants is associated with substantial increases in risk, particularly when these exposures occur during the third trimester of pregnancy or within the first 6 months of life, with increases of up to 15-fold reported among infants exposed to acid suppressants during early infancy.9 In our cohort, prematurity (11.2%), low birth weight (10.5%) and NICU admission (16.8%) were frequent and are consistent with studies demonstrating significant increases in EoE risk associated with these conditions.10 In our study, perinatal antecedents including prematurity (11.2%), low birth weight (10.5%), caesarean delivery (50.3%) and neonatal intensive care admission (16.8%) were characterised as early-life exposures previously implicated in EoE pathogenesis. In the Danish population-based study, early life antibiotic exposure was frequent, with nearly half of EoE cases receiving antibiotics in the 1st year of life and a dose response increase in risk with cumulative courses. Infant exposure to acid suppressants was also substantially more common among cases than controls, with frequencies far exceeding those observed in non-EoE populations.8 In our cohort, 27.9% received antibiotics during the 1st year, reflecting a comparable exposure pattern that supports the relevance of early microbial perturbation. Additional early life variables in our cohort, including the introduction of complementary feeding before 5 months (5%) and formula feeding during the 1st year of life (62.2%), are consistent with observations from other cohorts in which early atopic phenotypes are reported more frequently among children who subsequently develop EoE. Witmer et al documented higher rates of prematurity, cow’s milk protein allergy, eczema, feeding difficulties, reflux and neonatal toxic erythema in children with EoE compared with healthy controls.9 Capucilli et al further quantified the magnitude of atopic comorbidity associated with EoE, demonstrating substantially higher prevalences of allergic rhinoconjunctivitis (60.0% vs 17.4%), asthma (59.8% vs 21.4%), atopic dermatitis (17.8% vs 6.6%), adrenal insufficiency (2.6% vs 0.4%), autism spectrum disorder (7.5% vs 1.9%), coeliac disease (5.6% vs 0.9%), connective tissue disorders (1.4% vs 0.1%), cystic fibrosis (0.9% vs 0.05%), inflammatory bowel disease (0.7% vs 0.2%) and type 1 diabetes (1.2% vs 0.3%). Although oesophageal atresia was the only comorbidity identified in our cohort (2.1%), we observed a high prevalence of family history of atopy (54.6%), consistent with the atopic burden reported in these studies. Collectively, our findings fall within the range of global estimates and reinforce the contribution of perinatal, environmental and atopic factors to the clinical expression of EoE.10 In addition, the presence of atopic antecedents was relevant in our cohort, with 54.6% reporting a family history of atopy and 4.9% having relatives with EoE. These findings align with evidence showing that conditions such as atopic dermatitis, IgE mediated food allergy and asthma independently increase the risk of developing EoE (HR 3.2, 9.1 and 1.9, respectively),11 although atopy alone has not been demonstrated to act as a direct causal factor.11 Moreover, 50.3% were born by caesarean section, complementary feeding was initiated at 6 months (IQR 5–6) with 5% starting before 5 months and 62.2% received infant formula during the 1st year of life, factors that have been linked to early disruptions in microbial colonisation.12 Furthermore, males predominated in our series (66.4%), and the median age at diagnosis was 92 months (IQR 56–144), which is consistent with what has been reported in paediatric populations. When integrating these results with early-life exposures that may influence immune maturation such as antibiotic use, mode of delivery and early feeding practices, the hypothesis is strengthened that these environmental influences, described as key modulators of the intestinal microbiome during the 1st years of life, may play a central role in the pathophysiology of EoE.12

The clinical presentation was dominated by upper gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that, in paediatric patients, EoE often presents with symptoms that overlap with other gastrointestinal disorders, complicating early diagnosis.13 14 Feeding difficulties and dysphagia were also observed, aligning with reports that such symptoms become more prominent with age and disease progression. The age of EoE diagnosis in the paediatric population has been reported to range between 5.9 and 12 years, with diagnostic delays from 1.2 to 3.5 years in children.15 The sample, composed mainly of school-aged children, is consistent with these reports and may reflect improved awareness and diagnostic practices in recent years. The diagnosis of EoE is based on clinical symptoms of oesophageal dysfunction, endoscopic features and histological evidence in oesophageal biopsies.16 However, eosinophilic infiltration is not pathognomonic of EoE and can also occur in other conditions such as gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, coeliac disease, Crohn’s disease, infections, hypereosinophilic syndrome, achalasia, collagen diseases, pemphigus and graft-versus-host disease. These differential diagnoses must be considered in atypical or refractory cases.15 16 Because endoscopic features alone lack specificity, the EREFS score was developed to standardise the assessment of key findings and to classify disease phenotype as inflammatory, fibrotic or mixed.16 In this study, the predominance of inflammatory rather than fibrotic features was consistent with relatively early disease stages at diagnosis. The high frequency of food allergen sensitisation also mirrored patterns described in previous paediatric series; however, its inconsistent association with clinical or endoscopic features highlights the complexity of translating sensitisation into treatment decisions. Sensitisation to food allergens—particularly cow’s milk (n=64/113; 56.6%) and egg white (n=43/113; 38.1%) in serum tests—was frequent in this study. When compared with the Food Allergy Research and Education (FARE) registry, which included 309 participants with EoE, sensitisation frequencies in that cohort were similarly high for major allergens: milk 189/309 (61%), egg 189/309 (61%), peanut 207/309 (67%) and tree nuts 206/309 (66%).17 For other allergens, FARE reported sensitisation to soy in 115/309 (37%), wheat in 102/309 (33%), shellfish in 105/309 (34%) and finned fish in 70/309 (23%). Overall, our rates for milk and egg sensitisation fall within the lower-to-middle range of those observed in the FARE EoE subgroup, while our sensitisation frequencies for soy (n=20/113; 17.7%) and wheat (n=31/113; 27.4%) appear comparatively lower. Differences may relate to age distribution, referral patterns and the heterogeneous use of serum, skin prick and patch testing in our sample. Importantly, consistent with European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN)/North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) guidelines, sensitisation alone does not reliably predict symptom profile or histological response to dietary therapy.18 These findings underscore that positive tests must be interpreted cautiously and support individualised elimination strategies guided by clinical phenotype and therapeutic response rather than sensitisation patterns alone. The primary goal in EoE management is to achieve and maintain histological and symptomatic remission while preventing fibrotic complications. Standard therapies include dietary elimination and pharmacological approaches such as PPIs and swallowed corticosteroids.19 Elimination diets have been widely studied and remain a cornerstone of paediatric EoE treatment. A meta-analysis by Arias et al reported histological remission in over 70% of patients following a six-food elimination, with progressively lower remission rates in four and two food protocols.19,21 Nearly 42% of patients underwent dietary management; among these, most followed targeted elimination strategies rather than empiric protocols. While some required elemental formulas, a stepwise exclusion approach was more common, consistent with current efforts to balance efficacy and dietary burden. From a pharmacological perspective, PPIs are now considered first-line therapy in EoE. Clinical trials have demonstrated histological remission rates of 50–57% in both children and adults.222,24 Our findings reflect high PPI use, mostly at appropriate paediatric dosing and align with international practice patterns. The predominance of esomeprazole use in our sample may reflect prescribing habits or availability in the participating centres. Swallowed topical corticosteroids remain effective, particularly for patients unresponsive to PPI or diet. Only 16% of patients received topical corticosteroids, which likely reflects a stepwise therapeutic approach, as well as variations in clinical practice or concerns about adherence and adverse effects. A study by Hsu et al reported adrenal suppression in approximately 5% of paediatric patients on these therapies, highlighting the importance of monitoring.25 Clinical trials have reported greater histological response with viscous budesonide compared with nebulised formulations26; in our cohort, viscous budesonide was the most frequently used topical corticosteroid, but the present study did not assess comparative treatment efficacy. Systemic corticosteroids and biologics, such as dupilumab, were rarely used—likely due to disease severity, cost or limited availability. Recent data show significant efficacy of dupilumab in children 1–11 years old,27 but access remains limited in many settings. Atopic comorbidities were managed in roughly one-third of cases, most frequently with antihistamines and inhaled therapies. Although not specific to EoE treatment, these interventions may contribute to symptom control in patients with overlapping allergic conditions.

This is the largest paediatric EoE cohort reported in Colombia to date, providing important baseline data for the region. This study has several strengths, including its multicentre design across major referral hospitals in Colombia and the systematic data collection using standardised forms. However, there are also limitations. The retrospective nature of the study may have introduced variability in clinical practice and incomplete data. Not all patients underwent the same allergy tests, which limits the ability to compare test performance. Information on urban versus rural residence was not collected, representing an additional limitation in the characterisation of the study population. Although treatment modalities were described, assessing treatment effectiveness was beyond the scope of this study. Finally, the sample size, although the largest in Colombia to date, may have limited statistical power to detect certain associations.

Conclusion

This multicentre study provides a detailed characterisation of paediatric EoE in Colombia, describing clinical presentation, endoscopic features and patterns of food allergen sensitisation. Associations were observed between sensitisation and certain symptoms and endoscopic findings, while other clinically relevant trends did not reach statistical significance, likely due to sample size limitations. Further prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether specific sensitisation profiles may be more informative in certain regional or clinical contexts.
