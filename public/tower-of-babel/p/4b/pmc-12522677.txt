Dexmedetomidine-dezocine versus propofol-remifentanil anesthesia for ultrasound-guided transvaginal oocyte retrieval: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Min Liu, Yong Ouyang, Shi-An Hu, Jie-Dong Zhou, Yu-Ting Xiong, Jin-Long Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesTransvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval, a core component of in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET) techniques, is an outpatient procedure that requires rapid awakening while ensuring adequate analgesia. The currently widely used regimen of propofol-remifentanil presents limitations. The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of dexmedetomidine-dezocine in transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval.MethodsA single-center prospective randomized controlled trial included 124 patients randomly divided into Group D (dexmedetomidine-dezocine, n = 62), and Group P (propofol-remifentanil, n = 62). The primary outcome measure was time to complete wakefulness (time from drug withdrawal to OAA/S score ≥ 4). Secondary outcome measures included intraoperative body movement scale, Ramsay sedation scale, hemodynamic parameters, and adverse reactions.ResultsThere were no statistical differences between the two groups in intraoperative limb movement grading (p = 0.12), operator satisfaction score (4.65 ± 0.78 vs. 4.55 ± 0.72, p = 0.3348), and patient satisfaction (7.39 vs. 7.10, p = 0.407). Group D had higher oxygen saturation than Group P (97.8% vs. 96.3%, p = 0.002) and a more stable respiratory rate (14.8 ± 2.3 vs. 13.0 ± 2.6, p < 0.001). Group D also had a shorter time to complete wakefulness than Group P (15.02 vs. 15.87 min, minutes, p = 0.019) and a higher Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) score at the end of the procedure (2.06 vs. 1.64, p = 0.011). Mean arterial pressure and heart rate were not significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.88 and p = 0.93, respectively). The incidence of major adverse events was similar between groups. However, this study did not assess embryo quality or pregnancy outcomes, which represents a limitation.ConclusionsThis exploratory study suggests that dexmedetomidine-dezocine regimens may represent a potential anesthetic option for transvaginal oocyte retrieval. However, further research is needed to validate these findings.Trial registration(TCTR20240601001) [Registered on: 01/06/2024]. This trial is registered with the Thailand Clinical Trials Registry https://www.thaiclinicaltrials.org/.

Introduction

In recent years, the maturation of in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET) technology has significantly revolutionized the treatment outlook for infertile patients [1]. As the core component of this technique, transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval requires multiple punctures of the follicle through the vaginal vault, but this maneuver may cause severe pain and discomfort [2]. Intraoperative movement of the patient due to pain not only interferes with the surgical process, but also increases the risk of puncture. As this procedure is mostly performed in outpatient clinics, its anesthetic management faces a double challenge: on the one hand, adequate analgesia is required to ensure patient cooperation, and on the other hand, rapid awakening is required to ensure safe postoperative discharge from the hospital.

Currently, propofol combined with remifentanil is the most commonly used anesthetic regimen for oocyte retrieval [3, 4]. Despite its rapid induction and significant analgesic effect, it has limitations such as large hemodynamic fluctuations and increased risk of respiratory depression [5, 6]. Meanwhile, remifentanil may have some adverse effects on postoperative embryo quality [7]. In recent years, the combination of dexmedetomidine and dezocine has gradually become a hot research topic due to its good hemodynamic stability and quality of awakening [8]. Dexmedetomidine has the property of “conscious sedation”, which can maintain voluntary respiration and reduce sympathetic activity [9], while dezocine produces highly effective analgesia by agonizing κ-receptors, and at the same time reduces respiratory depression and gastrointestinal adverse effects [10]. There are no original English-language studies reporting the use of dexmedetomidine in combination with dezocine for in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) transvaginal oocyte retrieval, but it is theorized that the combination of the two may provide a superior anesthetic option for IVF-ET oocyte retrieval. Based on the above background, the value of dexmedetomidine combined with dezocine as a potential anesthetic option for outpatient surgery in IFV-ET oocyte retrieval has not been thoroughly investigated. For this reason, the aim of this study was to investigate the clinical efficacy of this combination in vaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval and to provide new ideas for optimizing anesthesia strategies for assisted reproduction. The following are the information and methods of this study.

Materials and methods

Information and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was a single-center prospective randomized controlled trial that was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University (Ethics No. LLSC-2024-056, approval date: February 1, 2024). The study protocol was designed in compliance with the CONSORT statement and registration was completed at the Thailand Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20240601001). We note that patient recruitment began in February 2024 prior to trial registration due to an administrative oversight in the registration process. However, we confirm that ethical approval was obtained prior to the enrollment of the first participant, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards. All participants provided written informed consent. While recruitment began in February 2024, trial registration was completed on June 1, 2024, representing a limitation in our study protocol.

Study population

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of study subjects

One hundred and twenty-four patients who underwent vaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval in the Department of Reproductive Medicine of the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University between February 2024 and February 2025 were included in this study. These patients, aged 22 to 38 years, signed an informed consent form and passed a standardized preoperative anesthetic evaluation before surgery. Screening was done by attending title anesthesiologists and reproductive clinicians with more than 10 years of experience based on inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria

patients underwent controlled ovarian stimulation using a standard long protocol [11], ultrasound monitoring showed good development of dominant follicles, and the expected number of oocytes to be retrieved ranged from 8 to 20; age between 22 and 40; preoperative physical status of I-II according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists grading scale; no history of opioid abuse; and signed informed consent and approved by the ethics committee.

Exclusion criteria

severe cardiovascular disease; obvious abnormalities in liver and kidney function and coagulation mechanism; allergy to anesthetic drugs; long-term use of drugs for central nervous system function; BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²; exclusion of patients with reproductive system abnormalities (e.g., hydrosalpinx of the fallopian tubes, chocolate cysts of the ovaries with a diameter of > 3 cm, uterine adhesions, etc.) and severe endocrine or autoimmune diseases; and upper respiratory tract infections in the last two weeks, etc.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Primary outcome

Time to complete wakefulness, defined as the time from drug withdrawal to achieving an Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) score ≥ 4, which indicates the patient is almost fully awake and able to carry out commands.

Secondary outcomes

Intraoperative body movement scale; Ramsay sedation scale (measured at the time of follicle aspiration); Hemodynamic parameters (mean arterial pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate); Postoperative recovery variables (eye-opening time, OAA/S scores); Patient and operator satisfaction scores; Adverse events and complications.

Sample size and randomization

Based on G*power 3.1.9.7 calculations for the primary outcome (time to complete wakefulness), assuming a clinically meaningful difference of 2 min between groups with a standard deviation of 4 min, α = 0.05, and 80% power, the minimum required sample size was 128 patients (64 per group). Due to practical constraints, 124 patients were recruited. Patients were randomly assigned to either the dexmedetomidine combined with dezocine group (Group D) or the propofol combined with remifentanil group (Group P), with stratification factors including age (≤ 30 or > 30 years) and BMI (< 25 kg/m² or ≥ 25 kg/m²). The randomization sequence was generated by SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute) and maintained allocation concealment by an independent statistician.

Blinding

Due to the different administration methods and visible differences between the study drugs, complete blinding of anesthesiologists was not feasible. However, outcome assessors responsible for measuring recovery parameters and patients were blinded to group assignment. Patients remained unaware of their group allocation throughout the study period.

Interventions

Anesthesia program

Preoperative fasting protocol required abstinence from solids for > 6 h and clear fluids for > 6 h. This protocol was chosen to ensure patient safety in our clinical setting, although we acknowledge it deviates from current ASA guidelines that allow clear fluids up to 2 h before anesthesia. After the patient enters the operating room, tripartite verification of the patient’s identity is carried out by the surgeon, anesthesiologist and operating room nurse. The anesthesiologist administered oxygen to the patient through a nasal cannula with the flow rate set at 3 L/min for oxygen reserve. Heart rate, respiratory rate, mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation, and electrocardiogram were continuously monitored, and the anesthesia machine, ventilator, and emergency medications and supplies were routinely prepared.

In Group D, anesthesia was induced with dexmedetomidine (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, State Drug License H20183219) 1 µg/kg intravenously pumped based on actual body weight [12] and dezocine (Jiangsu Yangzijiang Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd, State Drug License H20080329) 0.08 mg/kg [13] intravenously for 10–15 min, and dexmedetomidine was given intraoperatively at 0.6 µg/(kg·h) and dezocine 0.06 mg/(kg·h) were pumped for anesthesia maintenance.

Group P anesthesia was induced by propofol (Sichuan Guorui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, State Drug Certificate H20030115) 1.5-2.0 mg/kg based on actual body weight [14] and remifentanil (Yichang Renfu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, State Drug Certificate H20030197) 0.5–1 µg/kg [15], injected intravenously for at least 60 s, and continuously pumped intravenously with propofol 5 mg/(kg·h) and remifentanil 0.1 µg/(kg·min) to maintain the depth of anesthesia. The propofol dose of 1.5-2.0 mg/kg was selected based on our institutional protocol for procedural sedation in oocyte retrieval, though we acknowledge this may be higher than some recommendations for conscious sedation. All infusions were discontinued at the completion of follicle aspiration. Patients in the P group received an induction dose of propofol (1.5–2.0 mg/kg). This induction dose range was selected for the following reasons: First, transvaginal oocyte retrieval, despite being a brief procedure, involves significant visceral traction and puncture pain, which requires a deep level of sedation to eliminate patient movement and ensure procedural safety and success. A lighter plane of sedation may lead to body movement, increasing the risk of traumatic complications. Second, this dose range represents a standard induction regimen for general anesthesia in many outpatient settings and is reported in the anesthesia literature. Although it typically induces a state of general anesthesia, and all patients in the P group were expected to lose verbal responsiveness. Respiratory parameters and recovery times were meticulously monitored and compared under this general-anesthesia induction protocol.

Vital signs management

The patient’s blood pressure and heart rate were controlled within ± 20% of the basal values. If the blood oxygen saturation drops below 93%, respiratory function assessment is immediately performed; when SpO₂≤92% and lasts more than 30 s, verbal and tactile stimulation is first attempted, and if SpO₂ still does not improve or continues to drop after assisted ventilation, it is immediately switched to manual ventilation. For patients with respiratory rate < 8 breaths/min or ineffective respiration (no significant rise and fall of the chest), manual ventilation (10–12 breaths/min frequency) was used directly to maintain a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg until respiratory function returned to normal. All respiratory interventions were recorded as important indicators for intraoperative respiratory management assessment. Respiratory rate was measured using impedance pneumography integrated into the patient monitoring system.

Surgical operation

After the onset of anesthesia, the patient took the cystotomy position, and the oocyte retrieval was performed transvaginally under ultrasound guidance, using an oocyte collector (WEGO, 17G (type II)), with the needle inserted through the vaginal fornix, connected to the negative-pressure suction device, with the pressure set at about 150 mmHg (approximately 20 kPa), and the follicles > 12 mm in the bilateral ovaries were extracted one by one, until all of them had been extracted.

Criteria for grading intraoperative body movements

Depth of sedation: use of the Ramsay sedation scale

The Ramsay Sedation Scale (RSS) was measured at the time of follicle aspiration to assess the depth of sedation. The RSS is a widely used tool to assess the depth of sedation of patients [17], with a 6-point scale. 1–2 points, the patient remains minimal sedation; 3–4 points (moderate sedation) : for short outpatient procedures, such as oocyte retrieval; 5–6 points (deep sedation): close to general anesthesia.

Quality of postoperative recovery

Speed of recovery of consciousness

Eye-opening time, defined as the time from the cessation of anesthetic drugs to the time when the patient is able to open his or her eyes voluntarily, is an important marker of the recovery of consciousness in patients after anesthesia, and is usually used to assess the pharmacokinetics of anesthetic drugs.

The Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation(OAA/S) scoring system is a standardized scale used to assess a patient’s recovery of consciousness from anesthesia and is usually assigned a score ranging from 1 to 5 [18].Score 1: unresponsive, unresponsive to external stimuli; score 2: responsive to minor stimuli but not fully awake; score 3: responsive to speech, able to open eyes but not fully awake; score 4: almost fully awake, able to carry out commands, but still mildly drowsy; Score 5: fully awake, responds immediately and appropriately to external stimuli. The OAA/S score is the central tool used by anesthesiologists to determine the quality of postoperative recovery. The time to full wakefulness (time from drug withdrawal to OAA/S score ≥ 4) is an important indicator for assessing the clearance of anesthetic drugs from the body and the patient’s recovery from anesthesia [19].

Assessment of degree of wakefulness

OAA/S value at the end of surgery (Immediate End of Surgery Score) [18]: reflects the patient’s depth of sedation at the immediate end of surgery and is used to assess the residual sedation and early recovery.

Eye-opening OAA/S value (score at first voluntary eye opening) [20]: records the patient’s level of consciousness at the time of first voluntary eye opening, which is a key indicator for determining the patient’s transition from unconsciousness to an awakened state.

Patient satisfaction and operator satisfaction evaluation

Patient satisfaction evaluation was performed 30 min post-procedure using the postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) [21], with a score range of 0 ~ 10 (0 = completely dissatisfied, 10 = very satisfied), to minimize bias from residual sedation effects.

Operator satisfaction evaluation was performed by the primary surgeon in each case using the Operator Satisfaction Rating Scale (OSRS), usually in the form of a Likert-type questionnaire with a 5-point scale: 1 point: Very Dissatisfied; 2 points: Dissatisfied; 3 points: Neutral; 4 points: Satisfied; 5: Very Satisfied. The distribution of operators was similar between both groups.

Anesthesia safety assessment

Hemodynamic stability

Hemodynamic parameters: Mean arterial pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation and respiratory rate were recorded at 5 time points. These 5 time points were: T0 (1 min before induction), T1 (immediately after induction), T2 (immediately after puncture oocyte retrieval and needle insertion), T3 (half of the time point of oocyte retrieval), and T4 (at the time of waking up at the end of the operation).

Respiratory safety (percentage of assisted and hand-controlled breathing)

The number of assisted and hand-controlled breathing cases was recorded. Assisted respiration refers to the anesthesiologist’s need to assist the patient’s respiration during anesthesia through the use of mechanical ventilation equipment (e.g., mask pressure ventilation) due to the patient’s insufficient voluntary respiration [22]. Manual control of breathing refers to the fact that during anesthesia, due to the cessation or severe insufficiency of the patient’s voluntary respiration, the anesthesiologist needs to manually perform respiratory control (e.g., by manually squeezing the resuscitation airbag) to maintain the patient’s respiratory function [23]. During anesthesia, recording the number of cases of assisted respiration and manual respiratory control is one of the important indicators to assess the quality and safety of anesthesia.

Observation of adverse reactions

Postoperative adverse reactions are important indicators for assessing the effectiveness and safety of anesthesia. Common postoperative adverse reactions include nausea/vomiting, postoperative agitation, upright hypotension, dizziness/headache and low oxygen saturation. Postoperative patients may experience nausea/vomiting and postoperative agitation, usually occurring within 1 h after surgery. Upright hypotension is a postoperative hypotensive condition that occurs when changing from the prone to the upright position and is usually defined as a drop in systolic blood pressure of more than 20 mmHg, with the patient experiencing symptoms such as dizziness and discomfort. An oxygen saturation of ≤ 92% was defined as hypoxemia.

Statistical methods and analysis

Statistical analysis was performed according to a pre-specified analysis plan. The primary analysis followed the intention-to-treat principle. A variety of statistical analysis methods were used in the study, including descriptive statistics for presenting baseline demographic characteristics and clinical data (age, height, BMI, etc.); independent samples t-tests to compare the differences in continuous variables between the two groups (postoperative wakefulness time, OAA/S scores, and VAS scores of patient satisfaction); chi-square tests (χ² tests) to analyze differences in categorical variables (intraoperative somatic mobility class, number of assisted breathing and hand-controlled breathing cases, and postoperative adverse reaction rates); analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare changes in hemodynamic parameters at multiple time points (T0-T4); nonparametric tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test to assess nonnormally distributed data (Ramsay sedation score and operator satisfaction scores); and the study was set at p < 0.05 as the criterion for statistical significance and was performed using G* power software for sample size estimation (independent sample t-test based on medium to large effect sizes of d = 0.5–0.8, α = 0.05, and efficacy = 80% to determine the minimum required sample size of more than 128), as well as generating randomization sequences through SAS 9.4 software, and using a stratified randomization strategy (with stratification factors that included age and BMI) to ensure the scientific validity and reliability of the study. All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software.

Discussion

Transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval, as a core part of assisted reproductive technology, requires anesthesia management that meets the dual requirements of adequate analgesia and rapid awakening. Currently, intravenous anesthesia is mainly used in clinical practice, mainly because of its reliable effect, convenient operation and suitability for outpatient environment [24]. In contrast, local anesthesia may lead to intraoperative body movements that may affect the precision of operation due to poor analgesia, while general anesthesia and intrathecal anesthesia require longer monitoring time, which is unsuitable for the rapid turnaround demand of outpatient surgery.

This study is the first to systematically evaluate the efficacy of dexmedetomidine combined with dezocine in transvaginal oocyte retrieval and compare it with the conventional propofol-remifentanil regimen. The results of this study showed that the two regimens were comparable in terms of sedative and analgesic effects, but the dexmedetomidine combined with dezocine regimen resulted in better outcomes in terms of respiratory function maintenance and postoperative recovery in outpatient surgery.

Advantages in respiratory function

The study data demonstrated that the Group D demonstrated superior respiratory stability compared to Group P. Group D maintained higher oxygen saturation (97.8% vs. 96.3%, p = 0.002) and more stable respiratory rate (14.8 ± 2.3 vs. 13.0 ± 2.6, p < 0.001) throughout the entire surgical procedure. This advantage was especially pronounced after induction of anesthesia (T1) and during the early maintenance phase (T2), which is crucial for ensuring surgical safety.

Dexmedetomidine, as a highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist, provides a “conscious sedation” effect, i.e., it provides a sufficient depth of sedation without significantly depressing the respiratory center [25, 26]. Meanwhile, dezocine provides analgesia through selective agonism of κ-receptors with a relatively low risk of respiratory depression due to its partial antagonism of µ-receptors [27, 28]. This pharmacological property allowed patients in Group D to maintain relatively stable voluntary respiratory function even when the need for surgical sedation was met.

It is noteworthy that despite the superior performance of Group D in terms of respiratory function parameters, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of the number of cases in which respiratory interventions (assisted and manual breathing) were actually required. This may reflect two things: first, the dose control of the current anesthetic regimen was more reasonable, so that the patients in Group P had not yet crossed the clinical threshold for intervention, although they reached a statistically significant difference in the degree of decline in respiratory function; and second, the anesthesiologists adopted an aggressive preventive strategy by intervening in the patients’ respiratory function before they developed significant abnormalities, which was in line with the principles of clinical safety.

Advantages in quality of awakening

The results of this study showed that patients in Group D demonstrated better outcomes in terms of emergence from anesthesia. Specifically, Group D had a significantly shorter time to full wakefulness (15.02 min vs. 15.87 min, p = 0.019), higher postoperative OAA/S scores (2.06 vs. 1.64, p = 0.011), and higher OAA/S scores at eye opening (4.49 vs. 3.92, p = 0.012). Together, these data suggest that the dexmedetomidine combined with dezocine regimen may has a faster emergence from anesthesia and a higher quality of recovery. However, time to full wakefulness in the dexmedetomidine-dezocine group reached statistical significance but represents a modest clinical difference of less than one minute. The clinical relevance of this difference in the context of outpatient oocyte retrieval procedures requires further evaluation.

This difference may stem from the unique pharmacokinetic properties of dexmedetomidine. Compared to propofol [29], dexmedetomidine is characterized by a shorter context-sensitive half-life and a more rapid decline in plasma concentrations after discontinuation [30]. In addition, dexmedetomidine produces a sedative effect that more closely resembles a physiologic sleep state than the suppression of consciousness caused by traditional anesthetic drugs [31], which allows patients to transition more naturally to wakefulness in the postoperative period. Dezocine also has a longer elimination half-life and more gentle clearance profile relative to remifentanil, which helps to provide sustained postoperative analgesia while avoiding withdrawal symptoms after abrupt discontinuation of the drug, further optimizing the postoperative recovery experience.

Faster awakening and higher wakefulness are particularly important for outpatient surgery, helping to shorten postoperative monitoring time and improve OR turnaround efficiency, while reducing the burden of postoperative monitoring and associated costs. In addition, high-quality postoperative wakefulness may also reduce patient anxiety and improve overall satisfaction.

Hemodynamic stability and safety

The results of this study showed no significant differences in mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR) between the two groups (p = 0.88 and p = 0.93), suggesting that the dexmedetomidine combined with dezocine regimen was comparable to the conventional regimen in maintaining hemodynamic stability. This finding is consistent with the results of previous studies [32, 33], reflecting the fact that dexmedetomidine is effective in preventing the stress response caused by surgical stimuli and maintaining the stability of the cardiovascular system by decreasing sympathetic nerve activity.

In terms of adverse effects, nausea/vomiting (9.7% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.82), postoperative agitation (4.8% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.42), upright hypotension (4.8% vs. 6.5%, p = 0.65), headache and dizziness (4.8% vs. 6.5%, p = 0.65), and hypoxic oxygen saturation (0% vs. 3.2%, p = 0.36) incidence were not statistically different. Notably, there was a trend toward a decrease in postoperative agitation and hypoxemia in Group D. Although it did not reach statistical significance, it may be of clinical significance, which needs to be further verified by studies with larger sample sizes.

Prospects for clinical application

Based on the results of this study, we can make the following recommendations for clinical practice: for outpatient procedures requiring rapid awakening, especially transvaginal oocyte retrieval, dexmedetomidine-dezocine regimen may be a feasible alternative, with the advantage of maintaining more stable respiratory function and providing a higher quality of postoperative awakening. In contrast, for prolonged procedures requiring deep sedation, the traditional propofol combined with remifentanil regimen still be indicated, but intraoperative respiratory monitoring and postoperative recovery management should be enhanced.

It is worth mentioning that previous studies have suggested that remifentanil may have potential effects on embryo quality [7]. Although embryo quality and pregnancy outcomes were not directly assessed in this study, the dexmedetomidine combined with dezocine regimen to avoid remifentanil may provide new ideas for optimizing assisted reproduction outcomes, which needs to be further explored in future studies.

Study limitations and future directions

There are several limitations of this study: first, the sample size was relatively limited (62 cases per group), which may affect the sensitivity of the statistical analysis, especially for the detection of low incidence events; The study was not designed to demonstrate equivalence or non-inferiority between the two regimens. second, this study did not evaluate embryo quality or pregnancy outcomes, which are critical endpoints in assisted reproductive technology. The potential impact of different anesthetic regimens on reproductive outcomes remains unknown and represents an important area for future research; The study only conducted short-term follow-up and failed to assess the long-term effects of anesthetic drugs on embryonic development and pregnancy outcomes; third, this study did not explore the optimal dose when combining drugs rationing, and the propofol induction dose of 1.5-2.0 mg/kg used in this study may be higher than some recommendations for conscious sedation and could potentially confound respiratory outcome comparisons. This dosing was based on our institutional protocol but may limit the generalizability of our findings; and finally, the study did not stratify the analysis for special populations (e.g., elderly and obese patients) and the retrospective trial registration after recruitment initiation represents a protocol deviation.

Based on these limitations, future studies could be conducted in the following directions: (1) expanding the sample size and prolonging the follow-up period, especially to assess the effects on the quality of embryonic development and pregnancy outcomes; (2) conducting dose exploration studies to determine the optimal dose ratio for the combination of dexmedetomidine and dezocine; (3) conducting stratified studies for different patient groups (e.g., elderly patients, patients with polycystic ovary syndrome, etc.) to provide more individualized anesthesia protocols that provide more individualized anesthesia protocols; and (4) designing a multicenter study to improve the external validity of the study results.

Conclusion

This exploratory randomized controlled trial suggests that dexmedetomidine-dezocine may provide a potential alternative anesthetic approach for transvaginal oocyte retrieval, with some advantages in respiratory stability and recovery characteristics compared to propofol-remifentanil and postoperative recovery profiles while ensuring adequate sedation and analgesia. However, the clinical significance of the observed differences is modest, and further research is needed to establish the role of dexmedetomidine-dezocine in oocyte retrieval anesthesia, particularly regarding embryo quality and pregnancy outcomes.
