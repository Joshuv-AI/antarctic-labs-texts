Environmental simulation and refrigeration demand analysis for shield tunnel construction in hot summer and cold winter regions
Authors: Zhe Zhang, Li Zhang, Huan Su, Zihan Yang, Xiaohua Li, Fan Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe thermal hazards generated during the tunnel boring machine construction process significantly impact the physical and mental health of personnel. A field test was conducted on the tunnel of Metro Line 1 in Changsha, China, where the average temperature in the work zone reached up to 33.16 °C, and the air velocity was generally below 0.2 m/s. These conditions were unfavorable for ventilation and heat dissipation. In this paper, a full-scale CFD simulation model was established to simulate the construction environment and analyze the cooling demand. The results indicated that increasing the fresh air velocity could lower the temperature in the work zone; however, this effect was limited. When the fresh air temperature exceeded a certain threshold, it actually raised the average temperature of the work zone. Consequently, there is a need for supplementary cooling of the fresh air during the hot summer months. At a fresh air velocity of 15 m/s, the maximum temperature in the work zone was recorded at 27.8 °C when the fresh air was cooled down to 22.5 °C through auxiliary cooling measures. This approach effectively controlled the temperature in the work zone, reduced flow dead zones, and significantly improved air distribution and heat dissipation. Additionally, it ensured the physical and mental well-being of the construction workers and the safe operation of the equipment. This study provides valuable guidance for managing thermal environments in underground tunnel construction.Keywords: TBM, Tunnel ventilation, Work zone environment, CFD simulation

Introduction

The underground engineering sector in China is experiencing unprecedented growth [1]. Tunnel boring machines (TBMs) are being widely utilized for their automation, efficiency in manpower, quick construction pace, and minimal impact on surface structures [2]. Factors such as dust, harmful gases, heat, humidity, sound, and light [3] within tunnels directly affect the productivity, safety, and health of workers [4]. The Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development of the People's Republic of China has introduced the ‘Code for Construction and Acceptance of Shield Tunneling Method (2017)', which is currently guiding the development of metro systems, highways, railways, hydraulic projects, municipal infrastructure, and tunnels in China [5]. This code recommends the use of mechanical ventilation to ensure compliance with safety standards related to hazardous gases and air temperature. For instance, it suggests a minimum air velocity of 0.25 m/s in tunnels and a maximum air temperature of 28 °C.

Previous research on tunnel construction environments has primarily focused on the generation [[6], [7], [8], [9]] and distribution [[10], [11], [12]] of dust and harmful gases, as well as the corresponding control methods [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. For example, Xiao [17] investigated the spatial and temporal variations of airflow and dust under three ventilation modes, and elaborated the dust control mechanism and the ratio of particle sizes. Li [18] investigated the characteristics of the longitudinal temperature distribution under the roof in tunnels with the combination of multi-point smoke exhaust and longitudinal ventilation. Liangliang Tao and Yanhua Zeng [19] built a 1:10 single-side centralized exhaust small-scale tunnel to study smoke and temperature distribution beneath ceiling under different longitudinal velocities, smoke exhaust volumes and HRRs. These studies have demonstrated that effective ventilation can help prevent the accumulation of hazardous substances in tunnels and maintain their levels within safe limits. Ventilation is therefore considered the primary method for environmental control in shield tunnel construction [20]. However, the widespread use of Tunnel Boring Machines (TBMs) has revealed challenges in maintaining suitable heat and humidity conditions within the work zone through ventilation alone. This issue is particularly pronounced in southern China [21]. For example, a study by Zhang et al. [22] found that temperatures in the work zone of a shield tunnel in Shanghai could exceed 40–45 °C during the summer, with temperatures exceeding 50 °C and relative humidity surpassing 90 % in a small-diameter shield tunnel in Guangzhou. Another study by Zhang et al. [23] investigated temperature distributions during continuous boring in a shield tunnel in Taishan, revealing that even with an outdoor temperature of 27 °C, the work zone temperature inside the tunnel remained at 34–36 °C. The high temperatures and humidity levels inside shield tunnels can significantly impact the physical and mental well-being of workers. Therefore, it is essential to examine the influence of tunnel ventilation systems on the thermal environment within the work zone.

Numerous studies have been conducted to investigate temperature distribution in underground spaces using numerical methods. Xiao et al. [24] calculated transient heat transfer from the wall surfaces of underground spaces, finding that the heat fluxes of the inner surface closely matched those calculated using numerical finite-difference methods, with a deviation of only 1 %. Szabó et al. [25]utilized the thermal balance method to develop unsteady mathematical models for wall and air temperatures in underground spaces, offering a novel approach for calculating heat loads in such environments. Yuan et al. [26] developed a mathematical heat transfer model for underground engineering envelopes, providing a rapid and accurate solution for heat transfer calculations. Their findings highlighted the significant impact of thermal conductivity of rock on heat transfer between air and rock. Wang et al. [27] focused on open-type full-section tunnel boring machine (TBM) construction tunnels, establishing an air temperature calculation model based on thermodynamic principles. They analyzed the effects of varying surrounding rock levels and temperatures on tunnel ambient temperature. Liu et al. [28] proposed a numerical model for simultaneous heat transfer between air and tunnel surfaces, validating the model with measured data and applying it to an operational underground tunnel. Yanhua Zeng [29] established a cross-sectional convection-conduction model to simulate a high geothermal tunnel with longitudinal ventilation, and studied the effects of ventilation frequency and longitudinal air velocity on the ambient temperature inside the tunnel by numerical simulation, taking the Gaoligongshan Tunnel as an example. The results show that the longitudinal velocity should not be lower than 3.5 m/s, and the ventilation frequency should not be less than three times a day. The experimental results are in good agreement with the numerical simulation results, which verifies the reliability of the numerical simulation method. Finally, Sasmito et al. [30] explored thermal management strategies for ventilation dead zones in underground mine pipelines, demonstrating the significant influence of control parameters such as original rock temperature, ventilation temperature, and ventilation volume on air temperature control. Habibi et al. [31] established a ventilation model to simulate airflow and heat conditions in a coal mine, calibrated based on pressure, volume, and temperature results. Li et al. [32] developed a mathematical model of heat dissipation in the enclosing rock of the ventilation annular zone using the finite volume method, calculating the thermal insulation performance of the ventilated interlayer. The predicted results closely matched measured data with a deviation of less than 10 %. Wang [33] studied the effects of RT (rock temperature), TBM utilization factor and construction ventilation on the air temperature in the TBM chamber. The results showed that the air temperature reached a steady state after 20 min. The stabilized air temperature increases linearly with increasing RT and TBM utilization factor. The air temperature decreased with the increase of fresh air volume and increased with the increase of fresh air temperature. Therefore, ventilation can be found to be a very useful method to reduce the air temperature in the TBM chamber. Xia et al. [34] utilized numerical simulation to analyze the distribution of ventilation and temperature fields in the TBM construction area within a small-diameter shield tunnel, with simulation accuracy verified by field test data. It was suggested to increase flow velocity in the work zone to 0.2 m/s to reduce flow dead zones in the tunnel. These studies have significantly contributed to understanding thermal environment distribution and predicting heat loads in underground spaces.

However, most of these studies simplified the equipment heat source to a constant. Some studies also proposed simplified methods for estimating equipment loads. For example, Liu [35] and Zhang et al. [36] used the heat balance principle. However, the actual TBM is operated intermittently. A constant heat source cannot reflect the transient temperature changes in the work zone due to intermittent operation of the machine. This will result in a large deviation of the actual tunnel thermal environment from the simulated values. There are few studies investigated the impact of intermittently operated equipment on the thermal environment of the work zone.

To investigate the impact of equipment operation and surrounding rock on tunnel thermal environment, this study focuses on the shield tunnel between Xiangxiu Station and Kaifu District Government Station of Changsha Metro Line 1. Field surveys were conducted to understand the temporal variation of internal heat sources during TBM construction. A numerical model was developed considering boundary conditions from intermittent equipment operation and various heat sources. All equipment is classified into two types based on its heat release characteristics.One category maintains a relatively constant heat release rate when the TBM is turned on and off; and the other is the electrically driven equipment listed in Table 2, where the heat release rate varies considerably when the TBM is turned on and off. The model was used to calculate the effects of different fresh air parameters on work zone temperature and tunnel cross-section air velocity. Additionally, fitting equations for fresh air parameters and temperature drops in the work zone were established. Hourly calculations of tunnel work zone temperature in Changsha City from June to August were performed to optimize the ventilation system. This research offers valuable insights for managing thermal environments in underground tunnels.

Research methodology

This study aims to simulate the internal environment of a tunnel using full-scale computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. To accomplish this, the paper first collects parameters for each type of equipment in the tunnel through field measurements. The equipment is categorized into two types based on their heat release characteristics. The accuracy of the developed numerical model is validated through experimental tests, after which the thermal environment within the tunnel is simulated.

Project profile

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the total length of the shield tunnel between Xiangxiu Station and Kaifu District Government Station on Changsha Metro Line 1 in China is 3135 m. The left tunnel measures 1569 m, while the right tunnel measures 1566 m; the focus of this study is on the left tunnel. Two soil-pressure balance tunnel boring machines (TBMs) of type CTE6250H-0945 are employed, commencing at Xiangxiu Station and concluding at Kaifu District Government Station. The depth of the tunnel during the boring phase ranges from 18.6 m to 23.5 m, with a maximum soil layer thickness of 29.6 m above the tunnel. Mechanical ventilation is utilized throughout the TBM construction process, and the ventilation system is divided into two sections. As depicted in Fig. 2, fresh air from the cave is directed to the tail end of the TBM after being pressurized by a primary fan located in the starting shaft. Subsequently, fresh air is conveyed from the tail end of the TBM to the front end via a secondary fan. The primary duct consists of a 1.0 m diameter polyester hose, situated in the non-construction zone, with its length gradually increasing in accordance with the depth of the excavation. The secondary duct is a round steel pipe with a diameter of 0.6 m and a length of 76.4 m. The outlet air velocity is 7.14 m/s, and the air volume is 7267.63 m³/h. This section is located in the construction zone and is secured to the trolley truss. Currently, the majority of shield construction in China employs this dual-stage air supply method. Mechanical ventilation provides a certain degree of cooling capacity during winter and transitional seasons, which helps improve the hot and humid conditions in the tunnel work zone. However, the cooling capacity of mechanical ventilation is significantly limited during the summer months. The substantial heat dissipation from equipment during TBM construction is a primary factor contributing to the deterioration of the thermal environment within the tunnel. Therefore, it is essential to quantify the heat dissipation of the main equipment to accurately simulate temperature variations in the tunnel work zone.

Filed survey and experimental test

For the field survey, the equipment within the tunnel is enumerated as detailed in Table 1, Table 2. Initially, the location of each piece of equipment, identified by its trolley serial number, is recorded. Subsequently, testing instruments, including an infrared thermometer, clamp-on electric power meter, and laser range finder, are employed to measure the surface temperature, instantaneous power, and external dimensions of the equipment. The equipment is categorized into two types based on their heat release characteristics. The non-electric equipment listed in Table 1 exhibits a relatively constant heat release rate regardless of whether the TBM is on. This non-electric equipment is treated as a Dirichlet boundary condition in the subsequent simulations presented in this paper, with their surface temperatures derived from experimental tests. Conversely, the electrically driven equipment enumerated in Table 2 demonstrates significant fluctuations in heat release rates when the TBM is active or inactive. This electric equipment is regarded as a Neumann boundary condition in the subsequent simulations, with their heat fluxes approximated by instantaneous power.

For the experimental tests, Trolley 0 and the connecting bridge attachment serve as the primary zones where workers assemble the tube sheets. The temperature distribution in the main work zone (illustrated in Fig. 3a) and the average air velocities in the tunnel cross-sections (depicted in Fig. 3c) are measured to verify the accuracy of the model established in this paper. For the temperature assessment, twelve PT100 thermistor temperature sensors are strategically arranged and calibrated using an ice-water mixture prior to the test. The work zone is divided into four sections, each measuring 2 m, labeled as sections I, II, III, and IV (as shown in Fig. 3b). Each section comprises three sensors grouped together to obtain the average temperature for the respective sections. A paperless recorder is employed to document the real-time temperature at each measurement point.

Schematic diagram of the location of measurement points in the internal work zone of the tunnel.

The average air velocities in various sections of the TBM were measured. The inner side of the left-side trolley, utilized by construction personnel within the tunnel, is designated as the pedestrian side. Conversely, the inner side of the right-hand trolley, which houses the electromechanical and hydraulic systems, is referred to as the equipment side. An anemometer was employed to measure air velocities at each designated measurement point on both the pedestrian and equipment sides. Each measurement point is positioned 1.5 m above the height of the trolley, with an interval of 5 m between each point, as illustrated in Fig. 3c. The experimental test was conducted from 10:05 to 13:50 on July 13, 2023, totaling 225 min. During this period, the TBM was activated twice, resulting in a cumulative activation time of 132 min and a deactivation time of 93 min.

The wall surfaces of each piece of equipment within the tunnel are specified according to Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, depending on the type of equipment. For instance, the walls of equipment such as the foam original liquid water tank, bentonite box, fixed pressure tank, mortar box, sewage tank, and others are assigned Dirichlet boundary conditions. These surfaces are characterized as non-slip thermostatic surfaces, with temperatures determined through field experiments. Specific details are provided in Table 1. Conversely, the wall surfaces of equipment including the bentonite mixing motor, belt motor, mortar mixing motor, cutter replenishment pump, and hydraulic oil tank filter pump are designated as Neumann boundary conditions. The heat fluxes for these components are calculated based on the geometric external surface area of the equipment, in conjunction with the power and operational time recorded during field tests. Regarding inlet boundary conditions, the tunnel's fresh air outlet is configured as a velocity inlet, with the outlet temperature and velocity set at 31.5 °C and 7.14 m/s, respectively, reflecting the average values derived from long-term field tests. For outlet boundary conditions, the tail end of the tunnel is established as a pressure outlet with a relative pressure of 0 Pa.

Analysis and discussion

The impact of fresh air temperature and air velocity on tunnel thermal environment and ventilation is explored in this study. A numerical model is utilized to analyze the influence of varying fresh air temperatures (22.5 °C, 25.5 °C, 28.5 °C, 31.5 °C) and air velocities (7.14 m/s, 10 m/s, 15 m/s) on temperatures and cross-section air velocities within the work zone. Temperature curves of the four cross-sections in the work zone exhibit significant overlap (Fig. 6), leading to the selection of the average temperature across these sections to represent the overall work zone temperature.

Work zone temperature

As shown in Fig. 8, once the TBM is activated, the equipment begins to operate, resulting in a rapid increase in the average temperature within the work zone. At the conclusion of the first boring session (70 min), the average temperature in the work zone reaches its peak value. Under the initial ventilation conditions, with a fresh air temperature of 31.5 °C and an air velocity of 7.14 m/s, the average temperature at the end of the first boring of the tunnel attains a maximum of 33.32 °C. The impact of varying fresh air temperatures (28.5 °C, 25.5 °C, and 22.5 °C) on the peak temperature in the work zone is examined while maintaining a constant air velocity. At an air velocity of 7.14 m/s, the peak temperatures decrease by 1.76 °C, 3.56 °C, and 5.33 °C, respectively, compared to the original ventilation conditions. When the fresh air velocity is increased to 10 m/s, the corresponding temperature reductions are 1.83 °C, 3.66 °C, and 5.49 °C, respectively. Furthermore, with a fresh air velocity of 15 m/s, the peak temperatures decrease by 1.83 °C, 3.72 °C, and 5.61 °C, respectively.

Temperature variation in the work zone of the internal tunnel with different air fresh parameters.

When the TBM is off (70–150 min), a significant number of equipment in the are turned off as the average temperature in the work zone begins to decrease. The average work zone temperature reached a minimum of 29.40 °C at the onset of the second tunnel boring phase (150 min) under the original ventilation conditions. The air velocity is maintained at a constant rate while investigating the effects of fresh air temperatures of 28.5 °C, 25.5 °C, and 22.5 °C on the minimum temperature values in the work zone. At an air velocity of 7.14 m/s, the minimum temperature values in the work zone decrease by 1.81 °C, 3.60 °C, and 5.40 °C, respectively, compared to the original ventilation conditions. When the fresh air velocity is set at 10 m/s, the corresponding temperature reductions are −0.12 °C, 1.74 °C, 3.60 °C, and 5.45 °C. At a fresh air velocity of 15 m/s, the minimum temperature values in the work zone decrease by −0.30 °C, 1.62 °C, 3.55 °C, and 5.48 °C, respectively.

The duration of the second boring (60 min) is 10 min shorter than that of the first boring, which results in the peak temperature at the completion of the second boring being slightly lower than that at the completion of the first boring. For example, the temperature peaks at the fresh air temperature of 28.5 °C and air velocity of 10 m/s are 33.32 °C and 33.15 °C respectively.

As shown in Fig. 9, the temperature variations in the work zone before and after the second turn-on of the TBM are selected for further analysis. When the TBM is turned off (70 min), the average temperatures in the work zone begin to decrease, and this decline in temperature is further enhanced by an increase in the fresh air velocity.

Temperature changes in the work zone before and after the second start-up of the TBM with different fresh air parameters.

When the fresh air temperature is 31.5 °C, the average temperature of the work zone decreases to 30.3 °C (132 min), and the average temperature of the work zone from the high to the low of the fresh air velocities are 15 m/s, 10 m/s and 7.14 m/s. At this time, the average temperature of the work zone rises with the increase of the fresh air velocities. The TBM is turned on and the inside of the tunnel warmed up rapidly. When the average temperature of the work zone increases to 30.7 °C (175 min), the average temperatures of the work zone from high to low fresh air velocity are 7.14 m/s, 10 m/s and 15 m/s. At this time, the average temperatures of the work zone decrease with the increase of fresh air velocities.

When the fresh air temperatures are 28.5 °C and 25.5 °C, the average temperatures in the work zone decrease to 27.7 °C (142 min) and 25.9 °C (146 min), and then the average temperatures in the work zone from the high to the low fresh air velocities are 15 m/s, 10 m/s, and 7.14 m/s, respectively. At this time, the average temperatures in the work zone increase with the increase of the fresh air velocities. When the average temperatures of the work zone increase to 28.7 °C (170 min) and 26.4 °C (162.5 min), the average temperatures of the work zone from high to low fresh air velocity conditions are 7.14 m/s, 10 m/s, and 15 m/s, respectively. At this time, the average temperatures of the work zone decrease with the increase of the fresh air velocities.

However, when the fresh air temperature is reduced to 22.5 °C and the fresh air velocities are 15 m/s, 10 m/s and 7.14 m/s, the valley values of the average temperatures in the work zone are 24.01 °C, 23.95 °C and 23.92 °C. At this time, under any working conditions, the higher the fresh air velocity, the lower the average temperature of the work zone. This shows that if the temperature of the fresh air is high when the TBM is off, even if the fresh air volume is increased, it not only fails to cool down the tunnel, but even becomes a heat source to increase the tunnel temperature. This is due to the fact that conductive heat transfer from rock limits the convective heat transfer from air ventilation. Thus, it can be deduced that ventilation velocity marginally affect the tunnel thermal management. Therefore, in summer or when the temperature of the fresh air is high, the fresh air must be cooled down with auxiliary cooling.

The temperature cloud maps of the tunnel work zone cross-sections (x = 15 m from the tunnel palm face) are presented at the moments of TBM shutdown (Fig. 10) and TBM activation (Fig. 11). At the time of TBM shutdown (70 min), the equipment has released a significant amount of heat, which accumulates on the equipment side of the tunnel and in the upper section of the tunnel. The temperature within the tunnel decreases dramatically as the temperature of the incoming fresh air declines, and concurrently, an increase in air velocity also contributes to a reduction in tunnel temperature.

This finding is consistent with previous conclusions.

Research on air velocity in the tunnel

As illustrated in Fig. 14, Fig. 15, under the original ventilation conditions, air velocities are generally below 0.2 m/s, both on the equipment side and the pedestrian side. The simulation results indicate that as fresh air velocities increase, the air velocities at all measurement points also rise. When the air velocity reaches 15 m/s, the average air velocity on the pedestrian side is 0.23 m/s, while the average air velocity on the equipment side is 0.27 m/s, representing increases of 0.12 m/s and 0.14 m/s, respectively, compared to the original ventilation conditions. The distribution of air velocities along the axial direction of the tunnel reveals lower velocities in certain areas, particularly at locations 10m–25m, 30m, and 40m from the palm face. The presence of large equipment, such as screw machines, belts, connecting bridges, and the main control room, obstructs airflow at these measurement points, leading to overall lower air velocities.

Limitations

The region examined in this paper is Changsha, China, characterized by hot summers and cold winters. Consequently, the temperature drop equation developed in this study may not be applicable to other regions.

Conclusion

This study utilizes field tests and numerical simulations to analyze the shield tunnel between Xiangxiu Station and Kaifu District Government Station of Changsha Metro Line 1 North Extension in Changsha, China. The research focuses on the temperature field within the tunnel's internal work zone and the overall flow field. The findings provide insights for optimizing tunnel ventilation:

1.The equipment within the shield tunnel can be classified into electric and non-electric categories. Electric equipment is evaluated based on its instantaneous power, whereas non-electric equipment is evaluated based on its surface temperature. These performance indicators are influenced by the work conditions of the shield tunnel.
