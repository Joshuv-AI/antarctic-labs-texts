Bioengineered α-Hairpin peptide TKH with GKG linker: a LLPS-mediated biomimetic mineralization system for intrafibrillar remineralization and deep dentin tubule occlusion
Authors: Yeling Ou, Jing Wang, Luoyao Wang, Haisheng Wang, Fengfan He, Longjiang Ding, Yanping Zhou, Sili Han, Linglin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dentin hypersensitivity (DH) originates from collagen exposure and dentinal tubules (DTs) patency caused by mechanical abrasion or demineralization. For an effective long-term therapeutic desensitizing agent, the ability to achieve deep tubular occlusion, stable remineralization, and satisfactory biocompatibility simultaneously is essential. In this study, we designed a novel α-hairpin peptide, TKH, engineered by incorporating a flexible GKG linker into an α-helical template, and provide an analysis of its liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) and self-assembly properties. TKH effectively stabilized amorphous calcium phosphate and facilitated intrafibrillar mineralization within collagen fibrils. In vitro and in vivo remineralization studies combined with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations demonstrated that TKH promoted the peptide self-assembly through LLPS, hydroxyapatite (HA) adsorption, and amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) stabilization, ultimately achieving intrafibrillar and extrafibrillar mineralization to seal DTs, alongside excellent biosafety. These findings highlight the potential of TKH as a promising peptide-based biomaterial for dentin remineralization and the treatment of dentin hypersensitivity.

Introduction

Dental caries, characterized by the pathological demineralization of hard tissues, is one of the most prevalent global diseases, affecting 2.4 billion people worldwide with an overall prevalence of 35% across all ages.1 Unlike enamel, dentin’s lower mineral content and higher organic matrix proportion render it particularly vulnerable to caries progression and dentin hypersensitivity (DH)—a condition impacting 33% of the population due to exposed tubules and collagen degradation.2 Under pathological conditions, the balance of demineralization and remineralization on the surface of dental hard tissue is broken by the metabolism of cariogenic microorganisms or acids from food, triggering irreversible mineral loss, tubule enlargement, and collagen matrix exposure. These changes not only accelerate caries development but also exacerbate DH through hydrodynamic stimuli.3,4 Currently, the application of clinical desensitizers or remineralization agents including fluorides,5 bioactive glass,6 and resin adhesives, often show insufficient biocompatibility and occlusion depth (typically limited to ~10 μm from the tubule orifices), unstable effects under mechanical abrasion or acidic conditions, leading to unsatisfactory long-term efficacy.7,8 To address this challenge, biomimetic remineralization strategies capable of reconstructing both intrafibrillar minerals and tubular occlusion are urgently needed, offering a potential paradigm shift from restorative to regenerative dentistry.

Biomimetic remineralization has emerged as a promising approach to achieve deep dentinal tubules (DTs) occlusion by delivering mineral ions or mineralization templates into dentin tissues to induce in situ mineral formation.9 Physiologically, dentin remineralization is tightly regulated by non-collagenous proteins (NCPs). For instance, dentin matrix protein 1 (DMP-1) and dentin phosphoprotein (DPP) utilize their β-sheet domains to match the hydroxyapatite (HAP) lattice, guiding mineral deposition within collagen fibril gap zones and peritubular dentin.10–12 Intrafibrillar mineralization is critical for dentin mechanical properties restoration and depends on the infiltration and transformation of amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) within collagen fibrils.10,13 Successful biomimetic remineralization of dentin should therefore address both processes: (1) intrafibrillar mineralization to restore collagen’s mechanical integrity and (2) extrafibrillar mineralization to occlude DTs and reduce hypersensitivity.

Intrafibrillar mineralization—the deposition of HAP within collagen fibrils—faces a critical challenge: ACP precursors must first infiltrate the nanoconfined (~2 nm) gap zones of collagen fibrils.13 This process is facilitated by liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), which generates metastable ACP droplets capable of penetrating collagen fibrils via capillary forces.12,14 Unlike classical ion-by-ion crystallization, this non-classical pathway enables HAP deposition within collagen’s confined gap zones.15 The efficiency of intrafibrillar mineralization depends on the ability of NCPs to stabilize calcium and phosphate ions.16 In polyelectrolyte-regulated systems, mineralization requires simultaneous maintenance of electroneutrality and osmotic equilibrium. The establishment of Gibbs-Donnan equilibrium between extrafibrillar and intrafibrillar compartments generates an osmotic driving force, further promoting ACP infiltration and subsequent intrafibrillar mineralization.16,17

Self-assembly peptides form charged aggregates through LLPS in response to environmental changes,18–20 which match the two key links in the mineralization within fibers: (1) LLPS-mediated ACP precursor infiltration through collagen’s gap zones12,14,21 and (2) Gibbs-Donnan equilibrium-driven mineral deposition.16,17 Therefore, the self-assembly of peptide is promising to be applied to achieve the bionic regeneration of dentin.

In this study, based on structural analysis of the self-assembly elastin-like peptide ELP16,22 we designed a GKG linker segment and investigated its ability to provide structural turns for α-helical peptides and facilitate their conformational transition into α-hairpin peptides. This linker was incorporated into TVH-19—a mineral-promoting antimicrobial α-helical peptide previously developed by our group,23 — to engineer a novel α-hairpin peptide, TKH. We subsequently confirmed LLPS of TKH using fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), while transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the formation of uniform nanoparticles via peptide self-assembly. The adsorption affinity of TKH to both collagen and hydroxyapatite (HA) was systematically evaluated. Furthermore, the peptide’s remineralization effects on collagen fibrils and demineralized dentin models were investigated, and the underlying mechanism was explored through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Collectively, our results demonstrate that TKH promotes biomimetic remineralization within collagen fibrils and dentinal tubules through enhanced LLPS and self-assembly, highlighting its potential as a safe and effective peptide-based material for treating dentin hypersensitivity.

The α-hairpin peptide TKH with LLPS propensity showed remarkable self-assembly behavior

Analytical HPLC and mass spectrometry confirmed the successful synthesis of the TKH peptide, showing a purity of 92.83% and a molecular weight of 4 882.40 Da, which aligned with the theoretical value of 4881.83 Da (Figure S1). In the CD spectra, TKH exhibited two characteristic negative bands near 208 nm and 222 nm in SDS solution, accompanied by a positive band around 195 nm, indicating a typical α-helical conformation. In contrast, the CD spectrum of TKH in PBS displayed a broad negative band around 200 nm, suggesting that TKH mainly adopted a random-coil conformation (Fig. 1b). Such environment-dependent conformational transition suggested that PBS may promote TKH intermolecular interactions and partial aggregation of peptides, leading to the formation of dynamic condensates, which corresponds to the former results of LLPS.24

Since LLPS is a key pathway in peptide self-assembly, TKH peptide solution under phosphate-induced conditions was observed under confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) and droplets formation were captured as displayed in Fig. 1c. Furthermore, the dynamic nature of these condensates was verified by FRAP. Selective photobleaching of a 2 μm circular region within an individual Rhodamine 6 G (Rh6G)-labeled TKH droplet resulted in a rapid fluorescence recovery (Fig. 1c), which increased to approximately 87.2% of its original value within 20 s according to the time-dependent recovery curve (Fig. 1d). Rh6G-labeled TKH molecules remained highly mobile inside the droplets, confirming that these condensates maintained a liquid-like internal environment rather than forming static aggregates. Such rapid molecular exchange was characteristic of LLPS-derived assemblies. Partial phase diagram of TKH was obtained through turbidimetry at different concentrations in PBS (Figure S2a), indicating the LLPS tendency of TKH and the necessity of further investigation into its self-assembly property. Consequently, the self-assembled morphologies of TKH and TVH-19 peptides were observed under TEM. As shown in Fig. 1e, both peptides formed spherical nanoparticles with an average diameter of approximately 50 nm.

The ThT fluorescence intensity increased with TKH concentration and was significantly higher than TVH-19 at the same concentrations in PBS (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1f), suggesting that TKH undergoes self-assembly with a greater propensity at higher concentrations. When excited at 380 nm with emission scanning from 400 to 580 nm, TKH showed a stronger fluorescence peak at 480 nm (Figure S2b). The intensity of the Tyndall effect correlated positively with the degree of peptide self-assembly. Under laser light exposure, TKH exhibited a clear Tyndall effect even at a low concentration (3.125 μmol/L) (Figure S2c), which was observed at 50 µmol/L in TVH-19, collectively confirming its superior self-assembly capability.

The adsorption capacity of self-assembly peptide promoted collagen and demineralized dentin remineralization in vitro

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was employed to quantitatively characterize the interaction between the TKH peptide and type I collagen. The typical thermogram exhibited progressively diminishing exothermic peaks upon successive injections, indicating that the binding process between TKH and collagen was strongly exothermic (Fig. 3a). Fitting of the binding isotherm yielded the dissociation constant (Kd = 3.52 × 10−7 M), which demonstrated a high binding affinity between TKH and collagen. Peptide adsorption on dentin surface was illustrated in the FTIR spectra. After peptide TKH coating on the demineralized dentin surface, significant increases were observed in the following characteristic peaks: Amide N–H stretching vibrations (3 006–3 500 cm−1), Amide I band (1 657 cm−1, attributed to C = O stretching), Amide II band (1 545 cm−1), PO₄3− functional group (1 018 cm−1) (Fig. 3c). These spectral changes indicated substantial retention of peptides on the HA surface or exposed collagen matrix. Following deionized water (DDW) washing, the characteristic peaks of TKH showed only a slight reduction compared to TVH-19 (Figure S3b), suggesting better binding affinity between TKH and the dentin collagen matrix.

As shown in Fig. 3d, the difference between the initial and final peptide concentrations reflected the peptide adsorption capacity on HA particles—a larger difference indicated stronger binding affinity. Both the TKH-HA and TVH-19-HA groups showed a significant unbound peptides reduction (P < 0.01) in the supernatant. Over 80% of both peptides were adsorbed onto HA particles (Figure. S3c), confirming the high affinity for HA. While even without mixing with HA particles, the concentration of unbound TKH peptide decreased, potentially due to the self-assembly of TKH into larger molecules in the solution, resulting in partial sedimentation after centrifugation.

In the Ca/P stabilization assay, the control group without any stabilizer exhibited obvious turbidity, with visible CaP deposits observed under microscopy (Figure S3d). Conversely, the TKH peptide at 50 μmol/L effectively stabilized the suspension, with clarified solution after 24 h incubation. No CaP formation was detected microscopically even after centrifugation. TVH-19 peptide achieved comparable stabilization at 400 μmol/L, indicating that TKH has an excellent ability to stabilize calcium and phosphate (Figure S3d). After 5 days of collagen mineralization, collagen fibrils remained unmineralized internally in the DDW group, with only apatite crystals deposited on the surface (extrafibrillar mineralization) (Fig. 3e). The PAH group exhibited intrafibrillar mineralization, characterized by the appearance of periodic banding patterns. The TKH group demonstrated the most effective intrafibrillar mineralization, with minerals aligned orderly along the long axis of the collagen fibrils and displaying typical periodic banding patterns. Extrafibrillar mineral deposition was also observed. The majority of the homogeneous mineral exists exterior to fibrils in TVH-19 group, along with slightly inferior periodic banding patterns (Fig. 3e).

The self-assembly peptide exhibited excellent dentin remineralization effects in vivo

To evaluate the remineralization efficacy of TKH and TVH-19 peptides under oral conditions, an in vivo animal model was established using human dentin slices. The experimental procedure is shown in the Fig. 5a. Acid-etched dentin specimens were fixed to the palatal surface of Sprague–Dawley rats to mimic the oral environment (Fig. 5c). DSSP analysis revealed distinct conformational behaviors between TVH-19 and TKH during both adsorption and remineralization. TVH-19 retained a relatively rigid architecture with only minor loop formation at the termini, indicating limited structural rearrangement upon HA binding. In contrast, TKH displayed pronounced flexibility, particularly around the GKG linker, with frequent transitions between bend and loop states while maintaining its overall hairpin topology (Fig. 6b). The binding energies of peptide–HA interactions (Fig. 6c) were consistently negative, confirming that both peptides could spontaneously adsorb onto the HA (010) surface.

The distance between the peptide and HA centers of mass along the Z-axis (COMz–COMz) was monitored over time (Fig. 6d). Both peptides reached stable adsorption within 10 ns, after which the COMZ fluctuations became limited. To further evaluate conformational stability, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and the residue-based root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) were calculated (Fig. 6e, f). RMSD curves indicated that TVH-19 reached equilibrium at 50 ns, while TKH stabilized earlier, around 25 ns. RMSF analysis showed higher flexibility at the terminal regions of both peptides, with central residues exhibiting lower fluctuations. The RMSF profile of the second TVH-19 segment in TKH closely resembled that of free TVH-19, consistent with their similar adsorption modes mediated by Lys and Arg residues.

Upon addition of Ca²⁺ and H₂PO₄−, TKH not only maintained 2nd TVH-19 stably adsorbed on HA via Lys/Arg residues but also recruited H₂PO₄− through the positively charged residues of its 1st TVH-19. In the interaction energy analysis, the lower energy values between TKH and H₂PO₄−/HA compared to TVH-19 indicated stronger binding affinity between TKH and H₂PO₄−/HA (Fig. 7c). This finding was consistent with previous findings that H₂PO₄⁻ played a vital role in inducing peptide self-assembly. Discussion

Classical ion-mediated crystal nucleation theory was initially proposed to explain collagen intrafibrillar mineralization in bone and dentin.11 However, recent studies on particle-mediated crystallization suggest a non-classical pathway, where prenucleation clusters are formed through the sequestration of calcium and phosphate ions by NCPs or NCPs analogs.13,17,25 The prenucleation clusters, stabilized by biomimetic analogs of NCPs, aggregate into larger liquid-like ACP nanoparticles. Subsequently, these ACP nanoparticles infiltrate the intrafibrillar water compartments of collagen matrix and undergo self-assembly to form metastable crystalline phases. This polymer induced liquid precursor (PILP) is considered to be a key transient state in mineral infiltration into collagen fibrils prior to crystallization.12 LLPS is the basis of PILP theory, as a liquid or liquid-like phase adopts a flexible shape before solidification, which enables effective penetration of minerals into collagen fibrils, thereby promoting intrafibrillar mineralization. The presence of NCPs (DMP1, DSPP) or charged polymers (PASP, PAA, and PAH)—can stabilize ACP and promote the formation of a liquid-like precursor under specific conditions.26–28 This approach has been widely utilized to generate crystals with non-equilibrium morphologies.28–31

Notably, growing evidence indicates that peptide self-assembly can also proceed through LLPS, which serves as a fundamental mechanism in biomolecular self-assembly, contrasting with classical models that involve stepwise monomer-by-monomer addition.32,33 LLPS of homogeneous peptide solutions into a solvent-rich liquid phase and solute-rich droplets represents the initial step prior to self-assembly.18,20,32,33 In order to simulate the mechanism of NCPs driving ACP to penetrate into collagen fibers through PILP pathway, we focused on self-assembled peptides that also underwent LLPS process. Based on analysis of the reported self-assembly peptide ELP16,22 GKG was identified as a key assembly motif exposed on peptide surface, which also contributes to the turn conformation within the ELP structure (Fig. 1a). Specifically, Lys (K) provides functional amino groups that form hydrogen or coordination bonds with adjacent molecules, serving as a cross-linker to promote fibrous assembly. Arg (G) makes the structure of the polypeptide more flexible. Meanwhile, its electrostatic interactions counteract hydrophobic aggregation among ELP16 molecules, regulating peptide self-assembly.22 In this study, incorporation of GKG linker promoted the formation of a novel helix-turn-helix α-hairpin structure, which exhibits features of both α-helix and β-turn. While β-turn structure has long been considered fundamental to peptide self-assembly and the formation of higher-order structures such as fibers or nanoparticles,34,35 our findings on TKH demonstrate that α-hairpin structure regulated by GKG linker provides more possibilities for the peptide self-assembly. The direct microscopic visualization of droplets, FRAP-validated molecular dynamics and the turbidity-derived partial phase diagram collectively indicated that TKH undergoes LLPS under phosphate-containing conditions, providing a mechanistic basis for its subsequent self-assembly behavior. Although the GKG linker significantly enhanced LLPS behavior of TKH in this study, its general applicability as a universal self-assembly–promoting motif remains to be experimentally validated by circular dichroism and molecular dynamics simulations. Further investigations using different peptides are needed to confirm whether the GKG linker exerts similar effects across broader peptide systems.

The result of CD spectra further revealed that the conformational landscapes of TKH were strongly solvent-dependent, consistent with the reported behaviors of amphiphilic peptides.36 In previous study, TVH-19 also adopted α-helical structures in SDS solution.37 While TKH exhibited a far more pronounced structural reorganization in PBS compared to TVH-19 (Fig. 1b). Such a folding transition is further amplified in PBS, where PO₄³⁻ and ionic strength may promote intermolecular contacts and early-stage clustering, significantly increasing the peptide’s ability to self-assembly, as evidenced by ThT fluorescence assays and Tyndall effect experiments (Fig. 1f, Figure S2b, c).

The antimicrobial efficacy of peptide primarily depends on the α-helical monomer conformation and its positive charge. Although TKH exhibited a weaker antibacterial effect than TVH-19, this trend is mechanistically reasonable. TVH-19 functions as a monomeric antimicrobial peptide under acidic conditions, where its amphipathic α-helical conformation facilitates membrane disruption. In contrast, This enhancing self-assembly tendency of TKH might reduce its effective monomeric concentration and impedes its ability to insert into bacterial membranes, resulting in reduced antibacterial potency. However, such assembly-driven behavior enhances its mineralization performance, as multivalent interactions allow TKH to more effectively stabilize calcium–phosphate precursors and promote intrafibrillar mineralization. This trade-off between monomeric antibacterial activity and assembly-mediated mineralization efficacy should therefore be considered an intrinsic property of TKH’s design.

Furthermore, the thermodynamic parameters from the ITC experiments demonstrate the high affinity between TKH and collagen. It is therefore proposed that TKH interact with type I collagen primarily through their positively charged Lys/Arg residues, forming multiple hydrogen bonds and electrostatic linkages with surface carboxyl and hydroxyl groups of collagen. Such interactions likely stabilize the adsorption of TKH on collagen fibrils at the molecular level, providing a favorable local environment for subsequent peptide droplet accumulation and intrafibrillar mineral nucleation. MD analysis indicates that more amino acid residues in TKH are involved in adsorption to the HA surface (Fig. 6b), and the decrease in interaction energy further confirms a high degree of binding stability between TKH and HA (Fig. 6d). In the adsorption experiments on demineralized dentin surface, FTIR analysis showed that TKH exhibits more stable adsorption behavior on the dentin surface (Fig. 3c, Figure S3b), consistent with the MD simulation. The adsorption capacity to hydroxyapatite and exposed collagen on demineralized dentin surfaces underlies TKH application in dentin remineralization.

The enhanced self-assembly capability enables TKH to stabilize Ca and P, which aligns with the process of NCPs-mediated intrafibrillar mineralization under physiological conditions. In collagen fiber remineralization experiments, TKH induced significant intrafibrillar mineralization at a lower concentration (100 μmol/L), with less extrafibrillar mineral nucleation compared to the DDW group and TVH-19—which exhibits lower capacity of Ca/P stabilization (Figure S3d). Consequently, the TKH group exhibited distinct periodic banding patterns within collagen fibers, indicative of well-organized mineral deposition. It was attributed to the ability of TKH to stabilize calcium and phosphate ions outside collagen fibers and facilitate the formation of ACP precursors, and maintain osmotic balance and electrical neutrality between the interior and exterior of collagen fibers, thereby facilitating the infiltration of ACP into intrafibrillar gaps and enabling intrafibrillar mineralization, achieved through its self-assembly into a polyelectrolyte-like charged macromolecular structure. Moreover, MD simulations of the remineralization process revealed a strong attractive interaction between TKH and H₂PO₄− (Fig. 7c), corroborating its role in ACP stabilization and enhanced remineralization. These computational results align well with the significant remineralization effects observed in the demineralized dentin model. Based on its strong adsorption capacity to demineralized dentin surfaces and its ability to induce intrafibrillar mineralization of type I collagen, TKH demonstrated effective mineralization induction in demineralized dentin model in vitro. After remineralization treatment with TKH, calcium and phosphorus deposition was observed by EDS on the dentin surface, with mineral precipitation evident within DTs (Fig. 4a). XRD analysis further confirmed the presence of HA diffraction peaks (Figure S4b). Finally, TKH also exhibited favorable biosafety and mineralization-inducing effects, even under the more complex conditions of the oral environment in vivo (Fig. 5d). In conclusion, this study successfully engineered a novel α-hairpin peptide, TKH, through the strategic incorporation of a flexible GKG linker. This design significantly enhanced its capacity for LLPS and self-assembly, facilitating efficient biomimetic mineralization process that mimics the function of non-collagenous proteins. Although clinical efficacy and detailed mechanisms require further validation, TKH has shown its therapeutic potential by effectively promoting intrafibrillar remineralization and achieving deep dentinal tubule occlusion, alongside its inherent antibacterial properties. This peptide system establishes a novel biomimetic strategy, transforming the therapeutic paradigm for dentin from passive restoration to active regeneration.

Materials & methods

Peptide self-assembly property analysis

Peptide structure analysis

To investigate the structural modulatory effect of the GKG motif on peptides, α-helical peptides ranging from 11 to 37 amino acids were selected (Table S1).38–42 Both the α-helical monomers and the helix-GKG-helix constructs were first subjected to structural prediction using AlphaFold 3.43 Subsequent structural visualization and analysis were performed with ChimeraX 1.10.1.44

Peptide synthesis

Peptides TKH (TKRQQVVGLLWHLLHHLLHGKGTKRQQVVGLLWHLLHHLLH), TVH-19 (TKRQQVVGLLWHLLHHLLH) were synthesized through solid-phase Fmoc-based chemistry by Bintuo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). The synthesized peptides were subsequently purified (>90%) by high-performance liquid chromatography and stored at −20 °C.

Circular dichroism (CD)

CD spectra were obtained in the range of 190–260 nm at room temperature using a CD spectrometer (Chirascan, Applied photo physics, England). The peptide TKH solution was diluted with 4.2 mmol/L PBS, 25 mmol/L SDS buffer (pH 7.0) to final concentration at 200 μmol/L. The measurements were conducted after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C. All spectra were obtained using a 2 nm bandwidth and a 1 nm/s scanning rate and were normalized to mean residual ellipticity values.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)

The TKH peptide solution (5 mg/mL) was prepared in 10 mmol/L PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The peptide was labeled with the fluorescent dye Rh6G (Solarbio, IR2030) at a final concentration of 10 μmol/L, followed by incubation in a shaking incubator at 37 °C and 200 r/min for 1–2 h.32 After sonication for 10 min, the sample was applied onto a glass slide and allowed to equilibrate in the dark for 15 min. TKH solution (5 mg/mL) and 10 μmol/L Rh6G were prepared in parallel as the control. Droplet formation was observed using confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Olympus FV3000, Japan). A circular region with a radius of 1 μm was photobleached using a 555 nm laser at 100% intensity with 10 iterations. Fluorescence recovery in the bleached area was subsequently monitored using the same wavelength. Data acquisition was performed using Zen Blue software (Zeiss). For data normalization, the pre-bleach fluorescence intensity was set as 100%, while the first post-bleach measurement was set as 0%. The relative fluorescence recovery rate was calculated according to the equation\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,{I}_{{norm}}\left(t\right)=\frac{I\left(t\right)-{I}_{{bleached}}}{{I}_{{pre}-{bleach}}-{I}_{{bleached}}}$$\end{document}Inormt=It−IbleachedIpre−bleach−Ibleached. The final FRAP recovery curve represents the average of recovery curves obtained from three independent samples.

Phase diagram construction via turbidity assay

Buffer systems with pH values of 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, and 7.5 were prepared using 20 mmol/L Tris, 0.2 mol/L NaCl, and 10 mmol/L PBS. TKH peptide solutions were then prepared in these buffers at final concentrations of 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 mmol/L. The turbidity of each solution was measured in triplicate (n = 3) using a 96-well plate at λ = 350 nm under varying pH and concentration conditions. The phase transition pH corresponding to different concentrations were plotted on a concentration–pH coordinate system to generate experimental data sets.45

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TKH and TVH-19 peptides were dissolved in 10 mmol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to a final concentration of 200 μmol/L and allowed to equilibrate at room temperature for 2 h. The peptide solutions were then deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids. After the samples were dried. high-resolution TEM images were acquired using transmission electron microscope (JEM-2100 Plus, JEOL, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

Thioflavin T (ThT) spectroscopy assay

TKH/TVH-19 peptide solutions with concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μmol/L were prepared in PBS and mixed with a 10 μg/mL ThT solution. After incubation at room temperature for 10 min, the fluorescence intensity was measured using an Infinite M200 PRO plate reader (Tecan) with λex = 438 nm and λem = 495 nm. Similarly, different concentrations peptide solutions were prepared, and the ThT fluorescence intensity was measured with λex = 380 nm and λem = 400–580 nm. The fluorescence values were normalized to the ThT fluorescence intensity of the PBS group. Each sample was done in triplicate.

Tyndall effect detection

The Tyndall effect of different concentrations of TKH (0–200 μmol/L) and TVH-19 (0–400 μmol/L) prepared with 10 mmol/L PBS was detected by shedding light on the tubules using a small laser light.

Antibacterial properties of self-assembly peptides

Crystal violet assay

48-well plates were pre-coated with human saliva (37 °C, 2 h) and UV-sterilized to form a mature acquired pellicle. Biofilms of S. mutans (1 × 10⁴ CFU/mL in BHIS) were grown anaerobically for 24 h in the presence of TKH or TVH-19 peptides (2.5–40 μmol/L, prepared with DDW). After incubation, biofilms were washed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Biofilm formation was qualitatively assessed by microscopy (OLYMPUS SZX16) and quantified by measuring the OD595 of the dissolved dye after elution with 33% glacial acetic acid.

pH drop assay

The pH drop assay was used to evaluate the peptide effects on metabolic activity of S. mutans biofilms. Acquired pellicle-coated 12-well plates were inoculated with 1 mL of S. mutans suspension (1 × 104 CFU/mL) in BHIS medium and incubated anaerobically for 24 h to form biofilms. After removing the supernatant, biofilms were gently rinsed three times with PBS to remove residual medium. Subsequently, 2 mL of 10 mmol/L glucose solution (PBS-based, pH = 7.0) containing different concentrations of TKH/TVH-19 peptides was added to each well. The pH changes were monitored and recorded at 30-min interval.

Demineralized dentin specimen preparation

Approved by the Ethics Review Committee of West China School of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2022-117), the extracted caries-free human third molars were gathered at West China Hospital of Stomatology and stored in the PBS solution containing 0.5% thymol at 4 °C. Dentin disks (3.50 mm ± 0.50 mm thick) were sectioned from the crowns near the cementoenamel junction using a water-cooled, low-speed diamond saw (EXAKT, Germany) and embedded in acrylic resin. The surfaces were then polished sequentially with 800- to 3 000-grit carbide paper (Struers, Denmark) and ultrasonically cleaned for 5 min to remove the smear layer. Specimens with a Vickers microhardness (25-gf load, 10 s dwell time) between 0.60 and 0.80 GPa, determined from five indentations per disk, were selected. A 4 × 4 mm² window was exposed on each specimen’s surface by coating the surrounding area with acid-resistant nail varnish. Demineralization was performed by immersing the specimens in 0.5 mol/L EDTA (pH 8.0) for 30 min. Finally, the demineralized dentin specimens were ultrasonically cleaned and stored in PBS at 4 °C until use.

Characterization of the binding capacity of self-assembly peptides

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

ITC was performed to quantify the thermodynamic parameters of the interaction between TKH peptide and type I collagen using a Nano ITC microcalorimeter (TA Instruments, USA) at 25 °C. All samples were dissolved in the same buffer (20 mmol/L HEPES, 150 mmol/L NaCl, pH 7.4) and degassed under vacuum for 10 min prior to use. The TKH peptide solution (1 μmol/L) was loaded into the sample cell, while the type I collagen solution (10 μmol/L) was placed in the titration syringe. A total of 20 injections (2 μL each) were performed at 200 s intervals with a stirring speed of 300 r/min. The heat flow (μJ/s) versus time was recorded, integrated, and corrected for baseline to obtain the heat released per injection. The resulting binding isotherm was fitted using a one-site binding model in NanoAnalyze software (TA Instruments) to calculate thermodynamic parameters (Kd is the dissociation constant, N is the number of TKH that bind to a single collagen molecule). Based on the equation ΔG = RTlnKd = ΔH − TΔS, where R is the gas constant, T is the absolute temperature (in Kelvin), the values of ΔG and ΔH can thus be determined.46

Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) analysis

The demineralized dentin specimens were coated using 100 μL of TKH (100 μmol/L) and TVH-19 (200 μmol/L) solution (prepared with 10 mmol/L PBS) and air-dried at room temperature after 1 h treatment. The FTIR spectra of demineralized dentin specimens, TKH/TVH-19-coated dentin specimens and dentin specimens rinsed with DDW after coating were obtained by ATR-FTIR analysis (Nicolet i50; Thermo Scientific, USA).

The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay

The BCA method was used to test the adsorption amount of TKH/TVH-19 on HA particles (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). 200 μL TKH/TVH-19 peptide solutions (0.5 mg/mL, 1 mg/mL, prepared with DDW) were mixed with or without HA particles (20 mg per tube) in each group (n = 3).47 Tubes were placed at room temperature for 12 h for complete reaction. Tubes containing the peptide solution and HA were centrifuged (13,000 rpm, 3 min) and the supernatant was collected to detect the unbound peptide concentration using the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime).

CaP stabilization assay

Mineralization media containing 4.5 mmol/L CaCl₂, 2.1 mmol/L Na₂HPO₄, and varying concentrations of TKH (3.125–200 μmol/L) or TVH-19 (6.25–400 μmol/L) peptides were prepared in DDW by sequential addition of phosphate and peptide stocks to the calcium stock. Following a 24 h incubation at 37 °C, turbidity was visually assessed. The precipitates obtained by centrifugation (4 000 r/min, 5 min) were microscopically examined for calcium phosphate crystals.

Collagen mineralization

Type I collagen from rat tails (Sigma-Aldrich, C7661) was dissolved in 0.1 mol/L acetic acid to prepare a stock solution (3 mg/mL) and stored at 4 °C. For collagen reconstitution, a glycine buffer (50 mmol/L glycine, 200 mmol/L KCl, pH 9.2) was freshly prepared. A working solution (50 μg/mL) was obtained by mixing 16.7 μL of the collagen stock with 983.3 μL of the glycine buffer, followed by vortexing and incubation at room temperature for 20 min.48 Subsequently, 14 μL aliquots of the reconstituted collagen solution were applied to nickel grids placed in a six-well plate. Humidity was maintained by adding DDW to the vacant wells. The plate was sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The grids were then washed with DDW, fixed on droplets of 0.5 wt% glutaraldehyde (200 μL) for 2 h, washed again, and air-dried. For mineralization, the collagen-coated grids were incubated at 37 °C for 5 days in a humidified chamber while floating on one of the following solutions: 200 μg/mL PAH (positive control), 100 μmol/L TKH, 200 μmol/L TVH-19, or DDW (negative control). Finally, the grids were rinsed with DDW, air-dried, and examined by transmission electron microscopy (JEM-2100 Plus, JEOL, Japan).

Dentin remineralization in vitro

The demineralized dentin samples were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 15), and the window surfaces were treated with the following agents: the negative control group: PBS; the positive control group: 1 000×10-6 NaF; the peptide groups: TKH (100 μmol/L), TVH-19 (200 μmol/L). Each specimen was exposed to the above-mentioned solutions for 1 h once a day. Then, they were rinsed three times with DDW, followed by immersion in artificial saliva (AS) (0.5 mmol/L CaCl2, 0.9 mmol/L KH2PO4, 130 mmol/L KCl, 20 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.0, each specimen for 7 mL) in a 37 °C incubator for 4 weeks.49 The AS (pH 7.0) was freshly prepared daily.

SEM and EDS mapping analysis

The dentin specimens were immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 4 h at room temperature, rinsed 3 times with PBS solution, and then dehydrated with gradients of ethanol in different volume fractions (30, 50, 70, 90, 100%) for 30 min each time. The specimens were sprayed with gold, and the structures and compositions of the occlusal and longitudinal sections of the dentin specimens were observed by SEM (Apreo S, FEI, Eindhoven, The Netherlands). The content and distribution of calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) were determined by EDS elemental mapping.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

After 4 weeks, specimens of four groups were removed from AS and air dried for XRD (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan) analyses. At the same time, sound dentin and demineralized dentin disks were used as controls.

Dentin remineralization in vivo

All animal experiments complied with the Chinese Council on Animal Care guidelines and received ethical approval from the West China College of Stomatology Animal Experimentation Committee (WCHSIRB-D-2022-215). Human dentin specimens were processed into oval-shaped slices (4 mm diameter × 2 mm thickness) with smooth edge (Fig. 5b). The samples were demineralized by etching with 37% phosphoric acid for 15–20 s, followed by ultrasonic cleaning for 15 min. Two holes were symmetrically drilled on both sides of each slice to facilitate fixation in the rat oral cavity. Specific pathogen-free Sprague-Dawley rats (6-8 weeks old, 200-300 g body weight, n = 8 for each group, Chengdu Dashuo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.)) were selected for the study. A 0.50 mm stainless steel ligature wire was inserted through the interproximal space between the first and second molar and anchored to the anterior teeth, securing the dentin sample to the rat palate.9,50 Excess wire was trimmed, and sharp edges were coated with resin to prevent mucosal injury. Rats were randomly divided into four groups and treated daily for 28 days with: PBS (negative control), 1 000 ppm NaF (positive control), 100 μmol/L TKH, or 200 μmol/L TVH-19 (100 μL applied locally). Body weight was monitored, and a soft diet was provided with water withheld for 1 h post-treatment. After 28 days, dentin samples were collected for SEM/EDS analysis. Major organs and oral tissues were harvested for histological examination (H&E staining), and blood samples were taken for hematological analysis.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of self-assembly peptides on dentin adsorption and remineralization

The initial structures of peptides TKH and TVH-19 were predicted using AlphaFold 3. Since HAP is the primary inorganic component of dentin and the acid-etched dentin surface predominantly exposes the (010) crystal plane, the HAP (010) surface was selected as the target model.51 The HAP surface model was constructed using CHARMM-GUI, after which TKH and TVH-19 peptides were individually placed on the HAP surface to generate the initial adsorption configurations (Figure S6). MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2019.1, with two systems established: (1) the peptide–HA adsorption system; (2) the peptide–HA remineralization system. In the remineralization simulations, ions were added according to the experimental remineralization solution (2.58 mmol/L CaCl₂·2H₂O, 1.55 mmol/L KH₂PO₄, 10 mg/L NaF, 180 mmol/L NaCl, and 50 mmol/L Tris–HCl, pH 7.6).52 Specifically, 65 Ca²⁺, 40 H₂PO₄−, and 6 F− ions were inserted into the equilibrated adsorption structure, and additional Cl⁻ ions were introduced to neutralize the system. This setup ensured consistency between the simulated ionic environment and the actual remineralization conditions. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method, followed by equilibration under NVT and NPT ensembles. Temperature was controlled at 298 K via the V-rescale thermostat, and semi-isotropic pressure coupling was maintained at 1 bar (Z direction) and 0 bar (X/Y direction) using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat. The production simulations for both adsorption and remineralization systems were each run for 100 ns. Interaction energies between peptides and (i) HAP surface, (ii) Ca²⁺ ions, and (iii) H₂PO₄− ions were computed using GROMACS energy modules, and the average binding energies were obtained from the equilibrated trajectories. Structural stability and conformational changes were analyzed through RMSD, RMSF, and DSSP secondary structure calculations. Visualization and data analysis were performed using Pymol and Matplotlib, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated at least 3 times. All results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 9.5 and Origin 2024 software were used to perform statistical analyses. Statistical analyses between two groups were performed using Student’s t-tests. Differences among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was accepted at P < 0.05.
