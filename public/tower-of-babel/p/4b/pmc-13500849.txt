B-type host stars revealed by exoplanets around massive secondary-clump giant stars
Authors: Wen-Xu Lin, Sheng-Bang Qian, Qi-Bin Sun, Li-Ying Zhu, Wen-Ping Liao, Ting Li, Fu-Xing Li, Liang Liu, Xiang-Dong Shi, LinJia Li, Ergang Zhao, Huiting Zhang, Ping Li, Jing Li, Ming Lian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exoplanets around massive young stars, including hot spectral-class B (B-type) stars, are rare but important for understanding how planets form and survive. Most known examples are either very distant companions found by direct imaging or extremely close-in planets found by transit surveys, leaving planets at intermediate orbital distances largely unexplored. Here, we show that this missing population can be revealed through evolved stars. Using space-based stellar oscillations and stellar-evolution models, we identify four known planet-hosting catalogue stars, HD 2952, HD 120084, HIP 65891, and HIP 67537 (Henry Draper and Hipparcos catalogue identifiers), as massive helium-burning giant stars. We reconstruct their birth properties and find that all four were originally B-type stars hosting giant planets at intermediate orbital distances. Orbital-evolution calculations show that the present systems can survive the expansion of their host stars after the main sequence. These systems uncover a hidden population of planets around B-type stars and extend tests of planet formation and survival into a previously inaccessible regime.

Introduction

Massive, early-type stars (hot O-, B-, and A-type stars) are known to host companions at the most extreme ends of parameter space, yet planets around bona fide B-type dwarfs (main-sequence stars of spectral class B) at moderate orbital separations remain largely unexplored. At the close-in extreme, the ultra-hot Jupiter KELT-9 b, discovered by the Kilodegree Extremely Little Telescope (KELT) survey, orbits a late-B/early-A star and reaches dayside temperatures of about 4600 K, offering a striking laboratory for atmospheric escape under intense irradiation; here “ultra-hot Jupiter” refers to a strongly irradiated gas-giant planet on a very short-period orbit, but it probes only the innermost tail of the orbital distribution1. In standard exoplanet naming, the lowercase suffix “b” denotes the first reported planetary or substellar companion orbiting a host star; subsequent companions are typically assigned “c”, “d”, and so on. At the opposite extreme, direct-imaging surveys of young B stars have revealed massive substellar companions on very wide orbits, including κ Andromedae b (κ And b; about 13 MJup at tens of au), HIP 78530 b (20–25MJup at about 700 au), and b Centauri (AB) b (about 11 MJup beyond 500 au), tracing formation in massive protoplanetary disks2–4. Greek letters in stellar names, such as κ (kappa) in κ Andromedae and μ (mu) in μ2 Sco, are Bayer designations: historical labels assigned to stars within a constellation, often roughly following apparent brightness rather than representing a physical parameter. Bayer designations can also use Latin letters, as in the leading “b” of b Centauri; this is distinct from the final companion suffix in b Centauri (AB) b. Here, MJup denotes Jupiter mass and au denotes astronomical unit. The B-star Exoplanet Abundance STudy (BEAST) survey has further reported substellar companions around μ2 Sco, reinforcing the conclusion that massive hosts can assemble giant companions under favorable conditions5.

Between these extremes lies a conspicuous demographic gap at a few to a few tens of astronomical units. This regime is precisely where traditional detection techniques are least effective for B-type main-sequence stars: rapid rotation, line broadening, pulsations, and stellar activity degrade radial-velocity precision and suppress transit detectability, while direct imaging rapidly loses sensitivity at intermediate separations6–8. The debated case of HD 100546 b illustrates these challenges vividly, with thermal-infrared detections subsequently challenged by optical and near-infrared analyses that favor disk-related scattered light rather than a bona fide protoplanet9,10. Filling this gap is critical for understanding planet formation and migration in high-mass disks, because planet occurrence rates, architectures, and survival pathways depend sensitively on stellar mass.

Evolved descendants of massive stars provide an indirect route to planetary systems whose B-type main-sequence hosts are observationally difficult to study. Among these evolved phases, the massive core-helium-burning secondary-clump stage provides a favorable evolutionary anchor: stellar luminosities and interior structures are relatively stable on million-year (Myr) timescales11, and for 2–3 M⊙ stars (where M⊙ denotes solar mass) the core-helium-burning phase typically persists for about 102 Myr, compared to only a few Myr for brief transitional phases immediately after the red-giant branch (RGB). This stability reduces degeneracies when present-day stellar parameters are mapped back to initial conditions. At the same time, such systems cannot be treated as isolated secondary-clump snapshots. Before reaching core-helium burning, the hosts ascend the RGB and pass through the tip of the red-giant branch (TRGB), during which rapid stellar expansion can enhance tidal dissipation and threaten close-in planets. For planets at moderate separations, however, tidal decay is expected to be comparatively weak, while stellar mass loss mainly drives adiabatic orbital expansion12–16.

Asteroseismology plays a critical supporting role in this approach by providing robust evolutionary classification, enabling secondary-clump stars to be cleanly distinguished from hydrogen-shell-burning RGB and subgiant populations17–19. This seismic classification is essential because reconstructing zero-age main-sequence (ZAMS) progenitors for evolved, massive planet hosts requires an observationally constrained present-day evolutionary state. Large, vetted exoplanet catalogues such as the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Exoplanet Archive provide the parent samples of confirmed planetary systems and stellar properties20,21, while uniform space-based photometry from the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) mission and its Science Processing Operations Center (SPOC) pipeline enables homogeneous asteroseismic measurements for bright evolved stars22,23.

Here, we show that four known planet-hosting giants, HD 2952, HD 120084, HIP 65891, and HIP 67537, are massive secondary-clump stars whose ZAMS progenitors are B-type hosts of giant planets at intermediate orbital separations; HD and HIP denote identifiers from the Henry Draper and Hipparcos stellar catalogues, respectively. Using TESS asteroseismology, homogeneous radial-velocity re-analysis, stellar-evolution modeling, and planetary orbital integrations, we reconstruct their present evolutionary states, birth properties, and orbital histories. These systems reveal a hidden population of planets around B-type stars and demonstrate that such planets can survive post-main-sequence evolution to the observed secondary-clump stage.

Results

Asteroseismic evolutionary classification

We focus on four evolved, massive planet-hosting stars that show clear solar-like oscillations: HD 2952, HD 120084, HIP 65891, and HIP 67537. All four hosts occupy the intermediate- to high-mass regime at their current evolutionary stage ( > 2.0 M⊙). To establish their current evolutionary states, we used asteroseismic mixed-mode diagnostics. From the space-based light curves, we isolated the dipole mixed-mode oscillation forests and applied the period-stretching technique to directly recover the dipole gravity-mode (g-mode) period spacing, ΔΠ1, from the power spectra. When combined with the large frequency separation Δν (the spacing between consecutive radial pressure modes), this seismic information provides a robust and well-tested discriminator between core-helium-burning stars, including classical red-clump and secondary-clump stars, and hydrogen-shell-burning red-giant-branch or subgiant stars, as demonstrated in large samples from the NASA Kepler mission19,24.

All four hosts exhibit clear and unambiguous dipole mixed modes, placing them firmly within the secondary-clump locus in the ΔΠ1–Δν plane (Fig. 1). Radial-velocity surveys are strongly biased against early-type main-sequence stars because rapid rotation and line broadening degrade Doppler precision. As a result, giant-planet companions to 2–3 M⊙ stars are preferentially detected after the hosts have evolved off the main sequence. ZAMS reconstruction and B-type progenitors

With the present-day fundamental parameters and the seismic evolutionary classification in hand, we reconstructed the prior histories of the four host stars using the Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (MESA) stellar-evolution code within the MESA Isochrones and Stellar Tracks (MIST) framework33–39. For each target, we constructed a two-dimensional grid in the free parameters of initial mass Minit and global metallicity [M/H], while adopting a fixed and internally consistent set of input physics provided by MIST.

Along each evolutionary track, we restricted the likelihood evaluation to post–red-giant-branch models compatible with a core-helium-burning secondary-clump evolutionary state. Model predictions were compared to the observed effective temperature, stellar radius, and surface gravity using a multivariate Gaussian likelihood that accounts for the full covariance among these quantities. To classify the main-sequence progenitors, we adopt a conservative, temperature-based criterion. In standard Morgan–Keenan (MK) spectral-type calibrations, the transition between A- and B-type dwarfs occurs near Teff = 104 K, while B-type stars span a broad temperature range extending above 2 × 104 K 40–42. Because ZAMS spectra cannot be reconstructed for evolved stars, we rely solely on the inferred Teff,ZAMS and classify progenitors with Teff,ZAMS > 104 K as B-type. Under this definition, all four host stars are identified as B-type dwarfs at ZAMS. Based on the inferred ZAMS effective temperatures rather than present-day stellar masses, all four hosts are consistent with late B-type progenitors on the main sequence, according to standard spectral-type and temperature calibrations40,41.

Planet survival through giant-branch evolution

Beyond the reconstruction of stellar progenitors, we also examine planetary orbital evolution as a consistency check on the inferred histories. As the host stars evolve toward the secondary clump, tidal dissipation within the star–planet system acts to shrink the semi-major axis and damp eccentricity; this effect strengthens markedly during giant-branch expansion12–14. By contrast, stellar mass loss tends to expand planetary orbits (adiabatically when the loss is slow), and can partially offset or even dominate tidal decay depending on the evolutionary phase and system parameters15,16. We therefore integrate an orbital-evolution model along the fiducial stellar track to test whether the observed planets can be reproduced through continuous co-evolution from the ZAMS to the present configuration under tides and mass loss. Alternative channels (e.g., dynamical capture scenarios discussed in the literature) have been proposed in other contexts, but are not required for the interpretation adopted here43,44.

We modeled planetary orbital evolution by coupling tidal dissipation, stellar mass loss, and wind drag to the best-fitting MESA tracks of the four hosts. The integrations show that, within the parameter space explored here, stellar evolution induces only modest changes in the planets’ orbits (see Figs. 2d–5d). Physically, the dominant equilibrium tide in convective envelopes scales steeply with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${({R}_{\star }/a)}^{8}$$\end{document}(R⋆/a)8, so tidal decay is weak at the observed separations unless the stellar radius grows to a substantial fraction of the semi-major axis; orbital eccentricity is damped by the same process12–14.

For stars with initial masses near and above 2 M⊙, core helium ignites non-degenerately on the upper RGB and the system avoids a violent helium flash, limiting the most extreme radius excursions that would otherwise drive rapid inspiral24,45. In addition, the post–main-sequence evolutionary timescales of such higher-mass stars are comparatively short, so wind-driven mass loss and associated drag have limited time to substantially reshape the orbits; when mass loss proceeds slowly, the adiabatic response is outward expansion that can partially offset tidal decay15,16.

Taken together, our integrations indicate that the planets’ semi-major axes and eccentricities remain close to their present-day values throughout the hosts’ evolution to the secondary clump, supporting the plausibility of continuous star-planet co-evolution from the ZAMS to the observed configurations for all four systems.

Discussion

Placing the hosts’ ZAMS effective temperatures together with the planets’ ZAMS orbital periods into the census of all confirmed systems (Fig. 6) shows that these four B-type host-planet systems extend the period-temperature plane into a region that has remained largely unexplored. In particular, they occupy the intermediate-orbital-separation regime around B-type progenitors, lying between the ultra-close-in systems accessible to transit surveys and the wide-separation companions detected by direct imaging. Crucially, for planets at moderate separations around B-type progenitors, evolved descendants such as secondary-clump hosts currently provide a powerful observational route for anchoring the zero-age main-sequence properties of the host stars. Direct detections of planets around bona fide B-type dwarfs in this regime remain prohibitively difficult owing to rapid stellar rotation, broadened spectral lines, and the loss of sensitivity in both radial-velocity and transit techniques. By contrast, asteroseismology enables precise evolutionary classification and parameter inference for evolved stars, allowing their present-day properties to be robustly traced back to the ZAMS.

Among the four systems studied here, HD 2952 is particularly illustrative. In the seismic diagnostic diagram, it lies well separated from the secondary-clump locus, and its acceptable evolutionary tracks indicate a late core-helium-burning phase approaching the asymptotic giant branch (AGB). Nevertheless, its reconstructed ZAMS properties place it firmly above the canonical A/B temperature boundary, demonstrating that even stars that have undergone substantial post-main-sequence evolution can retain clear imprints of their B-type progenitor phase.

These systems therefore constitute an observational legacy of planets originally hosted by B-type stars. By anchoring the ZAMS properties of massive progenitors at intermediate orbital separations, they provide empirical constraints on planet formation and migration in massive, luminous disks—conditions under which disk temperatures, scale heights, and migration timescales differ fundamentally from those around lower-mass stars. In particular, they test whether giant planets can assemble and persist at several tenths to a few astronomical units around 2–3 M⊙ stars without undergoing dramatic orbital reshaping during subsequent stellar evolution25,46,47.

Future facilities will substantially expand this framework. The PLAnetary Transits and Oscillations of stars (PLATO) mission will deliver homogeneous stellar masses and ages from space-based asteroseismology for bright targets, enabling mass-stratified planet demographics across a wide range of stellar progenitors48. The Chinese Space Station Survey Telescope (CSST) will provide wide-field optical imaging and slitless spectroscopy, and its planned Cool Planet Imaging Coronagraph will add sensitivity to giant planets at intermediate separations around hot stars49. Together, these advances will transform evolved B-type progenitor systems from rare case studies into a quantitative laboratory for testing how stellar mass, disk physics, and stellar evolution jointly shape planetary architectures.

Stellar evolution models

We computed stellar evolutionary tracks for each source using the MESA (version r11701) code within the framework of the MIST models33–39. The MIST framework adopts a calibrated and internally consistent set of input physics suitable for intermediate- and high-mass stars, including updated opacities, convective overshooting, and mass-loss prescriptions. Starting from the publicly available MIST grids, we constructed for each target a two-dimensional grid in initial mass Minit and global metallicity [M/H]. The grid is sampled with uniform resolutions of ΔMinit = 0.01 M⊙ and Δ[M/H] = 0.01 dex, and its boundaries are chosen to bracket the parameter ranges suggested by independent observational constraints. The explored domain in the (Minit, [M/H]) plane for each target is illustrated by the likelihood heat maps in the corresponding stellar-evolution figures. All other input physics and numerical controls are kept fixed across the grid and are documented in the inlist files accompanying each source.

For each evolutionary track, we restrict the likelihood evaluation to models that have evolved beyond the tip of the red-giant branch (TRGB). The motivation for this choice is not to enforce a strict classification of the evolutionary phase, but to ensure that the comparison with the observations is performed only on post-RGB models that are physically compatible with the secondary-clump properties inferred from asteroseismology. Operationally, along each evolutionary track we identify TRGB as the epoch at which the stellar luminosity reaches its maximum during the RGB ascent. All model points following this epoch are retained for the likelihood evaluation, encompassing the core-helium-burning phase and immediately subsequent post-RGB evolution.

The observables entering the stellar-model likelihood are the effective temperature Teff, stellar radius R, and surface gravity łog glogg. The likelihood-weighted uncertainties derived above quantify the propagation of observational errors within a fixed stellar-evolution framework. To assess systematic effects associated with stellar rotation, we do not repeat the full likelihood analysis on an equally fine rotating grid, which would require substantially greater computational resources. Instead, we adopt the rotating MIST models with an initial rotation rate of v/vcrit = 0.4, where vcrit is the critical break-up velocity, and evaluate the resulting offsets in the ZAMS parameters relative to the non-rotating models.

These rotation-induced differences are quantified at the ZAMS level and propagated to the accepted non-rotating models through interpolation, providing an estimate of the systematic uncertainty associated with stellar rotation. The resulting offsets are incorporated into the final error budget reported in Table 1. We note that stellar-evolution models may be affected by additional sources of systematic uncertainty, such as uncertainties in input physics or calibration choices; in this work, such effects are mitigated by adopting the widely used and internally consistent MIST framework, while only the impact of stellar rotation is explicitly quantified. A detailed breakdown of the rotation-induced systematics is provided in the Supplementary Information (see Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Table 2).

In the MK classification system, spectral types are formally defined by optical spectral features rather than by a single physical parameter. For main-sequence stars, however, effective temperature provides a robust and widely used proxy for spectral type. Standard calibrations show that the boundary between A- and B-type dwarfs lies near Teff = 104 K, with A0 V stars having Teff = 9.7 kilokelvin (kK) and B9.5 V stars Teff = 10.4 kK, while B-type stars as a class span a much broader temperature range extending above 3 × 104 K66.

Because ZAMS spectra cannot be reconstructed for evolved stars, a strict spectral classification is not feasible in this context. We therefore adopt a conservative, temperature-based criterion and classify a star as B-type at ZAMS when its inferred zero-age main-sequence temperature satisfies Teff,ZAMS > 104 K, i.e., above the canonical A/B spectral boundary. No explicit mass threshold is imposed, as the mapping between mass and spectral type is model-dependent and subject to additional systematic uncertainties when inferred retrospectively. Under this definition, all four host stars are classified as B-type at ZAMS.

Planetary orbital evolution

In a single-star, single-planet system, the onset of post-main-sequence evolution fundamentally alters the star-planet interaction. The dominant mechanisms that reshape the orbit are: (i) loss of mass from either body; (ii) tidal dissipation raised by the expanding star; and (iii) hydrodynamic coupling to the stellar wind15.
