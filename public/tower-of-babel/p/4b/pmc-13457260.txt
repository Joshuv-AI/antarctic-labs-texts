Comparative Evaluation of Pain Perception Following Topical Application of Clove Oil, EMLA 5%, and Lignocaine Hydrochloride Gel 2% as a Pre-anesthetic Agent in Children Aged 7–10 Years: A Randomized Controlled Study

Introduction

Administering local anesthetic injections is a routine yet often anxiety-inducing part of pediatric dental care. The fear and discomfort associated with these injections can significantly affect children's cooperation, making pain management crucial for successful dental treatment.1 Topical anesthetics, applied before needle insertion, can reduce injection pain and thus enhance the treatment experience.

Among the various topical anesthetics available, lignocaine hydrochloride 2% remains a standard due to its quick onset and minimal side effects. EMLA 5%, a eutectic mixture of prilocaine and lignocaine, is effective due to its deeper mucosal penetration. Clove oil, with eugenol as its active compound, represents a natural alternative with analgesic and antimicrobial properties.2 Despite their popularity, comparative clinical data on the effectiveness of these agents in pediatric patients remain sparse. The present research aims to assess and compare the pain perception in children aged 7–10 years after the application of clove oil, EMLA 5%, and lignocaine hydrochloride gel 2% prior to local anesthetic injections.

Materials and Methods

This study was a randomized, controlled, single-blind, three-arm clinical trial that compared the efficacy of three topical anesthetic agents—clove oil, lignocaine hydrochloride 2% gel, and EMLA 5%—in alleviating pain perception during local anesthetic administration in children aged 7–10 years. The duration of the study was 1 year 6 months.

The clove oil was prepared by mixing clove oil with glycerine to make a 5% clove oil solution, which was stored in an airtight container.

Participants were randomly allocated into three groups using computer-generated randomization:

Group I: Clove oil

Group II: EMLA 5% gel

Group III: Lignocaine hydrochloride 2% gel

The patient was seated on the dental chair, and the patient proforma was filled by the principal investigator. The patient was allocated to one of the three groups. The site of local anesthetic application on the oral mucosa was dried using cotton held with tweezers. A sterile cotton bud was then used to apply the selected topical anesthetic to the dried mucosa with gentle rubbing for 1 minute. This was followed by a waiting period of 2 minutes. Thereafter, local anesthesia (LA) was injected.

After administering LA, pain was assessed using both an objective measure [Sound-Eye-Motor (SEM)] and a subjective scale [Wong-Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale (WBFPRS)]. The responses were subsequently documented. The data obtained were statistically evaluated (Fig. 1).

Statistical Analysis

The collected data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21. The dependent variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation. The Kruskal–Wallis test was performed to compare pain levels among groups. The significance threshold was set at 0.05.

Results

All three topical anesthetics demonstrated efficacy in reducing injection pain. Clove oil showed pain reduction comparable to EMLA and lignocaine. There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in mean pain scores on both the WBFPRS and SEM scales.

A total of 48 participants were included in the study, of which 23 were males (47.9%) and 25 were females (52.1%), with a mean age of 9.04 ± 1.38 years.

In the present study, for objective pain assessment, SEM score was recorded for all participants. The highest mean SEM score was recorded in group III (1.25 ± 2.0), followed by group I (0.75 ± 0.9), and the lowest mean value was recorded for group II (0.69 ± 0.7) (Table 1).

Discussion

Pain and anxiety are closely interlinked, especially in pediatric dentistry, where dental procedures can evoke significant fear in young patients. In pediatric patients, anxiety can enhance pain perception, leading to greater distress and a less favorable dental experience. Studies have shown that children with higher anxiety levels tend to report greater pain perception. This highlights the necessity of adopting comprehensive pain management techniques that consider both the physiological and psychological components of pain perception.

Needle phobia is a major cause of anxiety in children undergoing dental procedures. The fear of needles can cause significant distress, leading to avoidance of dental visits and reluctance to cooperate during procedures.3 Topical anesthesia has a substantial function in mitigating anxiety-related pain amplification by providing a painless and stress-free experience before LA injection. Needle-related anxiety is minimized postapplication of topical anesthetic, reducing anticipatory fear and promoting a more positive attitude toward dental care.4 Effective pain control through topical anesthesia not only ensures better cooperation from pediatric patients but also prevents the development of long-term dental anxiety and phobia, which can adversely affect future dental visits.5

Numerous studies6–10 have been conducted to evaluate the perception of pain associated with lignocaine hydrochloride 2% and EMLA 5%, but based on current knowledge, there are a limited number of studies comparing the pain perception associated with lignocaine hydrochloride 2%, EMLA 5%, and clove oil as topical pre-anesthetic agents. Therefore, the current study aims to test and compare the efficacy of the three topical anesthetics—clove oil, EMLA 5%, and lignocaine hydrochloride 2% gel—as pre-anesthetic agents in 7–10-year-old children. The results were analyzed using objective and subjective pain assessment methods, namely the SEM scale and the Wong-Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale (WBFPRS), as used in a previous study by Anantharaj et al.11

The random allocation of participants was done into three groups using computer-generated randomization. It was a parallel-arm study design, where each group received one of the three pre-anesthetics. After completely drying the area of application, the selected topical anesthetic was applied for 1 minute. According to a study conducted by Gupte et al.,12 it was reported that the maximum anesthetic property of lignocaine hydrochloride was attained at 1 minute. A waiting time of 2 minutes was given before the local anesthetic injection at the site of application of the pre-anesthetics, as recommended by Malamed.13

While delivering the local anesthetic injection, the SEM score was used to assess pain perception among the participants in the three groups. The mean SEM scores indicated that the highest pain perception was recorded in group III (1.25 ± 2.04), followed by group I (0.75 ± 0.93), and the lowest was found in group II (0.69 ± 0.70). The findings suggest that lignocaine hydrochloride gel 2% (group III) resulted in the highest pain perception, followed closely by clove oil (group I), while EMLA 5% (group II) exhibited the lowest pain perception. In a similar study conducted by Sruthi and Jeevanandan,14 it was concluded that EMLA had lower pain scores compared to lignocaine hydrochloride. However, the current study revealed no significant variation in pain perception among the three groups.

The Wong-Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale (WBFPRS) assessed the subjective perception of pain, which was conducted after the administration of the local anesthetic injection, yielding findings similar to those of a study by Anantharaj et al.,11 which also used the WBFPRS to record pain experienced by children. The highest mean WBFPRS score was observed in group III, followed by group I, with the lowest score in group II. Statistical analysis demonstrated no significant difference among the three groups, indicating that all three agents provided relatively similar pain management efficacy based on subjective assessments.

A gender-based analysis of SEM and WBFPRS scores was carried out to assess potential variations in pain perception between male and female participants. The data revealed no significant difference between male and female participants in any of the three groups, suggesting that gender did not influence pain perception in this study. These findings align with previous studies by Paschos et al.,6 Al-Melh and Andersson,15 and Deepika et al.,17 indicating that pain perception in children was more influenced by psychological factors, past dental experiences, and anxiety levels rather than biological sex differences.

The findings of this study hold significant clinical relevance for pediatric dentistry. No statistically significant differences were observed among the three anesthetics. All three agents—clove oil, EMLA 5%, and lignocaine hydrochloride gel 2%—can be considered effective options for pain management prior to local anesthetic injections in children. Alqareer et al.,18 in his study, concluded that clove oil had similar anesthetic properties as benzocaine 20%. To our knowledge, limited research has compared clove oil, EMLA 5%, and lignocaine hydrochloride 2% as pre-anesthetic agents in pediatric patients.

In the present study, 5% clove oil was used in a glycerin base, consistent with the study conducted by Alqareer et al.,18 who used a homemade clove gel in a glycerin base.

In 1978, clove was considered safe (GRAS) for human use at dosages below 1,500 ppm by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The established maximum quantity of cloves that an individual can consume per day, as suggested by the World Health Organization (WHO), is 2.5 mg/kg of body weight.1,19

Many available anesthetics, such as lignocaine, have been associated with adverse reactions, including localized irritation, methemoglobinemia, and hypersensitivity.20 Given the limited research available on the applications of clove oil as a topical pre-anesthetic agent in children, there remains a significant gap in the existing literature. More studies are needed to assess the pharmacokinetics of herbal anesthetics to understand how these substances are absorbed, metabolized, and eliminated in the pediatric population.21–24 Further research is also necessary to refine formulations and enhance efficacy, though the current findings support clove oil as a potentially safe and effective pre-anesthetic agent.

Conclusion

This study found that lignocaine hydrochloride gel 2% resulted in the highest pain perception, whereas clove oil and EMLA 5% demonstrated similar pain perception. These findings highlight clove oil as a promising natural alternative to conventional anesthetics. The differences were statistically nonsignificant.

Limitations of the Study

Further trials should assess not only the anesthetic effect but also the dosage, concentration, and potential for adverse reactions, including allergic responses or systemic toxicity. Further research with larger sample sizes is required to confirm these results and refine pain management strategies in pediatric dentistry.
