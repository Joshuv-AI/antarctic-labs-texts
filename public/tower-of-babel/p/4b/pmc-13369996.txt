Unveiling climate transformations in the northwestern Caucasus: the Middle Holocene divergence of landscape evolution
Authors: P I Kalinin, V A Trifonov, N I Shishlina, O G Zanina, I Y Kudrevatykh, P G Panin, G V Mitenko, I A Idrisov, D V Petrov, E A Bulanova, Huihui Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change during the Holocene (5500–4000 year BP) transformed the altitudinal zonation of the northwestern Caucasus, affecting the development and distribution of prehistoric populations, whose specialized economic system were based on dry farming and cattle breeding. This study analyzes reliably dated palaeosols buried beneath Early Bronze Age Maikop culture kurgans, comparing these findings with palaeorecords from the Eastern Mediterranean–Black Sea–Caspian Corridor. Our results demonstrate that the forest–steppe boundary in the foothills (100–1100 m a.s.l.) is highly sensitive to climatic fluctuations. Palaeosol and palaeobotanical analyses show that, where luvisols under broadleaved forests are widespread in the foothills of the northwestern Caucasus today, between 5300 and 4900 year BP, Black Soil (Chernozem) under meadow and meadow–steppe ecosystems was present. The general cooling trend between 5000 and 4000 year BP, culminating in the ‘4.2 ka event’, shifted the boundary between the broadleaved forests and the forest–steppes by 20 km northwards (descending from 800 to 400 m a.s.l.) while the meadow steppes retreated to the adjacent plains. Such a large–scale landscape restructuring has not been recorded in either the mountainous Caucasus or the East European Plain steppes. This forest expansion triggered persistent soil transformations, driving the evolution of Chernozems into Luvisols and establishing a new altitudinal zonation. The proposed reconstruction demonstrates that the ‘4.2 ka event’, typically associated with cooling and aridification, can trigger differential responses across adjacent landscapes. While it caused intensified droughts and xerophytization in the southern East European Plain, the same cooling on the humid slopes of the northwestern Caucasus led to glacial expansion and a shift toward wetter forest landscapes. Taking these divergent landscape responses into account is essential to understanding how ancient populations of the northwestern Caucasus responded to the climatic changes of the Holocene.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51669-7.Keywords: Palaeosol, Maikop culture, Broadleaved forest, Climate change, Mountains, Siberian High, 4.2 ka eventSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Mountains are regions of high significance in the context of contemporary global climate change and regional resilience to its impacts. They are at the intersection of accelerated warming1 and large populations (approximately 15% of the world population2 that directly or indirectly depend on them. The northwestern Caucasus is located on the boundary of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), and the Siberian High (SH) played a pivotal role in the formation of the European climate during the Holocene3–5. However, against the background of the general regional climate, the northwestern Caucasus differs significantly from the Eastern Caucasus and the Lesser Caucasus. In the northwestern Caucasus region, the influence of the SH is mitigated by the influence of the Black Sea6, which determines the uncertainty of the response of mountain ecosystems to global climate change. Significant regional disparities in climate trends necessitate the synthesis of sedimentological and landscape palaeoarchives7–9. In this context, palaeosols serve as reliable records of climatic change, the decoding of which provides a vital tool for the verification and refinement of contemporary climate models10–14.

The altitudinal zonation of the Caucasus foothill landscape is strongly responsive to climate change, as this is where the boundary between contrasting forests and steppes passes15. It is natural to assume that under the changing climate conditions of the Holocene, this boundary was quite mobile, but until now, the causes, chronology and boundaries of the distribution of the cool and temperate deciduous forest zone in the Caucasus have remained uncertain16; this also holds true for climate impact on soil cover, landscape structure and formation of altitudinal zonation. Understanding the changes in the foothill landscapes during the Middle Holocene (7000–4500 years ago) is very important because, at this time, the northwestern Caucasus was in a zone of Western Asian cultural influence, and its ancient population not only mastered the skills necessary to engage in producing economy and develop advanced technologies but also passed them on to the Eurasian steppe populations.

Effective adaptation strategies in mountain regions must be based on landscape and climate change models to mitigate the severity of risk17–19. However, constructing such models is difficult because of the complexity of interacting factors in the mountains20–22. Modern reconstructions of key characteristics of the Caucasus climate in the Last Glacial Period and the Holocene are based on geomorphological descriptions of tills23,24 as well as studies of lacustrine25–27, cave28 and palustrine deposits29,30. It is challenging to compare these data because of discrepancies between the regional and local reconstructions of the climate in the mountains, edaphic changes, replacement of landscapes, anthropogenic impacts and other factors31. In addition, most studies have been conducted in the mid–mountain and high–mountain bands of the central Caucasus and the Lesser Caucasus, whereas the changes in the most populated low–mountain and foothill areas, including the northwestern Caucasus, remain understudied.

The palaeobotanical materials of the Guamka grotto layers, which are multilayered settlements dating to the Eneolithic–Middle Ages32,33 and Lake Khuko34, are still the main sources of data for reconstructing the palaeoclimate in the northwestern Caucasus. However, studies of the soils buried underneath the early Bronze Age kurgans conducted recently have approached this scientific problem differently35.

Recent excavations of a Bronze Age kurgan in the Republic of Adygea (Klady cemetery, Novosvobodnaya, former Tsarskaya village) that have been reliably dated provide an opportunity to capture new palaeoecological information about landscape evolution and to compare it with palaeorecords from other regions in the Caucasus and the adjacent steppes. Understanding the response of landscapes to global Holocene climate events is key to modelling the response of modern ecosystems to rapid climate change. The aim of this research is to reconstruct climate change in the Middle Holocene and assess its impact on the altitudinal zonation and landscapes of the northwestern Caucasus. For this purpose, a thorough study of modern soils and palaeosols preserved without alteration under the Bronze Age kurgan at Klady was conducted via palaeopedology, palynology, phytolith analysis and geochemistry methods. The results were compared with the data from the palaeorecords of the Eastern Mediterranean–Black Sea–Caspian Corridor (EMBSeCBIO), and the reconstruction of northwestern Caucasus climate and landscape change during 5500–4000 year BP was prepared. A key feature of this research is the integrated approach that combines palaeosol and palaeobotanical reconstructions with modern climate analysis methods. The palaeoclimatic interpretation and reconstruction of the dynamics of the boundary shifting between the key landscape zones within the northwestern Caucasus will help determine the impact of natural processes on lifestyle of the ancient societies and provide a deeper understanding of how cultural landscapes emerged and developed in the study region during the Holocene.

Objects and methods

Region setting and archaeology

In 1898, kurgan 1 at the Klady cemetery of the Maikop culture located in the northwestern Caucasus foothills (Republic of Adygea, Novosvobodnaya village; 44,388189 N, 40,387788 E) was excavated by N.I. Veselovsky36; since 2013, it has been reinvestigated by the joint expedition of the Institute for the History of Material Culture (Saint–Petersburg) and the State Historical Museum (Moscow). It is located on the slopes exposed to the south at an altitude of 600 m above sea level (a.s.l.) (Fig. 1). Kurgan 1 was built over megalithic burial constructions (a dolmen and a wooden tomb). This kurgan is composed of two mounds (Fig. 2). The initial mound with a diameter of 16 m and a height of 2.55 m was made over the earliest burial in a wooden tomb (grave 4), preserving palaeosol 1 (P–1 soil section). The dolmen (grave 1) was subsequently constructed near the southeastern sector of the earliest mound. It was also covered by a larger mound up to 10 m high and 70 m in diameter, which preserved palaeosol 2 (P–2 soil section). For the first time, the14 C AMS dating of the animal bones retrieved from both graves made it possible to obtain data on the construction time of kurgan 1, which covers the interval within the range of 3300–2900 calBC37(Fig. 2, C, Supplementary file 1, Table 1). The determined interval sets clear chronological boundaries for two preserved palaeosols, which makes it possible to carry out a comparative analysis of Holocene palaeosols (P–1 and P–2 soil sections) buried underneath two mounds of kurgan 1 at Klady and modern Luvisol (M–1 soil section). For comparison, palaeosols from the Maikop culture settlements of Novosvobodnenskoye (palaeosol NV; 44.390230 N, 40.402600 E) and Starchiki (palaeosol ST; 44.393081 N, 40.404850 E), located in the immediate vicinity of the Klady cemetery, were studied (Supplementary file 2, Fig. 1). The Novosvobodnenskoye settlement and grave 1 are of a similar age, with a 14C AMS date of 3400 cal BC. The Starchiki settlement, which the14 C AMS analysis technique dates to 2500 cal BC, allows for tracing the soil evolution in the study area over additional 500 years compared to the palaeosols buried under kurgan 1 (Supplementary file 3, Table 2). Although the palaeosols at the settlements have been significantly transformed by anthropogenic activity, they have retained their original morphological structure, making it possible to reconstruct their formation conditions.

Field research methods

The diagnostics of the soils were performed by the IUSS Working Group WRB (2022)42, the soil horizons were classified in accordance with the FAO classification system43, the description of the soil horizon morphology was prepared according to Rozanov44. We then selected samples of palaeosol from two soil profiles with a sampling space of 5 cm. A modern soil section was cut with a sampling space of 10 cm at a distance of 500 m from kurgan 1 at the same altitude and on the slope with the same exposure. A total of 37 palaeosol P–1 and P–2 samples and 20 modern M–1 soil samples were taken. The main morphological features and soil horizons were identified within the NV and ST settlement palaeosols.

Laboratory research methods

The key analyses were performed at the Laboratory of Geochemistry and Soil Mineralogy and the Chemical and Analytical Centre of the V.A. Kovda Institute of Physicochemical and Biological Problems in Soil Science, RAS, in Puschino (Russia). The palaeosol study methodology included analysis of physical and chemical features, soil magnetic susceptibility, chemical composition, and phytolith and palynological analyses.

The chemical composition of the soil samples was measured by X–ray fluorescence analysis via a SPECTROSCAN MAX–GV WDXRF desktop crystal diffraction scanning spectrometer (Russia). Major elements are present in the form of oxides. The soil grain size, pH, CO2 content of carbonates (CO2 carb) and organic carbon (SOC) content were determined via standard analysis methods45. The specific magnetic susceptibility (MS: ×10− 8 m3 kg–1 was studied under laboratory conditions on a Kappabridge (AGICO, Brno; Czech Republic) according to the standard method46. The sieved soil was weighed, and MS was measured in three replications.

Palaeobotanical studies were based on 30 samples of Spores and Pollen, 6 samples Phytoliths taken from the upper 10 cm of the soil organic horizon (Fig. 4). Complex analysis of microfossils (palynomorphs, phytoliths, sponge spicules and microremnants of fungi and algae) was performed via a 30 g sample, which was collected by a quartering method, boiled in 10% HCl, separated from silt removed by flotation in distilled water, and then enriched in heavy liquid (KI+KdI2), with a density of 2.1–2.2 g/cm3.

Morphology of the palaeosol (P–1 and P–2) and modern soil (M–1) sections and spots of selecting samples for palaeobotanical.

The samples for palynological and phytolith analyses were processed via a separation method without treating the macerates with an acetolysis mixture for phytoliths. Microfossils were identified via a Carl Zeiss Axoiscope 5 light microscope at a magnification of ×10 in brightfield observation mode and at a magnification of ×400 in phase 2. Water-free glycerol was used for the preparation of microscopic slides. The microscopic analyses revealed sufficiently high pollen concentrations and good preservation of palynomorphs, making it possible to count 300 grains. The results of the palynological analyses are presented as percentage diagrams, where pollen and spore taxa are given as percentages of the total number of all palynomorphs identified as 100%. The phytoliths (approximately 300 samples) were divided into broad categories on the basis of their shape, after which the percentages of different types of phytoliths were analysed. The identification was based on the phytolith collection of the V.A. Kovda Institute of Physicochemical and Biological Problems in Soil Science RAS. Morphological details and images of the phytoliths were taken via a Vega 3 LSU (SEM) scanning electron microscope and a Vega3 LMU camera. SEM images were taken with the LMU camera and BSE detector using a 20 kV accelerating voltage, 15 mm WD, 1024 × 1224 matrix sizes, 146 dpi resolution, and 16 bits. The final figures were assembled in the Adobe Photoshop program. The dimensions in the illustrations are given in micrometres (µm). We named the phytolith morphotypes according to the principles of the International Code of Phytolith Nomenclature 2.047.

Methodology of climate and landscape reconstruction

A distinctive feature of this analysis is the comparison of modern soils and palaeosols preserved underneath the layers of the kurgan over graves 1 and 4, which have reliable dates of construction.

Using the analysis of palaeosol properties and the palaeobotanical analysis, we determine key characteristics of mountain landscapes such as soil type and plant associations and then compare them with climate characteristics such as the MAT and the MAP typical for similar modern landscapes in the northwestern Caucasus. To combine these characteristics in the climate belts of the northwestern Caucasus, we used the USSR Climate Book of 1965 (140 meteorological stations, including 37 stations in the mountainous and foothill areas), which is a definitive book containing information on the climate in the study region39. After 1965, the number of meteorologic stations in the Caucasus decreased substantially. The aridity index (IDM) proposed by De Martonne was calculated for each observation point (meteorological station)48,49 using the following formula:

– IDM = P/(T + 10), where P is the MAP and T is the MAT48.

The IDM is strongly correlated with soil and vegetation zonation in the south of the East European Plain and the Fore–Caucasus50–52 as well as with the Köppen climate classification53. However, the IDM has certain limitations that need to be taken into account. As an empirical formula, it does not fully capture the seasonality of precipitation and potential evapotranspiration, factors that are particularly significant in mountainous terrain54. Besides, the sensitivity of the index can decrease in the areas with abrupt short–term fluctuations in temperature and humidity55. Still, despite these limitations, we consider the IDM to be a robust climatic proxy for this study because its values are well calibrated against modern soil and vegetation belts of the northwestern Caucasus and the Fore–Caucasus. Consequently, this index can ensure reliable spatial extrapolation of local palaeoreconstructions to the regional level.

Results

Soil morphology and physicochemical properties

Chernozem (Clayic, Pachic) palaeosol was identified underneath the kurgan in the P–1 soil section, and its profile consists of horizons A–AB–B–BCk–Ck (Fig. 4). The palaeosol in the P–2 soil section is also characterized as Chernozems (Clayic, Pachic), and its profile is described as A–AB–B–BCk. The modern soil in the M–1 soil section is represented by Ferric Stagnic Luvisols (Cutanic, Differentic) with horizons A–AEt–Ab–Bt–BCkt. A detailed morphological description of these soil profiles is provided in Supplementary file 4. The NV profile consists of the Ah–AhBkg–BCkg horizons, the palaeosol is characterized as Chernozem (Loamic, Pachic, Protostagnic). The ST palaeosol profile consists of the Ah–Bg–BCg horizons, and it is represented by Vertic Chernozems (Loamic, Stagnic) (Supplementary file 5, Fig. 2).

The pH of the P–1 palaeosol ranged from neutral to alkaline (7.2–8.0) (Fig. 5). Because of diagenetic changes due to long periods of conservation, the SOC content is low and decreases gradually with depth from 1.1% in the A horizon to 0.19% in the Сk horizon. The content of СО2 carb in the upper horizons does not exceed 0.8%, increasing abruptly in the ВСk horizon to 4.7%. The palaeosol grain size of the P–1 section differs slightly: the clay content (< 0.001 mm) varies from 34% in the AB horizon to 21% in the Сk horizon. The MS values range from 11–23·10− 8 m3kg− 1, increasing gradually with depth and reaching their highest values in the Ck horizon.

Physical and chemical properties of the modern soil (M–1) and palaeosols (P–1 and P–2).

The P–2 palaeosol has an alkaline pH (7.6–7.9). The SOC content is 1.2% in the A horizon and decreases with depth to 0.4% in the BCk horizon. The content of CO2 carb in the upper horizons is 1.1–1.4%, and its content increases abruptly in the BCk horizon, reaching 6.3%. The clay content varies slightly in this soil section (23–29%). The MS values range from 7–30·10− 8 m3kg− 1, as is observed in the P–1 palaeosol, reaching its maximum value in the BCk horizon.

In the M–1 modern soil, the clay content varies from 7.2% in the A horizon to 29.1% in the Ab horizon. The lower part of the profile consists of clay loam, and the upper part includes loam. The distinctive feature of this soil is the presence of a tight second humus Ab horizon lying beneath a more light–coloured AEt horizon at a depth of approximately 80 cm. This horizon is made up of clay loam and has a prismatic and subangular blocky structure with a high clay content. The MS value does not correlate with the clay distribution and basically does not vary in the profile; its value ranges from 7–12·10–8 m3 kg–1. The maximum content of SOC is recorded in the A horizon (5.8%), and it gradually decreases with depth, reaching its lowest value in the BCkt horizon (0.23%). The SOC value in the Ab horizon increases slightly, without exceeding 0.8%. The soil is characterized by a low content of СО2 carb, and its value exceeds 1% only in the Ab horizon (1.1%) and the BCkt horizon (2.2%). Detailed physicochemical properties of these soils are provided in Supplementary File 6, Table 3.

Geochemistry

The distributions of major elements in modern soil and palaeosols vary significantly (Fig. 6). The palaeosols are characterized by an even distribution of silica (SiO2). Its concentration varies from 59% to 67%, decreasing only in the carbonate horizons because of an increase in the calcium (CaO) content. The А and АB horizons have slightly lower concentrations of aluminium (Al2O3), iron (Fe2O3), potassium (K2O), magnesium (MgO) and sodium (Na2O). The CaO content increases in the BCk horizons as well as the A horizon because of the penetration of calcite from the kurgan mound.

Chemical compositions of the modern soil (M–1) and palaeosols (P–1 and P–2).

The M–1 modern soil section is characterized by the accumulation of SiO2 in the upper 80 cm (70–76%) and its decrease in the lower horizons to 64–66%. This process is accompanied by the movement of Al2O3 from the upper to the lower soil horizons, with its content increasing from 7% in the A horizon to 13–15% at depths below 80 cm. Compared with the profile lower section, the A and AEt horizons have significantly lower contents of Fe2O3, K2O, MgO, Na2O and CaO. As the concentration of carbonates increases in the BCkt horizon, the content of CaO increases to 4.1%. Detailed chemical compositions of the palaeosols are provided in Supplementary File 7, Table 4.

Comparative analysis of soils

The palaeosols P–1 and P–2 are characterized by the A–AB–B–BCk–Ck profile structure, which is typical for Chernozems, alkaline рН and carbonate horizons. However, the gleying and redoximorphic features, initial leaching of carbonates and major elements also took place. The MS indicator also does not have an accumulative distribution in the soil profile, which is typical for more arid Chernozems and is determined by the reduced pH and good moisture supply of the soils. Such Chernozems are similar to rather moist modern soils common in the Fore-Caucasus.

However, a comparative analysis of buried Chernozems with modern soils demonstrated their significant differences, primarily due to the strongly pronounced textural differentiation of the Luvisols profile. Compared with clay loam, the underlying horizons of the upper 60 cm of the Luvisols are dominated by loam. The A and AEt horizons have very low concentrations of major elements, with the exception of SiO2. The carbonates were completely removed from the soil profile, suggesting the active development of lessivage, leaching and podzolization processes in modern soils. Compared with the A and AEt horizons a second humus Ab horizon has higher contents of clay and lower contents of SOC.

Despite significant differences in structure and properties, the studied soils are genetically similar. They have loamy to clay loam textures, form on carbonate loess with similar chemical compositions and have signs of redoximorphic features and gleying. Bioturbation activities occurred in all the soils. This process is expressed in widespread krotovinas of up to 10–15 cm in the middle part of the buried Chernozem profile and scolithus–type worm tubes filled with carbonates in palaeosols and organic matter and iron in modern soils (Fig. 4). Gleyed krotovinas that serve as important evidence of the steppe stage of soil development56 were preserved in Luvisols. Therefore, the physical and chemical properties of the soils show that the P–1 and P–2 palaeosols are similar and differ from modern soil M–1 at the highest taxonomic level.

NV palaeosol is morphologically similar to P–1 and P–2, exhibiting an even lower degree of carbonate leaching. In contrast, the younger ST palaeosol appears fundamentally different. It is characterized by significantly more pronounced processes of lessivage, leaching, and gleying, alongside with well–developed textural differentiation and the formation of a vertic horizon. Carbonates were completely leached from the profile, while iron was translocated downward along worm tubes, analogous to the processes observed in modern soil. These features indicate significantly wetter conditions during the formation of the ST palaeosol.

Phytolith analysis

The biogenic fraction separated from the upper horizons of the palaeosols contains a spectrum that encompasses a rather large number of phytoliths (357–389) of various types (26–33 morphotypes), which differ in percentage contents of the groups (Supplementary File 8, Table 5). The average size of phytoliths is approximately 50 µм, and a small number of large phytoliths ranging from 100 to 150 µм have been documented. The phytolith morphotypes of the Trapezoid, Elongate entire and Acute bulbosus type 2, Elongate sinuate varieties are dominant. Acute bulbosus type 1, Irregular sinuate, Trapezoid bilobate, Spheroid psilate, Tabular entire, and Crenate are represented by a smaller number (Fig. 7). The content (up to 37%) of Acute bulbosus type 2 phytoliths, which are morphotypes with a small base and a long awn, is high. These morphotypes are characterized as “meadow trichomes”. Spicules of sponges that are mainly small fragments, soil mites (cf. Acarina), fungi and single algae grains such as Pediastrum, Botryoccocus, Zygnema–type, Pseudoschizaea, and redeposited spores were identified in very rare cases. One of the samples revealed detritus, represented mainly by tissue from gramineous grasses and root residue, which is characteristic of the upper soil horizons.

The Phytolith reference material of palaeosol (P–1 and P–2): (А) Crenate; (В, С) Acute bulbosus type 1; (D) Acute bulbosus type 2; (E, I-J) – Trapezoid; (F) Elongate sinuate; (G, H) Elongate entire; (K) Spheroid psilate.

The phytolith assemblage of modern soil is quantitatively the smallest (297 phytoliths counted) and monotonous (15 morphotypes). The phytoliths are rather small, with sizes ranging from 20 to 30 µм, indicating low vegetation and rather sparse grasses. The dominance (23%) of Elongate entire throughout the spectrum suggests that mature pioneer vegetation with a high share of dicotyledonous plants was common (Fig. 8). Forms of Irregular sinuate characteristics of tree vegetation in broadleaved forests57,58 and forms of Acute bulbosus type 1 characterized by a large size, a large base and a lack of thorns previously described as “forest trichomes”59 were found in significant quantities.

Distribution diagrams of phytoliths, spores and pollen in the palaeosols (P–1 and P–2) and the modern soil (M–1).

Overall, the highest content of phytoliths, which can be attributed to trees and shrubs in modern soil, was approximately 24%. The content of these phytoliths in the P–1 palaeosol was 16%, whereas no such phytoliths were identified in the P–2 palaeosol. The results of the phytolith analysis of palaeosols revealed the spread of rather diverse grassy vegetation of mesophytic meadows, with a predominance of medium-grown and high-grown gramineous grasses and some dicotyledonous plants (Fig. 8).

Palynological analysis

The families of herbs and dwarf shrubs are dominant in the spectra of the upper horizons of the P–1 palaeosol (up to 65%), where Poaceae pollen dominates (Fig. 8, Supplementary File 9, Table 6). Asteracea pollen was identified at much lower percentages. The share of other identified taxa did not exceed 3% or were identified in rare cases. Approximately 22% of the arboreal species are represented by pollen grains of Pinus sp., Picea sp., Alnus sp., Acer sp., Quercus sp., Carpinus sp., and Moraceae. Spores account for approximately 13%. This group is represented mainly by ferns: the Ophyoglossum, Botrichium and Polypodiaceae. The palynological spectra are suggestive of dominant meadow vegetation.

The nonarboreal pollen from the Asteraceae family and the subfamily Cichoriaceae is dominant in the spectra of the P–2 palaeosol (up to 67%). The percentage of arboreal taxa was 23%. Pinus sp., Picea sp., Betula sp., Alnus sp., Acer sp., Quercus sp., and Moraceae were detected in the P–2 pollen assemblages. Spores (up to 10%) are represented by Ophyoglossum, Botrichium, Lycopodium sp., Polypodiaceae and Bryales (Fig. 8). These spectra clearly indicate dominant meadow vegetation.

The pollen spectra of the M–1 modern soil included arboreal pollen (38%), herb and dwarf shrub pollen (53%) and spores (9%). Coniferous trees are represented by pine, fir and spruce pollen. Broadleaved trees are represented by Fagaceae, Quercus sp., Alnus sp. and, in rare cases, Betula sp., Salix sp., species of Moraceae, and Juglans pollen. Families of Asteraceae, Caryophyllaceae, and Poaceae dominate the herb plants. Therefore, the palynological spectrum of modern soil is typical for forest–steppe landscapes comprising meadow vegetation and mixed forests.

The pollen spectra of the analysed palaeosols are characterized by substantially lower percentages of pollen from arboreal species than modern pollen (Fig. 8). The share of trees and shrubs in the Chernozems does not exceed 23%, with the most significant input resulting from species with increased pollen production. Pollen grains are picked up by mountain and valley breezes, which carry them away and transfer them up and down the slopes, increasing their dispersal range; for this reason, pollen often occurs outside the area where it is released by trees60. Beech pollen remains mainly under the canopy of its source61. The percentage of non–wind–pollinated broadleaved species in palaeosols does not exceed 10%.

Therefore, the data from the palaeobotanical analysis suggest that the slopes of the hills 5300–4900 year BP were grassy and included mixed grass and herb, meadow and steppe plant communities covering the watersheds and trees growing along the rivers. Broadleaved forests grow in the region, and their arborescent strata include oak, hornbeam, beech, maple, birch tree, and elm; mountainous slopes are covered with fragments of dark coniferous forests made up of fir trees and spruce.

Natural zone classification

We divided the northwestern Caucasus and the plains abutting it in the north into five natural zones. Each zone has its own climate (IDM, MAP and MAT), soil type, plant community, and altitudinal and latitudinal zonation (Fig. 9; Table 1). The altitudinal zonation of the northwestern Caucasus repeats the latitudinal zonation of the adjacent plains, including the zonation of soil types62.

Discussion

Pedogenic transformation

The discovery of two palaeosols of the Chernozem type with mature carbonate Bk horizons and remains of plant pollen and phytoliths of meadow and mixed grass–gramineous vegetation under kurgan 1 at Klady led to the development of a working hypothesis stating that modern altitudinal zonation differs immensely from that of the northwestern Caucasus foothills in 5300–4900 year BP. The upper foothill areas where kurgan 1 was built are now located in Zone 4 of our classification, where the aridity index is 40–45. This zone is Luvisols under oak–beech forests (Table 1). Chernozems with the Bk horizons discovered beneath kurgan 1 formed if the precipitation rate was less than 760 mm/y63. Presently, soils with these properties form under meadow–steppe plant associations 70–130 km further to the north in Zone 2, with aridity indices ranging from 30 to 35. The climate conditions of these zones suggest that around 5300–4900 year BP the MAP and the MAT in the foothill areas may have been 100–200 mm/y and 0.5–2°С less than they are now.

This scenario can be made more complicated by possible asynchronicity of biota and soil transformations as well as nonlinear impact of microclimatic factors (e.g. exposure of the slope and terrain), slope processes and potential anthropogenic impacts. Increased humidity and forest expansion trigger the leaching of carbonates and lessivage, leading to the degradation of the characteristic granular structure and the development of a texture–differentiated profile. As a result, even deforestation is unable to restore carbonate profile and replenish clay fraction. We did not detect diagnostical signs of pronounced anthropogenic disturbance in the studied P–1 and P–2 soils, as there are no traces of arable and pyrogenic horizons, and there is no pedoturbation by tree uprooting in soils. Consequently, we suggest that the Chernozem transformation was triggered by natural climatic fluctuations. This transformation exhibited high resistance to reversal under Holocene conditions, as the mechanisms for Chernozem regradation from Luvisols are absent in the natural environments of the northwestern Caucasus foothills.

Our findings demonstrate that Luvisols with a second humus horizon are widespread across various slope exposures, occurring in both humid hornbeam–beech communities and more xerophytic oak forests. The development of these soils appears independent of parent material lithology, as they occur on a broad textural range from sand to clay loam. Modern soil catena data indicate that the thicknesses of the AEt and Ab horizons remain consistent across all geomorphological positions; under dense forest cover with well–developed root systems, erosional processes are significantly mitigated. Evidence of erosion within the A horizons is confined exclusively to the lower slope positions (Fig. 3). This confirms that the AEt horizon, diagnostic for Luvisols, is not a product of colluvial redeposition but formed in situ through pedogenesis under forest cover. Consequently, Luvisols with a second humus horizon exhibit high resistance to contemporary environmental drivers, including erosion and accumulation rates, slope exposure, vegetation type, and lithology64–66, which underscores the polygenetic nature of these soils. The origin of the Ab horizon in the Luvisols of the northwestern Caucasus has long been a subject of extensive debate35,67–69. However, our observations, coupled with the identification of Chernozems buried beneath the kurgans, support the hypothesis that this horizon represents a relict of a former steppe stage, specifically the residual lower part of an ancestral Chernozem profile.

Regional validity of palaeorecords

Although the limited number of palaeosols (two profiles under a single kurgan) restricts a full–scale reconstruction of altitudinal zonation shifts, the specific characteristics of Zone 4 allow for a conceptual extrapolation of these findings to the northwestern Caucasus foothills (100–1100 m a.s.l.). This extrapolation is supported by three key premises. First, Zone 4 features environmental conditions representative of the broader region, including high humidity and similar lithological and topographical patterns. Second, this zone effectively records shifts between forest and steppe landscapes, which are characteristic of several altitudinal zones. Third, the reliability of this extrapolation is reinforced by the high degree of consistency between our local findings and the EMBSeCBIO palaeorecords.

For instance, regional palaeoclimatic data indicate a significant cooling trend in the northwestern Caucasus around 5500 year BP34. This shift aligns with a broader climatic transition observed in the adjacent Mediterranean region, which became characterized by cold and arid conditions at approximately 5200 year BP70. Furthermore, the onset of glacier advancement in the Caucasus between 5000 and 4500 year BP serves as a compelling environmental indicator of this cooling, marking the beginning of significant altitudinal zonation shifts71. Coinciding with this period, between approximately 4500 and 4400 year BP, beech forests began to expand across the central part of the Northern Caucasus30. Concurrently, vertic horizons started to develop in the soils of the lower foothill areas (100–400 m a.s.l.)72. Given that the upper foothills (400–1100 m a.s.l.) are situated at the boundary between broadleaved forests and meadow–steppe plant associations and experienced more humid conditions, it is plausible that Vertic Chernozems began to form in this altitudinal zone at an earlier stage. This is further evidenced by the identification of a Vertic Chernozem at the Starchiki settlement, which had formed under humid conditions by 4500 year BP. This finding is consistent with palynological records from the Guamka Grotto73 and Starchiki74, which indicate that a progressive increase in moisture triggered the expansion of broadleaved forests by this period. At the Starchiki site, pollen spectra already exhibited high proportions of broadleaved tree taxa, closely resembling modern vegetation patterns. These findings align with data suggesting that steppes in the upper foothills were entirely replaced by forest communities by 4200–4100 year BP, whereas in the lower foothills, the transformation of Chernozems into Vertic Chernozems occurred after 4000 year BP35,38.

The presence of more open landscapes in the northwestern Caucasus foothills between 5200 and 4900 year BP, followed by their gradual replacement by denser, more humid forests, is consistent with regional reconstructions and complements the existing data from the EMBSeCBIO palaeorecords. A possible interpretation of these landscape shifts is the response of altitudinal zonation to prolonged cooling between 5000 and 4000 year BP. This process was likely accelerated by short–term climatic perturbations associated with the ‘4.2 ka event’, which is linked to pronounced aridification across the southern East European Plain75,76, the Mediterranean77,78, West Asia79 and China80. To date, the precise chronological boundaries of this interval remain a subject of ongoing debate78. Two recent large–scale studies of this event have used palaeorecords with control points spanning the 5300–2900 year BP interval81, while an analysis of data by McKay et al.82 constrains this anomaly to 4600–3400 year BP. In other regions, the ‘4.2 ka event’ is also seen not as an abrupt climate shift, but as a long–term transition81,83–85. This aligns with palaeorecords from the adjacent southern East European Plain, which reveal a persistent reduction in the MAP between 5000 and 4000 year BP, suggesting a probable direct link between this protracted climatic shift and the ‘4.2 ka event’. The identification of the ST palaeosol dated to 4500 year BP, featuring a significantly degraded Chernozem profile, provides crucial evidence that this climatic transition also impacted the northwestern Caucasus. Between 5500 and 4500 year BP, Chernozems underwent progressive degradation, characterized by leaching and textural differentiation, as evidenced by the Vertic Chernozems identified at the Starchiki settlement. However, during the subsequent interval (4500–4000 year BP), an active forest expansion likely triggered the transformation of Vertic Chernozems into a different taxonomic level – Luvisols. This process coincides with the ‘4.2 ka event’, which may have acted as a catalyst for the final shift from steppe pedogenesis to a forest–dominated regime in this region. While a global scale of the ‘4.2 ka event’ remains debated80, the period between 5000 and 4000 year BP clearly represents a major climate shift in the south of the East European Plain and the Caucasus27,81,86 profoundly influencing both landscape evolution and social and economic models of local populations87.

While the extrapolation of the local reconstruction to the regional level is linked to the risk of distortions due to natural and anthropogenic factors, robustness of the patterns identified and their correlation with regional data provides an opportunity to offer a general exploratory model of climate and landscape change in the northwestern Caucasus foothills. This model can serve as a conceptual framework for further verification multi–proxy palaeodata.

Exploratory model of climate and landscape change

The properties of the analysed palaeosols suggest that the foothill landscapes of the northwestern Caucasus (100–1100 m a.s.l.) experienced initial transformation as early as 5500–5000 year BP, coinciding with the onset of cooling34.. At the time when the kurgan 1 was built at Klady, the meadow steppes (Zone 2) occupied most of the foothills (Fig. 10); however, the Chernozems already showed signs of degradation, reflected in the development of eluvial (lessivage and leaching) and hydromorphic (redoximorphic features and gleying) processes. Throughout the Holocene, the proximity of the Black Sea ensured a constant moisture supply to the western slopes of the Caucasus. Precipitation in the region remained relatively stable, whereas temperatures fluctuated significantly due to the shifting dynamics of the Siberian High81. Consequently, unlike the arid regions in the south of the East European Plain and West Asia, the primary driver of climate and landscape change in the northwestern Caucasus was temperature rather than precipitation.

Reconstruction of climate and landscape change in the northwestern Caucasus during the 5500–4000 year BP.

The further cooling and expansion of glaciers between 5000 and 4500 year BP triggered a downslope shift of altitudinal zones, bringing shrubs and woody plants into lower elevations. In the upper foothills (400–1100 m a.s.l.), lower winter temperatures reduced evapotranspiration and increased effective moisture levels, facilitating regional humidification despite the aridification of adjacent lowlands. Enhanced spring snowmelt promoted the leaching of carbonates, soluble salts, and clay, which decreased soil porosity while simultaneously increasing particle dispersity in the lower part of the profile.

Such alterations deteriorated the physical properties and structure of the local soils, driving the transformation of Luvic and Vermic Chernozems into Vertic Chernozems at altitudes of 400–1100 m a.s.l. In the lower foothill areas (100–400 m a.s.l) vertic horizons began to develop after 4500 year BP72. This sequence suggests that the development of vertic properties preceded the forest-driven transformation, indicating that Vertic Chernozems served as a transitional stage before the eventual shift toward Luvisols. Thus, the boundary between the meadow steppes and the forest–steppe shifted northwards by 20 km to an altitude of 400 m a.s.l. (Fig. 10).

The third and most active phase began around 4500 year BP reaching its peak by 4200 year BP. While cooling could not trigger an immediate replacement of soil cover due to pedogenic inertia, the high density of natural zones in the mountains meant that even minor climatic shifts were sufficient to drive vegetation turnover. The temperature drop initiated a secondary succession, as the forest-steppe associations of Zone 3 were replaced by the broadleaved oak–beech and hornbeam forests of Zone 4. The expansion of oak and especially beech accelerated soil acidification and podzolization, leading to mineral weathering, organic matter depletion, and the structural degradation of the Chernozems. Lessivage led to the illuviation of clay from the upper soil horizon, resulting in the formation of a light–loam AEt horizon in the upper part of the profile and a compact second humus (Ab) horizon in its lower section. Consequently, around 4100–4200 year BP35,38 Vertic Chernozems at altitudes above 400 m a.s.l. transformed into Luvisols with a second humus horizon. Meanwhile, in the lower foothills (100–400 m a.s.l.), Luvic and Vermic Chernozems degraded into Vertic Chernozems72. This forest expansion fundamentally altered the pre–existing landscape, triggering persistent changes in soil properties. As a result, the boundary between broadleaved forests (Zone 4) and forest–steppe (Zone 3) shifted 20 km northward to an altitude of 400 m a.s.l., while the meadow–steppe (Zone 2) retreated to the adjacent plains (Fig. 10).

Comparison of our data with regional palaeorecords suggests that climatic shifts between 5000 and 4000 year BP, likely driven by long–term cooling, profoundly transformed the entire foothill zone (100–1100 m a.s.l.). These changes were far more substantial than those observed in the southern East European Plain or the eastern Caucasus, and are comparable in scale to the postglacial forest expansion of the early Holocene34,88,89. In the upper foothills, the landscape evolved rapidly, mirroring the latitudinal zonation shifts seen on the adjacent plains. Broadleaf forests expanded down to 400 m a.s.l., forest–steppe landscapes were restricted to the lower elevations (100–400 m a.s.l.), and meadow steppes were displaced from the foothills entirely. Notably, the aridification spreading from the north failed to impact these lower foothills, as consistent moisture from the Black Sea mitigated the drought associated with the ‘4.2 ka event’. By 4000 year BP, the altitudinal zonation of the northwestern Caucasus had likely reached a state similar to that of the present day.

Climate change in the northwestern Caucasus can be explained by a reconfiguration of the atmospheric interactions that drive modern climate patterns in Eurasia90. Since the Last Glacial Period, the Caucasus and the East European Plain steppes have been influenced by the interplay between the NAO and SH91,92. Currently, the SH is a dominant force of climate variability, exerting a particularly strong influence on the eastern part of the EMBSeCBIO93. The conceptual framework of the ‘4.2 ka event’81 suggests an abrupt northward shift of the westerly jet stream over the Black Sea–Caspian Corridor. In our proposed mechanism, an intensification of the SH acts as the primary factor capable of blocking NAO influence across this area. Although the SH has seldom been considered a major driver of the ‘4.2 ka event’ in Europe94, atmospheric blocking is a well–established factor that obstructed air masses and induced circulation variability throughout the Pleistocene and Holocene95–97.

According to our conceptual interpretation, between 5000 and 4000 year BP, the influence of the SH expanded westward, covering the southern East European Plain, the Caucasus, and West Asia. The intensification of the SH caused mid–latitude westerlies and Mediterranean cyclone tracks to diverge southward and northward98,99(Fig. 10), inducing a cold and dry climate across the region. Consequently, the middle Holocene climate dynamics of the northwestern Caucasus became synchronized with those of more arid regions, such as the central and eastern Caucasus26,30 as well as the southeastern Lesser Caucasus27,100. The Middle–Late Holocene cooling, including the ‘4.2 ka event’, had divergent impacts on the northwestern Caucasus and adjacent regions. While the southern East European Plain experienced a drop in winter temperatures, intensified droughts, and the xerophytization of plant associations76, the humid slopes of the northwestern Caucasus responded differently. Here, excessive moisture from Mediterranean cyclone tracks, combined with cooling, led to glacial expansion and a northward shift of natural boundaries, where wetter forest landscapes replaced relatively dry, open meadow steppes. This divergence aligns with the hypothesis of Nan et al.81 that synchronous drying and wetting are characteristic hydroclimatic features of the ‘4.2 ka event’.

Despite the limited number of palaeorecords, their precise chronological control and location within a zone sensitive to altitudinal shifts provide a robust basis for extrapolating our reconstruction to the northwestern Caucasus foothills. The dissemination of basic economic strategies related to producing economic forms based on dry farming and cattle breeding by the populations was attributed to the Maikop culture, and the early stage of the Dolmen culture was driven by climate oscillation. Landscape changes altered the boundaries and economic patterns of the ancient populations of the Caucasus by incorporating new croplands and pasture systems previously occupied by other cultures. Future studies of Maikop palaeosols beneath younger kurgans, combined with the analysis of lacustrine deposits, remain highly relevant; such research will provide more reliable data on mountain landscape evolution and its role in global climatic events. Ultimately, this paper represents an attempt to integrate the complex history of the northwestern Caucasus foothills into a general model of global climate change.

Conclusion

Climate change during the Middle–to–Late Holocene transition (5500–4000 year BP) transformed the altitudinal zonation (100–1100 m a.s.l.) of the northwestern Caucasus, affecting the development and distribution of prehistoric populations, with a specialized economic system. Our reconstructions reveal that, unlike the adjacent arid regions of the southern East European Plain and West Asia, the environment of the northwestern Caucasus was primarily driven by temperature rather than precipitation. This shift aligns with palaeorecords from the EMBSeCBIO and reflects the sensitive response of altitudinal zones to long–term cooling between 5000 and 4000 year BP, which was further accelerated by the short–term climatic perturbation known as the ‘4.2 ka event’.

Within this framework, we propose several key stages of environmental change: (1) 5500–5000 year BP: open meadow steppes were the dominant landscape in the northwestern Caucasus foothills. These areas featured a mosaic of meadow and meadow–steppe plant associations on slopes and watersheds, while broadleaved forests were restricted to river valleys and ravines. The soil cover was dominated by Luvic and Vermic Chernozems, which already exhibited signs of degradation, as evidenced by the development of eluvial and hydromorphic processes; (2) 5000–4500 year BP: climatic cooling triggered a reduction in evapotranspiration, resulting in elevated effective moisture levels and, consequently, intensified soil leaching. These factors drove the transformation of Luvic and Vermic Chernozems into Vertic Chernozems at altitudes of 400–1100 m a.s.l. Consequently, the boundary between meadow steppes and forest–steppes shifted 20 km northward to an elevation of 400 m a.s.l; (3) 4500–4000 year BP: the temperature drop associated with the ‘4.2 ka event’ triggered a further shift in altitudinal zones, initiating a secondary succession in the foothills. During this stage, broadleaved oak–beech and hornbeam forests began to replace forest–steppe plant associations.

Forest expansion fundamentally altered pre–existing landscapes and triggered changes in soil properties, with persistent consequences. In the upper foothills (400–1100 m a.s.l.), Vertic Chernozems were transformed into Luvisols with a second humus horizon, while in the lower foothills (100–400 m a.s.l.), Luvic and Vermic Chernozems degraded into Vertic Chernozems. Consequently, cooling–driven shifts moved the forest and forest–steppe boundary 20 km northward (descending from 800 to 400 m a.s.l.). Furthermore, the meadow steppe zone shifted to the north by more than 70 km and left the foothill area. No landscape alteration of comparable scale has been recorded in either the mountainous Caucasus or the East European Plain, highlighting the extreme sensitivity of the foothill forest–steppe boundary to climatic fluctuations.
