Beyond business as usual for lymphatic filariasis mass drug administration in hotspot districts
Authors: Dziedzom K de Souza, Jeffrey G Sumboh, Nii A Laryea, Odame Asiedu, Bright Alomatu, Sedzro K Mensah, Joseph Otchere, Joseph Larbi Opare, Collins S Ahorlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to validate the ‘Engage & Treat’ (E&T) and ‘Test & Treat’ (T&T) strategies to treat individuals who seldom or never participate in lymphatic filariasis mass drug administration (LF MDA).MethodsThe study was conducted in a hotspot district of Ghana, with 20 years of MDA. Treatment registers documenting participation were used to identify non-participating individuals. Individuals willingly accepting treatment were given the drugs (E&T). Those who refused treatment were offered testing using the filaria test strip (FTS) and further engaged to receive treatment if positive (T&T). A portion of the E&T participants were also tested.ResultsOverall, 25 068 individuals who missed the last MDA were identified; 93.13% accepted the drugs; 9958 people were tested, revealing an overall prevalence of 7.26% (95% CI 6.76 to 7.79%). Positivity was higher in T&T (12.10%; 95% CI 10.43 to 13.92%) than in the E&T group (6.47%; 95% CI 5.96 to 7.01%). The odds of testing positive were higher (OR=1.99; 95% CI 1.66 to 2.39; p<0.0001) among the T&T population and missing three consecutive MDAs (OR=2.22; 95% CI 1.86 to 2.64; p<0.0001).ConclusionsThe study confirms the validity of the strategies in addressing the challenges with non-treatment during LF MDA. Their implementation in areas of persistent transmission can support elimination in these settings.Keywords: Engage & Treat, Ghana, lymphatic filariasis, Never Treatment, Test & Treat

Introduction

The World Health Assembly resolution WHA 50.29 in 1997 endorsed the elimination of lymphatic filariasis (LF) as a disease of public health concern.1 LF, also known as elephantiasis, is a parasitic disease caused by the filarial parasites Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi and Brugia timori.2 It poses a significant socioeconomic and psychological burden on affected individuals.

In 2000, the WHO launched the Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis (GPELF).3 This, coupled with the concerted efforts of governments, non-governmental organizations and pharmaceutical companies, has resulted in a significant reduction in the burden of LF and is considered one of the most cost-effective disease-elimination programs in modern times.4 The strategy behind the GPELF hinges on mass drug administration (MDA), in disease-endemic settings, with a combination of albendazole, ivermectin and/or diethylcarbamazole to break the transmission cycle and the provision of morbidity management services for affected individuals.5,6 Despite the successes, these strategies are not without challenges.7,8 One of the most significant hurdles is the non-treatment of people during MDA, resulting in inadequate treatment coverage and difficulties in breaking transmission. Several factors contribute to this phenomenon, including the fears of adverse events and low-risk perceptions.9 Misinformation and inadequate education can foster non-compliance.10 This underscores the importance of community engagement and the need for targeted interventions that address local and health system challenges. Addressing these is critical to meeting the 2030 WHO neglected tropical disease (NTD) elimination targets.11

In our previous studies, we explored the Engage & Treat (E&T) and Test & Treat (T&T) strategies with the aim of addressing the challenges of MDA non-treatment with a combination of ivermectin and albendazole, and improving coverage in a hotspot district of Ghana.12 In this study, we aimed to primarily validate the approach in another hotspot district while treating individuals who missed MDA and develop the tools for wider implementation and rollout.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the Ellembelle district in the Western Region of Ghana (Figure 1—inset). It is positioned in the southern part of the region, 312 km from Accra, the capital of Ghana, and hence was chosen based on its proximity compared with other hotspots in the northern part of Ghana. The main economic activities are agriculture, fishing and mining.13 The high prevalence of malaria and challenges with access to clean water and sanitation pose a threat to health.14 At the time of this study in 2023, the district had received 20 yearly rounds of LF MDA with ivermectin and albendazole, and had failed pre-transmission assessment survey (pre-TAS) in 2021. The total population is reported as 125 044, with the population targeted for treatment being 87 651. The baseline antigen prevalence in 2000 was 18%. In 2022, 71 916 people were treated with a therapeutic coverage of 82.05% and an epidemiological coverage of 57.51% (https://espen.afro.who.int/diseases/lymphatic-filariasis).

Distribution of predicted prevalence of LF in communities. The values on the map show the actual point prevalence for specific communities. The inset shows the map of Ghana and the location of the study district. LF: lymphatic filariasis.

Study design

This study was conducted as previously described.12 Briefly, following the routine LF MDA by the Ghana NTD program, community drug distributors (CDDs) and Community Health Nurses (CHNs) were trained, during a 1-d session, on the use of the treatment registers and snowballing approach to identify, engage and treat people who did not take part in the MDA. Information, education and communication materials were developed for the process and given to CDDs and CHNs. The CHNs, being already conversant with the use of malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), were also trained on the use of the filaria test strip (FTS). After the training, the CDDs conducted a thorough review of the registers and listed individuals who did not receive treatment. In addition to the registers, individuals who did not take part in the MDA were asked if they knew others who also did not. This led to identifying other community members whose names were not in the registers. All identified individuals were subsequently provided with information regarding MDA, through the poster designed and translated into the local language. All identified individuals12 who accepted treatment at the first engagement were treated by the CDDs (E&T). The names of those who refused treatment were provided to the CHNs who followed up for FTS testing and further engaged them for treatment if found positive (T&T). A portion of the E&T population was also tested, to provide information on the prevalence of infection among people who miss MDA. The study team visited the district, every 2 wk, to monitor and collect the data and samples. A questionnaire was administered during the visits to selected community members, CDDs and CHNs to assess the acceptability of the interventions. Age and gender information was collected for all participants in the E&T and T&T process. The demographic information of the participants was limited to what is available in the MDA registers. Other demographic information, such as occupation and education levels, was only captured for those selected for questionnaire surveys to assess the acceptability of the interventions.

Data analysis

The coordinates of the communities were retrieved from public databases online. Data processing was carried out using Stata v. 17 (StataCorp LLC. College Station, Texas, USA). The data were described using descriptive statistics including frequencies, percentages and proportions to provide insights into the distribution within communities. Point prevalence estimates were arrived at by dividing the number of FTS positivity against total numbers tested community-wise, alongside their 95% CIs by normal approximation. The associations between risk factors and FTS positivity were estimated using ORs together with their 95% CI and p values using the Wald's Method. Maps were drawn in ArcGIS 10.8. District prevalence spatial maps were created using the Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW) interpolation method, based on the prevalence observed at the community level.

Thematic analysis was performed on the qualitative data using MAXQDA 2020 software. The quantitative data from the questionnaires were analyzed to provide insights into the intervention's acceptability.

Results

Participant characteristics

Due to delays in sourcing FTS, the study could not be undertaken immediately after the MDA in 2022. It was therefore conducted 6 mo after the MDA. During the 4 mo of the study, 25 068 people, aged 5–95 y, were reached; 56.54% were females and 43.46% were males. Also, 6264 (24.99%) had their names in the treatment registers while 18 804 (70.01%) did not. Overall, 93.13% of people reached were treated, including 98.80% in the E&T group and 68.65% in the T&T group; 86 of 723 FTS positives refused treatment.

Infection prevalence

Testing was conducted on 9958 people. This revealed a prevalence rate of 7.26% (95% CI 6.76 to 7.79%). The prevalence in males (7.97%; 95% CI 7.13 to 8.86%) was higher than in females (6.81%; 95% CI 6.19 to 7.47%). Similarly, the prevalence in the T&T group (12.10%; 95% CI 10.43 to 13.92%) was higher than in the E&T group (6.47%; 95% CI 5.96 to 7.01%) (Tables 1 and 2). Supplementary Table 1 shows the prevalence among the different age groups in the population. The highest prevalence of 11.47% (95% CI 8.52 to 15.00%) was reported among children aged 5–9 y, followed by the older population aged >40 y. The odds of testing positive for LF were higher in males (OR=1.18; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.38; p=0.03); the T&T group (OR=1.99; 95% CI 1.66 to 2.39; p<0.0001); and missing three consecutive rounds of MDA (OR=2.22; 95% CI 1.86 to 2.64; p<0.0001) (Table 3).

Supplementary Table 2 shows the prevalence among study participants and communities assessed. Infection was observed in 30 communities. The prevalence of LF in the communities varied from 0.5% to 100%. While there is heterogeneity in the infection prevalence, correlation analysis with non-compliance rates was not significant. Thus, other factors may be at play. The outputs of the IDW interpolation method were observed to have a >80% chance of LF infection in the southwest part of the district (Figure 1).

Re-estimated coverages

In the ESPEN portal, 71 916 individuals received treatment, achieving a therapeutic coverage of 82.05% during the routine MDA. The targeted population was 87 651, while the actual need was 125 044, resulting in an epidemiological coverage of 57.51%. In this study, an additional 23 347 people were treated from a reach of 25 068, bringing the total treated in the district to 95 263. This exceeded the initial target by 7612 individuals. After accounting for unregistered individuals, the eligible targeted population was re-estimated at 106 455, with a revised therapeutic coverage of 89.49% and an epidemiological coverage of 76.18%.

Adherence to MDA and LF positivity

Of those reached, 14 388, 6198 and 4481 people missed MDA once, twice and three times in the last 3 y, respectively. Among those tested by the FTS who missed MDA once, twice and three times, the antigen prevalence was 6.22%, 5.43% and 12.82%, respectively. A high percentage of people who missed MDA was observed in the southern half of the district (Figure 2).

Distribution of participants that missed one, two and three rounds of mass drug administration (MDA).

The reasons for missing MDA included travel, refusal, being unaware of MDA, absence, pregnancy or breastfeeding and sickness (Table S3). Among those tested, the highest prevalence was observed among those who traveled (8.04%), were absent during the MDA (8.92%) or were sick (7.06%). FTS positivity among those who refused MDA was 6.99%.

Discussion

The elimination of LF as a disease of public health concern requires the implementation of MDA in all disease-endemic communities, and at sufficiently high coverage to push infection below the transmission thresholds. However, this has not been without challenges; notable among them is the presence of hotspots of infections and the inability to pass pretransmission assessment surveys.15 Studies have assessed the social and other environmental risk factors of infection hotspots.16,17 In our earlier studies, we developed and evaluated the E&T and T&T strategies to address the challenges with MDA compliance and treatment coverage.12 This study revealed that these strategies do indeed result in the identification and treatment of individuals who failed to be part of the MDA. It also showed that many people in the district miss MDA and are not reached for various reasons.

Generally, higher infection rates were observed in males compared with females, as observed in other studies. However, this study underscores the importance of individuals in this district who seldom or never take part in MDA. Prevalence assessments revealed high antigen positivity among individuals who rarely or never participate in MDA, with a higher prevalence among those who refuse MDA compared with those who miss MDA for other reasons. Other studies have shown infection to be positively associated with not taking MDA medications.18 In this study, we also identified FTS positives who refused treatment despite all engagements. These individuals therefore represent potential reservoirs of infection, that could contribute to the continued transmission of LF in these settings and thus the persistent difficulties in achieving elimination.

Antigen prevalence was observed in all age groups. The prevalence in children is suggestive of ongoing transmission, as observed in Samoa.19 However, contrary to the Samoan results, the highest prevalence was observed among children aged 5–9 y, similar to previous observations in the Ahanta West District.12

The concentration of high prevalence of infections in the middle to the southern part of the district may also be due to the high MDA non-treatment rates of >60% observed in the southern part of the district. However, this could also be influenced by various factors such as environmental conditions, human behavior and vectors. Earlier studies have shown that the conditions in the Western Region of Ghana are suitable for the breeding of Anopheles melas, which are very effective vectors of W. bancrofti.20 Thus, the coastal areas of the district with its interlacing rivers may provide unique ecological conditions that impact vector distribution and LF transmission.21 In these settings, supplementing MDA with vector control may help in accelerating elimination.

Understanding the reasons behind non-treatment with MDA is crucial for designing effective intervention strategies.22 The main factors in this study that contributed to missing one, two or three successive rounds of MDA were travel, being unaware of MDA and absence. This was followed by the refusal to participate because of the fear of side effects and low-risk perceptions. Implementing community engagement programs, involving local leaders and influencers, as well as tailoring health education initiatives to address specific concerns within subpopulations, can improve awareness and acceptance.23 Indeed, the E&T and T&T interventions implemented in this study hinged heavily on the training of the CDDs and CHNs to address the concerns of the community members and the education materials developed and translated into the local language. This resulted in more people being treated, with an improvement in the treatment coverage. Thus, emphasis on the need for targeted community mobilization, promotional activities and a review of MDA practices to improve compliance and coverage are vital.24 The review of MDA registers for LF underscores the importance of identifying untreated individuals and addressing the effectiveness of MDA and efforts to eliminate LF at the community and program levels. By addressing these challenges, the effectiveness of MDA and the overall efforts to eliminate LF can be enhanced. In these settings of persistent transmission, the E&T and T&T strategies may further be enhanced by taking a census of the populations and comparing them with the records in the MDA registers to assess the number of people who miss MDA.

Given the experience of these strategies and using them to identify non-adherent community members goes a long way to improving MDA. These strategies would, however, have to be customized according to district specifics to achieve maximum output. The strategies can be expanded to other regions by replicating what has been done in this study, which is starting the process immediately after the MDA and making use of the MDA register to identify defaulters and then using the snowball strategy to locate those not in the register. The main tool for engagement is the information, education, and communication (IEC) poster, which must be developed for effective engagement in the local language.

The approach to engagement emphasized clear communication by outlining the study's objectives and benefits. Engagement efforts highlighted the importance of community participation. Posters were translated into local languages using simple, jargon-free language for better understanding. Building trust was crucial; participants received immediate results, leading many who tested positive for FTS to request prompt treatment. Involving CHNs improved the strategies, as they were seen as professional and trustworthy. Individuals were provided with detailed information about LF, explaining that one can live with the infection for a long time before symptoms appear. The information highlighted that participation in the program would help eliminate the infection and ensure safer communities for future generations. Potential side effects were discussed, and participants were assured that most would resolve within hours or a few days, with access to free healthcare for any treatment needed.

Many people were not captured in the MDA registers. The response from the CDDs regarding this point to the knowledge of seasonal workers and migrant populations in the district. These individuals may act as reservoirs carrying the infection between locations depending on the duration of their stay.25 Our earlier studies comparing W. bancrofti populations from Southern and Northern Ghana (albeit with small sample sizes) revealed that while there is considerable genetic variability within W. bancrofti populations in Ghana, the closeness of samples from both areas could be a result of migration of microfilaremic individuals.26 Gold mining in the district also leads to the resettlement of communities and may compound the challenges.27 Thus, migration, especially for economic activities, with the western regions experiencing one of the highest rates of internal migration in Ghana,28 could result in the transfer of parasites across the country. There is therefore a need to strengthen the ability of the NTD program and the health system to effectively identify and address the MDA non-treatment among this population. Undertaking MDA within specific periods has been shown to not always be adequate.29 Strategies such as the E&T and T&T extending treatment outside the routine MDA period may help address these challenges.

The intervention was shown to be acceptable (data not reported here). The cost of implementation in earlier studies has been modeled. While implementing the E&T and T&T approaches in combination with MDA is much more expensive, the high coverage may offset the increased cost of the interventions and help attain elimination faster.30 These strategies are also not expected to be implemented in all areas. Settings with persistent transmission, where investigations reveal a significant number of individuals who seldom or never participate in MDA, districts that have failed pre-TAS or TAS, and where the resurgence of infection has been demonstrated, would be ideal for implementation.30

Some limitations were observed in this study. Assessing FTS-positive individuals for microfilaria was challenging. In many cases, individuals requested to be treated immediately. Some communities were very remote and not easily accessible and safe for night blood collection by CHNs. When the night blood samples were collected into EDTA tubes, they were not properly mixed and coagulated, thus preventing the preparation of the slides for microscopy. The CHNs are used to perform malaria RDTs and thus the FTS. However, training to collect fingerprick blood into EDTA tubes is not a common practice and improved training should be considered in future studies. Despite this limitation, the collected samples have been stored and are archived as part of a biobank to support future studies. Another challenge was the refusal of some CDDs to take part in the interventions. These CDDs, limited to specific communities, requested higher remunerations that could not be implemented across the entire district. It is therefore very likely that the number of eligible people in the district who missed MDA is much higher than reported in this study.

In conclusion, the results of this study revealed a high prevalence of infections among people who seldom or never take part in MDA. Sustained infection rates among these individuals pose a potential threat to elimination initiatives. Implementing targeted interventions for the most hesitant individuals has the potential to improve the number and coverage of individuals receiving treatment. The E&T and T&T strategies can reach and treat the majority of these people.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the NMIMR Institutional Review Board (CPN 021/19-20). Consent was obtained from such individuals after securing verbal permission to share the information contained on the poster with them. The participants were then asked whether they were willing to participate. Once they accepted, they were taken through the consent process, which gives the first option for the E&T, followed by T&T if they refused to take the drugs at the first engagement. All treatments were provided through the structures established by the Ghana NTD Programme. Because the intervention was implemented as part of a public health program, written informed consent was only obtained from participants who were tested or interviewed by the study team.
