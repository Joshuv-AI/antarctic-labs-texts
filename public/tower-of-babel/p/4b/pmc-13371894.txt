A fungal subtilase-like protease SBT1 blocks rice immunity by erasing the MAPKKKα signaling node
Authors: Yuan Fang, Zhaoyun Wang, Wenjing Li, Ji Wu, Jing Chen, Qiutao Xu, Tom Hsiang, Xiaoyang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rice (Oryza sativa) is highly susceptible to a variety of devastating fungal diseases. However, whether these phylogenetically distinct fungal pathogens employ conserved effectors to disrupt common immune hubs remains unclear. Here, we identify a conserved virulence strategy in which multiple rice-infecting fungal pathogens employ a subtilisin-like protease effector, SBT1, to suppress host immunity. Upon secretion into plant cells, SBT1 directly targets the key immune signaling component OsMAPKKKα and mediates its degradation in a proteolytic activity-dependent manner. OsMAPKKKα acts in a linear signaling cascade: it is phosphorylated and stabilized by OsRLCK185 at Thr493, and subsequently phosphorylates OsMKK4 to activate downstream MAPK signaling. By degrading OsMAPKKKα, SBT1 disrupts the chitin-triggered immune signaling transduced through the OsCERK1–OsRLCK185–OsMAPKKKα–OsMKK4 module. Importantly, host-induced gene silencing (HIGS) targeting SBT1 confers broad-spectrum resistance against multiple fungal pathogens. Our study unveils a previously unexplored pathogen strategy wherein a secreted effector protease dismantles a central signaling node, and identifies OsMAPKKKα as a promising target for engineering disease-resistant crops.

INTRODUCTION

To counteract pathogen invasion, plants employ a biphasic immune system consisting of pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) (1, 2). These two tiers operate synergistically: PTI provides basal resistance through cell surface-localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), while ETI mediates stronger, often hypersensitive responses via intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) proteins (3). Immune activation elicits a defense cascade, including rapid Ca2+ influx, reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst, activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, callose deposition, and transcriptional reprogramming of defense-related genes (4). PTI is initiated upon the perception of PAMPs by PRRs at the plasma membrane (5). Chitin, a vital component of fungal cell walls, is one of the most extensively studied and classic PAMPs, and is recognized by LysM domain-containing receptor-like kinases (RLKs) on the plant plasma membrane (6). The receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase (RLCK) family, which lacks extracellular domains, has emerged as a central component that transduces PRR-mediated signaling into various early signaling events in plants (7). Plant MAPK cascades are highly conserved and convergent signaling pathways that transduce diverse extracellular signals to downstream targets, such as transcription factors and enzymes (8). The canonical activation of a MAPK cascade requires a three-component signal relay including a MAPKKK (MEKK), a MAPKK (MEK), and a MAPK (ERK) (9). The MAPKKK is activated by an upstream regulator upon stimulation and then phosphorylates the MAPKK in its activation loop (9). In rice, chitin is recognized by the receptor-like protein OsCEBiP, which then heterodimerizes with its co-receptor, chitin elicitor receptor kinase 1 (OsCERK1), to initiate intracellular signaling (10). OsCERK1 transduces chitin-triggered immune signals by phosphorylating downstream RLCKs, such as OsRLCK118/176/185 (11–13). However, the mechanistic details of how RLCKs regulate MAPK cascades in chitin signaling remain incompletely understood in rice.

To overcome PTI, pathogens deliver effectors into host plants to suppress chitin-induced immunity. For instance, the chitin-binding effector MoChia1 competes with host LysM receptors for chitin binding in the apoplast, thereby preventing immune activation (14). Protective effector SnTox1 shields fungal chitin from hydrolysis by plant chitinases, which blocks the release of immunogenic fragments (15). Polysaccharide deacetylase VdPDA1 deacetylates chitin oligomers into non-immunogenic chitosan to evade detection (16). Glycoside hydrolase UvGH18.1 targets the chitin receptor OsCEBiP and co-receptor OsCERK1 independently of its chitinase activity, thereby impairing chitin-induced dimerization of OsCEBiP and OsCERK1 (17). However, it remains unclear whether fungal-secreted effectors disrupt the transduction of chitin-triggered immune signals from RLCKs to the MAPK cascades.

Serine proteases constitute the largest and most important superfamily of proteases. Their core feature is a “catalytic triad” composed of serine (Ser), histidine (His), and aspartic acid (Asp), which work together to hydrolyze peptide bonds via a covalent catalysis mechanism. Serine proteases are divided into the S8 and S53 families. The S8 family is further divided into two subfamilies: S8A (subtilisin) and S8B (kexin). Subtilases/subtilisin-like proteases (SBTs) are members of the S8A subfamily (18). Most SBTs contain three relatively conserved domains. The first is the catalytic domain, termed the peptidase S8 domain or the subtilase domain (19). The other two are the protease-associated domain (PA domain) and the inhibitor I9 domain (20). The peptidase S8 domain is responsible for the catalytic function of the protease. The PA domain is involved in protein-protein interactions and substrate recognition. The inhibitor I9 domain is an autoinhibitory domain that maintains the zymogen in an inactive state, prevents the substrate from accessing the active site, and also acts as a chaperone that assists in protein folding (21). SBTs are widely distributed in plants, bacteria, and fungi, serve as important virulence factors for many plant-pathogenic fungi (22). For example, in Magnaporthe oryzae, knockout of the SBT gene SPM1 significantly reduces virulence and conidiation (23). Similarly, SBTs regulate virulence in the entomopathogenic fungus Beauveria bassiana (24). In Fusarium graminearum, the SBT gene FgPrb1 modulates virulence and toxin synthesis and is also involved in autophagy and lipid metabolism (25). The subtilisin-like protease UvPr1a, secreted by Ustilaginoidea virens, directly degrades the defense protein OsSGT1 to suppress rice immunity (26). In addition to fungi, in oomycetes such as Phytophthora infestans, the secreted PC2 protein requires cleavage by the tomato subtilisin-like protease P69B to activate immunity (27). In bacteria, rhizosphere symbionts secrete the subtilisin-like protease IssA via the type II secretion system, which cleaves the plant immune peptides flg22 and AtPEP1, thereby alleviating immune-mediated suppression of root growth (28). Despite their established roles in virulence, the molecular mechanisms by which fungal SBTs manipulate specific host targets to suppress immunity remain largely unknown.

Rice is a staple food for over half the world’s population, making its sustainable production critical for global food security (29). Nevertheless, its productivity is severely threatened by a diversity of fungal pathogens, including the rice blast agent M. oryzae, the rice sheath blight agent Rhizoctonia solani, and the rice false smut agent U. virens. In the ongoing co-evolutionary arms race between rice and these pathogens, the precise virulence mechanisms employed by diverse fungi have remained elusive. Here, we demonstrate that multiple rice-infecting fungal pathogens have convergently evolved a secreted effector, SBT1, which suppresses host immunity by directly targeting and degrading the key immune signaling component OsMAPKKKα. This degradation impairs the phosphorylation of the downstream kinase OsMKK4, thereby suppressing the chitin-triggered immune signaling cascade mediated by the OsRLCK185–OsMAPKKKα–OsMKK4 module. Our findings reveal a previously unexplored virulence strategy in which a secreted subtilase effector dismantles a central immune signaling node, thereby blocking plant immunity at the receptor-to-MAPK transduction level.

RESULTS

SBT1 is essential for the development and virulence of U. virens

From the infection secretome of U. virens (Fig. 1A), we identified 24 secreted proteins (Fig. 1B; table S1). One of the secreted proteins was UvSBT1 (UV8b_01827), which encodes a 531-amino acid protein with a signal peptide (SP), an Inhibitor I9 domain, and a Peptidase S8 domain, representing a typical SBT (fig. S1A). Phylogenetic analysis showed that UvSBT1 homologs are widely distributed across fungal species (fig. S1B). Predicted 3D structures indicated high structural similarity among SBT1 proteins from M. oryzae, Rhophitulus solani, and U. virens (fig. S1C). UvSBT1 was significantly upregulated during infection (Fig. 1C), suggesting a critical role in the U. virens-rice interaction. We confirmed the function of its predicted signal peptide using a yeast secretion assay. Yeast transformants expressing UvSBT1SP or the positive control Avr1bSP grew on both SD-Trp and YPRAA media and secreted invertase, as indicated by the formation of red 1,3,5-triphenylformazan (TPF) in a 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) assay. In contrast, cells carrying the empty vector pSUC2 failed to grow on YPRAA and produced no TPF (Fig. 1D). These results demonstrated that the predicted SP of UvSBT1 functionally directs SUC2 secretion.

To further verify whether U. virens secretes UvSBT1, we extracted total proteins from the filtrate and mycelia of the C∆Uvsbt1–1 strain cultured in shake flasks for 7 days. Western blot analysis revealed the presence of UvSBT1-Flag and actin in the mycelia, whereas only UvSBT1-Flag was detected in the filtrate, confirming that UvSBT1 is secreted from the mycelium into the extracellular space (fig. S2A). To determine whether UvSBT1 is secreted into rice cells, we performed immunofluorescence assays. UvSBT1-Flag signals were detected in both infected hyphae and stamen filaments at 6 days post inoculation (dpi) (Fig. 1E), confirming that UvSBT1 is secreted into rice cells. In addition, UvSBT1-Flag was detected in samples from spikelets inoculated with the C∆Uvsbt1–1, but not in those inoculated with the wild-type HWD-2, demonstrating that UvSBT1 can be secreted into plant cells during infection (fig. S2B). To investigate its subcellular localization in plant cells, we expressed a UvSBT1-GFP fusion in N. benthamiana leaves. Fluorescence signals were observed in the nucleus, at the plasma membrane, and in the cytoplasm (fig. S3A). After plasmolysis, UvSBT1-GFP fluorescence remained associated with the nucleus, plasma membrane, and cytoplasm, but not the apoplast (fig. S3B). Transient expression in rice protoplasts also showed localization at the plasma membrane and in the cytoplasm (fig. S3C). These results collectively demonstrated that SBT1 is secreted into plant cells during infection.

We generated UvSBT1 knockout mutants (ΔUvsbt1–1 and ΔUvsbt1–2) and a complementation strain (CΔUvsbt1–1) in U. virens, with all transformants verified by PCR (fig. S4, A to C). Deletion of UvSBT1 led to reduced mycelial growth compared to the wild-type strain HWD-2 and the complemented strain CΔUvsbt1–1 (Fig. 1F), but it did not noticeably affect conidia production (Fig. 1G). Pathogenicity assays showed that the ΔUvsbt1 mutants failed to form smut balls on rice spikelets, exhibiting a complete loss of virulence (Fig. 1, H and I). Scanning electron microscopy revealed that hyphae of HWD-2 and CΔUvsbt1–1 extensively colonized the inner tissues of rice spikelets at 6 dpi, whereas ΔUvsbt1 mutants hyphae were undetectable on filament surfaces (Fig. 1J), indicating a severe defect in early colonization or attachment. Similarly, deletion of MoSBT1 significantly reduced the virulence of M. oryzae (fig. S4, D to F). Functional complementation of the ΔUvsbt1–1 mutant with either MoSBT1 or RsSBT1 restored the virulence of U. virens (fig. S4G). Taken together, these results established SBT1 as a key and functionally conserved virulence regulator in rice-pathogenic fungi.

To assess the functional contributions of the Inhibitor I9 and Peptidase S8 domains, we generated truncated variants, UvSBT1∆I9 (lacking the Inhibitor I9 domain) and UvSBT1∆S8 (lacking the Peptidase S8 domain), and generated complementation strains in the ∆Uvsbt1–1 mutant background. Phenotypic assays demonstrated that both the Inhibitor I9 and Peptidase S8 domains of UvSBT1 are indispensable for U. virens virulence (fig. S5, A and B). Given that SBTs are an important class of serine-centered proteolytic enzymes (9), we next investigated whether the proteolytic activity of UvSBT1 is required for the development and virulence of U. virens. Based on the conservation of homologous SBT1 protein sequences, we introduced mutations at five sites. Through pathogenicity screening, we found that the CΔUvsbt1K244R-1 and CΔUvsbt1S389/392A-1 mutants significantly reduced the virulence of U. virens, whereas the CΔUvsbt1T213A-1, CΔUvsbt1T345A-1, and CΔUvsbt1K291R-1 did not (fig. S6A). Additionally, enzyme activity assays revealed that the S389/392A double mutation affected its protease activity (Fig. S6B). Therefore, the CΔUvsbt1S389/392A-1 mutant was selected as an enzymatic activity mutant for subsequent experiments and was renamed CΔUvsbt1M-1. This catalytic mutant CΔUvsbt1M-1 exhibited significantly reduced mycelial growth and virulence compared to the CΔUvsbt1–1 (fig. S6, C and D). Taken together, these findings indicated that the enzymatic activity of UvSBT1 is crucial for the mycelial growth and virulence of U. virens.

To investigate whether the SP of UvSBT1 is related to the virulence of U. virens, we generated a UvSBT1 construct lacking the SP sequence and introduced it into the ΔUvsbt1–1 mutant. The resulting CΔUvsbt1ΔSP-1 transformant showed no difference in mycelial growth compared to CΔUvsbt1–1 (fig. S7A). However, it produced significantly fewer smut balls (fig. S7B), indicating that secretion is essential for UvSBT1 to function as a virulence factor.

Overall, these results indicate that the effector SBT1 is required for the development and virulence of U. virens.

Heterologous overexpression of UvSBT1 compromises rice immunity

To assess the immunosuppressive role of UvSBT1, we generated transgenic rice overexpressing UvSBT1 (lines UvSBT1-OE1 and UvSBT1-OE3). These lines showed normal morphology compared to empty vector (EV, pCNG) controls (fig. S8, A to D). However, after inoculation with U. virens, the UvSBT1-OE plants produced significantly more smut balls than EV plants (Fig. 2A). Following spray-inoculation with M. oryzae, the UvSBT1-OE plants were also more susceptible to M. oryzae than EV plants (Fig. 2B). The UvSBT1-OE plants were also more susceptible to Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) than EV plants, as assessed by the scissor-clipping method (Fig. 2C). Likewise, the UvSBT1-OE plants showed increased susceptibility to R. solani relative to EV plants (Fig. 2D). These results indicated that the heterologous overexpression of UvSBT1 enhances the susceptibility of rice plants to multiple pathogens.

To verify further that UvSBT1 suppresses rice immunity, we examined the responses of UvSBT1-OE and EV plants to the PAMP elicitor chitin. Upon chitin treatment, UvSBT1-OE plants exhibited reduced callose deposition in their cell walls compared to EV plants (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, immunoblot analysis with an anti-Phospho-p44/42 antibody showed that the MAPK signaling pathway was suppressed in UvSBT1-OE plants (Fig. 2F). Luminol-based chemiluminescence assays also revealed a significant reduction in chitin-triggered ROS production in UvSBT1-OE plants relative to EV plants (Fig. 2G). Additionally, the expression of defense-related genes, including PATHOGENESIS-RELATED 1b (OsPR1b), OsPR10a, OsWRKY71, and OsRBOHA, was significantly downregulated in the spikelets of UvSBT1-OE plants following chitin treatment (Fig. 2H). These results demonstrated that heterologous overexpression of UvSBT1 suppresses chitin-induced rice immunity.

SBT1 interacts with OsMAPKKKα

To elucidate the mechanisms of UvSBT1 as a virulence effector, we screened a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) library constructed from U. virens-infected rice spikelets at 6 dpi, which identified 32 candidate interactors (table S2). OsMAPKKKα transcript levels were markedly lower after infection with U. virens (fig. S9A), suggesting that OsMAPKKKα is involved in rice resistance to U. virens. Therefore, we selected OsMAPKKKα as a putative target of UvSBT1 for characterization. Pairwise Y2H assays confirmed the interaction between UvSBT1 and OsMAPKKKα (Fig. 3A). In vitro pull-down assays showed specific co-precipitation of OsMAPKKKα-His with UvSBT1-GST, but not with GST (Fig. 3B). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) in rice protoplasts confirmed that OsMAPKKKα-Flag specifically co-precipitated with UvSBT1-GFP, but not with GFP (Fig. 3C). Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays revealed reconstituted YFP fluorescence in the cytoplasm when OsMAPKKKα-nYFP and UvSBT1-cYFP were co-expressed in rice protoplasts, confirming their interaction (Fig. 3D). Luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays detected luminescence signals when OsMAPKKKα-cLUC and UvSBT1-nLUC constructs were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. In contrast, no signal was detected when OsMAPKKKα-cLUC was co-expressed with nLUC or when UvSBT1-nLUC was co-expressed with cLUC (Fig. 3E). The interaction was further supported by the co-localization of UvSBT1-RFP with OsMAPKKKα-GFP in the nucleus and cytoplasm (fig. S10A). Importantly, GST-pull down, Co-IP, and LCI assays demonstrated that RsSBT1 and MoSBT1 also interact with OsMAPKKKα (fig. S11, A to C), demonstrating that the interaction with OsMAPKKKα is a conserved function of SBT1 effectors across multiple rice fungal pathogens.

SBT1 accelerates the degradation of OsMAPKKKα

Subtilases are proteolytic enzymes capable of degrading diverse substrates (22). Given this proteolytic function, we investigated whether UvSBT1 degrades OsMAPKKKα. We conducted an in vitro degradation assay by incubating recombinant OsMAPKKKα-His with UvSBT1-GST, UvSBT1M-GST (catalytic mutant), or GST. The assays showed faster degradation of OsMAPKKKα-His when incubated with UvSBT1-GST compared to UvSBT1M-GST (Fig. 3F). Consistently, the Peptidase S8 domain (UvSBT1S8) mediated the degradation of OsMAPKKKα, while the Inhibitor I9 domain (UvSBT1I9) did not (fig. S10B), suggesting protease activity-dependent degradation. Interestingly, upon co-incubation of OsMAPKKKα-His with UvSBT1-Flag, we did not detect a cleavage band (fig. S10C), suggesting that UvSBT1 may process OsMAPKKKα into small peptides or amino acids that are not detected under our experimental conditions. In rice protoplasts, co-expression of OsMAPKKKα-Flag with UvSBT1-GFP, but not with UvSBT1M-GFP or GFP, resulted in reduced abundance of OsMAPKKKα (Fig. 3G), confirming UvSBT1-mediated OsMAPKKKα degradation in vivo. In eukaryotes, proteins are primarily degraded through the ubiquitin-26S proteasome pathway or the autophagy pathway (23). Indeed, treatment with MG132 and 3-MA did not significantly affect the degradation of OsMAPKKKα by UvSBT1 (fig. S10, D and E), indicating that the 26S proteasome and autophagy are not involved in the degradation process. However, treatment with the protease inhibitor PMSF significantly reduced the degradation of OsMAPKKKα by UvSBT1 in vivo and in vitro (fig. S10, F and G), indicating that the degradation depends on the protease activity of UvSBT1. Transient overexpression of OsMAPKKKα triggered PCD in N. benthamiana leaves, which was suppressed by co-expression with UvSBT1, and immunoblot analysis confirmed that UvSBT1 reduced OsMAPKKKα protein levels (fig. S10H). This immunosuppressive function is conserved, as RsSBT1 and MoSBT1 also degraded OsMAPKKKα (fig. S11, D and E). Notably, despite the interaction between UvSBT1 and OsSGT1 demonstrated by GST-pull down, Co-IP, and LCI assays, UvSBT1 does not directly degrade OsSGT1 (fig. S12, A to E). UvSBT1 also does not degrade other proteins, such as OsCERK1, OsRLCK185, OsMKK4, and OsMAPKKKε in vitro (fig. S13), implying a substrate specificity for its proteolytic activity. In summary, these results indicate that UvSBT1 directly degrades OsMAPKKKα in a proteolytic activity-dependent manner, thereby suppressing OsMAPKKKα-mediated plant immunity.

OsMAPKKKα positively regulates multi-pathogen resistance

To determine the biological function of OsMAPKKKα, we generated OsMAPKKKα-overexpression (lines OsMAPKKKα-OE1 and OsMAPKKKα-OE2) and empty vector (EV, pU1301) transgenic rice plants. The OsMAPKKKα-OE plants showed normal morphology and agronomic traits (fig. S14, A to C). However, after inoculation with U. virens, the OsMAPKKKα-OE plants produced significantly fewer smut balls than EV plants (Fig. 3H). The OsMAPKKKα-OE plants also showed increased resistance to M. oryzae relative to EV plants (Fig. 3I). Furthermore, the OsMAPKKKα-OE plants were more resistant to R. solani than EV plants (Fig. 3J). Likewise, the OsMAPKKKα-OE plants were more resistant to Xoo than EV plants (Fig. 3K). In addition, ROS production was higher in OsMAPKKKα-OE plants than in EV plants, as measured by luminol-based chemiluminescence assays (Fig. 3L). In rice spikelets, defense-associated genes including OsPR1b, OsPAL4, OsWRKY71, and OsRBOHA were significantly upregulated in OsMAPKKKα-OE spikelets compared to EV following chitin treatment (Fig. 3M). These results demonstrated that OsMAPKKKα-overexpression activates rice immunity and disease resistance.

We also created OsMAPKKKα knockout mutants (mapkkkα) using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. These mutants displayed wild-type morphology and agronomic traits (fig. S15, A to C). Compared with ZH11 plants, mapkkkα plants exhibited enhanced susceptibility to U. virens, M. oryzae, R. solani, and Xoo (fig. S15, D to G), indicating that OsMAPKKKα positively regulates broad-spectrum resistance without affecting agronomic traits. Chitin-triggered immune responses were also impaired in mapkkkα mutants. We observed reduced callose deposition in mapkkkα plant cell walls compared to ZH11 leaves (fig. S15H). After exogenous chitin application, MAPK pathway activity showed significant inhibition in mapkkkα plants relative to ZH11 plants (fig. S15I). In addition, ROS production was lower in mapkkkα plants than in ZH11 plants, as measured by luminol-based chemiluminescence assays (fig. S15J). In rice spikelets, defense-associated genes including OsPR1b, OsPR10a, OsWRKY71, and OsRBOHA were significantly downregulated in mapkkkα spikelets compared to ZH11 following chitin treatment (fig. S15K). Collectively, these results demonstrated that OsMAPKKKα knockout compromises rice immune responses.

Given the importance of conserved lysine residue in active protein kinase (30), it has been reported in tomato that the K231M mutation leads to a complete loss of SiMAPKKKα kinase activity (31). Based on the high sequence conservation, we mutated the conserved lysine (K) in OsMAPKKKα to methionine (M) (OsMAPKKKαK276M) (fig. S16A). Transient expression of OsMAPKKKα triggers rapid cell death in N. benthamiana, while the kinase-deficient OsMAPKKKαK276M did not (fig. S16B). We performed in vitro Phos-tag phosphorylation assays, which confirmed that K276 is a critical residue for the kinase activity of OsMAPKKKα (fig. S16C). To investigate whether the kinase activity of OsMAPKKKα affects disease resistance in rice, we generated complementation plants in the mapkkkα-1 mutant background. These plants expressed either the full-length OsMAPKKKα or the K276M point mutant under the control of the native OsMAPKKKα promoter, designated as OsMAPKKKα-COM and OsMAPKKKαK276M-COM plants, respectively (fig. S16D). We found that the OsMAPKKKαK276M-COM plants were more susceptible to M. oryzae compared to the OsMAPKKKα-COM plants (fig. S16E). These results demonstrated that the kinase activity of OsMAPKKKα is critical for rice immunity.

OsRLCK185 stabilizes OsMAPKKKα through phosphorylation at Thr493

To explore how OsMAPKKKα functions in rice immunity, we screened for its interacting proteins using a Y2H assay (table S3). Among the candidates, we focused on OsRLCK185, a receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase known to function in chitin-induced plant immunity (11). To validate its role, we generated OsRLCK185 knockout plants (rlck185) via CRISPR/Cas9, which showed normal morphology but increased susceptibility to U. virens (fig. S17, A to D), indicating that OsRLCK185 positively regulates rice resistance to rice false smut. To confirm OsRLCK185 interaction with OsMAPKKKα, we first examined their binding by a Y2H assay and found that OsRLCK185 directly interacted with OsMAPKKKα (Fig. 4A). We also performed an in vitro pull-down assay using purified recombinant proteins OsRLCK185-GST and OsMAPKKKα-His. The results showed that OsMAPKKKα-His was pulled down by OsRLCK185-GST, but not by GST, confirming their direct interaction (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, a Co-IP assay revealed the formation of a complex containing OsMAPKKKα-Flag and OsRLCK185-GFP in rice protoplasts (Fig. 4C). In addition, an LCI assay conducted in N. benthamiana leaves detected luminescent signals when OsRLCK185-nLUC and OsMAPKKKα-cLUC were co-expressed (Fig. 4D). This interaction was further validated by a BiFC assay in rice protoplasts (Fig. 4E), which demonstrated that the interaction between the two proteins occurs in the cytoplasm.

Previous studies have demonstrated that OsRLCK185 phosphorylates OsMAPKKKε/18/24, thereby promoting chitin-triggered activation of OsMPK3/6 in rice (13). To explore whether OsRLCK185 phosphorylates OsMAPKKKα, we detected the phosphorylation level of OsMAPKKKα in protoplasts from wild-type ZH11 and rlck185 mutants. The results showed a reduced phosphorylation level of OsMAPKKKα in rlck185 mutants compared to ZH11 protoplasts (Fig. 4F). Consistently, OsMAPKKKα exhibited enhanced phosphorylation in OsRLCK185-OE plants relative to EV plants (fig. S18A). Furthermore, we confirmed the phosphorylation of OsMAPKKKα by OsRLCK185 in rice protoplasts co-expressing OsMAPKKKαK276M-Flag and OsRLCK185-HA (Fig. 4G). Additionally, we incubated purified OsMAPKKKαK276M-His protein with either OsRLCK185-GST or the kinase-deficient (11) OsRLCK185K108E-GST. OsRLCK185-GST effectively phosphorylated OsMAPKKKαK276M-His, while OsRLCK185K108E-GST showed no such activity (Fig. 4H). Altogether, these results indicated that OsRLCK185 can directly phosphorylate OsMAPKKKα both in vivo and in vitro.

To further identify the specific phosphorylation sites within OsMAPKKKα, we incubated OsMAPKKKαK276M-His with OsRLCK185-GST in an in vitro phosphorylation assay, and analyzed the tryptic peptides of OsMAPKKKα-His using mass spectrometry (MS). Thr493 in OsMAPKKKα was identified as a phosphorylation site targeted by OsRLCK185 (fig. S19A). We performed in vitro kinase assays using purified OsMAPKKKαK276M/T493A-His mutant proteins, confirming that Thr493 is the unique residue phosphorylated by OsRLCK185 (fig. S19B).

To investigate the role of OsMAPKKKα phosphorylation at Thr493 in regulating its protein abundance and stability, we performed cell-free degradation assays. These assays revealed that the purified OsMAPKKKαT493D-His fusion protein was more stable than either the OsMAPKKKα-His or the OsMAPKKKαT493A-His proteins when incubated with the total proteins from ZH11 leaves (Fig. 4I), indicating that phosphorylation of Thr493 stabilizes OsMAPKKKα in vitro. Furthermore, we expressed OsMAPKKKα-Flag, OsMAPKKKαT493A-Flag, or OsMAPKKKαT493D-Flag in protoplasts of ZH11 and rlck185 mutants. Immunoblot analysis showed that OsMAPKKKα-Flag was more prone to degradation in rlck185 mutants compared to ZH11 protoplasts. OsMAPKKKαT493D-Flag was more stable than OsMAPKKKα-Flag or OsMAPKKKαT493A-Flag when expressed in rlck185 mutant protoplasts (Fig. 4J). Accordingly, OsMAPKKKα-Flag was more stable in OsRLCK185-OE than in EV protoplasts. OsMAPKKKα-Flag was more stable than OsMAPKKKαT493A-Flag when expressed in OsRLCK185-OE protoplasts (fig. S18B), suggesting that OsRLCK185-mediated phosphorylation of OsMAPKKKα at Thr493 enhanced its stability in vivo. These results indicated that the Thr493 phosphorylation of OsMAPKKKα by OsRLCK185 enhances its stability.

To investigate the functional significance of Thr493 phosphorylation in OsMAPKKKα-mediated immunity, we generated transgenic rice expressing Flag-tagged OsMAPKKKα, its phosphorylation-deficient mutant (T493A), or phosphomimetic mutant (T493D) in mapkkkα-1 mutant background under the control of the native OsMAPKKKα promoter. The relative expression level of OsMAPKKKα in the selected complementation transgenic plants was comparable to that in the wild-type ZH11 (fig. S20A). Following spray-inoculation with M. oryzae, OsMAPKKKα-COM plants showed stronger resistance than OsMAPKKKαT493A-COM plants, but weaker than OsMAPKKKαT493D-COM plants (Fig. 4K). Accordingly, these plants showed consistent resistance to rice bacterial blight (fig. S20B). To investigate whether Thr493 is involved in the chitin-induced immune signaling pathway, we measured the phosphorylation levels of OsMAPKKKα in OsMAPKKKα-COM plants and OsMAPKKKαT493A-COM plants. We found that the phosphorylation level of OsMAPKKKα was significantly reduced in the OsMAPKKKαT493A-COM plants, indicating that Thr493 is critical for the chitin-induced immune response (fig. S21A). In addition, chitin treatment resulted in greater stability of the OsMAPKKKα protein in the OsMAPKKKαT493D-COM plants compared to the OsMAPKKKα-COM plants, and less stable in OsMAPKKKαT493A-COM plants than in OsMAPKKKα-COM plants (fig. S21B). These results demonstrated that Thr493 phosphorylation enhances the stability and immune function of OsMAPKKKα.

To determine whether OsRLCK185-mediated disease resistance depends on OsMAPKKKα, we overexpressed OsMAPKKKα in the rlck185–1 mutant background to generate rlck185–1/OsMAPKKKα-OE plants. Following spray inoculation with M. oryzae, we observed that rlck185–1 plants were more susceptible to the pathogen than rlck185–1/OsMAPKKKα-OE plants, whereas OsMAPKKKα-OE1 plants exhibited stronger resistance than rlck185–1/OsMAPKKKα-OE plants (Fig. 4L). These results indicate that the resistance conferred by OsRLCK185 is dependent on OsMAPKKKα.

OsMAPKKKα directly interacts with and phosphorylates OsMKK4

Having identified OsMKK4 as a candidate OsMAPKKKα-interactor from the Y2H screen (table S3), we performed targeted Y2H assays, which confirmed their interaction (Fig. 5A). Additionally, in vitro pull-down assays demonstrated that OsMKK4-His was specifically co-precipitated with OsMAPKKKα-GST, but not with GST, when incubated with glutathione agarose beads (Fig. 5B). Co-IP assays in rice protoplasts confirmed that OsMKK4-Flag specifically co-precipitated with OsMAPKKKα-GFP, but not with GFP (Fig. 5C). LCI assays detected luminescence signals upon co-expression of OsMAPKKKα-cLUC and OsMKK4-nLUC in N. benthamiana leaves. In contrast, no signal was detected when OsMAPKKKα-cLUC and OsMKK4-nLUC constructs were co-expressed with nLUC and cLUC, respectively (Fig. 5D). BiFC assays in rice protoplasts co-expressing OsMAPKKKα-nYFP and OsMKK4-cYFP fusion constructs reconstituted YFP fluorescence at the cytoplasm, confirming their interaction (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these assays indicated that OsMAPKKKα interacts with OsMKK4 in vitro and in vivo.

Having established the interaction, we next investigated whether OsMAPKKKα phosphorylates OsMKK4. The phosphorylation level of OsMKK4 was examined in protoplasts from wild-type ZH11 and mapkkkα mutants. The results showed a reduced phosphorylation level of OsMKK4 in mapkkkα mutants compared to ZH11 protoplasts (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, we confirmed the phosphorylation of OsMKK4 by OsMAPKKKα in rice protoplasts co-expressing OsMAPKKKα-Flag and the kinase-deficient (32) OsMKK4K122M-HA (Fig. 5G). Additionally, we performed an in vitro phosphorylation assay by incubating purified OsMKK4K122M-GST protein with either OsMAPKKKα-His or OsMAPKKKαK276M-His. Phos-tag SDS–PAGE analysis revealed that OsMAPKKKα-His effectively phosphorylated OsMKK4K122M-GST, while OsMAPKKKαK276M-His showed no such activity (Fig. 5H). OsMKK4 transcript levels were markedly lower after infection with U. virens (fig. S9B), suggesting that OsMKK4 is involved in rice resistance to U. virens. To determine the immunological significance of OsMKK4, we generated OsMKK4-overexpression (lines OsMKK4-OE3 and OsMKK4-OE6) plants. The OsMKK4-OE plants also showed increased resistance to U. virens, M. oryzae and R. solani relative to EV plants (Fig. 5I; fig. S22, A to D). This indicates that the resistance conferred by OsMAPKKKα is dependent on OsMKK4.

Collectively, these results indicated that OsMAPKKKα directly phosphorylates OsMKK4, thereby positioning OsMKK4 downstream of OsMAPKKKα in a rice immune signaling pathway.

UvSBT1 prevents the activation of immunity by the OsRLCK185-OsMAPKKKα-OsMKK4 module

To provide genetic evidence that UvSBT1 interferes with OsMAPKKKα, we inoculated wild-type ZH11 and mapkkkα mutant plants with the ∆Uvsbt1–1 strain. The ∆Uvsbt1–1 strain produced no false smut balls on ZH11 but a small number on mapkkkα mutants (Fig. 6A), indicating that OsMAPKKKα-conferred resistance depends on UvSBT1. Similarly, the ∆Mosbt1–1 strain infected ZH11 leaves at significantly lower levels than mapkkkα leaves (Fig. 6B), showing that OsMAPKKKα-conferred resistance also depends on MoSBT1. To test whether UvSBT1 secreted during infection affects OsMAPKKKα stability, we extracted total proteins from OsMAPKKKα-OE1 spikelets at 1 dpi with the indicated strains. Western blot analysis revealed that the OsMAPKKKα-Flag protein level was highest in samples infected with the ∆Uvsbt1–1 strain, intermediate with C∆Uvsbt1M-1 and C∆Uvsbt1∆SP-1, and lowest with the HWD-2 and C∆Uvsbt1–1 strains (Fig. 6C). These results indicate that UvSBT1, secreted by U. virens during infection, degrades the OsMAPKKKα. Consistently, OsMAPKKKα abundance was markedly reduced in the UvSBT1-OE plants compared to the EV plants (Fig. 6D). Moreover, OsRLCK185-mediated phosphorylation of OsMAPKKKα was not affected by UvSBT1 in vitro (Fig. 6E). Co-expression of UvSBT1-GFP with either OsMAPKKKα-Flag or OsMAPKKKαT493A-Flag in N. benthamiana revealed no significant difference in degradation, indicating that T493 phosphorylation does not affect UvSBT1-mediated degradation (Fig. 6F). We found that the phosphorylation levels of OsMKK4 was highest in samples infected with the ∆Uvsbt1–1 strain, followed by those infected with C∆Uvsbt1M-1 and C∆Uvsbt1∆SP-1, and lowest in samples infected with the HWD-2 and C∆Uvsbt1–1 strains (Fig. 6G), indicating that UvSBT1 secreted by U. virens during infection suppresses the phosphorylation level of OsMKK4. To investigate whether UvSBT1 impairs OsMKK4 phosphorylation, we measured OsMKK4 phosphorylation levels in EV plants and UvSBT1-OE plants using an anti-pSpT antibody. The results showed a significant reduction in the phosphorylation levels of OsMKK4 in UvSBT1-OE plants compared to the EV plants (Fig. 6H). There is no significant difference in the phosphorylation level of OsMKK4 between mapkkkα-1 and mapkkkα-1/OsRLCK185-OE plants (Fig. 6I), indicating the existence of an OsRLCK185-OsMAPKKKα-OsMKK4 cascade pathway. Given that OsMAPKKKα and OsMKK4 induce cell death in N. benthamiana, we treated rice leaves with purified OsMAPKKKα-His and OsMKK4-His and found that the treated leaves exhibited enhanced resistance to rice blast (fig. S23, A and B). We then treated EV and UvSBT1-OE1 rice leaves with purified OsMAPKKKα-His or OsMKK4-His protein. The results showed that callose deposition induced by these proteins was lower in UvSBT1-OE1 than in EV rice leaves (Fig. 6J). Subsequently, treatment of EV and UvSBT1-OE1 rice leaves with purified OsMAPKKKα-His or OsMKK4-His protein showed that ROS burst induced by these proteins was lower in UvSBT1-OE1 than in EV rice leaves, indicating that UvSBT1 reduced the ROS burst induced by either OsMAPKKKα or OsMKK4 (Fig. 6K). Collectively, these results demonstrated that secreted UvSBT1 promotes OsMAPKKKα degradation, thereby impairing OsMKK4 phosphorylation and inhibiting the chitin-induced signaling through the OsRLCK185-OsMAPKKKα-OsMKK4 cascade.

HIGS of UvSBT1 enhances multi-pathogen resistance

We identified a 342 bp fragment highly conserved in U. virens, M. oryzae, and R. solani SBT1 genes (fig. S24A). To test if silencing this fragment could confer rice resistance, we employed host-induced gene silencing (HIGS) targeting UvSBT1 by generating transgenic plants expressing an RNA interference (RNAi) construct specifically designed against UvSBT1 mRNA (fig. S24, B to D). Notably, T3 generation UvSBT1-HIGS plants (lines L1 and L2) exhibited agronomic traits indistinguishable from those of empty vector (EV, pDS1301) control plants (fig. S24E). When challenged with U. virens, UvSBT1-HIGS plants displayed significantly stronger resistance to U. virens compared to EV plants (Fig. 7A). Surprisingly, UvSBT1-HIGS plants also showed significantly enhanced resistance to M. oryzae and R. solani (Fig. 7, B and C). To confirm that the improved resistance observed in UvSBT1-HIGS plants was due to RNAi-mediated silencing of SBT1 within host tissues, infected spikelets and leaves were collected at 1 dpi for RT-qPCR analysis. As expected, a significant reduction in SBT1 transcript levels was detected in UvSBT1-HIGS plants compared to EV plants (Fig. 7D). To further verify that the reduction in fungal gene expression resulted from RNAi-derived small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), small RNA sequencing was conducted. The results indicated a substantial abundance of siRNAs corresponding to SBT1, accounting for 0.74% in T3-L2 plants. These siRNAs ranged from 18 to 30 nucleotides in length, with a predominant peak at 21 nucleotides (Fig. 7E). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) detected these siRNAs in both rice floral tissues and U. virens hyphae in infected T3-L2 plants at 6 dpi, whereas no signals were observed in EV plants (Fig. 7F), indicating that cross-kingdom RNAi transfers from host plants to fungal cells and effectively suppresses SBT1 expression during infection. Collectively, these results demonstrated that HIGS targeting UvSBT1 confers broad-spectrum resistance.

DISCUSSION

Rice blast, sheath blight, and false smut are among the most devastating fungal diseases of rice, causing substantial yield losses despite the plant’s sophisticated immune arsenal. Our study elucidates a common virulence strategy employed by these phylogenetically distinct pathogens: the deployment of a conserved subtilisin-like protease effector, SBT1. Since UvSBT1 contains a typical signal peptide, it likely follows the endoplasmic reticulum–Golgi secretion pathway, as do most secreted proteins (33). Alternatively, UvSBT1 may enter plant cells via vesicles and exosomes, a non-classical secretion pathway for transmembrane transport (34). The molecular mechanism by which UvSBT1 is transported into rice cells warrants further investigation. We establish that SBT1 is a secreted virulence factor, as evidenced by its translocation into plant cells during infection (Fig. 1G; fig. S2) and the severe virulence defects of SBT1 knockout mutants. Crucially, the enzymatic activity and secretion capability of SBT1 are indispensable for its function (figs. S6 and S7), and its heterologous expression in rice is sufficient to suppress immunity and enhance susceptibility (Fig. 2). Our data indicate that UvSBT1 has a dual function. First, its deletion impairs fungal growth and consequently compromises the virulence of U. virens. Second, UvSBT1 acts as a secreted effector protein that suppresses host immunity within plant cells. This finding is significant because it demonstrates that major fungal pathogens have convergently evolved to target central hubs of rice immunity through a conserved enzymatic effector.

To investigate the mechanism by which UvSBT1 suppresses plant immunity, we performed a Y2H screen and identified 32 candidate UvSBT1-interacting proteins, including OsMAPKKKα, OsSGT1, OsHDA705, OsADA2, OsbZIP1, and others (table S2). We showed that UvSBT1 interacts with PTI-related component OsMAPKKKα, and that UvSBT1 inhibits plant immunity by promoting the degradation of OsMAPKKKα (Fig. 3). The vast majority of subtilisin family members are endopeptidases (24), which cleave peptide bonds at specific internal positions of the polypeptide chain, thereby breaking down long-chain proteins or peptides into smaller fragments. Notably, the mechanism by which SBT1 degrades OsMAPKKKα appears to be distinct from typical fungal endopeptidases. While degradation depends strictly on its Peptidase S8 domain activity, we did not observe stable cleavage intermediates (fig. S10C), suggesting SBT1 may process its targets completely into small peptides or amino acids. Interestingly, UvSBT1 also interacts with positive immune regulator OsSGT1 (35), while UvSBT1 does not directly degrade OsSGT1, OsMAPKKKε, OsCERK1, OsRLCK185, and OsMKK4 (figs. S12 and S13). These results highlight the role of UvSBT1 as a subtilase with selective substrate specificity in targeted protein degradation during immune suppression. Consequently, a key question for future research is the basis of UvSBT1’s substrate specificity, particularly whether specific conserved sequences among its targets dictate their recognition and degradation. This efficient, “erasure”-like degradation represents a sophisticated virulence strategy, effectively removing key immune components. Given the broad conservation of SBT1 homologs and their targets in plants, the degradation of key immune signaling nodes MAPKKKα likely represents a widespread virulence mechanism in pathogenic fungi.

Plant pathogens secrete protease effectors to suppress or activate plant immunity. For example, the trypsin-like serine protease PsTry1 from Phytophthora acts as a “molecular scissors” that directly cleaves the immune central receptor kinase BAK1 in the apoplast, thereby disrupting the assembly of immune receptor complexes and blocking the transmission of apoplastic immune signals (36). The Pseudomonas syringae effector protein AvrRpt2 cleaves the intracellular protein RIN4 (RPM1-interacting protein 4), thereby activating the NLR protein RPS2 (Resistant to P. Syringae 2), which in turn activates immune responses (37). The nuclear inclusion protease NIa-Pro encoded by Sugarcane mosaic virus (SCMV) impairs the mRNA surveillance pathway by inhibiting the function of the ZmU2AF65B-ZmUPF3 molecular module, thereby facilitating viral infection (38). Here, we found that SBT1 degrades OsMAPKKKα, thereby suppressing rice immunity. These studies indicate that pathogen proteases regulate plant immunity by cleaving or degrading host target proteins.

RLCKs play a pivotal role in relaying immune signals from the activated PRR complexes to multiple downstream components (39, 40). For example, the Arabidopsis RLCK VII-8 subfamily BOTRYTIS-INDUCED KINASE 1 (BIK1) directly associates with FLS2, and the perception of flg22 by the FLS2–BAK1 complex leads to the phosphorylation and dissociation of BIK1 from FLS2 to mediate immune signaling (41). In rice, fungal-derived chitin fragments are recognized by OsCEBiP, which recruits the co-receptor OsCERK1 to form a heterodimer. OsCERK1-mediated chitin-triggered immune signaling is transduced via phosphorylation to downstream RLCKs or the OsRac1-associated immune complex (7). Subsequent immune signals are further relayed through phosphorylation to MAPK cascades, ultimately activating the plant immune system (8). RLCKs phosphorylate MAPKKKs to transmit immune signals. For instance, the Arabidopsis RLCK VII-1 subfamily member PBL27 phosphorylates MAPKKK5 to activate chitin-triggered MPK3/6 (42). In addition, the RLCK group XII member BSK1 interacts with and phosphorylates MAPKKK5, thereby positively regulating the activation of MPK3/6 and plant immunity (43). Similarly, OsRLCK185 has been shown to phosphorylate the OsMAPKKKε and promote chitin-triggered OsMPK3/6 activation in rice (13). MAPKKKα functions as a positive regulator of Pto-mediated programmed cell death (PCD) associated with host resistance to P. syringae pv. Tabaci (44). The coat proteins of beet black scorch virus (BBSV) target the MAPKKKα–14-3-3a defense signaling module to subvert antiviral innate immune responses in N. benthamiana (30). MAPKKKα plays a positive role in plant resistance to bacteria and viruses, but its role in fungal disease resistance remained unknown. Here, we demonstrated that OsRLCK185 interacts with OsMAPKKKα. Overexpression of OsMAPKKKα in rice significantly enhanced resistance to U. virens, M. oryzae, R. solani, and Xoo (Fig. 3), whereas knockout of this gene increased susceptibility (fig. S15). Furthermore, the OsMAPKKKα knockout impaired chitin-triggered MPK3/6 activation, ROS burst, and the expression of defense-related genes (fig. S15). In addition, OsRLCK185 phosphorylates OsMAPKKKα at Thr493 to enhance its stability and immune function (Fig. 4). The mechanism by which RLCK185-mediated phosphorylation of OsMAPKKKα at Thr493 stabilizes the protein deserves further study. It remains to be determined whether Thr493 phosphorylation disrupts the recognition site of an E3 ubiquitin ligase, leading to reduced ubiquitination and increased stability of OsMAPKKKα, and/or whether it alters the protein conformation of OsMAPKKKα. Furthermore, we demonstrated that OsMAPKKKα directly interacts with and phosphorylates OsMKK4 (Fig. 5). Overexpression of OsMKK4 activates MPK3/6 phosphorylation, which is consistent with the previously reported role of the OsMKK4–OsMPK3/6 phosphorylation cascades in chitin-triggered immune signaling in rice (9). Taken together, we propose that a new linear cascade consisting of OsRLCK185–OsMAPKKKα–OsMKK4 mediates chitin signal transduction.

HIGS represents a promising approach for limiting crop losses caused by pathogens (45). In this approach, transgenic host plants generate siRNAs that target essential pathogen genes, thereby inducing silencing during fungal infection (46). For instance, HIGS-mediated targeting of genes such as FgSTE12, FgSGE1, FgCYP51, FgFGP1, or FgChs3b has been shown to enhance resistance against Fusarium graminearum, the causal agent of Fusarium head blight (FHB) in wheat (47–49). Likewise, silencing PsCPK1 or PsFUZ7 through HIGS improved wheat resistance to Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici, the fungus responsible for stripe rust (50, 51). Additional successful applications of engineered resistance via HIGS have been reported in rice for controlling M. oryzae and U. virens (52, 53). The current study further demonstrates that HIGS targeting UvSBT1 effectively attenuates U. virens, M. oryzae, and R. solani infection in rice, offering a valuable framework for the HIGS-based development of stable transgenic rice with enhanced resistance to multiple fungal pathogens.

We present a working model elucidating the mechanism by which the fungal effector SBT1 suppresses rice immunity during pathogen infection (Fig. 8). Upon chitin perception, OsCEBiP recruits its co-receptor OsCERK1 to form a heteromeric receptor complex, initiating PTI signaling via OsCERK1 phosphorylation. The phosphorylated OsCERK1 subsequently activates OsRLCK185, which then phosphorylates OsMAPKKKα to initiate OsMKK4–OsMPK3/6 phosphorylation cascades, ultimately resulting in the upregulation of defense-related genes. As a virulence strategy, fungal pathogens secrete SBT1 into rice cells, where it interacts with OsMAPKKKα, promoting its degradation and consequently disrupting the OsCERK1–OsRLCK185–OsMAPKKKα–OsMKK4 immune signaling axis. Importantly, our work establishes the role of OsMAPKKKα in mediating broad-spectrum resistance and identifies it as a valuable genetic target for breeding resistant rice varieties.

A working model illustrating how SBT1 suppresses rice immunity during infection.

In rice, OsCEBiP recruits its co-receptor OsCERK1 to form a complex upon chitin binding, and initiates PTI signaling by activating the phosphorylation of OsCERK1; OsCERK1 phosphorylates OsRLCK185 to activate downstream OsMAPKKKα–OsMKK4–OsMPK3/6 cascades, leading to the upregulation of defense genes expression. In turn, the fungal effector SBT1 is secreted and transported into host cells, where it interacts with OsMAPKKKα and promotes its degradation, thereby inhibiting the OsCERK1–OsRLCK185–MAPK cascades-mediated immune pathways.

METHODS

Plant materials, strains, and inoculation assays

The coding sequences of UvSBT1, MoSBT1 and RsSBT1 were cloned from U. virens, M. oryzae and R. solani cDNA, respectively. The coding sequences of OsRLCK185, OsMKK4, or OsMAPKKKα were cloned from rice (Oryza sativa cv. ZH11) cDNA. The coding sequence of UvSBT1 (without the SP) was inserted into the overexpression vector pCNG (with a GFP tag). The coding sequences of OsMKK4, OsRLCK185, and OsMAPKKKα were inserted into the overexpression vector pCNF (with a 3 × Flag tag). The 342 bp coding sequence of UvSBT1 was inserted into the HIGS vector, pDS1301. The sgRNAs targeting OsRLCK185 or OsMAPKKKα were individually cloned into the CRISPR/Cas9 vector pTKC (54). These vectors were transformed into ZH11 background rice plants, to obtain corresponding overexpression and genome-edited rice plants. The rice transformation was performed by Wuhan Tianwen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

The pathogens, U. virens strain HWD-2, M. oryzae strain Guy11, R. solani strain HG81, and Xoo strain PXO99 were used for rice inoculation. For U. virens inoculation (53), rice plants at the booting stage were injected with 2 mL of a U. virens mycelial/spore suspension (1 × 106 conidia/mL) using a syringe. Inoculated plants were maintained in a greenhouse at 28°C and 100% relative humidity. Smut ball numbers per panicle were quantified at 21 dpi. For M. oryzae spray-inoculation (55), 14-day-old rice seedlings were sprayed with spore suspension (5 × 105 spores/mL in 0.5% Tween 20) of M. oryzae. Seedlings were incubated at 26°C and 100% relative humidity in a climate-controlled chamber. Disease lesions were evaluated at 5 dpi. Relative fungal biomass was determined using RT-qPCR for M. oryzae MoPot2 and normalized to rice OsUBQ1. For Xoo inoculation (56), rice plants at the late tillering stage were inoculated using the leaf-clipping method. Lesion lengths were measured at 14 dpi. For R. solani inoculation (57), mycelial blocks were inoculated onto the leaf sheaths of rice plants at the tillering stage. Moisture was maintained using cotton wool, and sheath blight lesion length was measured at 5 dpi.

LC-MS/MS analysis of secreted proteins during U. virens infection of spikelets

Rice spikelets infected with U. virens for 1 day were collected, washed with phosphate buffer saline (PBS), and centrifuged to isolate the supernatant. Proteins in the supernatant were precipitated using 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The resulting precipitates were washed with acetone, air-dried, and dissolved in protein extraction solution. For digestion, proteins were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (30 min, 56°C) and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide (15 min, RT, dark). The sample was diluted with 200 mM TEAB to reduce urea below 2 M. Trypsin was added at 1:50 (w/w, overnight) and 1:100 (w/w, 4 h) for digestion. Peptides were desalted using a Strata X SPE column, dissolved in solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile/water), and loaded onto a custom reversed-phase column (25 cm × 100 μm). Separation used solvent B (0.1% formic acid, 90% acetonitrile/water) with a gradient: 0–14.5 min (6–22% B), 14.5–17.5 min (22–34% B), 17.5–19 min (34–80% B), 19–20 min (80% B) at 700 nL/min (EASY-nLC 1200 UPLC). Peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (NSI source: 2300 V; FAIMS CV: −45 V). Full MS: 60 k resolution, m/z 350–1400. MS/MS: fixed 120.0 m/z, 15 k resolution, HCD-NCE 27%. AGC: 1e6, max IT: 22 ms. FDR was adjusted to <1%. Tandem mass spectra were searched against the U. virens genome database (NCBI database under BioProject accessions PRJNA606646).

Assays examining the function of the SP of UvSBT1

The 60-bp SP sequence of UvSBT1 was inserted into the pSUC2 vector. The pSUC2-Avr1bSP (positive control), pSUC2 (negative control), or pSUC2-UvSBT1SP constructs were individually transformed into yeast (Komagataella pastoris) strain YTK12, which lacks Suc2 activity (58). Transformed yeast cells were cultured in synthetic-defined (SD) medium lacking tryptophan (SD–Trp) or YPRAA medium to assess invertase secretion. Enzyme activity was determined by monitoring the conversion of 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) into the red-colored insoluble product 1,3,5-triphenylformazan (TPF).

Deletion and complementation of UvSBT1 in U. virens

Approximately 1.2 kb of upstream and downstream flanking regions of UvSBT1 were cloned into the knockout vector pGKO. A complementation fragment of approximately 4 kb was inserted into the complementation vector pNeo3300 (with a 3 × Flag tag). The Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 was then transformed with the following constructs: pGKO-UvSBT1, pNeo3300-UvSBT1, pNeo3300-UvSBT1ΔSP, pNeo3300-UvSBT1ΔI9, pNeo3300-UvSBT1ΔS8, or pNeo3300-UvSBT1M. The transformation of U. virens was performed according to previous protocols (59), and successful transformants were validated through PCR analysis.

Vegetative growth, conidiation, and rice infection assays

For assessment of vegetative growth, mycelial plugs taken from 14-day-old PSA plates were inoculated onto fresh PSA medium and incubated at 28°C. The colony diameter was measured after 14 days of cultivation. To evaluate conidiation, U. virens transformants were cultured in PSB liquid medium at 28°C with shaking at 180 rpm. After 7 days, the cultures were filtered through four layers of gauze, and conidia were harvested and quantified using a hemocytometer. For infection analysis, inoculated rice spikelets were collected at 6 dpi, fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and subjected to scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine infection hyphae. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates.

Subcellular localization of UvSBT1 in plants

The pCNG-UvSBT1 plasmid was electroporated into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Bacterial cultures carrying the construct were resuspended in infiltration buffer (OD600 = 1.0) and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal microscope 48 h post-infiltration. The pCNG-UvSBT1 construct was used to transform protoplasts, and fluorescence was examined under a laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) (Carl Zeiss, Germany) 16 h after transformation.

Small RNA sequencing, FISH, and immunofluorescence assays

Total RNA isolated from booting-stage spikelets of UvSBT1-HIGS (T3-L2) plants was processed for small RNA sequencing. RNA libraries were prepared using the NEB Next Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform by Wuhan IGENEBOOK Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Unique reads were aligned to the SBT1 target fragment.

For fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), a 21-nucleotide probe (5′-CGCGCCTACTTCTCCAACTAC-3′), corresponding to the most abundant siRNA identified in sequencing, was synthesized and labeled with digoxigenin (DIG) using the Alexa Fluor 594 Tyramide SuperBoost Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). FISH was performed following established protocols (59). Briefly, infected spikelets at 6 dpi were fixed in a fixative solution (Servicebio, China) for 6 h. To preserve tissue morphology, the samples were subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol/PBS (phosphate buffered saline) series: 75% (v/v), 50% (v/v), and 25% (v/v) ethanol, each for 10 min. The dehydrated spikelets were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned into slices, and stored at −20°C. Following dewaxing and rehydration, protein digestion was performed using Proteinase K (20 mg/L) at 37°C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, prehybridization solution was added to the mixture. Following incubation at 37°C for 1 h, the paraffin sections were covered with prehybridization solution. DIG-labeled probes were added to the solution, and hybridization was performed at 37°C for 16 h. After hybridization, sections were washed and sealed, and an anti-DIG antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (anti-DIG-HRP) was added to the samples. The samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 h, washed five times with PBS, and treated with Alexa Fluor 594 Tyramide (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The fluorescence signals of the DIG-labeled siRNA were visualized using a LSCM. Nuclei or plant cells were counterstained with DAPI (4′-6-diamidino-2-phenylindole).

For immunofluorescence, rice spikelets inoculated with U. virens strain CΔUvsbt1–1 were collected at 6 dpi, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, and paraffin-embedded. Antigen retrieval was performed on infected stamen sections, followed by incubation with anti-Flag monoclonal antibody and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody. Immunofluorescence of UvSBT1-Flag was visualized using a LSCM.

Yeast two-hybrid assays

The coding sequences of UvSBT1 (without the SP), UvSBT1I9, UvSBT1S8, OsMKK4, OsMAPKKKα, or OsRLCK185 were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector, and the coding sequence of OsMAPKKKα was cloned into the pGADT7 vector. mRNA from rice spikelets 6 dpi with U. virens was used to construct a cDNA library in the prey vector pGADT7. Y2H library screening for UvSBT1 interactors was performed as previously described (60). Briefly, the bait vector pGBKT7-UvSBT1 or pGBKT7-OsMAPKKKα was transformed into yeast strain Y2H Gold, and susceptible cells of transformants were prepared and transformed into a cDNA library in the prey vector pGADT7. Positive clones were screened on SD-Trp-Leu-His (SD − 3) medium, and plasmids were extracted from the transformants. Candidate interacting protein sequences were obtained by PCR sequencing.

For point-to-point interaction verification in yeast, for example, pGADT7-OsMAPKKKα and pGBKT7-UvSBT1 vectors were co-transformed into yeast strain Y2H Gold. Positive clones were selected on SD-3 medium, and confirmed on SD-Trp-Leu-His-Ade (SD − 4) medium containing X-α-Gal (Clontech, USA). The interaction between pGBKT7–53 and pGADT7-T was used as the positive control, while the interaction between pGBKT7-target and pGADT7 was used as the negative control.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

The coding sequences of SBT1, OsMAPKKKα, or OsRLCK185 were inserted into the pCNG vector, and the coding sequences of OsMKK4 or OsMAPKKKα were cloned into the pCNF vector. The resulting constructs, such as pCNG-UvSBT1 and pCNF-OsMAPKKKα, pCNF-UvSBT1, and pCNG were co-expressed in rice protoplasts. Total proteins were extracted 16 h post-transformation, and incubated with anti-Flag MagBeads (Yeasen Biotech, China). Proteins were eluted and subjected to immunoblotting using anti-GFP or anti-Flag antibodies.

GST pull-down assays

The coding sequences of SBT1 (without the SP), OsMAPKKKα, or OsRLCK185 were subcloned into the GST-tagged pGEX4T-2 vector. The coding sequences of OsMKK4 or OsMAPKKKα were inserted into the His-tagged pET28a vector. Each construct was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells. GST-tagged OsRLCK185, OsMAPKKKα, or SBT1 fusion proteins were affinity-purified and incubated with glutathione-agarose beads (Yeasen Biotech, China) at 4°C for 4 h under agitation. After centrifugation, the beads were washed thrice with PBS. Subsequently, the beads were incubated with His-tagged OsMKK4, or OsMAPKKKα at 4°C for 2 h under agitation, followed by additional PBS washes. Proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 40 μL SDS loading buffer at 100°C for 5 min and detected by immunoblotting using anti-His or anti-GST antibodies.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays

The coding sequences of UvSBT1, OsMKK4, or OsRLCK185 were cloned into the p1301-cYFP vector, and the coding sequence of OsMAPKKKα was cloned into the p1301-nYFP vector. The resulting pairs of constructs encoding UvSBT1-cYFP and OsMAPKKKα-nYFP, OsRLCK185-cYFP and OsMAPKKKα-nYFP, OsMKK4-cYFP and OsMAPKKKα-nYFP were co-expressed in rice protoplasts. The fluorescence signals in the protoplasts were examined under a LSCM at 16 h after transformation.

Firefly luciferase complementation imaging assays

The coding sequences of SBT1, OsMKK4, or OsRLCK185 were cloned into the p1301-nLUC vector, and the coding sequence of OsMAPKKKα was cloned into the p1301-cLUC vector. Each of the resulting constructs was transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 cells. Cultures were resuspended in infiltration buffer to an OD600 of 1.6, combined in a 1:1 volumetric ratio, and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. At 48 h post-infiltration, 1 mM luciferin (Gold Biotechnology, USA) was applied to the same inoculated sites. Bioluminescent signals were captured using a Chemi-Image System (Tanon 5200Multi, China).

Immunoblot analysis

The proteins were extracted from the samples using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Subsequently, the proteins were separated via 10% (w/v) SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck Millipore, USA) using a wet transfer method at 80 V for 3 h with a Bio-Rad electroblotting system. The membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST) and 10% (w/v) non-fat dry milk, and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Primary antibody incubations were performed using the following antibodies: anti-Flag, anti-HA, anti-GFP, anti-His, anti-GST, anti-Actin (ABclonal, China), anti-pSpT, and anti-phospho-p44/42 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk at room temperature for 2 h with gentle shaking, followed by six washes (5 min each) with TBST. Next, the membranes were incubated with goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP-conjugated) secondary antibodies (ABclonal, China) diluted in TBST containing 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk at room temperature for 1 hour with gentle shaking. After six additional TBST washes (5 min each), protein signals were visualized using Pierce ECL Western blotting substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, USA). ImageJ software (v.1.8.0) was used for quantification of the band intensities from the immunoblots.

Protein expression in rice protoplasts

The isolation and transfection of rice protoplasts were conducted following a previously described method (61). In brief, ZH11 rice seedlings were grown in darkness for 14 days, after which the seedlings were sliced into approximately 0.3-mm lengths and incubated in cell-wall digestion buffer for 3 h. After digestion, the tissues were rinsed with W5 solution and filtered through an 80-mm mesh. The protoplasts were centrifuged, washed with W5 solution, and resuspended in MMG solution (0.6 M D-mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2, and 4 mM MES at pH 5.7) at a concentration of 5 × 106 cells/mL. For protoplast transfection, the plasmids, protoplasts, and the PEG solution (0.6 M D-mannitol, 100 mM CaCl2, and 40% [w/v] PEG4000) were mixed. The mixture was incubated at 28°C for 15 min, after which W5 solution was added to halt the reaction. The protoplasts were pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in W5 solution, and incubated at 28°C in darkness for 16 h, after which they were used for assays.

Degradation assays of OsMAPKKKα by SBT1

Recombinant SBT1-GST, SBT1M-GST, GST, and OsMAPKKKα-His fusion constructs were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced by the addition of 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed under ultra-high pressure using a JN-3000PLUS homogenizer (JNBIO, China). Recombinant OsMAPKKKα-His proteins were purified using HisPur Ni-NTA Resin (Thermo Scientific, USA), while SBT1-GST, SBT1M-GST, and GST proteins were purified with GSTrap Resin (Thermo Scientific, USA). For in vitro degradation assays, the following recombinant protein combinations (SBT1-GST + OsMAPKKKα-His, SBT1M-GST + OsMAPKKKα-His, and GST + OsMAPKKKα-His) were incubated at 30°C for 0, 15, or 30 min. The samples were analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-His or anti-GST antibodies. For in vivo degradation assays, we co-expressed constructs harboring OsMAPKKKα-Flag and UvSBT1-GFP, GFP, or UvSBT1M-GFP in rice protoplasts. The abundance of OsMAPKKKα-Flag protein was then assessed by immunoblotting using anti-Flag antibody.

Assays for the suppression of OsMAPKKKα-induced cell death by UvSBT1

The coding sequence of OsMAPKKKα was cloned into the pVX vector (with a 3 × Flag tag). The resulting construct, pVX-OsMAPKKKα, was electroporated into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Bacterial cells were resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM MES, 0.2 mM acetylacetone) to an OD600 of 0.8 and injected into the leaves of 3-week-old N. benthamiana plants. To test whether UvSBT1 suppresses the programmed cell death (PCD) triggered by OsMAPKKKα, N. benthamiana leaves were co-infiltrated with A. tumefaciens strains carrying the pCNG-UvSBT1 and pVX-OsMAPKKKα constructs. PCD severity was then quantified at 4 days post-infiltration.

Cell-free protein degradation assay

Recombinant OsMAPKKKα-His, OsMAPKKKαT493A-His, and OsMAPKKKαT493D-His fusion proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells and purified. Total proteins were extracted from ZH11 rice spikelets. Then, equal amounts of the purified recombinant proteins were incubated with the ZH11 total protein extracts at 30°C for 0, 30, or 60 min and subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting using an anti-His antibody.

In vivo and in vitro phosphorylation assays

To investigate in vivo phosphorylation, OsMAPKKKα-Flag fusion proteins were expressed in rlck185 mutants and ZH11 protoplasts. After immunoprecipitation (IP) with Flag beads, the phosphorylation level of OsMAPKKKα was analyzed by immunoblotting with an anti-pSpT antibody. In another experiment, OsMAPKKKαK276M-Flag was expressed individually or co-expressed with OsRLCK185-HA in rice protoplasts. Total proteins extracted from the transfected protoplasts were probed with anti-Flag and anti-HA antibodies, and the phosphorylation level of OsMAPKKKα was assessed using the anti-pSpT antibody.

For in vitro phosphorylation assays, purified OsMAPKKKαK276M-His was incubated with either OsRLCK185-GST or OsRLCK185K108E-GST in kinase assay buffer (containing 0.5 mM ATP, 100 mM MgCl2, 100 mM MnCl2, 10 mM DTT, and 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5) for 1 h at 30°C. The reaction was stopped, and phosphorylation of OsMAPKKKα was assessed using Phos-tag PAGE followed by immunoblotting with an anti-His antibody. To identify the specific residues of OsMAPKKKα phosphorylated by OsRLCK185, we performed an in vitro kinase assay with recombinant OsRLCK185-GST and OsMAPKKKαK276M-His, using OsMAPKKKαK276M-His alone as a control. The resulting tryptic peptides of OsMAPKKKα were then analyzed by mass spectrometry (MS).

Assessment of ROS accumulation, callose deposition, and MAPK activation

To assess ROS production, 3 mm by 3 mm sections of rice leaves were excised and incubated overnight in distilled water in a 96-well plate. The next day, the water was replaced with 100 μL of assay solution (1 μM chitin, 5 μg/mL horseradish peroxidase, 50 μM luminol) was added to each well. Chemiluminescence was measured at 1-min intervals for 24 min using a SPARK-10 M microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland). Ten biological replicates were analyzed per sample, with distilled water serving as the mock control.

Leaves from 2-week-old rice seedlings were vacuum-infiltrated with 1 μM chitin for 30 min and then incubated at 28°C for 24 h. Samples were fixed in ethanol:acetic acid (3:1, v/v) for 6 h, with the solution changed frequently. The leaves were then washed three times and incubated in staining solution (150 mM K2HPO4, 0.01% [w/v] aniline blue) on an end-over-end shaker for 4 h. Callose deposits were visualized under a fluorescence microscope with a UV light source (340–380 nm).

Rice plants were treated with 1 μM chitin and sampled at 0, 15, 30, and 60 min after treatment. MAPK activation was analyzed by immunoblotting using a phospho-p44/42 MAPK antibody. Signal intensity was quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software, and the relative phosphorylation levels of MPK3/6 were normalized to the abundance of OsActin.

Phylogenetic and structural analyses of SBT1 homologs

Homologs of SBT1 from diverse fungal genera were retrieved using BLASTP against the NCBI database. Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MEGA 7.0, and a phylogenetic tree was inferred using the Neighbor-Joining method in the same software (62). Structural models of SBT1 homologs were predicted with AlphaFold 3 (63). The superimposed protein structures were visualized and analyzed with PyMOL (https://pymol.org/2/).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from samples using TRIzol reagent. First-strand cDNA was then synthesized from the extracted RNA using a cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix. Gene expression levels were normalized to the rice OsUBQ1 (ubiquitin) gene or the U. virens β-tubulin gene as an internal control. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. At least three biological replicates were analyzed for each experiment. All primer sequences used in this study are provided in table S4.

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The significance of the difference was analyzed by two-tailed Student’s t test or One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. A threshold of P < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 8.0.1).
