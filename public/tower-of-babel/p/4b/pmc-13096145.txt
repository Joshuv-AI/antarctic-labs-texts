The influence of diet-mediated exposure of avian influenza on adult survival, recruitment and territory occupancy in peregrine falcons
Authors: Bryan D Watts, Kathleen E Clark, Chance Hines
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current outbreak of highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) is the largest panzootic ever recorded, impacting millions of wild and domestic birds worldwide. Waterbirds including waterfowl, seabirds and shorebirds represent natural reservoirs for the virus and have facilitated its spread throughout major flyways. Peregrine falcons are obligate bird consumers and are considered HPAIV-susceptible. We investigated the influence of waterbird exposure on breeding peregrine falcons within the mid-Atlantic region of North America by comparing inland and coastal subpopulations. We monitored individually marked adults (N = 205) and breeding territories (N = 79) to estimate spatial and temporal patterns of adult survival, recruitment age and territory occupancy (2016–2025). Adult survival was comparable between inland and coastal locations prior to the arrival (date of first detection) of HPAIV but fell from 0.82 to 0.25 in coastal areas in the two years following arrival. Recruitment of juvenile-plumaged (second calendar year) birds was uncommon in both areas prior to the arrival of HPAIV but increased from 3.5% to 21.0% following arrival, suggesting that the recruitment pool may be diminished by the demand generated by increased adult turnover. Territory occupancy was high and stable prior to the arrival of HPAIV but declined by more than 50% in coastal areas following arrival. Our results document changes in adult survival, territory occupancy and juvenile recruitment that coincide with the appearance of HPAIV on the Atlantic Coast and suggest that impacts may be diet-mediated.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Zoology

Introduction

The current outbreak of highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) is global in scope, is the most significant panzootic event for wild birds ever recorded and represents the ongoing evolution of a virus of Asian origin. The H5 virus designated as A/goose/Guangdong/1/1996 (GsGd) first emerged in domestic geese in Guangdong, China in 19961. In the several years that followed, the virus was restricted to periodic outbreaks in domestic birds1. However, in 2002 the virus was discovered in wild birds initiating a period of regional expansion2. Between 2004 and 2013 GsGd spread throughout Asia, eastern Europe, Africa and the Middle East killing tens of thousands of migratory waterbirds3. The GsGd clade 2.3.4.4 began to dominate outbreaks in 2014 and for the first time reached the Eastern Pacific Flyway of North America4. In 2021 clade 2.3.4.4b was detected within the western Atlantic Flyway of North America (via Europe)5,6, and has since caused widespread mortality events in waterbirds throughout Atlantic Canada7,8 and beyond9. Migratory waterbirds have spread the virus throughout South America10. The virus has spread rapidly and has now been detected in bird populations on all continents. The most recent global assessment lists 572 bird species that have been infected with 337 being added to the list since 202111, including species of high conservation concern12,13.

In wild birds, waterbirds including the orders Anseriformes (ducks, geese, and swans) and Charadriiformes (shorebirds, gulls, and terns) represent natural reservoirs for both low pathogenic avian influenza (LPAIV) and HPAIV14,15. Because these birds are highly migratory, they may rapidly transmit the virus throughout global flyways9,15. Fecal-oral is the primary route of transmission between waterbird individuals16,17. Transmission is facilitated by the ability of the virus to remain viable in wet environments for months3,18. Spread of the virus may be further accelerated by common behaviors such as nesting in dense colonies or joining crowded communal roosts during migration8.

Consumption of infected birds either by preying on live birds or scavenging carcasses is a secondary mode of viral transmission and the primary mode for raptors19,20. Several raptor species have experienced reduced reproductive rates or elevated adult mortality rates associated with exposure to HPAIV from prey21–24. Vultures have been impacted by consumption of infected bird carcasses25,26 that in some cases have resulted in large die-offs27. Depending on temperature and moisture, HPAIV may remain infectious in carcasses for up to 20 days28 increasing the likelihood of transmission to scavengers.

Peregrine falcons (Falco peregrinus) are obligate bird consumers resulting in high exposure to avian-borne diseases29,30. Peregrines are vulnerable to infection by HPAIV via ingestion of infected prey31 and some populations have experienced significant impacts during the current outbreak32,33. Peregrine diet varies with geography. Peregrines along coasts that support high concentrations of waterbirds feed heavily on waterbirds34–36. Birds within inland locations feed primarily on land birds37–39. Variation with geography in the proportion of waterbirds in the diet suggests that coastal and inland peregrines may vary in their risks of HPAIV exposure. Here we test this hypothesis by comparing changes in adult mortality and territory occupancy during the current outbreak of HPAI between coastal and inland subpopulations within the mid-Atlantic region of North America.

Methods

Study area

The study area includes New Jersey and portions (Coastal Plain and Piedmont physiographic regions) of Virginia (Fig. 1). This area currently supports more than 70 breeding pairs of peregrine falcons40,41. The peregrine falcon population within the mid-Atlantic region of North America was extirpated by the early 1960s42 leading to an aggressive restoration program that included the release of captive-reared young43,44. Between 1975 and 1985, 307 captive-reared birds were released leading to the first nesting attempt in 197945. During the period of releases, the population proceeded through an establishment phase (1979–1985) as maturing birds colonized the region. During the establishment the population had an average doubling time of 1.3 years. After releases were concluded, the population entered a consolidation phase (1986–2007) with an average doubling time of 23.4 years.

We subdivided the study area and breeding territories into “coastal” and “inland” areas according to the distance to major shorebird staging areas46,47 and waterbird breeding areas48,49. Both Virginia and New Jersey support areas of international importance that support hundreds of thousands of shorebirds making their final spring refueling stop before flying to arctic breeding areas (https://www.manomet.org/project/western-hemisphere-shorebird-reserve-network-whsrn). Staging shorebirds become highly concentrated within these delineated sites and we considered territories to be “coastal” if they were within 8 km of recognized staging areas. We considered territories beyond 8 km of major staging areas to be “inland”. We assumed that pairs distant from these sites were not regularly hunting on birds staging there. Diet of peregrine falcons within the mid-Atlantic varies according to territory location relative to major shorebird areas. Pairs that nest on the outer coast near spring shorebird staging areas provision their broods primarily (> 70%) with shorebirds and other waterbirds50,51. The dominant prey used in these territories are medium-sized shorebirds including short-billed dowitcher (Limnodromus griseus), ruddy turnstone (Arenaria interpres), willet (Tringa semipalmata), and dunlin (Calidris alpina). Pairs that nest within inland locations provision their broods primarily with medium-sized passerines including blue jay (Cyanocitta cristata), northern flicker (Colaptes auratus), mourning dove (Zenaida macroura), American robin (Turdus migratorius) and common grackle (Quiscalus quiscula)51,52.

Data collection

We determined territory occupancy and the identification of breeding adults by regular monitoring (2016–2025). We monitored known territories 2–5 times from March through July to determine occupancy status. We considered territories that supported a pair of adults during the breeding season to be occupied53. Broods produced throughout the study area have been double-banded with standard USGS bands and field-readable alpha-numeric bands since the 1980s. We used a combination of cameras affixed to nesting structures that record adult activity and single-lens reflex cameras to take photographs of adults during nest visits to read band combinations. Breeding peregrines are suggested to have high (> 95%) site fidelity54–56. Of 205 adults followed in this study, 6 (2.9%) were documented to change territories during the study period. For this reason, we assume that adults that were replaced or disappeared from their breeding territory between years and were not detected elsewhere were lost from the population. We do not know the underlying cause of mortality for the majority of adults lost.

We evaluated birds that colonized territories following the loss of a breeding adult to assess patterns of recruitment. For birds that could be positively identified, we determined recruitment age. We considered birds entering their second calendar year to be one year old, birds entering their third calendar year to be two years old and so forth. We also examined plumage for both identified and unidentified birds within breeding territory in order to examine patterns of juvenile recruitment. Although variable with latitude, juvenile peregrines progress through their juvenile to prebasic I molt during their second calendar year from late spring through fall such that juvenile-plumaged birds are readily identified during the breeding season57. We recorded the age class (juvenile vs. adult) of identified and unidentified birds.

Statistical analysis

Survival

We used the package ‘marked’58 in Program R59 to fit POPAN capture–recapture models60. These models account for individuals entering the population throughout the study period and provide an estimate of population entry probability (pent) as well as apparent survival (). Candidate models included combinations of additive and interactive effects of region (Inland/Coastal) and time (Year) on and population entry probability (pent), while detection probability () and superpopulation size () were held constant. Model selection was based on Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC). When more than one model was effectively equivalent (ΔAIC ≤ 2.00) and one model was a nested version of the other, we evaluated whether the additional predictor improved model fit61 and retained the simpler model when it did not. To testfor regional differences in estimated survival and population entry probabilities, we conducted pairwise comparisons between inland and outer coast predictions derived from the POPAN survival models.

Recruitment

We provide descriptive statistics on known-age birds recruiting into the breeding population and compare age-at-recruitment for males and females using a two-tailed t-test. We compiled the frequency of juvenile-plumaged birds recruiting into the breeding population and investigated possible temporal patterns in frequency using a G-test with Yates correction.

Observed occupancy

We used the package glmmTMB62 to construct mixed models with observed occupancy as a binomial response (occupied/unoccupied). Because our dataset included only a single occupancy classification per territory per year, rather than repeated within-season visits needed to estimate annual detection probability, we did not fit a detection-corrected occupancy model. Instead, we modeled observed breeding territory status as a binomial response, assuming detection probability was high under standardized monitoring protocols.

Fixed predictors included year and region. Year was included as a categorical factor to allow non-linear and non-monotonic temporal patterns in occupancy. The model also included the interaction between year and region (inland vs. coastal) to test for differences in region-specific temporal dynamics and we included territory location as a random intercept to account for repeated observations of the same territory over multiple years. We compared models using all combinations of additive and interactive effects of region and year and selected the most parsimonious based on AIC following the same procedure as we did for survival. To test for differences in predicted occupancy between regions while accounting for site-level random effects, we used a parametric bootstrap approach based on our top model. For each bootstrap iteration, we simulated a new response vector from the fitted model using the estimated fixed and random effects and refit the model to the simulated data. Using each refitted model, we generated predicted occupancy probabilities for all observed site × year combinations, including random site effects. We then averaged predicted probabilities across sites within each region and year and calculated the difference in mean occupancy between regions. This procedure was repeated across 10,000 bootstrap replicates, producing an empirical sampling distribution of the regional occupancy difference for each year.

Results

Survival

We identified and followed 205 individuals within the breeding population including 89 (43.4%) males and 116 (56.6%) females (Table 1). Obtaining full reads on alpha-numeric bands is often easier for females since they spend more time at the eyrie (affording more photos from stationary cameras) and are more aggressive during nest visits (allowing closer SLR photos) compared to males. Four POPAN models had nearly identical AIC scores (Table 1) but all predictors in the top model were nested within the others and the additional predictors included in the lower ranked models were uninformative. Due to the near-perfect detection (p) probabilities (p = 0.99, 95% CI = 0.97:0.99), the superpopulation size () was not estimable (95% CI = 0.00:∞). This estimate is not biologically meaningful and our focus is instead on apparent annual survival () and population entry (), which are robust despite the low . The overall main effect of region on Φ was not statistically significant (βcoastal = -0.58, -1.88:0.71) but year × region interactions show greater survival within inland areas during 2017, 2023, and 2024 (Fig. 2). Apparent survival was relatively stable over time within inland areas but declined dramatically within coastal areas from 0.82 ± 0.07 (mean ± SE) in 2022 to 0.42 ± 0.08 in 2023 and 0.25 ± 0.08 in 2024. Of 44 identified adults in coastal areas during 2022, 34 (77.3%) were not present by 2025. Temporal trends for pent were similar to those observed for Φ. The overall main effect of region (βcoastal = 0.49, -0.63:1.61) was not significant but year × region interactions showed greater pent in coastal areas (Fig. 2).

Recruitment

We recorded 78 recruitment events involving known individuals including 44 females and 34 males. Recruitment age varied between 1 and 7 with 85% of recruits falling between the ages of 2 and 5 years. Males recruited later than females with modal ages of 2 and 3 for females and males respectively. Mean recruitment age was higher for males (3.4 ± 0.26) compared to females (2.9 ± 0.20) but the difference was not statistically significant (df = 76, t-statistic = 1.4, p = 0.16). Recruitment during the second calendar year (1 year old) was uncommon representing less than 10% of all individuals and half of those were after 2022.

We recorded 138 recruitment events (known and unknown individuals) over the 10-year study period. Recruitment events that involved juvenile-plumaged birds were relatively uncommon accounting for 10.1% of the total. Juvenile females were significantly (G-statistic = 5.97, p < 0.05) more likely to be recruited compared to juvenile males (11 of 76 vs. 3 of 62) accounting for 78.6% of individuals. We observed a temporal pattern in the recruitment of juvenile-plumaged birds. Juvenile-plumaged birds were significantly (G-statistic = 17.2, p < 0.001) more likely to be recruited into the population after 2022 (11 of 52) than before (3 of 86).

Observed occupancy

We monitored occupancy for 79 breeding territories (2016–2025) including 45 and 34 within inland and coastal areas respectively. The full model was the best fitting and most parsimonious among those in our candidate set (Table 2). Occupancy was generally high across years and regions. Occupancy was lower on the outer coast (βCoastal = -4.99 ± 1.28, p < 0.001). Predicted occupancy probabilities were generally high and stable from 2016 to 2023 within both regions but declined sharply at coastal sites during 2025 (Fig. 3). The 50.6% decline in occupancy rate between 2024 and 2025 within coastal sites was the greatest year-over-year reduction in occupancy. The next largest decline was from 2023 to 2024 (14.9% decrease). All other annual changes were comparatively small (≤ 6%).

Discussion

Declines in adult survival and territory occupancy within coastal areas coincided with the arrival (date of initial detection) of HPAIV along the Atlantic Coast of North America and we have assumed that these impacts were caused by infection with HPAIV. Although we do not know the cause of death for all of the adults that were documented to disappear, several instances of peregrine mortality within the study area have been attributed to HPAIV infection. The United States Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service lists six peregrine falcons that have tested positive (2022–2025) for HPAIV within the study area63. We are aware of six others that have tested positive and two that are pending. This pattern of impact is consistent with other peregrine falcon populations21,32,33,64 and with other raptor species that apparently are exposed to HPAIV through their diet22,24,27.

Temporal patterns of adult survival and territory occupancy differed between inland and coastal areas. While pairs within coastal areas experienced significant declines in both metrics after 2022, pairs within inland areas did not. We contend that coastal pairs consume “riskier” diets compared to inland pairs resulting in higher exposure to HPAIV. Within the mid-Atlantic region, the diet of coastal peregrines is dominated (> 70%) by waterbirds and in particular staging shorebirds50,51 while the diet of inland peregrines is dominated by passerines51,52. Waterbirds including waterfowl and shorebirds are considered natural reservoirs and vectors for avian influenza17. Positivity for HPAI is often high within these groups14,65. Resistance is also high for some species66,67 but not for many raptors21,24 setting up a pathogen-prey-predator relationship that is detrimental for specialist consumers like peregrines. Of the 332 birds that have tested HPAI positive within the study area (2021–2025) 54.5% are waterbirds and 41.3% are scavengers or predators of avian prey63(USDA 2025).

The study area is an international crossroad for migratory waterbirds (e.g.46,47,68, and has been the focus of long-term avian flu surveillance69. Infection dynamics vary between waterbird hosts67. Local occurrence of avian influenza in shorebirds is highest during the peak (mid-April through early June) of spring northward migration through the area. Prevalence of avian influenza in ruddy turnstones increases after arrival, peaks in mid-May and declines before departure. Antibody prevalence increases through the residency period suggesting local infection and recovery prior to departure. Antibody prevalence in red knots declines through the stopover period suggesting exposure prior to arrival. Both viral and antibody prevalence was low in sanderlings (C. alba) and varied directly with ruddy turnstones suggesting spillover. Turnstones appear to serve as an amplifying host rather than a reservoir. In contrast to shorebirds, local avian influenza occurrence in migratory ducks peaks during southward fall migration69. Peregrines within the study area time their breeding to take advantage of staging shorebirds51. The increased metabolic demand of the brood during May results in high consumption of shorebirds during a window when exposure is high.

The diet-exposure pattern observed in this study is consistent with suggestions from other regions. Caliendo et al.32 investigated declines in breeding and wintering peregrine falcons in the Netherlands related to outbreaks of HPAI. They found that peregrine mortality coincided with the two prominent (2017/2018, 2022/2023) outbreaks with population decreases of more than 15%. The authors determined that viral sequences taken from peregrines clustered more closely with clusters in prey rather than with other peregrines suggesting that infection was from prey rather than peregrine to peregrine transmission. They also demonstrated that declines were higher in geographic areas clustered around areas with infected waterbirds compared to areas isolated from waterbird concentrations. In Japan, Moriguchi et al.64 projected that the highest HPAIV exposure for peregrines was along the Pacific Coast and Sea of Japan where waterbirds are more available. In a similar way, Wilson et al.24 found that the greatest declines in reproductive rates in Scotland for golden eagles (Aquila chrysaetos) and white-tailed eagles (Haliaeetus albicilla) were in areas where birds had access to marine and coastal areas and the waterbirds they support.

Recruitment patterns connect adult mortality and occupancy within the outer coast. We documented a significant increase in juvenile-plumaged birds entering the breeding population as the wave of avian influenza unfolded. After 2022 juvenile-plumaged birds represented 21% of recruitment events compared to 3.5% before 2022. Many populations include pools of “floaters” (breeding-age individuals that do not hold territories) that rapidly fill territory vacancies70,71. For peregrine falcons, Newton72 has suggested that healthy populations maintain one or more floaters for every breeder in the population and that larger floater pools are associated with stable breeding populations. Large floater pools serve to prevent juveniles from entering the breeding population as older birds outcompete them for open spaces70. Large floater pools also serve as a buffer against perturbations in adult survival and are believed to make small populations more resilient against extinction71. Many peregrine populations (e.g.56,73, have documented regular replacement of adult losses within the same season. Our monitoring suggests that lost adults are replaced within three months and that often multiple floaters are competing for vacancies. The surge in juvenile recruitment suggests that the floater pool is being depleted by the increase in vacancies as territory holders are lost but may also be reduced directly by avian influenza.

Although some waterbirds show evidence of HPAIV-resistance66,67, peregrine falcons are considered HPAIV-susceptible74. Although we do not have serological samples from our breeding population to assess prior exposure, it is likely that some birds have shown resistance to the virus. Of 44 adults that were present in 2022, 22.7% were still present in 2025. Given the high incidence of HPAIV in staging shorebirds within the study area67,75 and the large number of shorebirds used to provision broods, these birds would have had a high likelihood of being exposed to the virus. Monitoring past exposure to the virus in living birds is needed to better understand the long-term capacity of predator populations to withstand this event.

A population’s capacity to sustain mortality reflects its underlying demographic traits76. Species like the peregrine falcon with a long generation time including low reproductive potential and delayed sexual maturity depend on high adult survival for population maintenance77. Sustained perturbations in adult survival generally result in population declines. Within the study area, differences in exposure to infected prey have resulted in a mosaic where we would expect differences in population trends between inland and coastal areas. This expectation is consistent with patterns observed for peregrines in The Netherlands where populations are declining along the coast and around wetlands that support waterbirds while pockets away from waterbirds continue to increase32. The breeding population within our study area has been increasing since reintroductions were initiated during the late 1970s45. Since 1986 the population has been in a consolidation phase with a slow (average doubling time of 23.4 years) growth rate. The decline in occupancy on the outer coast has resulted in both a decline in the population and the loss of associated young production. The decline to present would be expected to require more than 20 years to recover in the absence of immigration from inland populations. These results highlight the importance of understanding spatial variation in diet and exposure risk in making management decisions. Continued monitoring as this wave of HPAI recedes will help to clarify how the breeding population responds to changes in breeding adult and floater survival.
