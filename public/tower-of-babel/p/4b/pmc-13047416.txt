The Value of Nasal Nitric Oxide Measurement in the Diagnosis of Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia
Authors: Eric Gerardus Haarman, Joël Israëls, Niels Willem Rutjes, Tamara Paff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNasal nitric oxide (nNO) is widely used as a first‐line test in the diagnostic evaluation of primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD). Although low nNO is characteristic of classic PCD forms, its diagnostic performance varies across age groups, measurement techniques, and genetic subtypes. This review summarizes current evidence on the diagnostic accuracy of nNO and its evolving position within PCD guidelines. In recent prospective referral cohorts using comprehensive diagnostic work‐ups, pooled sensitivity and specificity remain high, though slightly lower than in earlier, more selected cohorts. Previous European Respiratory Society (ERS) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines took different positions on the role of nNO, but the newly published joint ERS–ATS guideline harmonizes these approaches and recommends nNO as an important, though not stand‐alone, component of the diagnostic pathway. Growing recognition of PCD genotypes with preserved ultrastructure and relatively normal nNO values has broadened the clinical spectrum. In these individuals, as well as in patients classified as “probable PCD” (no genetic or electron microscopic confirmation), diagnostic performance is reduced, with greater overlap between PCD and non‐PCD populations. Age‐adjusted reference values improve discrimination but do not eliminate this overlap. Additional factors, including technique, cooperation, ambient NO, and infection status, further influence interpretation. In conclusion, PCD represents a heterogeneous spectrum of motile ciliopathies with substantial variation in ultrastructure, ciliary function, symptoms, and nNO. Given this heterogeneity, no single biomarker can be expected to perform uniformly across all subtypes. nNO should therefore be interpreted in conjunction with clinical features and complementary diagnostic tests.Keywords: diagnosis, diagnostic guideline, nasal nitric oxide, primary ciliary dyskinesia

Introduction

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare, genetically inherited disorder of motile cilia that leads to impaired mucociliary clearance and progressive airway disease [1]. Symptoms often begin in early life, including neonatal respiratory distress, chronic nasal congestion, persistent wet cough, and recurrent otitis media. Without timely recognition, ongoing inflammation and infection may result in bronchiectasis and long‐term decline in respiratory health and quality of life [2, 3, 4]. Clinical heterogeneity, with symptoms that overlap other respiratory conditions, especially early in life, contributes to diagnostic delay and underscores the need for clinical awareness and structured diagnostic pathways.

PCD diagnosis requires a multimodal approach that integrates clinical assessment with functional evaluation of ciliary motility, ultrastructural analysis, and genetic testing. Recent European Respiratory Society (ERS) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines emphasize the importance of such an integrated strategy and highlight the need for timely referral to specialized centers to ensure accurate evaluation [5, 6, 7]. Within this diagnostic framework, nasal nitric oxide (nNO) measurement has become an important first‐line tool. nNO is often low in individuals with PCD, and the test is non‐invasive, rapid, relatively inexpensive, and feasible in cooperative children aged ≥ 5 years [8, 9, 10]. Since the first description of markedly reduced nNO levels in individuals with PCD, numerous studies have explored its diagnostic value, leading to successive guideline recommendations from the ATS and ERS, and more recently, to a joint ERS–ATS guideline. These developments have paralleled a rapidly expanding understanding of the genetic architecture of PCD, with a growing number of causal genes identified, each associated with diverse functional defects, clinical phenotypes, and biomarker profiles, including variable nNO production. In this review, we outline key technical aspects of nNO measurement, summarize the strengths and limitations of the test, and discuss the major studies that have shaped current guideline recommendations. Finally, we examine nNO patterns across recently described genetic subtypes and consider the evolving role of nNO within the broader diagnostic framework for PCD.

Biological Background of Nasal Nitric Oxide

Nitric oxide (NO) is a small inorganic free‐radical gas produced from l‐arginine by the nitric oxide synthase (NOS) family [11]. Although NO has a wide range of physiological functions, its relevance here concerns its production and roles within the upper respiratory tract [12]. The three NOS isoforms, endothelial (eNOS), neuronal (nNOS), and inducible (iNOS), are all expressed in airway tissues. Epithelial iNOS is generally considered the predominant contributor to NO generation in the upper airways and may form an important component of innate mucosal defence [12, 13, 14]. Whereas eNOS and nNOS are Ca²⁺‐dependent and generate low, continuous NO output, iNOS is Ca²⁺‐independent and is transcriptionally induced by pro‐inflammatory cytokines and microbial stimuli [15, 16]. The paranasal sinuses represent the dominant anatomical source of nasal NO. Early work demonstrated continuous NO production driven by abundant NOS2 expression in sinus epithelium, yielding concentrations one to two orders of magnitude higher than in the lower airways [13]. Although sinus‐derived NO contributes substantially to measurable nasal NO (nNO), modelling and physiological studies indicate that direct transfer of sinus NO into the nasal cavity is limited by physiological and convection constraints. Sinus occlusion seems to lead to modest reductions in nNO [17, 18]. These findings suggest that nasal epithelial NO also contributes to measured nNO. nNO output thus reflects a combined contribution from sinus and nasal mucosal surfaces, modulated by epithelial integrity and sinus ventilation.

Functionally, NO may play several key roles in maintaining airway homeostasis. It seems to enhance mucociliary clearance by increasing ciliary beat frequency [19, 20], and may exert antimicrobial, antiviral, and immunological effects by impairing pathogen viability and modulating local immune responses [20, 21]. Additional NO‐mediated processes relevant to the airway environment include regulation of local vascular tone and modulation of afferent neural pathways [22]. Together, these functions position nasal NO as a central mediator of epithelial defense and sinonasal homeostasis.

nNO levels are characteristically low in primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), although partial overlap exists with other conditions such as cystic fibrosis and acute or chronic sinonasal inflammation [23]. The mechanisms underlying profoundly reduced nNO in PCD are not fully understood, but several biological processes likely contribute. The hypothesis of impaired NO synthesis is supported by observations of reduced NOS2 expression in nasal epithelial cells and lower NO concentrations measured directly from sinuses lacking ciliated epithelium. Additional evidence includes elevated asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), an endogenous nitric oxide synthase inhibitor, together with decreased nitrite and nitrate levels, the stable oxidation products of NO, and the positive association between residual ciliary beat frequency and higher nNO values [24, 25, 26, 27]. Increased NO consumption may further reduce measurable nNO through oxidative reactions in stagnant mucus driven by reactive oxygen species [28]. Impaired delivery of sinus‐derived NO into the nasal cavity, due to reduced sinus ventilation and entrapment of NO in poorly aerated sinus spaces, may also contribute [29, 30]. In addition, structural abnormalities characteristic of PCD, including absent or hypoplastic paranasal sinuses, reduce the anatomical reservoir for sinus‐derived NO and may contribute to low nNO measurements [31].

Technical Aspects of Nasal NO Measurements

Two main technologies are used for nNO measurement: chemiluminescence analyzers and electrochemical sensors. Chemiluminescence analyzers are considered the gold standard because of their high sensitivity and their real‐time graphical display of nNO output, which helps identify stable plateaus for accurate analysis [5, 6, 32]. Electrochemical devices are more affordable, portable, and easier to use, but generally less sensitive and slower in response. However, several studies suggest that they can provide clinically acceptable results if used with validated protocols [33, 34].

Several sampling techniques are employed depending on the child's age and cooperation. The preferred technique in older cooperative children and adults is exhalation against resistance while gas is sampled from the nose, which induces reliable velum closure and thereby avoids contamination and dilution with air from the lower airways [35]. This technique allows for highly reproducible readings and stable NO plateaus. However, it is not feasible in children under 5 years of age. In this younger group, breath holding (to achieve velum closure) or tidal breathing techniques are recommended, in which the child breathes quietly through the mouth while a nasal probe samples continuously at a fixed flow rate. (typically 0.3 L/min.) [36]. Though values obtained by tidal breathing are somewhat lower, the discrimination between PCD and health remains relatively robust [36].

Age influences nNO values [37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. As shown by Marthin et al. (2018), median tidal‐breathing nNO in healthy infants increases by ~5.4% per month from 2 weeks to 2 years of age, rising from 46 ppb in newborns (< 2 weeks) to 283 ppb at 2 years [37]. This increase parallels sinus pneumatization and maturation of nasal epithelial function. Consequently, reference values and diagnostic cut‐offs must be age‐adjusted, particularly in the first years of life. The ERS guideline proposes cut‐offs of 30 nL/min for children 1–2 years of age and 44 nL/min for > 2 years using tidal breathing, and 77 nL/min for children ≥ 5 years when nNo is measured during velum closure (achieved by exhalation against resistance or breath‐hold) at a sampling flow rate of 0.3 L/min [10].

Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) significantly affect nNO readings. During RTIs, nNO can transiently fall by 70%–80%, reaching levels indistinguishable from those seen in PCD [37]. This reduction likely results from mucosal inflammation and sinus blockage, which temporarily hinder NO diffusion. Therefore, nNO should be measured during infection‐free periods or, if possible, at least 2 weeks after an infection, and repeated if initial values are low [10]. Beyond PCD, nNO can also be reduced in other conditions such as cystic fibrosis, chronic sinusitis, nasal polyposis, and diffuse panbronchiolitis. These diseases share a common pathophysiological feature of impaired sinus ventilation or chronic inflammation, leading to reduced NO output. To ensure reliability, both ERS and ATS recommend controlling for ambient NO (< 10 ppb), verifying sampling flow accuracy, and using trained personnel to interpret signal quality [10, 35].

Diagnostic Performance of Nasal NO in PCD‐Testing

Exhaled nitric oxide (NO) was first identified in humans by Gustafsson et al. in 1991 [42]. Three years later, Lundberg et al. demonstrated a link between nasal NO (nNO) levels and primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), showing that affected patients had markedly reduced nNO concentrations [43]. Since then, numerous studies have examined the diagnostic accuracy of nNO in identifying PCD.

Two major manuscripts were published in 2017 summarizing the most important studies addressing this issue, but using a different approach and thus including different studies with different results [5, 9].

One of the most comprehensive reviews on this topic was published by Shapiro and colleagues in 2017 that was performed as part of the development of the 2018 American Thoracic Society (ATS) diagnostic guideline for PCD [6, 9]. Their objective was to systematically assess the utility of nNO measurement as a diagnostic tool for PCD, comparing it with the reference standard of transmission electron microscopy (TEM) evaluation of ciliary defects and/or the detection of biallelic mutations in known PCD genes. The review focused on studies involving cooperative patients, typically over 5 years of age, who had a high clinical suspicion of PCD based on a compatible phenotype. By ensuring that only patients with a confirmed diagnosis of PCD based on TEM and/or genetics were included, the authors aimed to eliminate any false positives and enhance the accuracy of their conclusions.

In total, 12 studies were included, encompassing 1,344 patients—514 with PCD and 830 without (Table 1.). Using TEM alone or TEM combined with genetic testing as the reference standard, the meta‐analysis found that nNO had a summary sensitivity of 97.6% (92.7%–99.2%) and specificity of 96.0% (87.9%–98.7%). This corresponded to a positive likelihood ratio of 24.3 (7.6%–76.9) and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.03 (0.01–0.08), with a diagnostic odds ratio of 956.8 (141.2–6481.5).

When studies using only TEM as the reference standard were excluded, the remaining studies that used a combined reference of TEM and/or genetic testing showed a slightly lower sensitivity of 96.3% (88.7–98.9%) and specificity of 96.4% (85.1%–99.2%), with a positive likelihood ratio of 26.5 (5.9–119.1) and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.04 (0.01–0.12). The overall certainty of the evidence was rated as moderate.

Following the comprehensive review by Shapiro et al. (2017), the ERS Task Force published updated guidelines for the diagnosis of PCD. In contrast to the approaches that primarily focused on studies using TEM and/or genetic confirmation in pre‐selected cohorts, the Task Force adopted a clinically grounded strategy. Studies were included only if they reported nNO performance in an unselected referral population of patients suspected of PCD who subsequently received a PCD‐diagnosis (PCD‐positive or PCD‐negative) based on standard diagnostic modalities, including high‐speed video microscopy (HSVM), TEM, and/or genetic testing. This methodology directly assesses how nNO performs in real‐world diagnostic pathways, rather than in pre‐classified or highly curated disease cohorts. Four key studies, Marthin et al., Leigh et al., Beydon et al., and Jackson et al., met these criteria and formed the evidence base for the nNO diagnostic accuracy estimates presented [35, 36, 45, 49].

The study by Leigh et al. was the only nNO study included in both the Shapiro review and ERS task force guideline. It was a large multicenter investigation designed to establish standardized protocols for nNO measurement in patients with suspected PCD. The authors included 143 PCD‐confirmed patients diagnosed through TEM and/or genetic testing. nNO results were compared with 78 healthy controls and 146 disease controls, including individuals with asthma, cystic fibrosis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Using a standardized chemiluminescence protocol, they identified a disease‐specific nNO cut‐off of 77 nL/min, which yielded a sensitivity of 99% and a specificity of 100% in the case–control cohort after exclusion of cystic fibrosis patients. Although the cut‐off clearly distinguished PCD from non‐PCD controls, overlap with cystic fibrosis underscored the need to rule out alternative diagnoses when interpreting low nNO values. The cut‐off was subsequently evaluated in a referral cohort of 155 patients across multiple centers, demonstrating similarly high sensitivity (99%) but a lower specificity (75%). These findings provided a robust foundation for nNO standardization.

In the study from Marthin and Nielsen three nNO sampling techniques were evaluated as first‐line diagnostic tests for PCD: breath hold (BH‐nNO), oral exhalation against resistance (OE‐R‐nNO), and tidal breathing (TB‐nNO). A total of 282 participants were included, among whom 59 had confirmed PCD, 117 were consecutive referrals (including 20 PCD patients). Diagnosis of PCD followed ERS Task Force criteria and was based on combined assessment of ciliary beat pattern and frequency (CBP/CBF) using high‐speed video microscopy, supplemented by TEM in 78% and pulmonary radioaerosol mucociliary clearance in 68% of the PCD cohort. When zooming in on the consecutive referrals, sensitivity varied from 92% to 100% and specificity from 80% to 96% using the different sampling methods (Table 1).

In the multicenter prospective study by Beydon and coworkers the technical and practical performance was evaluated of tidal breathing nasal nitric oxide (TB‐nNO) measurements in 142 children referred for suspected or confirmed PCD. Because many young children cannot reliably perform velum‐closure (VC) maneuvers such as breath hold or exhalation against resistance, the authors assessed three TB‐nNO calculation methods and compared these with VC‐nNO when feasible. Among the 142 children, 49 were ultimately diagnosed with PCD, 37 were classified as non‐PCD, and 56 had inconclusive diagnostic outcomes. Although TB‐nNO values were consistently lower than VC‐nNO values, diagnostic accuracy was high across all methods. Using method‐specific thresholds, TB‐nNO demonstrated sensitivities of 90–94% and specificities of 92%–97%, which were comparable to VC‐nNO (sensitivity 91%, specificity 86%). In Table 1, the accuracy estimates were calculated solely from the subgroup with a definitive diagnosis (49 PCD and 37 non‐PCD), with inconclusive cases excluded. The study also highlighted several practical considerations relevant for clinical use, including substantial inter‐nostril variability with TB‐nNO, age‐related increases in nNO among non‐PCD children, and methodological influences such as ambient NO concentration and sampling flow. Overall, the findings support TB‐nNO as a reliable and feasible screening approach in children, particularly in younger age groups where VC‐dependent techniques are often not achievable.

In the previous ERS and ATS diagnostic guidelines, nNO occupies different positions in the PCD work‐up [5, 6]. In the 2017 ERS Task Force guideline, nNO is recommended as part of the diagnostic assessment in patients > 6 years, ideally using chemiluminescence and velum‐closure maneuvers, but it is explicitly not a stand‐alone diagnostic test. A definitive PCD diagnosis in the ERS algorithm requires either hallmark TEM defects or biallelic pathogenic variants in a PCD gene. Low nNO contributes to the category “PCD highly likely” when combined with abnormal high‐speed video microscopy (HSVM). Patients in this category are advised to undergo further testing and follow‐up rather than being labelled definitively PCD‐positive. In the 2018 ATS guideline considers nNO as a potential primary diagnostic test in carefully selected patients [6]. For cooperative individuals ≥ 5 years old with a high pre‐test probability of PCD and cystic fibrosis excluded, two reproducibly low nNO values (< 77 nL/min with a standardized chemiluminescence protocol) are accepted as sufficient to confirm PCD, alongside TEM or genotyping, and may replace these reference tests in settings where they are unavailable [6]. This recommendation is graded as conditional but clearly positions nNO closer to a diagnostic “endpoint” than in the ERS document. Shoemark et al. explicitly highlight this divergence, noting that the ATS pathway allows a PCD diagnosis to rest on low nNO alone, whereas the ERS framework regards nNO mainly as an adjunctive, early triage tool within a broader multimodality algorithm [7]. This reflects differences in how the diagnostic questions were framed in the ATS/ERS guidelines, test availability (especially HSVM and TEM in Europe), and healthcare systems between North America and Europe.

A recent study by Raidt et al. (2022) evaluated nNO in a large, well‐characterized cohort of 301 individuals referred for PCD diagnostics. Of these, 180 patients met ERS criteria for a definite PCD diagnosis, supported by hallmark findings on genetics (n = 160), TEM (n = 130), immunofluorescence (n = 172), or combinations thereof. Individuals with abnormal HSVM alone were classified separately as probable PCD (n = 16) and were not included in the diagnostic accuracy analyses. Within the definite PCD group, 47 patients (26%) had genotypes associated with normal ciliary ultrastructure, whereas 133 patients (74%) had defects leading to abnormal ultrastructure. This is notable because normal‐ultrastructure variants are typically underrepresented in earlier nNO studies. Raidt and colleagues demonstrated that the widely used 77 nL/min cutoff performed less well in these normal‐ultrastructure subtypes: sensitivity fell to 85% (7/47 false negatives), compared with 95% in those with abnormal ultrastructure. To improve detection of these variants, the authors proposed a higher cutoff of approximately 108 nL/min., which increased sensitivity for normal‐ultrastructure PCD to 89%, though specificity decreased to 78%.

Interestingly, in the group of definite PCD patients (n = 180), 15 individuals presented with nNO production rates within the normal range. These patients included 13 genetically defined individuals carrying pathogenic mutations in 12 PCD‐associated genes (DNAI1, DNAH5, DNAH9, DNAH11, CCDC103, RSPH1, SPEF2, CCNO, FOXJ1, RPGR, OFD1, NEK10). In the 2 remaining individuals, only one mutant allele was detected in the genes DNAH5 and ODAD1. Though the second allele was not discovered, TEM and immunofluorescence confirmed the ODA defects. In addition, 9 out of 16 individuals with a probable diagnosis based on HSVM, thus without genetic or TEM confirmation of the diagnosis, had nNO production rates within the normal rates.

In 2025, the joint ATS/ERS diagnostic guideline for PCD was published, including a PICO on nNO in PCD diagnostics. To answer the question about the diagnostic accuracy of nNO in a consecutive referral population, the guideline included five studies that evaluated nNO in populations undergoing comprehensive PCD work‐up using TEM and/or genetic testing as reference standards. Across these five cohorts, nNO demonstrated consistently high diagnostic accuracy. The pooled sensitivity was 93% (95% CI 88%–95%) and the pooled specificity was 88% (95% CI 79%–93%), indicating that low nNO values are strongly associated with a confirmed PCD diagnosis. When applying the frequently used cut‐off of 77 nL/min, a sensitivity analysis showed nearly identical accuracy, with a sensitivity of 94% (95% CI 87%–97%) and a specificity of 83% (95% CI 77%–87%). Based on this evidence, the guideline issues a strong recommendation to use nNO during velum closure as part of the diagnostic pathway in patients with a clinical phenotype suggestive of PCD. Importantly, the guideline emphasizes that nNO should not be used as a stand‐alone diagnostic test. Although most patients with PCD have markedly reduced nNO, a subset, particularly those with PCD variants exhibiting normal ultrastructure, may have values above traditional cut‐offs. Conversely, low nNO values can occur in other conditions, underscoring the need for integrated interpretation with clinical features and definitive diagnostic tests. For children unable to perform velum closure, evidence for tidal‐breathing measurements was analyzed. It was noted the accuracy is substantially lower and more variable (sensitivity 77%–100%, specificity 57%–90%), resulting in only a conditional recommendation. Because no universally accepted reference standard for diagnosing PCD exists, the guideline limited its evidence synthesis to studies that confirmed PCD by genetic testing and/or TEM. This strategy may have excluded individuals with PCD variants that are not detectable by these methods. As previously reported, the sensitivity of both TEM and genetic testing is estimated to be approximately 70%, meaning that a considerable number of patients remain unidentified when analyses are restricted to those with a definite diagnosis [50, 51, 52]. However, with the expanding number of identified PCD‐related genes, the sensitivity of genetic analysis is probably higher when using the most up‐to‐date gene panels.

The genotype–nNO relationship has now become one of the central considerations in interpreting nNO results. RSPH1 mutations, first described by Knowles et al. (2014), are a typical example: affected individuals often exhibit preserved beat frequency, absent laterality defects, subtle or absent ultrastructural abnormalities, and nNO values that can exceed classical cutoffs. Similarly, the CCDC103 p.His154Pro founder variant, studied reported by Shoemark and colleagues, shows striking phenotypic variability [53]. Many patients have normal or borderline TEM findings, relatively preserved motility, and nNO levels well within the range of non‐PCD individuals. Even within the same family or genotype, nNO values may vary considerably. Other genotypes associated with normal ultrastructure or partial function, such as GAS8 demonstrate nNO values that may fall into borderline or normal ranges. Perhaps the most striking deviation from classical expectations comes from genes involved in ciliogenesis, like FOXJ1 with nNO production rate falling within the normal range. For CCNO, incidentally, a normal nasal NO production was described [26], but in in general levels are low [54].

Because the number of PCD‐associated genes has expanded considerably in recent years, including many in which TEM abnormalities are absent and nasal NO values may fall within the normal range, the sensitivity of nasal NO testing as reported in earlier studies may be overestimated. PCD should not be regarded as a single disorder, but rather as a broad group of motile ciliopathies with variability in ultrastructure, ciliary function, symptomatology, and associated biomarkers, including nNO. Against this background of genetic and clinical heterogeneity, it is unlikely that a single biomarker will perform uniformly across all subtypes, and nasal NO should be interpreted in this context.

Conclusion

In conclusion, accurate nNO measurement requires age‐appropriate techniques, standardized protocols, and awareness of factors such as infections and comorbidities that influence readings. Chemiluminescence analyzers remain the gold standard, but electrochemical devices offer a practical and lower‐cost alternative. Nasal nitric oxide remains a powerful diagnostic tool for PCD, particularly in older children and adults with classical genotypes characterized by severe motility impairment and ultrastructural defects. However, its sensitivity and specificity decline meaningfully in real‐world populations that include a broader diversity of genotypes, younger children, patients with preserved ultrastructure, and those tested during acute infection. Contemporary evidence from genotype‐specific studies and multicenter cohorts such as the ERN‐Lung database demonstrates that nNO should not be regarded as a universal gatekeeper test, but should be part of an integrated diagnostic strategy, interpreted within clinical and genetic context.
