INTRODUCTION

Fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC) is a rare primary liver cancer with distinctive histology, molecular biology, and clinical presentation that predominantly affects adolescents and young adults. Its characteristic histology was first described by Edmondson in 1956 following resection of a liver carcinoma from a 14-year-old patient.1 It was subsequently described as a distinct clinical and histological subtype of conventional hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in 1980 by Craig et al.2 based upon the presence of abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and lamellar bands of intratumor fibrosis on histology and diagnosis in adolescents and young adults with noncirrhotic livers and a normal alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). Before the 2014 discovery of a recurrent molecular driver absent from conventional HCC, FLC was considered a subset of HCC defined primarily by its distinctive pathology and epidemiology.3–5 Pediatric and adult oncologists’ therapeutic approaches to FLC evolved separately, with systemic therapy regimens derived from those used in hepatoblastoma and conventional HCC, respectively, even in the absence of FLC-specific rationale.3,4 However, the response of FLC to these regimens has been poor, and the use of these therapies has not improved long-term survival rates.3,6,7 As a result, patients with FLC have often been excluded from trials designated specifically for hepatoblastoma or conventional HCC.3,8–10

NEW UNDERSTANDING OF FLC BIOLOGY: DISCOVERY OF THE DNAJB1::PRKACA FUSION

In 2014, the discovery of a novel chimeric fusion transcript, DNAJB1::PRKACA, found in nearly all FLC tumors transformed understanding of the disease, as the fusion protein is believed to be the driver of FLC oncogenesis and persistence.4,11–18 This fusion results from a heterozygous deletion of ~400 kb in chromosome 19 that links exon 1 of the heat shock protein DNAJB1 to exons 2–10 of PRKACA, the catalytic subunit of protein kinase A (PKA; Figure 1A).11 The deletion’s relatively small size had previously prevented it from being discovered with conventional cytogenic techniques, requiring the use of RNA sequencing for detection.4,11 Induction of this deletion and expression of the DNAJB1::PRKACA transcript in mouse liver and human hepatocytes have resulted in the production of tumors resembling FLC histologically and cytologically.15,19 FLC has been shown to be oncogenically addicted to the fusion protein, as its inhibition results in tumor cell death in vitro and inhibition of tumor growth in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models.15,20 While the fusion has predominantly been detected in FLC tumors, it has also been shown to occur in intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms (IOPNs) of the pancreas and bile duct, which share some histopathologic and cytologic similarities to FLC.21,22 While the biochemical mechanisms by which the DNAJB1–PRKACA fusion protein (DNAJ–PKAc) drives FLC are still under investigation, work to date suggests that increased expression/activity, mislocalization, and altered physical interactions may play roles.3 As detailed below, downstream transcriptional, metabolic, and signaling phenotypes driven by DNAJ–PKAc have been defined in FLC cell models and tumors, confirming the distinctions between FLC and conventional liver cancers. The fusion gene has demonstrated the ability to suppress differentiation of liver progenitor organoids into hepatocytes, and FLC tumors have been shown to co-express biliary, hepatocellular, and hepatic progenitor/stem cell markers, suggesting the tumor cells may be arrested at a specific stage of differentiation.23–26

The dependency of FLC on the DNAJB1::PRKACA driver is underscored by the infrequency of other somatic genetic alterations. FLC has a low tumor mutational burden, with infrequent mutations in genes reported to be implicated in other liver carcinomas.3,13 Genomic alterations besides the fusion detected in FLC specimens include activating mutations in the TERT promoter (found in the tumors of 7 of 31 patients studied) and mutations in MUC4 (found in tumors of 4 of 10 patients studied).13,27 Of note, mucin 4 can complex with HER2 to promote cell proliferation and migration.28 FLC has also been shown to overexpress ERBB2, translating to HER2 cell surface expression, neurotensin, and FGFR1, and shows activation of mTORC1.29–32 Unlike conventional HCC and hepatoblastoma, FLC generally lacks mutations in CTNNB1 and TP53, mismatch repair defects, or, relatedly, aberrant beta-catenin staining.3,14,18,33–35 However, p53 has been shown to be dysregulated, and MDM4, its negative regulator, is present in the nucleus at elevated levels.3,36 This mutational spectrum is distinct from those of other hepatocellular tumors.

FLC also has distinct and consistent transcriptomic, microRNA (miRNA), long intergenic non-coding RNA (lincRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), and proteomic profiles that may be linked to FLC’s distinguishing histology and other phenotypic findings including elevated aromatase levels and clinical findings of gynecomastia sometimes observed in young male patients, and occasional occurrence of hyperammonemic encephalopathy (HAE).18,19,31,37,38 Comparison of FLC’s transcriptomic, miRNA, lncRNA, and lincRNA profiles with those seen in conventional HCC show them to be highly distinct, with FLC demonstrating unique and consistent transcriptomic, miRNA, lincRNA, lncRNA, and proteomic signatures.18,19,31,38 Examination of FLC’s proteomic profile suggests that the effects of the fusion disrupt metabolism and lead to mitochondrial dysregulation.37 Notably, these proteomic changes appear to explain the occurrence of HAE in FLC in a mechanism independent from liver failure.37 Interestingly, in extremely rare events, PRKAR1A loss due to the Carney complex has also been reported to induce FLCs similar in transcriptomic signature, histology, and clinical presentation to those driven by the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion.19,39 In addition, alternative fusions between the catalytic subunits of PKA and ATP1B1 have also produced IOPNs of the pancreas and bile duct with some histopathologic similarity to FLC that also share FLC’s transcriptomic signature and drug response profile.19,22,40 In contrast, HCC with PKA-activating BAP1 mutations exhibit a mixed FLC/HCC or FLC-like phenotype in older patients.24 The transcriptomes of these tumors cluster separately from FLC, with shared but non-overlapping transcriptional markers of PKA activation.19 Overexpression of DNAJB1::PRKACA, ATP1B1::PRKACA, or PRKACA (consistent with PRKAR1A loss) all cause similar transcriptomic changes in human hepatocytes mirroring those seen in FLC, suggesting that changes in PKA localization and activity rather than the DNAJB1 domain itself may determine the FLC phenotype.40 In conclusion, discovery of the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion has not only revolutionized the understanding of FLC’s biology but also continues to drive investigation and spark novel efforts to more effectively model and treat the disease.3,4,15,17,41–49

EPIDEMIOLOGICAL IMPLICATIONS

Advances in understanding FLC’s biology have translated to an increased understanding of the tumor’s epidemiology. FLC is known to be a very rare cancer affecting adolescents and young adults, but the accurate incidence remains in question. Before the discovery of the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion, many FLCs were likely misdiagnosed as conventional HCC and vice versa, and the lack of a unique ICD-10 code may continue to contribute to underestimations.3,4 Indeed, in an analysis of RNA-sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), half (3 of 6) of tumors from adolescents and young adults with the fusion and FLC histology were incorrectly annotated as conventional HCC.3,18 Relatedly, a 2017 study with expert pathology re-review of 124 tumors initially classified histologically as FLC, revealed that while 102 out of 103 tumors of verified “typical FLC” histology had the fusion, 8 tumors were histologically unlikely to be FLC and lacked the fusion, and 3 out of 12 tumors with “possible” FLC histology lacked the fusion.50 Notably, the 8 tumors found to be histologically unlikely to be FLC were resected from patients with a median age of 61 years, compared with an average age of 23.8 years for histologically confirmed FLC.50 The one fusion-negative patient with histologically confirmed FLC had a family history of the Carney complex, which, as noted, has been reported to cause clinically and histologically typical but fusion-negative FLC in rare cases.39,50 Analysis of Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) data from 1986 to 1999, 2000 to 2010, and 2000 to 2016 suggests an annual incidence of ~0.2 per million in the United States; the first analysis suggested that FLC comprises 0.85% of primary liver cancer overall and 13.4% of primary liver cancers in patients <40 years old.51–53 However, other estimates of FLC report an incidence as high as 1%–9% of overall primary liver cancers, but may be skewed by increasing incidence of conventional HCC or tertiary care referral bias.3,33,54–56 A comprehensive analysis of electronic medical record and payer data has revealed that FLC incidence is likely 5–8 times higher than the older SEER-based estimates.55 While FLC has been reported with relatively similar prevalence around the world, its incidence has not been systematically tracked outside the United States, and its global prevalence remains uncertain.3,33,56 Systematic reviews and a 2022 analysis of the Fibrolamellar Registry based on patients enrolled from 2016 to 2021 found FLC’s median age of diagnosis to be ~22 years, with ~38% of patients diagnosed before age 20.3,6,7,33,57,58 The Fibrolamellar Registry is a patient-reported registry, which may introduce bias in reported age based on comfort and familiarity with participation in such an initiative. While some SEER data collected before the discovery of the fusion suggest a higher median age at diagnosis and a purported “second peak” in incidence later in life, more recent studies, retrospective series of pathologically confirmed cases from original studies, and expert observation do not support this finding, which may represent a population of patients separate from classical FLC.3,6,7,24,33,50,52,53,55,57

There have been reports of liver cancers, usually in older patients, presenting with mixed histological features of both FLC and HCC.3,59–61 These tumors have been shown to be biologically and clinically distinct from “pure” FLC, with presentation and behavior usually more closely resembling conventional HCC than FLC, including an older age of onset, distinct genetic and transcriptomic background, and an HCC-like natural history.3,24,29,59,61 While tumors with mixed histology and the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion have been reported, most mixed tumors are believed to have a different genetic basis.3,24,62 In a genomic and transcriptomic study examining 15 histological FLCs and 10 tumors of mixed FLC/HCC histology, FLC and mixed FLC/HCC clustered into distinct subgroups based upon RNA sequencing: one group of 13 FLC and 2 mixed tumors that all harbored the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion and originated from young patients (median age 26 years) and one group of 17 tumors including 6 mixed, 2 histologically “FLC” tumors from older patients, and 9 HCC tumors.24 Notably, the latter group of tumors all harbored inactivating alterations in the tumor suppressor BAP1, lacked the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion, and originated in older patients (median age 52 years).24 In all, 83% of these BAP1-altered tumors showed a chromosome gain at PRKACA and deletions at 3p21.24 These deletions encompassed both BAP1 and the negative regulatory subunit of PKA PRKAR2A, leading to an elevated PRKACA/PRKAR2A ratio in mRNA and protein and increased PKA activity, the latter of which is similar to that seen in the fusion-positive FLCs, supporting an alternative mechanism of PKA activation.24 In addition to sharing a genomic profile, these patients showed clinical and histological characteristics distinct from the younger, fusion-positive patients with FLC.24 Later transcriptomic analysis integrating multiple datasets revealed that unlike ATP1B1::PRKACA and PRKAR1A-altered FLC and FLC-like tumors, BAP1-mutated FLC-like tumors did not cluster with fusion-positive FLC.19 The discovery of this BAP1-altered subgroup of HCC that sometimes exhibits FLC-like or mixed histological features, along with misdiagnoses, may explain the purported “second peak” in FLC incident age reported in some SEER data collected before the fusion’s discovery.3,4,50,55

CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND NATURAL HISTORY

FLC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage and frequently presents differently from conventional HCC. FLC occurs in patients without apparent liver disease, and unlike conventional HCC and hepatoblastoma, has no known genetic, environmental, or clinical risk factors.3,4,33,56,63,64 Unlike conventional HCC and hepatoblastoma, there is no male predominance; studies have reported gender parity or slight female predominance.6,33,65,66 Patients with FLC classically present with symptoms such as abdominal pain, gastrointestinal symptoms, a palpable mass, weight loss, and malaise, as well as occasionally abdominal distension from ascites and, rarely, gynecomastia resulting from elevated aromatase expression or mental status changes due to hyperammonemia.3–5,33 As noted previously, the median age of onset is often much younger than conventional HCC. Unlike in conventional HCC and hepatoblastoma, AFP is typically normal, but serum vitamin B12 binding capacity may be elevated, and elevated procalcitonin has been noted in a few case reports.4,6,33,67–70 Histologically, tumors exhibit abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and lamellar bands of intratumor fibrosis, and immunohistochemical stains demonstrate consistent co-expression of CK7 and CD68.3,4,23,33,50,67,71 Investigators have more recently stained tumors more consistently for HER2, given the prospect of HER2 targeted therapies; while frequency and intensity of staining have not been formally reported, tumors are believed to variably express cell-surface HER2. The primary tumor typically presents as a large, lobulated mass often exhibiting a central scar on cross-sectional imaging.2,4,6,33,53,67,72 FLC may be more commonly located in the left lobe of the liver; in the 18 cases in Craig et al.,2 in which the involved liver lobes were identified, 13 cases involved the left lobe (including 1 case involving both lobes). FLCs are described to usually appear heterogeneously hypervascular with avidly enhancing tumor vessels on contrast-enhanced CT, with variable enhancement on portal-venous phase and delayed-phase images, as opposed to the arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE) and washout pattern more typically seen in HCC.73,74 Case series spanning institutions demonstrate that FLC has a particular propensity to metastasize to local lymph nodes, and other common sites of metastasis include the lungs and peritoneum.3,4,6,56 While once believed to follow a more uniformly indolent course, FLC’s natural history is now understood to be heterogeneous, with some patients surviving for many years as their disease progresses slowly in an oligometastatic pattern and some patients succumbing very quickly to aggressive disease.6,7,57

PROGNOSIS

While complete resection or recurrent resection is associated with prolonged survival, FLC ultimately carries a poor prognosis, and long-term survival is unfortunately rare. Due to the vague and nonspecific nature of most patients’ symptoms as well as their young age and typically otherwise good health, FLC often presents as advanced disease, with regional lymph node involvement and distant metastases in 30%–60% and 30%–50% of patients, respectively.3,4,6,33,67,75 Due to this advanced pattern of disease, potentially curative resection is not possible for many patients with FLC at the time of diagnosis.3,4 While once believed to have a better prognosis than conventional HCC, FLC has since been found to have a comparable prognosis to pediatric conventional HCC and adult HCC occurring in noncirrhotic livers.4,65,67,75,76 Patients eligible for curative-intent resection have the best prognosis, with reported 5-year survival ranging from about 60% to 80%.4,58,77,78 Unfortunately, however, a reported 50%–100% of completely resected tumors recur, and most patients eventually die of their disease.3,6,19,58,75 While a small case series has suggested that most recurrences have been reported to occur within 4 years of surgery, recurrence has been reported as far out as 18 years.77,79 For patients with unresectable disease at diagnosis, reported outcomes are poor, with 5-year survival of <10% and median survival <12 months.4,6,78 Lymph node involvement, metastases at diagnosis, extrahepatic disease, vascular invasion, and the presence of 10 or more tumors in the liver have all been associated with worse prognosis, while resectable disease, undergoing surgery, and female gender have been associated with better prognosis.6,7,33,57,58,78 A transcriptomic analysis reported in 2015 generated an 8-gene expression signature reported to predict prognosis in 22 patients, but this has not been validated in a larger cohort.14

METHODOLOGY

We performed a systematic literature review of PubMed, ASCO, and ASPHO publications using 2 approaches. First, we searched PubMed and the ASCO database of journals, abstracts, and presentations for the search term “fibrolamellar” and then reviewed the results (935 from PubMed, 37 results from ASCO) to identify published trials and case reports potentially supporting the use of different interventions in FLC, defined here as reports of therapies producing clinical benefit of stable disease (SD) or better in a patient with FLC. Second, individual structured interviews were performed in the panel of 20 FLC expert authors, including 6 medical oncologists, 4 pediatric oncologists, 4 surgeons, 2 pathologists, 1 radiation oncologist, 1 interventional radiologist, 1 pediatric palliative care specialist, and 1 pediatric clinical social worker on their diagnosis and treatment of FLC. Based on the results of these interviews, a second direct search of therapies was performed using PubMed, ASCO, and ASPHO to identify any further regimens reported in literature to fulfill the same criteria of producing clinical benefit of SD or better in a patient with FLC. Using these 2 methods, we compiled a list of regimens reported in the literature to produce clinical benefit in FLC and formally surveyed the panel of oncologists regarding which regimens they would utilize for the treatment of FLC in different settings. The panel of surgeons was also surveyed on which surgical approaches they would pursue for the treatment of patients with FLC in specific settings. This voting process formed the base of our guideline recommendations. A medical recommendation was included as “strong” if it received votes from at least 7 oncologists and as “weak” if it received votes from 4–6 oncologists. A surgical recommendation was included as “strong” if it received votes from at least 3 surgeons and as “weak” if it received votes from 2 surgeons. If either panel had fewer than the minimum number of votes designating a recommendation as “weak,” the treatment or local control approach was not recommended. The panel then reviewed the final collated recommendations and provided feedback.

The diagnosis of FLC requires histologic, molecular, and clinical components. FLCs display a characteristic histology exhibiting large polygonal tumor cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and large, centrally located nuclei with vesiculated chromatin and prominent macronucleoli.67,80 The tumor cells are usually embedded within the lamellar bands of fibrosis that give the tumor its name, although the fibrosis can also show a more irregular pattern of deposition (Figure 1B).67,80 Nearly all FLCs are moderately differentiated.33 Other common histologic features of FLC are pale bodies and hyaline bodies, which are present in about half of cases, and prominent lysosomes.67,80 FLCs should also stain positive for co-expression of CK7 and CD68.3,4,33,50 FLC metastases usually show the same histologic features as the primary tumor.67 While FLC’s histology is quite distinctive, the diagnosis of FLC should not be made purely upon histology, as conventional HCC, particularly of the scirrhous subtype, can sometimes show significant histological overlap with FLC.67 For purposes of diagnosis, core needle biopsies are recommended over fine needle aspiration (FNA), as FNA may aspirate malignant hepatocytes without fibrotic lamellae and thus obscure the characteristic histology.70

Molecular support for the diagnosis of FLC requires confirmation of the presence of the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion, or, in extremely rare cases, biallelic loss of PRKAR1A in the context of the Carney complex (estimated ~1% of FLC).3,4,50,67 As the fusion results from a small deletion, it is easy to miss without a directed approach; it is not typically included on most NGS panels and cannot be detected via karyotyping or array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH). The fusion gene can be detected by a targeted RNA or DNA fusion NGS panel (RNA preferred) that targets DNAJB1 and/or PRKACA for rearrangements, reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) for DNAJB1::PRKACA, or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for DNAJB1, preferred, or PRKACA.4,12,67 Although less commonly used in clinical practice, an RT-PCR assay for paraffin-embedded tissues detected the fusion transcript in 24/24 successfully amplified FLC samples.12 FISH is easily performed on paraffin-embedded tissue and only requires ~50 tumor cells; a custom break-apart FISH assay set for the PRKACA locus designed to detect the fusion for a large, multinational study successfully detected the fusion in 103/104 cases.50,67 However, testing for the fusion via a commercially available break-apart probe targeting the PRKACA gene successfully detected the fusion in only 92% (11/12) of samples tested, raising some uncertainty about this assay.81 RT-PCR and RNA-based NGS can likewise be performed using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues and are highly sensitive for the detection of the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion.

Diagnosis of FLC also requires the incorporation of both clinical and radiographic elements. FLC is generally diagnosed in individuals between the ages of 10 and 35 without a history of liver disease with a normal or minimally elevated serum AFP.4,33 Characteristic appearance of a large, lobulated solitary tumor with a central scar on cross-sectional imaging, preferably abdominopelvic MRI or alternatively CT, also supports an FLC diagnosis.4,33,82 However, as not all FLCs display a central scar and other liver masses, such as focal nodular hyperplasia, can also display a similar central scar, its presence does not necessarily indicate FLC, and misdiagnoses of FNH as FLC have been reported.72,83,84 While other liver cancers are well accepted to show regional lymph node, hilar, peritoneal, and lung metastases, FLC’s propensity to spread to these sites (particularly the regional lymph nodes and hilum) compared with conventional HCC can also help support diagnosis.3,4,6,56,82

Ancillary testing can help guide the care of patients with FLC. Patients with suspected FLC should have somatic next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel testing for other potentially actionable alterations sent, although, as noted previously, such alterations are rarely found.3,13,33 In addition, IHC testing for HER2 overexpression should be pursued in light of evolving practice and the 2024 pan-tumor approval of the antibody–drug conjugate trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) for unresectable or metastatic HER2 IHC 3+ solid tumors.29,85 IHC is advised rather than FISH or other assay, as prior evidence has suggested that HER2 upregulation in FLC is transcriptional rather than due to genomic amplification.29,31 Because FLC can result in elevated serum ammonia levels mimicking the metabolic patterns seen in patients with ornithine transcarboxylase deficiency and, in extreme cases, HAE, measuring a baseline serum ammonia level is also recommended and can be followed throughout the course of therapy and correlated with symptoms of altered mental status.55,86 In addition, while not all patients with FLC have an elevated serum vitamin B12 binding capacity upon assay, this laboratory assessment can be used as a surrogate marker to follow tumor progression when it is elevated at diagnosis. Similarly, emerging data show serum procalcitonin is elevated in FLC versus conventional HCC and is sensitive to disease activity and treatment response.87 The use of Signatera’s circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) blood test for monitoring has also been reported in a retrospective study of 51 patients at a single institution.88 Negative Signatera values were reported to correlate with no evidence of disease on imaging 78% of the time, while positive values were reported to correlate with disease 94% of the time.88 Given the propensity for false negatives, use of this tool should be interpreted with caution until larger cohort data is generated.

A range of staging systems, including the AJCC TNM (American Joint Committee on Cancer Tumor, Nodes, and Metastasis) staging system, PRETEXT (PRE-Treatment EXTent of Tumor), and Evans Surgical Staging System, have been used to document disease extent for patients with FLC, depending on whether patients are treated in the adult or pediatric settings.7,89–91 A prospective study of 65 consecutive patients with FLC treated at a single academic institution revealed that the AJCC staging system fails to adequately stratify patients with FLC with respect to recurrence-free survival.75 PRETEXT and Evans staging have not been validated or paired with outcomes. Because none of these staging systems were developed for FLC, experts rely less on staging systems and base treatment more directly on clinical factors, particularly tumor resectability and growth rate, the presence of multifocal disease, lymph node and vascular involvement, and extrahepatic metastases, and the degree to which tumor sites can be addressed with definitive local therapies.4,75

Surgery is central to the treatment of patients with FLC, and complete resection or liver transplantation, when the disease is confined to the liver but otherwise unresectable, are essential for cure.3–5 Because FLC is generally less sensitive to many systemic therapies than conventional HCC and much less sensitive to chemotherapy than hepatoblastoma, surgery plays a prominent role in the treatment of FLC.3,4,56,92,93 The absence of cirrhosis in FLC also enables a much more aggressive surgical approach than is possible for patients with conventional HCC and cirrhosis, allowing for more extensive resections and multiple surgeries for recurrent disease.77,89,94–96 Some centers offer portal vein embolization followed by resection, or the associating liver partition and portal vein ligation for staged hepatectomy for FLC patients to allow aggressive resection when it would not otherwise be tolerated.

There is a wide range of surgical pathways available to patients with FLC, dictated by patterns of disease spread. For patients with a single liver lesion and/or a very characteristic radiologic appearance for FLC, the expert panel confirms that proceeding to resection without biopsy may be reasonable. There are a number of case reports and case series documenting the neoadjuvant use of systemic therapies as well as locoregional therapies to downstage FLC and enable resection. These options may be warranted for patients whose disease is initially considered unresectable or in patients for whom resection may be associated with significant risk or morbidity.7,97–102 In a retrospective series of 95 patients treated at 3 academic centers from 1986 to 2011, 31.5% of patients who underwent surgery received perioperative therapy, and 12% of patients in a retrospective series of 94 patients with FLC treated at a single academic center from 1992 to 2008 received neoadjuvant chemotherapy.6,7 Our expert panel felt that the use of neoadjuvant therapy has gained traction in recent years. Enabling surgery in patients initially considered unresectable is associated with superior outcomes: a retrospective analysis of 149 evaluable patients in the Fibrolamellar Registry from 2016 to 2021 found that of the patients initially diagnosed with inoperable tumors, those who subsequently underwent a surgical resection had a 5-year survival of 59.5%, compared with 22.5% for those who never received surgery (p<0.006).57

Surgery is often pursued in patients with advanced disease; the retrospective series of 95 patients with FLC from 3 academic centers described above revealed that although 50% of patients presented with metastatic disease at diagnosis, 77% of patients underwent resection and/or liver transplantation; surgery was performed with curative intent in 58 patients and as palliative debulking only in 15 patients.6 Similarly, although 28.1% of the 146 retrospectively evaluated patients from the 2016–2021 Fibrolamellar Registry manuscript had metastatic disease upon diagnosis, 85% of the 143 patients with available data underwent surgery for their disease.57 Metastasectomy may prolong survival; in the retrospective case series of 94 patients with FLC treated at a single academic center from 1992 to 2008, the 18 patients who underwent metastasectomy had significantly longer overall survival (OS) than those who did not (145 vs. 35 months).7 Debulking surgery has also been successfully used to manage refractory HAE in patients with FLC.86 With the advent of ammonia scavengers, such as Rivicti, there are alternatives to surgery for the treatment of hyperammonemia, but surgery continues to play a crucial role.103–105 There are also reports of cytoreductive surgery (CRS) with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) in patients with FLC, including a retrospective description of 3 patients who experienced a median tumor-free interval of 11 months following CRS with HIPEC.106–109

Many patients with FLC, particularly patients with an indolent, oligometastatic disease course, undergo recurrent surgeries for relapsed disease, and repeat surgery may be the most effective treatment for disease recurrence.77,78,95,107 Case reports document patients undergoing ultimately curative resections for recurrent disease, and multiple case series spanning 8 institutions report prolonged long-term survival for patients undergoing repeat resections for disease recurrence, including a number of patients who underwent 3 or 4 separate surgeries.77,95,96,107,108,110 For example, in a retrospective series of 10 patients treated at a single academic center from 1982 to 2004, repeated resections resulted in an overall median survival after initial relapse of 4.7 years and a 5-year survival following relapse of 48%, for a median overall survival of 9.3 years.96 Three of 10 patients remained alive at a median follow-up of 78 months, including a patient whose resection of recurrent disease was curative at 8-year follow-up.96 Similarly, 17 (61%) of the 41 patients in a retrospective study of patients with FLC treated at a single academic center from 1986 to 2003 underwent a second surgery for recurrent disease and achieved a median survival of 26 months post-second resection.78 Our expert panel strongly endorses the use of multiple resections where appropriate and feasible for patients with recurrent disease on the basis of patient fitness, lack of effective systemic therapies, and the data noted above. Similarly, the panel recommends consideration for debulking surgery with the aim of palliation or life prolongation, even when complete (R1 or R0) resection is not possible.

Multiple studies across institutions, consortia, and reporting on data from the National Cancer Database (NCDB) verify significantly increased lymph node involvement in FLC compared with conventional HCC (Figure 2).78,89,111,112 Indeed, Schultz et al.’s retrospective cohort analysis of 262 adolescent and young adult (AYA) patients in the NCDB with nonmetastatic FLC and conventional HCC from 2004 An analysis of the United Network of Organ Sharing (UNOS) database from October 1988 to January 2013 analyzing outcomes for patients with FLC and conventional HCC who underwent liver transplantation in the United States identified 63 patients with FLC in the cohort; these patients’ outcomes were comparable to those of patients undergoing liver transplantation for conventional HCC, with 1, 3, and 5-year overall survival at 96%, 80%, and 48% for patients with FLC compared with 89%, 77%, and 68%, for patients with HCC.122 Six of the 63 patients’ tumors recurred following transplantation, acute cellular rejection occurred in 4.8% of patients, and the Mean Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score and cold ischemic time most strongly predicted FLC patient survival following transplant.122 A 2012 review of treatment of patients with FLC reported on 79 patients with FLC who underwent liver transplant, with 1, 3, and 5-year survival ranging from 63% to 100%, 43% to 75%, and 29% to 55%, respectively, with varying rates of recurrence.58 A single-center retrospective study from 1980 to 1989 included 10 patients with FLC treated with liver transplantation, whose median overall survival of 51.4 ± 14.4 months was comparable to the median OS of 95 patients with conventional HCC treated with liver transplant (47.5 ± 5.5 mo).123 Successful multivisceral transplant in a patient with FLC with extensive portomesenteric venous involvement has also been reported (124; personal communication, Dr Allison O’Neill).

Dramatic progress has been made in systemic therapy for patients with FLC in recent years. However, there are no clear standards of care yet. Thus, we recommend offering all patients with unresectable, completely or incompletely resected, or relapsed FLC (termed here advanced) a clinical trial, as available. However, for patients who do not wish to participate in a clinical trial or lack available clinical trial options, there are also a number of regimens whose use is supported by the literature and expert experience. GEMOX has been reported to produce responses in patients with FLC, including a prolonged CR in a patient with advanced disease.133 In a separate case series including 9 patients with advanced disease treated with GEMOX, 1 patient achieved a PR, and 2 patients experienced SD.134 GEMOX has been used in the neoadjuvant setting to enable resection in multiple patients, including in patients with relapsed disease.97,98 GEMOX has also been reported to result in clinical benefit when combined with lenvatinib in case series.135,136 These findings come in the context of prior work studying the combination of GEMOX + lenvatinib in cholangiocarcinoma.137 In FLC, one case series of 67 patients treated with GEMOX + lenvatinib reported 1 CR, 12 PRs, 45 patients with SD, and 1 patient with progression by RECIST.136 Of note, there are no data to date comparing GEMOX + lenvatinib to GEMOX alone; pathophysiologically, combining an antiangiogenic TKI with chemotherapy could theoretically increase efficacy but may also incur the risk of added toxicity. Ipilimumab + nivolumab has also been reported in 2 separate case reports to result in a “near” complete response and a CR in patients with advanced disease that progressed following previous lines of therapy.138,139 Ipilimumab + nivolumab was approved by the FDA for unresectable or metastatic HCC in April of 2025; however, it should be noted that the sentinel CHECKMATE-9DW trial specifically excluded patients with FLC.7 Ipilimumab + nivolumab is also currently being evaluated in combination with a DNAJ–PKAc peptide vaccine in an ongoing phase I trial.8 Although the optimal dosing regimen of ipilimumab plus nivolumab in FLC has not been established, prior prospective studies and published case reports in this population have most commonly utilized ipilimumab 1 mg/kg combined with nivolumab 3 mg/kg administered every 3 weeks for four doses, followed by nivolumab maintenance. Based on the available evidence in FLC, this dosing schedule appears to offer a reasonable balance of antitumor activity and tolerability, and may be considered an appropriate regimen in the absence of data supporting an alternative dosing strategy. Preliminary results include a reassuring safety profile, PR by RECIST in 3/12 evaluable patients, and stable disease in 6 further patients.8

Given a paucity of reliably efficacious therapies for patients with FLC, there is minimal published guidance on the sequencing of therapies. The expert panel acknowledged this lack of guidance and the theoretical risk of rendering therapies less effective due to the order in which they are delivered (eg, dual checkpoint blockade following single-agent ICI or immunotherapy after marrow suppressive chemotherapy). As noted previously, GEMOX, GEMOX + lenvatinib, and ipilimumab + nivolumab have all demonstrated efficacy in patients with refractory or relapsed disease.133,136,138 In the relapsed/refractory setting, the panel also recommends modified FOLFOX, the use of which is supported by 2 case reports of patients with advanced FLC experiencing SD.132,134

The use of 5-fluorouracil (or capecitabine) + interferon alfa-2b in FLC has been intermittently studied. A phase II trial of fluorouracil (5-FU) and recombinant interferon alfa-2b (rIFNalpha2b) published in 2003, which included 9 patients with FLC, reported 1 CR and 4 PRs in 8 evaluable patients.140 Since that time, many modifications to this regimen have been tried in patients with FLC, including substituting the oral fluoropyrimidine capecitabine for 5-FU and substituting peginterferon alfa-2a (Pegasys) for recombinant interferon alfa-2b, which has limited availability.7 More recent work focuses on the combination of 5-FU or capecitabine + IFN + nivolumab, with conclusions limited given reliance on progression free survival in a heterogenous cohort, many of whom had no imageable disease.141 5-FU + IFN + nivolumab is also currently being evaluated in an ongoing phase I/II study for patients with unresectable FLC (NCT04380545), with an early evaluation reporting 2 responses.142

Beyond ipilimumab + nivolumab and 5-FU + IFN + nivolumab, there are also reports supporting the use of alternative regimens containing immune checkpoint inhibitors. One combination for which clinical benefit has been reported is atezolizumab + bevacizumab; 2 patients have been reported to experience clinical benefit, 1 as a bridge to surgery.99,143 However, in another case report of 2 patients treated with the same combination, neither patient experienced clinical benefit.9 Pembrolizumab was reported to induce SD as a single agent in 1 patient with FLC and a PR followed by a definitive CR when combined with radiotherapy in a second patient.144 Single-agent pembrolizumab was previously studied in stage I of a phase II trial for pediatric patients with conventional HCC and FLC; the trial is now being amended to study dual checkpoint inhibition with ipilimumab + nivolumab (NCT04134559). A retrospective case series of immune checkpoint inhibition for FLC included 4 patients treated with pembrolizumab monotherapy and 9 patients treated with nivolumab monotherapy, among other ICI regimens; while 3/19 patients treated with any ICI regimen experienced PRs, it is unclear which regimens the responders received.34 Use of nivolumab + lenvatinib has also been reported, with several responses reported in a retrospective case series of 20 patients.145

Additional chemotherapy regimens weakly supported by the panel in the relapsed/refractory setting include cisplatin + doxorubicin (PLADO) and PLADO + sorafenib. Pediatric oncologists are more likely to have experience with PLADO, which has traditionally been used to treat hepatoblastoma and conventional HCC in the pediatric population. Use of PLADO in patients with FLC, as well as PLADO + sorafenib, has been reported.90,110,146 Multiple reports describe patients with FLC benefiting from PLADO, including a case series of 6 patients with advanced FLC, 2 of whom achieved PR and 3 of whom experienced SD.134 Other reports show SD or a mixed response, including in the neoadjuvant setting.90,110 In the case series documenting the 6 patients treated with PLADO, 8 additional patients with advanced FLC were treated with PLADO + sorafenib; 1 achieved a PR and 5 experienced SD.134 PLADO and PLADO + sorafenib were both evaluated in the recently concluded Children’s Oncology Group trial for newly diagnosed patients with liver carcinoma, which included enrollment of patients with FLC (NCT03533582). PIAF (cisplatin, interferon, doxorubicin, and 5-FU) has also demonstrated clinical benefit in patients with FLC; a retrospective series including 10 patients treated with PIAF reported 1 patient with a mixed response, 6 patients with SD, and 1 patient with progressive disease; 2 additional patients were treated in the adjuvant setting.7

While additional regimens have been published in case reports, none were recommended by the panel. Among these were capecitabine monotherapy (1 PR, 2 SD) and doxorubicin monotherapy (1 SD).147 FLC patients have also been treated with nivolumab + lenvatinib + quercetin, with responses and stable disease reported, although the number of patients treated was not clearly reported.148 In a case series, 25 patients with advanced FLC were also treated with gemcitabine + lenvatinib + nivolumab, for which 1 CR, 6 PRs, and 10 experiences of SD were reported.149 There was also a single report of the use of pazopanib + vorinostat in a patient with FLC in the context of a phase I trial, although the results were not reported.7 Several reports reference the use of sorafenib monotherapy, with some patients experiencing clinical benefit.7,150

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with cancer face particular challenges. Biologically, AYA cancer may have some fundamental differences from cancer occurring in younger children and older adults, and AYA patients with cancer have not seen the same improvements in overall survival as patients in other age groups.151,152 AYA patients can face obstacles to accessing care; in the United States, young adults are more likely to be uninsured than patients of any other age group, and cancer treatment can pose a particular financial burden at this stage of life.152 Fewer clinical trials enroll this age group, and AYAs who are older, uninsured, and not being treated at a pediatric center are particularly unlikely to be enrolled in a clinical trial.152 Cancer diagnosis and treatment can cause significant social and developmental concerns for AYA patients, including interrupted socialization, delays in high school graduation and higher education, interruptions in employment, barriers to financial independence, and difficulty in obtaining adequate health insurance.152 AYA patients with cancer also face difficulty in developing and maintaining relationships with significant others and problems with sexual function, intimate relationships, fertility, and parenthood.152 AYA patients often face particularly difficult decisions surrounding fertility preservation and may require nuanced support to make such decisions.153 Diagnosis of FLC during pregnancy has also been reported, which can entail a host of additional management dilemmas.154,155 In addition to impaired fertility, survivors of AYA cancers are at risk for chronic conditions, secondary cancers, poor psychosocial outcomes, and financial toxicity, which long-term follow-up care could help alleviate.156

CLINICAL TRIALS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

While there remain several unanswered questions regarding disease pathophysiology and biology, research performed over the last 2 decades has informed the ongoing study of several novel therapies and anticipated therapies to come. While historically therapy has relied upon the use of standard chemotherapeutics and generalized immunotherapies, recent clinical trials have been designed to exploit the specificity of the DNAJ–PKAc fusion protein as a neoantigen to induce immune responses against FLC tumors. Two trials of peptide vaccines targeting DNAJ–PKAc are currently enrolling. In the United States, a phase I/II trial (NCT04248569) combines a peptide vaccine against DNAJ–PKAc with dual checkpoint inhibition for patients with unresectable or metastatic FLC. Sixteen patients were enrolled in the first phase of the study, of whom 4 discontinued therapy before the first post-treatment evaluation, most often for early clinical progression. (47; personal communication, Dr Mark Yarchoan). Among the 12 evaluable patients who received at least one dose of study treatment and underwent the 10-week post-treatment scan, DNAJ–PKAc-specific T-cell responses were detected in 9 of 12 patients. Three patients achieved partial responses (PRs) and demonstrated no evidence of active disease following surgical debulking of residual lesions, and 6 further patients experienced stable disease (47; personal communication, Dr Mark Yarchoan). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients who experienced clinical response were analyzed, and T-cell receptors (TCRs) with specificity for DNAJ–PKAc were identified and characterized functionally, with the potential goal to develop off-the-shelf TCR–T cell therapies for FLC.157 In addition, the FusionVAC22_01 trial in Tubingen, Germany, combines a DNAJ–PKAc peptide vaccine with the PD-L1 inhibitor atezolizumab (NCT05937295) in patients with locally advanced or metastatic FLC.42 Before the trial’s initiation, this vaccine produced long-lasting T-cell responses and prolonged disease-free survival in a patient with FLC.41 A second Tubingen trial of the DNAJ–PKAc peptide vaccine in the adjuvant setting is planned to open soon (NCT06789198).

Recent research into biologic vulnerabilities of FLC has informed additional work intended to target DNAJ–PKAc-driven alterations in metabolism and apoptosis. The DNAJ–PKAc fusion is believed to drive metabolic rewiring in FLC, resulting in glutamine dependence, which generates a nutrient-depleted tumor immune microenvironment (TiME) enriched for immunosuppressive metabolites such as ammonia.37,158,159 This environment is postulated to inhibit successful antitumor immunity.159 A currently recruiting phase Ib/II trial of the glutamine antagonist DRP-104 combined with durvalumab in patients with advanced FLC who have progressed on prior anti-PD-(L)1 therapy (NCT06027086) aims to test the hypothesis that glutamine antagonism can reverse resistance to immune checkpoint inhibition by modulating the TiME.159 In addition, an unbiased, high-throughput small-molecule drug screen in FLC PDX models identified the combination of Bcl-xL and TOPO1 inhibition as a potentially effective strategy for FLC. The combination of the Bcl-xL proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) DT2216 and irinotecan demonstrated synergy in vitro in cells derived directly from patients with FLC and in FLC PDX models.160 The PROTAC DT2216 was developed to mitigate the risk of severe thrombocytopenia seen with the use of small-molecule Bcl-xL inhibitors attributed to Bcl-xL expression in platelets.161 A phase I/II trial of DT2216 combined with irinotecan recently began recruiting through the Children’s Oncology Group early phase clinical trial network (NCT06620302); while the phase I is disease-agnostic in its recruitment, the phase II is FLC-specific based upon the aforementioned preclinical data. In the theme of TOPO1 inhibition, unrelated preclinical work has shown FLC PDX and orthotopic models to be sensitive to PLK1 inhibition, with the combination of PLK1 inhibitors and irinotecan achieving substantial growth suppression in an orthotopic model, suggesting a potential future therapeutic strategy.162

Trials that have been recruiting for several years include the noted phase I/II trial of nivolumab, fluorouracil, and interferon alfa-2b (NCT04380545), building off a fluorouracil and interferon alfa-2b backbone previously studied. Early results include 2 patients with responses enabling resection of the disease.140,142 A phase II trial of pembrolizumab in patients with pediatric liver cancer, soon to be amended to study dual checkpoint inhibition, is likewise accruing (NCT04134559). A disease-agnostic phase I/II trial of the anti-TIM3 antibody cobolimab combined with the anti-PD-1 antibody dostarlimab in pediatric and young adult patients with relapsed/refractory cancer is also currently recruiting and allows enrollment of patients with FLC (NCT06521567).

There are clear future opportunities to advance care for FLC by alternatively targeting the unique biologic pathways driven by the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion. Early efforts to develop selective PKAc inhibitors led to the development of highly selective molecules. However, efficacy studies in mouse models demonstrated a limited therapeutic window for DNAJ–PKAc inhibition with reversible ATP-competitive inhibitors.45,163 This limitation highlights the essential role of PKA activity in normal biology as opposed to its failed impact in FLC. Loss and gain of function genetic studies confirm that PKA activity can drive an FLC phenotype and is necessary for FLC tumor maintenance, and a recent pre-print provides preclinical proof of concept for the use of a PROTAC to degrade DNAJ–PKAc.15,40,164 These studies provide clear impetus to further develop selective inhibitors and degraders of the PKA fusion. Given the challenges of targeting DNAJ–PKAc directly, attention has focused on the potential interruption of downstream effects. Screening efforts in FLC models and engineered FLC systems have highlighted mRNA translation, metabolism, and apoptosis as key mediators of PKA-driven oncogenesis, with some phenotypes broadly shared across PKA-driven malignancies and others potentially unique to FLC and/or the DNAJ–PKAc fusion.17,18,31,37,38,48,158,165

The care of patients with FLC has dramatically improved over the last decade due to growing provider awareness and collaboration, the recognition that diagnosis is aided by biology, enhanced patient advocacy, and the advent of novel immunotherapeutic strategies. Significant limitations in the data, however, leave expert opinion as a key driver of clinical care. Even as research evolves and therapeutic innovation improves care, there remains a strong impetus to improve the quality of published data so that care decisions can rest on a firmer foundation. We conclude that several fundamental questions remain: (1) the optimal chemotherapy regimen, the role for tyrosine kinase inhibition, and whether combination therapy should include immunotherapeutics; (2) the correct sequencing of chemotherapy and immunotherapy; and (3) the correct regimen and duration of adjuvant therapy. There is general agreement in the treating community that surgery remains a cornerstone of treatment. Particularly essential to addressing these questions will be the use of historical or contemporary data cohorts so that the added value of each component of a treatment combination can be more readily assessed. As these questions are addressed, the role of existing blood/plasma-based biomarkers or the development of predictive biomarkers that can guide decision-making surrounding therapeutic response, surgical readiness, and remission will be crucial. With an active patient advocacy community, the conduct of prospective clinical trials, and improved access to de-identified data through clinical informatics resources, we are hopeful that in the years to come, we will not only generate new therapies for patients with FLC but also meaningfully refine the application of therapies already in use for patients with FLC (Figure 3).
