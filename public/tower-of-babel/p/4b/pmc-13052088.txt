Aortic Valve Perforation in Pediatric Granulomatosis With Polyangiitis: A Case Report
Authors: Libor Svoboda, Martin Scholten, Corinna Jugel, Johanna Buschner, Christian Schürer, Alexandra Kiess
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGranulomatosis with polyangiitis, formerly Wegener's granulomatosis, is a rare antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)–associated vasculitis primarily affecting the respiratory tract and kidneys. Cardiovascular involvement, particularly valvular disease, is rare, especially in pediatric patients. We report a case of a 12‐year‐old boy newly diagnosed with granulomatosis with polyangiitis, who developed an aortic valve perforation. The patient presented with systemic inflammatory symptoms and was found to have aortic regurgitation secondary to aortic valve perforation. Laboratory findings confirmed markedly elevated c‐ANCA titers. Imaging revealed pulmonary involvement, and echocardiography confirmed a perforation in the noncoronary cusp of the aortic valve. Immunosuppressive therapy led to clinical improvement and stabilization of cardiac function, without the need for immediate surgical intervention. This case highlights the importance of considering vasculitis in pediatric patients with systemic inflammation and unexplained cardiac murmurs. Early recognition and prompt immunosuppressive treatment can prevent severe complications.Keywords: aortic valve perforation, cardiac involvement, case report, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, pediatric

Introduction

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) is a necrotizing vasculitis primarily affecting medium and small blood vessels, with highly variable clinical presentations regarding organ involvement and disease severity. The upper and lower respiratory tracts, along with the kidneys, are the most frequently and severely affected organs [1]. It is rare in the pediatric population, with an incidence rate of approximately 1.4–6.39 per million children per year [2, 3]. Cardiovascular involvement in GPA in children is reported in about 5% of cases [1]. We report on a pediatric patient with significant cardiovascular involvement, emphasizing the importance of early cardiac diagnosis and treatment to prevent severe complications.

Patient Information

A previously healthy 12‐year‐old Caucasian boy, weighing 52 kg, presented with a 4‐week history of recurrent joint and limb pain primarily localized on the soles of both feet, knees, and shoulders. He also suffered from progressively worsening fatigue and weakness. Furthermore, pallor was noted by the parents. The patient did not report any fever or significant respiratory symptoms, nor any recent episodes of nausea, vomiting, dizziness, or urinary symptoms. Vegetative symptoms were largely unremarkable, with a single episode of diarrhea reported 2 days prior to admission. There was no history of acute illness within his close contacts.

Clinical Findings

On physical examination, the patient appeared pale with noticeable fatigue. Cardiac auscultation revealed a Grade 3/6 diastolic murmur in the right second intercostal space. A dermatological examination identified older petechial lesions on the left foot sole (see Figure 1). Despite complaints of arthralgia, no swelling, inflammation, or deformity was revealed on examination. The remaining organ systems and neurological assessment were unremarkable.

The remaining biochemical and coagulation assessments were unremarkable, including normal renal markers, cardiac enzymes, and the absence of eosinophilia. Urinalysis showed mild proteinuria and microhematuria. Furthermore, elevated calprotectin levels and a positive Hemoccult test were noted in the stool. Extensive microbiological and serological evaluations revealed no evidence of viral, bacterial, or fungal infections.

Imaging and Diagnostics

An echocardiographic evaluation revealed an eccentric moderate aortic regurgitation of the tricuspid, morphologically normal aortic valve. The left ventricle was of normal size and function. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the regurgitation and showed a perforation measuring 3.5 × 6 mm (see Figure 2) in the noncoronary cusp. The heart showed no further pathologies such as endocarditis, myocarditis, pericardial effusion, or left ventricular stress. No significant mitral insufficiency was present. In the ECG, no abnormalities, particularly no repolarization disturbances, were detected.

Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrating the perforation (arrow) of the noncoronary cusp of the aortic valve in B‐mode imaging (left panel). The right panel shows the color Doppler imaging, illustrating the regurgitation jet.

Chest X‐ray revealed two pulmonary dense band‐like opacities. The consecutive MRI of the chest confirmed infiltrate‐suspicious changes in Segment 3 of the right lung, corresponding to the X‐ray findings. A hypoplastic right maxillary sinus with mucosal swelling and partial mucosal swelling in the ethmoidal cells was noted. The MRI of the brain showed no pathologies. Abdominal ultrasound showed no significant abnormalities except for mild splenomegaly. The ENT examination revealed no nasal or cartilaginous involvement, but a slight conductive hearing loss on the left side was observed.

Diagnosis

Alternative diagnoses, including infective endocarditis and other autoimmune or congenital conditions, were excluded based on the clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and imaging results. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with GPA according to the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria [4], with a total score of 9 (a score of ≥ 5 is required). Due to the absence of accessible lesions, no biopsy was performed to confirm the diagnosis.

Treatment and Management

Following the diagnosis of GPA in accordance with classification criteria and acute heart valve destruction, immunosuppressive therapy was promptly initiated with an intravenous methylprednisolone pulse over 3 days (1000 mg/day), followed by oral prednisolone and rituximab (375 mg/m2). Anemia was corrected with an erythrocyte concentrate transfusion, followed by iron supplementation. At discharge, maintenance therapy included oral azathioprine (2 mg/day) as an immunosuppressant and candesartan (4 mg/day) to reduce afterload and mitigate the effects of aortic regurgitation. Close echocardiographic and blood pressure monitoring were implemented to minimize the risk of cardiac decompensation.

Despite significant cardiac involvement, the patient's overall condition improved with immunosuppressive therapy and afterload reduction. Clinical parameters and echocardiographic function including LV size and function remained stable. Surgical intervention was not necessary in the inflammatory stage, although aortic valve replacement may be indicated in the future due to the persistent aortic regurgitation.

Discussion

Cardiac involvement in GPA is rare but can manifest as pericarditis, myocarditis, coronary artery disease, or valvular damage [5]. Aortic valve perforation is exceedingly uncommon in pediatric cases. Early recognition and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy are crucial to prevent permanent organ damage. The use of corticosteroids in combination with rituximab has proven effective in managing severe GPA cases, reducing disease activity, and improving long‐term outcomes. However, close monitoring for adverse effects, particularly infections and immunosuppression‐related complications, is necessary.

Conclusion

This case highlights the necessity of including GPA in the differential diagnosis for pediatric patients who present with systemic inflammatory symptoms and unexplained cardiac murmurs. Diastolic murmurs are inherently abnormal and require prompt diagnostic evaluation. Timely diagnosis and treatment can avert severe complications and improve patient outcomes.
