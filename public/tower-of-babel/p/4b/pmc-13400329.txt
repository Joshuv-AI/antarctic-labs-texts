Functional enhancement of traditional taralli through lentil hulls and tritordeum flour integration: nutritional, metabolomic, and sensory insights
Authors: Giusy Rita Caponio, Federica De Bellis, Marica Troilo, Anna Luparelli, Leonardo Caputo, Michele Faccia, Graziana Difonzo, Linda Monaci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using alternative ingredients and agro-industrial by-products is an effective strategy to improve the nutritional quality of traditional baked goods. This study evaluated the effects of lentil hulls and Tritordeum flour on chemical composition, metabolic profile, and sensory quality of taralli. Products were formulated using different flour blends, with or without functional ingredients. Addition lentil hulls significantly increased fiber and ash contents, while metabolomic analysis showed up to a 550-fold increase in major phenolic compounds compared to control samples. Tritordeum flour contributed higher levels of betaine and aldehydes, enhancing the volatile profile. Enriched taralli displayed a more complex aromatic profile, with increased aldehydes, ketones, pyrazines, and furans, and showed a significant reduction in predicted glycemic index. Sensory analysis confirmed good overall acceptability, enhanced aroma, greater color intensity, and reduced hardness. These results highlight lentil hulls and Tritordeum flour as promising ingredients for functional taralli with improved nutritional and sensory properties.

Introduction

Taralli are a traditional baked product typical of the Apulia region (Southern Italy) and are widely recognized as a typical local specialty. With a history dating back several centuries, it is one of the most popular snacks in Southern Italy, mainly due to their simple production process and technological versatility. Taralli are ring-shaped products with a texture comparable to that of breadsticks, typically characterized by a diameter ranging from 2 to 3 cm to 9–10 cm, and a thickness between 7 and 8 mm and 1.5 cm and are suitable for consumption at any time of day. In Italy, the production of taralli is not defined by a dedicated product standard; rather, it falls within the broader regulatory framework established for bakery products (Presidential Decree of 20 October 1998No. 502, 1998), which sets general requirements for composition, processing, and labeling. This framework ensures food safety and product quality while allowing flexibility in traditional artisanal practices. The basic ingredients include flour, water, vegetable oil or other fats, salt and yeast. Depending on the variety, they may also be enriched with wine, fennel seeds, aromatic herbs, or spices. The industrial production process involves the use of a specific machine called a “tarallatrice” and follows a unique process that might include a boiling phase before baking. This step is essential to prevent the product from rising and to give it its characteristic crunchiness (Pagani et al., 2007). In contrast, traditional Italian artisans continue to produce taralli manually, preserving artisanal techniques that are gradually adapted in industrial settings. Traditional taralli are continuously evolving, offering opportunities to develop products with enhanced nutritional profiles to meet the growing demand for healthier and nutrient-rich foods that contribute to the promotion of human health. Research has shown that cereal-based products, such as pasta and baked goods, could represent an effective and cost-efficient vehicle for delivering phytochemicals, or health promoting compounds thereby enhancing the nutritional quality of the diet (Di Cairano et al., 2022). These products contain fats that contribute to texture, flavor, crumbliness and crunchiness (Caponio et al., 2013).

Various types of vegetables oils can be added to the dough, including corn, palm, soybean, sunflower and, more commonly, olive oil (Durante et al., 2019). Furthermore, fat influences the oxidative processes of food, directly affecting its preservation. The type of oil used plays a key role in the formation and release of volatile aromas during the storage of taralli (Caponio et al., 2011).

On the other hand, in recent years, the food industry has increasingly focused on sustainability and waste reduction, with one of the most effective strategies being the valorization of food by-products and processing waste. Lentils (Lens culinaris) are known to be a rich source of protein, containing both essential and non-essential amino acids, as well as having a high total polyphenols content (Li et al., 2024). Legumes are typically processed to remove their outer seed coats, or hulls, in order to improve texture and digestibility (Nartea et al., 2023). The by-products generated during this process include lentil hulls, which can be further processed into flour containing starch and exhibiting particularly high protein content (Dhull et al., 2023). This high-fiber ingredient can be incorporated into cereal-based products, enhancing their nutritional profile. Currently, this is primarily intended for animal feeding. In the case of lentils, the by-product resulting from its processing accounts for about 10–21% of the total (Luzardo-Ocampo et al., 2020) and could be further valorized by being transformed into a high-protein product. Although lentil hulls have limited use, they could represent a source of added value from a health perspective. As highlighted by Kanatt et al. (2011), extracts from lentil hulls have a high phenolic content, strong reducing power, and effective free radicals scavenging activity, even at low concentrations. Additionally, it has been shown that lentil hulls are rich in phenols (Oomah et al., 2011), dietary fibers (Tosh & Yada, 2010) and exhibit efficient antimicrobial activity (Kanatt et al., 2011). The consumption of lentil-based products offers a healthier alternative compared to snacks rich in carbohydrates and low in proteins and phytochemicals.

This innovation is especially promising for individuals suffering from obesity, coronary heart diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, and diabetes (Saeedi et al., 2019).

Studies on the glycemic index (GI) have shown that adding lentil flour to various food products significantly lowers the GI compared to control products (Turco et al., 2019).

Furthermore, it has been shown that lentil hulls are particularly rich in calcium (approximately 70%), iron (approximately 50%), dietary fibers, phenols, antioxidants and minerals. These findings highlight the importance of consuming and incorporating lentil hulls into food formulations (Rudra et al., 2023; Tiwari & Singh, 2012). For these reasons, lentil hulls were selected as an ingredient for the development of functional taralli, aiming to enhance their nutritional profile by increasing fiber, protein, and bioactive compounds.

In addition, Tritordeum was selected as a second enriching ingredient due to its characteristics: it is an hexaploid hybrid obtained by crossbreeding of a species of wild South American barley (Hordeum chilense) with durum wheat (Triticum turgidum subsp. durum), combining the excellent traits of Hordeum, such as high carotenoid content in the endosperm and enhanced tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses, with the favorable technological qualities of wheat (Martin et al., 2000). Tritordeum is rich in fiber and natural antioxidants, such as lutein, a carotenoid which protects the eyes and skin (Caponio et al., 2022). Additionally, it contains phenolic compounds with strong potential to protect the colon from cancer development (De Caro et al., 2024). Since Tritordeum is rich in fructans, it could be expected to exacerbate Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) symptoms in affected individuals.

However, according to a recent study, significant reductions in gastrointestinal symptoms were recorded in patients with IBS-Diarrhea (IBS-D) who were fed a 12-week diet based on Tritordeum (Russo et al., 2022). This effect is probably due to the overall improvement in gastrointestinal barrier function, as evidenced by decreased Intestinal Permeability (IP) and changes in the gut that restore barrier integrity, alongside reduced mucosal inflammation and fermentative dysbiosis (Hanning et al., 2021). Despite the nutritional value of lentil hulls and growing interest in Tritordeum (Bautista-Expósito et al., 2022; Nartea et al., 2023; Papadopoulos et al., 2024) literature on the use of lentil hulls in traditional baked goods and Tritordeum as a functional ingredient in dry snacks is limited. Moreover, few studies have examined the combination of Tritordeum and legume by-products in a single formulation (Šmídová & Rysová, 2022).

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and optimize an innovative taralli formulation by combining wheat flour, Tritordeum and increasing percentages of lentil hull flour. Specifically, the work aimed to: (i) valorize a by-product rich in bioactive compounds, such as lentil hulls; (ii) assess the technological, antioxidant and sensory properties of the resulting products; and (iii) characterize the metabolite composition and assess the nutritional profile of the innovative taralli.

Materials and methods

Taralli preparation

Taralli preparation was performed in a pilot plant belonging to the Department of Soil, Plant and Food Sciences of the University of Bari (Italy). Taralli were produced following a traditional Apulian recipe and prepared in five different variants. The recipe used was as follows: 100 g of flour, consisting of wheat flour, Tritordeum flour, and lentil hulls powder combined in different proportions (Table S1), 37.3 g of white wine (Rubestro, 11%, v/v, Ruvo di Puglia, BA), 25.2 g of high oleic sunflower oil (Selex) and 2 g of iodized salt, yielding approximately 15 taralli per batch.

To obtain different types of taralli, wheat flour (type 0) was mixed with Tritordeum flour (Intini & C.S.A.S. Putignano, BA) and lentil hulls cv. Crimson (Terre di Altamura S.r.l.), finely ground into a powder of 500 μm, at different percentages. Five different formulations were developed by varying the proportions of wheat flour (type 0), Tritordeum flour and lentil hulls powder: a) S-Ctr: 100% wheat flour (type 0); b) S-Tri: 80% wheat flour (type 0) and 20% Tritordeum flour; c) S-Len: 80% wheat flour (type 0) and 20% lentil hulls; d) S-TriLen: 60% wheat flour (type 0), 20% Tritordeum flour and 20% lentil hulls; e) TriLen: 80% Tritordeum flour and 20% lentil hulls.

S-Ctr represents the control sample made entirely with wheat flour, while S-Tri, S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen include increasing contributions of Tritordeum flour and lentil hulls, either alone or in combination, to enrich the nutritional profile of the taralli. The dough was kneaded until smooth and homogeneous, then covered with plastic film allowed to rest for about 15 min. The dough was placed on a work surface and shaped into cylinders about 11 cm length, which were then cut transversely into pieces weighing about 10 g each. The dough pieces were then rolled into cords on a wooden board until reaching a length of about 12 cm. Meanwhile, the fan oven was preheated to 200 °C. The taralli were placed on a baking tray lined with parchment paper and baked for 20 min, or until fully golden. After baking, all taralli samples were ground using the waring blender and vacuum-packed. Two production batches were prepared for each formulation, and the samples were stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

Proximate composition of raw materials and taralli formulations

The proximate composition of the raw materials used for taralli production was first determined, focusing on Tritordeum flour and lentil hulls powder each analyzed in triplicate. Subsequently, the same analyses were performed in triplicate on the five taralli formulations (S-Ctr, S-Tri, S-Len, S-TriLen, TriLen) to assess how the incorporation of these ingredients influenced the overall nutritional composition of the final products.

Protein content (calculated as total nitrogen multiplied by a factor of 6.25) and ash were determined according to the AOAC methods 979.09 and 923.03 respectively (AOAC, 2019). Total dietary fiber was measured using the enzymatic-gravimetric procedure described in the AOAC 985.29 method (AOAC, 2019). Lipid content was determined using the Soxhlet method with diethyl ether as the extraction solvent, according to AOAC 945.38F method (AOAC, 2019). Moisture content was determined at 105 °C using an automatic moisture analyzer (Radwag Wagi Elektroniczne, Radom, Poland). Approximately 1 g of sample was weighed, and the moisture content was obtained directly from the instrument.

In vitro starch hydrolysis of taralli

In vitro starch hydrolysis of taralli was determined according to Caponio et al. (2024) by simulating in vivo starch digestion. Briefly, 1 g of each sample was subjected to starch determination. Hydrolyzed starch was measured as the amount of glucose released, using the d-glucose assay kit (GOPOD format; Megazyme, Wicklow, Ireland). Starch was calculated as glucose (mg) × 0.9. Simulated digestions were dialyzed (membrane cut-off: 12.4 kDa) for 1 6 h. Aliquots of dialysate, containing free glucose and partially hydrolyzed starch, were sampled after 3 h and at the end of the digestion, after 16 h. These were further treated with amyloglucosidase. Free glucose was determined using the above enzyme-based kit and finally converted to hydrolyzed (digested) starch in taralli. The predicted glycemic index (pGI) was calculated using the equation pGI = 0.549 × HI + 39.71 as described by (Caponio et al., 2022).

UHPLC coupled to high-resolution mass spectrometry for metabolite profiling of lentil hulls powder and taralli extracts

The metabolic profile of lentil hulls and taralli samples was carried out using ultra high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS/MS). The acquired data were processed and interpreted using dedicated bioinformatics and commercial software, supported by curated databases, to enable the annotation and identification of low- and medium-molecular-weight metabolites.

Metabolite extraction procedure

The extraction of metabolites and bioactive compounds from taralli and lentil hulls was carried out through a multi-step protocol, adapted from a previously published method (Galgano et al., 2021). Samples from each formulation (S-Ctr, S-Tri, S-Len, S-TriLen, TriLen) and lentil hulls were subjected to the metabolite extraction protocol in duplicate. An aliquot of 300 mg of ground material was extracted with 10 mL of MeOH:H2O (70/30, v/v) + 0.1% HCl. Samples were incubated at 25 °C under agitation at 170 rpm for 7 h in an Orbital shaker (KS 4000 i-control shaker, IKA Works GmbH & Co. KG, Staufen, Germany), then centrifuged at 3000g for 10 min. And the supernatant was collected. The remaining pellet was re-extracted by adding an additional 10 mL of extraction buffer, followed by incubation at room temperature (25 °C), under agitation at 170 rpm for 15 h (overnight) in an orbital shaker. Finally, the samples were centrifuged again at 3000g for 10 min and the supernatant was collected and combined with that from the first extraction to obtain a pooled metabolite extract. The extracts were then diluted to reduce the methanol content below 60% to ensure compatibility with the ultrafiltration filters. They were then filtered through 1.2 μm regenerated cellulose (RC) syringe filters and subsequently purified by ultrafiltration using 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) filters. For ultrafiltration, 1 mL of diluted sample was applied to filters preconditioned with 4 mL of Milli-Q water and centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 1 h. The ≤10 kDa filtrate fraction was collected.

Prior to HPLC–high-resolution Orbitrap MS analysis, the sample was passed through 0.45 μm RC syringe filters.

UHPLC-HRMS analysis of taralli extracts and lentil hulls: Instrumentation and conditions

Analysis was performed using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to a Q-Exactive Plus quadrupole-Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer (Q-Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Metabolites separation (injection volume of 20 μL) was performed using an AcclaimTM 120, C18 column (3 mm, 100 Å, 1.0 mm × 15 cm) at a flow rate of 200 μL/min, according to a binary gradient consisting of solvent A (water +0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (methanol +0.1% formic acid). The gradient was programmed as follows: 5–30% B over 40 min, 30–50% B over 15 min, 50–90% B over 1 min, held at 90% B for 10 min; then returned to 5% B over 1 min and equilibrated for 18 min for column conditioning. During the entire chromatographic run, the column temperature was kept at 25 °C.

Mass spectrometry (MS and MS/MS) analyses were performed in both positive and negative ionization modes (HESI+, HESI−) using an untargeted approach. For each extraction replicate, two technical injection replicates were acquired. Data were acquired in Full-MS/dd-MS2 mode, with the instrumental parameters set as described below: Full-MS: microscan 1, resolution 70 k, AGC target 16, maximum injection time 30 ms, scan range 80–1200 m/z; count 10, isolation window 2.0 m/z, stepped collision energy 25, 35, 55; dd settings: minimum AGC target 5101, intensity threshold 8.3102, charge exclusion 4–8, >8, exclude isotopes on, dynamic exclusion 10s. Working in this mode, the instrument performs two different dependent scan events along the entire chromatographic run. The first event consists of a normal analysis conducted in Full Scan MS mode. In the second event, 10 peaks whose intensity exceeds a certain threshold value are subjected to fragmentation (MS/MS scan) by means of HCD (Higher Energy Collision Dissociation). The following HESI interface and ion optics parameters were set as follows: Sheath Gas 25 (a.u.), Auxiliary gas flow rate 15 (a.u.), Spray Voltage 3.4 kV, Capillary temperature 320 °C and S-Lens RF level 55.

Bioinformatics-mediated metabolite identification

Raw UHPLC-HRMS data were processed using the commercial software Compound Discoverer v.3.3. (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for metabolite identification. Compound Discoverer 3.3 is a software comprehensive data processing platform designed for small-molecule MS analyses, enabling both targeted and untargeted workflows. It provides customizable node-based processing sequences for chromatogram alignment, peak detection, compound grouping, and spectral library matching, and supports compound annotation through integrated libraries and database searches. In addition, it offers statistical analysis and visualization tools for comparative studies and pathway exploration, facilitating the identification of unknown metabolites and interpretation of complex datasets. The most important values for signal extraction were set as follows: mass of tolerance on the precursor and the maximum time shift set at 5 ppm and 0.2 min respectively. Moreover, only signals detected with intensity ≥300.000 and S/N greater than 3 were taken into consideration. The identification of the molecules was carried out using two different approaches both based on the use of databases and online platforms, namely ChemSpider and mzCloud. Compound identification using the ChemSpider database was performed by comparing the experimentally measured mass of the precursor ion of each unknown compound, accurately determined by the Orbitrap mass analyzer of the Q-Exactive spectrometer, with the corresponding theoretical mass.

In contrast, using the mzCloud platform, unknown metabolites were annotated by matching the fragmentation spectra acquired from UHPLC-HRMS/MS analyses with reference spectra in the mzCloud database. This approach enables more reliable attribution of unknown compounds.

The ChemSpider databases queried for compound identification were the following: Carotenoids Database, ChEBI, ChemBank, ChEMBI, BrugBank, FDA, FooDB, LipidsMAPS, Peptides, Phenol-Explorer, PubMed.

While the databases queried for mzCloud-based identification were: Endogenous Metabolites Database, natural products/medicines, natural toxins, herbicides/pesticides and small molecule chemicals. The identification of molecules was performed according to the criteria established by the “Metabolomics Standard Initiative” (Schymanski et al., 2014; Sumner et al., 2007). In particular, the compounds were identified according to two approaches in order to satisfy the standard criteria of “Level IIa” and “Level III”, that is, the identification of the molecules based on the comparison between the HRMS/MS fragmentation patterns of the unknown molecules with those deposited in the mzCloud database (level IIa) and putatively identified (level III) by using the accurate mass obtained, for each compound, thanks to the analyses on a high-resolution mass spectrometer based on Orbitrap technology (HRMS). Accordingly, compounds were assigned to Level IIa or Level III based on the applied identification approach and the available spectral and mass accuracy information.

The experimental and theoretical m/z comparison was conducted, in the latter case, using the ChemSpider database. For a more reliable identification, the results from ChemSpider and mzCloud were visually inspected by the operator and only compounds satisfying more stringent criteria defined internally by our research group were considered. In particular, the compound lists obtained from ChemSpider were refined by considering only those compounds that showed an experimental/theoretical mass shift ≤3 ppm along with an isotopic pattern in which the ionic precursor and the isotopes M + 1 and M + 2 were correctly recognized. In this second step (compound identification), a more stringent mass tolerance threshold of ≤3 ppm (experimental vs theoretical m/z error) was applied, compared to the 5 ppm tolerance used in the initial feature detection step. The fragmentation spectra of each molecule identified through mzCloud was also visually inspected.

In particular, only molecules showing a number ≥ 2 of correctly recognized most abundant fragments were considered. After identification each molecule was searched in the PubChem database (NCBI, 2026) in order to retrieve the class to which the compound belongs and/or to obtain any other useful information to clarify its function. Finally, the data matrix containing the peak area values provided by Compound Discoverer for all the identified compounds was used to conduct a survey aimed at identifying the compounds contributed by the addition of food waste in the lentil-enriched taralli.

Analysis of volatile compounds conducted on taralli by gas-chromatography interfaced with mass spectrometry

The volatile compounds of taralli were analyzed using an Agilent 6890 gas-chromatography (GC), coupled to an Agilent 5975 mass spectrometry (MS) (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Solid phase microextraction was performed in the headspace. Each sample was analyzed in replicate. The sample was placed in a 12 mL vial and added with 100 μL of 1-propanol solution as an internal standard and 4 mL of saturated aqueous NaCl solution. Subsequently, the individual vials were sealed with butyl rubber septa and aluminum caps. Before the extraction of volatile compounds, the samples were homogenized for 2 min using a vortex mixer. Volatile compounds were extracted by exposing 75 μm SPME fiber on carbox in/polydimethylsiloxane (CAR/PDMS) in the sample headspace at 40 °C for 50 min. The fiber was desorbed for 6 min in the injection port of the gas-chromatography, operating in split-less mode at 230° for 3.5 min. Separation of volatile compounds was performed on a HP-Innowax (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) polar capillarity column (60 m length x 0.25 mm ID x 0.5 μm film thickness) with specific conditions: inlet temperature 250 °C, flow rate 1.5 mL/min, pressure (helium) equal to 30 kPa.

The temperature was set at 35 °C for 5 min, then it increased by 5 °C which was maintained in isothermal condition for 5 min. The mass detector was set to certain conditions: interface temperature 230 °C, source temperature 230 °C, ionization energy 70 eV and scan range 33–260 amu. The internal standard used for peak standardization was 1-propanol.

Texture analysis

Texture parameters of taralli were determined using a three-point bending test, according to Caponio et al. (2025a). Briefly, a Texture Analyzer Z1.0 TN (Zwick Roell, Ulm, Germany), equipped with a support bars set 3 cm apart was used. The probe moved downward at a speed of 5 mm/min until the taralli fractured. The maximum force recorded at break, representing hardness (N/mm2), was considered. A total of 25 taralli (five per type, each measured in triplicate) were analyzed.

Sensory analysis of taralli

Sensory analysis was carried out by a semi-trained group of 9 panelists. The analysis was carried out according to the ethical guidelines and the protocol of the Laboratory of Food Science and Technology of the Department of Plant, Soil and Food Science of University of Bari, Italy. Panelists were informed about the study's objectives and provided written informed consent by agreeing with the statement: “I acknowledge that my responses will remain confidential and consent to participate in this survey.” Participation required an affirmative response. Additionally, panelists confirmed they had no food intolerances or allergies and were free to withdraw from the survey at any time without providing a reason (Caponio et al., 2025b). All tested products were confirmed to be safe for consumption.

A training session was held to familiarize panel members with the sensory vocabulary, establish anchor points for the scale, and define the descriptors and their intensity levels. Participants were given a 10-g sample of each taralli in white plastic containers, which were coded with the sample abbreviations.

Following each tasting, a 5-min pause was observed, during which the tasters were required to consume water to cleanse their mouths. The order of presentation of the samples was randomized across participants and replicates. This was done to minimize potential interference due to the order of administration and carry-over effects (Torrico et al., 2018). Eleven descriptors were selected for the analysis, covering various sensory attributes, and they were evaluated using a 10-point structured scale from 0 to 9. These included visual aspects such as color intensity (scale: 0 = white to 9 = dark brown), color homogeneity (scale: 0 = inhomogeneous to 9 = uniform), olfactory sensations such as odor intensity (scale: 0 = not perceived to 9 = very intense), and taste attributes such as sweet, salty, bitter, legume note and other attributes (scale: 0 = not perceived to 9 = very intense). Textural attributes during tasting were also evaluated, including hardness, dryness, and friability (scale: 0 = absent to 9 = high).

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All analysis were performed in triplicate. Significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's HSD (honestly significant difference) test for multiple comparisons. The statistical analysis was performed using Minitab 17 (Minitab Inc., State College, PA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (Boston, MA 02110) statistical software. The matrix bubble and the polar heatmap were performed using Origin 2021 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA).

Lentil hulls showed higher protein, ash, and fiber content, whereas Tritordeum flour was richer in moisture, lipids, and carbohydrates. Protein, lipid, ash, carbohydrates and dietary fiber were expressed on a dry matter basis (% of dry weight), while moisture was determined on a wet weight basis. Expressing results on a dry matter basis allows the elimination of variations due to water content and enables reliable comparison among samples with different moisture levels (as shown in Table 1). The moisture values, expressed as a percentage, of the two types of experimental flour analyzed, fall within a range between 9 and 12%. Therefore, the moisture values are below 15.5%, the limit imposed by Italian legislation. These moisture levels help ensure adequate product shelf life by minimizing the risk of mold growth and proliferation of spoilage and/or harmful microorganisms (Presidential Decree of 9 February 2001, No. 187, 2001).

Tiwari and Singh (2012) observed that incorporation of lentil hulls in baked products can significatively increase the water holding capacity. Differences in protein content were observed in the experimental flours analyzed; in particular, the lentil hull had significantly higher protein values (13.63 g/100 g) than the Tritordeum flour (12.38 g/100 g) (Table 1). The greater protein richness of the lentil hulls is related to the primary function of the hull as a protective barrier for the seed. Lipids were significantly higher in tritordeum flour (1.43 g/100 g) than in lentil hulls (0.95 g/100 g) (p < 0.05), although both matrices exhibited overall low-fat contents, consistent with their botanical origin.

A significant difference in the fiber content of flour can be observed; in particular, the lentil hull has a fiber value of 43.25 g/100 g, significantly higher than the values observed in the Tritordeum flour (2.55 g/100 g). This part of the seed is rich in dietary fiber, mainly insoluble fibers like cellulose, hemicellulose and the lignin, which constitute the rigid cellular structure of hull. In the other part, Tritordeum flour was obtained by grain grinding, which represents a hybrid between durum wheat and wild barley. During the milling process, most of the bran and germ (which contain most of the fiber in the grain) are removed to produce a finer, more consistent flour.

This process can help significantly reduce the fiber content in the final flour, leaving mainly the endosperm, which is rich in starches and poor in fiber. Lentil hulls had a significantly higher ash content than Tritordeum flour (3.15 g/100 g e 0.82 g/100 g respectively). This translates into a richness of minerals in the hull that can contribute positively to the nutritional contribution of enriched products.

Protein content ranged from 8.04 to 9.40 g/100 g, with the highest value observed in S-Ctr and the lowest in S-Tri (p < 0.05). The combined formulation (S-TriLen) showed protein levels comparable to the control, indicating a compensatory effect of ingredient blending.

Regarding fiber content, a significant increase was observed in samples S-Len, S-TriLen and TriLen, linked to the high fiber content of the lentil hulls (Table 1). Samples S-TriLen and TriLen, which consist of Tritordeum flour and lentil hulls, presented the highest ash content (Table 2). This further confirms the findings of the characterization of the experimental flours (Table 1). It should be noted that the inclusion of lentil hulls in taralli can increase not only the fiber content, but also the mineral content. At the same time, Tritordeum flour could contribute significantly to minerals.

UHPLC-HRMS/MS-based metabolite profiling of lentil hulls extracts

Polyphenols are a large class of compounds found in lentil hulls and represent a group of plant secondary metabolites that act as defense mechanisms against environmental conditions. The polyphenol profiles of cultivated lentil seed coats have been characterized using a variety of liquid chromatography – mass spectrometry (LC-MS) although the advent of last generation mass spectrometers integrated with advanced software for data mining has allowed to customize untargeted LC-MS workflow for a more comprehensive metabolite identification and in-depth analysis of lentil polyphenols (Cavalluzzi et al., 2022; Elessawy et al., 2021; Farag et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2022; Llorach et al., 2019; Purves et al., 2025; Zuchowski et al., 2014).

Results of lentil hulls analysis were carried out by UHPL-HRMS/MS in positive and negative ionization modes (HESI +, HESI−) followed by data treatment based on Compound Discoverer 3.3 software to process the whole raw data. Table S2 reports an overview of classes of metabolites identified in lentil hulls by LC-HR Orbitrap based MSMS analysis.

According to Table S2 compounds belonging to several classes were identified in lentil hulls extracts, including organic acids, flavonoids, polyphenols, amino acids, nucleotides, lipid and small peptides. Organic acids such as citric acid and ascorbic acid (vitamin C) have crucial role as natural antioxidants, helping to prevent lipid oxidation and improving the shelf life of food products (Mandal et al., 2010). The most representative classes of compounds detected in lentil hulls extracts and endowed with high antioxidant activity are polyphenols and flavonoids (Cavalluzzi et al., 2022). Flavonoids are among the most widely studied phytochemicals in food and include a large number of different molecules that can give rise to different biological activities (Liga et al., 2023). The most abundant compounds identified were catechin and its enantiomer, cyanidanol, which have relevant antioxidant properties (Amarowicz et al., 2010; Mustafa et al., 2022); tiliroside, which has renowned antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic and hepaprotective properties (Ramadan, 2025); and kaempferol, which has anti-carcinogenic, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity (Periferakis et al., 2022). Our findings are in agreement with what reported in a recent paper where a total of 85 polyphenols were identified by LC-UV and MSMS analyses including proanthocyanidins, flavanols, flavones, and hydroxybenzoic acids (Purves et al., 2025). Therefore, taralli enriched with lentil hulls could therefore benefit from an increase in antioxidant activity. This would improve consumers' health and contribute to the product's stability by preventing fat rancidity (Del Rio et al., 2010). Additionally, saponins, also detected in the extracts, are known to reduce cholesterol and also exhibit anti-inflammatory properties (Mustafa et al., 2022).

Different types of fatty acid were also detected in lentil hulls extracts, which plays an essential role in maintaining the integrity of cell membranes and act as precursor for the synthesis of other bioactive metabolites (D. Chen et al., 2025). Interesting is the presence of “corchorifatty acid F", a polyunsaturated fatty acid with antioxidant and antifungal properties (Pal et al., 2017). In accordance with our data, a previous work (Guo et al., 2022) confirmed the presence of free and bound polyphenols in lentil hulls using advanced MS-based analysis. The same study also explored the anti-inflammatory mechanisms of their digestive products by analyzing the NF-κB and Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathways in the HT-29 cell model (Guo et al., 2022). Moreover, it was also confirmed that the polyphenols in lentil hulls represent a promising source of anti-inflammatory ingredients. (Panaro et al., 2024) demonstrated that some of the compounds identified in the hull's extracts had proven anti-inflammatory and antispasmodic properties. The most abundant of these were niacinamide, apocynin, and p-coumaric acid. These findings suggest that consuming lentil hull extract could contribute to maintaining overall intestinal health.

As regards the sugar content, a variability in signal intensity within samples was displayed. The presence of these sugars could derive from the Maillard reactions that develop during the cooking phase of taralli, but also from hydrolyzation processes of starches and carbohydrates that occur during this phase leading to the production of simple sugars such as glucose (Tamanna & Mahmood, 2015). It was very interesting to observe, in the samples of taralli enriched with lentil hulls (samples S-Len, S-TriLen and TriLen), the clear increase in the content of molecules belonging to the class of polyphenols compared to the samples containing only wheat flour (S-Ctr) and a mixture of wheat flour and Tritordeum flour (S-Tri). As already described, polyphenols such as gallocatechin, catechin, quercetin and procyanidin C1 (Fig. 1), have well known antioxidant properties protecting against oxidative stress and cellular damage caused by free radicals.

Flavonoids are the most powerful natural antioxidants. Specifically, gallocatechin is a phenolic compound that displays different functions: i) act as a powerful antioxidant capable of neutralizing free radicals in the body, preventing cell damage (Bernatoniene & Kopustinskiene, 2018); ii) has anti-inflammatory properties, can help in reducing inflammation in the body, which is a common factor in many chronic diseases (Fan et al., 2017); iii) it is a lipid metabolism modulator, thus contributing in reducing the levels of LDL cholesterol (“bad” cholesterol) and improving the levels of HDL cholesterol (“good” cholesterol); iv) it is an antimicrobial that can help to inhibit the growth of bacteria and other microorganisms (Rovaldi et al., 2025). Catechin also has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial functions and can develop benefits for the cardiovascular system (Kim & Heo, 2022). Quercetin is a powerful antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antibacterial, cardiovascular support and may have anticarcinogenic effects (Azeem et al., 2023). Procyanidin C1 has been shown to have powerful antioxidant activity and a high reactive oxygen species scavenging capacity (L. Chen et al., 2018). Furthermore, recent research suggests that these phytochemicals may modulate immune function (Sung et al., 2018). The integration of these four polyphenols – gallocatechin, catechin, quercetin and procyanidin C1 – in taralli can transform this traditional baked product into a functional food with several health benefits for the consumer. Thanks to their powerful antioxidant proprieties, these polyphenols can extend the shelf-life of taralli, preventing fat oxidation and product spoilage. Antimicrobial proprieties can further help limit the growth of microorganisms, improving food safety. The inclusion of these polyphenols can add nutritional value to taralli, offering benefits ranging from improved cardiovascular health to reduced inflammation and immune system support.

In conclusion samples enriched with lentil hulls (S-Len and S-TriLen) exhibited higher peak areas of amino acids and polyphenols, indicating an enrichment in bioactive compounds, whereas tritordeum-based samples (S-Tri) were characterized by a relatively higher contribution of sugars and related metabolites. Overall, the control sample (S-Ctr) showed a less complex metabolite profile compared to the enriched formulations.

In vitro starch hydrolysis of taralli

Nowadays, there is a growing tendency to reduce the consumption of glycemic carbohydrates by increasing the levels of indigestible carbohydrates and slowly digestible starch to achieve a wide range of beneficial effects (Agama-Acevedo et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2024).

The hydrolysis index (HI) calculated from the rate of starch hydrolysis over time, and the respective pGI of the analyzed products are shown in Fig. 2. Sample S-Ctr (100% type 0 flour) achieved the highest values of HI and pGI (as shown in Fig. 2). It should be noted that the addition of lentil hulls and Tritordeum flour resulted in lower pGI and HI values. In particular, the taralli made with the addition of 20% Tritordeum flour (sample S-Tri) and lentil hulls (sample S-Len) showed a significant reduction in pGI compared to sample S-Ctr. This significant reduction was also recorded in formulations S-TriLen and TriLen. However, no significant differences were found between S-Tri, S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen samples. Lentils and other legumes are rich in bioactive compounds such as polyphenols, fatty acids, carotenoids and antioxidants (Amarowicz & Pegg, 2008; Perri et al., 2024), which can significantly inhibit α-glucosidase activity, providing to explain their low postprandial glycemic response (Ramdath et al., 2018). It has also been suggested that the low glycemic response of lentils is related to the presence of an intact cell wall structure, which limits starch gelatinization and enzymatic hydrolysis. Thus, cell wall disruption, particle size and changes in the different starch fractions (e.g. resistant starch) during processing further influence starch digestion (Brummer et al., 2015; Raigond et al., 2015). The pGI is a parameter used to classify foods according to their postprandial glycemic response (Kaur et al., 2020). Dietary fiber contents are recognized as one of the factors most implicated in the reduction of pGI. In particular, the speed of starch digestion significantly influences the release of glucose and its subsequent absorption during digestion (Graça et al., 2021).

Foods with a high pGI are quickly digested and adsorbed, resulting in a spike in blood glucose (Brennan, 2005). Scientific evidence confirms the positive effect of fiber-enriched foods on reducing glycemic index, as seen in studies conducted on low-carb pasta and bread (Difonzo et al., 2022). Indeed, in S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen samples added with lentil hulls, rich in fiber, a reduction in the pGI was recorded compared to sample S-Ctr. These results are in line with the existing literature that demonstrates a significant reduction in the glycemic index of food rich in fiber (Palaniappan et al., 2021; Reis & Abu-Ghannam, 2014). Therefore, a possible correlation between the addition of lentil hulls and the pGI was demonstrated. In addition to the higher fiber content, the lower pGI found in the supplemented lentil hulls samples could also be related to the higher levels of polyphenols and total flavonoids compared to the control sample (as highlighted in the paragraph 3.4 by metabolomic analyses).

This hypothesis is supported by studies that attribute a modulatory effect of polyphenols on the digestion and metabolism of carbohydrates, since polyphenols allow to delay the digestion of starch through the inhibition of digestive enzymes (for example, α-amylase), with consequent reduction of the glycemic index of food (Adedayo et al., 2018; Caponio et al., 2022).

From a nutritional perspective, reducing the glycemic index of food is a desirable factor not only for diabetics, but also for a wider population, since it helps prevent the incidence of chronic metabolic diseases (Augustin et al., 2015). Compared to other cereals, barley is particularly rich in β-D-glucans, known to lower blood cholesterol, increase satiety and reduce the postprandial glycemic response (AbuMweis et al., 2016; Wirkijowska et al., 2012). Since Tritordeum is a hybrid between wild barley and durum wheat, it may contain small amounts of β-glucans, although not in concentrations comparable to those of common barley. Thus, the observed reductions in pGI and HI in samples S-Tri, S-TriLen, and TriLen could instead be attributed to its overall fiber content and the grain structure.

For example, 2-Butanone, associated with pungent and sweet notes, was more abundant in samples containing lentil hulls (S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen), confirming previous observations by Khrisanapant et al. (2019) in legumes, where ketones have also been reported in significant amounts. These mechanisms can also be explained through the increase in pyrrole concentration, particularly in sample S-TriLen (26.23 μg/g), as well as the increase in alcohols, which are likely to considerably affect sensory perception (Karolkowski et al., 2021). Notably, 1-octen-3-ol, commonly found in legume-based products (Khrisanapant et al., 2019), was more abundant in samples S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen. This compound is associated with musty/earthy, floral, and sweet notes (Kaczmarska et al., 2018; Vurro et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2022).

In addition, a significant increase in aromatic heterocycles, including oxazoles and pyrroles, was observed in samples S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen, indicating a greater intensity of Maillard reactions during baking (Bolkvadze et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022). Pyrazines were absent in samples S-Ctr, whereas they were detected in concentrations of 111.5 μg/g, 371.5 μg/g, and 146.7 μg/g in samples S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen, respectively. These compounds —responsible for roasted and toasted notes typical of baked goods (Difonzo et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022) — are formed through Strecker degradation of α-amino acids and α-dicarbonyls compounds and are closely linked to thermal degradation (Fischer et al., 2022; Karolkowski et al., 2021; Sanmartín et al., 2024). The high production observed in samples with lentil hulls and Tritordeum flour suggests a more pronounced Maillard reaction, likely due to the greater availability of amino acid and carbohydrate precursors present in these matrices (Ali & Alabdullatif, 2025). In line with (Vurro et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2022), furans—contributing caramel-like and roasted notes—were also generated through Maillard reactions, enriching the aromatic complexity of the products.

Finally, an additional contribution in samples S-TriLen and TriLen derived from sulfur compounds (dimethyl disulfide, dimethyl trisulfide), known to impart pungent and sulfurous notes, closely related to the amino acid composition of legume flours (Chigwedere et al., 2022). At the same time, higher levels of carboxylic acids and related esters (hexanoic acid, octanoic acid ethyl ester, butanedioic acid diethyl ester) were observed, metabolites already reported as markers of fermentative and oxidative processes (Bleicher et al., 2022; Vurro et al., 2024b).

Overall, clustering analysis confirmed a clear separation between samples formulated exclusively with cereal flours (type 0 and Tritordeum), characterized by a simpler profile dominated by alcohols and aldehydes, and those containing lentil hulls, where the presence of polyphenols and the interaction between the protein fraction of legumes and the carbohydrate fraction of Tritordeum promoted the formation of typical Maillard and lipid degradation compounds, enriching the volatilome and conferring a complex aromatic profile.

The results showed a significant difference in hardness value, with the control sample (S-Ctr, 100% type 0 flour) that exhibited the highest value (47.57 N), whereas the progressive inclusion of Tritordeum flour and lentil hulls led to a significant decrease in hardness, up to 16.69 N in the TriLen sample (80% Tritordeum +20% lentil hulls).

This could be attributed both to a modification in the gluten network and to an increase in total dietary fiber. Tritordeum flour, although characterized by a higher protein and fiber content, is known to have lower gluten levels and to form a weaker gluten network than soft wheat, which interferes with gluten development and starch gelatinization (Papadopoulos et al., 2024; Suchowilska et al., 2021). Similarly, the addition of lentil hulls, rich in dietary fiber (Table 1), could interfere with the continuity of the gluten network, promoting the formation of a more porous structure, which requires less force to fracture the samples.

These results are in line with previous observations in legume-enriched baked goods, where the addition of alternative ingredients leads to greater friability and lower hardness due to reduced development of the gluten network. For example, Oskaybaş-Emlek et al. (2021), Yaver (2022) showed a reduction in hardness compared with the control sample, in cracker and biscuit containing carob flour and green lentil flour and raw and germinated lentil flour, respectively. In another study, (Düşkün et al., 2025) highlighted a clear decrease in hardness with increasing oleaster powder addition due to lower gluten content and the effect of dietary fibers, which led to a weaker and more friable structure.

Sensory test

Fig. 4 illustrates the comparative sensory profile of the taralli, highlighting significant differences in visual, olfactory, gustatory, and textural attributes. From a visive perspective, the addition of lentil hulls (samples S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen) led to a notable increase in color intensity, with values ranging from 7.11 to 7.89 (p ≤ 0.01), compared to S-Ctr (100% type 0 flour) and S-Tri (80% type 0 flour and 20% Tritordeum) samples, which exhibited values of 4.11 ± 0.19 and 3.89 ± 0.19, respectively. Color homogeneity was greater in products containing lentil hull powder, with the highest score observed in sample TriLen. Regarding odor intensity, although statistical differences were detected, absolute values did not clearly differentiate the samples. However, slightly higher values were found in samples formulated with type 0 flour and lentil hull (S-Len), suggesting an aroma modulation attributable to legume-derived volatile compounds, as also shown in Fig. 3.

Indeed, the addition of lentil hull introduced new sensory notes, particularly related to legume flavor, herbaceous, toasted, and astringency.

In samples S-Len and TriLen, the presence of lentil hull was perceived, which scored 5.56 ± 0.19 and 4.89 ± 0.20, respectively, in agreement with (Kotsiou et al., 2023), who reported legume notes in bread samples assessed by trained panelists. This perception may be related to high concentrations of 2-hexenal and other aldehydes. Bitterness and astringency observed in the same samples are likely associated with higher polyphenol content (Caponio et al., 2025b; Osakabe et al., 2024), confirming the relationship between lentil hull incorporation and these sensory attributes.

Other attributes may be linked to alcohols such as 1-octen-3-ol and ketones (2-butanone), which are known to significantly impact sensory characteristics. Likewise, the high content of pyrazines contributes to the typical roasted notes generated during Maillard reactions (Sanmartín et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022).

Tritordeum, a hybrid of durum wheat and barley, can present a mild sweet perception. This characteristic is supported by its chemical composition, which includes a higher content of total sugars compared to conventional grains, thus providing precursors for sweet taste perception (Shewry et al., 2023).

Texture analysis showed that the addition of lentil hulls directly influenced taralli hardness. Although variations were not statistically significant, samples formulated with lentil hulls alone or in combination with Tritordeum (S-Len, S-TriLen, and TriLen) exhibited slightly lower values, in agreement with instrumental data reported in Table 4. As highlighted by (Yaver, 2022), this effect may be due to reduced gluten network formation, resulting from increased alternative flour content and the formation of a weaker matrix. This structural effect makes products enriched with lentil hulls less rigid and more friable.

Finally, S-TriLen and TriLen showed a lower perception of dryness, highlighting the effect of the formulation on the structural attribute.

Conclusions

Using lentil hulls and Tritordeum flour to enrich taralli has proven to be an effective strategy for improving the nutritional properties and sensory quality of the product. Lentil hulls, which are often discarded during the husking process, are a valuable source of fiber, plant proteins and phenolic compounds. These contribute to intestinal health and provide antioxidant properties. Including them in taralli significantly increases the fiber and functional compound content. Additionally, the incorporation of Tritordeum flour and lentil hulls led to a notable increase in fiber content, that may contribute to a more favorable glycemic response. Advanced UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis confirmed the presence of high levels of phenolic compounds, which are known for their antioxidant properties and are retained in the enriched taralli. In addition to chemical analysis, a sensory evaluation was conducted to assess the impact of these ingredients on the product's organoleptic characteristics. The results revealed a more complex sensory profile for the enriched taralli, with herbaceous, toasted and slightly astringent notes. The taralli also had a more intense color, likely due to the browning reactions that occur during baking and the inherent characteristics of the added ingredients. In summary, using agro-industrial by-products such as lentil hulls and Tritordeum flour to improve the nutritional profile of taralli without compromising their sensory qualities has proven to be an innovative and effective strategy. This combination offers consumers a product that respects tradition, adding value in terms of essential nutrients and bioactive compounds.
