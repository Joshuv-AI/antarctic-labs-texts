Associations of children’s urinary metals with blood RNA N6-methyladenosine and 2′-O-methyladenosine
Authors: Carolyn Gigot, Ya Li, Haotian Wu, Guadalupe Estrada-Gutierrez, Mari Cruz Tolentino, Alejandra Cantoral, Julio Landero, Martha M Téllez-Rojo, Robert O Wright, Yinsheng Wang, Allison Kupsco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Post-transcriptional RNA modifications (the epitranscriptome) regulate gene expression and represent a pathway through which environmental exposures can affect health. Two RNA modifications important for health and disease are N6-methyladenosine (m6A) and 2′-O-methyladenosine (Am). However, the effects of metals and metalloids (hence, metals), on m6A and Am are less well understood. We aimed to investigate associations between children’s metals exposures and global m6A and Am in blood.Methods:We measured essential (cobalt, copper, selenium, zinc) and nonessential (arsenic, cadmium, chromium, cesium, mercury, antimony) metals in urine and total m6A and Am in blood using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry in 150 children aged 9–11 in the Mexico City Programming Research in Obesity, Growth, Environment and Social Stressors (PROGRESS) cohort. We determined cross-sectional associations of log2-transformed metals mixtures with m6A and Am using quantile g-computation and linear regression.Results:Median m6A (expressed as %m6A/A) was 0.075 (interquartile range [IQR] 0.069, 0.079); median Am (%Am/A) was 2.53 (IQR 2.44, 2.66). Each quartile increase in the overall metal mixture was associated with a decrease of −0.095 (95 % confidence interval [CI] −0.15, −0.044) in %Am/A, adjusted for other metals, sex, age, visit date, and total RNA concentration. Each doubling of arsenic was associated with a decrease of −0.047 (−0.089, −0.0056) and each doubling in cesium was associated with a decrease of −0.096 (−0.18, −0.015) in %Am/A.Conclusions:Exposure to nonessential metals may be related to global Am levels in children. Further research is warranted to evaluate potential roles of Am and m6A in metal-induced toxicity.

Introduction

Metals exposures can cause a range of neurological (Bjorklund et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022), cardiovascular (Tellez-Plaza et al., 2018), metabolic (Maull et al., 2012), renal (Orr and Bridges, 2017), and other health effects. Childhood can be a vulnerable period for environmental metals exposures, which may lead to disease onset in adulthood (Haugen et al., 2015; Nguyen et al., 2010). Nonessential metals (e.g., arsenic [As], cadmium [Cd], chromium [Cr], cesium [Cs], mercury [Hg], antimony [Sb]) are generally toxic at any exposure level, while essential metals (e.g., cobalt [Co], copper [Cu], selenium [Se], zinc [Zn]) are necessary for biological processes and can be harmful at levels that are either too low or too high. These associations are complicated by exposures to complex mixtures of metals, rather than individual metals. As such, the body’s response to various metals is multifaceted and may be regulated at several levels of biological organization. Oxidative stress and inflammation are key mechanisms of toxicity for several metals, including arsenic, cadmium, mercury, and copper (Cicero et al., 2017; de Melo et al., 2024; Maull et al., 2012). However, the downstream cellular pathways that coordinate the responses to toxicity are not fully understood.

An emerging mechanism that coordinates the cellular stress response is reversible, post-transcriptional chemical modifications of RNA. Collectively called the epitranscriptome, over 170 types of modifications on ribosomal RNA (rRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), messenger RNA (mRNA), and small RNA molecules have been described (Cerneckis et al., 2024). These modifications control RNA splicing, localization, translational efficiency, folding, and stability, and represent an additional level of regulation on top of gene expression. Recent research suggests the epitranscriptome may be a mechanism by which the environment can affect health and disease (Cayir et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023).

Among the most common and well-studied RNA modifications is N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the addition of a methyl group to the N6 position of adenine (Boulias and Greer, 2023). While m6A is abundant on mRNA, with an average of 5–6 modifications per transcript, it is also found on rRNA (comprising 80–90 % of cellular RNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), and several other regulatory RNAs (Liu et al., 2023a; Zaccara et al., 2019). This modification affects mRNA stability, transport, splicing, and translation, playing important roles in a wide range of biological processes (Boulias and Greer, 2023). Dysregulation of m6A has been linked with cancer initiation and progression (Sun et al., 2019), metabolic diseases (Matsumura et al., 2023), and neurodegenerative disorders (Yen and Chen, 2021). Research in rodents has also shown the importance of m6A during development in childhood and adolescence; m6A levels in the hypothalamus are associated with puberty onset (Zang et al., 2023). Environmental factors have been shown to affect m6A in vivo and in vitro (Cayir et al., 2019; Kupsco et al., 2020; Malovic et al., 2021). Oxidative stress in particular may induce m6A at specific mRNA sites and play a role in the physiological stress response (Anders et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2023). As such, exposures to a range of metals have been associated with m6A modifications or related gene expression in vitro or in vivo, including arsenic (Bai et al., 2018; Cayir et al., 2019), cadmium (Yue et al., 2022), chromium (Wang et al., 2022), and cobalt (Tang et al., 2020).

Another important RNA modification is 2′-O-methylation (Nm), the addition of a methyl group to the 2′-hydroxyl group of ribose. This modification is abundant on rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, and mRNA (Zhou et al., 2024), impacting their stability and fate, and dysregulation in Nm has been connected with cardiovascular disease, neurological problems, and cancer (Dimitrova et al., 2019). Modifications on each ribonucleoside (2′-O-methyladenosine [Am], 2′-O-methylcytidine (Cm), 2′-O-methylguanine [Gm], 2′-O-methyluridine [Um]) are approximately equally common (Wiener and Schwartz, 2021). However, Am is of particular interest because it can also be further modified to N6,2′-O-dimethyladenosine (m6Am), an abundant modification in mRNA linked to enhancement or inhibition of translation and metabolic health (Boulias and Greer, 2023). Less research has been conducted on associations between environmental exposures and Am. In mice, acute exposure to polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) has been associated with increased overall RNA Am abundance in the liver (Klinge et al., 2021) and chronic exposure to PCBs has been associated with decreased overall RNA Am and mRNA Am abundance in the liver (Piell et al., 2023).

Though emerging research suggests that metals may influence the epitranscriptome in experimental models, there has been little work conducted in human populations. We hypothesized that perturbations in m6A and Am may be mechanisms by which metals exposures affect health. In this pilot study, we aimed to evaluate the association of urinary metals mixtures with global blood RNA m6A and Am levels cross-sectionally among children 9–11 years old. Enhancing our understanding of the epitranscriptome in response to metals in human populations may lead to development of biomarkers for prevention or intervention or to future therapeutic targets for environmental health research.

Methods

Study design and population

We used data from the Programming Research in Obesity, Growth, Environment, and Social Stress (PROGRESS) prospective birth cohort study. The PROGRESS study enrolled pregnant women between 2007 and 2011 through the Mexican social security system (Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social), as previously described (Braun et al., 2014; Burris et al., 2013) (N = 948). Mothers and children were followed through 8–12 years after birth. The institutional review boards of participating institutions approved all study protocols (Instituto Nacional de Salud Publica de Mexico project #560, Icahn School of Medicine Human Subjects Management #12–00751, Instituto Nacional de Perinatologia project 2025–1-12, and Columbia University Human Research Protection Office protocol #AAAR0689).

Urinary metals and creatinine

At the 96-month visit (at which all children were at least 8 years old), urine specimens were collected in metal-free tubes and stored at −80 °C before shipment to the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai for metals measurements. There, metals were measured using an Agilent 8900 ICP triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (ICP-QQQ), with quality controls as previously described (Levin-Schwartz et al., 2021). Urinary creatinine was measured using Arbor Assays’ Urine Creatinine Detection Kit. Samples were diluted 1:100 with water and added to a 96-well plate with creatinine reagent, then analyzed using a SpectraMax Plus 385 plate reader (Molecular Devices). Limits of detection (LOD) are often censored in lab outputs or data analyses, but because values below LODs were available, we used these in further analyses instead of a constant (e.g., LOD/2) (Braun et al., 2014; Kupsco et al., 2019). To account for urine dilution, we divided urinary metals levels (μg/L) by urinary creatinine levels (g/L) to express urinary metals levels in terms of μg/g creatinine for further analyses.

Global epitranscriptomic measures

Blood specimens were collected in Tempus blood RNA tubes (ThermoFisher). Total RNA was extracted using magMAX mirVana total RNA isolation kits (ThermoFisher). RNA quantity was determined using a spectrophotometer (Implen). There were 151 samples from the 96-month visit with sufficient RNA for global m6A and Am analysis (one of these samples was excluded from our analyses because no urinary metals measurements were available, for a sample size of 150 participants). RNA samples were stored at −80 °C before shipment to University of California Riverside for total RNA modification analysis.

Global m6A and Am levels in total RNA were measured using LC-MS/MS with stable isotope-dilution technique, as described previously (Fu et al., 2015; Kupsco et al., 2020). While we also sought to measure global m6Am level, signal intensity and reproducibility were poor; hence, the results for m6Am are not reported. Levels of m6Am in total RNA are generally low. m6Am was not detectable in the majority of samples; in those samples where m6Am was detectible, its average level was approximately 2.5 % of the average level of m6A.

In brief, 100 ng of total RNA from each sample was digested with 1 unit of nuclease P1 (Sigma-Aldrich) in a 25-μL buffer containing 25 mM NaCl and 2.5 mM ZnCl2 at 37 °C for 2 h. To the resulting mixture was then added 1 M ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3) until its final concentration reached 100 mM. The mixture was incubated in the presence of 1 unit of quick calf intestinal phosphatase (New England Biolabs) at 37 °C for another 2 h, and the solution was subsequently dried using a SpeedVac. To 10 ng of the digested RNA were added 2.825 pmol [13C5]-labeled adenosine ([13C5]-rA) and 32.51 fmol [D3]-m6A as internal standards. The enzymes were removed from the digestion mixture by chloroform extraction, and approximately 25 % of the nucleoside mixture was injected for nano liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (nLC-MS/MS) analysis.

The nLC-MS/MS experiments were conducted on a TSQ-Altis triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer operated in the multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, where the mass spectrometer was coupled to a Dionex Ultimate 3000 HPLC module (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The LC setup included a PepMap Neo Trap-Cartridge C18 trapping column (0.3 × 5 mm, 5 μm in particle size, 100 Å in pore size, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a homemade analytical column (75 μm × 200 mm) packed with Zorbax SB-C18 stationary phase material (5 μm in particle size, 100 Å in pore size, Agilent). The LC separation was conducted by using 0.1 % formic acid in H2O (mobile phase A) and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B). For the measurements of m6A and Am, the samples were loaded onto the trapping column with mobile phase A at a flow rate of 2.5 μL/min in 8.5 min, and the nucleoside mixture was separated on the analytical column by using a 36.5-min gradient of 1–20 % B in 9.2 min, 20–60 % B in 9.2 min, 60–95 % B in 0.6 min, then, at 95 % B for 9 min, 95–1 % B in 0.5 min, and finally, at 1 % B for 8 min. The flow rate was 300 nL/min. The electrospray voltage was 2.0 kV, and the ion transfer tube temperature was set at 275 °C. Precursor and fragment ion selection widths were 0.7 and 0.4 m/z units, respectively, with a collision energy being set at 20 V. The MRM transitions used for the quantifications of rA, m6A, and Am are listed in Table S1.

The calibration curves for the quantifications of modified ribonucleosides (m6A and Am) were constructed by spiking different amounts of unlabeled standard nucleosides (rA, m6A and Am) and fixed amounts labeled standards, 2.825 pmol of [13C5]-rA and 32.51 fmol of [D3]-m6A. The mixtures were then subjected to LC-MS/MS analyses under the same conditions as described above for the samples. The results are reported as the percentage of m6A or Am relative to total adenosine (%m6A/A or %Am/A), calculated based on the calibration curve equations shown in Fig. S1. The mean relative standard deviation of results from replicate measurements of m6A and Am was 4 % and 9 %, respectively, and was less than 30 % for each sample.

Covariates

At baseline and at the 96-month visit, study staff interviewed mothers using validated questionnaires to obtain demographic, lifestyle, and environmental information. We estimated SES with the Mexican Association of Market and Public Opinion Research Agencies index, which uses 13 factors (e.g., head of household education level, number of appliances in household, car ownership) to classify households into 6 levels (Carrasco, 2002). We further grouped these levels into low, medium, and high. We categorized environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) as any smoker(s) in the household or no smokers in the household. Study staff collected child weight and height using a professional digital scale (Health-o-meter). We calculated population standardized BMI z-score for age using WHO guidelines (WHO, 2006). We measured complete blood cell counts in whole blood samples using the AcT 5diff Cap Pierce hematology analyzer (Beckman Coulter).

We identified child sex, age, body mass index (BMI) z-score for age, socioeconomic status (SES), and exposure to environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) a priori as factors likely to affect both urinary metals levels and m6A and Am (Boulias and Greer, 2023; Kupsco et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024). We also found that visit date and total RNA concentration were highly correlated with m6A and Am, likely representing technical variability associated with storage time (Table S2). Because of our small sample size and collinearity of covariates, we adjusted for what we believed to be the most relevant covariates (child sex, age, visit date, and total RNA concentration) in our primary analyses (Boulias and Greer, 2023; Kupsco et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024). We also report results from models adjusted for all covariates (child sex, visit date, total RNA concentration, age, BMI, SES, and ETS). As a sensitivity analysis, we additionally adjusted for leucocyte cell proportions (percent neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes) because leucocytes are generally the most transcriptionally active cells in blood and RNA content varies by cell type (Monaco et al., 2019). We organized hypothesized relationships between exposures, covariates, and outcomes in a directed acyclic graph (Fig. S2).

Statistical analyses

Because urinary metals distributions were right-skewed and linear regression is sensitive to extreme values, we log2-transformed metals levels for analysis. We evaluated correlations between each pair of log2-transformed metals using Pearson correlation coefficients. To determine if relationships fit linear modeling assumptions, we fit generalized additive models (GAMs) for each outcome and each metal using the R package mgcv (Wood, 2017). We fit the effect of each metal as a smooth function using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to select smoothing parameters, and adjusting for sex, age, visit date, and total RNA (Figs. S3–S4). Because no associations between metal and outcome with p < 0.05 had effective degrees of freedom (reflecting degree of nonlinearity of a curve) greater than one, we modelled metals linearly in further models.

To estimate the relationship of essential and nonessential metal mixtures with m6A and Am, we used quantile g-computation. We used the R package qgcomp (Keil et al., 2020) to run separate models for each outcome for overall, essential, and nonessential metals mixtures, each as both non-bootstrapped and bootstrapped with 10,000 iterations and adjusting for covariates and other urinary metals. We estimated the effect, Ψ, of a one quartile increase in all components of the overall, essential, or nonessential metal mixture on m6A or Am (bootstrapped), the effects in both positive and negative directions (directional scaled effects, non-bootstrapped), and the relative contribution (weight, non-bootstrapped) of each individual metal to the directional scaled effects. As a sensitivity analysis, we quantized exposures as deciles (instead of quartiles) and found comparable results.

We also estimated the relationship of individual urinary metals with m6A and Am using multivariable linear regression models. We ran separate models for each outcome and metal, adjusted for covariates. As a sensitivity analysis, we also ran models additionally adjusted for other urinary metals.

Because effects of environmental contaminants frequently differ by sex, we also evaluated whether child sex modified the relationship of metal mixtures with m6A and Am. We ran separate quantile g-computation models for male and female children and used the R package qgcompint (Keil, 2025) to test for interaction. We also ran separate multivariable linear regression models for male and female children and tested for interaction by running additional models with interaction terms for each metal and sex. We considered a p-value <0.1 suggestive of possible interaction in these exploratory analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2022).

Most mothers were of low socioeconomic status (57 %) and had a high school degree or less (57 %). Median BMI z-score for age was 0.79 (range −2.0 to 4.0). Most children lived in households with no smokers (88 %). These characteristics were similar to those of the PROGRESS cohort children overall.

In individual-metal multivariable linear regression models, a doubling in urinary As was associated with a minimally adjusted decrease of −0.047 (95 % CI −0.089, −0.0056) and Cs −0.096 (95 % CI −0.18, −0.015) in global %Am/A in blood (Fig. 1, Table S5). Additionally adjusting for BMI, SES, and ETS did not qualitatively change these associations. However, with these additional adjustments, a doubling in urinary Cr was associated with a decrease of −0.048 (95 % CI −0.095, −0.00041) and Co with a −0.053 (95 % CI −0.099, −0.0061) decrease in global %Am/A in blood. Additionally adjusting for leucocyte proportions, only As and Cs were associated with decreased global % Am/A (Table S4). Including all metals in models, only As and Cs were associated with decreased global %Am/A (Table S6).

In sex-stratified quantile g-computation models, there was some evidence for effect measure modification. The overall metals mixture was associated with lower Am among female participants (−0.18 %, 95 % CI −0.27 to −0.092 %) but not male participants (p-value for interaction with sex = 0.11) (Table S11). The essential metals mixture and Am trended towards a negative association among female participants but trended towards a positive association among male participants (p-value for interaction with sex = 0.057) (Table S12). The nonessential metals mixture was inversely associated with Am among female participants only (−0.14 %, 95 % CI −0.23 to −0.056 %, p-value for interaction with sex = 0.25) (Table S13). In sex-stratified individual-metal multivariable linear regression models, As was also inversely associated with Am among female participants only (−0.063 %, 95 % CI −0.11 to −0.011 % per doubling As) (Table S10).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the associations between essential and nonessential urinary metals mixtures and global m6A and Am in blood in children aged 9–11. Better understanding the relationship between metals exposures and these RNA modifications could provide insight into mechanisms of metals toxicity leading to novel biomarkers of exposure or effect. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine associations between metals and m6A and Am in a human population. We found that higher levels of the overall and nonessential metals mixtures were associated with lower Am, likely driven by As and Cs. However, we did not find associations between essential metals and Am or between any metals and m6A.

m6A

We did not find any associations between urinary metals and global m6A in blood. Several metals exposures have been associated with m6A in vitro or in vivo (Bai et al., 2018; Cayir et al., 2019; Qi et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2020; Yue et al., 2022). However, this relationship has yet to be explored in other epidemiological studies. Still, global blood m6A levels in human populations were previously associated with airborne black carbon (Kupsco et al., 2019), suggesting that total m6A content may be a marker of environmental exposures. Kupsco et al. (2019)found associations in a study population of Beijing truck drivers with high air pollution exposures. Exposure levels in our study may have been too low to elicit a strong effect. Further, experimental studies measured m6A in target tissues, e.g., lung cells exposed to particulate matter (Cayir et al., 2019). Different metals have different target tissues, e.g., As exposure is known to affect the skin, bladder, liver, and lungs (ATSDR, 2007), and Cd accumulates primarily in the kidney and liver (ATSDR, 2012). It is possible that blood is not a target tissue for metals that would result in changes to m6A. Additionally, m6A plays important roles in development during childhood and adolescence (Yen and Chen, 2021). We found an association between child age and m6A, suggesting potential development-related associations (Table S2). Although all models were adjusted for age, changes in m6A during development may have masked potential associations with metals. As this was a pilot analysis, we also had a relatively small sample size, which may have been insufficient to detect a small effect. In summary, relatively low exposure levels, tissue specificity, developmental stage susceptibility, and limited statistical power might have contributed to the null m6A results.

Am

We found associations of urinary metals mixtures and individual metals As and Cs with lower global Am in blood. Am generally increases RNA stability, can enhance translation efficiency, and plays important roles in regulating RNA function and fate (Zhou et al., 2024). Accordingly, lower global Am might result in faster RNA turnover, altered translation of critical transcripts, and disruption of regulatory RNAs that modulate cell signaling. Dysregulation of Am has been linked with a range of diseases, including neurological conditions, cancer, autoimmune conditions, and asthma (Dimitrova et al., 2019).

Our observation that urinary metals—particularly nonessential metals As and Cs—are associated with lower global Am may reflect known pathways of metal toxicity. Nonessential metals have been implicated in oxidative stress (Liu et al., 2023b), inflammation (Zhang et al., 2020), and mitochondrial dysfunction (Reddam et al., 2022), pathways which can lead to changes in RNA methylation. Am has been shown to play a functional role in cellular stress response. For example, upon cellular stress, the regulatory small nucleolar RNA SNORD80 guides 2′-O-methylation at A496 within the regulatory long noncoding RNA GAS5, enhancing its stability and leading to its upregulation—GAS5 is a member of the growth arrest-specific (GAS) gene family important in cellular stress response (Xu et al., 2025). Hence, it is plausible that metals exposures could modulate global RNA Am through stress-related pathways. Though research on environmental exposures and Am is scarce, exposure to PCBs, metabolic- and endocrine-disrupting chemicals persistent in the environment, has been shown to affect global Am levels in rat livers, along with other epitranscriptomic modifications (Klinge et al., 2021; Piell et al., 2023). Nonessential metals As, Cd, Pb, and Hg cause toxicity through some of the same mechanisms as PCBs, including endocrine disruption and oxidative stress (Liu et al., 2023b). However, PCBs also cause toxicity via additional mechanisms, such as aryl hydrocarbon receptor activation (ATSDR, 2000).

In mixture models, Cs, As, and Cr had the greatest weights contributing to the association between urinary metals and lower Am. In quantile g-computation, weights represent the proportion of the overall effect in a particular direction, with all weights summing to 1, suggesting that these metals contributed most to the mixture’s association with decreased Am. This pattern was consistent with linear models, which indicated significant associations of As and Cs with lower Am and a trend toward an association of Cr with lower Am. Arsenic is a naturally occurring element dispersed by both natural (e.g., in groundwater) and anthropogenic processes (e.g., mining and smelting of ores containing As; use in alloys or in pesticides) (ATSDR, 2007). Our study participants from Mexico City had lower urinary total As levels (median 10 μg/g creatinine) compared to a study of women in six states in northern Mexico during 2007–2011 (median 20.5 μg/g creatinine) (Rangel-Moreno et al., 2022) but higher (median 9.6 μg/L) compared to children aged 6–11 in the US general population National Health and Nutritional Examination Survey (NHANES) 2009–2010 (geometric mean 6.6 μg/L) (CDC, 2018). We did not measure arsenic species and cannot differentiate between nontoxic arsenobetaine and arsenosugars and toxic inorganic As exposure. However, seafood intake, a major source of arsenobetaine and arsenosugars, was low in the PROGRESS study population (Mullin et al., 2019). Inorganic As exposure can lead to cancer, skin disorders, cardiovascular disease, and other adverse health effects (ATSDR, 2007). Arsenic can affect health via endocrine disruption, oxidative stress, and DNA damage (Rahaman et al., 2021). However, global RNA Am in blood encompasses many processes, many of which are still not well understood (Zhou et al., 2024), limiting the interpretation of our findings.

Similarly, the negative association between Cs and global Am could be due to biological disruption. Cesium is a naturally occurring element with low toxicity, and occurs in soil, water, and air primarily from rock erosion and weathering (ATSDR, 2004). Radioactive Cs is of greater health concern and is released by nuclear power plants or weapons, but levels are low in the general population (ATSDR, 2004). People are generally exposed to all forms of Cs primarily by diet (ATSDR, 2004). Our study population had higher levels of urinary Cs (median 8.9 μg/L) compared to children aged 6–11 in the US general population NHANES survey 2017–2018 (geometric mean 4.1 μg/L) (CDC, 2018). Some epidemiological research has linked urinary Cs with markers of inflammation (Li et al., 2022), adverse adipokine profiles (Wang et al., 2021), and high blood pressure (Rahman et al., 2022). However, the biological underpinnings of these associations remain unknown. Thus, interpretation of the negative association between Cs and global Am requires caution.

Little observational research in human populations has been conducted on Am, so there is limited information on how levels vary by characteristics. In our study population, Am was not associated with sex, child age, or BMI z-score for age (Table S2). We found some evidence for effect measure modification by sex. The essential metals mixture and Am trended towards a negative association among female participants but a positive association among male participants (p-value for interaction with sex = 0.057). In sex-stratified models, nonessential metals and As were inversely associated with Am among female participants only, although p-values for interaction were greater than 0.1. Metals toxicity can vary by sex, and As generally affects males more significantly, potentially due to reduced As methylation capacity compared to females (Lu et al., 2025). Our pilot study population was small (n = 150), resulting in limited statistical power for subgroup analyses, so these results should be interpreted with caution.

Associations of nonessential metals, As, and Cs were robust to additional adjustment for leucocyte cell type proportions (percent neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes). Nm has been shown to vary between tissues (Liu et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024), and because we measured global RNA Am, it is possible that the associations we observed were due to changes in cell type proportions. Nonessential metals can exert toxicity by promoting inflammation and interfering with immune homeostasis, and neutrophil counts have been correlated with exposure to Cd, Pb, and Hg (Zhang et al., 2020). However, we were not able to measure immune cell activation, which could be related to metals exposures and/or total Nm.

Strengths and limitations

Our study had several limitations. We measured global proportion m6A and Am in blood at a single timepoint, so we could not detect tissue-specific or transient changes. We also measured m6A and Am in total RNA and so cannot distinguish between changes in mRNA versus other RNA biotypes. While most research focuses on the role of m6A on mRNA, mRNA typically makes up only about 1 % of cell RNA (Boulias and Greer, 2023). Limited understanding of the roles of m6A on rRNA and other RNA biotypes as well as limited understanding of the roles of Am make the interpretation of our results less clear. Global m6A and Am also do not provide information on specific pathways of epitranscriptomic regulation that might be affected.

We also did not measure As speciation and Cs isotope ratios, which affect the toxicity of these metals, although seafood intake (a major source of less toxic forms of As) was low in our study population and radioactive Cs tends to be low in the general population. Urinary As and Cs primarily reflect acute or ongoing exposure but do not reliably capture long-term or cumulative exposure, which may be more relevant for health outcomes (ATSDR, 2004; Martinez-Morata et al., 2023). These limitations could introduce measurement error; however, because the error is likely non-differential with respect to our outcomes, any resulting bias would most likely attenuate associations toward the null.

Additionally, our study design was cross-sectional; we cannot establish exposure preceded outcome and there is potential for reverse causation. Renal function has been associated with urinary Cs excretion (Jin et al., 2018). If some participants had suboptimal health relating to both problems with renal function and altered global Am levels, this could also affect urinary metals measurements. There is also potential for residual or unmeasured confounding (e.g., by diet, genetic background, other environmental pollutants, or other factors). Although creatinine-adjusted urinary metals concentrations are widely used to account for urine dilution, particularly in pediatric populations, this approach may still be subject to residual bias due to interindividual differences in creatinine excretion related to age, body composition, or hydration status. Future studies may benefit from using additional or alternative normalization methods, such as specific gravity, when available.

Nonetheless, we present information on associations between metals and two epitranscriptomic marks, which represent novel and potentially important pathways of investigation for mechanisms and biomarkers. We hypothesize that Am could act as a novel biomarker of metal-induced cellular dysregulation, potentially capturing subclinical effects before adverse health outcomes occur. To our knowledge, this is the first study in a human population investigating associations of metals with Am and m6A. Strengths of this study also include multiple high quality metals measurements and our use of quantile g-computation to examine mixture effects.

Conclusions

We investigated cross-sectional associations between urinary metals levels and global levels of two epitranscriptomic marks in RNA in blood among a population of children aged 9–11 in Mexico. Overall nonessential metals mixtures, As, and Cs concentrations were associated with Am. We did not find associations between essential metals and Am or essential or nonessential metals and m6A. Quantile g-computation allowed us to examine overall mixture effects of essential and nonessential metals. Our findings warrant further investigation of Am as a potential marker or mechanism of nonessential metals’ effect on health. Further research should investigate RNA-biotype-specific and mRNA-transcript specific Am and m6A associations with environmental exposures to better understand effects and pathways at the molecular level.
