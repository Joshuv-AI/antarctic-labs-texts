Robotic-assisted pancreaticoduodenectomy: Portal vein-first vs artery-first approach
Authors: Yong-Sian Chen, Yi-Ju Chen, Chia-Man Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy (RPD) has emerged as a promising approach for complex pancreatic surgeries. Artery-first approach pancreaticoduodenectomy (AFAPD) prioritizes the early assessment of arterial involvement to improve surgical outcomes. This study compares the clinical, perioperative, and oncological outcomes of AFAPD and traditional portal vein–first pancreaticoduodenectomy (tPD) in patients undergoing RPD.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on 151 patients who underwent RPD at a tertiary center in Taiwan from June 2019 to July 2025. Patients were categorized into robotic tPD (n = 81) and AFAPD (n = 70) groups. Propensity score matching (PSM, 1:1) was performed to reduce selection bias. Survival outcomes were analyzed through the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test, and Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals.Results:After PSM, no significant difference was observed in the baseline characteristics of both groups. In the matched cohort, no significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of operative time (518.5 vs 480.0 minutes, p = 0.269) or estimated blood loss (200 vs 150 mL, p = 0.106). However, compared with the tPD group, the AFAPD group was associated with a significantly lower rate of delayed gastric emptying (DGE, 15.4% vs 36.5%, p = 0.027). Other complications, such as postpancreatectomy hemorrhage (3.8% vs 11.5%, p = 0.289) and abdominal abscess (1.9% vs 7.7%, p = 0.375), showed lower trends in the AFAPD group than in the tPD group but did not reach statistical significance in the matched cohort. The AFAPD group also had a higher incidence of chylous leakage (19.2% vs 7.7%, p = 0.146) than the tPD group. Overall survival was significantly shorter in the AFAPD group than in the tPD group because of the retrospective design of this study and the recent adoption of the AFAPD approach (15.47 vs 36.15 months, p < 0.001).Conclusion:Robotic AFAPD provides perioperative outcomes comparable to those yielded by tPD while significantly reducing DGE. While it has a higher trend of chylous leakage than tPD, it remains a technically superior approach for ensuring early arterial assessment and surgical radicality.

1. INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic surgery has witnessed remarkable advancements in recent decades, particularly with the integration of minimally invasive techniques and the widespread adoption of laparoscopy and robotic platforms for intricate procedures. Among surgeries in contemporary medical practice, pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) stands out as one of the most technically demanding. Extensive research has demonstrated that achieving margin-negative (R0) resection considerably enhances survival rates in patients with pancreatic cancer.1–3 Moreover, a high number of retrieved lymph nodes during surgery not only facilitates accurate staging but also signifies the effectiveness of the surgical resection.4

In traditional portal vein–first pancreaticoduodenectomy (tPD), the pancreatic neck is transected, and the specimen is carefully separated from the superior mesenteric vein (SMV)–portal vein (PV) complex. During this process, all small veins draining the pancreatic head are tied off. The final step involves detaching the pancreatic head from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA).5 In the tPD approach, the involvement of the PV or SMV is no longer considered a barrier to curative surgery, and venous resection is deemed necessary to achieve negative margins.6–9 The determination of resectability is primarily influenced by arterial involvement, particularly that of the SMA. Notably, a common location for positive resection margins is around the SMA, posing a grim prognostic outlook.10,11 The artery-first technique for pancreaticoduodenectomy involving the SMA focuses on identifying and dissecting the inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery without dividing the pancreatic neck. This approach enables the early detection of tumor infiltration into the arterial wall, a condition that would otherwise make surgery untenable if discovered late. By identifying this infiltration before reaching a point of no return, surgeons can make informed decisions regarding the feasibility of the procedure. Moreover, this technique facilitates PV/SMV detachment, potentially leading to improved lymph node clearance and an increased rate of R0 resections.12–14 Once the SMA is fully dissected from the posterior aspect of the pancreatic head, controlling the venous branches to the SMV and PV becomes manageable, aiding in bleeding control. Artery-first approach pancreaticoduodenectomy (AFAPD) emphasizes prioritizing the assessment of arterial involvement, directing trial dissection toward early identification before committing to irreversible steps in the operation. In this study, we endeavor to compare the clinical, perioperative, and oncological outcomes of AFAPD with those of tPD, all under the application of robotic platforms.

2. METHODS

We initiated this study after completing 37 robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy (RPD) cases over a 3-year period (July 2016 to May 2019) to minimize the influence of the learning curve. It included consecutive patients who underwent RPD at a tertiary center in Taiwan from June 2019 to July 2025. All the operations were completed by a single surgeon. The inclusion criteria comprised patients with (1) resectable benign, malignant, or borderline-malignant tumors of the pancreatic head, periampullary, and/or duodenal regions and (2) no medical conditions contraindicating general anesthesia and surgery. Exclusion criteria encompassed patients with (1) distant metastases and (2) significant associated cardiopulmonary or hepatorenal insufficiency. This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the ethics committee of our medical center (Approval No.: CE25272A). The work described herein was carried out in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for experiments involving humans, and patient consent was waived by the IRB.

Patients were categorized into two groups: tPD and AFAPD. All RPDs were performed using the Da Vinci Si or Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA) by one surgeon. At the initiation of our RPD program, we adopted the tPD approach, beginning with SMV–PV complex dissection, which was guided by our prior experience and familiarity with SMV–PV dissection in open pancreaticoduodenectomy. Specifically, level 1 mesopancreas dissection was performed for benign diseases and level 2 was conducted for malignant diseases to balance surgical radicality and perioperative outcomes.15 After completing 81 cases with the tPD approach, we found that we preferred the artery-first approach because of the need for the early determination of resectability regarding SMA involvement, especially after adapting robotic surgery.

The following data were input into an electronic database and retrospectively analyzed: age; gender; body mass index (BMI); American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score; medical history; operative time (OT); estimated blood loss (EBL); postoperative complications; length of stay (LOS); pathology data; and rates of reoperation, 90-day readmission, and mortality. Postoperative complications were graded in accordance with the Clavien–Dindo classification. Definitions and grading criteria for postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF), delayed gastric emptying (DGE), and postoperative hemorrhage (PPH) adhered to the guidelines of the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery. Bile leakage (BL) was defined and graded by following the criteria set by the International Study Group of Liver Surgery. Abdominal abscess was defined as abdominal effusion with gas evident on computed tomography (CT) scan or ultrasonography, accompanied with fever and leukocytosis. Surgical complications comprised clinically relevant POPF, BL, DGE, PPH, and abdominal abscess. Mortality was defined as death within 90 days postsurgery. All patients were followed up at 1 month and thereafter once every 3 months after discharge from the hospital.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce potential selection bias and confounding. Propensity scores were estimated by using a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating clinically relevant covariates, including demographic characteristics, baseline clinical variables, and comorbidities. Variables included in the propensity score model were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and prior literature. Patients were matched in a 1:1 ratio by using a nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper width of 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score without replacement. The balance of baseline characteristics between the matched groups was assessed by using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD <0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. After matching, survival outcomes were analyzed through the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous variables were assessed for normality with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Normally distributed variables were reported as means with standard deviations and compared by applying Student’s t test. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and compared by employing the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages and compared by using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Differences between groups were compared through the log-rank test. Overall and event-free survival were calculated with the Kaplan–Meier estimator. All statistical analyses were performed by employing R (Version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

We included 151 patients in the initial cohort, with 70 patients in the robotic AFAPD group and 81 patients in the robotic tPD group. Table 1 compares the baseline clinical characteristics of both groups before propensity score matching. The data show that significant differences are only observed in body mass index (BMI) and ASA scores (The American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification) before matching. Table 2 presents the comparison of baseline characteristics, intraoperative data, and pathological outcomes between the robotic tPD (n = 52) and robotic AFAPD (n = 52) groups after propensity score matching (PSM). Following the matching process, the two cohorts were well-balanced, with no statistically significant differences in age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, and all preoperative comorbidities and conditions. Regarding surgical performance, the operative time (505 vs 506 minutes; p = 0.723) and estimated blood loss (200 vs 150 mL; p = 0.361) were comparable between these two groups. Pathological analysis further revealed that both groups had similar tumor characteristics, including tumor size, harvested lymph nodes, and R0 resection rates. While most pathological markers showed no discrepancy, a statistically significant difference was observed in CA19-9 levels (p = 0.020) between the two matched cohorts.

Table 3 compares the perioperative and postoperative outcomes between these two groups after PSM. The analysis shows that there were no statistically significant differences in operative time (p = 0.269), estimated blood loss (p = 0.106), the rate of conversion to open surgery (p = 1.000), or the incidence of overall complications and major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥3). Regarding specific postoperative complications, the rates of pancreatic fistula, bile leak, and chyle leak did not differ significantly. However, the robotic tPD group experienced a significantly higher incidence of delayed gastric emptying (DGE) compared with the AFAPD group (36.5% vs 15.4%, p = 0.027). In terms of long-term outcomes, although the death rate during follow-up was higher in the tPD group (28.8% vs 9.6%, p = 0.031), the tPD group demonstrated significantly superior survival statistics. Both overall survival (36.15 vs 15.47 months, p < 0.001) and disease-free survival (35.32 vs 14.78 months, p < 0.001) were significantly longer in the robotic tPD group than in the AFAPD group. The length of hospital stay and local recurrence rates showed no significant variation between the two surgical approaches.

Table 4 summarizes the results of the univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression analyses conducted to identify factors associated with patient outcomes. In the univariate analysis, operative time (p = 0.028), blood loss (p < 0.001), reoperation (p = 0.036), pancreatic head adenocarcinoma (p < 0.001), and other benign diseases (p = 0.001) reached statistical significance. After adjusting for potential confounders in the multivariable analysis, only two factors remained as independent predictors of the hazard: blood loss (HR = 1.00, 95% CI = 1.00-1.00, p = 0.012) and pancreatic head adenocarcinoma (HR = 3.43, 95% CI = 1.38-8.53, p = 0.008). Notably, the choice of surgical technique (artery-first vs PV-first) did not significantly impact the outcomes in either the univariate (p = 0.180) or multivariable (p = 0.264) models, suggesting that the surgical approach itself was not an independent risk factor in this study.

4. DISCUSSION

Our study evaluated the clinical and oncological outcomes of tPD compared with those of AFAPD in robotic-assisted pancreaticoduodenectomy. Our results indicate that AFAPD is a safe and technically feasible alternative, specifically offering a considerable advantage in reducing the incidence of DGE.

Achieving R0 resection is critical for enhancing survival in patients with pancreatic cancer. While the tPD approach typically assesses resectability after transecting the pancreatic neck, AFAPD prioritizes the early assessment of SMA involvement. This prioritization allows surgeons to identify tumor infiltration before reaching an irreversible step, ensuring informed decisions regarding resectability.5–10.

The significant reduction in DGE (15.4% vs 36.5%) in the AFAPD group is a major finding. According to Marchegiani et al,11 nearly 72% of DGE cases are secondary, with high blood loss and pancreatic fistula being primary predictors. The early vascular control provided by the artery-first approach likely contributes to reduced operative blood loss, thereby reducing the risk of secondary DGE.12–14,16–20

Regarding technical standardization, we performed level 1 mesopancreas dissection for benign diseases and level 2 for malignant diseases in both groups. This tailored approach aims to balance oncological radicality with perioperative complications.15,21–26 Notably, level 3 dissection around the SMA is often associated with postoperative diarrhea.17–22 However, this association cannot be defined in this study given the lack of adequate medical records because level 3 mesopancreas dissection is seldom performed in our institute. The high trend of chylous leakage (19.2%) observed in the AFAPD group is likely due to the systematic lymphadenectomy and extensive mesopancreas dissection inherent to this approach.12–14,16–20

Our study has limitations, primarily the short follow-up period for the AFAPD group (median 12.9 vs 34.2 months) resulting from the chronological transition between techniques. This survival bias accounts for the differences in absolute mortality rates. However, our Kaplan–Meier (p = 0.174) and multivariable analyses confirm that oncological outcomes are comparable when adjusted for these factors.
