An integrated edge–cloud IoT framework for resilient disaster prevention in fire detection and forest carbon assessment
Authors: Li-Hsien Chen, Shubham S. Kolhe, Jie Hu, Kuo-Hsiung Tseng, Meng-Yun Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Humanity’s ongoing overuse of natural resources is degrading the Earth’s environment, intensifying climate change impacts. Recent record temperatures and frequent wildfires threaten humans directly and disrupt ecosystems, especially by damaging forest carbon sinks, which accelerates global warming in a harmful cycle. To combat this, the study developed a forest fire monitoring and management system using Internet of Things (IoT) technology. The system consists of four layers: (1) The sensing layer collects environmental data—meteorological, air quality, images, and thermal imaging—to detect fires early. It uses image recognition to identify flames and smoke, cross-checking with real-time weather data to reduce false alarms. (2) The transmission layer utilizes 4G networks for high-bandwidth, real-time data and image transfer. (3) The application layer provides real-time images, satellite monitoring maps, and field sensor data for forest management needs. (4) The power supply layer adapts to different environments, using solar energy and batteries outdoors and grid electricity indoors. Testing shows the system achieves 84.4% average precision in object detection and only 0.45% average error in infrared temperature measurement. Integrating satellite images and geographic information system data allows precise mapping of fire impact zones and severity. This innovative IoT-based forest fire system is vital for early detection and enhanced wildfire management, offering a powerful tool to help preserve ecological balance and address climate change effectively.

Introduction

Forest fires are a serious natural disaster that significantly impacts the ecological environment, human society, and the economy1. The effects of global climate change and human activities have led to an increasing frequency and severity of forest fires2. Many countries and regions have experienced or are currently facing the threat and devastation of forest fires. For instance, Spain is among the top five European countries for forest fire incidents, with 1,676 occurrences reported in 20193. In 2021, Turkey experienced significant losses, with 27,000 hectares of forest affected. India also faces substantial forest fire challenges, with approximately 11,000 square kilometers burned during 2019–2020, which is close to 1.6% of its total forest cover4. Globally, out of 40.6 million square kilometers of forest coverage, about 0.98 million square kilometers were affected by fires in 2015, representing roughly 2.41% of the total forest area. It is estimated that around 3.4% of the Earth’s land surface burns each year5. These fires not only damage forest ecosystems6, but also trigger a series of issues such as air pollution, soil erosion7, and water source contamination8, posing major challenges to ecological restoration and conservation9.

In the context of global climate change, the importance of the forest fire issue is further highlighted. As climate change intensifies, the frequency and scale of forest fires are on the rise10, having increasingly severe effects on the ecological environment and carbon sink functions. Forests play a crucial role on Earth and are considered one of the most important carbon sinks11, absorbing and storing large amounts of carbon dioxide, which helps mitigate global warming. However, the extensive burning of trees and carbon emissions caused by forest fires not only reduces the capacity of carbon sinks but may also trigger further greenhouse gas releases12, creating a feedback loop that accelerates climate change.

Current forest fire management systems often lack real-time monitoring and predictive capabilities, leading to delayed responses and increased damage13. This study addresses these gaps by developing an integrated IoT system that leverages advanced technologies such as edge computing, image recognition, and satellite data. The novelty of this approach lies in its comprehensive integration of diverse data sources and real-time analysis, which enhances the accuracy of fire predictions and the efficiency of disaster response. While existing IoT-based forest fire detection systems primarily focus on isolated sensing or basic alerts, this study introduces a pioneering four-layer architecture that uniquely integrates edge computing for on-device image recognition (flame/smoke detection with 84.4% precision), real-time 4G transmission, adaptive power supplies (solar/grid), and multi-source fusion of meteorological, satellite, and GIS data. Unlike prior frameworks limited to pre-fire detection, our system extends to comprehensive lifecycle management, encompassing predictive fire risk modeling via carbon sink calculations, intra-fire personnel tracking and push notifications, and post-fire vegetation impact assessment, achieving 0.45% infrared measurement error and precise burn severity mapping. This holistic, end-to-end approach, validated in real-world scenarios, represents a significant advancement in scalable, intelligent wildfire mitigation.

Taking the domestic situation as an example, current disaster prevention measures primarily focus on prevention. However, once a fire breaks out, a delayed response can lead to the rapid expansion of the forest fire, causing significant damage to precious forest resources. With the rise of Internet of Things (IoT) technology14 and edge computing15, employing appropriate sensing devices, edge computing, data analysis, and real-time notification systems to establish an intelligent disaster prevention IoT can significantly shorten disaster response times16. This approach can quickly inform rescue teams about the severity of the situation, thereby improving rescue efficiency and reducing disaster losses.

This study aims to achieve fire warning and forest management goals by developing an integrated IoT system. This system combines meteorological stations and environmental sensing data, incorporating image recognition and edge devices for real-time monitoring. By integrating meteorological open platforms and low-orbit satellite data, the system calculates forest carbon storage and wildfire predictions. It provides diverse observational data and push notifications. Experiments are conducted to build the system based on site demands, validating various sensors, and ultimately carrying out overall validation of the system in actual scenarios as a reference for future fire and forest monitoring.

To address the impacts of forest fires and their aftermath, this study combines civil engineering (demand side) and electrical engineering (supply side) to focus on a forest fire management system. It establishes a universal IoT system with edge devices and equipment for both indoor and outdoor use. Utilizing meteorological stations and environmental sensors (one-dimensional) alongside real-time image monitoring (two-dimensional), the system conducts data analysis before, during, and after forest fire events. This analysis helps predict fires and alert relevant personnel beforehand, implement push notifications during disasters, and assist firefighters in rapid rescue operations. After the disaster, the system compares vegetation changes before and after the incident to analyze the impact of tree loss on carbon sinks. This highlights the effectiveness of the system’s early warning capabilities and implementing forest fire monitoring. Additionally, by incorporating personnel identification and carbon sink calculation, the system aims to achieve effective management objectives.

Materials and methods

IoT system

To achieve the goals of monitoring forest fires and managing forests, the system construction must meet the requirements of real-time performance, stability, and long-term operation. Therefore, this study designs IoT architecture for fire and forest management, as shown in Fig. 1. The details of this architecture are explained as follows.

Sensing Layer.

Sensing Layer.

Sensing Layer.

In the sensing layer, this study utilizes various sensing items to monitor the scene, including one-dimensional weather stations, environmental sensors, and two-dimensional image recognition technology. The data from the weather stations and environmental sensors can be cross-referenced to reduce false alarms. During a fire event, this data can help assess the on-site safety conditions, such as predicting fire spread based on wind direction and identifying the presence of toxic gases, thereby increasing the effectiveness of disaster response and ensuring personnel safety.

Two-dimensional optical imaging allows for the rapid detection of large volumes of smoke and flames caused by forest fires. Additionally, the system supports the identification of heat sources and hot spots, providing valuable reference for emergency disaster response. Finally, edge computing devices process the collected data and issue real-time alerts to relevant units, enabling timely decision-making.

2.Transmission Layer.

2.Transmission Layer.

Transmission Layer.

In the transmission layer, this study employs a combination of wired and wireless transmission methods to accommodate different modules and scenarios. Fixed-position modules, such as the weather station module and environmental sensing module, utilize wireless Wi-Fi for its wide coverage and high transmission capabilities. In contrast, the mobile image recognition module can dynamically switch between Wi-Fi and 4G LTE modes, depending on availability. In mobile, outdoor settings, 4G LTE ensures continuous connectivity, while areas with strong Wi-Fi coverage allow for reduced power consumption by switching modes. For indoor deployments, the wired Ethernet is preferred, offering faster transmission speeds and a more stable connection.

3.Application Layer.

3.Application Layer.

Application Layer.

This study adopts the Google Cloud Platform as the central server, where sensor data from the transmission layer and public meteorological data (sourced from open platforms) are stored in MySQL and InfluxDB databases17. Subsequently, the data is visualized through Grafana dashboards, enabling real-time monitoring of environmental values. The Flask-based web application displays real-time images, observational data from low-orbit satellites, time-series graphs, and fire hazard potential maps. Users can also review historical data for analysis and decision support.

4.Power Supply Layer.

4.Power Supply Layer.

Power Supply Layer.

The IoT system includes three subsystems: the environmental sensing module, the image recognition system, and the weather station module. The environmental sensing module and the image recognition system can be installed indoors or outdoors according to needs, which features flexibility to switch power supply modes, including grid power and green/renewable energy sources. Both are also equipped with an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) system to provide short-term backup power during outages. In contrast, the weather station module needs to be permanently deployed outdoors. Due to its special geographical location and lack of access to the grid power supply, it is powered exclusively by solar energy and rechargeable batteries for power supply.

To emphasize the architectural distinction central to this study, the Edge–Cloud collaborative framework explicitly separates edge-level processing from cloud-level analytics, thereby highlighting its distributed computing paradigm. Figure 1 illustrates the complete IoT methodology. Environmental sensors continuously capture weather, temperature, gas concentration, image, and infrared data, which are transmitted to edge devices. Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi platforms execute YOLO-based inference for flame and smoke detection, while local decision logic cross-checks weather thresholds to trigger on-site alerts within one second. Validated image data and metadata are subsequently transmitted via LTE/4G to the cloud, where the database stores the information for GIS georeferencing, Flask-based processing, satellite NDVI analysis, carbon risk assessment, and fire spread modeling. The application layer then generates alerts, including LINE notifications and email messages accompanied by severity-based risk maps. The diagram clearly distinguishes edge processing (local, real-time YOLO inference) from cloud analytics (satellite data fusion and risk modeling), which constitutes the core advantage of the distributed architecture.

While deployment on Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi platforms achieved stable real-time detection during field testing (Fig. 1), systematic profiling of inference latency, CPU/GPU utilization, and edge–cloud communication delays was not conducted. This study prioritized integrated system feasibility over individual component benchmarking, which is appropriate for initial deployment validation. Future work will quantify these edge-computing metrics under varying network conditions to comprehensively validate real-time performance claims.

Climate Factors

Climate factors play a crucial role in the ignition, development, and spread of forest fires. Below are the key climate variables influencing wildfire behavior:

Relative Humidity.

Relative Humidity.

Relative Humidity.

Relative humidity plays a critical role in forest fire risks, significantly influencing the development and spread of fires. When relative humidity drops, the air becomes drier, leading to rapid drying of leaves, grasses, and forest litter, which in turn becomes more flammable. This dryness not only amplifies the speed and extent of fire spread but also makes active fire suppression more challenging. Additionally, low relative humidity makes fires more difficult to control, requiring firefighting teams to allocate more resources to manage the flames. Lower humidity also heightens the dryness of vegetation, making it easier for flames to ignite. When vegetation becomes extremely dry, even a small ignition source, such as lightning or human activity, can trigger large-scale fires. Therefore, continuous monitoring and understanding changes in relative humidity is crucial for early fire detection, risk assessment, and effective response planning.

2.Temperature.

2.Temperature.

Temperature.

Temperature is a primary climatic factor affecting the likelihood and intensity of forest fires. Higher temperatures generally indicate increased evaporation rates and drier vegetation and soils, which in turn raises the likelihood of fire occurrence. Moreover, elevated temperatures can also accelerate the speed at which fires spread, causing fire hazards to propagate more rapidly.

3.Wind.

3.Wind.

Wind.

In wildfire-prone regions, rising temperatures are strongly correlated with increased fire frequency and severity. Monitoring temperature trends is therefore essential for identifying high-risk periods and enabling proactive fire prevention strategies. Elevated temperatures reduce fuel moisture content, increase vegetation flammability and accelerating ignition potential. Wind speed and wind direction are among the most influential factors governing wildfire dynamics, as they directly affect the rate and trajectory of fire spread. High wind speeds can rapidly propagate flames across large areas and generate spot fires far beyond the primary fire front. Wind direction determines the path of fire advancement, influencing flame length, combustion intensity, and oxygen availability. Sudden shifts in wind direction can unpredictably alter fire trajectories, posing significant risks to human life, infrastructure, and ecological systems. Consequently, accurate and real-time wind data are critical for fire spread modeling, evacuation planning, and the effective allocation of firefighting resources.

4.Precipitation.

4.Precipitation.

Precipitation.

Precipitation plays a dual role in forest fire behavior. Sufficient rainfall can reduce the likelihood of forest fires and assist in extinguishing flames after a fire has started. Rainwater moistens vegetation and the ground, significantly reducing flammability and lowering the risk of ignition. Additionally, precipitation can wash airborne particles to the ground, reducing the available fuel for intense fires, thereby aiding in fire control.

However, On the other hand, if rainfall is insufficient or if dry weather follows a rain event, vegetation and the surface can quickly dry out, increasing the risk of fire occurrence. Therefore, Monitoring rainfall patterns, seasonal droughts, and soil moisture content is essential for forecasting fire risks and designing effective fire mitigation strategies.

2.Thermal Imaging Temperature Calibration.

2.Thermal Imaging Temperature Calibration.

Thermal Imaging Temperature Calibration.

Infrared thermal imaging is a sensing technology that generates visual representations based on the infrared radiation emitted by objects. Thermal cameras detect this radiation and convert it into temperature-mapped images, enabling visualization of surface temperature distributions and identification of abnormal heat patterns or potential fire hazards. To validate the accuracy of infrared temperature measurements, a controlled calibration experiment was conducted using an electromagnetic heating stirrer as the heating source. During the calibration process, the thermal imaging camera was positioned at a fixed distance of 50 cm from the heating plate. The temperature gradually increased from 20 °C to 200 °C in increments of 10 °C. For each temperature setting, readings were continuously recorded from a central area of approximately 4 cm² on the heating surface. This calibration procedure ensured measurement consistency and validated the thermal camera’s reliability for subsequent fire detection applications in field environments.

Low Earth Orbit Satellite Imagery

Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites have unique advantages in monitoring the Earth’s surface. Equipped with multispectral imagers and high-resolution cameras, they can provide rich surface information. By utilizing specific spectral bands, LEO satellites can effectively identify different surface conditions and changes, such as droughts, wildfires, air pollution, and land-use transformations with exceptional precision. For example, by analyzing near infrared and thermal infrared bands, the moisture content of surface vegetation can be accurately determined, enabling drought monitoring and early warning18. The use of thermal infrared bands to detect surface temperature changes allows for the rapid and precise location and monitoring of wildfires. Additionally, specific bands can be used to detect atmospheric pollutants, enabling real-time monitoring of air pollution and providing quantitative data for public health. LEO satellites can also capture surface changes over time through periodic imaging, such as land-use changes, urban expansion, and the impacts of natural disasters. Time-series analysis can then be applied to identify trends and patterns in surface changes. The multispectral imagers and high-resolution cameras on LEO satellites provide abundant surface information. By applying specific spectral bands, LEO satellites can effectively identify various surface conditions and changes. Forest Carbon Storage Calculation

Forest carbon storage refers to the ability of forest ecosystems to absorb and store large amounts of carbon in trees and soil. Through photosynthesis, trees convert carbon dioxide into organic matter, fixing carbon within tree tissues, while part of the carbon is also stored in the soil. This carbon storage not only helps reduce the concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, slowing the pace of global climate change, but also contributes to maintaining the stability and health of forest ecosystems. This section provides a detailed explanation of the methods and processes used in this study to calculate forest carbon storage. By analyzing and estimating carbon stocks, we can better understand the changes in carbon dynamics within forests, which are of great significance for forest management and climate strategies. Potential and Push Notification Alerts

This study focused on the Nantou Renlun station area for monitoring and classification, with analysis conducted using NDVI and NDMI to classify areas with lower vegetation cover and drier conditions, creating a fire risk potential map, as shown in Fig. 6. This map can be updated dynamically, and by comparing old and new data, surface change trends can also be monitored over time.

The sensing layer will trigger push notifications when certain conditions or thresholds are met, as shown in Fig. 7. Different edge modules have specific threshold values for various physical parameters, as detailed in Table 1, allowing for observation of different module alarms during an event to reduce false alarm rates. Additionally, the system can also modify threshold settings remotely based on different environmental requirements, achieving the goals of adaptability, modularity, and convenience. In this study, fire risk and alert generation mechanisms are implemented using a rule-based multi-indicator fusion strategy, as illustrated in Fig. 8. Environmental parameters (temperature, relative humidity, VOC index, and PM2.5) are continuously compared against predefined threshold values at the edge level. These parameters generate an environmental risk flag when threshold conditions are exceeded. Simultaneously, the edge-based image recognition module performs real-time flame and smoke detection, producing confidence scores that serve as image-based risk indicators. Within the decision engine, environmental risk flags and image detection results are combined using multi-threshold rule-based logic. An alert is triggered only when environmental risk conditions are met in conjunction with positive image-based detection results, thereby reducing false alarms and improving reliability. In parallel, satellite-derived vegetation indices (NDVI and NDMI), processed at the cloud level, are used to classify background dryness and vegetation vulnerability. These indices contribute to the generation of a fire risk potential map by providing contextual susceptibility assessment rather than real-time ignition detection. The decision engine integrates edge-level detection outputs with cloud-level vegetation susceptibility classification to form a hierarchical risk evaluation framework.

Figure 8 summarizes this multi-layer fusion process, explicitly distinguishing environmental sensing, edge inference, cloud-based satellite analysis, decision logic, and alert generation. The overall system adopts a transparent threshold-based decision logic to ensure interpretability and operational reliability in disaster prevention scenarios. Environmental sensing and image recognition are processed at the edge level to generate preliminary risk indicators. Satellite-derived vegetation indices are analyzed at the cloud level to classify background susceptibility. A rule-based decision engine integrates multi-source indicators to trigger alerts and generate fire risk potential maps.

Exploration of Thermal Sensing Bands in Low Earth Orbit Satellite Imagery

This study racks and analyzes the temporal changes in NDVI and NDMI values before and after the occurrence of forest fires, as shown in Fig. 12, and the corresponding numerical variations summarized in Table 4. Through observation and analysis of satellite imagery data before and after the fire, it was found that both NDVI and NDMI values showed a downward trend after the fire, indicating a significant impact on vegetation cover.

Prior to the fire, the NDVI and NDMI values reflected the health and moisture content of the vegetation. However, after the fire, the heat and flames damaged the vegetation, altering its spectral characteristics, which resulted in the decrease of NDVI and NDMI values. This decline is likely due to the reduction of green biomass, increased soil exposure, and possible water pollution, among other environmental disturbance factors.

Therefore, it can be inferred that there is a certain correlation between the changes in NDVI and NDMI values and the fire event. This suggests that these two indices are of great significance for monitoring and assessing forest fire events. Through these indices, it is possible to detect fire events more promptly and accurately and to assess their impact on vegetation and the environment. This provides essential scientific data and support for the restoration and response efforts related to forest fires.

In addition to NDVI and NDMI, this study also employs the Normalized Burn Ratio (NBR) and its differential form, dNBR, which are widely used as important indices for assessing fire impact. NBR reflects the health status of surface vegetation by using the difference between infrared and near-infrared spectra, while dNBR measures the impact of fire on vegetation by comparing the NBR values before and after the fire. Specifically, dNBR subtracts the post-fire NBR value from the pre-fire NBR values, with larger differences indicating more severe damage to the vegetation. In this study, a comparative analysis of dNBR before and after the fire was conducted in practical applications. Based on the differences in dNBR values, the burned area was classified into different severity levels. A larger dNBR value indicates more severe damage to the vegetation. In this event, the burned area was categorized into three levels: no burning, minor, and moderate. Finally, the research results were displayed in image form on Google Earth Pro. By combining dNBR values with geographic information system (GIS) data, the extent and severity distribution of the fire-affected area were accurately marked. According to the analysis, the fire spread over an area of 11.61 hectares, with 2.035 hectares classified as more severely affected.

2. Push Notification Alerts

When the system detects that the monitored values exceed the activation thresholds, it will issue alarms based on different conditions. In this study, five trigger conditions were set, namely: excessively high temperature, excessively low humidity, the presence of harmful gases, suspicious personnel activity, and the appearance of fire or smoke. The system will issue corresponding alerts for each condition: an alert for detecting excessively high room temperature; an alert for detecting excessively low relative humidity; an alert for detecting harmful gases; an alert when image recognition identifies suspicious personnel; and an alert when image recognition detects the presence of flames or smoke. The alert content will be sent via LINE and email to the relevant personnel’s accounts, along with a captured image from the real-time monitoring feed, to facilitate immediate decision-making by the relevant personnel.

Discussion

Fire Hazard Factors Data.

Fire Hazard Factors Data.

Fire Hazard Factors Data.

In monitoring forest fires, climate factors are particularly important. Ambient temperature and relative humidity provide baseline cues for ignition risks, while wind parameters like speed and direction, are critical for estimating the potential spread and behavior of active fires. On the fuel factors level, changes in air quality can be used as a double confirmation of fire occurrence, providing information on smoke and toxic gas concentrations at the site. This information, combined with wind direction and speed, can help adjust the positioning of rescue personnel and equipment to minimize exposure and improve rescue efficiency. Furthermore, the smoke and pollutants released by forest fires may have long-term effects on the ecosystem. Monitoring air quality allows for early detection of these impacts and enables the implementation of measures to reduce environmental damage, protecting the integrity and stability of ecosystems.

2.Two-Dimensional Time-Series Data.

2.Two-Dimensional Time-Series Data.

Two-Dimensional Time-Series Data.

To enable fast on-site detection, this study uses lightweight platforms such as Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi as edge computing devices for real-time fire detection. Given their limited computing power, YOLOv7-tiny was selected as the detection model, which streamlines the YOLOv7 architecture for edge devices. Compared to other models, it requires less computational resources while delivering fast detection speeds and adequate accuracy. Result analysis showed average precisions of 84.8% for fire and smoke detection and 84.0% for personnel detection, confirming the model’s reliability for rapid fire and personnel identification. Although these results show strong performance under the current experimental setup, we did not systematically have benchmark robustness against environmental variations such as illumination changes, weather conditions, or differing smoke densities. The dataset included heterogeneous imagery from multiple sources, but this study lacked stratified evaluation across these factors. Thus, the reported metrics represent deployment-oriented validation rather than comprehensive robustness certification. Future work will incorporate expanded datasets and condition-specific evaluations to better assess generalization in diverse real-world scenarios. This study also employed the Lepton 3.5 infrared thermal imaging module for non-contact, wide-range temperature measurement, visualized as thermal images. Data from laboratory calibration yielded an average error of just 0.45% (Table 5), highlighting both its advantages and high accuracy. However, this controlled setup does not fully reflect outdoor forest variability. During field deployment at the Nantou Renlun station, the system operated continuously amid fluctuating ambient temperatures and high humidity, verifying stability and reliability. Due to safety and environmental constraints, we could not conduct large-scale controlled fire validation in forests. Future efforts will include outdoor thermal validation to quantify environmental impacts on accuracy.

3.Thermal Sensing Bands of Low Earth Orbit Satellites.

Thermal Sensing Bands of Low Earth Orbit Satellites.

This study used satellite imagery from the European Space Agency’s Sentinel-2 as the data source and employed EO Browser and a geospatial information system for data analysis and backend processing. The research found that after observing forest fires using Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, NDVI and NDMI values significantly decreased, indicating that the fire had a substantial impact on vegetation cover. Before the fire, NDVI and NDMI reflected the health of the vegetation, but after the fire, the damage to the vegetation caused changes in spectral characteristics, resulting in a decrease in these indices. This suggests that changes in NDVI and NDMI can be used to monitor fire events and assess damage. In the comparative analysis of dNBR values before and after the fire, the burned area was classified into different severity levels. A higher dNBR value indicates more severe damage to vegetation. The final research results were displayed as images on Google Earth Pro. By combining dNBR values with geographic information system (GIS) data, it was possible to accurately mark the extent and severity distribution of the fire-affected area. According to the analysis, the fire spread over an area of 11.61 hectares, of which 2.035 hectares were classified as more severely affected, closely matching the burn area reported by the Fire Department. Although the satellite-derived indices were consistent with the officially reported burn area, in-situ spectral measurements and ground-based vegetation surveys were not conducted in this study due to terrain accessibility and safety constraints following the wildfire event. Therefore, the satellite analysis should be interpreted as an indirect assessment based on established remote sensing methodologies. Future work will incorporate ground-truth validation, including field vegetation sampling and UAV-based multispectral measurements, to further enhance the quantitative accuracy of satellite-based fire impact assessment. Furthermore, with the integration of carbon storage calculations, this approach not only enables rapid estimation of forest carbon sequestration in the area but also allows for post-disaster calculations of carbon emissions caused by the fire, providing valuable data support for forest management.

4.Intelligent IoT System.

4.Intelligent IoT System.

Intelligent IoT System.

The IoT system displays both one-dimensional sensor values and two-dimensional visualizations, automatically detecting thresholds to trigger frequency conversion for faster data acquisition and upload. It sends push notifications via LINE and email upon threshold breaches, including current monitoring images to aid user decision-making. Edge computing integration minimizes power consumption and processor demands. The webpage’s lower section features time-series data from low Earth orbit satellite images, NDVI, and NDMI over the past three months, rendered as GIF animations. Accompanying line charts depict numerical trends, alongside fire risk potential maps of the monitored area, enabling intuitive assessment of surface and vegetation changes over time. Users can access the cloud-based application layer to review historical data from both public and private clouds for long-term site analysis. Overall, the system fuses 1D/2D values and imagery from public/private clouds with threshold-based alerts, enhancing situational awareness to mitigate major wildfires, protect flora and fauna, and support planetary sustainability. Although edge devices enabled real-time fire and smoke detection during field deployment, this study did not quantitatively benchmark inference latency, hardware utilization (CPU/GPU load), or edge-cloud communication delays. The focus remained on validating feasibility and stability under resource constraints. Future work will profile inference time, frame rates, resource usage, and transmission latency to quantify edge computing benefits.

While the experimental validation demonstrates the system’s effectiveness, it was conducted at a single case study location, which limits the generalizability of results across diverse forest ecosystems, climates, and topographies. Factors such as regional vegetation types, weather patterns, and terrain variations (e.g., mountainous vs. flatlands) were not fully explored, potentially affecting performance metrics like detection precision (84.4%) and temperature error (0.45%) in other settings. This scope constraint is common in initial IoT deployments but underscores the need for multi-site validation. Future research will expand validation to multiple locations, including varied ecosystems (e.g., tropical, temperate, boreal forests) across different countries, to enhance generalizability and refine system adaptability.
