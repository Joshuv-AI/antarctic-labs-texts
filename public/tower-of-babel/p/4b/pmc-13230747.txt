Genome-wide ribosome profiling reveals a dynamic translational landscape in Arabidopsis seedling roots under simulated microgravity
Authors: Ting Zhang, Lishuang Zhang, Yilin Yang, Jinbo Hu, Huiqiong Zheng, Weiming Cai, Peipei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants can adapt to environmental change through a variety of transcriptional and translational mechanisms. Although extensive knowledge exists about plant stress responses at the transcriptional level, understanding of these responses at the translational level is limited. This study aimed to evaluate the translational dynamics of plants in a simulated microgravity environment through the utilization of a ribosome profiling assay. A comparative analysis of ribosome profiling data and RNA-seq on a global scale demonstrated a modest correlation in the alteration of gene expression between transcriptional and translational levels. However, under one third of responsive genes exhibited concurrent transcriptional and translational activities. We found that simulated microgravity simulation elicited separate transcriptional and translational responses in plants. The findings indicated a significant alteration in the translational efficiency (TE) of 1081 genes under simulated microgravity conditions. Subsequent analysis identified specific sequence characteristics, including GC content and coding sequence length, as influential factors on gene TE. The abundance of upstream open reading frames (uORFs) within gene promoters indicates a possible influence on the translation efficiency of primary open reading frames located downstream. These results suggested that in response to simulated microgravity, plants had a responsive translational mechanism that interacted with transcription in a coordinated manner.Subject terms: Tropism, Plant molecular biology

Introduction

The gravitational field provides a constant mechanical stimulus to plants on Earth, a phenomenon that has existed since the beginning of life on the planet1. The evolution of plants has been greatly influenced by this constant force. They have shaped their developmental patterns and optimized their ability to obtain light, water, and mineral salts by adapting to a level of gravity at 1×g2. Any changes to this essential parameter would significantly alter the physiological systems of plants3. Understanding these changes is critical to improving our fundamental understanding of plant physiology and advances in the field of plant space biology4. Gravity is the primary factor that distinguishes the environment of Earth from that of other celestial bodies. Plants are crucial for sustaining life on Earth as they facilitate the absorption of CO2 and the release of O2 and water vapor, in addition to serving as a vital food source5. Consequently, their importance in bioregenerative life support systems for extended human space missions cannot be overstated5.

The scarcity, high cost, and time constraints of studying plant growth in microgravity are limiting factors for research. Microgravity plant biology research is made possible by space flights to the International Space Station (ISS)6 and the Chinese Space Station (CSS)7. However, the limited number of flights available restricts both research opportunities in low Earth orbit and access to the CSS or ISS. As a result, ground-based microgravity facilities play a critical role in the preparation of spaceflight experiments and independent studies. These ground-based facilities serve as cost-effective platforms for gravitational research, and several have been established to provide simulated microgravity conditions for research purposes. Consequently, to overcome limitations, experiments can be conducted on Earth using a 3-D clinostat to simulate microgravity conditions by compensating for the unilateral influence of gravity8. The creation of a simulated microgravity environment can be accomplished by subjecting a biological object to 3-D rotation within a Random Positioning Machine9,10. This study endeavored to use the 3-D clinostat technique to examine the translational effects of simulated microgravity on the growth of Arabidopsis seedling roots.

Ribosome profiling enables precise measurement of protein synthesis at single-nucleotide resolution through deep sequencing of ribosome-protected mRNA fragments, has rapidly emerged as a significant methodological advancement11,12, and these fragments are consequently referred to as ribosome footprints (RFs). This approach has been used to investigate translational dynamics across a range of organisms, including yeast, fruit flies, zebrafish, mice, and humans11–15. The method has also been used in Arabidopsis research for the analysis of photomorphogenesis in plants16,17. The article delved into the simulated microgravity translational response in Arabidopsis, as well as a genome-wide ribosome profiling analysis to investigate the translation regulation of Arabidopsis seedling roots. The ribosome profiling data revealed a variety of mechanisms implicated in translation, demonstrating the diverse tactics used by plants to modulate gene expression in response to stimuli such as light, heat, hydrogen peroxide, and hypoxia16,18. These mechanisms regulated ribosome occupancy to either promote or inhibit translation. Occasionally, frameshifts or stop codon readthroughs may occur. In addition, research has shown that open reading frames upstream (uORFs) could have a significant impact16,19.

Here, we conducted an analysis of ribosome profiling and RNA-seq data obtained from 6-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings exposed to simulated microgravity conditions. Our findings revealed a moderate association between alterations in gene expression at the transcriptional and translational levels in response to the simulated microgravity environment. Additionally, less than one-third of genes displaying differential expression were common to both levels, with a notable proportion of genes demonstrating obviously modified translational efficiencies (TEs) in response to simulated microgravity. The TE of several genes exhibited a twofold difference and was significantly impacted by attributes in their sequences. Furthermore, a notable quantity of uORFs was identified as possessing translational capacity. These uORFs possessed the capability to impede the downstream primary ORFs translation. The study illustrated that plants possessed the capacity to react to simulated microgravity by alterations in dynamic translational changes that functioned independently and in coordination with transcriptional changes.

Results

Characteristics of ribosome profiling analyses

To examine the molecular reaction to simulated microgravity, ribosome profiling and RNA-seq analyses were conducted on Arabidopsis seedling roots subjected to normal growth conditions and simulated microgravity (Fig. 1a). The expression abundance profiles of the Ribo-seq and RNA-seq datasets were largely comparable (Fig. 1b). A total of ~166 million and 154 million ribosome-profiling reads were obtained under normal-growth and simulated-microgravity conditions, respectively (Table S1). Furthermore, RNA-seq yielded roughly 226 million and 238 million high-quality reads for the control and simulated-microgravity libraries, respectively (Table S2).

The abscissa represents log10 FPKM values, and the dotted line clearly distinguishes the transcription and translation abundances of the CK and Col-3D groups.

Transfer RNA (tRNA) recognizes codons on mRNA and transfers the corresponding amino acids during translation. It plays a crucial role in determining protein synthesis and translation speed. The concentration of tRNA has a significant impact on these processes. To avoid the precipitation of tRNA along with the ribosome-nascent peptide chain complex during the precipitation process, we compared the sample data to GenBank and Rfam databases after removing ribosomal RNA using BLAST. The ribosome profiling raw reads underwent initial processing to remove rRNA sequences. Subsequently, samples were aligned to TAIR10 Arabidopsis reference genome, as described in the experimental procedures.

Ribosome profiling in various organisms exhibits common characteristics related to the translation process. These characteristics can be considered indicative of effective ribosome profiling libraries12. To assess the impact of simulated microgravity stress, we conducted a comparison of ribosome profiling data between normal growth and simulated microgravity seedling roots. Our analysis revealed that the read length of RFs were between 32-35 nucleotides in both samples, which is consistent with the previous report and also the expectations. (Fig. 2a)11,16. The observed read length was longer than the 28 nucleotides reported in yeast11,12. The distribution of read density by ribosomes showed an advancement of three nucleotides at a time during translation, leading to the characteristic triplet periodicity12. Strong evidence of a three-nucleotide periodicity was identified when analyzing sequences in normal growth and simulated microgravity seedling roots (Fig. 2b). Meta-gene analysis revealed distinct peaks of read density surrounding the start and stop codons (Fig. 2c), indicating that translation initiation and termination processes may serve as the limiting factors for translation in Arabidopsis seedlings. In addition, an elevated level of normalized read density was detected close to the start of the coding sequence (CDS), extending up to around 200 nucleotides. Tuller et al.20 reported that rare codons slowed down the start of translation at the start of the CDS, so the next part of the sequence could be translated more quickly and efficiently20.

Red, green, and blue square bars indicate the biological replicates. b Three-nucleotide periodicity analysis in the simulated microgravity and normal growth seedling roots at first and last 40 nt of the coding sequence. c Meta-gene analysis of ribosome-protected fragments density along the coding sequence start and stop codon regions in normal growth (blue) and simulated microgravity (orange) seedling roots. d The percentage of RFs reads located in the coding sequence, 5’ untranslated region and 3’ untranslated region in normal growth (blue) and simulated microgravity (orange) seedling roots.

Read density distributions within the first 200 nucleotides of the CDS were found to be similar between normal growth and under simulated microgravity conditions in seedling roots (Fig. 2c). The findings with yeast under hydrogen peroxide stress differed from this. Stress led to more ribosomes at the start of translation. Additionally, small ORFs have been identified in the untranslated regions of various species, including yeast, mammals, and plants21,22. In the seedlings under normal growth, 2.0% and 1.4% of RFs were observed in the 3’ UTRs and 5’ UTRs, respectively, whereas 96.4% of RFs were located in the gene body (Fig. 2d). Under simulated microgravity conditions, the proportions of reads mapped to the gene body, 3’ UTRs and 5’ UTRs were 96.5%, 2.0%, and 1.3%, respectively (Fig. 2d). The comparable proportions of RFs mapping to 3’ UTRs and 5’ UTRs suggest stable translation profiles in these regions, consistent with previous findings in Arabidopsis19,23.

Simulated microgravity affects gene expression both transcriptionally and translationally

It is reported that simulated microgravity affects plant growth and development10,24,25. By the conduct of ribosome profiling coupled with RNA-seq methodologies, we analyzed the impact of simulated microgravity on seedling roots cultivated under normal growth and simulated microgravity conditions.

Our initial study focused on delineating the alterations in gene expression induced by simulated microgravity. At the transcriptional level, we found 356 genes upregulated and 67 downregulated, whereas, at the translational level, 331 genes were upregulated and 49 were downregulated (Fig. 3a). The genes with upregulation expression level surpassed downregulation at both translational and transcriptional levels, suggesting a widespread enhancement in gene expression. It is noteworthy that a considerable proportion of genes within enriched pathways displayed disparate responses at the two levels. As illustrated in Fig. 3b, only under one-third of responsive genes (31.6% upregulated and 20.8% downregulated) were found to be shared at both the transcriptional and translational levels (Fig. 3b), which suggested discordant changes at the two levels.

Simulated microgravity induced both translational and transcriptional responses.

a Simulated microgravity induced 423 differentially expressed genes at the transcriptional level (356 upregulated, 67 downregulated) and 380 differentially expressed genes at the translational level (331 upregulated, 49 downregulated); q ≤ 0.01. b Only 31.6% of upregulated and 20.8% of downregulated genes were shared between the two levels, with ~70% being discordantly regulated—consistent with microgravity-induced cellular reprogramming.

Subsequently, a functional analysis was conducted on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) through Gene Ontology (GO) analysis. A significant overlap in enriched pathways was found at both the transcriptional and translational levels. Specifically, pathways related to response to stimuli, response to stress, wounding, defense response, hormone signaling pathway, and response to oxygen levels, etc. (Fig. S1). In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that genes were enriched at both transcriptional and translational levels in photosynthesis, hormone signaling pathway, plant–pathology interaction, nitrogen metabolism, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, etc. (Fig. S2)26. Further analysis revealed that when Arabidopsis seedlings were exposed to microgravity, the DII-Venus reporter revealed a complete dissipation of the auxin gradient at the root apex, concomitant with a loss of polar localization of PIN proteins27. The Aux/IAA corepressor IAA19 forms heterodimers with ARF7/ARF19 to repress transcription of early auxin-responsive genes, including SAUR, GH3, LBD, and PIN family members28. Integrated omics analyses demonstrated that IAA19 transcripts and proteins were upregulated 29.6- and 18.3-fold, respectively, relative to the 1 g control (Supplementary Data 1). This dramatic increase in IAA19 abundance was associated with reduced polar auxin transport in Arabidopsis under simulated microgravity and may have contributed to the observed defect in root gravitropism. Under microgravity conditions, the calcium-signaling network of Arabidopsis thaliana undergoes substantial reprogramming29. Subcellular localization and functional analyses indicate that the Ca²⁺ sensor CBL1 (Calcineurin B-like protein 1) is predominantly expressed in roots and leaves, where it perceives cytosolic Ca²⁺ transients elicited by abiotic stresses and forms complexes with CIPK kinases to decode the corresponding signals30. The calmodulin-like protein CML38, in contrast, accumulates preferentially in root tips and young leaves, functioning as a Ca²⁺-signaling module in broad-spectrum stress responses31. Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic profiling revealed that microgravity exposure increased CBL1 transcript abundance 2.4-fold and translational output 2.9-fold, whereas CML38 transcripts and protein levels were elevated 3.1- and 4.0-fold, respectively (Supplementary Data 1). These findings suggest that CBL1 and CML38 could be involved in calcium-dependent regulation of root gravitropic responses under microgravity and provide novel molecular insights into plant gravity-sensing mechanisms in space.

To assess the collective pattern at the two distinct levels concurrently, the fold change of gene expression measured by FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) was computed. The findings indicated a moderate correlation between altered expression at the transcriptional and translational levels (Fig. 4). Subsequently, genes were categorized into nine groups based on the FPKM fold change. Subsequent analysis revealed that about 18% of the responsive genes fell into the (classes C and G) concordant groups, which exhibited coordinated regulation either increasing or decreasing to a comparable degree at both levels.

Approximately 82% of responsive genes were found within the other six discordant groups. Further analysis was conducted on genes within four discordantly regulated groups. The classes D and F genes showed regulation without significant changes in transcription. In class D, translationally downregulated genes were enriched in transporter activity and multi-organism process (Fig. S3). In addition, in class F, 1204 upregulated genes were observed to be enriched in toxin activity, immune system, and carbon utilization at the translational level (Fig. S3). Genes within classes B and H exhibited transcriptional regulation without translational alterations at the expression level. Specifically, there were 960 upregulated genes at the transcriptional level in class B, associated with molecular function regulator, transcription regulator activity, and reproductive process (Fig. S3). Class H, on the other hand, featured 536 genes with reduced transcription, showing significant enrichment for molecular function regulator, toxin activity, and multi-organism (Fig. S3).

The influence of simulated microgravity stimuli resulted in notable changes in the translational efficiencies of numerous genes

Translational efficiency (TE) is a metric that directly assesses the effectiveness of RNA utilization and is a significant factor in the process of translation12. Significantly, defense response and photosynthesis have been recognized as prominently enriched pathways in developing Arabidopsis seedlings under a microgravity environment, demonstrating alterations at the mRNA levels32. This changed mRNA level may be related to the altered TE of these pathway genes (Fig. 5a).

a The histogram illustrates the fold change in translation efficiency between seedling roots grown under normal growth and those subjected to the simulated microgravity treatment. b The figure provides a comparative analysis of TE levels in normal and simulated microgravity seedlings, with genes exhibiting significantly increased and decreased TE represented by red and green colors, respectively. A z-score threshold of 2 was utilized for both datasets. Genes with altered translational efficiency were specifically enriched in toxin activity, immune system process, carbon utilization, transporter activity, and multi-organism interaction, indicating that simulated microgravity prioritizes the translation of defense, metabolic, and host–microbe interaction pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We aimed to investigate the likely mechanism of plants' response to simulated microgravity through alterations in TE. Despite observing a high degree of similarity in global mRNA abundance and TE among simulated microgravity seedlings, our analysis revealed 552 genes with decreased TE and 529 genes with increased TE (z-score >2) following simulated microgravity treatment (Fig. 5a). These findings suggested that modulating gene TE may be an effective mechanism in the response of plants to simulated microgravity stimuli. To further evaluate this phenomenon, we conducted a detailed analysis of the data.

Plants demonstrate a wide range of responses to stress. To better understand changes in TE, we analyzed the distribution of genes showing decreased and increased TE across nine predetermined responsive categories. Our findings indicated that modifications in transcriptional and translational abundance influence changes in TE, as illustrated in Fig. S4. Nevertheless, a majority of the genes displaying significantly altered TE were found to be regulated exclusively at either the transcriptional or translational level. For example, genes belonging to groups F and H were solely regulated at one of these levels. Collectively, these groups account for the majority of genes exhibiting a notable increase in TE, as indicated by a z-score above 2. Moreover, genes within groups B and D, which are subject to both transcriptional and translational regulation, may experience a substantial reduction in TE (Fig. S4).

Gene translational efficiencies are influenced by their specific sequence characteristics

Gene sequence features influenced mRNAs associated with polysomes33. The potential impact of typical characteristics (sequence length, normalized minimal free energy (NMFE), and GC content) on TE was evaluated. Our analysis revealed that genes with higher TE tended to have higher GC content, NMFE, and shorter lengths as shown in Fig. 6a–c in CDS region. Conversely, differences in these characteristics between genes with higher and lower TE in 3’ UTRs were minimal. Specifically, only genes in the highest TE group exhibited shorter length and lower NMFE in the 3’ UTRs compared to other groups, as depicted in Fig. S5. Interestingly, for 5’ UTRs, genes with higher TE exhibited a higher NMFE, shorter length, and lower GC content (Fig. S5). The trends observed in the normal growth seedlings were similar to those observed in seedlings subjected to simulated microgravity (Fig. S6).

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test results demonstrated that genes within the concordant group, located in the overlapping regions of Fig. 3b, showed comparable GC content and NMFE values in the CDS to those of genes in the discordant group (Fig. 6d, e).

Widespread upstream open reading frames

Previous findings in yeast subjected to starvation12,34, suggesting a potential role of uORFs in the stress response. These observations highlighted the significance of elements within the 5’ UTRs in modulating cellular responses to stress, particularly through the regulation of uORFs. It is widely recognized that uORFs, which are small ORFs situated in the 5’ UTRs, can inhibit the translation of main ORFs by reinitiation and leaky scanning. uORFs have been reported in various species22. This study facilitated the comprehensive identification of uORFs in Arabidopsis seedling roots at a genome-wide level. To identify genes containing uORFs accurately, our study focused on all Arabidopsis genes. We revealed that ~20% of these genes, totaling 4023 genes, were predicted to contain uORFs. The Fig. S7 indicates the length distribution of all predicted uORFs, statistics for the number of predicted uORFs per gene, and the length distribution of translated uORFs in the normal growth and simulated microgravity seedling roots.

The translation of the main ORFs was repressed by the translated upstream ORF

To demonstrate the distinctions between untranslated and translated uORFs, three parameters related to the mORF reinitiation (including uORF length, 5’ UTRs length, and NMFE) were compared22. Our results suggested that translated uORFs exhibited shorter length compared to untranslated uORFs (Fig. 7a). Conversely, the longer 5’ UTRs genes more likely contribute to translated uORFs (Fig. 7b). Upon further examination of the impact of 5’ UTRs length, it was observed that the distance from the uORF to the transcription start site (TSS) in translated uORFs were obviously longer and the distance from the uORF to the start codon of the mORFs in translated uORFs were obviously shorter (Fig. 7c–e).

The Kozak consensus sequence, denoted as “GCCA/GCCAUGG,” is a conserved motif found in the mRNA of certain eukaryotes35. ORFs containing Kozak sequences exhibit a greater likelihood of translation and increased translation efficiency. Statistical analysis should be conducted on all small open reading frames (sORFs) that contain Kozak sequence features. Motif analysis was conducted in the vicinity of the start codon for all full-length gene sequences. The sequences flanking the start codon in the genes exhibited a high degree of similarity to the Kozak sequence. Higher GC content was observed in the translated uORFs flanking sequence, as depicted in Fig. 7f.

To examine the likely impact of translated uORFs on the downstream mORFs translation, we analyzed three groups TE: those predicted uORFs lacking translation, those with a single translated uORF, and those with multiple translated uORFs. Translated uORFs have been shown to significantly reduce the TE of corresponding genes, with this effect becoming more pronounced as the number of translated uORFs increases (Fig. 7g). Previous studies have indicated that uORFs can be influenced by sugar, light, and pathogenic infections16,36. Consequently, we performed an examination of the translation efficiency (TE) of upstream open reading frames (uORFs) in seedlings cultivated under simulated microgravity conditions. Our findings indicated that the TE of uORFs exhibited comparable levels in both normal growth and simulated microgravity environments (Fig. 7h).

Discussion

The combined effects of transcriptional and translational responses to simulated microgravity

Through the acquisition of ribosomal profiles and RNA-seq data, our study enabled the examination of global gene expression alterations at the translational level following exposure to simulated microgravity and investigated the interplay between translational and transcriptional responses (Supplementary Data 2). While clinostat rotation induces low-level mechanical stimuli, control samples were maintained under identical environmental conditions with the sole exception of the absence of rotation. Thus, any rotational side effects are restricted to the simulated microgravity group, and this comparison effectively isolates gravity-related responses from general growth artefacts. Our findings revealed a correlation among the various changes in transcriptional and translational gene expression induced by simulated microgravity.

In the study, we observed that more than two-thirds of the simulated microgravity response genes exhibited inconsistent changes in their transcription or translation. This observation suggested independent simulated microgravity responses occurring at the two levels. Specifically, hundreds of genes were found to be regulated solely at the translational level. This translational response, which does not necessitate the synthesis of new mRNA, is recognized for its rapid environmental adaptation capabilities37,38. While translation is viewed traditionally as having a fine-tuning function34, our findings highlighted the significance of the independent role it plays in the simulated microgravity response. In contrast, a total of 1496 class B and H genes were identified as being exclusively regulated at the transcriptional level, without observable alterations in translation. These genes played crucial roles in fundamental physiological processes. In a study involving yeast treated with sodium chloride, it was observed that mRNA levels at a specific time point exhibited a stronger correlation with protein levels at a subsequent time point, suggesting a delay in protein expression following the transcriptional activation of stress response genes39.

Hence, we proposed that alterations in mRNA levels of class B and H genes may serve as predictive markers for subsequent changes in translation (Fig. 4). Among these genes analyzed, only an elevation in class B transcription was noted, suggesting that plants may modulate mRNA abundance to offset reduced TE and sustain essential cellular functions. An alternative interpretation is that heightened mRNA levels following stress relief could provide a reservoir for expedited translation in the future40.

Genes sequence characteristics can influence translation efficiency

In addition to encoding protein functions, genome sequences also harbor a significant amount of information related to expression regulation. Our results revealed that genes exhibiting varying TEs exhibited distinct sequence characteristics, particularly within the CDS of normal growth and simulated microgravity seedling roots (Fig. 6). In our study, genes with higher TE tended to have 5’ UTRs with lower GC content and higher NMFE. Furthermore, high-TE genes exhibited significantly shorter coding sequences with elevated GC content and higher NMFE, a signature that remained robust across both control and microgravity conditions. In Arabidopsis, shorter 5’ UTRs length and lower GC content correlate positively with translation efficiency under normal conditions, whereas shortening CDS length significantly promote translation efficiency during recovery from mild dehydration stress41. Tea plants exhibited similar patterns, with high-TE genes possessing shorter, GC-rich coding sequences and shorter 5’ UTRs with increased NMFE, and this architecture was conserved between etiolated and green leaves42. Under control, drought, or heat stress conditions, high-TE genes in potato likewise exhibited shorter coding sequences with lower GC content and higher NMFE, whereas their 5’ UTRs were lengthened, GC-poor, and exhibited elevated NMFE43. Rice subjected to an abrupt transition from drought to flooding, with or without subsequent nitrogen application, exhibited the same trade-offs between CDS and 5’ UTRs, as evidenced by shorter coding sequences with reduced GC content and higher NMFE and longer 5’ UTRs with lower GC content and higher NMFE44. In rainbow trout, high-TE genes shortened the CDS while elevating NMFE, and concurrently shortened the 5’ UTRs while increasing its GC content under both control and heat shock conditions45. In yak testis, high-TE genes harbored shorter coding sequences with lower GC content and higher NMFE; the same CDS length reduction was observed in cattle-yak testis, although GC content rose and NMFE declined46. Collectively, CDS shortening appears to represent among the most evolutionarily conserved correlates of high TE, whereas directional shifts in GC content and NMFE may be context-dependent, with variations observed across species, tissue types, and environmental conditions. The 5’ UTRs uORF is a well-documented cis-regulatory element known for its translation-inhibitory function36. Additionally, the internal ribosome entry site located in the 5’ UTRs was another regulatory element that facilitated gene translation under simulated microgravity conditions through a cap-independent mechanism, as demonstrated in previous research47.

Furthermore, it was discovered that the translation of multiple Arabidopsis genes in the 5’ UTRs containing the TAGGTTT motif was augmented following exposure to light16. Experimental findings also indicate that these motifs can react to light by upregulating downstream gene expression. In addition, codon usage, poly(A) tail length, and potential secondary structures have been implicated in translational responses20. The underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be discovered.

Various translation repression mechanisms are mediated by upstream ORFs

Previous research has indicated that multiple uORFs in mammals had a greater inhibitory effect on protein accumulation compared to transcripts with one or no uORFs48. The translation repression mechanisms of uORFs in plants and mammals appeared to share similarities and can be categorized into two distinct groups: one involved the competition between uORFs and downstream mORFs for translation initiation complexes, and the other involved the interference with the restart of mORF translation. The initiation codon of uORFs competes vigorously with the initiation codon of mORFs for translation initiation complexes, and accurate recognition of the uORF initiation codon is essential for uORF-mediated inhibition22. We compared the nucleotide frequencies in the flanking sequences surrounding the start codons of translated and untranslated uORFs. Our results show that the flanking sequences of translated uORFs exhibit a significantly higher GC content. In addition, the frequency of guanine at position +4 is markedly elevated in translated uORFs relative to their untranslated counterparts (Fig. 7f).

Hence, the initiation codon of uORFs can either be disregarded or acknowledged, potentially resulting in scan leakage and translation of mORFs. Conversely, the start codon of uORFs recognition can impede the translation elongation and termination, hindering the resumption of mORFs48. The uORFs characterized by greater length and shorter proximity are deemed disadvantageous for the initiation of mORF translation, thus suggesting that inhibition may be more efficacious22.

In this study, it was observed that the translated uORF exhibited a shorter distance from the CDS compared to the untranslated uORF, as depicted in Fig. 7a–e. The findings supported the view that the length and sequence background of the uORF were factors influencing its function in plants. Specifically, research conducted by49 demonstrated that the seven peptides encoded by uORFs, known as gDcADC8, found in carnations, possessed the ability to inhibit the translation of mORFs. Additionally, recent findings by Ebina et al.50 have revealed that frameshift mutants containing conserved peptide uORFs have been shown to inhibit mORF translation in Arabidopsis50. TE was defined as the ratio of ribosome-protected fragment FPKM to RNA-seq FPKM. Given that transcriptomic profiling quantifies full-length transcripts rather than sub-regional fragments, region-specific TE calculations necessarily use full-transcript expression values as the baseline denominator. Thus, uORF TE was calculated as (uORF-translated FPKM)/(full-transcript FPKM). Analysis of translation accumulation curves revealed no significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 7h). Furthermore, studies have indicated that peptides derived from uORFs function in a cis-acting manner to suppress the translation of mORFs. The detailed underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be discovered in plants in the future. After internal review, we concluded that the current sample size and data structure are insufficient for robust multivariable regression, forcing multiple covariates into the model risks overfitting. Consequently, no additional confounder-adjusted analyses were added in this study. The association between uORF–mORF distance and reinitiation efficiency is purely a statistical inference derived from publicly available ribosome-profiling data and has not been corroborated by mutant or reporter-gene assays. We plan to validate these predictions with targeted small-peptide sequencing in future work.

Methods

Plant growth conditions and treatment

Surface-sterilized Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) seeds were sown on Petri dishes containing half-strength Murashige and Skoog medium and stratified at 4 °C for 3 days. After stratification, the Petri dishes containing the seeds were placed in a culture room with an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1 °C and 16 h light at 8000 lx. After 3 days of germination and initial growth on the plates, the seedlings were transferred to either normal growth conditions or simulated-microgravity treatment. A 3D clinostat (SM-31 tow-axis driving clinostat) was designed and constructed for the simulated weightlessness (made by Center for Space Science and Applied Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences, 2005). The main power of rotation was provided by two geared stepping motors, and the sample stage was three-dimensionally rotated by changing the rate and direction of rotation at random from 1 to -1 (reverse direction) rpm every 1 min. The light source moved synchronously with the plants during gravistimulation51. The ground 1 g control plates were placed in the same room as the 3D clinostat and grown on matched stationary hardware. Plants grown in both normal conditions and under simulated microgravity were incubated under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of 100 µmol m−2 s−1 and 60% relative humidity at 22 °C for 6 days. The Arabidopsis seedling root tips were then harvested and stored at −80 °C.

RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Integrity was verified on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN ≥ 7) and by RNase-free agarose-gel electrophoresis. Poly(A) mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT) beads and fragmented. Strand-specific libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #7530) according to the supplier’s protocol: end-repair, dA-tailing, adapter ligation, 1.0× AMPure XP size selection, and 12-cycle PCR enrichment. Final indexed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (2 × 150 bp paired-end) at Gene Denovo Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). Raw reads were adapter/quality-trimmed (fastp) and mapped to TAIR10 with HISAT2. Gene counts were obtained using feature Counts; only uniquely mapped, high-quality (MAPQ ≥ 10) reads were retained.

Recovery of ribosomal footprints

The ribosomal profiling technique was conducted following the methodology outlined in a previous study11, with some adjustments. Tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to powder in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle, then dissolved in 400 μL of lysis buffer. The resuspended extracts in lysis buffer were transferred to new microtubes, pipetted several times, and incubated on ice for 10 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 20,000× g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. To generate ribosomal footprints (RFs), 10 µL of RNase I (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) and 6 µL of DNase I (NEB) were added to 400 µL of lysate, which was then incubated for 45 min at room temperature with gentle mixing on a Nutator mixer. The nuclease digestion was halted by the addition of 10 µL of SUPERase·In RNase inhibitor (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). Following this, 100 µL of the digested RFs were introduced to the column and centrifuged at 600×g for 2 min. Subsequently, 10 μL of 10% (wt/vol) SDS was incorporated into the elution, and RFs with a size exceeding 17 nt were isolated by using an RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research; R1017)52. Subsequently, short (50–80 bases) antisense DNA probes complementary to rRNA sequences were added to the solution containing RFs, then RNase H and DNase I enzymes were used to degrade the rRNA and any remaining DNA probes. The RFs were then subjected to additional purification using magnetic beads from Vazyme (Nanjing, China).

Library construction and sequencing

Following the acquisition of RFs, ribo-seq libraries were constructed using NEBNext® Multiple Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina® (catalog no. E7300S, E7300L; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). This involved the addition of adapters to both ends of the RFs, followed by reverse transcription and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. The 140–160 bp size PCR products were subjected to an enrichment process to generate a cDNA library, which was then sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq X10 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Translational efficiency (TE) calculation

Translational efficiency (TE) is the ratio of translating mRNAs to the total mRNAs of a gene. The formula is shown as follows:

TE = (FPKM in Ribo-seq) / (FPKM in RNA-seq).

TE of genes was calculated and compared between samples and groups12.

Reads filtering

Reads were subjected to quality filtering by removing those with over 50% low-quality bases or over 10% N bases. Additionally, adapter sequences were trimmed, and reads falling within the 20–40 bp range were retained for further analysis. Subsequently, short reads were aligned to the ribosome RNA (rRNA) database, GenBank, and the Rfam database using Bowtie2. Reads that aligned to rRNAs, tRNAs, snRNAs, snoRNAs, and miRNAs were subsequently excluded from the dataset.

Reference genome alignment

Reads were aligned to the genome using STAR with the 2-pass setting enabled, followed by the exclusion of reads mapping to noncoding RNAs. The remaining reads were then categorized into different genomic features based on their alignment position.

Three-nucleotide periodicity analysis

Three-nucleotide periodicity analysis was conducted using the riboWaltz R package53. Reads with a specific length (e.g., 30 nt) were assigned to frames 1, 2, or 3 based on their position in the 5′ ends in transcripts of protein-coding genes. Subsequently, the proportion of total reading frames in frames 1, 2, and 3 was calculated across all protein-coding genes.

Identification of accordantly and discordantly regulated genes

We classified genes into nine categories according to both the horizontal and vertical coordinates are divided based on the threshold of log2FC > 1 or log2FC < -1, without significance. Genes upregulated or downregulated simultaneously at transcriptional and translational levels were defined as accordantly regulated genes. Other categories, except for the category without change at two levels, were defined as discordantly regulated genes. The fold changes at transcriptional and translational levels were used to calculate the Pairwise Pearson correlation. IGV (Version 2.3.55) was used to show the expression changes at transcriptional and translational levels simultaneously.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment

To identify differentially translated genes across sample groups, the DESeq254 was used (and by edgeR between two samples). Genes with fold change ≥2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 in a comparison were considered as significant DTGs. Initially, all DTGs were associated with corresponding GO terms within the GO database. GO terms that satisfied this criterion were considered significantly enriched in DTGs, providing insight into the primary biological functions of these genes.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Genes usually interact with each other to play roles in certain biological functions. Pathway-based analysis helps to further understand genes' biological functions. KEGG55 is the major public pathway-related database. Pathway enrichment analysis identified significantly enriched metabolic pathways or signal transduction pathways in DTGs compared with the whole genome background. The calculating formula is the same as that in GO analysis.

Here N is the number of all genes that with KEGG annotation, n is the number of DTGs in N, M is the number of all genes annotated to specific pathways, and m is the number of DTGs in M. The calculated P value was gone through FDR Correction, taking FDR ≤ 0.05 as a threshold. Pathways meeting this condition were defined as significantly enriched pathways in DTGs.

Identification of ORFs

The longest transcript from each protein-coding gene was identified and subsequently analyzed for ORFs within noncoding regions, specifically the 5’ UTRs and 3’ UTRs of established protein-coding genes. Non-canonical ORFs, ranging in size from 60 to 450 nucleotides and initiated by the start codon ATG, were extracted, encompassing both uORFs and downstream ORFs (dORFs). All uORFs were identified solely from publicly available ribosome-profiling data33,56–58, no additional proteomic or in vitro translation experiments were performed.

Translation quantification for ORF

The reads aligned to classical noncoding RNAs were excluded from the analysis. Subsequently, the remaining RFs were used to assess the TE of all ORFs, encompassing mORF of protein-coding genes, uORFs, and dORFs. The read counts within the ORFs were tallied, and the TE was normalized using the FPKM method. The bioinformatic pipeline followed established guidelines and is intended to predict potentially translated uORFs, not to assert that every identified uORF is indeed translated.

Differentially translated ORF analysis

The DESeq2 package was used to detect differentially transcribed ORFs among various sample groups. ORFs exhibiting a fold change of ≥2 and an FDR of <0.05 in a given comparison were deemed to be statistically significant. DESeq2 was exclusively used for differential expression analysis between the transcriptome and translatome datasets, while differences in translation efficiency were specifically assessed using RiboDiff.

Evaluating the coding potential of non-canonical ORFs

Two methods, including Ribosome Release Score (RRS) and ORFscore, were used to assess the coding potential of non-canonical ORFs. ORFscore was used to evaluate the coding potential of non-canonical ORFs. Briefly, reads mapping to each position within the ORF were enumerated, excluding the first and last coding codons. Positions with artifactual enrichment were filtered by masking the most abundant site if its read count exceeded 70% of total ORF reads56. RRS was calculated as the ratio of ribosome density between the CDS and 3’ UTRs, divided by the corresponding RNA density ratio to control for annotation artifacts57. We additionally used the Fickett score and hexamer score from the CPAT tool58 to assess the coding potential of different ORFs.

Sequence feature analysis

The lengths and GC content of both translated and untranslated ORFs were quantified, followed by the determination of secondary structure stability using the NMFE algorithm. This stability metric was computed through ViennaRNA RNAfold and subsequently normalized59. P values were derived from a Student’s t test comparing translated and untranslated ORFs. The presence of the Kozak consensus sequence, GCCA/GCCAUGG, surrounding the start codon was found to be crucial for start codon recognition and translation initiation60,61. Motif analysis around start codons of all ORFs was conducted using MEME, with significance assessed using a chi-squared test.

The influence of uORFs on mORFs

The potential impact of uORF sequence and expression on mORF translation was investigated. Scatter plots were generated to analyze the differences between uORFs and downstream mORFs in each comparison group (with FPKM ≥ 1 in at least one sample). In addition, the influence of the Kozak sequence and the distance between uORFs and mORFs on the translation of mORFs was examined.

Annotation of small ORFs

Small ORFs (sORFs, average FPKM ≥ 1) were annotated with sORF databases (PsORF62 and SORFS.ORG63,64) to identify their potential for translation. The on-canonical ORFs were also analyzed with the Pfam database to predict potential domains.

Statistics and reproducibility

R v.4.1.2 (https://www.r-project.org) was used for all statistical computations. Significance between two groups was assessed by Student’s t test (α = 0.05), and differences among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA. Effect size and statistical analysis for the data shown in figures, are available in Supplementary Data 3. For each experimental condition, three independent biological replicates were collected and subjected to RNA-seq/Ribo-seq analysis.
