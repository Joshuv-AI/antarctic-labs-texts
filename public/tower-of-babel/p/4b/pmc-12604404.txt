Exploring the relationships between academic burnout and well-being in adolescents: the mediating role of optimism
Authors: Pablo Molina Moreno, María del Mar Molero Jurado, María del Carmen Pérez-Fuentes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examines the impact of academic burnout on adolescents’ psychological and social well-being, emphasizing the role of optimism as a mediator in this relationship. The aim is to understand how burnout influences these dimensions to inform targeted interventions that promote resilience and mental health in educational contexts.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 1,092 adolescent students (53.5% female, aged 12–17 years, M = 14.5, SD = 1.11) from six schools in Almería, Spain. Participants were selected through a cluster-based random sampling method and completed the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Student Survey, Ryff’s Psychological Well-Being Scale, Keyes Social Well-Being Scale, and the Optimism-Pesimism Questionnaire. Descriptive, correlational, and mediation analyses were performed using SPSS and the PROCESS macro.ResultsHigh levels of academic burnout were associated with significantly lower psychological and social well-being, as well as reduced optimism. Optimism was found to partially mediate the relationship between academic burnout and well-being, accounting for its negative associations with both psychological and social dimensions.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the role of optimism as a mediator in the relationship between academic burnout and adolescents’ well-being. The study underscores the need for intervention strategies that focus on promoting optimism to reduce burnout and enhance mental health in educational settings. Future research should further explore these dynamics across different educational stages and cultural contexts.Keywords: Optimism, Academic burnout, Psychological Well-Being, Social Well-Being, Adolescents

Background

Adolescence is marked by major biological changes and complex social influences that, along with various risk and protective factors, shape behaviors with long-term health implications [1]. Pubertal changes facilitate the development of new skills and decision-making capacities, aiding social adaptation. Therefore, promoting adolescent health globally requires strengthening peer interactions in school settings and addressing contextual risk and protective factors [2].

Well-being is a lifelong pursuit and essential for positive development, extensively examined across diverse theoretical frameworks [3, 4], including Ryff’s multidimensional model of positive psychological functioning [5, 6], built on the integration of clinical, developmental, existential, and humanistic perspectives. The model comprises six dimensions of well-being: purpose in life, personal growth, positive relations with others, environmental mastery, self-acceptance, and autonomy. Ryff and Singer [7] advocated for a eudaimonic perspective on well-being, emphasizing the realization of personal potential and alignment with one’s values, in contrast to hedonic models focused on pleasure and the absence of discomfort. Eudaimonic well-being provides a rich framework within which to understand well-being outcomes, and over the past decades, Ryff’s model has undergone extensive validation across diverse cultural settings and populations [8], García et al., 2023 [9]; Lindfors et al., [10–12].

Adolescent well-being has been recognized as a global priority within the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals International, and several initiatives have emphasized the need for coordinated efforts to address it [13]. Promoting well-being during this developmental stage is of critical importance, given that low mood can negatively impact cognition, emotional functioning, behavior, and physical health [14]. Additional recent work with adolescents has has further explored a range of factors linked to well-being, including resilience [15], health-related fears [16], as well as the role of religiosity and spiritual intelligence [17], and the relationships between problematic internet use, social anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms [18] with well-being. Family-related variables such as functionality [19], parent–child triangulation [20], and perceived social support [21] have also been identified as key correlates of emotional adjustment. Other variables such as personal values [22, 23], growth mindsets [24], and self-compassion [25] have also been addressed in studies about adolescent well-being. Intervention studies have assessed programs aimed at promoting adolescent well-being [26], and school-based programs have been proven to be useful to address adolescent well-being [27, 28]. Additional work has addressed the roles of poverty stigma [29], marginalization [30], exposure to natural disasters [31], and socioemotional coping strategies in school-based racial discrimination [32]. These findings reflect the expanding scope of variables under investigation in adolescent well-being research and its cross-sectional validity. In addition, psychological well-being measures have demonstrated strong psychometric properties [33], and its applicability in youth populations has been reinforced by studies that confirm its theoretical and empirical suitability [34].

Recent research on adolescent psychological and social well-being has expanded considerably, integrating a diverse range of constructs and variables across different thematic areas. Psychological and social development during adolescence is critical for long-term well-being, as it supports identity formation, social competence, and positive interpersonal relationships [35]. Longitudinal evidence confirms a bidirectional relationship between psychological well-being and social competence, highlighting their developmental stability and the importance of fostering both to promote healthy trajectories [36].

According to Keyes [37], social well-being is a multidimensional construct encompassing social integration, acceptance, contribution, actualization, and coherence. These dimensions reflect individuals’ capacity to engage meaningfully with their social environment and derive a sense of belonging and purpose. Prior research suggests that higher levels of social well-being are linked to positive psychological traits and better mental health, whereas lower levels are associated with negative affect and increased psychological distress [38]. Similarly, higher perceived social support is associated with enhanced adjustment, life satisfaction, and emotional well-being [39]. Consistent with this, recent studies underscore the importance of promoting social well-being in high school students as a means of enhancing overall mental health [40].

Several factors can impact adolescent well-being within the academic environment, including school dropout [41] and cybervictimization [42]. In recent years, authors have increasingly focused on “academic burnout.” Maslach et al. [43] were pioneers in studying this phenomenon, defining it as a psychological syndrome resulting from prolonged exposure to stress in the workplace. Although initially workplace-oriented, this concept has been adapted to academic contexts, where burnout has become prevalent among adolescents. For instance, Pérez-Fuentes et al. (2020) validated an instrument to assess this syndrome in university students, focusing on dimensions such as emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and academic efficacy.

Given the educational environment’s role in promoting students’ well-being and emotional regulation [45], academic burnout has gained attention as a multidimensional phenomenon with adverse effects on adolescent mental health. Conceptualized as a dynamic process that intensifies during educational transitions, burnout has been strongly linked to reduced psychological well-being [46]. Emerging evidence also highlights the importance of socioemotional competencies in relation to these effects [47]. More recently, Angelini et al. [48] found that parental interference in academic decision-making heightens school-related anxiety, which fully mediates its impact on academic exhaustion and depressive symptoms, increasing their vulnerability to burnout.

Prior research consistently shows that students experiencing high levels of academic burnout report significantly lower psychological well-being, social well-being, and optimism compared to their peers with lower burnout [49–52].

Optimism has emerged as a key psychological resource in this context, as it is negatively associated with academic burnout, particularly with emotional exhaustion [53, 54]. Optimism is closely linked to key well-being indicators in adolescence, such as psychological adjustment, life satisfaction, and positive emotions, which explains its growing relevance in youth research. For instance, Vizoso et al. [52] identified optimism as a negative predictor of emotional exhaustion and, when combined with adaptive coping strategies, burnout, while also being associated with enhanced academic performance. Similarly, fostering optimism through resilience-building and stress management has been associated with overall well-being [55, 56]. These findings position optimism as a modifiable psychological resource that can be strengthened through intervention. Supporting this view, Shoshani and Steinmetz [57] demonstrated the effectiveness of a school-based positive psychology program in promoting well-being and reducing distress. Other studies have explored the broader impact of optimism on students’ academic functioning and emotional health. For example, Azila-Gbettor et al. [58] found that optimism supports academic engagement and self-efficacy and, complementing this, Arnold et al. [59] reported that optimism moderates the relationship between academic stress and depressive symptoms, further highlighting its role as a psychological resource in educational contexts.

Despite these insights, research examining the mediating role of optimism between academic burnout and both psychological and social well-being in high school students remains limited [60–62]. Therefore, promoting psychological and social well-being in adolescents is crucial, and initiatives aimed at fostering the development of positive competencies, such as optimism, are vital for enhancing peer interactions and coexistence in the school environment.

Aims

The present research aims to explore how academic burnout, a chronic stress condition linked to academic demands, is associated with lower levels of psychological and social well-being of high school students. By including optimism as a mediating variable, the study aims to identify potential mechanisms through which academic burnout is associated with these dimensions of well-being. This understanding could be valuable in the development of interventions that promote resilience and well-being within educational settings. Thus, the objective is to analyze the mediating role of optimism in the relationship between academic burnout and psychological and social well-being among adolescent students. To address this objective, the study tests the following hypotheses (see Fig. 1):

Hypothesized mediation model

H1: There is a negative correlation between academic burnout and the variables of psychological well-being, social well-being and optimism in high school students.

H2: High school students with high academic burnout have significantly lower levels of psychological well-being, social well-being, and optimism compared to students with low academic burnout.

H3: Optimism mediates the relationship between academic burnout and psychological well-being in high school students, so that the negative effect of academic burnout on psychological well-being will decrease when optimism is controlled.

H4: Optimism mediates the relationship between academic burnout and social well-being in high school students, so that the negative effect of academic burnout on social well-being will decrease when optimism is controlled.

Methods

Participants

The present study was conducted in six secondary schools in the province of Almería (Spain), with students in the 2nd, 3rd and 4th years of Compulsory Secondary Education. The participants ranged in age from 12 to 17 years (M = 14.15, SD = 1.11). Initially, the sample included 1,280 adolescents; however, 188 were excluded after data processing due to incomplete attendance to sessions, language difficulties, or random test responses, resulting in a final valid questionnaire rate of 87.01% and a response rate of 98.05% (see Fig. 2). The final sample consisted of 1,092 students, all originally from Spain. Of these, 53.44% (n = 584) were female (Mage = 14.14, SD = 1.04) and 46.56% (n = 508) were male (Mage = 14.17, SD = 1.17). The sample distribution by academic year of secondary education was as follows: 2nd year (n = 400; 194 males, 206 females), 3rd year (n = 342; 143 males, 199 females), and 4th year (n = 350; 171 males, 179 females).

Flowchart of the final sample selection process

To ensure the representativeness of the sample, an estimation was conducted based on a population of 38,180 adolescents aged 12 to 16 years from the province of Almería (Spain). Using a 95% confidence level and a 3% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 1,039 participants. The final sample recruited for the study comprised 1,092 students, thus meeting criteria for population representativeness. Moreover, this sample size was sufficient in terms of statistical power, given that mediation analyses were conducted using a bootstrap approach with coefficients estimated from 5,000 bootstrap samples. The standardized coefficients for indirect effects ranged from − 0.13 to − 0.31 in the psychological well-being model, and from − 0.04 to − 0.20 in the social well-being model (with the exception of social acceptance, whose indirect effect was not statistically significant). Based on the simulations by Fritz and MacKinnon [63], detecting small indirect effects (a × b ≈ 0.02) with a statistical power of 0.80 [64] using bootstrap methods would require a sample size between 462 and 558 participants. Therefore, considering our sample exceeded 1,000 participants and observed indirect effects were greater than 0.04, it can be reasonably inferred that the statistical power surpassed the 80% threshold for most effects examined.

Measures

Academic burnout

Maslach Burnout Inventory – Student Survey (MBI-SS; Maslach et al., [43]; validated in Spanish adolescent students by (Pérez-Fuentes et al., 2020) [44]. This study employed the Spanish-adapted version of the Maslach and Jackson [65, 66] Burnout Scale for adolescents, which demonstrated good reliability (ω = 0.86). The scale is designed to assess students’ feelings and attitudes toward their academic activities by measuring burnout based on its frequency and intensity. It consists of 12 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (from 0, “Never”, to 6, “Always/Every day”). The scale is divided into three subscales: (1) Emotional exhaustion (ω = 0.83), with items such as “I feel emotionally drained by my studies’’; (2) Cynicism (ω = 0.85), including items like ‘’I have become less enthusiastic about my studies’’; and (3) Academic efficacy (ω = 0.81), featuring items such as ‘’In my opinion, I am a good student’’.

Psychological well-being

Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWB; Ryff [5],; Spanish adaptation and validation by Díaz et al., 2006). Based on the Ryff model of eudaimonic well-being that encompasses six separate dimensions, this study used a Spanish-adapted version, which demonstrated a reliability of ω = 0.92. The scale consists of 39 items rated on a 6-point scale (from “Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree”). Illustrative items are provided for each dimension: (1) Self-Acceptance (ω = 0.84), measured through six items such as “In general, I feel confident and positive about myself”; (2) Positive Relationships (ω = 0.76), assessed through six items like “I don’t have many people who want to listen to me when I need to talk”; (3) Autonomy (ω = 0.70), measured with eight items, e.g., “I tend to worry about what other people think of me”; (4) Environmental Mastery (ω = 0.65), assessed through six items like “In general, I feel that I am responsible for the situation in which I live”; (5) Personal Growth (ω = 0.63), using seven items, e.g., “I find it difficult to try to make major improvements or changes in my life”; and (6) Purpose in Life (ω = 0.80), measured with six items, such as “I am not clear about what I am trying to achieve in life”. This instrument evaluates psychological well-being across multiple domains, with items such as “Are important decisions made together as a family?”.

Social well-being

Keyes Social Well-Being Scale (SWB; Keyes [37],; Spanish adaptation and validation by Blanco & Díaz [67],. Confirmatory factor analyses conducted by these authors supported the five-factor model of social well-being. This scale demonstrated good reliability for this study (ω = 0.86). The scale consists of 25 items designed to evaluate individuals’ perceptions of various aspects of their social environment that contribute to psychological well-being. It encompasses five specific dimensions of social well-being: (1) Social Integration (ω = 0.72), assessed by five items such as “I feel that I am an important part of my community”; (2) Social Acceptance (ω = 0.85), with six items like “I think people are not to be trusted”; (3) Social Contribution (ω = 0.73), measured by five items such as “I think I can contribute something to the world”; (4) Social Actualization (ω = 0.66), assessed by five items like “I see that society is continuously developing”; and (5) Social Coherence (ω = 0.68), measured by four items such as “The world is too complex for me”. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), providing a comprehensive assessment of the extent to which individuals perceive these aspects in their social environment.

Optimism

Optimism–Pessimism Questionnaire [68]. It demonstrated a reliability of ω = 0.78 and consists of 9 items, such as “I have confidence in the future”. It has a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”). The overall score is calculated by summing the responses across all items, providing a comprehensive measure of the adolescent’s level of optimism.

Sociodemographic information

A specific ad hoc questionnaire was designed to collect sociodemographic information from the students, including age, sex, educational level, and country of origin. In addition, the survey explored other variables related to the school environment, academic performance, the presence of violent behaviors, and personal interactions with key figures in their immediate environment, such as parents, legal guardians, teachers, and peers.

Procedure

Several Compulsory Secondary Education centers in the province of Almería were randomly selected and contacted. Initial communication was established with the individuals responsible for each center, either the administration department or school office. Ultimately, six schools agreed to participate, and data collection took place during the 2023/24 academic year.

During the initial contact with the secondary schools, the key details of the research and its specific objectives were shared with those in charge, along with all the necessary information to facilitate data collection. They were also provided with the questionnaire booklet that would later be administered to the students.

The schedules were organized based on each school’s availability, allocating two hours per group per week over a period of two weeks to minimize fatigue and enhance performance. The questionnaire included a designated stopping point labeled “End of session 1” to guide students on where to pause. After obtaining permission from the schools and informed consent from parents, data collection started. To maintain anonymity and ensure continuity between sessions, students created an anonymous personal code using an easy-to-remember pattern, which they recorded on the first page of the booklet at the initial session.

The sessions began with an explanation of the study’s objectives and detailed instructions for completing the questionnaires individually. Students were assured that their privacy would be protected through confidential statistical processing, and it was emphasized that participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential. No costs were incurred for participation, and no financial compensation was provided. The research was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Almería (reference: UALBIO2020/046).

Data analysis

This cross-sectional study employed a quantitative, descriptive, and correlational approach. Data were analyzed and processed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29 (IBM Corp., 2023) [69]. To assess the reliability of the data collection instruments, McDonald’s omega coefficient (1999) [70] was calculated, following the guidelines recommended by Ventura-León & Caycho [71].

First, correlational and descriptive analyses of the study variables were conducted. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationships between variables. To interpret the magnitude of the correlations, Cohen’s (1988) guidelines were followed, where: rxy < 0.3 weak correlation, 0.3 ≤ rxy < 0.5 moderate correlation, 0.5 ≤ rxy strong correlation.

To identify different profiles based on the scores in the dimensions of academic burnout, a two-stage cluster analysis was performed. Once the groups or clusters were identified, a comparative analysis of means for optimism and psychological and social well-being was conducted using Student’s t-test for independent samples, and Cohen’s d statistic (1988) to determine the effect size of the observed differences.

Finally, simple mediation analyses were conducted using the level of academic burnout (low/high), as determined by the clusters, as the predictor variable, and optimism as the mediating variable. The response variables were the dimensions of psychological well-being and social well-being, respectively. Mediation models were computed using the PROCESS macro for SPSS [72], applying the bootstrapping technique with estimated coefficients from 5000 bootstrap samples.

Negative correlations are found between the variables of emotional exhaustion and cynicism (dimensions of academic burnout) and all dimensions of psychological well-being: self-acceptance, positive relationships, autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, and purpose in life. In contrast, academic efficacy (another dimension of academic burnout) showed positive correlations with each of these dimensions of psychological well-being.

In all cases, except for the autonomy dimension, both the direct and indirect effects are significant, indicating that high levels of academic burnout can negatively impact psychological well-being. These effects, however, can be partially mitigated through the mediating role of optimism.

In the case of social acceptance, 3.49% of the effect is mediated (p = 0.281), while 96.51% is direct and significant. For social contribution, 32.70% of the effect is mediated, and 67.30% is direct. For social actualization, 10.60% of the effect is mediated, while 89.40% is direct. Lastly, in the social coherence dimension, 11.90% of the effect is mediated, and 88.10% is direct.

In all cases, except for social acceptance, the indirect effects are significant. These results suggest that high levels of academic burnout can negatively impact social well-being, highlighting the relevance of optimism as a key mediator in these relationships.

Discussion

The purpose of this research was to analyze the mediating role of optimism in the relationship between academic burnout and psychological and social well-being among high school students. Additionally, the study analyzed the correlation between academic burnout and the variables of psychological well-being, social well-being, and optimism in this population. Lastly, it aimed to determine whether students with high levels of academic burnout exhibit significantly lower levels of psychological well-being, social well-being, and optimism compared to those with lower burnout levels.

The findings suggest that academic burnout is negatively associated with students’ psychological and social well-being, as well as with their optimism levels, reinforcing previous research that identifies burnout as a significant risk factor for adolescent mental health [49, 50, 52]. Specifically, emotional exhaustion and cynicism – two core components of burnout – were negatively correlated with all dimensions of psychological [5] and social well-being [37], while academic efficacy showed positive correlations. These findings are consistent with prior studies that conceptualize the academic context as both a potential stressor and a setting for the development of competence and motivation [46].

Moreover, students with lower academic burnout reported significantly higher levels of psychological well-being, social well-being, and optimism compared to their peers with higher burnout, as supported by prior research [49–52]. The most notable differences were observed in dimensions such as self-acceptance and social coherence, two dimensions strongly linked to adolescents’ psychological and social well-being [5, 37]. These results underscore the importance of targeting specific facets of psychological and social well-being, as they play a relevant role in identity development, social competence, and long-term adjustment during adolescence [35, 36].

This study found that optimism partially mediated the relationship between academic burnout and both psychological and social well-being. Specifically, optimism helped explain how burnout is linked to core dimensions of psychological well-being – such as autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, and purpose in life – as well as social dimensions including integration, contribution, actualization, and coherence. These results are consistent with prior research identifying optimism as a correlate of lower emotional exhaustion and academic burnout [53, 54].

When supported by adaptive coping mechanisms, optimism has been associated with lower emotional exhaustion, reduced burnout, and enhanced students’ academic outcomes [52]. Further evidence suggests that optimism moderates the relationship between academic stress and depressive symptoms, highlighting its important role in educational settings [59]. Given its predictive and modifiable nature, optimism emerges as a psychological resource that can be strengthened through interventions. Schools are particularly well positioned to implement school-based programs that foster adolescents’ optimism and well-being, as supported by evidence of their effectiveness in promoting psychological adjustment [26–28, 55–58].

The findings reinforce the view of academic burnout as a developmental process shaped by educational transitions, personal trajectories, and socioemotional competencies [46]. For instance, contextual factors such as parental interference in academic decisions have been linked to increased school-related anxiety, which mediates academic exhaustion [48]. This suggests the need for interventions that involve both schools and families, promoting autonomy-supportive parenting to strengthen adolescents’ resilience and well-being. Additionally, the present study highlights optimism as a key socioemotional resource for adolescent well-being, consistent with evidence showing that socio-emotional skills are linked to students’ engagement–burnout profiles [47].

However, this study is not without limitations. Data collection among adolescents involves several challenges, including the potential influence of age and peer presence when completing the questionnaires, which can affect the accuracy of the responses. In addition, the questionnaires were completed during school hours, which could have contributed to student fatigue and, consequently, less reliable answers. A further limitation concerns language barriers, as some participants did not fully master the language used in the questionnaire, potentially impacting their comprehension and the validity of some responses. Additionally, while cross-sectional design provides valuable insights, it limits the ability to draw causal inferences. To address these limitations, some improvements are suggested. Enhancing privacy during questionnaire completion could reduce social influence, while adjusting the administration schedule, either using shorter blocks or conducting it outside of school hours, could minimize fatigue. Providing the questionnaire in multiple languages would help overcome language barriers, and motivating students by highlighting the study’s relevance could lead to more accurate responses. Future research would also benefit from adopting longitudinal designs to better understand the potentially causal relationships between academic burnout, optimism, and psychological well-being.

Conclusions

The findings of this study contribute to the field of educational psychology by emphasizing the dual significance of academic burnout and optimism in relation to adolescent well-being. Academic burnout was linked to lower levels of psychological and social well-being, pointing to its relevance as a potential risk indicator during this developmental stage. Conversely, academic efficacy was associated with more favorable outcomes, suggesting that academic environments can also serve as contexts for growth.

Importantly, optimism emerged as a mediating factor, partially explaining how burnout is related to lower levels of students’ well-being, and offering a more comprehensive view of adolescent development by addressing both psychological and social well-being, thereby reflecting the relational and multidimensional nature of well-being during this life stage.

Given the cross-sectional nature of the design, the findings reflect associations and do not allow causal inferences. Nevertheless, these results suggest that optimism can be considered not only as a personal resource but also a potentially trainable competency that, together with well-being-based programs, may support adaptive functioning and healthy development during adolescence. Fostering this capacity in students could also help promote healthier school climates, improve peer relationships, and strengthen emotional adjustment. Future research could further explore the longitudinal interplay between burnout, optimism, and well-being, as well as examine how contextual factors such as family involvement may enhance the effectiveness of interventions.
