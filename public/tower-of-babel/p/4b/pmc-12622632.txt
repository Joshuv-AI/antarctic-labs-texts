Low bone mineral density as a causal risk factor for cervical and thoracolumbar intervertebral disc degeneration: A Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Longao Huang, Qinghua Yang, Hongyuan Xu, Junfei Feng, Dun Liu, Hua Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bone mineral density (BMD) is a key indicator of osteoporosis, which may influence the development of intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD). However, the causal relationship and underlying signaling pathways between BMD and IDD remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the potential causal relationship and underlying signaling pathways between BMD and IDD. A two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was employed to analyze the causal effects of BMD levels across 3 anatomical sites [total body BMD (TB-BMD), lumbar spine BMD (LS-BMD), and femoral neck BMD (FN-BMD)] on risks of IDD. In this study, IDD was divided into 2 groups, namely cervical IDD (C-IDD) and thoracic/thoracolumbar/lumbosacral IDD (TTL-IDD). Additionally, multivariable MR (MVMR) was applied to explore whether type 2 diabetes (T2D) and body mass index (BMI) would mediate the identification of causal pathways. Moreover, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) colocalization analyses were utilized to examine the potential mechanisms and shared genes involved in osteoporosis and IDD. The results showed that genetic predispositions to TB-BMD, LS-BMD, and FN-BMD were associated with increased risk of C-IDD. Furthermore, TB-BMD, LS-BMD, and FN-BMD were identified as significant risk factors causally associated with TTL-IDD. The MVMR analysis revealed that even after adjusting for T2D and BMI, lower TB-BMD could significantly increase the odds of C-IDD and TTL-IDD. In the subgroup analysis, a significant causal effect of TB-BMD on TTL-IDD was observed in individuals aged 30 to 45, 45 to 60, and > 60 years. Polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 3 (GALNT3) and homeobox C4 (HOXC4) were the most prominent co-located genes illustrated by GO enrichment analysis and eQTL analysis. In conclusion, we found that lower BMD exerts significant causal effects on elevated IDD risk (both C-IDD and TTL-IDD).

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) and intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) are very common conditions in the aging population.[1,2] Both conditions significantly reduce quality of life and impose a heavy financial burden on society. OP is a common metabolic skeletal disease, characterized by a decrease in bone mineral density (BMD). This leads to an increase in the vulnerability of bones in the elderly.[3] IDD can cause disc herniations, spinal stenosis and degenerative spondylolisthesis, which can result in low back pain and, at the worst, to total incapacity and disability.[4]

The potential interplay between these 2 conditions has long been a subject of debate, with observational studies yielding conflicting results. In the past decades, some scholars argued that a less dense vertebral body accelerated disc degeneration, while a healthy vertebral bone structure protected the disc.[5–7] They suggested that OP may be the cause of IDD. Conversely, some studies indicated that denser vertebral bone accelerated disc degeneration, whereas lower vertebral BMD decelerated disc degeneration.[8–10] Additionally, still others believed that BMD of vertebral body did not correlate with disc degeneration.[11,12]

The advent of Mendelian randomization (MR), a genetic epidemiology approach that leverages genetic variants to infer causality while minimizing confounding and reverse causation biases,[13] has provided new insights into this controversy. Recent studies employing MR have begun to clarify the causal relationship between BMD and IDD. Zhao et al[14] conducted a bidirectional MR analysis and identified a significant positive causal effect of lower BMD – measured at the heel, total body (TB), femoral neck (FN), and lumbar spine (LS) – on IDD, with no evidence of a reverse effect. Similarly, Li et al[15] reported that FN-BMD, heel BMD (eBMD), LS-BMD, and TB-BMD were associated with an increased risk of IDD, with eBMD, LS-BMD, and TB-BMD remaining significant after multivariable MR adjustments for confounders such as type 2 diabetes (T2D), body mass index (BMI), hyperthyroidism, and smoking. In contrast, Liu et al[16] found no causal link between OP or forearm BMD (FA-BMD) and IDD, but identified increased TB-BMD and LS-BMD as significant risk factors for IDD, with potential effects from eBMD and FN-BMD. These studies collectively suggest a causal role of BMD in IDD, though the specific BMD measures implicated and their effect sizes vary, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Despite these advances, gaps remain in understanding the nuanced relationship between BMD and IDD. Previous MR studies[14–16] have largely treated IDD as a homogenous condition, without distinguishing between degeneration at different spinal regions, such as cervical IDD (C-IDD) and thoracic/thoracolumbar/lumbosacral IDD (TTL-IDD). Moreover, the influence of age-specific effects and the underlying biological mechanisms linking BMD and IDD remains underexplored. In this study, we employed a two-sample MR approach to investigate the causal effects of TB-BMD, LS-BMD, and FN-BMD on both C-IDD and TTL-IDD. We further utilized multivariable MR (MVMR) to assess whether these associations persist after adjusting for T2D and BMI, key confounders implicated in both OP and IDD. Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses by age for TB-BMD to examine age-specific effects and performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) colocalization analyses to elucidate potential molecular pathways.

Our findings aim to refine the understanding of how BMD contributes to IDD across different spinal regions and age groups, offering insights into the pathophysiology of these conditions and informing targeted prevention strategies. By integrating these novel approaches with the latest evidence, this study seeks to advance the field beyond existing observational and MR-based investigations.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. MR study design

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) used as an instrumental variable (IV) must satisfy the following assumptions: relevance assumption: IVs are closely related to BMD exposure. Exclusion restriction assumption: IVs are not associated with confounders between BMD exposure and both C-IDD and TTL-IDD. Independence assumption: IVs have no direct effect on C-IDD and TTL-IDD, and their impact on C-IDD and TTL-IDD can only be manifested through BMD exposure. These 3 key assumptions are illustrated in Figure 1. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) checklist (Supplementary Checklist, Supplemental Digital content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q626).[17]

Three major hypotheses of MR to investigate the causal relationship between BMD exposure and C-IDD with TTL-IDD. BMD = bone mineral density, C-IDD = cervical IDD, FN-BMD = femoral neck bone mineral density, IDD = intervertebral disc degeneration, IVM = inverse variance weighted, LS-BMD = lumbar spine bone mineral density, MR = Mendelian randomization, MR-PRESSO = MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier test, SNP = single nucleotide poly morphism, TB-BMD = total body bone mineral density, TTL-IDD = thoracic/thoracolumbar/lumbosacral IDD.

2.2. Data sources and populations

No ethical approval was required as this study utilized publicly available summary results from the genome-wide association studies (GWAS). The summary-level data encompass 3 phenotypes related to BMD exposure, which include TB-BMD, LS-BMD, and FN-BMD. Additionally, the analysis incorporated disease outcomes such as C-IDD and TTL-IDD, along with covariates (T2D and BMI) acting as risk factors, all of which were obtained from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Open GWAS (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/).

2.3. Exposure phenotypes

In this research, 3 GWAS datasets of BMD from European ancestry populations were collected. BMD was used to characterize 3 phenotypes of OP measurements at various locations, including TB-BMD (n = 56,284),[18] LS-BMD (n = 28,498), and FN-BMD (n = 32,735).[19] The summary statistics of BMD were obtained from IEU GWAS database of MRC IEU at the University of Bristol. For covariates, the BMI GWAS dataset (n = 461,460) and the T2D GWAS dataset (n = 468,298),[20] both of European ancestry, were chosen.

2.4. Outcome phenotypes

From FinnGen R9 dataset, summary statistics data for TTL-IDD (International Classification of Diseases-10 [ICD-10] M51) and C-IDD (ICD-10 M50) was derived. The TTL-IDD dataset includes 270,964 controls and 37,636 cases, with 21,280 females and 16,356 males. The mean age at the first event is 49.87 years, and the unadjusted prevalence is 9.98%. Patients with other specific diseases, including ICD-9 7220, 7224, 7227, 7228A, and ICD-8 7250, were excluded. Data concerning C-IDD consist of 270,964 controls and 13,394 cases (7144 females and 6250 males). The mean age at the first event is 49.23 years, and the unadjusted prevalence is 3.55%. The GWAS of IDD data was performed using the REGENIE software with sex, age, genotyping batches, and 10 principal components incorporated as covariates.[21] The effect size was estimated for the alternative allele on the log-transformed odds ratio scale.

2.5. Selection of genetic instruments

In order to fulfill the 3 fundamental MR assumptions mentioned above and guarantee the credibility of genetic variations as IVs, an extensive range of quality control measures were implemented. First, a rigorous evaluation of SNPs encompassing the entirety of the genome was carried out. This entailed applying a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−8 in order to discern SNPs of exceptional significance. Second, a linkage disequilibrium clustering approach was used to further refine our results and exclude SNPs in strong linkage disequilibrium. A threshold of r2 = 0.001 and kb = 10,000 was utilized, which could ensure accurate and precise clustering.[21] The F-statistic was calculated using the equation: F = R2(SampleSize − 2)/(1 − R2).[22] Here, R2 represents the variance of exposure explained by each IV individually. Furthermore, the R2 statistic was calculated via the equation: R2 = [2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × beta2]/[(2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × beta2) + (2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × N × SE(beta)2)].[23] EAF denotes the effect allele frequency. Beta is the estimated genetic effect on physical activity. Ν is the sample size of the GWAS for the SNP-physical activity association. SE (beta) refers to the standard error of genetic effect. IVs with an F-statistic < 10 were considered weak and subsequently excluded.[24]

To satisfy the third MR assumption, SNPs associated with C-IDD and TTL-IDD with a significance level of P < 5 × 10−8 were excluded. Besides, additional measures to ensure the reliability and consistency of IVs were imposed. The exposure and outcome data were harmonized by taking the conservative action = 2: inferring favorable strand alleles for palindromes using allele frequencies. Next, SNPs with incompatible alleles and those with palindromic sequences and intermediate allele frequencies were weeded out. To correct heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy, we employed MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO), an approach utilized for removing outliers (SNPs deemed unqualified).[25] After these rigorous quality control protocols were implemented, a carefully-selected collection of SNPs serving as the ultimate genetic IVs for the subsequent MR analysis was obtained. Additionally, IVs were selected according to their reliability and adherence to established standards.

2.6. Two-sample MR (TSMR)

Analyses were carried out using the R software version 4.3.1. For the Univariable MR (UVMR), MVMR, and bidirectional MR (BMR) analyses, we utilized the R packages TwoSampleMR, MR-PRESSO, and MendelianRandomization. Two-Sample MR (version 0.5.6) provides various computational methods and a wide range of published GWAS datasets.[26] This package was used to perform MR-Egger, inverse variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode analyses. MendelianRandomization offers several useful approaches and compensates for the limitations of TwoSampleMR in certain aspects.[27]

The TwoSampleMR package was used to conduct MVMR analyses, which facilitates the acquisition of multivariable IVW outcomes. Additionally, the MendelianRandomization package was employed to obtain multivariable MR-Egger results. IVW method, one of the frequently employed MR analysis methods, combines the impacts of multiple independent genetic variants on the exposure factors into one weighted average. IVW can provide a more accurate estimate of overall causal effect. MR-Egger is an extension of MR, which assists in detecting and rectifying any bias caused by horizontal pleiotropy. The correct estimates can be generated using the weighted median method, even when as many as 50% of SNPs are IVs that are deemed invalid.[28] MR-PRESSO identifies and removes outliers with a P-value < .05 and provides an adjusted estimate of causal effect. Odds ratios (ORs) were used to report the findings along with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). To determine statistical significance, we considered the IVW estimate to have an OR higher than 1, the 95% CI excluding 0, and a P < .05.[29]

To take multiple testing of exposure factors into account, Benjamini–Hochberg approach was introduced to control the statistical significance of the MR effect estimates, ensuring a false discovery rate (FDR) <5%. In UVMR, the IVW approach was considered as the primary outcome, with the other 4 methods serving as secondary outcomes. The consistency of results among the IVW and the other 4 methods was taken as an indication of reliability. In MVMR, both multivariable IVW and multivariable MR-Egger models were considered reliable when they showed positive results.

2.7. Sensitivity analysis

Heterogeneity was assessed by using Cochran Q statistic, and statistical significance was determined at a P-value of < .05. MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO were employed to identify potential horizontal pleiotropy effects. If the MR-Egger intercept was significantly different from 0 (P < .05), it indicated the presence of horizontal pleiotropy among the IVs. MR-PRESSO was utilized to identify heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy in estimating the causal effects. A P-value of < .05 typically implied the existence of such an effect. A leave-one-out analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of results. In this analysis, the influence of any individual SNP on the outcome was examined by sequentially removing each SNP and repeating the IVW analyses.

2.8. Subgroup analysis of age

To address the potential decrease in MR accuracy caused by employing IVs from GWAS analyses for all age cohorts,[18] a comprehensive investigation categorized by different age groups was conducted. Moreover, causal effects of individual exposures and outcomes were investigated by adopting IVW method with random effects. On the basis of age, TB-BMD data were divided into 4 age groups: 15 to 30 years (n = 4180), 30 to 45 years (n = 10,062), 45 to 60 years (n = 18,805), and > 60 years (n = 22,504) (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q625).[18] The correlation between IDD and the corresponding phase TB-BMD was estimated by MR analysis.

2.9. GO enrichment analysis

The GO,[30] which includes 3 independent categories (molecular function, biological process, and cellular component), was analyzed to further validate whether the potential targets were indeed related to IDD. Associated lead SNPs identified in C-IDD and TTL-IDD symptoms reported in OP GWAS were connected to adjacent genes. GO enrichment analysis was performed by the web-based tool Functional Mapping and Annotation (https://fuma.ctglab.nl).

2.10. eQTL analysis

The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Program has implemented a comprehensive data repository and tissue repository in order to investigate the correlation between genetic variations and gene expression in a wide spectrum of human tissues.[31] The probabilities of colocalization between GWAS and eQTL signals were computed by using Coloc47.[32] To investigate the associations between IDD and OP features, information from the GTEx version 8 dataset was extracted via the GTEx Portal. Special focus was given to BMD-related SNPs for examining their effects on the expression of adjacent genes in musculoskeletal tissues.

3. Results

3.1. Data description

When C-IDD or TTL-IDD was defined as outcomes, SNPs significantly associated with BMD at 3 different sites were screened and selected respectively. For C-IDD, 78 (TB-BMD), 23 (LS-BMD), and 19 (FN-BMD) IVs were identified, while for TTL-IDD, 76 (TB-BMD), 23 (LS-BMD), and 18 (FN-BMD) IVs were identified (Tables S2–S7, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q625). These SNPs were specifically associated with BMD, but not strongly associated with either C-IDD or TTL-IDD. In Table S14 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q625), F-statistic values for every single SNP are presented. All of these values exceed the set threshold of 10. For C-IDD, mean F-statistic values were 63.95 (TB-BMD), 48.24 (LS-BMD), and 53.43 (FN-BMD) respectively. The variances explained by the IVs were 0.09% (TB-BMD), 0.04% (LS-BMD), and 0.03% (FN-BMD) respectively. For TTL-IDD, the mean F-statistic values were 64.57 (TB-BMD), 48.24 (LS-BMD), and 52.36 (FN-BMD) respectively. The variance explained by these IVs was 0.09% (TB-BMD), 0.04% (LS-BMD), and 0.03% (FN-BMD) respectively. In the outcome dataset, certain SNPs were excluded due to unavailability or ambiguity in A/T or G/C alleles. To assess potential reverse causation, we performed bidirectional MR analyses evaluating the effects of C-IDD and TTL-IDD on BMD phenotypes (TB-BMD, LS-BMD, FN-BMD). Genetic instruments for C-IDD and TTL-IDD were rigorously selected to meet MR assumptions (Tables S8–S13, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q625). These rigorous selection criteria and high F-statistic values support the validity of the IVs for robust causal inference in the MR analysis. The bidirectional approach further helps to clarify the directionality of the causal relationship between BMD and IDD.

3.2. UVMR analysis of causal relationship between BMD exposure and C-IDD or TTL-IDD

UVMR reflected that there was a causal relationship between lower BMD at various locations and an increased risk of developing C-IDD (TB-BMD, OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.12–1.30, P = 3.60 × 10−7; LS-BMD, OR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.12–1.35, P = 1.00 × 10−5; FN-BMD, OR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.09–1.40, P = 8.60 × 10−4) (Fig. 2; Figs. S1–S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q627). Likewise, lower BMD showed causal relationships with an increased risk of TTL-IDD (TB-BMD, OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.10–1.21, P = 4.86 × 10−9; LS-BMD, OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.23, P = 1.37 × 10−5; FN-BMD, OR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.17, P = 9.25 × 10−3) (Fig. 2; Figs. S1–S6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q627). The MR-estimated effects of these exposures are robust in weighted median mode. There was insufficient evidence for heterogeneity in effect sizes for FN-BMD and LS-BMD in both C-IDD and TTL-IDD (Tables S15 and S16, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q625). However, heterogeneity was observed in Cochran Q statistic results for TB-BMD in both C-IDD and TTL-IDD groups. After the outlier was removed, MR-PRESSO did not identify any further outliers in the final data used for MR analyses. Except for FN-BMD in C-IDD, MR-Egger intercept test and funnel plot symmetry indicated no horizontal pleiotropy. All sensitivity analyses yielded results similar to those of main analyses.

The forest plots of reversed UVMR analyses explored the relationship between TB-BMD, LS-BMD, FN-BMD on C-IDD and TTL-IDD on using different MR statistical models. The causal estimates are given as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). 3.3. MVMR analysis of causal relationship between BMD exposure and C-IDD or TTL-IDD

After MVMR analysis controlling for T2D and BMI individually, substantive evidence emerged indicating a direct causal impact of TB-BMD on risk of C-IDD (controlling for BMI: IVW OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.07–1.26, P < .001; controlling for T2D: IVW OR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.14–1.33, P < .001) (Table 1). After these confounders were jointly adjusted, the effect size of association was attenuated slightly (IVW OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.07–1.26, P = 1.00 × 10−3). Similar results were observed in TTL-IDD (controlling for BMI: IVW OR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.10–1.24, P < .001; controlling for T2D: IVW OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.11–1.25, P < .001). After these confounders were adjusted for simultaneously, the effect size of association did not change appreciably (IVW OR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.09–1.25, P < .001). After BMI was individually controlled, evidence for a causal relationship of LS-BMD on C-IDD and TTL-IDD was weaker (C-IDD, OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.00–1.25, P = .054; TTL-IDD, OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.02–1.22, P = .013). Regardless of controlling BMI and T2D individually or adjusting for these confounders jointly, no evidence for a causal effect of FN-BMD on C-IDD and TTL-IDD was demonstrated.

3.4. BMR analysis of causal relationship between C-IDD, TTL-IDD, and BMD

IVW analyses were performed to explore the potential causal effect of BMD on C-IDD and TTL-IDD in the analysis of reverse causal relationships (Tables S15 and S16, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q625). The results stated that no causal relationship between IDD and BMD could be determined. Among them, the causal effects of C-IDD and TTL-IDD on LS-BMD were as follows: C-IDD (OR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.01–1.26, P = .03, FDR = 0.09) and TTL-IDD (OR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.17, P = .02, FDR = 0.09). After Bonferroni correction, the FDR-adjusted P-values (FDR = 0.09) did not reach the predefined significance threshold (α = 0.05).

3.5. Subgroup analysis by age

Table 2 shows subgroup analyses by age. TB-BMD had a strong causal effect on TTL-IDD (P = .016 and P = .028) in individuals aged 30 to 45 and those above 60 years. In individuals aged 45 to 60, TB-BMD exhibited a strong causal effect on all the IDD phenotypes. It is noteworthy that TB-BMD had a strong causal effect on all the IDD phenotypes in participants aged 45 to 60 years (C-IDD, P = .018; TTL-IDD, P = .002). However, in participants below age 30, no valid data for MR estimates were found (data not shown). Due to the unavailability of subgroup data on FN-BMD and LS-BMD across various age groups, the corresponding MR analyses could not be performed.

3.6. GO enrichment analysis

Findings of GO enrichment analysis are shown in Figure 3. According to GO enrichment analyses, the relationship between BMD and C-IDD and TTL-IDD revealed high enrichment in a number of important regulatory pathways. For C-IDD, the involvement of 34 GO biological processes including skeletal system development, canonical wingless-related integration site (WNT) signaling pathway, ossification, positive regulation of bone resorption, bone remodeling, and regulation of bone remodeling was observed (Fig. 3A). Similarly, a total of 66 GO biological processes were identified for TTL-IDD. These processes include key biological processes such as the canonical WNT signaling pathway, ossification, bone remodeling, and positive regulation of bone resorption (Fig. 3B).

3.7. eQTL colocalization analysis

Ten BMD susceptibility genes had variants co-located on musculoskeletal tissues, as shown by the eQTL colocalization analysis (Table 3). From the results, the polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 3 (GALNT3) gene was associated with TB-BMD and LS-BMD, with its variants in LS-BMD (e.g., rs11680288) being the most prominent in colocalization. In addition, the homeobox C4 (HOXC4) gene was present in LS-BMD and FN-BMD. The effect sizes of genes target of Myb1-like 2 (TOM1L2) and BTB domain containing 16 (BTBD16) were the largest among all susceptibility genes discovered with co-located variants (normalized effect size = 0.3).

4. Discussion

The association between BMD and IDD has garnered significant attention to date. However, previous MR investigations predominantly conceptualized IDD as a homogeneous pathological entity. Our study pioneers the stratification of IDD into C-IDD and TTL-IDD subtypes, thereby establishing novel causal relationships between site-specific BMD measurements and anatomically distinct degenerative phenotypes. The findings from our UVMR analysis provide substantial evidence supporting the causal relationship between site-specific BMD measurements (TB-BMD, LS-BMD, and FN-BMD) and both C-IDD and TTL-IDD. In addition, the MVMR considering BMI and T2D status revealed causal relationships between TB-BMD and C-IDD, TB-BMD and TTL-IDD, LS-BMD and C-IDD, and LS-BMD and TTL-IDD, independent of those traits. In the subgroup analysis, a significant causal effect of TB-BMD on TTL-IDD among individuals aged 30 to 45, 45 to 60, and > 60 years was observed. However, FN-BMD showed no significant association with C-IDD or TTL-IDD by employing an MVMR approach. In our BMR, limited evidence was found to support a causal link between IDD and BMD.

These findings are consistent with emerging evidence in the field. Zhao et al[14] employed bidirectional MR analysis and reported that lower eBMD, TB-BMD, FN-BMD, and LS-BMD were linked to an increased IDD risk, aligning with the causal effects observed in our study. However, their analysis did not distinguish between IDD subtypes, whereas we offer more granular evidence by separating C-IDD and TTL-IDD. Similarly, Li et al[15] demonstrated causal associations between FN-BMD, eBMD, LS-BMD, and TB-BMD with IDD, with TB-BMD and LS-BMD effects remaining robust after adjustment for T2D, BMI, hyperthyroidism, and smoking. While their findings parallel ours for TB-BMD and LS-BMD, they noted a diminished FN-BMD effect following adjustment, contrasting with our findings, where FN-BMD retained a causal influence on both C-IDD and TTL-IDD, though attenuated in MVMR. Liu et al[16] further corroborated a positive causal link between TB-BMD and LS-BMD with IDD, with suggestive effects for eBMD and FN-BMD, though they found no associations with OP or FA-BMD, nor did they stratify IDD by anatomical region.

Notably, available observational studies have provided conflicting results regarding the association between BMD and IDD. On one hand, several studies have reported that there is a positive correlation between IDD and BMD, i.e., higher vertebral bone density is related to lumbar disc degeneration.[33–40] Conversely, several studies[5–7,40–53] support a positive correlation between vertebral OP and disc degeneration, indicating that lower BMD is associated with more severe IDD. They suggest that OP may be the cause of IDD. For example, loss of vertebral height and spinal instability can occur due to low LS-BMD, leading to facet joint and disc degeneration.[54] A prospective study shows lower bone density in older subjects correlates with a tendency to reduce disc volume.[43] Moreover, some animal studies using pharmacological interventions have reported that treatments for OP, such as the administration of alendronate and calcitonin in ovariectomized rats, retarded IDD.[50,55–57] The inconsistent findings regarding the connection between BMD and IDD in different studies could potentially be explained by the presence of confounding factors and reverse causation. Equating associations discovered in observational studies with causation would be inappropriate. To address these limitations, our approach aims to surpass them by investigating the causal connections between BMD and IDD through rigorous MR analysis. The results of our investigation align closely with the above observational and animal studies, indicating that a low BMD might elevate the likelihood of developing IDD. Consequently, the research reveals that low BMD is a significant risk factor causally associated with IDD.

After adjusting for confounding factors, the correlation between FN-BMD and IDD was no longer significant. Similar results have also been reported by a study analyzing data from 168 postmenopausal women.[4] No significant association was found between FN-BMD and the mean disc degeneration grade of all intervertebral discs (L1-L2 to L4-L5) in LS. The association was not significant when each vertebral level was compared separately to the FN-BMD. Consequently, FN-BMD could potentially serve as a more dependable indicator in the diagnosis of OP among postmenopausal females suffering from degenerative alterations within the LS. The causal relationship that was confirmed in this study adds to our knowledge on actors that increase the risk of IDD development and progression, potentially paving the way for innovative creation of more focused strategies for prevention and treatment.

GO enrichment analysis showed that ossification,[58] positive regulation of bone resorption,[59] regulation of bone remodeling,[60] and canonical Wnt signaling pathway[59] emerged as important biological processes in the potential causal relationship between low BMD and IDD. According to Luo et al,[58] increased ossification was associated with reduced IDD severity. Drugs available for OP treatment include antiresorptive agents and osteoanabolic agents. Although these agents have the ability to increase BMD, they may also promote the deposition of phosphate in cartilage, leading to a certain degree of cartilage calcification.[59] Because intervertebral disc cartilage endplate is composed of hyaline cartilage, therapies for OP might have implications for the cartilage endplate. Additionally, activating the WNT signaling pathway, a widely utilized therapeutic target for OP, is frequently employed. The activation of the WNT signal can mediate calcification within cartilage.[61] This is primarily due to the fact that WNT signals facilitate the osteogenic differentiation of multipotent progenitors throughout the process of endochondral ossification.[62] According to the research conducted by Sun and Luo,[59] it further suggested that therapies targeting WNT activation had the potential to induce cartilage endplate calcification within the disc. Since BMD SNPs were substituted for OP, it is reasonable to assume that OP also has these biological processes. Combination therapies targeting both low BMD and IDD may exhibit antagonistic effects due to overlapping biological pathways. The results serve as a reminder that mechanisms of action should ideally be taken into consideration during the development of combination strategies.

Through eQTL colocalization analysis,[32] 2 SNPs (rs7586085 and rs11680288) were co-located at the GALNT3 gene. Wang et al[63] revealed a notable association between the GALNT3 gene and OP, suggesting that decreased expression of the GALNT3 gene may accelerate the onset and progression of OP. HOXC4 is one of the OP susceptibility genes identified by large-scale meta-analyses of GWAS.[64] Our study demonstrated that 2 SNPs (rs4759320 and rs894738) associated with BMD were co-located within this gene. Based on the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements RNA sequencing data, HOXC4 was determined to be preferentially expressed in osteoblasts compared with other cell types.[65] Moreover, our data showed that TOM1L2 had the strongest eQTL effects with OP. TOM1L2-knockout mice were observed to develop kyphosis.[66] In addition, microRNAs predicted to regulate TOM1L2 expression have been shown to be involved in osteogenic differentiation and skeletal muscle development.[67] Through eQTL colocalization analysis, some novel susceptibility genes for OP were validated, providing new functional insights into genes with previously established functions.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in context of several limitations. First, the GWAS data used in this study were derived from individuals of European descent, and thus care should be taken when generalizing its findings to other racial/ethnic groups. Second, MR studies proxy for the effect of exposure over the lifetime and produce larger effects than expected with a change in the exposure during adulthood. For this and other reasons, the magnitude of MR effects should be viewed cautiously.[68] Third, the absence of data on BMD subgroups, such as stratification by sex or ethnicity, weakened the statistical power and prevented subgroup analyses; therefore, it is not possible to detect potential sources of heterogeneity from the selected study characteristics. Fourth, the results of GO and eQTL were based solely on bioinformatic analyses, and additional research on ex vivo or in vivo is needed to further validate our findings. Fifth, although sensitivity analyses were conducted to address potential confounding, our MR analysis may not fully eliminate residual confounding arising from environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, and age-related changes affecting disc involvement over the lifetime.[69] These unresolved issues raise concerns about the robustness of our conclusions and highlight the need for further studies to validate and refine our findings.

5. Conclusion

This study suggests that OP may be causally associated with an increased risk of IDD. In view of our findings, measures to increase BMD may serve as preventive strategies against IDD in clinical practice.
