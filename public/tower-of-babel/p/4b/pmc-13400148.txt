Predictive Modeling of Bacterial Inactivation With Hydrogen Peroxide Over a Cobalt Ferrite Catalyst
Authors: Viktor Husak, Nazarii Danyliuk, Hynek Roubik, Olena Bobrova, Stacy Denise Hammond Hammond, Alexander Shyichuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen peroxide activated by a cobalt ferrite catalyst generates hydroxyl radicals that effectively inactivate bacteria. A continuous‐flow packed‐bed reactor containing cobalt ferrite was developed and tested for bacterial disinfection. The inactivation kinetics were highly nonlinear. The classical Weibull model accurately described the initial decline in bacterial counts at low H2O2 concentration (3 mM) but lost accuracy at longer contact times and higher concentrations (6–10 mM). To improve prediction, 10 machine learning algorithms were evaluated. Gradient boosting (GB) and random forest (RF) showed the best performance and were assessed using cross‐validation, error metrics, and independent validation with previously unseen data at 4 mM H2O2. GB demonstrated superior generalization, whereas RF was more accurate under moderate oxidative stress. A combined GB + RF model, based on averaging predictions from both algorithms, provided the most balanced results, with average errors below ±10% at low‐to‐moderate peroxide concentrations. The findings highlight the limitations of conventional kinetic models for cobalt ferrite‐catalyzed peroxide disinfection and provide a predictive framework for selecting peroxide dose and contact time in continuous‐flow treatment. Independent validation confirmed high predictive accuracy, with larger relative errors observed only at very low residual bacterial counts.Keywords: disinfection, heterogeneous catalysis, machine learning, peroxides, radical reactions, Weibull equation

Introduction

Disinfection is a crucial step in the water purification process. Commonly used disinfectants include chlorine gas and chlorine dioxide, which effectively combat bacteria, fungi, and viruses [1]. However, chlorine‐based disinfectants produce toxic by‐products, such as trihalomethanes, haloacetic acids, haloamines, and halonitriles [2]. These compounds exhibit various harmful effects in the human body, including genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, neurotoxicity, and so on [3, 4, 5].

Alternatively, hydrogen peroxide is considered a “green” disinfectant. The bactericidal effect of H2O2 is mainly due to forming active oxygen species such as hydroxyl radicals HO• and superoxide anions O2 –•. Aggressive radicals react rapidly with unsaturated fatty acids and amino acids, resulting in numerous adverse effects on bacteria [6]. The decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into radicals does not occur spontaneously and requires the action of an external factor. Typical methods for activating H2O2 include UV irradiation [7, 8, 9, 10] and the use of suitable catalysts [11, 12].

The catalytic production of HO• radicals can provide more effective disinfection than the H2O2/UV system [13]. A typical homogeneous catalyst is ferrous sulfate (also known as the Fenton catalyst), which can be effectively combined with ferric chloride as a coagulant [14]. Heterogeneous catalysts for the H2O2 activation usually contain ions of transition elements [15, 16]. These so‐called Fenton‐like catalysts are well suited for continuous flow mode, which is advantageous for practical applications [17, 18]. Among Fenton‐like catalysts, cobalt ferrite is distinguished by its stable catalytic activity and low leaching of metal ions [19, 20]. The versatile catalytic properties of cobalt ferrite are due to the two active transition elements (Co and Fe) forming complementary electron transfer reactions. The spinel structure of cobalt ferrite allows the coexistence of Co and Fe ions in both low and high oxidation states, thus facilitating redox cyclic transitions. The resulting partially inverse spinel structure is rich in structural defects, which serve as catalytically active centers [21]. The first use of cobalt ferrite in Fenton‐like bacterial inactivation in flow mode was by Tatarchuk et al [18].

This study, for the first time, compares classical regression models and ensemble machine learning (ML) models for bacterial inactivation by catalytically activated H2O2. The description of bacteria inactivation during the disinfection process is a vital task necessary for operational process control. Explicit mathematical functions, such as exponential or logarithmic, allow for the description of general trends in bacterial inactivation but are often not flexible enough to account for multifactorial influences. Typical mathematical approaches to simulate disinfection processes include the pseudo‐first‐order kinetic equation [22], the Weibull model [11, 23], and the Gompertz and the Hom models [24, 25, 26]. Different disinfectants differ in their mechanisms of action and thus require different models for an accurate description of the disinfection dynamics [13, 27]. This is where ML algorithms come into play, enabling the creation of nonlinear, adaptive models based on experimental data from water disinfection [28, 29, 30]. ML models accurately predict the bacterial inactivation and degradation of antibiotic‐resistant genes using different disinfectants (chlorine, UV254, ozone, and UV/H2O2) [31]. ML models can select optimal disinfectant dosing rates to minimize the formation of disinfection by‐products [32]. Successful algorithms include the light gradient boosting (GB) machine, GB with categorical features, and GB decision tree [31].

Recently, hydrogen peroxide was used for water disinfection in a plug‐flow catalytic reactor [33]. That study confirmed the stability and catalytic activity of cobalt ferrite under flow conditions and described bacterial inactivation using a regression equation based on a second‐order exponential decay combined with a logarithmic dependence on the initial bacterial concentration. However, the previous model was developed mainly for a fixed H2O2 concentration of 10 mM and therefore did not fully address the predictive modeling of disinfection over a wider operational window. In particular, the influence of different peroxide concentrations, bacterial loads, and contact times on the reliability of classical kinetic models and ML algorithms has not yet been systematically evaluated for cobalt ferrite‐catalyzed H2O2 disinfection.

The novelty of the present study should therefore be considered in the context of process prediction rather than catalyst synthesis. While the preparation and catalytic activity of cobalt ferrite in peroxide‐based bacterial inactivation have been reported previously, a systematic predictive framework for continuous‐flow disinfection under variable operational conditions has not yet been established. In particular, the present work extends the previously reported catalytic system from a single‐condition regression description to a broader kinetic dataset including three initial H2O2 concentrations, different initial bacterial loads, and a wide range of contact times controlled by the flow rate. This allows the limits of conventional empirical disinfection models to be evaluated and compared with nonlinear ensemble learning methods.

The aim of this study is to develop and validate a reliable model for predicting bacterial inactivation kinetics in a continuous‐flow cobalt ferrite/H2O2 catalytic reactor. For this purpose, an extensive series of disinfection experiments was performed using three H2O2 concentrations, namely, 3, 6, and 10 mM, initial Escherichia coli loads ranging from 1.0 × 103 to 5.0 × 104 CFU/L, and water flow rates corresponding to different contact times. Two empirical regression models, the Weibull and Gompertz equations, were first tested to assess the ability of conventional kinetic descriptions to reproduce the observed disinfection behavior. In parallel, 10 supervised ML algorithms were evaluated using contact time, initial bacterial load, and H2O2 concentration as input variables.

The main contribution of this work is the development and validation of a data‐driven model capable of predicting bacterial survival in a heterogeneous Fenton‐like flow reactor. The comparison between Weibull/Gompertz‐type models and ML algorithms provides insight into the nonlinear character of peroxide‐activated bacterial inactivation and demonstrates where classical kinetic equations lose predictive accuracy. Among the tested algorithms, random forest (RF) and GB showed the best predictive performance. Their averaged RF + GB model was additionally evaluated using independent experimental data obtained at 4 mM H2O2, a concentration not included in the training dataset. Therefore, the proposed model is intended not only as a statistical fitting tool but also as a practical basis for estimating the peroxide dose and contact time required to achieve a target bacterial reduction under changing influent conditions.

Materials and Methods

The Flow Catalytic Reactor

The synthesis of cobalt ferrite was described in detail elsewhere [34]. The preparation of catalyst granules involves pressing into pellets, sintering at 1150 °C, crushing, and sieving to obtain particles with a size of 0.8–1 mm. The obtained cobalt ferrite granules were placed in a stainless‐steel reactor with dimensions of 100 cm × 12 mm. The bacterial suspension and H2O2 solution were pumped at a total flow rate of 2.5–150 mL/min.

Disinfection Testing

E. coli strain ATCC 35218 was used for disinfection experiments. Bacteria from the solution were collected by filtration using a 0.9 μm nitrocellulose membrane. The sample volume of the solution was 10 mL or 100 mL, depending on the estimated bacterial concentration. Inoculated membrane filters were placed on standard Endo agar medium (Farmaktiv, Ukraine) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The grown E. coli colonies appeared as dark red colonies on Endo agar. Replicate measurements were treated as individual observations in the modeling dataset. This approach allowed the models to include the experimental variability associated with plate counting and reactor operation. Prediction errors were therefore interpreted not only as model uncertainty but also as reflecting the inherent variability of bacterial enumeration.

Dataset Used for Modeling

The modeling dataset comprised continuous‐flow disinfection experiments at three initial H2O2 concentrations (3, 6, and 10 mM), spanning initial E. coli loads (N 0) from 1.0 × 103 to 5.0 × 104 CFU/L and six nominal contact times per run (0.46, 1.70, 3.46, 6.90, 13.82, and 27.64 min). Individual replicate measurements were retained as separate observations rather than averaged, resulting in a dataset of 208 observations. Plate counts followed Section 2.2, with 10–100 mL sample volumes, depending on N 0. For ML models, N 0 was log‐transformed and used together with contact time and H2O2 concentration as input variables.

An independent set at C_H2O2 = 4 mM, with N 0 = 1.7 × 103 and 1.62 × 104 CFU/L, was held out as an unseen interpolation validation set. The dataset should be considered small and structured rather than large‐scale. Therefore, the modeling results were interpreted within the experimental domain covered by the training data, namely, the tested ranges of H2O2 concentration, contact time, and initial bacterial load. The independent 4 mM H2O2 experiments were used to evaluate interpolation ability, not broad extrapolation ability.

For group‐based validation, each kinetic curve was treated as one experimental group. A group was defined as all measurements obtained at the same initial H2O2 concentration and the same initial bacterial load, including all contact times for this condition. Therefore, all time points belonging to the same kinetic curve were kept together during validation and were assigned either to the training subset or to the validation subset, but not to both.

Bacterial Inactivation Modeling

The survival of E. coli bacteria in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was modeled using the following numerical parameters: initial and final numbers of bacteria (in CFU/L); hydrogen peroxide concentration (in mM); and contact time of bacteria with the catalyst (in minutes). The modeling was performed using the Python 3.12 environment with the following core libraries: Pandas (for reading, processing, and structuring data), NumPy (for mathematical operations and logarithms), scikit‐learn (for implementing ML models and accuracy assessment), and Matplotlib and Seaborn (for visualizing the results). To improve the model's ability to learn from heterogeneous data, the spread of the bacterial counts was reduced by using a logarithmic transformation: df[‘log_CFU’] = np.log(df[‘CFU_initial’]) and X = df[[‘Time’, ‘log_CFU’, ‘H2O2_conc’]].

In conventional point‐level k‐fold cross‐validation, individual observations are randomly split into training and validation subsets. However, because the present dataset consists of kinetic curves containing neighboring time points obtained under the same experimental conditions, random point‐level splitting can place correlated data from the same kinetic curve in both the training and validation subsets. Therefore, point‐level cross‐validation was used only as an internal fitting check.

To obtain a more conservative estimate of predictive performance, group‐based cross‐validation was additionally applied. Each kinetic curve was treated as one group, defined by the same initial H2O2 concentration and the same initial bacterial load. All time points belonging to the same kinetic curve were assigned to the same fold. Thus, the model was validated on entire kinetic curves that were not used during training.

Because of the limited and structured nature of the dataset, in‐sample R 2 values were not used as the sole indicator of predictive robustness. Greater emphasis was placed on group‐based cross‐validation, MAE, RMSE, relative error analysis, and the independent validation dataset at 4 mM H2O2.

Results and Discussion

Regression Models and Their Limitations

The Weibull Model

Therefore, the scatter of the experimental data reflects the inherent variability of bacterial enumeration and continuous‐flow reactor operation. The corresponding graphs in Figure 2a show that the Weibull model generally well reproduces the decline of bacterial viability over time. However, the accuracy of the approximation is relatively low and varies significantly depending on the hydrogen peroxide concentration and the contact time. The best fit to the experimental values is observed at the lowest concentration of 3 mM (Figure 2a). Within the first 2 min of contact, the model curve closely fits the experimental points. At a concentration of 6 mM, slightly larger discrepancies are observed between the model and the experimental data. At 10 mM, the deviation increases significantly, especially after 3 min.

Figure 2b confirms the decrease in the Weibull model accuracy with increasing hydrogen peroxide concentration. The smallest relative errors are observed for 3 mM and the largest for 10 mM. The error values also increase with increasing contact time (Figure 2b). At longer times (13.8 and 27.6 min), the error values are the highest, which indicates a significant deviation of the model from the experimental values. Overall, the Weibull model appears to have limited use in predicting water disinfection with activated H2O2.

The decrease in accuracy with increasing contact time may be due to the presence of bacteria resistant to H2O2. The advantage of the Weibull model is that it assumes heterogeneity in the bacterial population [37]. The Weibull exponent n (Equation (1)) decreases if bacterial resistance varies widely [23]. Typically, values of the Weibull exponent in the range of 0.3–0.4 indicate the presence of bacterial subpopulations with a high resistance to disinfectant [23]. In this study, the Weibull exponent was found to be n = 0.42 (Equation (4)), indicating the presence of highly resistant bacteria. Bacteria defend themselves against H2O2 with enzymes, mainly catalase and peroxidase, which break down peroxides [38]. It can be concluded that the Weibull model is suitable for predicting only the initial stage of bacterial inactivation at low initial H2O2 concentrations.

The Gompertz Model

The Gompertz equation is used to model the decline of bacterial numbers with a time delay. This type of bacterial inactivation kinetics occurs under high‐pressure processing, a heat‐free pasteurization technique. The lag phase corresponds to the time needed to reach the adequate pressure [24]. The Gompertz model also suits thermal inactivation at mild temperatures [39, 40]. The terms ln(N 0) and C_H2O2 account for the bacterial load and hydrogen peroxide concentration, respectively. The Gompertz model demonstrated a satisfactory approximation of bacterial survival at low H2O2 concentrations (3 mM), with close agreement between predicted and experimental data, particularly in the early stages of exposure (Figure 3a). However, its accuracy declined as the peroxide concentration increased to 6 and 10 mM. In these cases, the model systematically overestimates bacterial survival, especially in the mid‐phase of treatment (3–14 min). The relative error analysis (Figure 3b) supports this observation, showing a peak error of over 400% at 13.8 min under 10 mM H2O2. This highlights a limitation of the Gompertz model in capturing the inactivation kinetics under stronger oxidative stress, likely due to its limited flexibility in modeling sharp declines or shoulder effects.

Approximation of bacterial survival by the Gompertz model (a) and its error (b) under the action of H2O2 of different concentrations. Experimental points represent individual replicate measurements rather than averaged values.

Despite this, the model exhibits improved accuracy at longer exposure times (≥27.6 min) across all peroxide concentrations. This suggests that the Gompertz function, with its asymptotic decay behavior, is well suited for predicting late‐stage inactivation when the remaining bacterial population is low and the inactivation rate slows. This contrasts with the Weibull model, which has demonstrated superior performance during the early phases (0–3 min) and at lower concentrations, where its flexibility enables a better representation of concave survival curves and initial resistance. This performance trend aligns with existing literature, which supports the application of the Gompertz model, especially in its modified form, for microbial inactivation under nonthermal conditions. For instance, in high‐pressure processing (HPP) experiments, the modified Gompertz model achieved high coefficients of determination (R 2 ≥ 0.95) and RMSE values as low as 0.020–0.130 when applied to pathogens such as Listeria innocua and Enterobacter sakazakii [24, 37]. These findings are consistent with our results at low oxidative stress levels, where the bacterial decline followed a sigmoidal profile and the Gompertz model adequately captured both the shoulder and tail phases of the inactivation curve.

However, as observed in our study at elevated H2O2 concentrations (6–10 mM), the Gompertz model tends to overestimate bacterial survival in the intermediate exposure window (3–14 min), with error peaks exceeding 400%. Similar overestimations were reported in studies involving HPP of Listeria innocua in milk, where the Gompertz model slightly underestimated lethality but remained conservative—microbial counts did not exceed model predictions, thereby retaining practical applicability despite the fit limitation [24].

Alternative empirical models such as the Weibull and log‐logistic functions have demonstrated enhanced flexibility in capturing nonlog‐linear inactivation kinetics, especially during the early phases. For example, inactivation studies of Yersinia enterocolitica found that both log‐logistic and Weibull models produced higher R 2 values (0.946–0.982) compared to Gompertz and offered better fitting performance across the entire time course [41]. These models have shown particular advantage in adjusting to concave or convex survival curves and accounting for initial shoulders or rapid drops in cell numbers. Other models, such as quasichemical, integrated, and new first‐order variants, provided mechanistic insights and offered flexibility for varied curve types, although they received less empirical validation [42].

Thus, the Gompertz model is a viable option for modeling long‐term microbial inactivation dynamics, primarily where the disinfection process extends beyond the initial rapid decay phase. However, its tendency to overpredict survival during intense and short exposures indicates that its application should be carefully assessed in high‐stress scenarios. In contrast, the Weibull model provides a better fit for the early disinfection kinetics, particularly under more pronounced peroxide action.

A promising direction for future modeling work is the hybrid or piecewise approach that combines the early‐time accuracy of Weibull‐type models with the long‐term predictive strength of the Gompertz function.

ML Models

The results in Section 3.1 indicate that the relationships between experimental variables in the advanced oxidation disinfection are complex. Therefore, ML models were also tested to predict bacterial survival.

Preliminary Testing of ML Models

Ten ML models were tested to fit the experimental data (Table 3). The GB and RF models were found to be the most suitable for predicting bacterial survival. This conclusion is based on the low values of MAE and RMSE compared to those of other models (Table 3). The RF algorithm creates a large number of independent trees and calculates the prediction by averaging the results. This reduces the risk of overfitting and accounts for nonlinear dependencies between experimental parameters. In turn, the GB algorithm is based on sequential training of trees, each of which minimizes the errors of the previous ones. This allows for achieving higher accuracy, especially when there are complex relationships between the experimental data. The data in Table 3 indicate that the GB model, with MAE = 203 and RMSE = 308, performs slightly better than the RF model (MAE = 308, RMSE = 505). RF and GB models are known to often outperform other ML methods, especially in large and noisy datasets [30, 43].

Figure 5a shows that the RF model accurately predicts bacterial survival even at a low number of trees (n_estimators = 10). Further increases in the number of trees to 50 and 200 do not significantly alter the shape of the curve, and only local deviations are slightly decreased. The model with one tree (n = 1) has a markedly lower accuracy, although it still predicts general trends. Thus, RF provides high‐quality forecasting even with a moderate number of trees.

In contrast to the RF model, the GB model with a single tree exhibits very low accuracy (Figure 5b). This model is largely insensitive to variations in the input data. At n_estimators = 10, the GB model partially corresponds to the experimental trends (Figure 5b), but still underestimates the experimental values. A good agreement with the experimental values is observed only at n_estimators = 50 and n_estimators = 200 (Figure 5b). Slight local discrepancies remain at high bacterial numbers. This indicates that GB is sensitive to the number of trees and requires a larger number of trees to achieve an accurate prediction.

The R 2 values calculated for the entire dataset were very high (>0.999), indicating excellent in‐sample fitting (Table 3). However, because the dataset is relatively small and contains repeated measurements at a limited number of H2O2 concentrations and nominal contact times, these values alone cannot be considered sufficient evidence of robust predictive ability. Therefore, the model comparison was based not only on in‐sample R 2 but also on cross‐validation, independent validation, and error analysis.

The initially applied point‐level cross‐validation results should be interpreted with caution because random splitting of individual observations may divide neighboring time points from the same kinetic curve between the training and validation subsets. Such points are not fully independent because they originate from the same experimental run and share the same initial bacterial load and H2O2 concentration. Therefore, group‐based cross‐validation was additionally performed as a more conservative validation strategy. In this approach, each kinetic curve was kept intact, and all of its time points were assigned either to the training subset or to the validation subset.

As expected, the group‐based validation produced lower performance metrics than point‐level cross‐validation, confirming that random point‐level splitting can overestimate predictive ability (Table 4). Nevertheless, the GB and RF models retained the best performance among the tested algorithms, indicating that their predictive advantage was not solely caused by information leakage between neighboring time points. The group‐based validation results are therefore used as the main estimate of model generalization, whereas the point‐level cross‐validation results are considered only as an internal fitting check. Overall, the GB model showed more stable group‐level validation performance than the RF model, although both models were retained for further analysis and for constructing the combined RF + GB prediction model.

The shape of the surfaces in Figure 7 slightly varies depending on the initial H2O2 concentration. When 3 mM hydrogen peroxide is used, the decrease in bacterial numbers is gradual. An increase in H2O2 concentration to 6 mM results in a steeper decline in bacterial numbers (Figure 7b). This effect is due to increased oxidative damage to bacterial cells. The RF model for 10 mM shows a steeper decline in bacterial numbers (Figure 7c), which is due to the increased oxidative effect of hydrogen peroxide.

Figure 8a,b illustrates the prediction accuracy of bacterial survival when exposed to different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide. Figure 8a shows a satisfactory agreement of the predicted values with the experimental data at all H2O2 concentrations (3, 6, and 10 mM). In the initial stages (0.46–3.46 min), good agreement with the experimental numbers is observed. For example, at a concentration of 3 mM and a time of 3.46 min, the error is –5.7% (Figure 8b). At a concentration of 6 mM and a time of 3.46 min, the error is +3.9%. This indicates a good ability of the RF model to reproduce the decrease in bacterial survival with moderate oxidant doses. Figure 8b shows that the largest deviations are observed at the later stage of oxidative action (at 13.82–27.64 min). The largest positive deviation +37.9% is observed at 13.82 min and 10 mM. At 27.64 min and 6 mM, the error reaches +25%. This indicates an insufficient performance of the RF model in predicting the disinfection efficiency under higher oxidative stress. Despite this, the accuracy of the RF model is significantly higher compared with the Weibull or Gompertz models. The average error for the RF model is only +9.7%, while it is +136% for the Weibull model and 105% for the Gompertz model. This low value confirms the high predictive ability of the RF model.

Approximation of bacterial survival by the random forest (RF) model (a) and its error (b) under the action of different concentrations of H2O2. Experimental points represent individual replicate measurements rather than averaged values.

Overall, the RF model presents a tradeoff between accuracy and stability. The RF model provides good accuracy in reproducing complex relationships between the initial bacterial population, contact time, and peroxide concentration. The better prediction of bacterial survival is observed at lower H2O2 concentrations, while the model predictability at higher concentrations is poorer. It can be concluded that the RF model is well suited for modeling bacterial survival under contact with activated hydrogen peroxide, especially in the range of low and medium H2O2 concentrations.

Predictive Performance of the GB Model

Figure 9 shows the 3D models of bacterial survival versus contact time at three hydrogen peroxide concentrations, plotted using the GB model. All three models show a similar pattern of decline in bacterial number.

Generalized 3D models of bacterial survival prediction built using gradient boosting at 3 mM (a), 6 mM (b), and 10 mM (c) of H2O2.

All three models show the typical decrease in bacterial survival with increasing contact time, which is especially noticeable at high values of N 0. The graphs indicate that an increase in H2O2 concentration results in a more rapid decrease in bacterial survival. This means that the modeling algorithm adequately accounts for the H2O2 dose factor.

Figure 10 shows the results of predicting the bacterial survival using the GB model under the action of H2O2 at concentrations of 3, 6, and 10 mM. Figure 10a shows that the model effectively predicts the decrease in bacterial number, particularly in the early stages (up to 6.9 min), where the expected values closely match the experimental ones. Figure 10b shows the relative errors of the GB model. During the first 10 min of exposure, the relative error does not exceed ±10%. The largest deviation was recorded at 10 mM and 27.6 min, where the error was −49.7%. In other words, the GB model significantly underestimates the number of surviving cells at long contact times. The average error value for the GB model is +11%.

Approximation of bacterial survival by the gradient boosting (GB) model (a) and its relative error (b) under the action of H2O2 of different concentrations. Experimental points represent individual replicate measurements rather than averaged values.

Thus, the GB model provides a stable balance between prediction accuracy and the absence of systematic biases. Compared to the 3D models built using the RF algorithm (Figure 7), the GB models are smoother and contain less noise, indicating better interpolation between experimental points. This is also consistent with the lower average errors of the GB model. It can be concluded that the GB models have high predictive ability for bacterial survival under the influence of activated hydrogen peroxide over a wide range of H2O2 concentrations and initial bacterial numbers.

The Combined RF + GB Model

Both the tested RF and GB models have increased inaccuracy at longer contact times (Figures 8b and 10b). However, the absolute values of prediction errors have opposite signs. The RF model tends to overestimate bacterial numbers (Figure 8b). In comparison, the GB model tends to underestimate (Figure 10b). For this reason, a combined model was used, which predicts the bacterial number as the average of the values obtained from both models, (RF + GB)/2. This approach enables the compensation of errors in individual models. There are examples of successful combinations of RF models with other models in the literature [44, 45].

Figure 11 shows the effect of averaging the predictions of the RF and GB models. The combined model accurately predicts the decrease in bacterial survival with contact time for each experimental concentration of hydrogen peroxide (3, 6, and 10 mM). For concentrations of 3 and 6 mM, the predicted curves precisely fit the experimental points (Figure 11a).

Approximation of bacterial survival by the (RF + GB)/2 model (a) and its error (b) under the action of H2O2 of different concentrations. Experimental points represent individual replicate measurements rather than averaged values.

At the H2O2 concentrations of 3 and 6 mM and within the first 10 min of contact, the error values mostly do not exceed ±10% (Figure 11b). At the H2O2 concentration of 10 mM, the model errors increase at the end stage (13.8, 27.6 min). However, the errors remain within ±18% which is better compared to the individual RF and BG models (Figures 7b and 9b). The higher prediction errors at the highest H2O2 concentration (Figure 11b) are probably not only related to experimental uncertainty in measuring very low bacterial counts but also to the physicochemical complexity of the catalytic process. At 10 mM H2O2, rapid radical generation on the cobalt ferrite surface may cause a sharp initial decrease in bacterial viability, whereas peroxide depletion and self‐decomposition can change the effective oxidant concentration during passage through the catalyst bed. Moreover, the packed‐bed reactor may introduce local differences in residence time, mass transfer, and contact between bacterial cells, H2O2, and active catalytic sites, leading to spatially nonuniform inactivation. At longer contact times, the survival curves may also exhibit tailing due to more resistant bacterial subpopulations or cells temporarily protected by physiological heterogeneity, which explains the increased deviations of both classical regression models and individual ML models under strong oxidative stress.

Thus, the use of the averaged model allows for reducing the variability of errors, especially in time intervals where the individual RF or GB models show less accurate predictions. This approach increases the overall accuracy of the bacterial survival prediction.

Predictive Performance of ML Models With Independent Experimental Data

To verify the predictive performance of the trained models with independent experimental data, the concentration of hydrogen peroxide was set at 4 mM. The experimental data at this initial H2O2 concentration were not present in the training data set. The obtained results confirm that the models accurately predict disinfection outcomes with different input variables.

Figure 12 illustrates the prediction accuracy using the RF and GB models, as well as the average model (RF + GB)/2. All three models fit well with the decline in bacterial counts as a function of contact time (Figure 12a,c). The fit appears to be less accurate at the lower initial bacterial count of 1700 CFU/L (Figure 12a) than at the higher count of 16 200 CFU/L (Figure 12c).

Prediction of bacterial survival (a,c) by the RF, GB, and (RF + GB)/2 models using independent experimental data obtained at [H2O2] of 4 mM. The values of the relative error of the models used (b,d). The initial bacterial numbers were N 0 = 1700 CFU/L (a,b) and N 0 = 16 200 CFU/L (c,d). Experimental points represent individual replicate measurements rather than averaged values.

Figure 12b,d illustrates the relative prediction errors. At the lower initial bacterial count of 1700 CFU/L, the relative error was initially low but increased at longer contact times, reaching approximately 60%. This high percentage error should be interpreted with caution because the residual bacterial count was very low; therefore, even a small absolute deviation produced a large relative error. Because individual replicate measurements were used for modeling, the prediction errors include experimental variability as well as model uncertainty. This variability is expected for plate‐count‐based enumeration, especially at low surviving cell numbers, and may also arise from local differences in residence time and contact conditions in the packed‐bed reactor. At the higher initial bacterial count of 16 200 CFU/L, the relative errors were substantially lower (Figure 12d). At short contact times (0.46–1.7 min), all three models slightly overestimated bacterial survival, with errors below or equal to +10%. At longer contact times (6.9–27.6 min), the models underestimated bacterial survival, with errors down to –15%. These results confirm good predictive accuracy for the independent 4 mM validation dataset, especially at the higher initial bacterial load.

Conclusion

Water disinfection using hydrogen peroxide activated by a cobalt ferrite catalyst in continuous flow mode is characterized by a high variability in the final bacterial count, depending on the initial bacterial count and initial hydrogen peroxide concentration. This results in complex nonlinear dependencies between the process parameters. For that reason, the regression models have rather low accuracy. Both the Weibull and the Gompertz models perform well at the early disinfection phase (up to 3 min) at low peroxide concentration (3 mM). At higher peroxide concentrations of 6–10 mM, the Weibull model provides more accurate results. The Gompertz model performs better at longer contact times. Unlike the regression models, ML models were found to be effective in predicting bacterial survival. The cross‐validation and accuracy analysis indicate that the GB model and the RF model are the most suitable for accurately predicting bacterial survival. The RF model achieves high accuracy on the training set, but lower accuracy on the cross‐validation set, and is prone to overtraining. The RF model shows stable accuracy even with 10 trees, making it suitable for use with limited computational resources. The GB model requires significantly more trees (≥50) to achieve similar accuracy and provides a stable increase in quality with sample expansion. When properly tuned, the GB model better generalizes complex nonlinear relationships. To compensate for errors of the individual ML models, the results of the RF and GB models were averaged. The combined model showed better performance in predicting bacterial number at the catalytic reactor outflow. The obtained results indicate that ML models are well suited for controlling catalytic peroxide disinfection. Using the initial bacterial count as an input parameter, ML models can quickly predict the hydrogen peroxide concentration and contact time needed to achieve the required final bacterial count. The bottleneck in the practical application of ML models will be the speed of obtaining reliable input data. The standard plate count is too slow (24 h), while fast methods allow detection within 9 h. The model showed good predictive performance under group‐based validation; however, because group‐level scores were lower than point‐level scores, its predictive ability should be interpreted within the tested operational range and further validated before extrapolation to other reactor geometries, water matrices, catalyst batches, or peroxide concentrations.
