A bright future for multidisciplinary approach to cancer care in the setting of limited resource
Authors: Mary-Ann Dadzie, Joel Yarney, Andrew Yaw Nyantakyi, Judith Naa Odey Tackie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Cancer management is complex and care should not be provided by any single specialty.•Multidisciplinary approach is key to providing wholistic care to cancer patients and should be the practice of all cancer centres.•International collaboration between low and high resourced centres provides a platform for training and support in establishing and maintaining an effective MDT in low resourced settings.Keywords: Head and neck, Pediatric, Rhabdomyosarcoma, Multidisciplinary, Limited resource

Introduction

Cancer care is an evolving and complex paradigm requiring several disciplines and expertise at various points of care which can lead to poor coordination and subsequent delays. Increasing body of evidence has shown that implementing a multidisciplinary strategy in the management of cancer is advantageous to patients, health care providers and the health system.

Although the use of Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) in cancer care is endorsed internationally, uptake varies by resource setting. Whiles its adoption has been rapid in several Western countries; it is a less common model of care in developing countries.

We present the multidisciplinary management of a girl with rhabdomyosarcoma of the pterygopalatine fossa, whiles highlighting the progress, benefits and challenges of MDT in low resourced setting.

Case presentation

An eight-year-old girl was referred from a regional hospital to the Pediatric Oncology Unit of the Child health Department on 10th November 2013, with a one year's history of a growing mass in the right buccal cavity. Initial assessment showed a cachectic looking girl with a 15 × 10 cm necrotic mass filling the oral cavity. Her weight was 17 kg and Hb was 7.6 g/dl. Renal and liver function test were essentially normal (Fig. 1).

Pre - chemotherapy disease extent.

A CT scan done reported a large mass in the right pterygopalatine fossa extending into the buccal, masticator and parapharyngeal spaces with involvement of the pterygoid and masseter muscles (Fig. 2). A chest x-ray and abdominal ultrasound were normal.

Pretreatment CT scan image.

Incisional biopsy of the lesion was taken and histopathology revealed an ulcerated tumor composed of short spindle cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scanty cytoplasm. Elsewhere there were cells with relatively abundant cytoplasm. On the basis of history, clinical, radiographic and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of parameningeal rhabdomyosarcoma was established.

Initial discussions were held in a multidisciplinary setting with the decision to optimize child's nutritional status via an nasogastric (NG) tube prior to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and followed by radiotherapy. She received 6 cycles of Cyclophosphamide, Vincristine, Actinomycin D and Doxurubicin which was commenced two weeks after admission with good response. Clinical response evaluation showed a 6 × 6 cm fleshy lesion in the left inner check extending into the retromolar trigone (Fig. 3).

Post chemotherapy response (3 Cycles).

A second MDT was held with the decision to have 3-D conformal radiotherapy to a dose of 50.4 Gray (Gy) in 28 fractions at 1.80Gy per fraction. Child commenced external beam radiotherapy with 6 Mv Linear accelerator which was at the time available at a private radiotherapy facility and completed without any significant acute toxicity. She completed six cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy (Vincristine and cyclophosphamide) without interruptions.

A follow- up Ct scan done for response assessment 3 months after radiotherapy revealed a residual mass in the masticator space necessitating a third MDT discussion. The decision was for child to have surgery. Right maxillary swing and infratemporal fossa biopsies was done and histopathology reported no residual tumor.

Follow up with clinical examination was carried out 3 monthly for the first 2 years, then 6 monthly until 5 years, and yearly afterwards. MRI done 6 months after surgery and yearly thereafter has shown no radiological evidence of recurrence as at last review date on 4th March 2024. Child returned to school 6 months after surgery and is currently in high school (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Multidisciplinary care (MDC) can be broadly defined as an integrated team approach to health care in which medical and allied health care professionals consider all relevant treatment options and develop an individual treatment plan for each patient [1]. An MDT approach is considered the gold standard for diagnosis and treatment of cancer and is recommended by cancer organizations as best practice [2,3]. When MDT meetings are focused on oncological patient's care, they are called Tumor Boards (TBs) [4].

Tumor boards were historically general weekly meetings where cases were presented at the discretion of attending physicians. However, with increasing sub specialization in medical and surgical oncology, as well as related pathology and imaging, most academic centers have subspecialty, organ-specific tumor boards A more recent variant of a tumor board is called a molecular tumor board where basic molecular, and microarray translational research is applied [5]. In countries where teams have to treat patients living in geographically diverse areas and with the Corona virus outbreak, video-conferenced meetings gained rapid popularity. Whichever, the structure used, MDTs should be standardized in terms of coordination, documentation of discussion and final report.

Developing countries are striving to breach the existing care gap between high- and low-income countries by incorporating multidisciplinary approach into cancer care. The increase in training of clinical oncologists and other subspecialties led to the interest in implementing this novel approach to care in our facility. This began with the breast multidisciplinary tumor board in 2005, which has over the years grown to become the major decision-making body in breast cancer management in the hospital and country as a whole. The success observed as well as experience gained with the breast MDT, provided a benchmark for the head and neck MDT which began in 2010, a pediatric MDT in 2013, gastro-intestinal MDT in 2015, gynecology MDT in 2018 and recently the pediatric neuro-oncology MDT in 2023. An average number of 500, 400, 300, 200 and 150 cases are discussed annually in the respective MDTs stated above.

The pediatric oncology unit of the teaching hospital had made several attempts to establish an MDT since 2012 without success. However, on the 28th of November 2013, the first successful MDT was held to discuss the case above, which at the time included the pediatric oncologist, radiation oncologist, Otorhinolaryngologist and radiologist. Initially, the pediatric MDT was held on demand and was erratic with poor participation of team members. However, with the incorporation of an MDT chair and coordinator, meetings are held weekly, and has experienced tremendous growth to involve other pediatric oncology centers across the country as well as participation from neighboring countries. There are currently dedicated radiation oncologists, general pediatric surgeons, radiologist, pathologist, maxillofacial and otorhinolaryngologist, orthopedic surgeons, neurosurgeon, hepatobiliary surgeon, pharmacists and pediatric oncology nurses in the pediatric MDT. Other specialists are invited as and when their expertise is required.

Currently, all MDT discussions are held weekly and has become an integral component of cancer care in our setting. This has become possible as a result of increased training in sub special areas such as head and neck surgery and hepatobiliary surgery, neuro-radiology and interventional radiology among others. Patient participation and contribution is a major factor in every discussion, because of the immense impact of socioeconomic, cultural and spiritual beliefs on patient's perception, acceptance and affordability of cancer treatment. In many resourced poor countries where cancer care is paid out of pocket, patient participation is key since it impacts on the kind of treatment a patient can afford. In the case study above, the child received external beam radiotherapy using 6Mv linear accelerator which was at that time, only available in a private radiotherapy center due to the benefits of parents’ private insurance coverage.

Over the years, a solid body of literature has supported the benefits of multidisciplinary approach to patients, healthcare providers and the system as a whole. Adherence to clinical guidelines is one major benefit [6]. The use of treatment guidelines has increased remarkably over the past few years leading to uniformity of treatment in cancer centers locally. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) harmonized guidelines is one of such commonly used guideline. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), American Society for Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and The International Society for Pediatric Oncology (SIOP) guidelines are also used and easily assessable online for health care providers and patients. These guidelines are however modified in some cases based on local experience and availability of resources.

Several studies have observed survival benefit from MDT meetings [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. These studies are however highly heterogeneous usually with small numbers of patients making the effect on survival unclear. Although there is no data to support improvement in outcome for patients managed under the multidisciplinary setting since its inception in our hospital, many successful outcomes would not have been possible, had these cases not been managed within combined clinics.

Coordination of care is one aspect that has been greatly improved since the introduction of tumor boards in our facility. In major referral centers, where various departments are separated geographically, administratively, and sometimes even by health delivery and treatment concepts, the care of cancer patients can be disorganized leading to delays and subsequently poorer outcomes. As seen in the case discussed, tumor board platform allows for open discussion and clear definition of patient's treatment trajectory which has led to a remarkable reduction in delay for surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and palliative care support. In the case discussed, patient had no delays in treatment because the treatment plan was well laid out and all parties concerned were enthusiastic about the success of this first MDT case discussion.

The educational opportunities for health workers and trainees cannot be overemphasized. With the forum bringing together experts from various specialties, a rich discussion of cases with variety of opinion are brought forward resulting in continuous education of members. Collaborations between our tumor boards and various educational societies such as well as Oncology centers in advanced countries have provided training opportunities and mentorship for health care providers. The members of the pediatric MDT have enjoyed the benefit of international collaborations with advanced centers such as St Jude Children's research hospital with massive support in training, mentorship and research. The current pediatric neuro-oncology MDT which commenced in June 2023 and supported by international experts was born out of the experience gained by members of the team from the St Jude Neuro -oncology training Seminar.

Despite the many benefits discussed above, the practice of MDT is fraught with many limitations which can be grouped into three main factors: Health care system, Health care providers and Patient related.

Health care system in many developing countries is highly fragmented with significant regional disparities and tortuous referral systems. Many government hospitals do not have complete oncology care capabilities whiles those with oncological facilities have autonomous and poorly connected specialty departments. Many patients are therefore managed initially without the input of oncologists and later referred to the oncologist. In these instances, retrieval of images, surgical reports and histopathology reports is challenging and leads to delay.

One major barrier related to health care providers is the issue of time. With the very high patient to doctor ratio, most practitioners are overwhelmed, making it difficult to find the appropriate time that is convenient for all stakeholders. Conflicting health professional roles, inadequate communication between specialists and primary care givers as well as hierarchical boundaries are some of the factors that hinder equal participation.

Patient related barrier, is probably the most influential in comparison to previously discussed challenges. In societies like ours, the diagnosis and management of cancers is highly dependent on patient's socioeconomic status, cultural and spiritual beliefs.

Lasting solutions to the limitations mentioned above would be the nationwide implementation of the electronic medical system that gives permission to assess medical records quickly and efficiently to facilitate MDT discussions and avoid delays in retrieving physical copies of medical information. Also, advocacy for sponsorship for training and exchange programs for MDT members will serve as a motivation for medical personnel. Finally, incorporating MDT into Continuous Medical Education (CME) program of the medical council will further enhance commitment of members and foster growth of tumor boards.

Conclusion

Multidisciplinary approach to cancer care is fundamental to providing the best possible care to cancer patients. International collaboration, expansion of current MDTs in major referral centers to include other hospitals in the country via virtual platform, as well as the implementation of comprehensive health coverage will have positive impact on the operation and success of MDTs in low resourced countries.
