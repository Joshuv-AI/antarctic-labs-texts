Comparative efficacy of liver resection vs. radiofrequency ablation for small hepatocellular carcinoma stratified by alpha-fetoprotein status: a retrospective cohort study using restricted mean survival time and number needed to treat analysis
Authors: Hong Chen, Junbin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe choice between liver resection (LR) and radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for small hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ≤3 cm remains controversial. This study evaluated whether alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) status modulates the comparative efficacy of these modalities using absolute benefit metrics.MethodsPatients with T1N0M0 HCC (≤3 cm) treated with LR or RFA were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program database. After 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), cancer-specific survival (CSS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) and number needed to treat (NNT) were utilized to quantify the absolute clinical gain of LR over RFA.ResultsAmong 1,119 identified patients, 235 matched pairs were analyzed. LR demonstrated superior survival in the overall cohort (P<0.001). A significant interaction was observed between treatment modality and AFP status (Pinteraction<0.01). In AFP-negative patients, LR provided a profound survival advantage [CSS: hazard ratio (HR) =0.362; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.220–0.594; P<0.001], translating into a substantial RMST extension of 8.1 months and a low NNT of 4. Conversely, in the AFP-positive subgroup, this surgical advantage was notably attenuated (CSS: HR =0.800; 95% CI: 0.555–1.152; P=0.23), with the RMST extension dropping to 3.5 months and the NNT increasing to 12.ConclusionsThe superiority of LR over RFA in small HCC is significantly modulated by AFP-defined tumor biology. AFP-negative patients derive profound absolute survival gains from surgery, whereas benefits in AFP-positive cases are marginal, supporting RFA as a viable alternative for biologically aggressive tumors.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), liver resection, radiofrequency ablation (RFA), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), propensity score matching (PSM)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Liver resection (LR) provides a profound absolute survival dividend over radiofrequency ablation (RFA) in alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)-negative small hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (≤3 cm), yielding ana restricted mean survival time (RMST) extension of 8.1 months and a number needed to treat (NNT) of 4.

• This surgical advantage is significantly attenuated in AFP-positive patients (NNT =12).

What is known and what is new?

• While LR and RFA are both curative options for small HCC, treatment triage traditionally relies on tumor size rather than intrinsic tumor biology.

• By utilizing AFP status as a biological stratifier and integrating RMST and NNT analyses, this study moves beyond relative risks to quantify the absolute clinical gains of surgery.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The superiority of surgery is not uniform but heavily dictated by tumor biology.

• Multidisciplinary teams should adopt a “biology-first” approach: LR should be strongly prioritized for AFP-negative patients, whereas RFA is a highly viable, less invasive alternative for biologically aggressive (AFP-positive) tumors.

Introduction

The global burden of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) continues to escalate, maintaining its position as a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. For patients diagnosed at an early stage, achieving long-term curative outcomes remains the primary clinical objective (1). Currently, both liver resection (LR) and radiofrequency ablation (RFA) are endorsed by major clinical guidelines—including AASLD 2023, EASL 2024, and the newly updated Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) 2026 strategy—as the standard of care for tumors ≤3 cm (2,3). While these guidelines increasingly favor an “ablation-first” approach for early lesions, the optimal therapeutic choice (RFA or LR) remains a persistent dilemma for multidisciplinary tumor boards.

The debate centers on the trade-off between procedural morbidity and oncological radicality (4-7). Proponents of RFA emphasize its minimally invasive nature and lower complication rates—a priority for preserving liver function (2,8). Conversely, surgical advocates point toward the “anatomic” advantage of LR, which provides wider margins to eradicate occult microvascular invasion (MVI) and microsatellite lesions (9). Crucially, the BCLC 2026 update introduces the “CUSE” (Complexity, Uncertainty, Subjectivity, and Emotion) framework, acknowledging that rigid algorithmic decisions often fail to account for the intrinsic biological diversity of HCC (1). Liu and colleagues found that patients undergoing surgery had better survival than patients undergoing RFA (10). While another randomized controlled trial conducted by Kawaguchi and colleagues showed no significant survival differences between treatment groups (11). Despite numerous clinical trials, findings remain heterogeneous (10-13), likely attributable to the fact that treatment selection still relies heavily on anatomical dimensions rather than biological signatures.

Emerging evidence suggests that tumor size alone is a crude surrogate for prognosis. The European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) 2024 guidelines now strongly advocate for the routine measurement of serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) as a pivotal window into the tumor’s underlying molecular aggressiveness, specifically its correlation with high-grade differentiation and MVI (2). Elevated AFP levels serve as a biological marker for tumors that are likely to bypass the localized thermal zone of RFA through early vascular dissemination (14-16). Despite this consensus on its prognostic value, there is a profound lack of evidence regarding whether AFP status can act as a predictive biomarker to guide the selection between LR and RFA. Furthermore, clinical decision-making is hindered by a reliance on relative risks [hazard ratios (HRs)]; consistent with the growing emphasis on “personalized benefit assessment”, clinicians require intuitive metrics of absolute gain, such as the restricted mean survival time (RMST) and the number needed to treat (NNT) (17,18), to justify the higher risks of surgery over ablation. Recently, a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program-based study elegantly utilized competing risk models to confirm the overall survival (OS) advantage of LR over RFA in solitary HCC ≤5 cm, highlighting the limitations of traditional Cox models in overestimating cancer-specific mortality (19). However, identifying which specific subset of patients derives the maximum absolute benefit from surgery remains a critical unmet need. Current treatment allocations heavily rely on tumor size, often neglecting the intrinsic biological aggressiveness of HCC. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether AFP status can stratify the absolute clinical dividend of LR over RFA in small HCC.

By employing propensity score matching (PSM) and advanced statistical metrics including RMST and NNT, we aimed to delineate the survival disparity between LR and RFA across different AFP strata. We hypothesized that the “survival dividend” of LR over RFA is not uniform but is instead modified by the tumor’s biological signature as represented by AFP status, providing a data-driven basis for a “biology-first” approach in small HCC management. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0781/rc).

Methods

Data source and patient selection

Data for this retrospective cohort study were extracted from the SEER database (http://www.seer.cancer.gov/). Since the SEER database contains de-identified, publicly available data, institutional review board approval and informed consent were waived. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The latest data resource used was the SEER*Stat Database: Incidence - SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2024 Sub [2000–2022].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) diagnosis of HCC [identified by International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd edition (ICD-O-3) code 8170–8175] between 2010 and 2017; (II) stage T1N0M0 disease according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Staging Manual; and (III) patients who underwent either RFA or LR as their primary local treatment. The exclusion criteria included: (I) survival time of less than 1 month; (II) multiple primary tumors or HCC not being the primary tumor; (III) unknown AFP status; (IV) solitary tumor >3 cm in maximum diameter; and (V) receiving chemotherapy or radiotherapy. No artificial intelligence tools were employed in the research design, data analysis, or manuscript preparation.

Variables and endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study was cancer-specific survival (CSS), and the second primary endpoint was OS. CSS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death specifically attributable to HCC, while OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. Baseline demographic and clinicopathological variables were extracted, including patient ID, age at diagnosis, sex, race, income, year of diagnosis, marital status, seer stage, pathological grading, tumor size, tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, fibrosis score (Ishak score), AFP status (negative vs. positive), chemotherapy, radiotherapy, treatment modality (RFA vs. LR), survival time, survival status, and cause of death.

Statistical analysis

Given the retrospective nature of the study and the inherent selection bias in treatment allocation, a PSM analysis was performed to balance the baseline confounders between the RFA and LR groups. We utilized a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm without replacement, applying a strict caliper width of 0.1 standard deviations to balance all confounders available. The balance of covariates before and after matching was evaluated using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with an SMD <0.1 considered to indicate optimal balance.

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Survival curves for CSS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. To identify independent prognostic factors, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression models were constructed. Crucially, to assess whether the treatment efficacy was modified by tumor biology, a pre-specified interaction term between treatment modality and AFP status (treatment × AFP) was incorporated into the multivariate model. Because only one primary interaction was tested, no multiplicity adjustment was applied.

To further quantify the absolute clinical benefit of LR over RFA without relying on the PH assumption, we calculated the RMST at a predefined follow-up time point. Furthermore, the NNT, defined as the number of patients who need to be treated with LR rather than RFA to prevent one additional death, was calculated based on the absolute risk reduction. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.2, and a two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The following R packages were used in this study: tidyverse, MatchIt, and cobalt packages for data processing and PSM; survival and survminer packages for survival analysis; forestplot package for generating forest plots; survRM2 packages for RMST analysis.

Results

A flow diagram of the patient selection process is detailed in Figure 1. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 1,119 patients diagnosed with small HCC (T1N0M0, ≤3 cm) were initially identified from the SEER database, including 829 patients treated with RFA and 290 patients treated with LR. To mitigate potential selection bias and balance baseline confounding factors, a 1:1 PSM was performed. After matching, 235 patients were retained in each group. As shown in Table 1, all baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups, with all SMDs less than 0.1, indicating a highly comparable matched cohort (Figure S1).

Survival outcomes and prognostic factors in the overall PSM cohort

In the matched cohort, the LR group demonstrated significantly higher survival rates compared to the RFA group (Figure 2). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSS rates were 94.4%, 86.0%, and 76.9% for LR vs. 93.5%, 72.9%, and 61.8% for RFA. Similarly, the OS rates favored the LR group at all time points (all P<0.05) (Table S1).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves of CSS and OS comparing LR vs. RFA in the propensity score-matched cohort. CSS, cancer-specific survival; LR, liver resection; OS, overall survival; RFA, radiofrequency ablation.

To identify independent risk factors and explore potential modifiers of treatment efficacy, univariate and multivariate Cox PH regression models were constructed (Table 2). In the multivariate analysis incorporating all variables, independent risk factors for CSS included age, race, year of diagnosis, marital status, and treatment. Similarly, sex, age, race, year of diagnosis, marital status, and treatment were identified as independent risk factors for OS. Notably, while AFP status itself was not an independent prognostic factor for CSS (P=0.92) or OS (P=0.41), the interaction term between treatment modality and AFP status (treatment × AFP) was identified as a significant independent predictor for both CSS (P=0.005) and OS (P=0.007). This critical finding indicates that the survival benefit derived from LR vs. RFA varies significantly depending on the patient’s AFP status.

Survival outcomes stratified by AFP status

To further elucidate the impact of tumor biology on therapeutic efficacy, we performed a landmark survival analysis within the AFP-negative and AFP-positive subgroups.

The AFP-negative subgroup: The survival advantage of LR was most pronounced in this cohort (Figure 3). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSS rates for the LR group were 93.7%, 89.4%, and 83.9%, respectively, which were significantly higher than the 94.2%, 68.5%, and 58.9% observed in the RFA group (P<0.001). For OS, the LR group also maintained a substantial lead with 1-, 3-, and 5-year rates of 93.7%, 87.4%, and 79.0%, compared to 89.8%, 58.8%, and 48.4% in the RFA group (P<0.001) (Table S1). Subgroup analysis confirmed that LR was associated with a 63.8% reduction in the risk of cancer-specific death [HR =0.362; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.220–0.594; P<0.001] and a 60.9% reduction in the risk of all-cause mortality (HR =0.391; 95% CI: 0.257–0.597; P<0.001) in this subgroup (Figure 4A).

The AFP-positive subgroup: in contrast, the survival disparity between the two treatments narrowed significantly in the AFP-positive cohort (Figure 3). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSS rates for the LR group were 94.9%, 83.6%, and 71.8%, while those for the RFA group were 93.1%, 75.3%, and 63.5% (Table S1). Despite a numerical lead for LR, the difference did not reach statistical significance (HR =0.800; 95% CI: 0.555–1.152; P=0.23). Similarly, for OS, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates showed no significant difference between LR (92.8%, 76.8%, and 60.5%) and RFA (90.4%, 67.1%, and 53.4%), with the HR failing to cross the threshold of significance (HR =0.744; 95% CI: 0.551–1.005; P=0.052; Figure 4A).

Quantification of absolute clinical benefit: RMST and NNT analysis

To translate statistical probabilities into actionable clinical metrics, we quantified the absolute therapeutic advantages of LR over RFA through RMST and NNT (Figure 4B). Regarding CSS within the entire matched population, LR yielded a mean survival extension of 5.315 months compared to RFA (P<0.001), corresponding to an NNT of 7. However, the magnitude of this absolute benefit exhibited a striking divergence when stratified by AFP status. In the AFP-negative subgroup, the survival dividend of surgery was particularly robust; patients undergoing LR gained an additional 8.100 months of RMST, resulting in a remarkably low NNT of 4 (P<0.001). This advantage was notably attenuated in the AFP-positive cohort, where the RMST extension dropped to 3.485 months and the NNT rose to 12, indicating a significantly diminished absolute benefit for surgical resection in this biologically aggressive subset (P=0.09). Consistent patterns were observed in the OS analysis across all corresponding groups.

Discussion

The optimal local therapeutic strategy for small (≤3 cm) HCC remains a subject of ongoing debate (11,20,21). While RFA is favored for its minimally invasive nature, LR is traditionally considered to offer superior oncological radicality (6,20). In this population-based study utilizing rigorous PSM, we demonstrated that LR provides better OS and CSS than RFA for small HCC. The results were similar to those reported by Liu and colleagues (10). Our findings align with and further refine recent literature. For instance, Zeng recently demonstrated the statistical superiority of LR over RFA by mitigating the competing risks of non-cancer-related mortality (19). While their use of the Fine-Gray model provided more accurate relative risks, relative metrics often fail to convey the tangible clinical magnitude of a treatment effect to surgeons and patients. By employing RMST and NNT, our study translates these relative advantages into absolute time-domain metrics. More importantly, we demonstrated that the surgical superiority observed in generalized cohorts is not uniform; it is profoundly dictated by AFP status. The remarkable NNT of 4 in AFP-negative patients provides compelling, data-driven justification for surgical intervention, whereas the NNT of 12 in AFP-positive patients suggests a diminished return on the surgical trauma, favoring RFA.

Our baseline observation that LR generally outperforms RFA in the matched cohort aligns with several robust studies (20,22,23). The surgical advantage primarily stems from the anatomical resection of tumor-bearing portal territories and the achievement of wider margins. These factors are essential for eradicating occult MVI and microsatellite lesions, which are widely recognized as the primary culprits of early recurrence (2). Moreover, for tumors approaching the 3 cm limit, the efficacy of RFA can be compromised by the “heat sink effect” near major vessels or the physical limits of the thermal zone, leading to incomplete ablation (24,25). However, our interaction analysis suggests that relying solely on anatomical dimensions—a practice challenged by the BCLC 2026 “CUSE” (Complexity, Uncertainty, Subjectivity, Emotion) framework—may lead to suboptimal treatment allocation.

However, the most compelling finding of our study is the significant treatment × AFP interaction, which fundamentally redefines patient selection. It has been reported that the positive rate of AFP in HCC is only 60–80% (26,27), indicating HCC heterogeneity and suggesting AFP stratification may guide treatment choices. In the AFP-negative subgroup, the superiority of LR was overwhelming. This cohort represents the ideal candidates for a “biology-first” surgical approach. AFP-negative HCCs are frequently associated with well-differentiated phenotypes and a lower incidence of MVI or early systemic dissemination. For these biologically indolent and truly localized tumors, the primary mode of failure is local recurrence rather than distant metastasis (28). Consequently, when LR is applied to an indolent localized tumor, the surgical advantage in local control directly translates into a profound, long-term survival benefit.

Conversely, in the AFP-positive subgroup, the survival advantage of LR over RFA was heavily attenuated and failed to reach statistical significance (P=0.23). This phenomenon highlights the biological limits of localized surgery. Elevated AFP is a well-established surrogate marker for aggressive tumor biology, poor differentiation, and a high propensity for early vascular invasion and occult systemic micro-metastasis (28). In such high-risk patients, the systemic biological threat “competes” with the local therapeutic benefit. Even if LR achieves a marginally better local clearance than RFA, these patients frequently succumb to distant metastasis or multifocal intrahepatic recurrences driven by circulating tumor cells (29). In other words, the aggressive systemic nature of AFP-positive tumors dilutes the local control advantage provided by the LR. Therefore, for AFP-positive small HCC, RFA might be a reasonable and less invasive alternative, or alternatively, these patients might require rigorous adjuvant systemic therapies (such as targeted therapy or immunotherapy) following local treatment. Emerging immunotherapy strategies may interact with local treatment selection in biologically meaningful ways. RFA is known to induce immunogenic cell death, potentially enhancing responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas LR removes the bulk tumor and its immunosuppressive milieu, which may create a more favorable environment for subsequent systemic immunotherapy. Given that AFP-positive tumors often exhibit a more immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, combining local therapy with immunotherapy could be particularly promising in this subgroup. Ongoing clinical trials are expected to clarify the optimal sequencing and combination of these modalities, and future studies incorporating AFP stratification will be critical to personalizing such approaches. However, AFP is an imperfect surrogate for underlying tumor biology. Other biomarkers, including protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist II (PIVKA-II), systemic inflammatory indices, and molecular subclassifications, may provide complementary prognostic information. Integrating these markers into future prospective studies could enable a more refined biology-driven approach to treatment selection.

A major methodological strength of this study is the integration of RMST and NNT analyses, which offer a more robust and clinically relevant evaluation than traditional Cox regression alone (17,18,30). While HR are widely used to provide measures of relative risk, their validity depends heavily on the PH assumption—a condition frequently violated in long-term oncological follow-ups where the treatment effect of surgery vs. ablation may evolve over time. In contrast, RMST provides a robust, area-under-the-curve interpretation of the survival benefit that is independent of the PH assumption. By quantifying the absolute survival extension in terms of actual months gained (e.g., 8.1 months in the AFP-negative cohort), RMST translates abstract statistical probabilities into tangible temporal gains that are easily interpreted by clinicians and patients alike. This temporal granularity is complemented by the NNT analysis, which serves as a direct metric of therapeutic efficiency: in AFP-negative patients, only four surgeries are needed to prevent one additional death compared to RFA, whereas this requirement triples to 12 in the AFP-positive cohort. Together, the use of RMST and NNT moves the analysis beyond simple relative risks, directly supporting the call for moving beyond rigid algorithms toward a more nuanced, personalized benefit assessment.

Despite its robust design, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective study utilizing the SEER database, unmeasured confounding variables may exist despite rigorous PSM. Crucial clinical parameters detailing the background liver disease—such as the Child-Pugh score, indocyanine green retention rate at 15 minutes (ICG-R15) clearance, and specific viral hepatitis [hepatitis B virus (HBV)/hepatitis C virus (HCV)] etiologies—are unavailable in the SEER registry. These factors are known to influence both treatment selection and prognosis, potentially biasing the observed superiority of LR toward an overestimate. In addition, SEER lacks data on tumor location relative to major intrahepatic vessels, bile ducts, or the liver capsule. Perivascular or subcapsular tumor location can influence RFA feasibility, completeness of ablation, and local recurrence rates. This unmeasured factor represents another source of potential residual confounding. Second, the database lacks granular procedural details regarding RFA (e.g., ablation margin sizes, equipment used) and LR (anatomical vs. non-anatomical resection). Third, because SEER records AFP only as a binary categorical variable, we were unable to evaluate alternative thresholds (e.g., 200 ng/mL, 400 ng/mL) that might better discriminate biologically aggressive tumors. Future studies employing continuous AFP measurements or standardized cutoffs are warranted to determine the optimal threshold for treatment stratification. Finally, data on tumor recurrence and subsequent salvage treatments, which heavily influence OS, were not available for analysis. Future large-scale, prospective randomized controlled trials stratified by AFP status are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings suggest that for T1N0M0 HCC ≤3 cm, therapeutic selection must transcend simplistic tumor-size criteria and instead prioritize biological risk stratification via AFP status. The survival dividend of LR is profoundly concentrated in the AFP-negative population; for these patients, surgical resection remains the definitive gold standard and should be prioritized whenever hepatic reserve is sufficient. Conversely, in the presence of an AFP-positive biological signature, the oncological advantage of LR appears to be substantially eroded, rendering RFA a compelling and less invasive alternative for this high-risk subset.
