Immersion Deaths in Seoul: Implications of Decomposition for Postmortem Diagnosis of Drowning
Authors: Dong Yeon Lee, Hajin Kim, Ga Eun Kim, Eun-Byoul Jung, Soong Deok Lee, Moon-Young Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrowning is defined as a process of respiratory impairment caused by submersion in water and is a significant cause of death worldwide. However, postmortem diagnosis of drowning remains a major challenge in forensic pathology, primarily because it relies on the exclusion of all other possible causes based on only a few pathognomonic findings during autopsy, particularly in cases involving decomposition. This study reviewed a series of immersion deaths in Seoul by analyzing demographic and epidemiological factors, their associations with the cause and manner of death, and the impact of decomposition on drowning diagnosis.MethodsAutopsy data from 113 immersion death cases examined at the Medical Examiner’s Office of Seoul National University College of Medicine between April 2016 and October 2023 were collected and analyzed. Drowning findings were assessed with a focus on foam in the airway, emphysema aquosum, fluid in the sphenoid sinus, petromastoid hemorrhage, drowning fluid in the stomach and duodenum, aortic hemolytic staining, and the diatom test results.ResultsThe majority (47.8%) of immersed bodies were recovered from the Hangang River and determined to be drowning suicides. The cases in which the manner of death was undetermined exhibited more advanced decomposition compared to suicides, with epidemiological patterns suggesting social isolation or financial hardship. In terms of drowning findings, decomposition significantly affected the detection of foam in the airway, emphysema aquosum, aortic hemolytic staining, and the diatom test results, whereas other findings, such as fluid in the sphenoid sinus, petromastoid hemorrhage, and fluid in the stomach and duodenum, appeared relatively resistant to decomposition.ConclusionThis study outlines the landscape of immersion deaths in Seoul, emphasizing the impact of decomposition on diagnosing drowning and determining the manner of death. Further research is warranted to enhance diagnostic accuracy and to develop standardized guidelines for drowning diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Drowning is defined as the process of respiratory impairment due to submersion or immersion in liquid water, and water often plays a role in fatal outcomes. A systemic postmortem diagnosis of drowning is crucial because various circumstances, from natural death to homicide, are possible in water.1 However, the diagnosis of drowning is challenging because it relies on ruling out other causes and the lack of objective criteria.2 Specific pathological changes help in diagnosing drowning, including foam in the airways, emphysema aquosum, fluid in the sphenoid sinus, hemorrhage in the petromastoid bone (petromastoid hemorrhage), drowning fluid in the stomach and duodenum, and the presence of diatoms.34 These findings vary significantly depending on the situation and require careful interpretation.1 This is particularly difficult in decomposed bodies because drowning findings may change over time, compounded by a lack of extensive studies. Aortic hemolytic staining, recently proposed as a potential diagnostic indicator of drowning, requires further investigation because its effects during decomposition have been assessed in only a few cases.56 Environmental factors such as location also complicate its diagnosis.2

Owing to the high suicide rate in the Republic of Korea, the Hangang River in Seoul is a common site for suicide attempts and fatalities through drowning. Approximately 20% of the population in the Republic of Korea resides in Seoul. The Hangang River runs through the center of Seoul, spanning approximately 490 km in length and averaging about 1 km in width.7 Its width is comparable to that of the Hudson River (average 1.5 km) and is significantly wider than those of the Thames (average 265 m) and Seine (average 200 m) rivers. With over 30 bridges crossing it, the Hangang River is highly accessible to residents and is frequented not only for various water activities but also for suicide attempts. These conditions highlight the need for systematic analysis of drowning cases in this region from both epidemiological and forensic perspectives.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a comprehensive analysis of immersion cases in Seoul by examining their epidemiological patterns, postmortem findings, and the relationship with decomposition.

METHODS

Sample and data collection

Overall, 1,003 autopsies conducted at the Medical Examiner’s Office in charge of northeastern Seoul between April 2016 and October 2023 were retrospectively reviewed, whereas 113 (11.3%) immersion cases were identified. For each case, personal information (sex, age, medical conditions, residence type, and family type), discovery details (date, time, and place), last known survival times, autopsy findings, and causes and manners of death were collected. The last known survival time was determined from a police investigation report, which primarily relied on statements from family members, acquaintances, or witnesses, as well as records from CCTV footage and mobile phones.

Autopsy findings

The postmortem time interval (PMI) was calculated from the last known survival time to the autopsy, as the exact time of death could not be determined. Decomposition was assessed using the total body score (TBS) system constructed by Megyesi et al.8 and Moffatt et al.9 The aquatic decomposition scoring (ADS) method developed by van Daalen et al.10 for immersion bodies was also applied in this study. The drowning findings assessed included foam in the airway, emphysema aquosum, fluid in the sphenoid sinus, petromastoid hemorrhage, drowning fluid in the stomach and duodenum, aortic hemolytic staining, and the diatom test. Foam in the airway and fluid in the sphenoid sinus were classified as present or absent, and the latter was measured in milliliters. Emphysema aquosum, petromastoid hemorrhage, drowning fluid in the stomach and duodenum, and aortic hemolytic staining were graded as completely present, partially present, or absent (Fig. 1). Emphysema aquosum was classified as complete when both lungs are inflated and meet anteriorly to the heart and partial when they are inflated but do not meet or when only one lung was inflated due to pleural adhesions. Petromastoid hemorrhage was considered partial when unilateral or mild. Drowning fluid was classified as complete if the fluid layer is distinctly separated from the underlying food layer and partial if the food content’s contour remains visible beneath the fluid layer. Aortic hemolytic staining was scored as complete if the entire intima appeared red, similar in color to the myocardium, and partial if it was streaky or lighter in color than the myocardium. Diatoms were detected in the lungs, heart, liver, and kidneys. Toxicology tests for alcohol and other substances were conducted using peripheral blood from the femoral vessels (heart blood, if unavailable) or tissues from the liver, kidney, or muscle. For the decomposed bodies, n-propanol levels were measured to assess the postmortem intrinsic ethanol production.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean and standard deviation (SD) or, if not normally distributed, median and interquartile range. Spearman’s analysis was used to identify the correlations among the decomposition indices (PMI, TBS, and ADS), and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to examine the relationship between these indices and the manner of death. Fisher’s exact test was used to determine differences in autopsy findings between drowning and non-drowning deaths. Wilcoxon’s rank sum test and the Kruskal–Wallis test were used to assess the influence of decomposition on drowning findings, followed by logistic regression analyses. χ2 tests identified the association between stomach and duodenal fluid. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.1.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), Microsoft 365 Excel version 2406 Build 16.0.17726.20078 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), RStudio version 2024.04.0+735, and R version 4.4.0 (Boston, MA, USA), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sungkyunkwan University and the requirement for informed consent was waived (SKKU-2021-06-001).

RESULTS

General characteristics of immersion deaths

As provided in Table 1, 89 (78.8%) individuals were male and 24 (21.2%) were female, with ages ranging from 16 to 85 years (mean 48.4 years). The most frequent age group was the 50s, with only a few cases involving teenagers and individuals in their 80s. Most cases (90.3%) were diagnosed as having drowned. Sex, age, PMI, TBS, and ADS showed no significant differences between the drowning and non-drowning deaths. TBS, ranging from 0 to 15, and ADS, ranging from 0 to 13, were highly correlated (Spearman’s ρ = 0.913, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). PMI ranged from 20 to 10,137 hours and was significantly correlated with TBS (Spearman’s ρ = 0.737, P < 0.001) and ADS (Spearman’s ρ = 0.730, P < 0.001). In a few drowning cases, injury (7, 6.2%) or psychotropic drug intoxication (3, 2.7%) crucially contributed to death (Table 2). The non-drowning causes of death included 3 injuries, 3 ischemic heart diseases, 1 hypothermia, and 4 unknown causes.

In terms of the manner of death, most deaths were suicides (58, 51.3%) or were undetermined (47, 41.6%). After excluding cases highly suggestive of intrinsic alcohol production due to decomposition, 33.3% of suicides and 64.0% of undetermined cases were alcohol-positive, with an average concentration of 0.16% in the former. At least 1 drug, mostly legal except for a single case of methamphetamine, was detected in 39.7% of suicides. Mental illnesses were reported in 44.8% of the suicides, including 14 depressions, 7 schizophrenias, 5 chronic alcoholics, 4 insomnias, and 3 bipolar disorders. Circulatory diseases were prevalent in both suicides (36.2%) and undetermined deaths (51.1%). Among suicide cases, most victims lived with 1 (41.4%) or more (22.4%) family members and resided in general housing (81.0%), such as apartments, detached houses, or studios. These instances were relatively rare when the manner of death was undetermined. Instead, both family presence and residence type were unidentifiable in more than half of the undetermined cases (57.4% and 63.8%, respectively), despite the fact that the identities of all but 2 bodies were confirmed through fingerprint analysis. Interestingly, the decomposition indices of undetermined deaths tended to be higher than those of suicide, although there were no statistically significant differences after Bonferroni correction.

The Hangang River (90.3%) was the most common location for body recovery, with summer being the most frequent season (31.0%). TBS and ADS were higher in summer than those in other seasons (both P < 0.001 in spring and winter, P = 0.005 and P = 0.029 in autumn, respectively), resulting in a high proportion of undetermined cases (57.1%).

Autopsy findings of drowning deaths

The presence of drowning findings in drowning and non-drowning deaths is shown in Fig. 3. Fluid in the sphenoid sinus (P = 0.002), drowning fluid in the stomach (P = 0.022) or duodenum (P < 0.001), and aortic hemolytic staining (P = 0.025) were significantly more frequent in drowning deaths than in non-drowning deaths. The logistic regression analysis (Fig. 4) showed that the likelihood of observing foam in the airway and emphysema aquosum, either completely or partially, significantly decreased with advancing decomposition. In contrast, aortic hemolytic staining tended to appear more frequently as decomposition progressed; however, statistical significance was observed only when it was completely present. Because all decomposition indices were highly correlated, they yielded similar results for all drowning findings except for the diatom test. Diatom tests were more frequently positive with higher ADS (P = 0.018), whereas the PMI and TBS showed no significant differences.

Conversely, fluid in the sphenoid sinus, petromastoid hemorrhage, and drowning fluid in the stomach and duodenum showed no significant correlation with decomposition. This indicates that they do not disappear or are not artificially promoted by decomposition, making them robust markers of drowning against decomposition. For the cases with fluid in the sphenoid sinus, the average amount was 0.78 mL (0.1–5 mL, SD = 0.86), and the amount was not associated with decomposition. The presence of drowning fluid in the stomach and duodenum was correlated (P < 0.001).

Diatom test results

Thirty genera were detected in the diatom tests, with Aulacoseira, Cyclotella, and Navicula being the most common. Aulacoseira were most frequently detected in summer, with a sharp decline in spring and winter (Fig. 5). Other genera, except Melosira, were most frequently observed in spring, and their abundance decreased in summer and autumn. When focusing on non-pulmonary organs, diatoms were detected in the following cases: the kidney (5 cases), the liver (2 cases), both the heart and kidney (1 case), and simultaneously in the liver, heart, and kidney (1 case). In all these cases, drowning was confirmed as the cause of death. In the lungs with diatoms, the number of genera increased with the decomposition indices (P = 0.004, 0.003, and 0.002 for PMI, TBS, and ADS, respectively); however, the number of diatoms was not significantly different.

DISCUSSION

Both immersion and drowning cases in this study included highly decomposed bodies. In most medicolegal autopsies involving decomposed bodies, determining the precise PMI is challenging because of the frequent lack of the exact time of death. Decomposition indices have been proposed to mitigate this limitation and are extensively used in forensic research and practice. In this study, the TBS and ADS, which are representative scoring systems for assessing human decomposition, showed a strong positive correlation. The ADS was derived from the TBS to provide a standardized way to quantify the degree of decomposition of bodies found in water based on the understanding that patterns and rates of decomposition differ between terrestrial and aquatic environments.10 Although studies have compared ADS with accumulated degree days or postmortem submersion interval,111213 a direct comparison with TBS has not yet been reported. Additionally, although the PMI was estimated using the last known survival time, it showed a strong correlation with both the TBS and ADS.

When examined as a whole, the most common scenario of immersion deaths in this study was “suicidal drowning in the Hangang River,” which accounted for 47.8% (54 of 113) of all cases. Conversely, in Connecticut, USA, only 16.6% of 500 bodies retrieved from water were suicides, whereas 75.6% were accidents.14 In a 4-year study of 123 bodies recovered from the New York City waterways, 42% were suicides, and 41% were undetermined.15 Another study of 34 drowning cases in the Torrens River, South Australia, reported 44% accidents, 32% suicides, and 21% undetermined deaths.16 The findings of this study reflect the high suicide rate in the Republic of Korea and the high accessibility of the Hangang River to suicide attempts. Comparing the mortality rate of intentional self-harm by drowning in Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development countries from 2012 to 2014, the Republic of Korea (= South Korea) ranked third, with a rate of 0.89 per 100,000 individuals.17 According to Statistics Korea, although the rate of all drowning deaths has decreased from 1.3 to 0.9 per 100,000 individuals between 2016 and 2022, intentional drowning deaths, mainly suicides, have gradually increased.18 The 2023 report of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency on emergency department visits for drowning indicated that self-inflicted drowning was the most frequent, especially in the 15–44 year age group.19 Among the 3,086 suicide attempts from bridges over rivers or streams in Korea between 2016 and 2020, 78.1% occurred in the metropolitan areas around Seoul.20 This may be attributed to the high accessibility of the Hangang River, with 33 bridges by 2024. The Water Rescue Team of the Seoul Special Rescue Services and the Hangang River Police of the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency were established to conduct lifesaving and search operations. Their swift responses have contributed significantly to the high survival rate of individuals attempting suicide.21 According to the Seoul Metropolitan Government, the survival rate of individuals rescued after suicide attempts from Hangang River bridges increased from 96.7% to 99.6% between 2018 and 2023, whereas the number of emergency dispatches increased from 430 to 1,035 during the same period. Their active presence may explain the low rate of accidental drowning deaths in the Hangang River, despite the high frequency of recreational and water-related activities throughout the year.

Among the suicide cases in this study, 46.6% had a mental illness, and the most common were depression and schizophrenia. Additionally, 39.7% were positive for drugs, and 33.3% consumed alcohol before death. One previous study reported that 20.9% of those who intentionally drowned had a history of depression, while none of the accidental drowning cases did.22 Another study suggested that schizophrenia was a risk factor for choosing drowning as the suicidal method.23 In a Swedish study, alcohol consumption was reported in 44% of unintentional drowning cases, 24% of intentional drowning cases, and 45% of undetermined cases.24 Among the 52 suicidal drownings in Texas, half had a history of psychiatric disease and/or substance abuse; 21 were positive for alcohol, including 13 cases suspected of postmortem intrinsic production, and 24 were positive for drugs.25 In South Australia, 43.1% of suicidal drowning cases involved mental illness, 20.3% were positive for alcohol consumption, and 16.3% were prescription drugs.26 Both previous and current studies indicate that suicidal drowning victims tend to have mental illness and that drugs or alcohol can be detected at various frequencies depending on the population.

In most cases of undetermined deaths, the deceased lived alone or had no family members who could be contacted by the police. Such circumstances may prevent them from being reported as missing or considered at risk of suicide. Similar to cases of solitary death, which are often detected by distinctive odors and insect activity resulting from advanced decomposition,27 these immersed bodies were accidentally discovered by strangers or patrol officers only after surfacing due to decomposition. In most instances, actual residences were not identified. Some of them lived in insecure housing, such as Gosiwon or Jjokbang, which are unique types of poor housing in the urban areas of Korea, characterized by extremely limited private spaces of less than 6 m2 and minimal rental costs. This implies that these victims experienced social isolation or financial hardship, which could be a motivation for suicide, indicating that a substantial proportion of the undetermined deaths might indeed have been suicides.

Immersed bodies were recovered most frequently in summer, likely because higher water temperatures accelerate decomposition and reduce the psychological barrier to water entry, as suggested in a previous study.28 Moreover, bodies retrieved from the water decompose rapidly, further complicating the diagnosis of drowning.29 In this study, foam in the airway and emphysema aquosum tended to disappear over time, whereas aortic hemolytic staining increased with decomposition. The effect of decomposition on the presence of foam in the airway has been observed in a previous study that compared decomposed and non-decomposed drowning bodies.30 Another study focusing on fresh bodies reported a much higher frequency of foam formation in airways.31 A decreasing trend in emphysema aquosum in decomposed bodies has been reported, particularly in cases of advanced decomposition.1 Aortic hemolytic staining has been suggested as a drowning finding56; however, it has been observed in bodies with decomposition, sepsis, and burns.32 This implies that in a significantly decomposed drowning body, aortic hemolytic staining could be produced by the decomposition itself rather than by drowning. The relationship between aortic hemolytic staining and decomposition observed in this study supports this hypothesis, as pointed out in previous studies.33 Further research is needed to understand the mechanisms underlying drowning hemolysis and aortic intima staining. In this study, the positivity rate of the diatom test and the number of diatom genera increased with the degree of decomposition. The number of diatoms detected in the lungs was not associated with decomposition, indicating that even with advanced decomposition, the number of diatoms can support drowning if it is sufficiently high. The use of the diatom test as a diagnostic tool for drowning is considered reliable, even for decomposed bodies.34 However, these results should be interpreted cautiously, especially when diatoms are detected only in the lungs, owing to the reported correlation between decomposition and diatom positivity,3536 as observed in this study. Mild seasonal variations were observed in the distribution of the genera, with the highest diversity observed in summer.

In contrast to the above drowning findings, fluid in the sphenoid sinus, hemorrhage in the petromastoid bone, and drowning fluid in the stomach or duodenum were not affected by decomposition. Previous studies on fluid in the sphenoid sinus have confirmed that it is not affected by body decomposition37 or submersion time.38 Hemorrhage in the petromastoid bone is frequently observed in drowning cases39 and is important for distinguishing drowning from other types of asphyxia.40 However, no studies have investigated the relationship between petromastoid hemorrhage and decomposition. Drowning fluid in the gastroduodenal tract, particularly the duodenum, is considered evidence of water inhalation before death. Although a small amount of water may enter the stomach postmortem, its movement is restricted by the anatomical constrictions of the esophagus. The transfer of fluids from the stomach to the duodenum is more challenging because it requires sufficient relaxation and opening of the gastric pylorus. It is unlikely that a large volume of drowning fluid would enter the duodenum solely because of the passive postmortem relaxation of the pyloric muscle. If the duodenum is fully filled with drowning fluid, this suggests that the fluid was actively propelled by the gastrocolic reflex before death. A previous study proposed that the absence of fluid in the stomach may indicate either rapid death from drowning or death before submersion.41 However, no studies have specifically examined the relationship between decomposition and the presence of drowning gastroduodenal fluid.

This study has certain limitations. It was based on a limited number of autopsies conducted at a single medical examiner’s office responsible for a specific region of Seoul. Additionally, the PMI may have been overestimated, as it was calculated based on the last known time of survival rather than the exact time of death. Nevertheless, this study provides valuable insights into diagnosing drowning in immersed bodies and understanding their epidemiological backgrounds, particularly in cases involving advanced decomposition. Further research on drowning-related deaths is warranted to enhance diagnostic accuracy and establish comprehensive guidelines and diagnostic criteria.
