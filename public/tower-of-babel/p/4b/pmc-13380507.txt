Injectable microsphere-based delivery strategies for stem cells and their derivatives in tissue regeneration
Authors: Rongji Liang, Jiayou Chen, Jingtao Huang, Penghua Fu, Zhijian Shi, Yaohang Yue, Haojian Wang, Haochi Lun, Quan Lin, Fuyang Cao, Hui Zeng, Xintao Zhang, Jianjing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tissue and organ injury, degeneration, and functional loss represent a growing global health challenge, exacerbated by an aging population and the limitations of current clinical interventions. Regenerative medicine offers a promising alternative by restoring tissue function through the delivery of stem cells and their bioactive derivatives, particularly exosomes. However, direct administration of these therapeutic agents faces significant hurdles, including low retention, rapid clearance, and poor spatiotemporal control. Injectable microsphere-based delivery systems have emerged as a versatile and minimally invasive platform to overcome these limitations. This review systematically outlines the latest advances in microsphere-mediated strategies for delivering stem cells and their derivatives across multiple tissue types, including bone, cartilage, neural, skin, dental pulp, cardiac and lung. We critically evaluate the design principles of natural and synthetic polymers, fabrication techniques such as microfluidics and electrospray, and the biological functions of microspheres in providing three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds, immune protection, and sustained release. Furthermore, we highlight emerging frontiers, including the delivery of novel regenerative cues such as apoptotic bodies and the integration of microsphere-based organoid models. This comprehensive analysis aims to accelerate the clinical translation of next-generation microsphere-enabled regenerative therapies.

Introduction

Tissue and organ injury, degeneration, and functional loss have become an increasingly prominent medical predicament in modern medicine. The irreversible global demographic shift toward aging is presenting this challenge to clinicians and medical researchers with unprecedented urgency [1]. Although current clinical interventions—such as organ transplantation, autologous tissue grafting, and implantation of artificial devices—have alleviated patient suffering to some extent, they are each constrained by fundamental limitations. Organ transplantation is frequently hindered by shortages of donor organs and the risk of immune rejection. Worldwide, the annual number of organ transplant surgeries fulfills less than 10% of the actual demand; this vast supply–demand gap causes countless patients to deteriorate or even die while awaiting transplantation [2]. Autologous tissue transplantation is frequently constrained by the anatomical uniqueness of the injured tissue and inevitably introduces additional donor-site morbidity [3,4]. Artificial implants are further constrained by incomplete functional biocompatibility. Consequently, these modalities are inadequate to meet the escalating clinical demand. Against this backdrop, regenerative medicine—designed to restore tissue function by activating endogenous repair pathways or delivering exogenous regenerative units—has emerged as the most promising frontier strategy.

Tissue regeneration is fundamentally a spatiotemporally orchestrated biological process that begins with the recruitment, migration, and proliferation of functional or progenitor cells at the injury site, proceeds through lineage-specific differentiation and extracellular-matrix (ECM) deposition, and culminates in structural remodeling that re-establishes physiological function [5,6]. Among regenerative strategies, stem cells are regarded as the central engine driving this process because of their robust self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential [7]. The therapeutic potential of stem cells is multidimensional: in addition to direct differentiation into functional cells that replace damaged tissue, they critically release a complex bioactive cargo—including growth factors, cytokines, exosomes, and even mitochondria—via paracrine pathways. These signaling molecules act as an intricate command network that re-sculpts the local immune microenvironment, curbs excessive inflammation, fosters neovascularization, and ultimately awakens the host's intrinsic regenerative capacity[[8], [9], [10]]. Stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), especially exosomes, are now at the forefront of tissue regeneration research. Laden with proteins, nucleic acids, and other bioactive molecules, these vesicles modulate target-cell function through diverse mechanisms and serve as pivotal mediators of intercellular communication; their nanoscale dimensions, low immunogenicity, and pleiotropic actions confer substantial therapeutic promise in regenerative medicine, oncology, and immune modulation [11,12]. However, the clinical application of stem cell therapy faces challenges such as low cell viability, inefficient delivery to target sites, and immune rejection.

To meet this challenge, biomaterial-based microspheres have emerged as a highly appealing multifunctional micro-carrier platform. Their distinctive physicochemical and architectural attributes enable a systematic, multi-level resolution of the bottlenecks associated with direct delivery. Structurally and physically, the high specific surface area of microspheres offers cells an extensive adhesion interface, while their internal voids and inter-particle pore networks furnish the indispensable space required for cell growth, migration, proliferation, and metabolic exchange [13]. Notably, microspheres can serve not merely as functional fillers to reinforce composite scaffolds, but also as modular building blocks that self-assemble “bottom-up” into macroscopic three-dimensional (3D) constructs whose mechanical properties rival those of cancellous bone [14]. Biologically, microspheres provide a biomimetic 3D ECM microenvironment for encapsulated cells or cellular components and create an “immune sanctuary” that shields them from host immune assault and the harsh biochemical milieu [15,16]. As “living-drug” delivery vehicles, microspheres allow spatiotemporally precise release of their payloads through rational design of material composition and architecture, thereby furnishing sustained and stable instructive cues for tissue regeneration [17,18]. Critically, the cell-laden microsphere construct is readily injectable, enabling minimally invasive placement with high fidelity into irregular defect sites[[19], [20], [21], [22]].

Building upon these advantages, the research landscape of injectable microspheres has expanded significantly in recent years, showcasing remarkable advancements particularly in precision immune regulation, the delivery of complex stem cell derivatives, and the development of organoid-microsphere composite systems. Firstly, microsphere platforms are evolving from passive delivery vehicles into active modulators; they can orchestrate the spatiotemporal release of bioactive factors to drive macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward a pro-regenerative M2 phenotype to resolve chronic inflammation [23,24]. Moreover, these systems can simulate and reverse CD8+ T cell exhaustion within complex tumor microenvironments, significantly bolstering the cytolytic potential of T cells through the construction of biomimetic physical barriers [25,26]. Secondly, the scope of therapeutic cargo has shifted from whole stem cells to refined components such as mitochondria and decellularized extracellular matrix fragments. By leveraging the protective encapsulation of microspheres, the bioactivity of these organelles and matrix fragments is preserved, which enhances the repair efficiency of damaged myocardial or skeletal tissues through metabolic support and niche mimicry[[27], [28], [29]]. Furthermore, the profound integration of microsphere technology with organoids has pioneered new dimensions in disease modeling. Microfluidic-based microsphere-organoid systems can precisely recapitulate complex pathological features, such as the tumor microenvironment, thereby addressing the lack of biophysical signaling in conventional models while providing a high-performance platform for high-throughput drug screening and personalized medicine[[30], [31], [32], [33]]. These frontier developments not only enrich the functional repertoire of microspheres in tissue engineering but also establish a robust foundation for their clinical translation into precision therapy.

Given the remarkable potential and rapid expansion of these multifunctional platforms in regenerative medicine, the following sections will first dissect the design principles of diverse natural and synthetic polymers and critically evaluate mainstream fabrication techniques. Unlike prior reviews that predominantly position microspheres as generic carriers for cells or drugs, this review systematically delineates microsphere platforms for precision delivery of stem cells and their derivatives in multiple tissue repair, while prospectively integrating emerging frontiers such as organoid composite systems assembled with microspheres (Fig. 1). Materials for preparation of microsphere used in biomedicine

Injectable microsphere-based systems have emerged as versatile platforms for stem cell delivery and tissue regeneration, owing to their minimally invasive administration, high surface-area-to-volume ratio, and capacity for spatiotemporal control over cell–matrix interactions and bioactive cue presentation. The selection of the base polymer is the primary determinant of the microsphere's physiochemical properties, including its degradation kinetics, mechanical stiffness, surface topography, and biochemical signaling capacity. In general, polymeric materials used for microsphere fabrication can be broadly classified into natural and synthetic polymers, each possessing distinct advantages and limitations in terms of biocompatibility, mechanical performance, processability, and biological functionality. Natural polymers, derived from ECM components or polysaccharides, intrinsically provide cell-recognition motifs and biomimetic microenvironments but often suffer from batch variability and limited mechanical robustness. In contrast, synthetic polymers offer highly controllable composition, reproducibility, and tunable degradation and mechanical properties, making them attractive for engineering load-bearing and long-term regenerative constructs [34]. This section provides a comprehensive overview of the most prominent polymeric materials employed in microsphere fabrication, emphasizing how their unique structural characteristics dictate the success of stem cell delivery and subsequent tissue integration.

Natural polymeric materials for microsphere

Collagen, the most abundant protein in vertebrates, constitutes the principal scaffold of the native ECM, endowing connective tissues such as bone, cartilage, tendon, and skin with tensile integrity and architectural order [35,36], while simultaneously preserving their long-term structural and biological homeostasis [37]. Owing to its exceptional biocompatibility, minimal immunogenicity, predictable biodegradability, and absence of cytotoxic by-products, collagen has become an archetypal biomedical polymer exploited for haemostasis, wound closure, defect filling, functional tissue reconstruction, controlled drug delivery, and 3D tissue engineering. The abundant ε-amino and carboxylic side-chain functionalities enable mild, site-specific cross-linking, covalent ligand grafting, or affinity-based sequestration of growth factors, whereas the native nanofibrillar architecture promotes integrin-mediated adhesion, proliferation, and lineage-directed differentiation of anchorage-dependent cells such as MSCs [38,39]. Consequently, collagen microspheres have attracted intense interest as injectable niches for ex vivo stem cell expansion and in vivo targeted delivery in regenerative medicine[[40], [41], [42]]. Liu et al. encapsulated MSCs in collagen microspheres and subjected them to chondrogenic culture. This resulted in a homogeneous cartilage-specific extracellular matrix containing abundant glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and collagen II. The resulting artificial cartilage microtissues closely recapitulated native cartilage phenotypes [43]. Nevertheless, native collagen is mechanically weak and rapidly enzymolysed in vivo, restricting its use to low-load anatomical sites. In application, the mechanical and degradation profiles of collagen can be readily tuned to match target-tissue demands by selecting appropriate chemical cross-linkers (e.g., formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, or EDC/NHS) or by hybridising with stiffer biopolymers such as polylactide or chitosan, thereby maximizing its therapeutic efficacy in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [44,45]. Emulsification is the most widely adopted technique for fabricating collagen microspheres, yields particles whose final size and morphology are sensitively modulated by collagen concentration, emulsification cycles, and the interfacial hydrophilic–lipophilic balance value [46,47].

Gelatin is a natural polypeptide produced by mild hydrolysis of collagen, retaining Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) sequences and low-immunogenic short helical fragments while losing the fibrillar architecture; it dissolves in water below 35–40°C and forms a reversible physical gel upon cooling through hydrogen-bond-driven renaturation, a sol–gel transition widely exploited for injectable delivery [38]. The gelatin biopolymer is well recognised for the intrinsic variability of its non-covalent interactions and phase behaviour that are sensitive to pH and temperature [48]. Notably, the preservation of RGD motifs endows gelatin with excellent cell-adhesive capacity comparable to that of native collagen [49,50]. Its low cost and favorable degradability further accelerate the widespread adoption of gelatin in bone, cartilage, neural and other tissue-engineering applications. Fabrication routes for gelatin microspheres mainly include desolvation, coacervation phase separation, spray drying and emulsification techniques [51]. Among them, the water-in-oil emulsification method is most frequently employed owing to its operational simplicity. The resultant gelatin microspheres can co-encapsulate cells and bioactive agents, and be delivered via minimally invasive injection or assembled ex vivo into millimetre-scale modules, enabling seamless integration of “cell–drug” co-delivery with 3-D macro-scaffolds, and have demonstrated great promise in cartilage defects, nerve conduits and vascularized bone regeneration [52].

Chitosan, composed of randomly distributed β-(1-4)-linked D-glucosamine (glucosamine) and N-acetyl-Dglucosamine (N-acetylglucosamine) structure units, is a type of natural-origin polysaccharides derived from deacetylated chitin [53]. Chitosan is a pivotal component of the bone matrix and cell surface, modulating the bioavailability and activity of various osteoclastic and osteogenic factors [54]. The novel properties concluding natural-based nontoxicity, biocompatiblity, biodegradablity and intrinsic antibacterial activity make chitosan a versatile biomaterial for regenerative medicine [55,56]. In bone tissue engineering, the preparation of apatite coatings on the surface of chitosan microspheres, which directly contact the tissue, is expected to enhance cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation [57,58]. Furthermore, the apatite coating is deposited using simulated body fluid, a process that does not require specialized equipment or excessively high processing temperatures, thus closely mimicking the natural biomimetic process. This approach offers a promising strategy for improving the biological performance of chitosan microspheres in tissue regeneration applications. Several preparation methods for chitosan microspheres have been explored, including emulsification [59,60], ionotropic gelation [61,62] and freeze-drying [63], spray-drying [64]. Among those techniques, the emulsion method is simple and has high product yields, while freeze-drying and phase separation can create porous structure in chitosan microspheres, and spray drying is desirable for powder formulation. When chitosan microspheres are used for stem cell delivery, the preparation method significantly influences their effectiveness in tissue regeneration. For instance, Xu et al. investigated the effects of different preparation methods on the bone formation potential of apatite-coated chitosan microspheres. Their study found that microspheres prepared using the emulsion cross-linking method exhibited superior cell proliferation, differentiation, and in vivo bone regeneration compared to those prepared by the coacervate precipitation method [65]. In addition, synthesized through a sol-gel transition process, nanofibrous chitosan microspheres (NCM) with an ECM-like nanofibrous structure demonstrate great potential for supporting stem cell anchorage, proliferation, and pre-differentiation, making them highly promising for tissue engineering applications [66]. Therefore, rational optimization of fabrication protocols and targeted functional modifications can further enhance the performance of chitosan microspheres and broaden their utility across bioengineering disciplines.

Alginate is a natural polysaccharide composed of β-D-mannuronate (M) and its C5-epimer α-L-guluronate (G) residues, ranking among the most abundant marine biopolymers extracted from brown algae and bacteria [67]. Alginate microspheres, offering excellent biocompatibility, non-toxicity, and the ability to form highly hydrated hydrogels under mild ionic cross-linking, have become a preferred platform for delivering cells, growth factors, proteins, genes and drugs in tissue engineering [68,69]. Although native alginate degrades slowly and lacks cell-adhesive motifs, controlled oxidation, partial oxidation-enzyme sensitisation or RGD grafting can accelerate its degradation to days-to-weeks while enhancing cell interactions, better matching the timeline of tissue regeneration [70]. In tissue-engineering applications, stem cells can be encapsulated within alginate microspheres and subsequently mixed with calcium phosphate cement (CPC) to create a moderately load-bearing scaffold for cell delivery [71]. During this process, the alginate microspheres effectively shield the cells from shear forces during mixing and injection, as well as from the setting reaction of CPC. To overcome the limitations of conventional microspheres, Zhou et al. developed fast-degrading alginate-fibrin microbeads several hundred micrometers in diameter that release encapsulated cells while preserving high cell viability. Experimental results showed that released human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (hUCMSCs) began to emerge from the microbeads after 4 days and exhibited robust proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and bone-mineral synthesis [72]. Alginate microspheres are commonly prepared by emulsification, spray drying, microfluidics or extrusion; among these, extrusion combined with ionotropic gelation is the method of choice for drug-loaded alginate microspheres owing to its simplicity and broad applicability [73].

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a naturally occurring non-sulfated glycosaminoglycan composed of repeating disaccharides of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, widely distributed in human tissues and intracellular fluids [74]. Due to its exceptional hydrophilicity, HA plays a central role in modulating the viscoelasticity of biological fluids, lubricating joints, filling connective tissues, maintaining hydration, and mediating water transport [75]. Its excellent biocompatibility, lack of cytotoxicity, and non-immunogenicity make HA an ideal material for tissue regeneration [76]. To impart controllable crosslinking ability, functional groups such as acrylates, methacrylates, or thioesters can be introduced at the hydroxyl, carboxyl, or amide positions of HA. Among these, methacrylated hyaluronic acid (MeHA) is a commonly used HA derivative in cell encapsulation and regenerative medicine [77,78]. After methacrylation in aqueous solution, MeHA can be crosslinked into a hydrogel simply by UV irradiation, and the mechanical strength of the gel can be precisely tuned by adjusting polymer concentration and degree of substitution [79,80]. Compared to MeHA, acrylated hyaluronic acid (AHA) can react instantaneously with thiolated molecules under physiological pH and without initiators, making it more suitable for in situ microencapsulation of live cells [81]. As a degradable cell delivery system, AHA microspheres exhibit higher swelling ratios, lower diffusion barriers, faster degradation rates, lower storage moduli, and larger average diameters compared to poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) microspheres, while maintaining excellent cell viability and good tissue compatibility [82].

However, natural polymer microspheres frequently exhibit poor stability, low mechanical strength, and rapid degradation, which can be effectively ameliorated through the synergistic application of modification and crosslinking strategies. Chemical modification of the inherent reactive groups—such as amino, hydroxyl, and carboxyl moieties—within natural polymer architectures enables the introduction of functional groups including vinyl, thiol, and norbornene, thereby facilitating subsequent functionalization and crosslinking reactions[[83], [84], [85]]. Based on material characteristics and the reversibility of crosslinking bonds, crosslinking strategies for natural polymers are classified into dynamic and non-dynamic approaches. Non-dynamic crosslinking encompasses covalent ester/amide bond formation, photopolymerization, and enzyme-catalyzed crosslinking, conferring durable structural integrity to microspheres. Conversely, dynamic crosslinking involves disulfide bonds, hydrogen bonding, ionic coordination, host-guest interactions, imine (Schiff base) linkages, boronate ester bonds, and Diels-Alder reactions, whose defining feature is the capacity for reversible cleavage and reformation under external stimuli such as temperature, pH, light, reducing agents, and competitive guest molecules[[86], [87], [88], [89]]. For instance, sodium alginate can form the classical "egg-box" structured gel microspheres via Ca2+ ionic coordination, or undergo permanent crosslinking through photopolymerization following methacrylate group introduction [90,91]. Chitosan can be thiolated to enable disulfide crosslinking upon oxidation, with subsequent de-crosslinking triggered by reducing agents [92]. Hyaluronic acid can be methacrylated for photocurable microsphere fabrication, or engineered with β-cyclodextrin and adamantane moieties to construct supramolecular injectable microsphere systems via host-guest recognition [93,94]. The synergistic integration of these modification and crosslinking methodologies not only substantially enhances the structural stability and mechanical properties of natural polymer microspheres, but also imparts intelligent functionalities including self-healing, stimuli-responsiveness, and controlled release, thereby expanding the application prospects of natural polymer microspheres in tissue engineering, drug delivery, and cell-based therapeutics.

Synthetic polymeric materials for microsphere

In biomedicine, cell-laden microspheres have traditionally relied on natural polymers.However, batch-to-batch variability, contamination risk, uncontrollable transport, swelling, poor long-term stability and mechanical fragility of natural polymers directly impair microcarrier immunogenicity and biocompatibility [95]. The introduction of synthetic polymers offers a superior alternative for cell micro-immobilisation. Based on degradation behaviour, synthetic polymers are divided into biodegradable and non-biodegradable classes [96]. In bone and cartilage engineering, biodegradable synthetic polymers with tunable degradation, superior mechanics (high tensile strength and elastic modulus) and adjustable structure have become the preferred materials for 3D scaffolds and microsphere carriers[[97], [98], [99]]. Representative examples include polylactic acid (PLA), poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and polycaprolactone (PCL). These synthetic polymers can be further categorized into water-soluble and water-insoluble classes based on their solubility characteristics, with each class requiring distinct microsphere fabrication methodologies. Water-soluble polymers, such as PVA and PEG, are typically processed via aqueous-phase techniques, including direct dissolution, inverse emulsification, or microfluidic-assisted crosslinking, enabling homogeneous encapsulation of bioactive cargoes under mild conditions. In contrast, water-insoluble polymers—including PLA, PLGA, and PCL—are predominantly fabricated through oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion-solvent evaporation methods, wherein the polymer is first dissolved in an organic solvent (e.g., dichloromethane or ethyl acetate), emulsified into an aqueous continuous phase, and subsequently precipitated into microspheres upon solvent removal [[100], [101], [102], [103]]. Compared with natural polymers, synthetic counterparts exhibit stable physicochemical properties, controllable quality, absence of immunogenicity, flexible processing and reliable supply [104]. Embedding or coating drug- or cell-loaded synthetic-polymer microspheres within 3D scaffolds enables spatiotemporally precise delivery of cues for cell proliferation, differentiation and vascularization through controlled microsphere degradation and sustained cargo diffusion, cooperatively creating a biomimetic microenvironment that accelerates regeneration and functional restoration of bone and cartilage [105]. However, insufficient mechanical strength of single-polymer materials still limits their widespread use in load-bearing tissue regeneration. Future studies should employ composite design, surface functionalization or structural optimization to further enhance mechanical compatibility and bioactivity.

However, merging natural and synthetic polymers into composite microsphere platforms introduces a fundamental obstacle: a pronounced mismatch in degradation kinetics that frequently undermines tissue integration. Natural polymers undoubtedly excel in bioactivity and cell adhesion, yet they are plagued by substantial batch-to-batch variability rooted in source heterogeneity and extraction protocols. Equally problematic is their susceptibility to rapid enzymatic surface erosion; uncrosslinked collagen, for example, typically resorbs within 4–6 weeks in vivo, jeopardising mechanical integrity long before tissue remodeling is complete [106]. This rapid surface degradation contrasts sharply with the slow, bulk hydrolytic erosion characteristic of synthetic counterparts such as PLGA, producing a temporal misalignment that composite designs must reconcile.

Achieving synchronized degradation is therefore central to successful composite engineering. Thoughtfully designed hybrid systems—whether core–shell microspheres, interpenetrating polymer networks, or electrospun hybrid fibres—exploit this complementarity by partitioning functional roles between phases: the natural component furnishes initial biological instruction and provisional support, while the synthetic counterpart maintains load-bearing capacity throughout the remodeling window, enabling a progressive transfer of stress to developing tissue. Such architectural strategies can be further complemented by molecular-level tuning, including modulation of molecular weight, copolymer composition (e.g., lactide-to-glycolide ratio in PLGA), or crosslink density, to align degradation trajectories with tissue-specific regeneration timelines [107,108].

Preparation of microsphere

The fabrication of microspheres is a critical determinant of their structural attributes, physicochemical properties, and biological performance in regenerative medicine. Over the past decades, microsphere manufacturing technologies have evolved from conventional bulk methods toward increasingly precise, scalable, and programmable platforms. Contemporary fabrication strategies encompass phase separation, emulsification, microfluidics, electrospraying, spray drying, and emerging additive manufacturing approaches, each offering distinct advantages in terms of particle-size control, encapsulation efficiency, compositional versatility, and translational scalability. A comprehensive understanding of these fabrication routes is therefore essential for rational microsphere design and for tailoring delivery systems to meet specific therapeutic requirements. Phase separation

Phase separation, or the solvent/non-solvent method, is a microsphere fabrication route grounded in physicochemical phenomena. Essentially an extraction process, it involves mixing drug and carrier polymer into an emulsion or suspension, followed by adding inorganic salts or non-solvents to decrease their solubility. This triggers polymer condensation and precipitation onto the drug surface, forming a protective coating. Finally, cross-linking, cooling, or solidification stabilizes the phase-separated particles into hydrogel microspheres [112]. Commonly used phase-separation variants include simple coacervation, complex coacervation, solvent–non-solvent, and temperature-shift methods. All rely on differential interactions among the material, solvent, non-solvent, or other components to initiate phase separation [120,121]. In phase separation, the preparation efficiency and structure of microspheres are influenced by multiple variables, including polymer molecular weight, solvent composition, and temperature. For instance, uniform particle size distribution, regular morphology, and smooth-surfaced composite microspheres can only be achieved under specific conditions, such as a particular CHCl3 solvent concentration, an appropriate oil-to-water ratio, and a suitable PLA-to-PC blending ratio [122]. Roudsari et al. employed phase separation to incorporate low-molecular-weight PEG as a porogen into agarose microspheres, producing tailored nanoporous architectures that markedly enhanced intra-particle molecular diffusion [123]. Huang et al. prepared chitosan microspheres with good biocompatibility and distinctive porosity via emulsion-based thermally-induced phase separation [124]. The high porosity facilitates nutrient diffusion and cell infiltration, offering a promising platform for in-vitro 3D cell culture. The method offers simple equipment and broad applicability, accommodating diverse drug encapsulation needs. Nevertheless, notable drawbacks include microsphere adhesion and aggregation, plus difficulty in precise control of preparation conditions.

Microfluidics

Microfluidics refers to the precise manipulation of sub-microlitre fluids via microchannel networks to produce highly uniform droplets. Its core mechanism relies on the synergistic interplay between shear stress and interfacial tension, enabling the controlled rupture of a continuous phase into micro- or nanoscale droplets [125]. Such droplets can subsequently be solidified or polymerised into uniform microspheres. The microfluidic toolbox includes, but is not limited to, droplet microfluidics, flow lithography, electrohydrodynamic co-jetting, and soft lithography [126,127]. Currently, the two most widely adopted microfluidic architectures are glass-capillary devices and photolithographically patterned polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) chips, both capable of producing emulsions ranging from single to double and even higher-order multiple emulsions. Droplet-based platforms stand out by enabling spatiotemporally resolved handling of multiphase streams at the microscale[[128], [129], [130]]. Under tightly regulated operating conditions, integration of droplet microfluidics with UV curing furnishes dextran hydrogel microspheres that exhibit exceptional uniformity, narrow size distribution, whose diameters can be modulated from 50 to 300 μm [131].Size uniformity ensures batch-to-batch reproducibility in release kinetics and cellular responses. Furthermore, by modulating microfluidic parameters such as flow rates and fluid viscosity, the droplet formation and solidification processes are optimized, ensuring structural integrity and uniform drug loading, thereby enhancing delivery efficiency. These attributes establish a robust foundation for critical biomedical applications, including tissue engineering [132,133] and stem cell therapeutics [134,135]. However, microfluidic technology currently faces challenges in scalable manufacturing, biocompatibility, and standardized characterization. Future advancements will require interdisciplinary integration to drive translational applications.

Emulsification

The dominant route to microspheres is emulsification, encompassing both single- and double-emulsion variants. The latter includes the water-in-oil-in-water (W1/O/W2), solid-in-oil-water (S/O/W), and water-in-oil-in-oil (W/O1/O2) emulsification methods [136]. Both rely on surfactant-stabilised emulsification of two immiscible solvents. Subsequent solid precipitation confines nucleation, growth and aggregation within spherical droplets [112,137]. The intensity of mechanical shear, combined with viscosity, interfacial tension, and surfactant concentration, dictates the droplet size distribution. [138,139]. Open-porous PCL microspheres were fabricated by coupling emulsion–solvent evaporation with particle leaching, yielding surface pores of 20–80 μm and internal porosity of 30–70%. Pore architecture was readily tuned by adjusting processing parameters [140]. Compared with non-porous controls, the microspheres enhanced cells adhesion and proliferation [141]. Porous chitosan microcarriers fabricated via a modified emulsion-cross-linking strategy promoted adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) proliferation, migration and paracrine activity versus conventional 2D culture [142]. However, microspheres prepared via emulsion methods typically exhibit poor size uniformity [143]. This broad size distribution generates a heterogeneous population, potentially leading to unpredictable in vivo distribution and variable therapeutic windows. Furthermore, challenges such as stability issues and residual organic solvents necessitate further optimization of processing protocols and material selection to enhance their performance and broaden their applicability.

Electrospray

Electrospray (ES) is a technique that exploits a high-voltage electrostatic field to disrupt a polymer solution or melt into micrometre-scale droplets. A standard ES set-up comprises four principal modules: a pumping system, a high-voltage supply, a metallic nozzle connected to the latter, and a grounded collector equipped with a substrate for material deposition. The liquid is driven through a capillary nozzle by a syringe pump, and under the action of the electric field its surface is shaped into a Taylor cone by electrostatic forces. Subsequently, the charged droplets are propelled toward the collector, solvent evaporates or reacts, and solid microspheres are ultimately formed[114,144,145]. Key determinants of the electrospray atomization process include nozzle geometry (structure and channel dimension), operational parameters (applied voltage, flow rate, working distance), and solution attributes (viscosity, conductivity, surface tension, polymer molecular weight)[[146], [147], [148]]. Yao et al. devised a contact-free microsphere generator that produced monodisperse alginate and alginate/gelatin microspheres exhibiting smooth spherical morphology and an interconnected “egg-box” porous architecture. These mechanically robust microspheres sustained long-term proliferation and adipogenic differentiation of hADSCs, and parameter optimization raised intra-microsphere cell viability to 95% [149]. Kim et al. [150] observed that the diameter of poly(methyl methacrylate) particles decreased monotonically with increasing voltage. Rietveld et al. [151] fabricated poly(vinylidene fluoride) particles and demonstrated that droplet size is governed by solution conductivity and flow rate. ES is conventionally conducted in air or vacuum; however, an immersed-electrospray variant that couples in-situ photopolymerization with a submerged jet enables the cytocompatible fabrication of synthetic hydrogel microspheres for cell encapsulation. Using a PVA macromer solution laden with fibroblasts, this approach consistently yields microspheres exhibiting high post-encapsulation viability [152].

Other methods

3D printing is emerging as a leading approach for producing hydrogel microspheres because computer-aided design (CAD) enables micron-scale precision and patient-specific geometry. The same platforms can co-print multiple bio-inks, yielding microspheres with graded or hybrid composition. 3D bioprinting simultaneously integrates cells, bioactive molecules, and biomaterials into open, porous scaffolds that mimic native tissue architecture and function, thereby accelerating translational applications [116,153]. Using digital light processing, He et al. produced monodisperse gelatin Methacryloyl (GelMA) microspheres whose shape was dictated by a user-defined CAD mask. This “all-in-one” microsphere platform supported long-term hADSC culture and subsequent tissue-engineering applications [154]. Shpigel et al. introduced SPHRINT, an ink-jet-like deposition of molten polymer onto a superoleophobic substrate that spontaneously solidifies into monodisperse microspheres. This solvent-free approach enabled one-step fabrication of ibuprofen-loaded PCL and PLGA microspheres. The resulting microspheres exhibited >95% sphericity, narrow size distribution, 85% drug encapsulation efficiency, 90% process yield, and sustained release over 7 days [155].

Spray drying is another well-established method for microsphere preparation, involving the atomization of a solution or suspension into fine droplets that are rapidly dried upon contact with a hot drying medium (usually hot air) to form microspheres [156]. Its merits are operational simplicity, rapid throughput, and direct scalability. Thermal degradation of bioactives such as proteins can be minimized by reducing inlet temperature and adding excipient protectants [157,158].

Synthesis: navigating the process–structure–efficiency landscape

Collectively, the choice of fabrication technology is the primary determinant of the microsphere's structural identity, which in turn dictates its functional performance. Microfluidics offers unmatched precision in size and architecture, yielding superior delivery predictability but at the cost of scalability. Emulsification provides robust scalability but sacrifices monodispersity and structural homogeneity, introducing variability in clinical outcomes. Electrospray excels in miniaturization and surface functionalization but may compromise long-term retention. Phase separation and spray drying offer simplicity and throughput but lack the architectural control necessary for advanced cell-therapeutic applications. 3D bioprinting bridges design precision and biological complexity but remains limited by manufacturing speed. Therefore, selecting a fabrication strategy requires balancing the desired structural attributes—size, porosity, mechanical strength, and surface chemistry—against the delivery objectives of the target tissue, thereby optimizing the process–structure–efficiency triad.

Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for tissue regeneration

Conventional 2D cell culture models lack the structural complexity and spatial organization characteristic of native tissues, which are composed of multiple cell types interacting with a dynamic ECM. 3D spheroids have emerged as advanced surrogates of the in vivo microenvironment, enhancing cell–cell interactions and adhesion, thereby yielding more physiologically relevant tissue architectures, functions, and intercellular crosstalk [159,160]. Microsphere-based delivery systems have rapidly evolved into a core technology in regenerative medicine, offering injectable, modular, and bio-responsive platforms that integrate materials science with cellular therapeutics.

Nevertheless, the formation of cellular microgel aggregates is governed by multiple determinants, encompassing the physical properties of microspheres (e.g., size, curvature) and biochemical characteristics (e.g., integrin-binding ligands)[[161], [162], [163], [164]]. For instance, large spheroids ranging from several hundred micrometers to millimeters are subject to restricted oxygen and nutrient supply, leading to central hypoxia that adversely affects cell viability and functionality. Conversely, smaller microsphere dimensions correlate with higher numerical densities of stable self-assembled cellular biomicrospheres [165]. However, a size threshold effect exists: submicron particles may undergo intracellular internalization. Notably, microsphere regions exhibiting higher curvature, particularly those with interconnected porosity, support oxygen and nutrient diffusion, thereby enhancing metabolic activity and cellular viability, and even potentiating directed cellular differentiation [166,167]. Furthermore, microgels functionalized with cell-adhesive surface moieties, such as RGD peptide motifs, promote cellular adhesion via the integrin–FAK–ROCK–myosin signaling axis; disruption of this pathway impairs cell–microgel assembly. By contrast, materials devoid of RGD peptide motifs, exemplified by PLGA or poly(oligo(ethylene glycol) methacrylate) (POEGMA), fail to sustain the assembly of cellular microgel aggregates [168,169]. Additionally, the surface topography of polymeric microspheres plays a critical role in modulating cellular behavior within microspheres; more directional topographical cues provide superior guidance for cellular surface extension, thereby enhancing cell adhesion to the microsphere surface [170,171]. At the structural level, core–shell and hollow architectures may partially reconcile immunoisolation, injection shear protection, and mass transfer regulation, yet they concurrently introduce risks of shell failure, mass transfer limitation, and localized hypoxia [172].

Beyond these architectural and biochemical cues, the mechanical microenvironment itself—particularly matrix stiffness and elasticity—functions as an instructive determinant that broadly modulates cytoskeletal architecture and the attendant mechanobiological signalling to govern cell fate. Microfluidic fabrication, for instance, enables the preparation of Fn-GelMA microspheres with independently tunable elastic moduli and ECM ligand densities. Within this system, a soft matrix (∼2 kPa) combined with low ligand density (2 μg mL−1) suffices to direct nucleus pulposus (NP)-like differentiation of intervertebral disc progenitor/stem cells via cytoplasmic retention of Yes-associated protein (YAP), entirely without exogenous chondrogenic factors [173]. Likewise, microspheres with an elastic modulus of 2.2–6.6 MPa markedly promote hematopoietic stem cell expansion through the Piezo1–Ca2+–NFAT–AP-1–IL-6 signalling cascade; by contrast, both softer PVA microgels (23.1 kPa) and rigid silica nanoparticles (232 MPa) prove ineffective [174].

Methods for constructing cell-microsphere systems include surface adsorption (microcarriers) and internal encapsulation (microcapsules). Generally, microcarriers are prefabricated, thereby minimizing any direct adverse effects on cells during the preparation process and consequently preserving high cell viability; however, this approach results in relatively low cell density. Common fabrication techniques encompass emulsification and spray drying [175]. In contrast, microcapsules are directly prepared from suspensions formed by mixing crosslinkable polymers with cells into microspheres, with cells encapsulated within the interior, enabling substantially higher cell loading densities.Moreover, microencapsulation leverages its selectively permeable membrane to isolate grafts from the host immune system, effectively circumventing immune rejection during allogeneic transplantation [176]. Following microencapsulation, BMSCs can sustainably secrete growth factors requisite for tissue regeneration. Co-microencapsulation may additionally harness cytokines secreted by ancillary cells to promote stem cell differentiation and augment intercellular signaling [177]. The high-density growth of cells within microcapsules is conducive to maintaining their normal secretory function and elevating intracapsular growth factor concentrations, thereby increasing the concentration gradient across the capsule membrane and enhancing the driving force for growth factor efflux [178]. However, the gelation process inherent to microencapsulation may inflict damage upon the encapsulated cells. Therefore, microcapsule fabrication methods necessitate mild conditions, such as electrostatic droplet generation, microfluidic technology, and 3D bioprinting, to minimize cellular damage during the preparation process [179,180].

Despite their promising regenerative performance, the clinical translation of microsphere-based delivery systems still faces several practical barriers. These include the need for scalable and reproducible manufacturing, long-term storage stability of cell- or exosome-loaded microspheres, standardized quality control of payload loading and release behavior, and rigorous biosafety evaluation. Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for bone regeneration

Skeletal tissue responds to trauma through a sequential and complex cascade encompassing inflammation, repair activation, and tissue remodeling [231]. Delayed or failed union enlarges the initial defect beyond the reparative capacity of conventional interventions. Bone regeneration proceeds principally via two distinct pathways: intramembranous and endochondral ossification. Intramembranous ossification denotes the direct differentiation of MSCs into osteoblasts that deposit a mineralized ECM. This mode predominates in rigidly fixed fractures with minimal gap distance and in metaphyseal fracture sites. Diaphyseal fractures characterized by poor mechanical stability and wide gaps heal through the canonical stages of endochondral ossification [232,233]. In this regenerative paradigm, the fracture hematoma is initially infiltrated by neutrophils and macrophages, which within 24h secrete IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α to recruit stem cells and initiate repair [234]. Subsequently, MSCs first differentiate into chondrocytes that lay down a cartilaginous matrix, which then calcifies and is ultimately substituted by bone. Chondrocytes exhibit robust metabolic adaptability, enabling survival in hypoxic regions while continuing to produce ECM required for mineral deposition, thereby contributing to optimal fracture healing. Additionally, stem cell-derived exosomes foster a phenotypic shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 macrophages via paracrine signaling, thereby amplifying anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative cytokine release that enhances osteogenesis [235]. Microspheres serve as injectable 3D protective niches that shield encapsulated cells and exosomes from immune clearance. These delivery systems enable spatiotemporally controlled osteogenic signaling, thereby empowering stem cells and their exosomes to orchestrate osteoimmunomodulation, angiogenic invasion, and lineage-specific osteogenesis within bone defects. The bottom-up assembly of complex tissue mimics using single-cell-sized building blocks facilitates nutrient exchange within the hydrogel layer [185]. Meanwhile, the incorporation of highly dispersed fullerene nanocrystals with enhanced bioreactivity into hydrogel microspheres enables in situ regulation of stem cell redox homeostasis, effectively promoting osteogenic differentiation through FoxO1 signaling activation [191]. Moreover, photoinduced ionic crosslinking enables rapid fabrication of cell-laden microgels with uniform networks and enhanced mechanical strength, and resulting granular hydrogels exhibit shear-thinning and self-healing properties [194]. Collectively, these microscale architectural features enhance microgel sensitivity to surrounding environmental stimuli, thereby establishing these cell-laden microgels as active regenerative micro-modules that integrate immune shielding, paracrine enrichment, and mechanical regulation within irregular bone defects. To this end, researchers developed rapidly degradable GelMA microspheres and slowly degradable ChitoMA microspheres. GelMA microspheres were loaded with exogenous BMSCs and stem cell homing peptides (SKP) to recruit endogenous BMSCs, whereas ChitoMA microspheres were loaded with angiogenic peptides (KLT) and osteogenic peptides (OGP) to support vascular maturation and osteogenesis. This sequential release design—early release of BMSCs and SKP followed by sustained release of OGP and KLT—aligns with the dynamic chronology of bone regeneration, thereby promoting angiogenesis and ameliorating inflammation in critical bone defects. Compared with conventional sustained-release systems, this hydrogel microsphere platform enables direct peptide-cell interaction at the defect site, circumventing issues of uneven distribution, rapid diffusion, and premature depletion of bioactive components, thereby underscoring the critical importance of well-optimized bone marrow-mimicking niches for skeletal regeneration [236].

Bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), a cytokine of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily, is widely recognised for its outstanding osteoregenerative potential. During the early phase of fracture healing, BMP recruits MSCs and directs their differentiation into chondrocytes and osteoblasts. The resulting cartilaginous callus stabilizes the fracture site through endochondral ossification, thereby accelerating union. Recombinant human BMP-2 is clinically approved for treating osteonecrosis of the femoral head, lumbar spinal fusion, and acute tibial fractures. However, its therapeutic utility is constrained by a short circulating half-life and the difficulty of achieving precise, localized dosing [189]. Microsphere-based delivery of genetically engineered stem cells offers a novel strategy to overcome these limitations. MSCs serve as an effective BMP-2 delivery vehicle, enhancing both its bioactivity and local retention. Zheng et al. encapsulated doxycycline-inducible, BMP-2-secreting rat bone-marrow-derived MSCs within phenol-functionalized carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-ph) microspheres. These uniformly sized microspheres (100–150 μm) preserved rBMSC viability in vitro and markedly enhanced the expression of osteogenesis-related transcripts and proteins, indicating strong osteoinductive capacity [189] (Fig. 3A–E). In addition, Mumaw et al. further developed a PEG hydrogel depot to encapsulate genetically modified MSCs and prolong in vivo BMP-2 expression. Their study demonstrated that cryopreserved microencapsulated MSCs retained high viability, sustained BMP-2 secretion, and effectively promoted bone formation [196]. The transcription factor FOXC2, a central regulator of osteogenesis, angiogenesis and skeletal metabolism, exerts potent osteoregenerative effects [237]. Huang et al. fabricated alginate–poly-l-lysine–alginate (APA) microcapsules via high-voltage electrospraying to encapsulate FOXC2-overexpressing BMSCs. This delivery platform reversed early-stage steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head in rabbits [181]. Injectable CPC has become one of the preferred carriers in bone tissue engineering due to its excellent bioactivity, biocompatibility, self-setting capability, and osteoconductivity [238,239]. However, this static cell seeding approach is limited by low seeding efficiency and poor cell infiltration into the scaffold interior, resulting in heterogeneous cell distribution and consequently compromising in vivo regenerative outcomes. The incorporation of microsphere technology enables the formation of a “microsphere-CPC” composite system, wherein the programmed degradation of the microspheres progressively enhances scaffold porosity, thereby creating interstitial space that facilitates the migration and proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells and endogenous osteoblasts within the construct, thereby overcoming the challenges of repairing complex bone defects. Rapidly degrading alginate microbeads pre-encapsulating hUCMSCs were co-injected with CPC paste into calvarial defects of nude mice; within 14 days live-cell density inside the beads increased >10-fold, osteogenic markers were significantly up-regulated, and mineral deposition was enhanced [71,186]. In a rabbit femoral critical-size defect, alginate-microencapsulated rBMMSCs combined with β-TCP/CPC produced significantly greater amounts of newly formed bone and marrow tissue than controls, confirming that microsphere loading markedly enhances the osteogenic efficacy of CPC [182] (Fig. 3F–H). To address the clinical challenge of osteomyelitis with osteonecrosis, Wu et al. developed a CPC-chitosan- penicillin-sodium-alginate microsphere scaffold co-encapsulating hUCMSCs, achieving triple synergy of “antibacterial-anti-inflammatory-osteogenic” effects [183]. To further optimize CPC scaffolds, investigators incorporated the bio-functional peptide RGD into the cement and then deposited oxidized-alginate-fibrin microbeads on its surface, creating a bifunctional substrate for rapid cell release and targeted adhesion [184,187]. Additionally, human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived MSCs were loaded into alginate-microsphere/macroporous CPC constructs [240], maintaining high viability while markedly enhancing osteogenic differentiation and mineral synthesis, thereby extending the microsphere-CPC platform to embryonic stem cell sources. Currently, microsphere-based bone cements have garnered considerable attention owing to their biomimetic architecture and capacity for minimally invasive filling of geometrically irregular defects. Alg/α-TCP composite microspheres, fabricated with the bone-biomimetic α-tricalcium phosphate as the inorganic phase, exhibit an organic–inorganic interpenetrating network analogous to native bone matrix. The hierarchical pore architecture arising from the spontaneous stacking of these microspheres establishes favorable microchannels that facilitate cell migration, nutrient transport, and neoosteogenesis, thereby representing a promising therapeutic strategy for the regeneration of large-segment bone defects [193,241].

Notably, in vitro pre-osteogenic differentiation constitutes a critical step for enhancing bone formation and repairing osseous defects during allogeneic MSC transplantation. Zhou et al. [242] demonstrated that donor MSCs subjected to osteogenic induction recruit significantly more host MSCs to the defect site than their undifferentiated counterparts.They postulated that pre-differentiated MSCs secrete a panel of cytokines—including SDF-1, FGFs, MMPs and TLR ligands—that collectively enhance the migratory and homing activities of host MSCs toward injured tissue. Angiogenesis, the process by which new vessels sprout from pre-existing vasculature, precedes osteogenesis and is essential for successful bone regeneration [244]. Early vascularization plays a pivotal role in nutrient and growth factor transport as well as in supporting long-distance gas exchange, especially in large bone defects. Neo-vessels supply the highly metabolic regenerative callus with oxygen and nutrients while serving as conduits for inflammatory cells and osteochondral progenitors to reach the injury site [245]. Angiogenesis is regulated by multiple growth factors, notably vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secreted by inflammatory and stromal cells to induce vascular ingrowth; VEGF also indirectly promotes osteogenesis by modulating osteogenic growth factor release and paracrine signaling [246,247]. Exosomes, which carry miRNAs, osteogenic proteins, and angiogenic factors, exhibit low immunogenicity and targeting capability, enabling intercellular transfer of pro-vascular and pro-osteogenic signals and serving as key mediators linking angiogenesis to osteogenesis [248,249]. Additionally, BMSC-Exos recruit endogenous stem cells for bone repair, while neovessels serve as the principal route for their migration to the defect; angiogenesis enhances osteoblast migration and proliferation, and these cells differentiate directionally along vessels to participate in skeletal regeneration [250,251]. Delivering exosomes precisely to the angiogenic phase following bone injury remains a formidable challenge. To deliver exosomes precisely during the angiogenic window, microsphere carriers exploit injectability, porous architecture, and surface functionalization to achieve programmed release. Han et al. developed an injectable, sequential-release PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogel containing chitosan-nanofiber microspheres co-loaded with VEGF and dental pulp stem cell-derived exosomes (DPSC-Exo) [195] (Fig. 5A–B). Rapid VEGF release initiates angiogenesis, whereas sustained DPSC-Exo release maintains osteogenesis. In vitro, the hydrogel promoted HUVEC angiogenesis and pre-osteoblast differentiation; in vivo, it significantly enhanced calvarial bone formation. Studies have revealed that MMP1 is highly expressed in vascularized bone tissue[[252], [253], [254]]. Using a microfluidic chip, Yang et al. fabricated an injectable, enzyme-sensitive hydrogel microsphere(KGE microsphere) that self-assembles from KLDL-MMP1 peptide, GelMA and BMSC-Exos and responds to neovascularization. KGE microspheres exhibit a narrow size distribution (50–70 μm) and are compatible with minimally invasive injection. By stably encapsulating exosomes, they enable spatiotemporally controlled release upon MMP1 activation that coincides with neovessel formation, thereby maximizing stem cell recruitment. In vivo, the system promoted angiogenesis-dependent bone repair by recruiting CD90+ stem cells via nascent vessels [190] (Fig. 5C–F). In addition, surface-functionalized porous PLGA microspheres with a polydopamine shell load hypoxia-preconditioned exosomes, sustain their release for 21 days, and promote vascularized bone regeneration in calvarial defects [17]. These innovative controlled-release systems amplify neovascularization and osteoblast function, providing a translational roadmap for bone regenerative therapy. Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for cartilage regeneration

Articular cartilage is composed of highly elastic hyaline cartilage that consists primarily of water, proteoglycans, and collagen fibres within the ECM. Cellular constituents account for only 5% of the wet weight and <10% of the total tissue volume of articular cartilage. Articular cartilage is avascular, resulting in minimal infiltration of oxygen, nutrients, and progenitor cells. Hence, spontaneous repair of articular cartilage is highly restricted[[255], [256], [257], [258]]. Various interventions, such as autologous osteochondral grafts and chondrocyte suspension transplantation, have been employed to restore or reinforce articular cartilage [259,260]. Nevertheless, the repaired tissues possess suboptimal biological and mechanical characteristics relative to native cartilage [261]. Present treatments for osteochondral lesions comprise microfracture, autologous chondrocyte implantation, and allograft transplantation. Although microfracture recruits marrow progenitors, it typically produces fibrocartilage containing types I and II collagen that exhibits inferior mechanical properties and elevated friction, leading to compromised joint function and accelerated deterioration[[262], [263], [264], [265]]. Additionally, donor-site morbidity remains a concern with grafting procedures. Consequently, cartilage regeneration has emerged as an attractive alternative. Effective cartilage restoration depends on enhancing chondrocyte proliferation and ECM production [266]. MSCs can differentiate into chondrocytes and secrete type II collagen and proteoglycans, positioning them as a key candidate for cartilage regeneration. However, the avascular nature of cartilage results in insufficient nutrient and oxygen supply, leading to apoptosis or functional decline of directly transplanted MSCs [267,268].

Avascular, hypoxic and load-bearing cartilage imposes concurrent demands for elastic support, controlled degradation and bio-induction on stem cell carriers. Microspheres leverage tunable pore size and surface chemistry to create a biomimetic platform that sequentially provides mechanical support, biochemical cues and immunomodulation, thereby aligning closely with cartilage regeneration requirements [269]. The agarose-collagen composite microspheres, due to their similar matrix composition to that of articular cartilage, significantly enhance the chondrogenic differentiation efficiency of hMSCs [197]. Qu et al. fabricated open-pore PLGA microspheres (OPMs) via alkaline treatment to serve as BMSCs delivery vehicles for cartilage regeneration. OPMs display accelerated biodegradation and enlarged pores; their highly porous architecture boosts BMSCs proliferation and up-regulates cartilage-specific mRNA and proteins [205]. Liu et al. embedded MSCs in 3D collagen hydrogel microspheres whose native triple-helix structure offers cartilage-matched stiffness and drives sustained secretion of glycosaminoglycans and type II collagen under a hyaline, low-hypertrophy phenotype, achieving concurrent matrix and cell biomimesis. Mechanical stress intensity governs distinct cells motility patterns. Moderate stress promotes directional cell migration and uniform distribution [270]. This enhances migration efficiency and facilitates subsequent tissue repair. The heightened sensitivity of chondrocytes to mechanical stress renders cartilage vulnerable to mechanical damage in OA. Researchers developed viscoelastic hydrogel microspheres with tunable stress relaxation properties that effectively alleviated mechanical stress on articular cartilage during movement, reduced endoplasmic reticulum stress in chondrocytes, and decreased cells apoptosis [202]. Beyond mechanical and biomimetic properties, microspheres potentiate the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory capacities of MSCs. The porous architecture and mechanical match of hydrogel microspheres compensate for deficient adhesion in OA, block MSCs anoikis, and sustainably boost their paracrine activity [201]. MSCs suppress pro-inflammatory immune-cell activation by releasing anti-inflammatory mediators such as TGF-β1, IL-10 and PGE2, and they further optimize the regenerative niche by driving macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [271]. Alginate microencapsulation tunes hMSCs paracrine signaling, eliciting an Osteoarthritis microenvironment targeted secretory response characterized by up-regulated pro-inflammatory (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8), anti-inflammatory (IL-1RA) and chemotactic (G-CSF, MDC, IP-10) cytokines, thereby enhancing hMSC therapeutic efficacy against established osteoarthritis [198] (Fig. 6A–B). Stem cell-derived exosomes recruit resident progenitors, induce chondrogenesis and dampen inflammation via paracrine signals, as confirmed in phase-III trials [272,273]. For example, cartilage-derived stem/progenitor cell (CSPC)-Exos simultaneously enhance chondrocyte proliferation and migration while down-regulating inflammation, further elevating regenerative efficiency [274]. Given that cartilage is avascular, injury triggers a steep drop in oxygen and glucose, exhausting ATP and forcing cells into a “low-power” quiescent state. Bio-energetic Suc-Exos reactivate BMSCs chondrogenesis and restore hyaline cartilage in vivo by reinforcing the tricarboxylic acid cycle and mitochondrial homeostasis [275]. Yet their short half-life and rapid clearance by the mononuclear-phagocyte system yield low intra-articular retention, hampering clinical translation. As a delivery vehicle, hydrogel microspheres lubricate articulation while shielding exosomes from OA-associated enzymes and inflammatory milieus, prolonging exosome stability and bioactivity [276]. Their controllable degradation further enables sustained, gradient release of exosomes, furnishing damaged cartilage with a prolonged regenerative cue. Using microfluidics, Yin et al. produced uniform, injectable hyaluronan hydrogel microparticles (HMPs) encapsulating miR-99b-3p-rich exosomes; ADAMTS-4 down-regulation yielded smoother cartilage, intact subchondral bone and higher proteoglycan content versus controls [204] (Fig. 6C–D). Recent engineering advances have endowed exosomes with high cartilage-targeting specificity, unlocking substantial regenerative potential. Surface modification and cargo loading (drugs or genes) markedly heighten exosome delivery efficiency within cartilage tissue. Chen et al. fused a chondrocyte-affine peptide (CAP) with CRISPR/Cas9-FGF18 cargo into hybrid exosomes, entrapped them in photocrosslinked hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA) microspheres, and generated an injectable microgel (CAP/FGF18-hyEXO@HMs) that genome-wide activates FGF18 while concurrently curbing inflammation and ECM degradation in vitro and in vivo [203] (Fig. 6E–F). Continued refinement of stem cell/exosome engineering, microsphere architecture and spatiotemporally controlled release is poised to yield off-the-shelf injectable therapeutics that recapitulate the biomechanical and biochemical fidelity of native hyaline cartilage and critically advance cartilage regeneration. Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for neural regeneration

Neural injuries—encompassing both central nervous system (CNS) trauma and peripheral nerve damage—represent among the most clinically intractable conditions globally, consistently yielding unfavorable prognoses owing to the intrinsic regenerative incapacity of neural tissue [277]. Spinal cord injury (SCI), for instance, disrupts neuronal circuitry and conductive pathways, resulting in permanent functional deficits [278,279]. Although autologous nerve grafting remains the gold standard for peripheral nerve repair, its clinical utility is constrained by surgical complexity, limited donor tissue availability, size mismatch, and the risk of secondary complications at the donor site. Consequently, robust regeneration of central or peripheral neural tissue remains elusive. Emerging evidence indicates that neuroprotective agents, growth factors, and stem cells can safely promote repair. MSCs are particularly attractive in this context, as they can differentiate into neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and Schwann cells upon sensing local physicochemical cues, while concurrently secreting trophic and anti-inflammatory factors that protect damaged axons and facilitate circuit reconstruction [185,191,194]. Nevertheless, several formidable barriers impede therapeutic efficacy: the intact blood-brain barrier (BBB) or blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB) during the subacute and chronic phases of stroke prevents systemically administered therapeutics from achieving therapeutic concentrations within the neural parenchyma [280,281]; moreover, the absence of extracellular matrix and the hostile inflammatory milieu following neural injury compromise the survival and differentiation of transplanted stem cells [282].

Injectable microspheres offer an ideal delivery platform to circumvent these challenges. Their tunable degradability, high biocompatibility, and porous architecture protect encapsulated therapeutics from enzymatic degradation, maintain therapeutically relevant concentrations at the injury site for weeks, and enhance local bioavailability. Within microsphere-assembled scaffolds, aligned nanofibers serve as topographical cues that effectively direct axonal extension and pathfinding, while precisely tuned biodegradation kinetics ensure scaffold resorption at a rate synchronized with de novo tissue formation. Mechanical robustness is equally critical, particularly in large-gap neural defects where structural integrity must be maintained to bridge lesion cavities and withstand physiological loads during the regenerative process [283].

Microspheres function as multifunctional hubs for the co-delivery of cells and bioactive factors during neural regeneration. Hydrogel microspheres releasing platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), fabricated via piezoelectric spraying, significantly enhanced neural stem cell survival, neuronal differentiation, and motor function recovery following SCI by stimulating axonal regeneration, synaptogenesis, and angiogenesis [208]. Heparin-functionalized chitosan microspheres (64 μm) immobilized FGF-2, providing a matrix that supported neural precursor cell proliferation and pluripotency maintenance without requiring soluble growth factors [207]. PEG-based microspheres co-encapsulating endothelial cells and neural stem cells preserved stem cell quiescence during transplantation while promoting neural stem cell migration toward vasculature, thereby fostering neurovascular integration [284]. Similarly, alginate microbeads containing hMSCs maintained paracrine output and, even in the absence of direct cell contact, polarized macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype when stimulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines [206].

Nerve guidance conduits (NGCs)—biomimetic alternatives to autografts—can be combined with microsphere-based scaffolds to achieve localized and sustained neurotrophic factor delivery. For instance, nerve growth factor (NGF)-loaded polyphosphoester microspheres embedded within silicone or polyphosphoester (PPE) conduits provided site-specific NGF gradients that promoted peripheral nerve regeneration [285]. Although ADSCs exhibit resistance to hypoxia and oxidative stress, conventional two-dimensional seeding within NGCs often fails due to cell suspension leakage and limited survival space. Inflammatory and immune-regulating microspheres as bridging systems for neurovascular repair. The early local inflammatory response following SCI induces BMSC dysfunction and suppresses endogenous repair mechanisms, culminating in neuronal necrosis and glial scar formation [286]. An ideal therapeutic strategy must simultaneously quench the acute-phase “cytokine storm” and establish a permissive microenvironment for subsequent neural regeneration. To this end, liposome-anchored hydrogel microspheres loaded with ginsenoside Rg1 and chemokine SDF-1α (R-L@S-GMs) were engineered to modulate the early inflammatory milieu through spatiotemporally controlled drug release while recruiting endogenous stem cells, thereby significantly promoting post-SCI neural regeneration and functional recovery [287]. Excessive vascular inflammation constitutes a principal barrier to neural repair. Ischemic stroke triggers an inflammatory cascade in which microglia—the brain's resident immune cells—rapidly activate and polarize toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, exacerbating vascular microenvironment deterioration, disrupting the blood-brain barrier, and establishing a vicious "neuroinflammation-ischemia" cycle that severely impedes vascular regeneration and functional restoration [288]. Leveraging microfluidic technology, the DJ-1 protein-derived ND13 peptide—possessing intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties—was covalently grafted onto HAMA hydrogel microspheres (mND13@HAMA). These 120 μm spherical porous architectures enabled MMP-2-responsive sustained release of ND13 peptide, achieving long-term immune training of microglia and suppressing their pro-inflammatory polarization. To further augment vascular regeneration, VEGF liposomes were co-immobilized on the microsphere surface, constructing the mND13-VEGF@HAMA composite system that promotes neural repair through synergistic immuno-vascular regulation [289]. The pathological process of SCI is further characterized by aberrant fluctuations in local metal ion concentrations. Core-shell microspheres offer an elegant solution for stage-specific ion regulation: a PLGA-Zn2+ core encapsulated within a GelMA-PBA-Mg2+ shell enables sequential release—early Mg2+ release attenuates inflammation and inhibits scar formation, while subsequent Zn2+ release promotes neural cell proliferation and regeneration [290].

Compared to stem cells themselves, stem cell-derived exosomes represent an off-the-shelf regenerative biologic that is readily isolated, amenable to long-term storage via lyophilization, and free from major ethical constraints. Their approximately 100 nm dimensions enable evasion of pulmonary and hepatic capture while permitting penetration across the blood-spinal cord barrier to reach damaged neural tissue, where they promote macrophage transition from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the reparative M2 phenotype, suppress neurotoxic A1 astrocyte activation, and create an anti-inflammatory microenvironment conducive to axonal regeneration [291]. However, their short half-life and rapid renal clearance necessitate a protective delivery vehicle. Injectable hydrogel microspheres address this limitation by shielding exosomes from enzymatic degradation and facilitating barrier penetration, establishing microsphere-mediated exosome therapy as a promising strategy for CNS repair.

A particularly promising direction lies in the integration of conductive components within microsphere architectures to recapitulate the bioelectrical signaling essential for neural regeneration. Neural tissue function relies fundamentally on electrochemical signal propagation; injury-induced disruption of endogenous electrical cues impairs axonal guidance, synaptic plasticity, and functional circuit reconstitution [292]. Conductive hydrogel microspheres—incorporating materials such as polypyrrole, polyaniline, carbon nanotubes, or graphene oxide—offer distinct advantages in this context: they provide physiologically relevant electrical conductivity within safe ranges to promote neurite outgrowth, enhance Schwann cell myelination, and prevent muscle denervation [[293], [294], [295], [296]], while maintaining the injectability, porosity, and biodegradability inherent to microsphere platforms. Notably, Shen et al. utilized supramolecular engineering and microfluidic strategies to construct an internal friction network hydrogel microsphere system through synergistic assembly of piezoelectric bismuth ferrite nanoparticles and slide-ring functionalized HAMA. These microspheres generate stable electric fields (95–110 mV/mm) under dynamic loading, achieving physiological electrical adaptation for degenerated tissue [297]. Unlike bulk conductive scaffolds, microsphere-based conductive systems enable minimally invasive delivery to deep or irregularly shaped lesion sites, with modular tunability of conductive domains at the single-microsphere level. Furthermore, the combination of conductive properties with stimuli-responsive release kinetics establishes a feedback loop between tissue electrophysiology and therapeutic delivery. This convergence of electroactive materials with microsphere technology positions conductive hydrogel microspheres as next-generation platforms capable of simultaneously addressing the structural, biochemical, and bioelectrical dimensions of neural repair, though their long-term biocompatibility, optimal conductivity thresholds for specific neural subtypes, and seamless integration with external stimulation devices require systematic investigation.

Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for skin regeneration

Normal cutaneous wound healing is an exquisitely orchestrated cascade of cell migration, proliferation, angiogenesis, ECM deposition and remodeling [298,299]; disruption of any phase precipitates chronic wounds or pathological scarring that impair quality of life and generate enormous healthcare costs. Among available scaffolds, microsphere-based delivery systems stand out for their injectability, tunable degradation and 3D microarchitecture that enables high-density stem-cell or exosome loading while conforming to irregular wound beds. The microsphere-based delivery system serves as a central carrier platform in regenerative medicine, wherein the precise engineering of its physicochemical properties directly governs the efficacy of stem cell behavior modulation and immune microenvironment remodeling, thereby dictating the orderly progression of cutaneous wound healing across the inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases. At the structural engineering level, the high-porosity (80%–95%) anisotropic architecture not only furnishes three-dimensional scaffolding for high-density encapsulation of stem cells or exosomes but also orchestrates nutrient diffusion kinetics and cellular infiltration trajectories through refined pore size distribution and interconnectivity optimization [23]. At the stem cell functional modulation dimension, the microsphere system achieves multilevel intervention over stem cell proliferation, differentiation, and paracrine functionality through spatiotemporally controlled factor release kinetics and biomimetic microenvironment construction. Immunomodulation represents another central mechanism by which microsphere systems promote chronic wound healing, with its critical foundation residing in the reprogramming of macrophage polarization states and spatiotemporal orchestration of the inflammatory microenvironment. Within the sEVs@MS delivery system, GelMA microspheres extended the in vivo retention of MSC-sEVs to 7 days, achieving a cumulative release rate of 89 ± 3%. These sEVs suppressed the p38 MAPK signaling pathway—manifested specifically through reduced phosphorylation levels of p38 protein and its downstream effector molecule MAPKAPK2—thereby blocking pro-inflammatory signal transduction into the nucleus and driving macrophage transition from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, effectively alleviating the persistent low-grade inflammatory state characteristic of diabetic and other chronic wounds [214]. Gel-SAGs MPs microspheres, constructed through composite integration of salamander skin-derived glycosaminoglycans (SAGs) with GelMA, activated the PPARγ nuclear receptor pathway to orchestrate macrophage glycolipid metabolic reprogramming, inducing differentiation toward the CD206+/Arg1+ reparative phenotype. This consequently attenuated sustained tissue damage from excessive inflammatory responses while concurrently promoting angiogenesis and ordered collagen deposition [302]. Curcumin-loaded flower-shaped microspheres leveraged negative regulation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, synergizing the co-delivery of BMSCs and anti-inflammatory polyphenols to achieve comprehensive remodeling of the inflammatory microenvironment in diabetic wounds [217](Fig. 8D–I). Furthermore, the mPDA-PEI@GelMA "microcage" architecture addressed the chronic inflammation dilemma triggered by excessive neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in diabetic wounds through contactless NETs capture [303], whereas MSC-gelatin microspheres constrained scar formation by downregulating TGF-β to shift the TGF-β1/β3 ratio toward an anti-fibrotic profile [212]. These multi-effect synergistic composite designs—integrating wet-adhesive moieties (such as the hydrogen bonding/π-π stacking interactions of SSAD@GelMA) [200], responsive modules (pH/ROS/enzyme sensitivity), and bioactive payloads (stem cells, cytokines, nanozymes)—collectively constitute the core strategy by which microsphere systems promote efficient healing and high-quality regeneration of chronic wounds. Although microsphere systems demonstrate precise stem cell modulation and immune reprogramming capabilities in cutaneous wound repair, their clinical translation remains constrained by the pathological complexity of human skin wounds (including metabolic dysregulation, immunosenescence, and polymicrobial infections in diabetic patients) and geometric scale disparities, alongside practical bottlenecks in scalable manufacturing, bioactivity preservation, and regulatory approval pathways inherent to multi-module composite fabrication processes.

Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for dental pulp regeneration

Dental pulp is a highly vascularized and innervated tissue with multipotent differentiation capacity; however, deep caries or trauma can trigger pulpitis and eventual necrosis, leading to nutrient deprivation, arrested root development and tooth weakening [304]. Conventional root-canal therapy replaces necrotic pulp with inert materials, irreversibly sacrificing pulp vitality; regenerative endodontics aims to restore a vascularized and innervated pulp–dentin complex, offering superior root strength and continued development [305]. Pulp-derived stem cells (DPSCs) and stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHEDs) combine high proliferative rate, multilineage potency and non-invasive harvest, making them the prime candidates for pulp regeneration [306,307]. The narrow, irregular root-canal space and limited apical blood supply render injectable microsphere carriers more advantageous than bulk scaffolds, as they can be delivered through minimally invasive apex approaches while providing a 3D niche for stem cell survival and controlled factor release. Injectable microsphere platforms have emerged as versatile vehicles for dental pulp regeneration by integrating cell-supportive bioactivities with tunable physicochemical properties. GelMA microspheres loaded with platelet lysate and nanoclay significantly enhance hDPSCs proliferation and odontogenic differentiation, while promoting robust in vivo microvessel formation to generate vascularized pulp-like tissue [219] (Fig. 9A–E). Similarly, RGD-functionalized alginate/apatite hydrogel microspheres exploit a multilevel architectural design in which RGD motifs facilitate cell–matrix interactions via integrin-mediated signaling, while bentonite incorporation enables tunable stiffness and controlled degradation kinetics [218]. These mechanical and structural attributes ensure sustained VEGF release, establishing a pro-angiogenic niche that recruits endothelial cells and accelerates neovascularization. Concurrently, the apatite and silica components release bioactive ions that potentiate hDPSC odontogenic commitment and drive organized collagen fiber deposition [309]. Beyond biochemical cues, the degradation profile of microspheres can be engineered to mirror the proliferation and differentiation dynamics of encapsulated cells, thereby preventing premature scaffold collapse while allowing seamless substitution by newly formed vascularized pulp tissue [220,221]. Furthermore, simvastatin-functionalized GelMA cryogel microspheres (SMS) continuously release simvastatin to enhance stem cell adhesion, proliferation, and angiogenic potential while mechanically shielding cells during the injection process [20]. Exosomes derived from distinct cellular sources contribute to pulp regeneration through complementary mechanisms [310]. Mesenchymal-, epidermal-, gingival- and pulp-derived exosomes have been shown to induce enamelin deposition, odontoblastic differentiation, Schwann cell-mediated nerve regeneration and angiogenesis, respectively[308,[311], [312], [313]] (Fig. 9F–G). However, dental pulp regeneration encompasses not only structural restoration but also functional recovery. Although the application of cell-microsphere systems in dental pulp tissue engineering enables the formation of pulp-like tissue to a certain extent, featuring vascularization and dentinogenesis, and even achieving full-length regeneration within the root canal, the restoration of its nutritional supply, sensory innervation, reparative and regenerative capacity, and defensive functions remains to be further validated. Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for cardiac regeneration

Myocardial infarction (MI) causes irreversible loss of cardiomyocytes and leaves a non-contractile fibrotic scar that precipitates heart failure; conventional revascularisation or pharmacotherapy cannot restore viable myocardium, creating an unmet need for biological regeneration [314,315]. Collectively, these effects increase ejection fraction and exercise tolerance, reduce fibrotic area and ventricular wall thickness, and augment myocardial vascular density. Direct injection of cells suffers from <5% retention within 24 h and poor survival under the harsh ischaemic milieu; microsphere-based carriers anchor transplanted cells at the injury site, prolong viability and amplify paracrine output [222,223]. The biodegradable and biocompatible PLGA copolymer is widely exploited as a cardiac tissue-engineering scaffold [319]. Following ischaemic injury, hypoxia triggers robust inflammation characterised by recruitment and M1 polarization of neutrophils and macrophages, which amplify tissue damage via cytokine/chemokine release, while lactate efflux from hypoxic cardiomyocytes creates an acidic microenvironment[[323], [324], [325]]. Microenvironment responsive microspheres can be exploited to target and release exosomes precisely within the infarct, promoting anti-inflammatory cytokine secretion and re-establishing homeostasis [326]. Leveraging microsphere biodegradability, functional exosomes are steadily released and enhance coronary endothelial cells angiogenesis via the miR-17-5p/PHD3/HIF-1α axis, promoting collateral vessel formation. Beyond exosomes, stem-cell-derived mitochondria represent an emerging therapeutic modality. Mitochondrial damage and dysfunction are now recognised as primary drivers of post-infarction tissue injury [327]. Chu et al. developed phosphatidylserine (PS)-decorated GelMA microcapsules (Mito@Microgels) to deliver ADSC-derived mitochondria [27] (Fig. 10F–I). In vitro and in vivo results demonstrate that Mito@Microgels efficiently transfer active mitochondria to cardiomyocytes, restore mitochondrial network integrity, attenuate local inflammation and alleviate oxidative-stress-induced injury while promoting angiogenesis. The system effectively delivers stem cells, exosomes, or mitochondria to the injured myocardium, significantly improving cardiac repair outcomes relative to conventional therapeutic approaches. However, clinical translation will require confirmation of long-term cardiac safety, maintenance of under repetitive mechanical contraction, and scalable production of uniformly sized microspheres that can be manufactured under GMP-compliant conditions.

Microsphere-based delivery of stem cells/exosomes for pulmonary fibrosis reversal

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF), particularly idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), autoimmune-associated interstitial lung disease, and chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, represents a progressive and often fatal outcome of chronic inflammatory disorders and repetitive tissue injury [331,332]. The pathological cascade is initiated by epithelial cell damage, particularly type II alveolar epithelial cell senescence, leading to basement membrane repair failure, fibroblast and macrophage activation, and excessive ECM deposition [333]. Despite the approval of pirfenidone and nintedanib in clinical, these agents merely attenuate fibrotic progression by inhibiting TGF-β1-induced collagen production without reversing established pathological remodeling or restoring normal pulmonary function [334,335]. MSCs have emerged as a promising therapeutic modality owing to their capacity to regulate epithelial permeability, mitigate inflammation, and promote tissue regeneration. Nevertheless, uncontrolled allogeneic immune responses, poor donor cell retention, and the inability to reverse established fibrotic lesions have severely constrained their clinical efficacy. Encapsulation of stem cells within micron-sized hydrogel matrices has been demonstrated to provide immunoprotection while facilitating nutrient and waste exchange, paracrine signaling, and targeted minimally invasive delivery, thereby significantly prolonging donor cell residence time in the lungs. Zhang et al. achieved microfluidic hydrogel microencapsulation of MSCs, which provided immunoprotection and enhanced their capacity for immunoregulation [228]. To further prolong the residence of encapsulated MSCs in the lungs, innate immune checkpoint molecules (pCD47) were introduced to the gel coating, which inhibited particle clearance by phagocytosis. This strategy helps maintain the presentation of soft matrix signals around donor MSCs while integrating host soluble signals. Through paracrine signaling, gel-coated MSCs regulate tissue macrophages and promote the transition of host macrophage subpopulations from a pro-fibrotic state to a transitional state, facilitating the resolution of collagen accumulation in PF [230]. In PF, aberrant tissue remodeling often leads to dysregulated ECM production, degradation, or post-translational modification, compromising physiological tissue mechanics and resulting in persistent organ dysfunction [336,337]. In principle, MSCs can regulate ECM turnover by responding to chemical and mechanical signals in their microenvironment, as native MSCs typically reside in interstitial areas surrounded by ECM. For example, MSCs increase ECM degradation by secreting certain isoforms of MMPs in pro-inflammatory cytokine environments, yet MSCs also synthesize more collagen molecules in response to ECM stiffening [338]. MSCs in soft hydrogels with physiological tissue stiffness exhibit enhanced c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling in response to TNF-α compared to those in rigid bulk hydrogels, resulting in increased secretion of interstitial collagenases that degrade collagen I. Reducing microgels to single-cell dimensions enables precise microenvironmental mimicry and enhanced in vivo delivery, while allowing exact control over the encapsulation volume and ligand spatial distribution for each cell [177]. Using microfluidics, MSCs are encapsulated within conformal gel coatings to define chemomechanical cues that guide the MSC secretome to promote matrix remodeling [229]. The gel coating provides tactile matrix signals to MSCs without requiring cells to traverse endothelial or epithelial barriers to contact interstitial ECM, thereby potentiating the ability of MSCs to respond to TNF-α, which results in increased downstream production of soluble interstitial collagenases by activating JNK, and thus reshaping host stroma through paracrine secretion (Fig. 11H–J).

Exosomes modulate pulmonary immunity by promoting alveolar macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, upregulating anti-fibrotic factors while downregulating pro-fibrotic mediators [339,340]. Cargoes such as miR-29b and miR-200 inhibit canonical fibrotic pathways including TGF-β/Smad3 and Wnt/β-catenin, thereby blocking fibroblast activation and collagen deposition [341,342]. A landmark clinical trial has provided the first human-based evidence confirming that nebulized inhalation of EVs derived from stem cells is safe and effective for patients with PF. Furthermore, ROS-responsive hydrogel microspheres with a calcium alginate (CaAlg) backbone were constructed using microfluidic technology [343]. Exosomes derived from hUC-MSCs were loaded via physical adsorption and chemical covalent coupling, and the surface was decorated with a ROS-responsive coating containing thioketal (TK) bonds (Exo@TK@CaAlg). This treatment enhances pulmonary capillary barrier integrity, reduces protein exudation, restores mitochondrial function, and promotes the transition of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. The strategy of “non-invasive nebulized inhalation + intelligent ROS response + exosome-targeted delivery” demonstrates exceptionally high clinical translational potential. Looking forward, By leveraging microenvironment-responsive systems that sense pathological cues—such as enzymatic activity, redox imbalance, ionic fluctuations, or mechanical stress—within fibrotic lesions, spatiotemporally precise delivery of anti-fibrotic cargoes may be achieved in PF.

However, given the pivotal role of macrophages in PF treatment, the importance of investigating patient-specific immune variability and its impact on therapeutic outcomes must be emphasized. Furthermore, long-term safety concerns—including gel persistence, off-target immune activation, and adverse paracrine effects—remain to be addressed.

Emerging frontiers

The integration of cell-derived biological vesicles and complex multicellular systems represents the next generation of regenerative medicine. The synergistic application of microspheres with apoptotic bodies and organoids are discussed, highlighting a transition from simple delivery to intelligent microenvironmental modulation.

Microsphere-based delivery of apoptotic bodies for tissue regeneration

Apoptotic bodies (ABs) are small vesicles containing cytoplasm, organelles and nuclear fragments that appear during the terminal phase of apoptosis [344]. As a major subclass of EVs, stem cell-derived ABs have recently emerged as critical messengers in intercellular communication, cargo transport and disease pathogenesis, exerting regenerative, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory functions that highlight their therapeutic and diagnostic potential [[345], [346], [347]]. Pre-clinical studies have documented the ability of ABs to ameliorate osteoporosis[[348], [349], [350]], intrauterine adhesions [351], muscle regeneration [352], articular cartilage repair [353], cutaneous wound healing [354] and M2 macrophage polarization [355]. For instance, ABs generated from ADSC apoptosis encapsulate functional cargo such as DNA, microRNA, mRNA, proteins and lipids. These molecules diffuse through the tissue and help to re-balance macrophage-driven inflammatory milieus, thereby preserving local immune homeostasis. Nevertheless, conventional ABs may suffer from insufficient activity, short half-life and rapid clearance, limiting their therapeutic efficacy. Mao et al. chemically induced ADSC apoptosis with staurosporine, isolated apoptosis-marked ABs by sequential differential centrifugation, and loaded them into gelatin microspheres to create an injectable AB@GMSs formulation [356] (Fig. 12). In vitro, ABs down-regulated the JAK-STAT1 pathway, suppressed ASS1 production, balanced macrophage polarization, controlled inflammation and promoted angiogenesis, accelerating wound closure. In vivo, the microspheres protected ABs from rapid systemic clearance and premature membrane fusion. As cellular metabolic products, apoptotic vesicles circumvent the tumorigenicity and ethical concerns associated with viable stem cells. Microsphere-based organoids

Under 3D in vitro culture conditions, stem cells can self-organize into organoids that recapitulate key aspects of native tissue architecture, cellular heterogeneity, and physiological function, thereby providing powerful biomimetic platforms for regenerative biology and translational studies [357,358]. The functional boundary between “microspheres” and “organoids” is defined by their complementary roles in the “top-down” and “bottom-up” engineering hierarchy: microspheres serve as engineered templates or delivery vehicles that provide external biophysical and biochemical cues, whereas organoids represent the biological self-organizing units that execute internal developmental programs [359,360].

During maturation, organoids continuously secrete EVs enriched in proteins, lipids, microRNAs, and mRNAs, which function as critical paracrine mediators of tissue repair, immune modulation, and disease intervention. Compared with conventional two-dimensional cultures, the 3D organoid microenvironment markedly enhances EV-mediated intercellular communication and bioactivity [361]. EVs from healthy cerebral organoids contain proneurogenic and synaptogenic factors that facilitate neural-circuit reconstruction, modulate excitation–inhibition balance, and suppress α-synuclein pathology, offering emerging therapeutic strategies for Parkinson's disease and related neurodegenerative disorders [362,363]. Similarly, EVs derived from kidney organoids co-cultured with monocytes up-regulate renal differentiation markers, reduce apoptosis, and enhance regenerative potential, highlighting their immunomodulatory–regenerative coupling effects. Epidermal-organoid (EpiO)-derived EVs promote angiogenesis and immune regulation via paracrine signaling and have demonstrated efficacy in skin-defect repair models [364]. Moreover, salivary-gland functional organoid-derived EVs exhibit therapeutic promise for ocular and salivary-gland tissue regeneration [365,366]. Notably, organoid-derived EV functions are highly cell-type- and state-dependent. EVs derived from pathological organoids (e.g., tumour organoids) may mediate deleterious signalling, underscoring the need for precise EV engineering to ensure therapeutic safety.

Microspheres with tunable stiffness, porosity and surface ligands provide an ECM-mimetic niche that enhances organoid differentiation uniformity and boosts EV yield per cell [357]. Specifically, microspheres regulate organoid morphogenesis by precisely delivering morphogens that govern key signaling pathways, such as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [367] (Fig. 13F–G). By encapsulating and locally releasing Wnt agonists (e.g., R-spondin-1), microspheres can stimulate the nuclear translocation of β-catenin and activate downstream target genes such as AXIN2 and LGR5 to maintain the stem cell niche and promote organoid expansion [369]. Conversely, the programmed depletion or inhibition of Wnt signaling via microspheres can overcome spatiotemporal instability and trigger directed differentiation into specialized lineages, such as mucus-producing goblet cells in intestinal models [370]. Furthermore, 3D micro-bead culture enables large-scale expansion of hMSCs while markedly increasing their EV secretion efficiency, providing a high-yield, high-activity vesicle source for cell-free therapies. High-throughput microfluidic fabrication of HAMA core–shell microspheres overcomes the batch-to-batch variability of traditional Matrigel, enabling scalable production of organoid-loaded beads [28,371]. The synergistic use of microspheres, organoids and their EVs creates a closed-loop “scaffold–model–signal” therapeutic ecosystem, spanning basic discovery to bedside application. This microsphere–organoid–EV ternary strategy has demonstrated broad translational potential across diverse regenerative-medicine models. In murine MI, EVs derived from silicate-stimulated endothelial progenitor cells encapsulated within hydrogel microspheres, significantly enhanced neovascularization and improved cardiac function [372]. For neural repair, brain-organoid-derived EVs incorporated into 3D bioprinted microsphere scaffolds facilitated reconstruction of complex neural architectures, providing promising approaches for central nervous system regeneration [373]. In musculoskeletal regeneration, co-delivery of outer membrane vesicles and Jagged1 within hydrogel microspheres synergistically promoted osteogenesis and angiogenic regulation [90]. Furthermore, digital light processing (DLP)-printed BMSCs-laden microspheres that self-organize into callus-like organoids accelerated osteogenesis and enhanced chondrogenesis in bone-regeneration models [368,374] (Fig. 13A–E). Despite their promise, microspheres, organoids and EVs still face several bottlenecks in regenerative medicine. Organoid differentiation consistency and scalable production are limited; while vertical-wheel bioreactors partially increase EV yield, patterned microsphere alignment and batch heterogeneity still require optimization [375,376]. Low EVs yield, insufficient targeting and off-target effects may compromise therapeutic efficiency, but surface modification, engineered cargo packaging and microsphere-based drug-delivery systems offer collaborative solutions[[377], [378], [379]].

In summary, the convergence of microsphere engineering with apoptotic bodies and organoid systems represents a paradigm shift from passive structural support to active, bio-instructive microenvironmental programming. By bridging the gap between cell-derived signaling and engineering precision, these emerging frontiers offer a transformative roadmap for personalized regenerative therapies and advanced disease modeling.

Challenges and future perspectives

Although preclinical studies across bone, cartilage, neural, skin, dental pulp, and cardiac regeneration have demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential, the transition of microsphere-based delivery systems from bench to bedside remains contingent upon resolving several cross-cutting translational prerequisites. These include the development of scalable manufacturing workflows that retain the precision of laboratory fabrication, formulation strategies that ensure payload bioactivity during extended storage and transport, standardized quality control metrics for batch release, and rigorous chronic biosafety profiling in large-animal models that recapitulate human disease complexity. Addressing these prerequisites will be essential for converting promising laboratory platforms into clinically viable regenerative therapies.

Material and biomechanical bottlenecks

A primary challenge lies in the insufficient stability of microspheres within dynamic mechanical environments, such as the continuous contraction of the heart or the weight-bearing movement of joints. In these contexts, microspheres may lack sufficient cohesion, leading to migration from the target site or premature structural failure [380]. Future designs must focus on “mechanically adaptive” materials that provide high adhesion and structural integrity under cyclic loading. Additionally, the long-term toxicity of degradation products remains a concern. For instance, the widely used PLGA microspheres generate acidic byproducts during degradation, which can create a highly acidic microclimate pH within the matrix [381,382]. This acidity not only risks triggering aseptic inflammatory responses but may also destabilize acid-labile bioactive entities like certain EVs or growth factors. Potential solutions include co-incorporating basic additives (e.g., magnesium carbonate) or blending with hydrophilic polymers like PEG to neutralize the microenvironment [383].

Metabolic adaptability and In vivo fate

Once implanted, encapsulated stem cells encounter a harsh, often hypoxic, pathological microenvironment. The metabolic adaptability of stem cells as a key factor in therapeutic failure; under hypoxic conditions, cells often switch to anaerobic glycolysis, leading to excessive lactate production and massive cell death [384]. Enhancing the survival of delivered cells requires strategies such as pre-conditioning cells or engineering microspheres that provide metabolic support (e.g., oxygen-generating components or nutrient-enriched matrices) [385]. Furthermore, the lack of long-term safety data regarding the foreign body reaction caused by residual microspheres remains a hurdle. Systematic studies are needed to evaluate the chronic immune response and the ultimate metabolic pathway of degraded carrier remnants in large-animal models.

Scalable production and clinical translation

From a manufacturing perspective, while microfluidic technologies offer superior control over microsphere size and encapsulation efficiency, their large-scale production feasibility is limited by high equipment costs and low throughput. Transitioning to GMP-compatible, automated fabrication processes is essential for commercial viability. Additionally, clinical translation requires rigorous standardization in storage stability and quality control to ensure the bioactivity of encapsulated stem cells or derivatives remains consistent throughout the supply chain. Future research should emphasize “translational value” by prioritizing biosafety assessments and validating therapeutic efficacy in clinically relevant models that accurately mimic human disease complexity.

Conclusion

In summary, regenerative medicine is shifting from simple tissue replacement toward in situ repair and functional reconstruction. Microsphere-based delivery systems, with their programmable architecture and spatiotemporal localization, have proven to be powerful platforms for the delivery of stem cells and their derivatives. These systems effectively address the limitations of traditional cell therapies by enhancing retention and providing a protective niche within injured bone, cartilage, neural, myocardial and pulmonary tissues.

While the field has advanced significantly with the inclusion of next-generation bioactive entities like mitochondria and apoptotic bodies, the path to clinical implementation requires overcoming the unsolved problems of material acidity, mechanical instability, and metabolic survival. By integrating rational biomaterial design with advanced fabrication techniques and a deeper understanding of the host-microenvironment interaction, microsphere-based platforms are poised to redefine the standards of cell-free and cell-based regenerative therapies.
