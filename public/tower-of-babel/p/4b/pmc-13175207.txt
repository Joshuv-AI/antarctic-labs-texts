The Synergistic Effect of Tanabesol and Calcium Hydroxide on Cryptosporidium parvum Oocyst Inactivation
Authors: Ayman Ahmed Shehata, Iqra Zafar, Hiroki Bochimoto, Kentaro Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cryptosporidiosis is a major diarrheal disease with high mortality, primarily in young animals, and poses a zoonotic threat worldwide. Currently, no available drugs are fully effective against Cryptosporidium, and most commercial disinfectants show limited efficacy against its oocysts, underscoring the need for improved control strategies.Aims:We aimed to investigate the ability of Tanabesol (an ortho-agent), calcium hydroxide solution, and their combination to inactivate Cryptosporidium parvum oocysts.Materials and Methods:Oocyst morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging. Vital dye staining combined with an in vitro excystation assay was applied to assess viability.Results:Ortho formulations induced oocyst wall disruption, rupture, and collapse, as revealed by SEM imaging. In addition, Tanabesol exhibited a dose-and time-dependent effect on Cryptosporidium oocysts, with higher concentrations and longer exposure times causing more significant inactivation. Hydrated lime has shown antimicrobial properties against enteric bacteria and viruses, but its impact on Cryptosporidium oocyst inactivation has yielded varying results. Limewater alone caused a significant decline in the total number of normal oocysts (i.e., >50%). Notably, no potentially viable oocysts were observed after sensitization with Tanabesol diluted in calcium hydroxide solution for 120 min, compared with using water as the diluent. Tanabesol with limewater reduced the viability percentage of oocysts to 30% and prevented sporozoite release by more than 84%.Conclusion:Adding calcium hydroxide to the preparation of Tanabesol should help in the control and prevention of cryptosporidiosis in livestock farms.

Introduction

Cryptosporidium spp. are important zoonotic enteric parasites that pose a major public health and veterinary concern, causing life-threatening diarrhea among children and immunocompromised adults.[1] Among diarrhea-producing pathogens in animals, Cryptosporidium spp. are considered the most prevalent invasive enteropathogen with high mortality, primarily in young animals.[2] Globally, Cryptosporidium prevalence in cattle ranges from 23.5% in South America to 33.5% in Europe, with similar rates in North America (25.5%) and Asia (24.5%).[3] Notably, high prevalence rates have been reported in Egypt, reaching up to 50.0% specifically among calves.[45] Transmission occurs by oral ingestion of thick-walled oocysts that can resist and survive against environmental stress and potent disinfectants.[6] This inherent resistance is due to the durable oocyst wall that contains a double layer of a protein-lipid-carbohydrate matrix forming a complex protective barrier.[7]

Cryptosporidium is a major zoonotic pathogen of public health concern, with transmission commonly occurring between infected animals, contaminated environments, and humans. Cleaning Cryptosporidium-contaminated animal farms remains a challenge; the commonly used disinfectants include chlorine, povidone-iodine solution, and glutaraldehyde.[8] However, when the disinfection procedures involving chlorine and sodium hypochlorite, which are widely used in water treatment, are ineffective, drinking water can be contaminated with Cryptosporidium, leading to zoonotic infections.[9101112] Ammonia is commonly used but has several drawbacks, including the nonpractical long contact time and irritating effect on animals and humans.[1314] Hydrogen peroxide is a less toxic alternative; however, the effective concentration needs to be at least 6, which is caustic, corrosive, and requires careful handling.[131415] Therefore, other disinfecting protocols are needed not only to maintain animal productivity but also to prevent zoonotic transmission and for public health.

Ortho-agents, normally used for routine cleaning of noncritical surfaces and as pesticides, have been used to clean animal farms. These agents were highly effective against oocysts of Cryptosporidium spp. and Eimeria, an apicomplexan protozoon with a similar life cycle to Cryptosporidium.[1617] Similarly, attempts have been made to use lime in its hydrated form (i.e., calcium hydroxide, Ca [OH]2) to disinfect premises against oocysts[1819] but no end-use products containing calcium hydroxide are commercially available according to the Environmental Protection Agency registration. Hydrated lime has long been employed to sterilize wastewater, with its potent antimicrobial properties attributed to its ability to elevate pH to around 12.[18] While liming has proven effective against viruses, fecal coliforms, and Salmonella and Giardia species,[101820] studies attempting to inactivate Cryptosporidium spp. oocysts using hydrated lime have yielded varying results.[192122] A preparation of the ortho-agent Triquil, using calcium hydroxide aqueous solution, was also examined.[16]

Tanabesol, an ortho-based agent, is commercially available and indicated for disinfecting of livestock and poultry houses against fly larvae and chicken coccidiosis oocysts. It comprises phenolic compounds which are well-documented for their potent disinfectant effect against a broad spectrum of pathogens, including oocysts of parasites. Therefore, in the present study, we evaluated the efficacy of Tanabesol diluted with calcium hydroxide aqueous solution compared to water in inactivating Cryptosporidium oocysts. In addition, we examined the efficacy of saturated calcium hydroxide solution without Tanbesol. Three disinfectant concentrations were tested across four exposure durations, with oocyst morphology assessed using electron microscopy.

Reliable assessment of Cryptosporidium oocyst viability is crucial in evaluating the efficacy of inactivation strategies. As oocyst morphology alone seems an unreliable indicator, vital staining and in vitro excystation assays are the most widely used methods for assessing oocyst viability.[23] Vital dyes or excystaion ability cannot measure the infectivity and have limitations regarding the ability to accurately enumerate oocysts or sporozoites.[24] However, previous studies used staining assays in conjugation with in vitro excystation demonstrated strong correlations with in vivo animal infectivity testing, which is costly and very time-consuming, in evaluating oocyst viability.[2325] As a result, in vitro assays are commonly used to evaluate oocyst viability and the efficacy of novel disinfection methods.[25] In this study, vital dye staining combined with an in vitro excystation assay was applied to assess viability.

Materials and Methods

Cryptosporidium parvum oocysts

In this study, we used oocysts of the Cryptosporidium parvum IOWA strain, purchased from Bunch Grass Farms, Geoff Pritchard, Deary, Idaho, USA, and stored at 4°C, for use in the following experiments.

Preparation of Tanabesol

Tanabesol® (Osaka Kasei Co., Ltd.), a commercially available ortho-agent, comprises two main active ingredients: orthodichlorobenzene (ODCB, 75% w/v) and cresol (7% w/v) according to the manufacturer. The diluents used for Tanabesol preparation were either Milli-Q water or limewater (calcium hydroxide aqueous solution; Fine Yakuhin Co., Ltd) frequently used in animal farms. Tanabesol was prepared at 30-fold, 50-fold, and 100-fold dilutions. Saturated calcium hydroxide solution alone (i.e., without Tanbesol) was also tested.

The saturated calcium hydroxide solution was prepared according to previously published reports[1626] as follows: an excess amount of calcium hydroxide fine natural particles was placed in a 50-mL conical tube, and 50 mL of Milli-Q water was added. The mixture was then inverted overnight, allowing the tube to stand for 24 h. The resulting supernatant was collected and used to dilute the ortho agent or was used alone. The pH was measured for the saturated calcium hydroxide solution and Tanabesol-containing formulations across each time point. In this study, the control condition consisted of Milli-Q water without Tanabesol.

Evaluation of Tanabesol sensitization

A total of 1.0 × 106 oocysts were transferred into a 1.5-mL tube and centrifuged three times with phosphate-buffered saline (phosphate buffer saline [PBS]; pH 7.4) at 4°C at 4000 rpm/3 min.[27] The supernatant was discarded, and 100 μL of diluted ortho-agent was added. The mixture was vortexed, and sensitization was conducted at room temperature for the designated times (5 min, 30 min, 60 min, and 120 min). After each exposure time point, the mixture was vortexed again, and 10 μL was placed onto a glass slide and examined under a 100x objective lens of a BZ-X810 Series all-in-one microscope (Keyence).

The inactivation of Cryptosporidium oocysts following Tanabesol sensitization was assessed based on morphological changes. Normal oocysts were counted for each disinfectant concentration and exposure time in triplicate trials. The same procedure was followed to evaluate the effect of limewater (without Tanabesol) on oocysts. In addition, to clarify the overall effect of the calcium hydroxide solution on oocyst inactivation, the total number of morphologically normal oocysts after 2 h of exposure was counted compared to the control. Oocyst suspensions were vortexed to ensure homogenization and then diluted 1:10 in either Milli-Q water or limewater. The preparations were incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Then, a 10 μL aliquot was loaded onto a Neubauer hemocytometer, and only morphologically normal oocysts were counted under microscopy. The total number of normal oocysts per microliter (oocysts/μL) was calculated according to the standard hemocytometer formula. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

In vitro excystation of Cryptosporidium parvum sporozoites following inactivation treatment

To assess oocyst viability, excystation of C. parvum sporozoites following treatment of oocysts with 30-or 50-fold dilutions of Tanabesol prepared in calcium hydroxide solution was attempted. Oocysts were pretreated with the Tanabesol preparations for 120 min at room temperature. Water treatment was used as a negative control. After 120 min had elapsed, oocysts were washed twice in PBS by centrifugation at 6000 rpm/5 min to remove the disinfectants.[27] Excystation of sporozoites was induced by incubation of oocysts in PBS with 0.15% sodium taurocholate for 1 h at 37°C.[28]

Vital dye staining of Cryptosporidium parvum oocysts

C. parvum oocysts were treated with 30-or 50-fold dilutions of Tanabesol prepared in a calcium hydroxide solution for 120 min at room temperature. Following the desired exposure time, the disinfectant was rinsed from the tubes by centrifugation twice with PBS, and pelleted oocysts were mixed at a ratio of 1:1 (vol/vol) with 0.4% trypan blue (Gibco) and incubated at room temperature for 30–60 min as previously described.[2930] The 0.4% trypan blue solution was prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Viability assessments involved counting both stained (nonviable) and unstained (viable) oocysts, with viability percentages subsequently being determined.

Scanning electron microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to evaluate oocyst surface topography after treatment with Tanabesol solutions and limewater. C. parvum oocysts exposed to treatments for 5 and 30 min were washed three times with PBS and then gently mixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer. The oocysts were washed with PBS three times, and settled onto silane-coated glass slides (Muto Pure Chemicals Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 30 min at room temperature. The oocysts were then washed three times in 0.1 PB and fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M PB for 1 h. Then, the oocysts were dehydrated in an ascending series of ethanol (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 3 × 100%, 5 min, respectively), transferred into 2-Methyl-2-propanol, and dried in a freeze dryer (VFD-21S; Vacuum Device Co., Ltd., Mito, Japan). After drying, the oocysts were coated with 5 nm osmium in an osmium coater (HPC-1SW; Vacuum Device Co., Ltd., Mito, Japan) and observed by use of a field emission SEM (Regulus 8100; Hitachi High Technologies Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analyses

Data for oocyst inactivation, trypan blue staining, and in vitro excystation assays were counted in five microscopic fields in triplicate trials. The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Where applicable, an analysis of variance was performed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test. For statistical analysis of the total count of potentially viable oocysts subjected to saturated calcium hydroxide treatment compared to the control, a t-test was conducted. P < 0.01 was considered significant.

Results

Microscopic evaluation of oocysts after Tanabesol sensitization

Under light microscopy (Keyence), normal C. parvum oocysts had a round to spherical shape, with sporozoites visible inside [Figure 1a]. In contrast, ortho-sensitized oocysts displayed morphological alterations in their wall, including uneven wall thickness, irregular shape, swelling, rupture, and leakage of their contents [Figure 1b]. Other oocysts presented in clumps [Figure 1c]. All these oocysts were considered inactivated [Figure 1b and c].

Effectiveness of tanabesol sensitization in Cryptosporidium parvum oocyst inactivation

Tanabesol caused a significant reduction in the count of morphologically normal oocysts regardless of the diluent type or exposure time [Figure 2; P = 0.01–0.001]. In addition, Tanabesol prepared with limewater showed a higher reduction in normal oocyst count across all exposure times compared to using the same dilution with water as the diluent, with significant differences (P = 0.001) at 5 min and 120 min. Increasing the Tanabesol dilution in limewater at each time point resulted in a nonsignificant difference in oocyst inactivation (P = 0.6–0.8). In contrast, significant differences (P = 0.01 and 0.001) were found between Tanabesol dilutions in water, mainly at 60 and 120 min, respectively. No oocysts with normal morphology were observed after sensitization for 120 min with 30- or 50-fold dilutions of Tanabesol in calcium hydroxide solution. However, even when oocysts were sensitized for 120 min, normal oocysts were still noted when ortho formulations were prepared with water. In the case of sensitization with 100-fold diluted ortho agent, the results showed lower oocyst inactivation compared to the higher Tanabesol concentrations, mainly at the longer exposure times (60 and 120 min).

Effect of Tanabesol on Cryptosporidium parvum oocyst viability

In the trypan blue staining assay, viable oocysts appeared regular, round or spherical, and unstained, whereas nonviable oocysts were swollen and stained [Figure 3a]. The nonviable oocysts constituted an insignificant portion (<2%) of all parasite oocysts observed under the microscope in the control samples. However, there were significant reductions in percent viability, to 29.8% and 32%, respectively, in the case of oocysts pretreated with 30-or 50-fold dilutions of Tanabesol in calcium hydroxide solution for 120 min, compared to the control (P = 0.0001). The percentage of oocyst viability showed no significant difference between the two Tanabesol dilutions used [P = 0.51; Figure 3b].

Statistical significance was determined by Tukey honestly significant difference tests, where the asterisks (***) indicate significant differences (P < 0.001) between groups; (ns) represents no significance

Regarding the in vitro excystation assay, Cryptosporidium oocysts excysted successfully under normal conditions, whereas the nonviable oocysts showed discernible damage to their walls, and/or their sporozoites were visible inside [Figure 3c]. A 2-h exposure with Tanabesol diluted in limewater significantly inhibited the mean number of sporozoites per microscopic view from 113 to 18 and 19 with the 30- and 50-fold Tanabesol formulations, respectively (P = 0.0001). A nonsignificant difference in excystation level was found between the two Tanabesol concentrations [P = 0.96; Figure 3d]. For both experiments, values are presented as the mean of three independent replicates.

Impact of saturated calcium hydroxide solution without tanabesol on Cryptosporidium parvum oocysts

At 5 and 30 min after exposure of parasite oocysts to saturated limewater, the proportions of damaged oocysts per microscopic view represented 57% and 55%, respectively. Microscopically, these damaged oocysts displayed irregular shapes, with most appearing ruptured and releasing their contents [Figure 4a]. However, this proportion consistently decreased in the sample over time (e.g., 26% and 11% at 60 and 120 min following exposure, respectively), allowing intact normal oocysts to re-emerge, as shown in Figures 4a and b.

After exposure for 2 h, the total number of normal oocysts in the sample was significantly reduced (P = 0.01) to 59.8 × 103 oocysts/μL compared to 139.4 × 103 oocysts/μL in the control group [Figure 4c]. The pH of the calcium hydroxide solution was 10.6 ± 0.2 during the exposure periods. In addition, Tanabesol prepared with calcium hydroxide solution was found to be strongly alkaline with a pH of 10.1 ± 0.1, whereas water-diluted Tanabesol exhibited a more neutral pH of 7.6 ± 0.2 [Figure 4d].

C. parvum oocysts under pure water conditions (a negative control) appeared round to spherical in shape, with intact walls and well-preserved coats [Figure 5a]. Oocysts treated with 30-fold diluted Tanabesol in pure water exhibited serious wall disruption and deformities, loss of their outer oocyst coat, and rupture at specific areas, allowing release of the contents, including sporozoites, as shown in Figures 5b and 6a. Discussion

Although many commercial disinfectants can be used in the field and laboratory against Cryptosporidium oocysts, they often show limited efficacy and are subject to certain restrictions.[82731] This challenge of developing effective inactivation methods for controlling cryptosporidiosis in both veterinary and human medicine prompted the present study, which investigated the ability of Tanabesol, an ortho-agent, diluted in water or calcium hydroxide solution, to inactivate C. parvum oocysts.

In this study, we found that Tanabesol significantly inactivated C. parvum oocysts, irrespective of the base diluent and concentration used. In addition, similar dose- and time-dependent effects of cresol-based disinfectants on Cryptosporidium oocysts and Eimeria tenella oocysts have been reported previously.[832] Interestingly, Tanabesol formulated with limewater significantly reduced potentially viable oocyst counts across all exposure durations compared to water-based dilutions. In addition, no oocysts with normal morphology were observed after sensitization with 30-or 50-fold diluted Tanabesol in calcium hydroxide solution for 120 min; however, normal oocysts were still noted when water was used as the diluent base. Similarly, an ortho-agent, Triquil, prepared using calcium hydroxide aqueous solution, maintained significantly higher inactivation ability against Cryptosporidium oocysts compared to using water for dilution.[16] In our study, calcium hydroxide solution alone or with Tanabesol exhibited a strongly alkaline pH, whereas water-diluted Tanabesol had a more neutral pH, as previously reported.[2633] Critically, there are no scientific data demonstrating that the chemical interaction of cresol, being a weak phenolic acid, in the strongly alkaline environment of limewater produces a synergistic disinfectant effect, and the only relevant historical observations conclude that the mixture has no added germicidal value in terms of chemical interactions.[34] Consequently, this difference in oocyst inactivation ability due to the difference in the base used for dilution might be attributed to a synergistic interaction between Tanabesol and calcium hydroxide, which created a strong alkaline pH environment that might be more favorable for membrane disruption and oocyst inactivation. The proven efficacy of the ortho-dichlorobenzene/cresol formulation must be accompanied by the proper application and handling to ensure animal safety and prevent toxicity in livestock and poultry farms.[3536] In addition, its environmental impact should be carefully considered, as improper use can pose ecological risks.[37]

Previous research has demonstrated the antimicrobial properties of lime on enteric bacteria and viruses;[182021] however, there is little information about the effect of lime on the inactivation of parasite oocysts. In this study, after 2 h of exposure to limewater without Tanabesol, the total number of potentially viable oocysts declined by almost half compared to that of the control group. Another report[26] attributed a similar finding of a 49.5% oocyst inactivation rate with 0.2% calcium hydroxide after 1 h of exposure to its strong alkaline pH. In the field, successful sanitation of a Cryptosporidium-infected farm was achieved by applying hydrated lime to calf pens,[19] while other studies reported minimal to no effect on disease outcomes or oocyst inactivation.[2122] Surprisingly, our results showed a significantly high proportion of inactivated to normal oocysts at 5 and 30 min after exposure to saturated calcium hydroxide solution; however, this proportion declined over time and eventually was nearly 10% after only 2 h. This sudden sharp shock of limewater on Cryptosporidium oocysts due to its high alkalinity has also been described elsewhere.[182638] Structural variation in oocyst wall morphology has been reported,[39] suggesting varying levels of robustness between oocysts. Notably, lime exposure was previously found to increase C. parvum infectivity over time, leading to the hypothesis that lime preferentially destroys nonviable oocysts upon short-term exposure,[21] which would explain the apparent rise in percentages of inactivated oocysts at 5 and 30 min in the present study.

The viability percentages using trypan blue stain significantly declined to around 29.8% and 32% in samples treated with 30-fold and 50-fold Tanabesol dilutions prepared in limewater, respectively, compared to the control. Few studies have assessed the viability of Cryptosporidium oocysts via staining following treatment with ortho-agents, although previous research has shown the value of using Trypan blue to evaluate other types of disinfectants against Cryptosporidium oocysts, providing an easy means to distinguish between viable and nonviable oocysts.[3040] Higher viability percentages were reported in studies evaluating the anti-protozoal effect of AgNPs,[40] bleach, and heated water.[4142] Given that these previous studies suggest that C. parvum oocysts exclude trypan blue dye when exposed to some treatment protocols while being noninfectious for mice,[4142] the results of the present study could be much more effectively applied in the field to combat animal infectivity.

Our results revealed that Tanabesol formulated with limewater reduced sporozoite release by more than 84% following a 2-h exposure to either a 30- or 50-fold dilution compared to the control group. Previous studies have shown the same inactivation rates of sporozoite release but only after 6 h of exposure to 70% or 100% ethanol or methanol, respectively.[28] While the excystation assay showed only 74.7% inactivation following treatment with 3% hydrogen peroxide plus silver nitrate for 30 min.[27] Similarly, cresol-based products inhibited sporulation of Eimeria oocysts by up to 85.5%.[32] Although previous studies examining ortho-agents or even calcium hydroxide effects on Cryptosporidium oocysts remain very limited, our findings highlight the potent and promising of Tanabesol formulated in limewater to significantly reduce the viability of oocysts and sporozoites release compared to traditional disinfectants on the market.

In SEM imaging, ortho formulations profoundly altered the oocyst wall, culminating in oocyst rupture, leakage of the contents, and collapse. The clumping appearance of oocysts and leakage of their contents have previously been noted after treatment with cresol-based or ozone disinfectants, which might suggest chemical destruction of the oocyst wall, releasing sporozoites.[843] Furthermore, oocysts previously treated with ortho-agents showed irregular shapes and uneven wall thickness.[16] Previous SEM analyses revealed oocyst damage, including content leakage due to ozone treatment,[29] as well as coat disruption and shape deformities caused by the combined treatment of trypsin with UV irradiation.[28] Tanabesol is composed of two main ingredients, ODCB and cresol. This formula combines the solvent properties of ODCB with the antimicrobial and disinfectant qualities of cresol. Therefore, it seems that Tanabesol in this study exerts its activity by disrupting the oocyst wall and breaking down it immediately,[17] following the known mode of action of cresols.[83244] For limewater alone or Tanabesol combined with limewater, SEM imaging highlighted the loss of coat roughness, surface wrinkling, and pitting. Cryptosporidium oocyst surface wrinkling and pitting have been reported after treatment with trypsin.[45] Previous reports discussed a similar mode of action of calcium hydroxide through wall disruption, protein denaturation, and its shock effect due to high pH.[2646] Thus, although lime disinfection alone showed a significant reduction in total count of potentially viable oocysts, our results suggest that it can be a more valuable complement in Tanabesol preparations as a control measure leading to better inactivation of Cryptosporidium oocysts in animal herds.
