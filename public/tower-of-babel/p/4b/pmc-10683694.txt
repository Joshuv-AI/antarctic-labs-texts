Quantitative evaluation of retinal and choroidal microcirculation in inactive ocular sarcoidosis
Authors: Kubra Ozdemir Yalcinsoy, Yasemin Ozdamar Erol, Gozde Hondur, Pinar Cakar Ozdal
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate retinal and choroidal alterations in eyes with inactive ocular sarcoidosis and to compare the findings with healthy controls.Methods:This cross-sectional study included 11 patients with inactive ocular sarcoidosis (study group) and 11 healthy individuals (control group). Retinal and choroidal vascular differences were evaluated using optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) and enhanced depth imaging–optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT).Results:A total of 21 eyes of ocular sarcoidosis patients without active inflammation and 22 eyes of healthy controls were analyzed. The mean whole-image deep capillary plexus vessel density was significantly lower in the study group than in the control group (P = 0.03). The acircularity index values were significantly higher, and the foveal density-300 values were significantly lower in the study group than in the control group (P = 0.01, both). The flow areas of the choriocapillaris at 1-, 2-, and 3-mm radius areas were also significantly lower in the study group (all P < 0.01). The mean choroidal thickness (CT) was significantly lower in all regions between nasal and temporal at 3000 μm in the study group (all P < 0.05). The choroidal vascularity index (CVI) values were also significantly lower in the study group (P < 0.01).Conclusion:Ocular sarcoidosis was associated with a reduction in CT and the CVI with a decrease in retinal vessel density and choriocapillary flow parameters. The combination of OCTA and EDI-OCT imaging may be useful in monitoring ocular sarcoidosis eyes to detect alterations in the retinal and choroidal layers.

Sarcoidosis is a multisystemic chronic disease characterized by noncaseating granulomas of unknown etiology, which may present with the involvement of the lungs, lymph nodes, skin, and eyes.[1] Involvement of any part of the eye or ocular adnexa may occur in patients with sarcoidosis. Ocular sarcoidosis usually presents with bilateral uveitis, at rates reported to range from 30% to 60%.[2] Uveitis often presents as granulomatous anterior uveitis but also as intermediate uveitis, posterior uveitis, panuveitis, and retinal vasculitis, as well as with optic nerve involvement.[1-3]

In recent years, new rapid and noninvasive imaging methods, such as optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) and enhanced depth imaging–optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT), have been used for the evaluation of retinal and choroidal microvascularity alterations in different uveitis entities.[4-12] Previous studies have reported that OCTA visualizes macular microvascular alterations and deep retinal vascular structures better than fluorescein angiography (FA) in patients with uveitis.[5-9]

With the use of EDI-OCT, visualization of the structural and vascular features of the choroid is possible. Increased choroidal thickness with active inflammation on EDI-OCT has been described in entities such as Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease, Behçet’s uveitis, sympathetic ophthalmia, and HLA-B27 uveitis.[11-15] However, choroidal thickness measurements may not specifically indicate vascular damage, as the choroid is composed of vasculature and stroma. Moreover, choroidal thickness is affected by many ocular and systemic factors. These limitations have led to the development of the choroidal vascular index (CVI), calculated as the ratio of the choroidal vascular area to the total choroidal area,[12,16] which has been reported to be more effective than the choroidal thickness in determining alterations in choroidal vascularity.[17]

The aim of this study is to evaluate the alterations in retinal microvascular and choroidal layers in eyes with inactive ocular sarcoidosis patients by relatively new imaging methods, OCTA and EDI-OCT.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted between August 2020 and February 2021 in the uvea clinic of a tertiary eye hospital. The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study population and data collection

Eleven patients who had bilateral posterior segment inflammatory involvement of ocular sarcoidosis (study group) and 11 healthy participants without any ocular disease (control group) were included in the study. The diagnosis of ocular sarcoidosis was previously made according to the diagnostic criteria of the International Workshop on Ocular Sarcoidosis (IWOS) by the same uveitis specialists.[18] The study group consisted of patients with inactive ocular sarcoidosis who had no evidence of active inflammation on slit lamp biomicroscopy, dilated fundus examination, OCT, and FA for at least 6 months after discontinuation of systemic corticosteroid therapy, and who did not receive dexamethasone intravitreal implants or periocular injections in the past 6 months. Healthy participants of similar age and gender, with the best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 1.0, and normal clinical ocular findings, were included in the control group.

All participant’s ages, gender, and BCVA according to the Snellen chart, auto-refractometry, tonometry, and anterior segment findings were obtained using a slit-lamp biomicroscope, and fundus examination findings after pupil dilation were evaluated. Visual acuity was presented as a decimal acuity. The clinical findings of the ocular sarcoidosis patients in the active uveitic period were recorded. The duration of disease in patients with ocular sarcoidosis was defined as the time from the onset of ocular symptoms to data collection. The duration of active disease was evaluated as the time during which patients had signs of active uveitis. The remission time was evaluated as to how long the patients did not show any signs of intraocular inflammation from the time of data collection. OCTA and EDI-OCT measurements were performed by the same specialist for all individuals.

Any eyes with spherical refractive errors of >+4 and >−4 diopter spherical equivalent, a history of ocular surgery or trauma, active inflammation, and media opacities, diseases such as glaucoma, optic nerve diseases, diabetic retinopathy, macular degeneration, choroidal neovascularization, retinal neovascularization and/or ischemia, macular edema, and other uveitic diseases, were also excluded. Additionally, patients with systemic vascular diseases (such as systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular events) were excluded from the study.

OCTA measurements

OCTA measurements were performed using the AngioVue software of the Avanti RTVue XR (Optovue, Inc., Fremont, CA, USA). OCTA images with a signal strength below 8, motion artifacts with poor fixation, and segmentation errors leading to a poor outline of the vascular network were excluded from the analysis. The OCTA system uses a split-spectrum amplitude-decorrelation angiography algorithm and receives an A-section of 70 000 A-scans per second to acquire OCTA volumes consisting of 400 × 400 A-scans. Fovea-centered 6 × 6 mm scanning fields were used for each eye.

In the foveal avascular zone, the foveal avascular zone area (in square millimeters), circularity index of the foveal avascular zone, foveal avascular zone perimeter (in millimeters), and foveal density-300 (vessel density of a region 300 μm around the foveal avascular zone) parameters were evaluated. Macular vessel density was calculated automatically by the device in the superficial capillary plexus and deep capillary plexus. The flow area (in square millimeters) of the choriocapillaris was determined in 1-, 2-, and 3-mm radius areas, centered on the foveal avascular zone using the OCTA flow assessment tool [Fig. 1].

Choroidal thickness and CVI measurements

Choroidal thickness measurements were performed using the EDI-OCT mode of Spectralis Domain-OCT (software version 6.0; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) between 10 and 11 am to avoid diurnal fluctuations. Each EDI-OCT image was averaged over 100 scans using the automatic averaging and eye tracking system. The choroid was defined as the layer from the outer portion of the hyperreflective line corresponding to the outer border of the retina pigment epithelium/Bruch’s membrane complex to the sclera–choroidal interface. Choroidal thickness was measured using the manual caliper provided by the software at the fovea (subfoveal choroid and at points 1500 and 3000 μm nasal and temporal to the fovea).

The EDI-OCT images were analyzed using ImageJ version 1.47 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MA, USA) according to the binarization protocol described by Agrawal et al.[16] After converting each image to an 8-bit image, the Niblack auto local threshold tool was used, which allows the demarcation of the choroidal stroma and its total vascular lumen. The binarized image was then transformed to the red, green, and blue (RGB) format, and luminal areas were highlighted using the color thresholding tool. The total choroidal area, luminal area, and stromal area were then calculated within the central 1500 μm [Fig. 2]. The ratio of the luminal area to the total choroidal area yielded the CVI. The luminal-to-stromal ratio was also calculated.[12,16] All the choroidal vascular parameters and measurements were analyzed separately by two investigators blinded to the participants.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated considering the large effect size (0.8) of Cohen’s d, a type I error of 0.05, and a type II error of 0.2 via G*Power v. 3.1.9.7. This calculation revealed that the sample size for each group should be 21 to find the difference between the two groups.

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as means ± standard deviations, medians, frequencies, and percentages. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables (frequencies and percentages) were compared using the Chi-square test. For comparisons between the study and control groups, the Student’s t test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonnormally distributed data. The correlations between best-corrected visual acuity with a duration of disease, duration of active disease, and other variables were calculated using Pearson’s and Spearman’s rho correlations analyses. P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 21 eyes of 11 ocular sarcoidosis patients (eight females and three males) and 22 eyes of 11 healthy controls (seven females and four males) were included in the study. One eye in the study group was excluded because of poor image quality due to cataract formation. The median age was 52.0 years (range: 33–60 years) in the study group and 47.0 years (range: 40–54 years) in the control group. The study and control groups were similar in age and gender (P = 0.71 and P = 0.65, respectively). The BCVA was 0.7 ± 0.2 (range: 0.1–1.0) in the study group and 1.0 in the control group.

Six patients (54.5%) had definite ocular sarcoidosis, three (27.3%) had presumed ocular sarcoidosis, and two (18.2%) had a probable diagnosis of ocular sarcoidosis according to the IWOS criteria.[17] The median duration of the disease was 50.0 months (range: 15 months–15 years). The median duration of active disease was 33.0 months (range: 9 months–5 years), and the median remission time was 15 months (range: 6 months–9 years). In the active uveitis period of ocular sarcoidosis patients, intraocular inflammation was panuveitis in eight eyes (36.4%), intermediate uveitis in eight eyes (36.4%), and isolated posterior uveitis in six eyes (27.2%). Vitreous haze and/or snowball opacities were seen in 14 eyes (63.6%). Peripheral chorioretinal lesions were present in two eyes (9.1%). Segmental/nodular periphlebitis was seen in eight (36.4%) eyes. Optic disc swelling, macroaneurysms, and solitary choroidal nodules were not observed in any eyes. During the active uveitis period, eight eyes (36.4%) had cystoid macular edema. Intraocular inflammation was controlled with corticosteroid therapy in three patients (27.3%), corticosteroid and immunosuppressive combination in eight patients (72.7%), and biologic agent (anti-tumor necrosis factor α) in one patient (9.0%) resistant to combined immunosuppressive therapy during active uveitis period. Based on the clinical condition of the patient, steroid therapy was discontinued by reducing the dose.

At the time of study data collection, all ocular sarcoidosis patients were in remission. There was no evidence of active inflammation in fundus examination and FA, and macula edema in OCT in any patient. No patient had an active choroidal nodule on EDI-OCT or OCTA. During the OCT evaluation, two patients (18.2%) were on methotrexate, four patients (36.4%) were on azathioprine (AZA), one patient (9.1%) was on AZA and cyclosporine combination, and one patient (9.1%) on AZA and adalimumab. Three patients (27.3%) were in remission without medication. Steroid treatment was discontinued in all patients. The median time from discontinuation of systemic corticosteroid to OCT evaluation was 9.0 months (range 6 months–8 years).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated decreased retinal perfusion and choriocapillary flow parameter, decreased choroidal thickness, and CVI in eyes with ocular sarcoidosis using OCTA and EDI-OCT imaging procedures. In addition, it has been shown with a quantitative method that the choroidal vascular structures are affected as well as the choroidal stromal structure in eyes with ocular sarcoidosis in the present study.

Previous studies have reported that OCTA is superior to FA in evaluating macular microvascular changes in uveitis.[7-9] A recent OCTA study reported that capillary nonperfusion and decreased perfusion were detected more frequently in the choroid, choriocapillaris, and deep capillary plexus than in the superficial capillary plexus in patients with intermediate uveitis of various etiologies.[9]

Studies evaluating active inflammation and retinal/choroidal involvement in patients with ocular sarcoidosis have reported characteristic fluorescence patterns on FA and indocyanine green angiography (ICG).[4,5,19,20] There were a few studies investigating retinal and choroidal microvasculature in ocular sarcoidosis eyes with posterior segment inflammatory involvement using OCTA.[5,6] Cerquaglia et al.[5] reported that retinal/choroidal vascular structure was damaged in ocular sarcoidosis patients using OCTA (Spectralis HRA + OCT2, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). However, this study consisted of heterogeneous ocular sarcoidosis patients with active and inactive inflammation.[5] Capillary nonperfusion areas and alterations in the capillary network were observed more frequently in the deep capillary plexus than in the superficial capillary plexus of ocular sarcoidosis.[5] They also found significantly diminished deep capillary plexus and choriocapillary vessel densities and voiding defects of choriocapillary flow.[5] Additionally, they reported that 74% of the patients exhibited perifoveal capillary perfusion and capillary abnormalities on OCTA, and ocular sarcoidosis patients had larger foveal avascular zone areas than controls.[5] The present study demonstrated lower deep capillary plexus vessel density and smaller choriocapillary flow area and foveal avascular zone parameters in eyes with inactive ocular sarcoidosis compared to control eyes. The current study’s findings were consistent with the study of Cerquaglia et al.,[5] but differently, the current study included only ocular sarcoidosis patients without signs of active inflammation.

In a previous study, deep capillary plexus vessel density values were reported to be significantly lower in ocular sarcoidosis patients, but superficial capillary plexus vessel density and foveal avascular zone values were similar to controls.[6] The study showed that deep retinal vessels are more affected than superficial vessels in ocular sarcoidosis patients, and our results are in line with this study. The important difference between our study from this study is that we evaluated choroidal involvement in addition to retinal vascular changes in inactive ocular sarcoidosis patients and choriocapillary flow area values were found to be lower in eyes with inactive ocular sarcoidosis in the current study.

Balci et al.[21] evaluated retinal and choroidal microvascularity with OCTA in sarcoidosis patients without ocular involvement and reported that significant changes were observed especially in the deep capillary plexus vessel density and foveal density-300 values in sarcoidosis patients compared to the control group. This study suggested that sarcoidosis patients without ocular findings may have microvascular changes in the posterior segment.[21] OCTA can be helpful in detecting subclinical posterior segment involvement in sarcoidosis, yet this may be clarified in the future by long-term and large-population studies.

Choroidal involvement in ocular sarcoidosis has been investigated using imaging modalities.[5,10,20-22] Güngör et al.[10] evaluated the choroid of ocular sarcoidosis patients in the inactive phase using EDI-OCT and compared the choroidal thickness values to controls.[10] They found that the mean choroidal thickness between 1500 mm nasal and 1000 mm temporal to the fovea was significantly lower in the ocular sarcoidosis group. Similarly, in our study, the choroidal thickness values in all quadrants were lower in inactive ocular sarcoidosis than in the controls. However, choroidal thickness measurements indicate the total thickness of the vascular and stromal regions and may not specify vascular damage. Therefore, the results of the aforementioned study may be of limited value for evaluating vascular status. Choroidal thickness values differ even between healthy individuals depending on many variables, such as age, gender, intraocular pressure, diurnal variations, axial length, and refractive errors.[16,17] Agrawal et al.[23] compared the choroidal thickness and CVI values of 19 patients with panuveitis at baseline and during a 3-month follow-up period. While they observed significant decreases in CVI values at 3 months compared to the baseline and resolution of active inflammation, they found no significant changes in choroidal thickness values.[23] The authors concluded that the CVI is more effective than the choroidal thickness in monitoring choroidal vascular changes due to inflammation.

To evaluate choroidal vascularity in eyes with ocular sarcoidosis, we used the CVI as a quantitative parameter known to be more resistant to biological variables than choroidal thickness, and also we compared choriocapillary flow areas in OCTA. This study is the first study to simultaneously evaluate the choroidal thickness, CVI, and choriocapillary flow area in inactive ocular sarcoidosis patients.

The CVI and OCTA measurements indicated that all the vascular structures of the choroid are affected in ocular sarcoidosis. In addition, our results showed that the stromal structure of the choroid is also affected. These can be explained by the chronic inflammatory response and/or primary involvement of the choroidal layer. Furthermore, the findings of the current study indicate that the retinal/choroidal vascular structure is disorganized even if patients are treated systemically and/or locally. Moreover, the results of the present study suggest that OCTA, OCT, and CVI measurements in the follow-up period may reveal permanent damage to the retinal and choroidal structures that cannot be detected by FA. These findings may provide insights into the visual capacity of patients during ocular sarcoidosis remission. If we could do ICG during active inflammation and follow-ups, we would have a clear idea about choroidal involvement. However, our study shows that the follow-up and progression of ocular sarcoidosis patients may be conducted with OCT and CVI measurements.

A noteworthy finding in this study was that in eyes with ocular sarcoidosis, BCVA demonstrated a stronger correlation with the active disease duration than the total duration of the disease. There was no significant correlation between BCVA and vascular parameters (OCTA and OCT findings) in our sample, yet correlation analyses should be reviewed in larger samples.

The main limitations of this study are its small sample size. However, we could not achieve a study with a larger number of patients because ocular sarcoidosis is a relatively uncommon clinical presentation. In addition, data from both eyes of the patients were analyzed due to the small sample size of the study. There may be intrinsic similarity in vessel density in both eyes of the same patient, this has led to the ignoring of dependency between the two eyes. Another limitation is that patients did not undergo ICG imaging. Further studies with larger samples in which all the findings are evaluated together using dye-based angiographic methods, such as FA and ICG, may provide a better understanding of alterations in eyes with ocular sarcoidosis. A longitudinal study would also be illuminating.

Conclusion

Ocular sarcoidosis was associated with reduced choroidal thickness and CVI with decreased retinal vessel density and choriocapillary flow parameters. OCTA demonstrated persistent changes in retinochoroidal microvascularity in ocular sarcoidosis patients without clinical and FA signs of active inflammation. Our observations in sarcoidosis concluded that it may be part of the spectrum of the choroidal changes occurring in uveitis. The combination of OCTA and EDI-OCT imaging may be useful in detecting alterations in the retinal and choroidal layers and monitoring ocular sarcoidosis.
