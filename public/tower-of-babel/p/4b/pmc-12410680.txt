Transpulmonary bubble transit in patients hospitalised with COVID-19 pneumonia
Authors: Hooman Poor, Adeel Abbasi, Daniel Howell, Alexandra Reynolds, Kaitlin Reilly-Kit, Kevin Rurak, Maya Cohen, Grayson Baird, Lilian Worst, Elizabeth Shin, Paul Yu, Alison Lee, Corey E Ventetuolo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe previously demonstrated a high prevalence of transpulmonary bubble transit (TPBT) using transcranial Doppler (TCD) in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, but these observations require confirmation.MethodsPatients at two academic medical centres, hospitalised with COVID-19 pneumonia and requiring any form of respiratory support, were studied. The first TCD study was performed at the time of enrolment and repeated approximately 7 and 14 days later if participants remained hospitalised.Results91 participants were enrolled. At the first TCD, 14 participants (15%) were receiving oxygen by nasal cannula, 41 participants (45%) were receiving oxygen by high flow nasal cannula, 8 participants (9%) were receiving non-invasive positive pressure, 28 participants (31%) were receiving mechanical ventilation and 2 participants (2%) were receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. 33 participants (36%) demonstrated TPBT at the first TCD. There was evidence that the presence of TPBT and increased heart rate together was associated with in-hospital death (p=0.02). For every one-unit increase in heart rate, the odds of death increased 11% (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.20, p=0.01) for those with TPBT; however, there was no evidence of this increase for those without TPBT (OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.05, p=0.76). For participants with subsequent TCD assessments, 55% demonstrated TPBT during the second TCD assessment, and 85% demonstrated TPBT at the third TCD assessment.ConclusionsThe prevalence of TPBT in hospitalised patients with COVID-19 pneumonia is higher than expected and the presence of TPBT increases over time in those that remained alive and hospitalised. In patients with TPBT, increased heart rate, a marker of hyperdynamic circulation, is associated with increased mortality.

Introduction

COVID-19 acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) has been characterised by hypoxaemia out of proportion to impairment in lung compliance1 and a disconnect between venous admixture and the fraction of non-aerated lung tissue,2 particularly early in the course of disease. This dissociation between gas exchange and both lung mechanics and radiographic findings raises the question of whether mechanisms of hypoxaemia in COVID-19 ARDS differ from those in classical ARDS. Autopsy studies in patients with COVID-19 have demonstrated pulmonary capillary deformation3 and prominent intrapulmonary bronchopulmonary anastomoses.4 5

We previously reported a high prevalence of transpulmonary bubble transit (TPBT) using transcranial Doppler (TCD) in 18 participants with severe COVID-19 ARDS.6 The degree of TPBT was inversely correlated with arterial oxygen tension/fractional inspired oxygen ratios, suggesting that pulmonary vasodilation may explain the disproportionate hypoxaemia in some patients with COVID-19 pneumonia. In this pilot study, there was a trend towards increasing TPBT and longer durations of hospitalisation, suggesting progressive pulmonary vascular involvement over the course of the disease.7 However, this small study was cross-sectional in design and only included patients requiring mechanical ventilation.

The detection of TPBT in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia may be analogous to hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS), a pulmonary vascular disorder of chronic liver disease characterised by pulmonary vascular dilations, hypoxaemia and hyperdynamic circulation with elevated cardiac index.8 9 Similarly, we previously demonstrated a relationship between elevated cardiac index and worse hypoxaemia in COVID ARDS.10 Patients with HPS have lower circulating bone morphogenetic protein-9 (BMP9), a member of the TGF-β superfamily involved in vascular homeostasis and regulation of angiogenesis, compared with cirrhotic controls, and lower BMP9 levels are associated with more severe HPS.11

The aim of the current prospective observational study was to investigate TPBT in a larger multicentre cohort of patients with COVID-19 pneumonia. Specifically, we sought to perform longitudinal TCD bubble studies on patients with COVID-19 pneumonia early and over the course of their hospitalisation, with variable degrees of respiratory failure, and in different positions (ie, seated vs supine). In a subset of participants with available biosamples, we also measured serum BMP9 levels to determine the association between BMP9 levels and the development of TPBT in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia. We hypothesised that patients with COVID-19 pneumonia with worse hypoxaemia and markers of hyperdynamic circulation would exhibit the most TPBT and lowest serum BMP9 levels.

Methods

Participants and clinical data

Patients at two academic medical centres who were greater than 18 years old hospitalised with COVID-19 pneumonia as documented by positive SAR-CoV-2 PCR and bilateral infiltrates on chest imaging, requiring supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula, non-invasive positive pressure (NIPPV), high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) or mechanical ventilation were eligible. Exclusion criteria, assessed by review of electronic medical records, included known patent foramen ovale (PFO) or other known intracardiac shunt, known pulmonary arteriovenous malformations, chronic liver disease (Child-Pugh Class B), history of stroke or transient ischaemic attack, chronic lung disease requiring home oxygen, pregnancy or incarceration. Patient demographics, including age, biological sex and race and ethnicity, were collected from the electronic medical records, along with medical comorbidities. Participants were followed until hospital discharge or death, whichever occurred first. Lab values and imaging studies were extracted from the medical chart as close as possible to the TCD(s). Orthodeoxia was defined as oxygen saturation measured in the supine position that was higher by 2% or greater compared with the oxygen saturation measured in the sitting position. Reverse orthodeoxia was defined as oxygen saturation measured in the sitting position that was higher by 2% or greater compared with the oxygen saturation measured in the supine position.1214

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or report, or dissemination plans of our research.

TCD bubble studies

The protocol for TCDs was the same at both sites. Anterior circulation cerebral vessels were insonated using a 2 mHz probe (either the Lucid manual system or the Lucid NovaGuide robotic system; NovaSignal Corp) and the clearest signal was selected to proceed with the bubble study with preference given to the middle cerebral artery if able. The manual signals were obtained by one of two neurosonologists (AR or KR-K). Vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate and oxygen saturation, and ventilator settings and mechanics if on mechanical ventilation, were recorded at the time of TCD. Agitated bacteriostatic saline was injected through either a peripheral intravenous line in the upper extremity, central line in the internal jugular vein or through a pulmonary artery catheter. The chosen cerebral vessel was insonated during the injection and for 20 s after the completion of the injection. Three injections were completed, 3 min apart between each injection. Participants were studied in both supine and sitting positions at 45°. The participant was placed in the second position (seated if the initial position was supine, and supine if the initial position was seated), vital signs and ventilator settings re-recorded, and the TCD bubble study repeated with three injections. The first TCD study was performed at the time of enrolment and repeated approximately 7 and 14 days later if participants remained hospitalised (for a maximum total of three studies). The TCD bubble studies were not performed during active continuous renal replacement therapy. After all participants had been enrolled, TCD studies were read independently by two neurosonologists (AR and KR-K) who were blinded to participant data and to each other (figure 1). The number of microbubbles present in the first 20 s after each injection was independently counted; the presence of any bubbles was treated as a categorical variable (yes/no) for the analyses. A given TCD bubble study was considered positive if any of the six injections (three supine and three seated) was positive. A given TCD bubble study was considered negative if all of the six injections were negative.

Measurement of BMP9

A subset of participants had blood banked on admission to the hospital (Mount Sinai Hospital cohort) or on admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) (Brown University Health/Rhode Island Hospital cohort) as part of established biobanks (Mount Sinai Hospital IRB approval # STUDY-20-00341; Rhode Island Hospital IRB approval # 411616). Serum was isolated from peripheral blood which was collected in serum separator tubes in the Mount Sinai Hospital cohort, while plasma was collected in EDTA tubes in the Rhode Island Hospital cohort to measure circulating BMP9 levels. We matched enrolled participants by sex and age (within 5 years) to controls with stored samples in the biobanks of the respective institutions. Control serum samples from the Mount Sinai Hospital cohort were obtained from outpatients that had both negative SARS-CoV-2 PCR and negative SARS-CoV-2 antibodies. Control EDTA plasma samples for the Rhode Island Hospital cohort were obtained from patients admitted to the ICU with negative SARS-CoV-2 PCR. All measurements (patients and controls) of BMP9 in serum or plasma samples (diluted 1:4 in diluent in a final volume of 50 µL) were averaged from triplicate measurements using the Simple Plex Ella automated microfluidic immunoassay system (SPCKB-PS-000474, Bio-Techne), which were confirmed to reflect circulating BMP9 levels assayed by a traditional ELISA assay previously described, and to comparable levels of BMP9 in external control cohort samples obtained from serum or plasma as previously described.15

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS Software V.9.4 (SAS). Modelling was conducted using generalised linear mixed modelling, where observations (supine and sitting) were nested within patient and by site (Mount Sinai Hospital and Rhode Island Hospital, respectively) using the GLIMMIX procedure. The presence of TPBT was treated as a binary exposure. Agreement concerning the presence of TPBT was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa with the FREQ procedure. Alpha was established a priori at the 0.05 level and all interval estimates were calculated for 95% CI.

Results

91 participants (n=58, 64% from Mount Sinai Hospital and n=33, 36% from Rhode Island Hospital) were enrolled from September 2020 to February 2021, underwent at least one TCD session and were included in the analysis. Participants had a median age of 63 years (IQR, 54–73). 68 participants (75%) were male. At the time of the first TCD, 14 participants (15%) were receiving oxygen via nasal cannula, 41 participants (45%) were receiving oxygen via HFNC, 8 participants (9%) were receiving NIPPV, 28 participants (31%) were receiving mechanical ventilation and 2 participants (2%) were receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Predictors of TPBT at baseline

There was excellent agreement between neurosonologists in assessing the presence of TPBT (kappa 0.95 supine, 0.92 sitting). 33 participants (36%) demonstrated TPBT at baseline (the first TCD performed). There was no association detected between the presence of TPBT and sex (OR 0.84; p=0.72), body mass index (OR 0.97; p=0.19) or comorbidities, including hypertension (OR 0.60; p=0.23), coronary artery disease (OR 1.20; p=0.74), congestive heart failure (OR 0.26; p=0.06), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (OR 1.21; p=0.81), renal failure (OR 0.51; p=0.40) or malignancy (OR 1.26; p=0.79).

Presence of TPBT at baseline as a predictor of outcomes

The in-hospital death rate was 34% (31/91). Although the presence of TPBT alone was not associated with in-hospital death (OR 0.85; p=0.69), there was evidence that the presence of TPBT and increased heart rate together predicted in-hospital death (p=0.02). As illustrated in figure 2, for every one-unit increase in heart rate, the odds of death increased 11% (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.20, p=0.01) for those with TPBT; however, there was no evidence of this increase for those without TPBT (OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.05, p=0.76). There was no association observed between the presence of TPBT and the type of respiratory support at the time of first TCD (p=0.73). There was no evidence that the relationship between in-hospital death and presence of TPBT differed between those who were intubated and those who were not (OR 0.90; p=0.54).

Trajectory of TPBT over the course of COVID hospitalisation

38 participants (42%) had at least two TCD assessments and 13 participants (14%) had three TCD assessments. The proportion of participants with TPBT increased with serial TCDs. 36% had TPBT during the first TCD assessment, 55% had TPBT during the second TCD assessment and 85% had TPBT during the third TCD assessment (table 2). Of the 17 participants without TPBT at the second TCD assessment, 16 did not have TPBT detected at the first TCD assessment as well.

BMP9 Measurements

In the Mount Sinai Hospital cohort, 19 COVID-19 cases and 14 non-COVID-19 controls had BMP9 measurements completed. In the Rhode Island Hospital cohort, 15 COVID-19 cases and 13 non-COVID-19 controls had BMP9 measurements. The median circulating BMP9 level was 190 pg/mL for cases, which was significantly lower than for age-matched and sex-matched controls, which was 286 pg/mL (p=0.03; figure 3). Discussion

In this two-site prospective cohort of participants with COVID-19 pneumonia requiring variable degrees of respiratory support, we noted three major findings. First, we observed a high rate of TPBT for participants admitted to the hospital with COVID-19 pneumonia (36%) at baseline, and for those that had repeat measures available, progressively higher rates of TPBT were observed (85% of participants at the third TCD assessment). Second, in participants found to have TPBT at the first TCD assessment, increased heart rate was associated with increased in-hospital mortality, a relationship not seen in those without TPBT nor tachycardia alone. And third, serum BMP9 levels were lower for participants with COVID-19 compared with non-COVID-19 matched controls, though serum BMP9 levels did not predict the presence of TPBT at the first TCD assessment.

Microbubbles from the injection of agitated saline are normally filtered by the lung as the microbubble diameter is larger than the normal pulmonary capillary.16 17 Therefore, detection of microbubbles by TCD suggests pulmonary vasodilatation or arteriovenous conduits; a lack of hypoxic vasoconstriction is not sufficient. Although we cannot rule out intracardiac shunt as a cause of observed microbubbles for some of the participants, the prevalence of PFO in patients with classical ARDS has been reported to be low (roughly 14%–19% vs 36% rate of TPBT detection here).1820 While ideally PFO would have been excluded as part of study procedures/echocardiography, we did exclude patients with a history of stroke as these patients have been shown to have higher rates of PFO.21 Notably, 7 of the 11 patients who had pulmonary artery catheters in place demonstrated TPBT when the agitated saline was injected directly into the pulmonary artery (thus bypassing any potential PFO), indicating detected microbubbles were TPBT. We also demonstrated the presence of orthodeoxia (32%) and reverse orthodeoxia (23%) in the subgroup of participants that were able to complete sitting and supine TCD studies, manifestations more likely to be associated with TPBT than intracardiac shunt.

The presence of TPBT in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia is supported by radiologic and autopsy findings published by other groups. Dual-energy CT of patients with COVID-19 pneumonia has evidence of pulmonary vessel dilation.22 Ackermann et al noted pulmonary capillary deformation and intussusceptive angiogenesis in COVID-19 autopsy specimens and the extent of intussusceptive angiogenesis correlated with hospitalisation duration.3 In follow-up studies, prominent intrapulmonary bronchopulmonary anastomoses have been detected at autopsy.4 5 These observations mirror our findings, in that the prevalence of TPBT increased over the course of hospitalisation, although we failed to detect an association between the degree of respiratory support and the presence of TPBT.

In our pilot study of 18 patients COVID-19 ARDS, we reported a TPBT prevalence of 83%.6 The patient population differed from our current study in that all the patients in the pilot study required mechanical ventilation (vs 31% in the current study). Additionally, the median hospitalisation day for performing the TCD in the pilot study was 24 days vs 6 days for the TCD assessment in the current study. In the current study, the third TCD assessment (for participants who underwent three TCD assessments) occurred at a median hospitalisation day of 19 days, and 85% of these participants demonstrated TPBT during this assessment, a value similar to that measured in the original pilot study. Notably, a recent study by Lau et al demonstrated a positive bubble study in only 17% of patients with COVID-19 ARDS patients receiving mechanical ventilation; however, these patients were studied early in the course of their disease (within 24 hours of initiation of mechanical ventilation).23 While our observations are subject to bias (we were only able to study participants longitudinally who remained in the hospital for 14 additional days; excluding those who died or were discharged), these results suggest that pulmonary vasodilation and/or arteriovenous conduits progressively develop over time during hospitalisation for COVID-19 pneumonia.

In our study, participants found to have TPBT at the first TCD assessment, increased heart rate was associated with increased in-hospital mortality. One explanation for this relationship is that TPBT could serve as a marker of endothelial dysfunction. Pulmonary vasodilation during SARS-CoV-2 infection is likely a sequela of endothelial dysfunction. It was appreciated early in the pandemic that COVID-19 causes significant endothelial dysfunction and pulmonary thrombosis.24 25 Autopsy series have demonstrated endothelial cell infection and endotheliitis,26 pulmonary microthrombosis3 and immune cell arteritis.27 Additional markers of endothelial dysfunction, including von Willebrand factor antigen, soluble thrombomodulin, angiopoietin-2 and soluble intercellular adhesion molecule-1, have been tied to the severity and course of COVID-ARDS and appear distinct from classical ARDS.28 29 In COVID-19 ARDS, markers of endothelial injury increase over time and are significantly associated with 28-day outcomes, relationships not seen with markers of epithelial injury.30 It is possible that patients with early TPBT may represent a specific pathophenotype, and increased heart rate may be a marker of more severe endothelial dysfunction, perhaps because of a need to increase cardiac output in the face of systemic vasodilation. To that end, we have previously demonstrated COVID-ARDS is characterised by pulmonary hypertension due to high cardiac output.10 Larger studies to better characterise the haemodynamics of patients with early COVID-19 are lacking.

The presence of pulmonary vasodilation and arteriovenous conduits may help explain the disconnect between gas exchange and both lung mechanics and radiographic findings seen in COVID-19 ARDS.1 Venous admixture is unrelated to the fraction of nonaerated lung tissue in COVID-19 ARDS, which does not hold in classical ARDS.2 Mathematical modelling that includes the presence of increased perfusion to injured lung from pulmonary vasodilation (deemed ‘reverse hypoxic vasoconstriction’) can explain disproportionate hypoxaemia observed in early COVID-19.31 The presence of pulmonary vasodilation and arteriovenous conduits may also explain the normal (or even low) pulmonary vascular resistance observed in COVID-19 ARDS,10 32 a finding in contrast to classical ARDS, where pulmonary vascular resistance is often increased.33 34

The detection of TPBT in our study may be analogous to HPS, a pulmonary vascular disorder of chronic liver disease characterised by pulmonary vascular dilations, hypoxaemia and high cardiac index.8 9 While we did not measure cardiac index directly in all participants in this study, we have previously demonstrated a hyperdynamic haemodynamic profile in COVID-ARDS and here we speculate that the relationship between TPBT, elevated heart rate and poor outcomes is a similar COVID subphenotype as in HPS. Orthodeoxia, a condition where PaO2 decreases from supine to upright by ≥4 mm Hg, is one of the hallmarks of HPS, with a prevalence ranging from 20% to 80%.13 14 35 Case reports have reported orthodeoxia in patients with COVID-19.36 37 To investigate the presence of orthodeoxia in our study, we used SpO2 as PaO2 was not systematically measured. A cut-off of 2% in SpO2 was used because in patients with ARDS, a PaO2 change of ≥4 mm Hg was equivalent to a SpO2 of ≥2%.12 In our study, 33% had orthodeoxia at the first TCD assessment. While the presence of TPBT seemed to increase over time, none of the eight patients with three TCD assessments demonstrated orthodeoxia, although with this small sample and attrition, it is difficult to draw conclusions about this disparate observation. Nonetheless, given at the third TCD assessment, 85% of participants demonstrated TBPT but none had orthodeoxia, the vascular changes at that time point are likely not preferentially occurring in the bases of the lungs, as they often do in HPS. Circulating BMP9 has been shown to strongly inhibit sprouting angiogenesis and likely plays a role in the control of blood vessel quiescence.38 As is seen in HPS,11 our study demonstrated lower serum BMP9 in COVID-19 participants compared with non-COVID-19 controls, a finding that has been reported before.39 However, serum BMP9 levels did not predict the presence of TPBT during the first TCD assessment and we did not have repeated blood samples from both sites to examine whether BMP9 levels fell as TPBT increased; therefore, the significance of reduced serum BMP9 levels in patients with COVID-19 is unclear.

Strengths of our study include a two-centre study, blinded assessments by two neurosonologists with excellent agreement, and repeat measurements on patients during their hospitalisation. Limitations of the study include sample size, lack of echocardiography to definitively rule out PFO as a cause of microbubble detection, and possible spectrum and selection bias, as noted above. Additionally, repeat TCD assessments were only done in patients who remained hospitalised and were not completed in patients who were discharged from the hospital. We used the same exact protocol at both institutions and re-read all studies in a blinded and independent fashion. We also included participants on variable degrees of respiratory support to address generalisability. We were not able to perform more sophisticated measures of gas exchange using multiple inert gas elimination technique given feasibility. It is unclear whether the location of the saline injection (peripheral intravenous line, central line or pulmonary artery catheter) affected the detection of TBPT. Finally, only a subset of participants had biosamples available for the measurement of BMP9.

Conclusions

COVID-19 pneumonia is associated with pulmonary vasodilation and/or arteriovenous conduits as detected by TCD. The rate of development of TPBT appears to increase over time for patients hospitalised with COVID-19. In patients with TPBT, increased heart rate is associated with increased mortality, suggesting compensatory high output state to overcome shunting. Serum BMP9 levels are lower for participants with COVID-19 compared with non-COVID-19 controls but do not predict the presence of TPBT.
