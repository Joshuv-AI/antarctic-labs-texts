Enhancing anomaly-based zero-day attack detection framework using CNN-driven feature extraction and OC-SVM
Authors: Sudhanshu Sekhar Tripathy, Bichitrananda Behera, D. Samuel Kollie, Ram Chandra Barik, Rahul Priyadarshi, Rakesh Ranjan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zero-day attacks exploit previously unknown vulnerabilities, posing a significant challenge for traditional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) that depend on known attack signatures. Detecting such threats requires models capable of identifying anomalous patterns in network traffic without prior knowledge. This study proposes a hybrid semi-supervised CNN + OC-SVM framework that integrates convolutional neural network (CNN)-based feature extraction with a One-Class Support Vector Machine (OC-SVM) for effective zero-day attack detection. A structured preprocessing pipeline, including feature selection, categorical encoding, and normalization, is employed to enhance data representation and discrimination capability. The proposed model is evaluated on two benchmark datasets, NSL-KDD and CIC-IDS2017, and compared with established unsupervised methods, including Isolation Forest, K-means, and DBSCAN, both with and without CNN-based feature extraction. Experimental results demonstrate that the CNN + OC-SVM model achieves an accuracy of 98.41% on NSL-KDD and 99.31% on CIC-IDS2017, outperforming baseline approaches. The model also attains precision, recall, and F1-score values exceeding 98%, indicating strong classification reliability across both attack and normal classes. These findings highlight the effectiveness of combining deep feature extraction with anomaly-based learning to improve detection accuracy and robustness against previously unseen cyber threats.

Introduction

Cybersecurity involves the application of advanced technologies, policies, and procedures to protect computer systems, networks, and data from a growing range of sophisticated cyber threats1,2. Its core objectives, maintaining confidentiality, integrity, and availability, are achieved by preventing unauthorized access, identifying vulnerabilities, and ensuring prompt response to security incidents3. Recent analyses indicate a significant rise in cyberattacks targeting critical infrastructure, with zero-day exploits representing a particularly severe threat4. These attacks exploit software vulnerabilities unknown to developers and unpatched, allowing adversaries to circumvent traditional defenses during the vulnerability window5. In today’s hyper-connected environment, zero-day exploits challenge security experts by exploiting undisclosed software vulnerabilities, allowing attackers to bypass defenses before patches are released6. Due to their stealthy and unpredictable nature, zero-day threats often evade conventional signature-based detection methods, leading to severe consequences including data breaches, financial losses, and operational disruption7.

According to the IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report 2024, the global average cost of a data breach has reached $4.88 million, representing a 10% increase compared to the previous year8. A major contributing factor behind this growth is the exponential rise of unpatched and previously undiscovered vulnerabilities that allow attackers to obtain unauthorized access without detection4. Zero-day vulnerabilities, in particular, allow adversaries to exploit unknown security flaws, facilitating prolonged system compromise while evading conventional security mechanisms that depend on predefined signatures or known threat patterns9. A recent example highlighting the severity of such threats occurred in March 2025, when Google disclosed a sophisticated cyber-espionage campaign exploiting a previously unknown vulnerability in the Chrome browser (CVE-2025-2783)10. The attack, termed Operation ForumTroll, successfully bypassed Chrome’s sandboxing protections and deployed malware through targeted phishing vectors. This campaign predominantly targeted high-risk groups, including journalists, academic researchers, and government agencies, emphasizing the critical need for proactive vulnerability detection and robust mitigation strategies.

Despite advances in cybersecurity, existing intrusion detection systems (IDS) still face major limitations in detecting novel and sophisticated threats, particularly zero-day attacks11. Traditional signature-based NIDS are ineffective against previously unseen attack patterns, while conventional anomaly-based systems often exhibit high false-positive rates, scalability issues12, and difficulty separating benign from malicious traffic. Furthermore, the increasing complexity and encryption of modern network traffic make detection even more challenging13. To address these limitations, this study proposes a hybrid (semi-supervised) framework that integrates CNN-based feature extraction with One-Class SVM for zero-day attack detection. The CNN module learns high-level spatial and temporal representations from network flows, and the OC-SVM identifies anomalies without requiring labeled attack data. The proposed CNN + OC-SVM model is systematically benchmarked against other unsupervised methods, including Isolation Forest, K-Means, and DBSCAN, both with and without CNN-derived features, using the NSL-KDD and CIC-IDS2017 datasets. This design enhances detection accuracy, reduces false positives, and improves flexibility compared to existing approaches.

Background

This section outlines the unsupervised learning approaches utilized for zero-day attack detection. Each algorithm applies distinct anomaly detection strategies by leveraging the structural and statistical properties of network traffic to identify deviations from normal behavior.

Zero-day intrusion detection systems

Zero-day intrusion detection represents a critical and escalating threat in cybersecurity, targeting previously undisclosed vulnerabilities before any security patches can be developed or deployed. The term “zero-day” reflects the lack of prior knowledge or response time available to defenders between vulnerability discovery and its exploitation14.

Traditional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS), especially signature-based models, are ineffective against zero-day attacks due to their reliance on known threat patterns15. Anomaly-based IDS offer better flexibility to novel threats but often produce high false positives and struggle to model normal behavior accurately, particularly with noisy or unrepresentative training data16. Recent advances in ML have enabled the development of intelligent IDS capable of detecting zero-day threats without labeled data17. Unsupervised techniques, including clustering, density-based methods, and outlier detection, have demonstrated strong potential in identifying anomalous traffic patterns associated with novel or stealthy attacks18.

As cyber threats become more advanced, it is essential to move from traditional signature-based IDS to resilient, behavior-based approaches19. By combining real-time anomaly detection, automated learning, and traffic behavior analysis, modern IDS can more effectively detect and respond to zero-day attacks, improving the security of critical systems and enterprise networks20. Benchmark datasets such as NSL-KDD and CIC-IDS2017 provide diverse, labeled network traffic samples that support the development and evaluation of Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) under both known and zero-day attack detections21. They cover a wide range of protocols, services, and contemporary attack vectors, facilitating the creation of robust and generalizable detection models22.

Unsupervised machine learning approaches for zero-day attack detection

One-class SVM model

The One-Class Support Vector Machine (OC-SVM) is a specialized unsupervised anomaly detection algorithm that learns the distribution of normal behavior from unlabeled data. Unlike traditional multi-class classifiers, OC-SVM constructs a decision boundary in the form of a hypersphere or hyperplane that encapsulates the majority of benign instances. During inference, any data point that falls outside this boundary is flagged as anomalous, indicating potential zero-day attacks or other security breaches23.

This one-class learning framework is highly effective in scenarios where malicious samples are unavailable or insufficient. By utilizing kernel functions such as the Radial Basis Function (RBF), OC-SVM maps input data into a higher-dimensional feature space, allowing the model to capture complex and non-linear patterns. This enhances its ability to distinguish subtle deviations from normal traffic behavior, thereby improving the detection of novel threats without relying on prior knowledge or labeled attack data.

Isolation forest

The Isolation Forest (iForest) is a fast and scalable unsupervised anomaly detection method, particularly well-suited for identifying outliers in high-dimensional datasets. Instead of modeling normal data, iForest isolates anomalies by constructing an ensemble of randomly generated binary trees, known as isolation trees (iTrees). Each tree isolates data points by recursively dividing the dataset using randomly chosen features and split values. Anomalies, being few and distinct, are typically isolated with fewer splits, resulting in shorter average path lengths compared to normal instances. This property enables iForest to detect zero-day attacks without relying on predefined signatures or prior knowledge. Its ability to identify outliers based on structural isolation rather than distance or density metrics makes it scalable and well-suited for real-time zero-day attack detection in complex network environments26.

K-means clustering

K-means is an unsupervised clustering method that organizes data into separate groups by assessing the closeness of data points, commonly using Euclidean distance as the similarity measure27. In zero-day attack detection, K-means is employed to model normal network behavior by identifying cluster centroids that represent typical traffic patterns. Data points that deviate significantly from these centroids are flagged as potential anomalies. This makes K-means a viable approach for detecting zero-day attacks, where the absence of prior knowledge necessitates the identification of novel and unexpected deviations in network activity9. DBSCAN

Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) is an unsupervised clustering algorithm that identifies dense regions of data points while labeling outliers in sparse areas as noise. Unlike centroid-based methods, DBSCAN does not require a predefined number of clusters and can discover arbitrarily shaped clusters28. In zero-day attack detection, it effectively models normal traffic by forming clusters of frequently occurring patterns, while low-density anomalies, often indicative of zero-day attacks, are isolated as noise. Related works

This section presents a focused review of recent advancements in NIDS, with particular emphasis on zero-day attack detection. The discussion centers on unsupervised machine learning techniques, which excel at identifying anomalous behaviors linked to previously unknown threats within dynamic and complex network environments. After the comprehensive survey of existing research reveals a notable gap, very few research work has been observed on the hybridization of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and One-Class Support Vector Machines (OC-SVM) within a semi-supervised framework for zero-day attack detection. Karim Ali et al. have suggested the combination of CNN and OC-SVM for effective anomaly detection29.

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) are software or hardware solutions developed to monitor computer networks or individual hosts for suspicious or malicious activities. IDSs are typically categorized into two main types: network-based IDS (NIDS) and host-based IDS (HIDS)30. NIDS analyzes network traffic and data exchanges between systems, whereas HIDS focuses on monitoring activities within a single host, such as system operations and log file analysis. IDS techniques are further distinguished by their detection methodologies. Signature-based IDS detects threats by comparing observed network behavior with a repository of known attack signatures, making them effective for previously identified threats31. In contrast, anomaly-based IDS identify deviations from established normal behavior, allowing them to detect complex or novel threats, including zero-day attacks.

Zero-day attack detection is hindered by the reliance of signature-based IDS on known patterns. Unsupervised algorithms like One-Class SVM and Isolation Forest overcome this by learning normal system behavior to identify anomalies without requiring labeled samples. One-Class SVM creates decision boundaries encapsulating legitimate data, whereas Isolation Forest isolates anomalies through recursive random partitioning. These approaches have demonstrated robust detection capabilities on datasets such as NSL-KDD and CIC-IDS2017, enabling efficient real-time identification of novel threats32. Almamy et al.9 proposed a framework designed to reduce the False Detection Rate (FDR) beyond standard hyper-parameter tuning in machine and deep learning models. Tested on an IBM industrial dataset and the NSL-KDD benchmark, the system detected an average of 71 anomalous flows per target. It achieved online learning accuracy rates of 98.4% on the IBM dataset and 96.6% on the NSL-KDD, demonstrating strong capability in identifying zero-day attacks.

Kumar and Sinha33 developed a cyber-attack detection model that addresses limitations in traditional ML and deep neural networks for zero-day attack detection. The system incorporates heavy-hitter analysis with graph-based methods to capture low-volume and subtle byte-stream anomalies often missed by conventional approaches. It follows a two-phase process: signature generation and evaluation. Experimental results on real-time data reported 91.33% accuracy for binary classification, 90.35% for multi-class, and 91.62% on the CIC-IDS2017 dataset, confirming the model’s efficacy in detecting unknown threats. Hanan et al.34 introduced an IDS framework leveraging autoencoders to mitigate the high false-negative rates associated with signature-based and traditional outlier detection methods. The model focused on enhancing recall while reducing missed detections. Evaluations on NSL-KDD and CIC-IDS2017 datasets, benchmarked against OC-SVM, demonstrated detection accuracies of 92.96% and 98.43%, respectively. The findings underscore the autoencoder’s capability to learn complex anomaly patterns and maintain a favorable balance between recall and false positives, supporting its potential for zero-day attack detection.

Perumal et al.21 investigated ML and DL methods for IDS with emphasis on zero-day attack detection. To address the shortcomings of signature-based systems, they implemented a unsupervised OC-SVM model aimed at improving recall and reducing false negatives. Experimental results on the CIC-IDS2017 and NSL-KDD datasets demonstrated that while OC-SVM effectively identified novel intrusions, autoencoder-based approaches achieved higher accuracies between 98.47% and 98.99%. The study highlighted the recall–false positive trade-off relevant to real-world deployments. Hairab et al.35 presented a distributed IDS framework leveraging deep neural networks in both supervised and unsupervised modes to detect zero-day attacks in interconnected environments, attaining a 94% detection rate on NSL-KDD, albeit constrained by limited training data. Sun et al.19 introduced a Bayesian model utilizing graph-based analysis to trace zero-day attack paths, demonstrating its potential through prototype validation. Duong and Hai36 employed unsupervised K-means clustering to eliminate outliers and retain benign instances, enhancing anomaly detection accuracy to 91% on NSL-KDD despite feature heterogeneity.

Jain et al. introduced an unsupervised detection framework designed to uncover zero-day threats by monitoring and identifying irregularities in network traffic patterns. Evaluated on real-world datasets, the model achieved high detection accuracy with minimal false positives, offering a viable alternative to supervised methods. The study emphasized key metrics such as precision, recall, F1-score, and robustness and recommended future improvements including context-aware analysis, real-time detection capabilities, and ethical considerations for deployment at scale27,37. SakthiMurugan et al.38 presented a deep learning-driven solution for identifying an intrusion framework utilizing an autoencoder to identify zero-day anomalies. The model was evaluated against One-Class SVM using NSL-KDD and CIC-IDS2017 datasets, achieving detection accuracies of 94.96% and 95.21%, respectively. The results demonstrated the autoencoder’s effectiveness in capturing complex behavioral deviations, establishing its potential for reliable zero-day threat detection.

Carrera et al.39 introduced unsupervised models for zero-day attack detection by integrating deep autoencoders with anomaly detection methods, including Isolation Forest and Gaussian Mixture Models. They evaluated architectures such as DAGMM-EIF, DA-EIF, and MemAE and EIF on KDD99, NSL KDD, and CIC-IDS2017 datasets. MemAE-EIF achieved the highest accuracy, 91.73% on NSL-KDD and 74.25% on CIC-IDS2017, surpassing standard deep learning baselines. The study also utilized SHAP for feature attribution, improving interpretability. Despite a slight increase in inference time from the EIF layer, the models remained suitable for near real-time applications, demonstrating strong potential for detecting unknown threats. These findings emphasize the importance of unsupervised hybridization in addressing the limitations of traditional classifiers when confronted with zero-day anomalies in evolving cyber environments.

Methodology

This section outlines the proposed methodology for zero-day attack detection and the data used to evaluate the effectiveness of the developed model.

Design and implementation approach of CNN-based zero-day attack detection

The proposed Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture is designed for effective zero-day attack detection by analyzing network traffic sequences. Figure 2 illustrates the CNN structure used in this work. The model accepts a pre-processed input containing selected critical network features, which is then passed through multiple one-dimensional convolutional (Conv1D) layers. Each layer employs several filters to extract salient spatial-temporal patterns from the traffic data. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function is applied after each convolution to enable the learning of complex, non-linear feature representations, thereby improving the model’s capacity to accurately distinguish anomalous from normal network behavior.

To improve processing efficiency while retaining salient feature information, the MaxPooling1D layer performs temporal downsampling on the feature maps, effectively reducing their dimensionality. These reduced representations are then flattened into a one-dimensional vector to ensure compatibility with the downstream fully connected layers for final classification. Dropout layers are incorporated after dense layers to prevent overfitting and improve generalization. These dense layers further refine the feature representation, yielding a compact and discriminative encoded vector suitable for final classification. The final component of the model is a final output layer employing a sigmoid activation function, facilitating binary classification of network traffic as either benign or indicative of a zero-day attack. This layered architecture effectively integrates feature extraction, representation learning, and classification, establishing the proposed CNN framework as a robust and efficient solution for zero-day attack detection in dynamic cybersecurity environments.

Proposed hybrid (semi-supervised) CNN + OC-SVM model for zero-day attack detection

This section presents the proposed model that integrates Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with a One-Class Support Vector Machine (OC-SVM) for zero-day attack detection in network traffic. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the model operates in three stages: data pre-processing, feature extraction using CNN, and zero-day attack detection using unsupervised OC-SVM. The procedure initiates with data pre-processing, which incorporates removing irrelevant columns, encoding categorical features like protocol type and service into numerical values, normalizing feature scales to ensure uniformity across all input dimensions, and encoding labels to effectively discriminate between normal network traffic and anomalous attack patterns indicative of zero-day exploits.

Hyper-parameter tuning

In this experimental configuration, hyper-parameter optimization is essential for improving model flexibility and stability in the hybrid (semi-supervised) CNN and one-class SVM architecture for zero-day attack detection. In the first stage, the model’s performance is influenced by carefully chosen CNN hyper-parameters, including batch size, dropout rate, and L2 regularization (λ = 0.1), which are fine-tuned to balance model complexity and performance. A dropout rate of 0.5 is applied to mitigate overfitting by randomly deactivating neurons during training. To further regulate training, an early stopping mechanism is employed with a maximum of 100 epochs; to prevent overfitting and avoid excessive training cycles, the process is configured to stop if there is no gain in validation accuracy over 30 consecutive epochs. The model is optimized using the Adam algorithm along with binary cross-entropy as the loss function, promoting fast and stable learning. These optimization techniques greatly improve the model’s ability to learn dynamic representations from network traffic, enhancing its effectiveness in identifying previously unrecognized intrusion attempts. Subsequently, the configuration of parameters was applied to OC-SVM, Isolation Forest, K-means Clustering, and DBSCAN algorithms.

At the second stage of the workflow, to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the OC-SVM model for zero-day attack detection, an extensive range of parameter configurations was systematically explored. The model was assessed using four kernel functions: Radial Basis Function (RBF), Linear, Sigmoid, and Polynomial, across various gamma and ν (nu) values. For the RBF kernel, gamma was set to fixed values (0.001, 0.002) and reactive options (“scale,” “auto”), with ν values ranging from 0.0125 to 0.3. The linear kernel was tested with gamma values of 0.001, 0.002, “scale,” and “auto,” combined with ν values between 0.0125 and 0.3. The sigmoid kernel configurations included both fixed and reactive gamma values, with ν ranging from 0.0125 to 0.2. Similarly, the polynomial kernel is evaluated using gamma values from 0.001 to “scale” and ν between 0.05 and 0.3. In total, over 35 unique kernel gamma ν configurations were assessed, providing a well-rounded analysis and supporting robust model performance in diverse attack scenarios.

Subsequent to the OC-SVM analysis, the Isolation Forest algorithm was employed to assess its efficacy in detecting zero-day attacks. A detailed evaluation was conducted using a diverse range of hyper-parameter settings. Specifically, the number of base estimators (n_estimators) was varied across 100, 200, and 300 to examine the ensemble’s depth and stability. The max_samples parameter, which controls the subset of training data used for each tree, was set to 0.5, 0.4, and 0.3 to evaluate its effect on anomaly sensitivity. Furthermore, the max_features parameter, which defines the proportion of input features used in constructing each tree, was configured at 0.7 and 0.5 to understand its impact on model diversity. To simulate different levels of anomaly prevalence, the contamination rate was varied at 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1. This configuration matrix, comprising nine distinct combinations, facilitated a comprehensive and balanced performance evaluation of the Isolation Forest model in identifying previously unseen network threats.

Following the evaluation of OC-SVM and Isolation Forest, K-means clustering was utilized to investigate its performance in unsupervised zero-day attack detection. The model was configured with multiple parameter settings to ensure comprehensive coverage. The number of clusters (n_clusters) was varied across 2, 3, 4, and 5 to assess how different cluster granularity levels impact anomaly grouping. Initialization strategies included both k-means + + and random, with n_init values set to 10, 15, and 20 to evaluate convergence stability. The maximum number of iterations (max_iter) was fixed at 300 to allow sufficient convergence time for each run. Additionally, both the lloyd and elkan algorithms were applied to compare standard and optimized clustering approaches. This configuration space enabled the analysis of six distinct K-means settings, ensuring a balanced assessment of its effectiveness in distinguishing benign and anomalous traffic patterns under varied conditions.

Finally, the DBSCAN algorithm was employed to detect zero-day attacks by identifying clusters of anomalous behavior without requiring prior knowledge of cluster quantity. A range of hyper-parameter configurations was explored to evaluate its sensitivity and clustering performance. The eps parameter, which defines the neighborhood radius for density estimation, was varied from 0.3 to 0.7 to capture local data densities. The min_samples parameter, indicating the minimum number of points required to form a dense region, ranged from 4 to 7. Multiple distance metrics were tested, including Euclidean, Manhattan, and Minkowski, to assess their influence on neighborhood construction. Additionally, the leaf_size parameter, controlling the tree-based index structure, was adjusted between 30 and 45 to optimize performance across different metrics. These six configurations allowed DBSCAN to be evaluated comprehensively, enabling insights into its robustness for identifying anomalies in diverse network traffic distributions.

Conclusion and future scope

In this research, a hybrid (semi-supervised) CNN + OC-SVM model is proposed for zero-day attack detection, incorporating a modified CNN architecture to enhance feature extraction compared to traditional CNN approaches. A comprehensive performance comparison was conducted among various unsupervised learning approaches such as Isolation Forest, K-means Clustering, and DBSCAN, both with and without CNN-based feature extraction. Experimental results demonstrate that integrating convolutional features significantly improves OC-SVM’s detection accuracy. Among all evaluated techniques, the CNN + OC-SVM model achieved the highest performance in detecting zero-day attacks. To further enhance generalization and detection capability, systematic hyper-parameter tuning was applied. Key parameters within the OC-SVM model, as well as algorithm-specific configuration settings for other methods, were carefully optimized. This tuning strategy improved the models’ ability to learn from complex data and detect previously unseen attacks. Overall, this research contributes to cybersecurity by presenting an effective framework for detecting zero-day attacks. The approach leverages semi-supervised learning with enhanced CNN-based feature extraction to improve detection accuracy and strengthen digital system security against emerging cyber threats. Future studies could explore additional hybrid (semi-supervised) models to enhance performance and improve detection accuracy for zero-day attack detection. Developing ensemble approaches that leverage deep learning techniques to boost prediction accuracy is another promising direction. Evaluating these hybrid (semi-supervised) models on large-scale datasets from specialized network environments, such as IoT and critical infrastructure systems, will provide valuable insights into their flexibility and effectiveness for zero-day attack detection. In addition, future research will address these limitations by performing a detailed latency and feasibility study of the CNN feature extractor and OC-SVM inference pipeline under real-time conditions. This includes measuring per-packet and per-flow processing delays, evaluating throughput on GPU and CPU-based platforms, and exploring lightweight or compressed CNN architectures suitable for edge and IoT devices. We also plan to integrate hardware-aware optimizations, such as model pruning, quantization, and mini-batch streaming, to ensure low-latency detection and practical deployment in real-world network intrusion detection systems.
