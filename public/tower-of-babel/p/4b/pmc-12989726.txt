Dual-engineering metalloimmunotherapy mediates Staphylococcus aureus virulence silencing and biofilm immune microenvironment reprogramming against implant-associated infections
Authors: Feng Jiang, Qize Xuan, Yujie Hu, Zun Ren, Anqi Wei, Jiazhe Cai, Boyong Wang, Mingzhang Li, Jinlong Yu, Changming Wang, Yi Yang, Geyong Guo, Jin Tang, Pei Han, Hui Li, Xiaohua Chen, Chao Chen, Hao Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Implant-associated infections (IAIs) caused by Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) are notoriously recalcitrant to treatment due to the self-reinforcing interplay between bacterial virulence and a suppressive biofilm immune microenvironment (BIME). Here, we present a dual-engineering metalloimmunotherapy nanoplatform that synchronously silences bacterial virulence and reprograms host immunity to eradicate IAIs. The nanoplatform, termed HMPF, integrates a fenoprofen-loaded, polydopamine-modified hollow MnO2 core that is further cloaked with a macrophage-erythrocyte hybrid membrane, enabling bacteria-targeted delivery and hemolysin-responsive drug release. By inhibiting the SaeRS two-component system of S. aureus, HMPF suppresses virulence factor expression and disrupts biofilm structure, dismantling the physical barrier for immune cell infiltration. Simultaneously, Mn2+ release and mild photothermal stimulation activate the cGAS-STING and pattern recognition receptor pathways, reprogramming host BIME to enhance both innate and adaptive immune responses. Crucially, HMPF establishes pathogen-specific immune memory, which prevents infection recurrence, outperforming vancomycin in murine IAIs models. This pathogen-host dual-engineering strategy remains effective against other clinical S. aureus strains without inducing drug resistance, bridging virulence disarmament and immunomodulation to offer a transformative antibiotic alternative for resistant IAIs.

Introduction

Implant-associated infections (IAIs) are a primary and devastating complication arising after medical device implantation, frequently resulting in surgical failures, multiple revision procedures, and a compromised quality of life for patients [[1], [2], [3]]. The pathogenic process of IAIs involves a dynamic interplay between the invading pathogens and the host immunity. Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), as a highly virulent bacterium, is the major pathogen responsible for IAIs [4], and it secretes a variety of virulence factors that not only damage host tissues but also drive biofilm formation. This dense extracellular matrix physically blocks immune cell infiltration while fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment [5,6]. This dual barrier, including physically dense biofilm barrier, and chemically suppressive biofilm immune microenvironment (BIME), creates a self-reinforcing cycle that renders conventional therapies ineffective. Current clinical management of IAIs relies on aggressive surgical debridement and removal of infected implants combined with prolonged multiple antibiotic regimens [7]. Nevertheless, the pace of antibiotic development lags severely behind the rapid emergence of bacterial resistance, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and some strains have even evolved resistance to vancomycin (reckoned as the last antibiotics), leading to unsatisfactory therapeutic outcomes for IAIs and a high rate of infection recurrence [[8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive antibiotic-alternative therapy to silence S. aureus virulence and reprogram host BIME, offering a new strategy for clinical management of IAIs.

Recent advances in nanotechnology have introduced innovative antimicrobial strategies, containing photothermal [11], photodynamic [12], and sonodynamic therapies [13]. However, these approaches primarily focus on direct bacterial killing, overlooking the challenge of virulence-driven biofilm persistence. Anti-virulence therapy, which disarms pathogens by targeting virulence regulatory systems rather than viability to attenuate pathogenicity, represents a promising route against drug-resistant bacteria in the post-antibiotic era [14,15]. The virulence of S. aureus is primarily regulated by two-component systems (TCS), among which the SaeRS two-component system (SaeRS TCS) plays a vital role in controlling the expression of over 20 virulence factors associated with biofilm formation and tissue damage, making it an ideal target for the development of anti-virulence agents [16,17]. Our prior work identified fenoprofen (Fen), an FDA-approved nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, as a potent SaeRS inhibitor capable of silencing virulence and destabilizing biofilms in S. aureus [18]. Nonetheless, solely silencing the virulence of S. aureus and destroying physical biofilm structural barrier is insufficient, it is equally imperative to address the immunosuppressive barriers imposed by BIME. The dynamic interplay between bacterial pathogenicity and host immune paralysis necessitates a dual-pronged approach that not only disarms pathogens but also reverses immune suppression by activating both innate and adaptive immunity.

Nanotechnology-driven immunotherapies have shown potential in cancer and infectious diseases by modulating antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and inflammatory pathways [19,20]. Yet in IAIs, the suppressive BIME impairs the maturation and antigen presentation process of APCs, and meanwhile promotes immunosuppressive cell populations, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) to release anti-inflammatory cytokines, further deteriorating immunosuppressive microenvironments [[21], [22], [23]]. While previous studies focused on the innate immunity regulation, for instance, macrophages [24] and neutrophils [25], the role of adaptive immunity, particularly immune memory in combating infections and providing long-term immune protection, remains underexplored. The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) signaling pathway senses intracellular DNA and triggers the production of interferons, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and chemokines, thereby bridging innate and adaptive immunity [26]. As a prominent focus of recent researches, it has been demonstrated to promote the expression of co-stimulatory molecules and enhance the cross-presentation capacity of APCs, leading to the activation of natural killer (NK) cells and the induction of T cell expansion [[27], [28], [29]]. Manganese ions (Mn2+) have been shown to bind to the active sites of cGAS, markedly increasing its DNA-binding affinity and further facilitating the activation of STING [[30], [31], [32]]. While Mn2+ ions have been explored as agonists of the cGAS-STING pathway in tumor therapies [[33], [34], [35]], their potential in biofilm-associated infections therapies, particularly in reprogramming BIME, remains untapped. Crucially, whether Mn2+-mediated activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway can establish pathogen-specific immune memory and further prevent infection recurrence remains unknown.

In this work, we propose a dual-engineering metalloimmunotherapy that synchronously silences bacterial virulence and reprograms host immunity to break the virulence-BIME interplay cycle in antibiotic-resistant S. aureus-induced IAIs. We engineered HMPF nanoparticles by encapsulating fenoprofen-loaded polydopamine (PDA)-coated hollow MnO2 within a macrophage-erythrocyte hybrid membrane (Scheme 1a). This design enables three synergistic actions: (i) Targeted virulence silencing. The hybrid membrane directs HMPF to infection sites, where hemolysin-responsive fenoprofen release inhibits SaeRS TCS to suppress virulence factors and disrupts biofilm integrity; (ii) BIME reprogramming. Mn2+ released from acidic biofilm microenvironments is expected to activate cGAS-STING and pattern recognition receptor (PRR) pathways, reversing BIME by enhancing APCs maturation, cytotoxic T/NK cell responses, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. (iii) Immune memory establishment. HMPF-treated mice are expected to develop pathogen-specific memory B/T cells and elevated immunoglobulins, providing durable protection against infection recurrence (Scheme 1b). The therapeutic efficacy and immunomodulatory mechanisms of HMPF will be validated based on various clinical S. aureus-triggered in vivo IAIs models and Sting−/− mice, and compared with the traditional antibiotics used in clinical practice. Results

Critical roles of bacterial virulence and host cGAS–STING signaling in IAIs

To investigate the relationship between S. aureus virulence and the pathogenic progression of IAIs, we analyzed 142 clinical isolates obtained from orthopedic patients with IAIs. Based on their hemolytic activity, the isolates were classified into high and low virulence groups (Fig. 1a). We observed a positive correlation between the virulence of S. aureus and serum procalcitonin (PCT) levels in infected patients (Fig. 1b), with PCT demonstrating superior performance in predicting infection control and recovery [36]. Survival analysis further showed that infection-free survival was lower in patients infected with high-virulence isolates than in those harboring low-virulence strains (Fig. 1c). Consistently, comparative X-ray and PET–CT imaging revealed more pronounced infection-induced osteolysis in patients infected with high-virulence S. aureus (Fig. 1d). The pathogenesis of IAIs involves a dynamic interplay between bacterial virulence and host immunity. Although the cGAS-STING pathway is well recognized for its role in antitumor immunity, its contribution to IAIs remains unclear. To address this, we established a knee periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) murine model using WT and ΔsaeRS strains of S. aureus in wild-type and Sting−/− mice (Fig. 1e and S1). Quantification of bacterial burden in implants and surrounding soft tissues revealed that ΔsaeRS strains were more readily cleared by the host, and Sting−/− mice exhibited impaired bacterial clearance capability compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 1f and g). Subsequent microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) and gait analysis demonstrated that Sting−/− mice infected with WT S. aureus showed the most severe infection-associated osteolysis and the worst walking ability (Fig. 1h, i and S2). We next established murine IAI models by infecting wild-type and Sting−/− mice with bioluminescent S. aureus strains (WT-lux and ΔsaeRS-lux) (Fig. 1j). Consistent with the findings from PJI model, these Sting−/− mice infected with WT S. aureus strains exhibited the highest bacterial burden and the most severe tissue necrosis (Fig. 1k–n and S3). These findings highlight the critical roles of S. aureus virulence and the cGAS–STING pathway in the pathogenesis of IAIs. To further explore the survival dynamics of high and low virulent S. aureus during IAIs in vivo, we established mixed-infection mouse models (WT-lux + ΔsaeRS and WT + ΔsaeRS-lux; 1:1 WT/ΔsaeRS inoculation), in which WT and ΔsaeRS strains were distinguished by bioluminescent labeling (Fig. 1o). Both mixed-infection models resulted in pronounced infectious outcomes (Fig. 1p–s); however, in vivo bioluminescence imaging revealed an obvious survival advantage of WT strains over ΔsaeRS strains (Fig. 1t). Quantitative analysis of strain proportions, together with blood agar imaging, further demonstrated that attenuation of S. aureus virulence markedly impaired its in vivo persistence (Fig. 1u–z). These results underscore the importance of bacterial virulence and host cGAS-STING pathway mediated immune activation in IAIs, identifying both as potential therapeutic targets for IAIs treatment.

Preparation and characterization of HMPF nanoparticles

To address above challenges, the hybrid membrane@HMnO2@PDA-Fen (HMPF) was prepared using an established co-extrusion method [37]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images confirmed the stepwise assembly process, revealing a spherical core-shell structure for the final HMPF nanoparticles (Fig. 2a). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) indicated that the membrane coating increased the average hydrodynamic diameter of nanoparticles from 148.0 ± 11.1 nm (MPF) to 180.7 ± 9.0 nm (HMPF), accompanied by a reduction in zeta potential negativity, demonstrating the successful membrane encapsulation (Fig. 2b). SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis verified the retention of key membrane proteins (e.g., CD11b for macrophages, CD47 for erythrocytes) on HMPF nanoparticles (Fig. 2c). Elemental mapping and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis validated the MnO2 core and hybrid membrane composition, with Mn existing in mixed valence states (Fig. 2d–f). We also evaluated the long-term in vitro stability of HMPF nanoparticles. The results showed that HMPF nanoparticles remained stable for up to 14 days in PBS, DMEM, DMEM+10%FBS, RPMI-1640 and RPMI-1640 + 10%FBS at pH 7.4 (Fig. S4). We quantified the drug loading capacity (DLC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) of MF, MPF, and HMPF nanoparticles. The DLC values were 14.62 ± 1.28%, 12.42 ± 1.13%, and 11.39 ± 1.14%, respectively, while the corresponding EE values were 78.29 ± 5.61%, 77.63 ± 6.22%, and 77.45 ± 5.89%. Drug release studies were then performed and demonstrated a pH-responsive behavior, in which HMPF released nearly 60% of fenoprofen within 48 h at pH 7.4, whereas uncoated MF released more than 80%, highlighting the role of hybrid membrane in preventing premature release (Fig. 2g). Under acidic conditions (pH 6.5, mimicking biofilm microenvironments), HMPF nanoparticles exhibited accelerated fenoprofen release (>80%) over 48 h (Fig. 2h), attributed to MnO2 core degradation and structural collapse, also evidenced by TEM images and DLS results (Fig. 2i and j). Concurrently, the time-dependent Mn2+ release profiles further support the acid-triggered degradation of the MnO2 core via proton-assisted reductive dissolution, in which low pH conditions facilitate Mn–O bond cleavage and accelerate the conversion of Mn(IV) to soluble Mn2+ (Fig. 2j–k). The PDA coating endowed HMPF with photothermal activity, achieving a maximum temperature of 45 °C (HMPF, 100 μg/mL) vs. 50 °C (MPF, 100 μg/mL) under NIR irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 15 min), with reduced efficiency attributed to membrane shielding (Fig. S5a). Such temperature is not only sufficient to induce mild photothermal destruction of the infecting pathogens, but also avoiding the damage to the surrounding healthy tissues [38]. Moreover, the photothermal performance of HMPF nanoparticles demonstrated both power- and concentration-dependent responses (Fig. S5b-d). We selected a power density of 1.0 W/cm2 for subsequent experiments, as this irradiation intensity has been widely reported to be biologically acceptable and does not induce noticeable tissue damage [39,40]. After several heating-cooling cycles, the photothermal performance of HMPF remained stable, with a calculated photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of 47.5% (Fig. 2l–n), underscoring its reliability. The cytotoxicity of HMPF nanoparticles on macrophages (Raw 264.7 cells) and fibroblasts (L929 cells) was then analyzed using CCK-8 method. Cytotoxicity results revealed that cell viability of HMPF nanoparticles was above 80% when the concentration ranged from 25 to 200 μg/mL (Fig. S6), with HMPF showing superior biocompatibility over MPF due to membrane shielding (Fig. 2o). In addition, no obvious cytotoxicity was observed in cells treated with nanoparticles under NIR irradiation (Fig. S7).

The pathogen-specific targeting ability will be helpful in the therapeutic precision and efficacy of antibacterial nanoparticles [41]. To this end, the S. aureus-targeting capability of HMPF was assessed both in vitro and in vivo. The red cell membrane@HMnO2@PDA-Fen (RMPF) was introduced as a special control. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed preferential adhesion of HMPF over red blood membrane-coated controls (RMPF) to S. aureus surfaces (Fig. S8). We then established a subcutaneous abscess model in mice to further evaluate the S. aureus-targeting capability of HMPF nanoparticles in vivo. Subcutaneous abscesses were induced by injecting PBS (left) or S. aureus (right) into the dorsal skin of mice, followed by tail vein injection of Cy5.5-labeled nanoparticles (Fig. S9a). In vivo imaging (IVIS) showed that HMPF nanoparticles accumulated at the S. aureus-infected abscess site within 6 h post-injection and reached the peak at 24 h, and persisted for up to 5 days. By contrast, MPF nanoparticles showed minimal accumulation and retention at the abscess sites and were rapidly cleared (Fig. S9b, d, e). The prolonged retention of HMPF nanoparticles at the infection site is attributed to the macrophage membrane coating, which preserves multiple pathogen recognition receptors (such as Toll-like receptors, TLRs) and adhesion-related molecules. These components enable specific recognition of and binding to PAMPs on the surface of S. aureus, thereby facilitating targeted adhesion of the nanoparticles to bacteria. Notably, ex-vivo imaging of major organs at 5 days post-injection revealed that the nanoparticles were primarily cleared through the liver and kidneys, and the fluorescence intensity of HMPF in the organs was significantly higher than that of MPF, indicating a longer in vivo circulation time for HMPF compared to MPF (Fig. S9c, f). Consistent with the above results, local injection evaluation also revealed that HMPF nanoparticles exhibited prolonged retention at the infected abscess site, whereas the fluorescence intensity of MPF at the abscess site decreased rapidly due to quick clearance through metabolic process (Fig. S10).

HMPF nanoparticles silence S. aureus virulence and disrupt the biofilm barrier

The antibacterial activity of HMPF nanoparticles was first evaluated in vitro. Without NIR irradiation, HMPF exhibited minimal bactericidal effects, whereas laser exposure (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2) reduced S. aureus survival to 71.72%, indicating mild photothermal lysis (Fig. S11a). Similar results were observed in the fluorescence images of bacterial live/dead staining (Fig. S11b). The bacterial membrane permeability was further investigated after the treatment with HMPF plus laser irradiation, and the results revealed that the levels of extracellular K+ and ATP increased significantly, indicating disruption of the bacterial membrane (Fig. S11c, d). This finding was also evidenced by the TEM and SEM images, in which HMPF nanoparticles adhered to the surface of S. aureus, and the bacterial membrane was damaged following mild photothermal treatment, ultimately resulting in the leakage of intracellular contents (Fig. S11e, f). Notably, sublethal photothermal stress upregulated heat shock proteins (HSPs) regulators (clpBC, dnaJK, grpE, and cstR) in S. aureus (Fig. S12). HSPs, as one of the typical bacterial PAMPs can be recognized by host pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) or interact with the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules on APCs, enhancing antigen presentation and activating adaptive immune responses [42]. These findings are consistent with our expectation that the mild photothermal effect of HMPF nanoparticles primarily serves as an immune-priming mechanism rather than a direct bactericidal modality. Next, the detailed interactions and action mechanism between HMPF nanoparticles and bacteria were explored. S. aureus secretes α-hemolysin, which binds to the ADAM10 protein on the erythrocyte membrane, assembling into a heptameric pore complex that disrupts the integrity of the erythrocyte membrane, leading to hemolysis [43]. The significant difference in hemolysis ability could be found between wild type and Δhla strains (Fig. 3a). To validate the toxin-responsive drug release, HMPF nanoparticles were co-incubated with purified α-hemolysin for 6 h in vitro. TEM images (Fig. 3b) showed intact hybrid membranes in control samples, whereas exposure to α-hemolysin led to pronounced membrane disruption and destabilization. Consistently, the addition of α-hemolysin resulted in a significantly accelerated release of fenoprofen from HMPF nanoparticles (Fig. 3c), indicating compromised membrane integrity. Additionally, the co-incubation of HMPF nanoparticles and the supernatant of WT S. aureus obtained similar results compared with Δhla S. aureus strain (Fig. 3d and e). To assess whether the HMPF-released fenoprofen suppresses virulence through SaeRS TCS regulation and further attenuates the pathogenicity, we quantified SaeRS-controlled virulence gene expression in S. aureus. After 24 h of co-incubation, treatment with HMPF, MF, and MPF nanoparticles significantly reduced the expression of key virulence factors regulated by the SaeRS TCS(Fig. 3f). Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) revealed that fenoprofen-loaded nanoparticles inhibited SaeR binding to the promoter regions of downstream genes (Fig. 3g). Leveraging a S. aureus strain with saeRS-GFP reporter [18], we further demonstrated that HMPF-released fenoprofen inhibited the function of the SaeR protein effectively, and silenced the virulence of S. aureus (Fig. 3h and i). Building on our prior demonstration of fenoprofen's biofilm-inhibitory effects [18], we investigated whether HMPF could retain this capability. The results showed that HMPF treatment significantly reduced biofilm formation by 49.2% and decreased the biofilm thickness from 35.5 ± 1.9 to 19.7 ± 2.8 μm (Fig. 3j and k). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images revealed that S. aureus biofilms exhibited a sparse and porous architecture after co-incubation with HMPF in both presence and absence of laser irradiation (Fig. S13a). Mechanistically, HMPF suppressed key biofilm matrix components, extracellular DNA (eDNA) decreased by 33.2%, and proteins by 60.1%, directly correlating with structural destabilization (Fig. 3l–q).

The biofilm of S. aureus in IAIs serves as a physical barrier, preventing the infiltration of host immune cells and facilitating immune evasion [44]. To investigate whether the disrupted biofilms due to nanoparticles treatment are vulnerable to infiltration and clearance by host immune cells, macrophages were introduced for further analysis. This structural destabilization of biofilms after HMPF treatment enhanced immune cells infiltration, the infiltration number and penetration depth of macrophage in HMPF group were 3.4-fold and 2.1-fold higher than those in the control group, respectively (Fig. 3r and S13b, c). Furthermore, time-survival curve demonstrated that intact biofilms could resist macrophage-mediated bacterial killing, while disrupted biofilms were more susceptible to macrophage clearance (Fig. S13d). These effects stem from HMPF's dual-responsive design: (i) pathogen-secreted hemolysins trigger pore formation in the hybrid membrane, releasing fenoprofen; (ii) acidic biofilm microenvironments degrade the MnO2 core to accelerate drug release. The liberated fenoprofen suppresses virulence via SaeRS inhibition while destabilizing biofilm architecture, ultimately exposing bacteria to immune clearance (Fig. 3s).

HMPF nanoparticles reprogram BIME by activating host immunity

The S. aureus biofilms in IAIs induce the establishment of an immunosuppressive BIME, which dualistically impairs host immunity: their dense architecture physically blocks immune cell infiltration, while secreted factors chemically suppress antimicrobial immune responses [21,44]. As demonstrated earlier, HMPF nanoparticles disrupted biofilm integrity (49.2% biomass reduction; Fig. 3k), dismantling the physical barrier and enabling 3.4-fold greater macrophage infiltration into infected regions (Fig. 3r). Concurrently, HMPF's mild photothermal effect (45 °C) induced bacterial membrane damage, triggering antigen leakage and upregulating PAMPs, including HSPs that prime host PRRs for immune activation (Figs. S11c-f and S12). These collective findings prompted us to investigate whether HMPF nanoparticles can act as potent activators of host immunity to reprogram the BIME. Based on the dual roles of Mn2+ as an immunological adjuvant and HMPF's pH-responsive degradation, we propose that acidic biofilm microenvironments trigger Mn2+ release, which synergizes with photothermally enhanced antigen exposure to promote dendritic cells (DCs) maturation and macrophages phagocytosis, thereby activating BIME and reverse immunosuppression through coordinated innate/adaptive immune activation.

Next, we assessed the effects of HMPF nanoparticles on bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). We observed a significant increase in the percentage of CD80+CD86+ cells (39.2% vs. 13.9% in control) and the highest expression of MHC II molecules in BMDCs, indicating that HMPF-treated BMDCs possessed the most effective antigen-presenting and immune-activating capabilities (Fig. 4j–l). Similarly, the gene expression and secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-12) significantly elevated in BMDCs in HMPF treatment group, while anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) decreased (Fig. 4m–o and S14c-e). Notably, HMPF induced a robust increase of IFN-β (HMPF vs. control: 456.2 vs. 123.8 pg/mL) and CXCL-10 (HMPF vs. control: 644.4 vs. 290.8 pg/mL) concentrations in the supernatants of HMPF-treated BMDCs, which are closely associated with the activation, recruitment, and chemotaxis of T cells and B cells (Fig. S14f, g). These findings confirmed that HMPF promotes the antigen-presentation process, contributing to the establishment of a microenvironment favorable for antigen presentation and immune responses.

To delineate the underlying transcriptional changes, we conducted an RNA-sequencing analysis of the BMDCs treated by the HMPF nanoparticles and S. aureus in vitro. Volcano plots and heatmaps revealed that the HMPF-treated BMDCs exhibited 379 upregulated and 87 downregulated genes compared to the control group (Fig. 4p and S15). Among the top 10 upregulated genes, Cxcl10, IFN-β, IL-1β, MHC II, and Rsad2 were identified, which are critical for the activation and chemotaxis of immune cells. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) demonstrated significant enrichment in immune-related signaling pathways, particularly the cytosolic DNA sensing pathway, antigen processing and presentation, and NF-κB signaling pathways (Fig. 4q). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on these pathways, further corroborated their activation in BMDCs after HMPF treatment (Fig. 4r and s). Gene ontology (GO) analysis also revealed the activation of immune-related biological processes, such as T cell activation and adaptive immune responses (Figs. S16 and S17a, b). More importantly, the upregulation of PRR pathways, including Toll-like receptor and NOD-like receptor signaling, in BMDCs was identified in the HMPF treatment group (Fig. 4q and S17c).

The cytosolic DNA sensing pathway plays a pivotal role in host immunity, wherein Mn2+ has been previously proved to enhance cGAS activity, thereby amplifying STING activation and potentiating the downstream interferon production. Consistent with RNA-sequencing findings, Western blot (WB) analysis demonstrated that HMPF treatment upregulated the key proteins, including p-STING, p-TBK1, p-IRF3 and p-p65, in both S. aureus-exposed BMDCs and Raw 264.7 macrophages, compared to untreated controls (Fig. 4t and u). To dissect the role of cGAS-STING signaling, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and BMDCs were isolated from wild-type (WT) and Sting−/− mice for flow cytometry analysis. The results showed a significant reduction in the percentage of M1 macrophages (CCR7+CD206- cells) in BMDMs and mature DCs (CD80+CD86+ cells) in BMDCs isolated from Sting−/− mice after co-culture treatments (Fig. 4v–x and S18), confirming STING-dependence of HMPF-mediated immune activation. Similarly, the BMDMs and BMDCs derived from Sting−/− mice exhibited decreased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-β and CXCL10, compared to cells isolated from wild-type mice (Fig. 4w–y and S19). To confirm the photothermal contributions, we compared HMPF treatment with or without NIR in BMDMs/BMDCs activation assays, with an additional 45 °C heat-treated group as the control. Relative to the control group, both 45 °C heating and HMPF (without NIR) enhanced the antigen-presenting capabilities of BMDMs (1.7-fold and 1.9-fold, respectively) and BMDCs (1.6-fold and 1.7-fold, respectively), HMPF with NIR treatment achieved a 2.9-fold enhancement in BMDMs and a 2.8-fold enhancement in BMDCs in WT mice (Fig. S20). Notably, HMPF + NIR retained partial efficacy (2.8-fold BMDMs and 2.3-fold BMDCs vs. HMPF without NIR) in Sting−/− mice (Fig. 4v–y), suggesting that these immunomodulation performances were partially derived from mild photothermal effects. These findings establish that HMPF synergizes Mn2+-driven cGAS-STING activation with photothermal-enhanced PRR signaling to together overcome the suppressive BIME.

Therapeutic efficacy of HMPF nanoparticles on primary murine IAIs model

To further assess the therapeutic efficacy of HMPF nanoparticles against IAIs in vivo, we developed a primary murine IAIs model using bioluminescent S. aureus, with in-situ injection of nanoparticles followed by NIR irradiation at the infection sites (Fig. 5a). NIR irradiation was applied for 15 min to ensure that the average temperature at the infection site reached 45 °C (Fig. S21). HMPF + NIR treatment significantly decreased the infection area compared to controls (Fig. 5b). Bioluminescence intensity decay curves confirmed progressive infection clearance, with HMPF + NIR achieving the most effective elimination of bacterial infections over 14 days (Fig. S22). Additionally, compared to the control group, HMPF + NIR reduced bacterial loads by 3.17 log10 CFU/g in implants (2.69 ± 0.55 vs. 5.86 ± 0.37) and by 3.61 log10 CFU/mL in peri-implant tissues (3.86 ± 0.47 vs. 7.47 ± 0.44), demonstrating its potent bacterial eradication capability (Fig. 5c, d and S23). Histopathological analysis of the surrounding soft tissues, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Giemsa staining, revealed the mild inflammatory exudation and tissue necrosis, along with reduced bacterial colonization in the HMPF-treated group (Fig. 5e). Both CLSM reconstruction and SEM imaging were further conducted to evaluate the biofilms on the implant surfaces, and the results revealed that HMPF + NIR significantly reduced biofilm thickness and bacterial coverage, effectively eradicating biofilm-protected S. aureus, whereas the biofilms on the implant surfaces remained intact and dense in the control group (Fig. 5f). More importantly, blood biochemistry and histology data confirmed the in vivo biosafety of HMPF, with no hepatic/renal toxicity and obvious tissues damage observed (Fig. S24). The superior therapeutic efficacy of HMPF nanoparticles correlated strongly with their immunomodulatory capacity based on the analysis of in vitro findings. To delineate immune reprogramming in vivo, we collected infection-associated draining lymph nodes (dLNs) and performed flow cytometry to comprehensively analyze immune reprogramming across multiple lineages at day 14 post-treatment (Fig. S25). As expected, HMPF + NIR increased the proportion of M1 macrophages (CD80+CD206−) by 2.9-fold (41.7% vs. 14.5% in controls) and mature DCs (CD80+CD86+) by 1.9-fold (39.2% vs. 20.5% in control) in dLNs (Fig. 5h and i). Immunofluorescence staining also revealed the highest population of CD80+CD86+ cells in the dLNs of the mice treated with HMPF plus NIR (Fig. 5g). MHC II expression on DCs also increased 2.1-fold in HMPF + NIR group, indicating enhanced antigen presentation capacity in vivo (Fig. 5j).

Macrophages and DCs, as the typical APCs, play a crucial role in capturing, processing, and presenting antigens, which is essential for T cell activation. In this context, we then analyzed the status of T cells-mediated cellular immunity in the dLNs and observed that HMPF + NIR expanded CD4+ (2.2-fold) and CD8+ (1.8-fold) T cell populations in dLNs (Fig. 5k–m). The proportion of natural killer (NK) cells (NK1.1+CD45+) also increased 2.4-fold (7.3% vs. 3.0% in control), enhancing direct antibacterial efficacy (Fig. 5n). Meanwhile, a significant reduction in the percentage of Treg cells (Foxp3+CD3+CD4+), which are known to suppress host immune responses and facilitate bacterial immune evasion, was observed in the dLNs of HMPF + NIR group (Fig. 5o). Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), known drivers of immunosuppressive microenvironments that perpetuate chronic infections of IAIs [45,46], were reduced by 47.7% (G-MDSCs) and 39.3% (PMN-MDSCs) in HMPF + NIR treated mice (Fig. 5p and q). From the perspective of humoral immunity, APCs-mediated B cell activation can drove plasma cell differentiation, which further produce specific antibodies that maintain the protective immunity against the invading pathogens. To this end, we further analyzed the percentage of plasma cells in the dLNs of mice at 14 days post-treatment. As shown in Fig. 5r, a significant increase (5.8-fold) in the proportion of plasma cells was observed in HMPF + NIR treatment, compared with the control group. Consistently, serum IgM and IgG levels increased 2.0-fold and 1.7-fold respectively, confirming the activation and enhancement of humoral immunity (Fig. 5s and t). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of peri-implant soft tissues was performed to evaluate immune cell infiltration. The Control group exhibited the highest level of neutrophil infiltration, indicating the most severe infection and tissue necrosis (Fig. S26a). In contrast, peri-implant soft tissues from the HMPF + NIR group showed increased infiltration of mature DCs as well as CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. S26b, c). Taken together, these results suggested that HMPF + NIR treatment remodeled the BIME by enhancing APC-mediated T/B cells immunity while suppressing inhibitory populations (Treg cells and MDSCs), ultimately establishing a comprehensive and robust antimicrobial immune response against biofilm infections in IAIs.

Based on prior findings of HMPF-induced immunomodulation in vitro, we further investigated the cGAS-STING pathway activation in vivo through phosphoprotein analysis. Western blot analysis demonstrated the increased phosphorylation levels of IRF3, TBK1, STING, and p65 in the peri-implant soft tissues of HMPF-treated mice, suggesting the activation of cGAS-STING pathway (Fig. 5u). To validate STING-dependence, we established IAIs in Sting−/− mice and administered HMPF + NIR treatment (Fig. 5v). The results showed that Sting−/− mice exhibited higher bacterial burdens of implants and peri-implants tissues, compared to WT mice (Fig. 5w, x and S27a). Comparative immune profiling revealed impaired APC maturation in Sting−/− mice, as evidenced by a 39.3% decrease in CD80+CD206- M1 macrophages and a 36.1% decrease in CD80+CD86+ mature DCs relative to WT mice (Fig. 5y and z). Furthermore, the activation effects of T cell- and B cell-mediated cellular and humoral immunity was attenuated in Sting−/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. S27b-r). These results mechanistically establish that HMPF requires intact cGAS-STING signaling to amplify antimicrobial immune responses.

In addition, to evaluate the importance of NIR irradiation for the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of HMPF nanoparticles, we compared the performance of HMPF with and without NIR irradiation in both the murine IAIs model and PJI model. In the IAIs model, mice treated with HMPF + NIR exhibited the lowest bacterial burden, the least tissue necrosis, and enhanced immune cell activity compared with those treated with HMPF alone (Fig. S31a-l). HMPF nanoparticles activate host immune memory against recurrent IAIs

The clinical management of S. aureus IAIs remains hindered by high recurrence rate, largely due to inadequate immune memory formation, a critical gap unaddressed by current nanotherapeutic strategies. To model clinical recurrence, we surgically replaced infected implants at day 30 post-primary infection and rechallenged mice with S. aureus, mimicking revision arthroplasty scenarios (Fig. 7a). We observed that HMPF + NIR prophylaxis reduced the area of recurrent infection, with bioluminescent signal intensity obviously lower than that of the control group (Fig. 7b and S32a, b). Quantification of CFU also showed the least bacterial load on implants and surrounding soft tissues in the HMPF + NIR group at 14 days after recurrent infection (Fig. 7c–f). Histopathological analysis further confirmed that HMPF minimized the inflammatory necrosis in the peri-implant soft tissues, along with minimal residual bacteria (Fig. 7g). Besides, both CLSM and SEM images revealed the presence of substantial biofilm and massive bacteria on the implant surfaces in control group. By contrast, the HMPF + NIR group exhibited almost thorough clearance of biofilm and internal bacteria, with only scattered bacterial microcolonies persisting (Fig. 7h). To further explore the underlying immune memory mechanisms, we quantified memory lymphocyte populations in dLNs of recurrent IAIs mice across treatment groups. Memory B cells (IgG+IgD−B220+), defined by their capacity for rapid pathogen-specific antibody recall immune responses, were first interrogated, and our results displayed that HMPF + NIR treatment expanded memory B cell frequency 4.0-fold (5.0 ± 0.6% vs. 1.2 ± 0.3% in control) in dLNs (Fig. 7i, j and S32d). This memory B cell expansion translated into functional humoral immunity, as evidenced by a 4.2-fold increase in serum IgG titers in the HMPF group compared to the control group (Fig. S32c), confirming the activation of B cell-mediated immune memory. Memory T cells, characterized by rapid antigen-specific reactivation, proinflammatory cytokine production and helper functions, were subsequently analyzed. Flow cytometric analysis of dLNs revealed HMPF significantly increased CD44highCD62L− effector memory T cells in both CD4+ (2.1-fold) and CD8+ (2.8-fold) subsets compared to controls (Fig. 7k–n and S32e, f), establishing robust cellular memory pools.

To elucidate the cGAS-STING dependency of immune memory formation, we challenged Sting−/− mice and WT mice with recurrent IAIs following HMPF + NIR treatment. Quantification of CFU revealed that Sting−/− mice treated with HMPF exhibited higher bacterial loads in recurrent infections than WT mice, suggesting the essential role of cGAS-STING pathway in the HMPF-mediated immune memory (Fig. S32g, h). Furthermore, flow cytometry revealed a lower percentage of memory B cells (IgG+IgD−B220+, 2.9% vs. 4.7%) and CD44highCD62L− memory T cells (29.7% vs. 40.5% of CD4+ subsets; 18.8% vs. 28.3% of CD4+ subsets) in Sting−/− mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 7o–q and S32j-l). Consistently, the Sting−/− mice exhibited lower serum IgG titers relative to WT controls (Fig. S32i). These findings demonstrated that HMPF effectively enhances host immune memory to combat recurrent IAIs, with the cGAS-STING pathway playing a critical role in this process.

HMPF nanoparticles are suitable for treating IAIs caused by drug-resistant S. aureus

Clinical S. aureus isolates exhibit genomic diversity that drives strain-specific virulence profiles and antibiotic resistance patterns. We picked 10 clinical S. aureus strains from patients with IAIs, including five MSSA and five MRSA strains, aiming to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of HMPF across genotypic variants. These clinical S. aureus strains were first co-incubated with HMPF, and the expression level of the hla gene was analyzed to evaluate the impact of HMPF on the virulence of clinical isolates. We observed a significant decrease in hla expression in these HMPF-treated clinical S. aureus isolates, both MSSA and MRSA, compared to controls (Fig. 8a), demonstrating broad-spectrum virulence suppression. Quantification of hemolysis ratio demonstrated a reduction in the hemolytic activity of clinical S. aureus strains treated with HMPF (Fig. 8b and S33). In addition, HMPF treatment reduced biofilm biomass across clinical MSSA and MRSA isolates, as quantified by crystal violet assays (Fig. 8c). Notably, CLSM 3D reconstruction images revealed HMPF-treated biofilms exhibited lower biomass density and increased porosity, structurally compromising their immune-evasion capacity (Fig. S34). Then, whether S. aureus could develop genetic mutations or resistance in response to HMPF-induced anti-virulence effects was further evaluated in vitro. A 6-week resistance assay was conducted, during which no mutations in the saeR sequence were observed, with qPCR confirming stable baseline expression of saeP and hla in S. aureus (Fig. 8d). To further benchmark HMPF against current standards, we compared its efficacy with vancomycin, a first-line antibiotic for MRSA infections, in murine IAIs models (Fig. 8e). Both vancomycin and HMPF demonstrated excellent therapeutic efficacy in primary IAIs mice model. In primary IAIs, both therapies achieved comparable peri-implant soft tissue bacterial clearance (vancomycin: 4.9 ± 0.6 vs. HMPF: 4.4 ± 0.6 log10 CFU/g) (Fig. 8f). However, HMPF outperformed vancomycin in eradicating implant-associated biofilms (vancomycin: 4.1 ± 0.5 vs. HMPF: 2.7 ± 0.5 log10 CFU/mL), highlighting its unique biofilm eradication capacity (Fig. 8g). Flow cytometry results of dLNs revealed that only the HMPF treatment group effectively activated robust host anti-infective immune responses, including innate immunity (NK cells) and adaptive immunity (T/B cells-mediated cellular and humoral immunity), while suppressed the expression of inhibitory immune cells populations (Treg cells and MDSCs) (Fig. 8h–o and S35). In recurrent IAIs, vancomycin failed to prevent relapse, including the infections of implants (5.2 ± 0.7 vs. HMPF: 2.1 ± 0.5 log10 CFU/mL) and peri-implants soft tissues (6.6 ± 0.6 vs. HMPF: 3.3 ± 0.7 log10 CFU/g) (Fig. 8p–r). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis of memory lymphocyte further demonstrated that the percentages of memory B cells and memory T cells in the dLNs of HMPF-treated mice were significantly higher than those in the vancomycin-treated and control groups, indicating the effective activation of immune memory responses (Fig. 8s–u and S36-38). These results suggested that while vancomycin shows potent bactericidal activity, its inability to eradicate biofilms or establish immune memory underscores HMPF's dual-action superiority.

Given the compelling efficacy data, we next assessed HMPF's activity against multidrug-resistant S. aureus (MDRSA), including MRSA strains (ATCC 43300 and USA300) and vancomycin-resistant (VRSA) strains (ATCC 700699) (Fig. S39). As shown in Fig. 8v, HMPF treatment significantly reduced SaeRS-regulated virulence genes expression by across all tested MDRSA strains. Whole-genome sequencing confirmed identical saeR nucleotide sequences (100% identity across 687 bp) among all strains, with conserved SaeR-DNA binding motifs, rationalizing the pan-strains efficacy (Fig. 8w and x). We further used three drug-resistant S. aureus strains to establish IAI mouse models and observed that HMPF significantly reduced bacterial loads in peri-implant soft tissues and effectively eradicated biofilms on implants in these MDRSA-infected mice (Fig. 8y and z). Collectively, these data demonstrate HMPF's unique ability to circumvent antibiotic resistance mechanisms through targeting evolutionarily conserved virulence pathways, providing a robust therapeutic alternative for recalcitrant IAIs. Compared to clinical antibiotics, HMPF nanoparticles offer advantages in addressing IAIs caused by drug-resistant S. aureus, highlighting its profound potential for clinical applications.

Discussion

The therapeutic management of IAIs is fundamentally constrained by two interconnected barriers, pathogen-derived virulence factors that drive biofilm formation and suppressive BIME that paralyzes host immunity [22,23,48]. Current biomaterial strategies predominantly focus on single-target approaches, direct bactericidal activity or innate immune activation, which suffer from collateral tissue toxicity and high recurrence rates [49,50]. Our work establishes a novel pathogen-host dual engineering strategy, comprising concurrent virulence silencing via SaeRS TCS inhibition and BIME reprogramming through cGAS-STING/PRR pathways activation, which represents a paradigm shift in combating IAIs and preventing infection recurrence.

For this purpose, we engineered HMPF nanoparticles to load fenoprofen (SaeRS inhibitor) and Mn2+ within a pathogen-responsive hybrid membrane, achieving spatiotemporal control over virulence silencing and BIME reprogramming. By targeting the evolutionarily-conserved SaeRS TCS, HMPF attenuated virulence factor production and degraded biofilm structural components, rendering biofilms permeable to immune infiltration. Synergistic Mn2+ release and sublethal photothermal stimulation (45 °C) co-activated convergent cGAS-STING and PRR signaling, driving APCs maturation and establishing pathogen-specific adaptive immunity and immune memory for the effective treatments of primary and recurrent IAIs. In addition, the nanoparticles underwent in vivo degradation and were primarily cleared through hepatic and renal pathways, indicating a favorable biosafety profile. Notably, HMPF maintained efficacy against 10 clinically-derived MRSA/MSSA strains over 6-week exposure without resistance development, and outperformed vancomycin in biofilm eradication and relapse prevention in three MDRSA-induced IAIs models, positioning it as a resistance-refractory therapeutic for recalcitrant IAIs.

S. aureus employs 16 kinds of TCSs to regulate virulence, with agrAC and saeRS emerging as master controllers of pathogenicity. While previously-reported nanotherapeutics targeting agr quorum sensing can suppress biofilm formation [51], clinical isolates from orthopedic IAIs frequently harbor agr-defective mutants that paradoxically enhance biofilm density and antibiotic resistance [44]. We previously demonstrated that identified agr-deficient mutants isolated from clinical cases of orthopedic S. aureus IAIs, which promoted the formation of dense biofilms and consequently enhanced bacterial resistance to antibiotics [52,53]. These evidences suggested that the agr system might not be a promising target in anti-virulence therapy of S. aureus-triggered IAIs. In contrast, the SaeRS TCS has been reported to regulate multiple virulence factors involved in biofilm formation, including fibronectin-binding proteins A/B (FnbpA/B) and clumping factors A/B (ClfA/B), while maintaining metabolic fitness essential for acute infection. Our previous work showed that knockout of saeRS significantly reduced the pathogenicity of S. aureus in IAIs, making it an ideal anti-virulence target [18]. Besides, previous studies have shown that the SaeRS TCS impairs macrophage functions at the early stage of infection by suppressing Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2)–mediated NF-κB activation [54]. Therefore, by inhibiting SaeRS-regulated virulence factors, HMPF alleviates S. aureus–mediated immune suppression, thereby enhancing immune recognition and host immune activation. Biofilm extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), comprising polysaccharides, proteins, and eDNA, create a diffusion-limited barrier that reduces antibiotic penetration and blocks the host immune cells infiltration [55]. Notably, our work revealed that the biofilms formed by HMPF-treated S. aureus exhibited a porous structure, losing the physical protective barrier function and becoming more vulnerable to immune cell infiltration and clearance. Our work establishes virulence pathway targeting, particularly of evolutionarily constrained systems like saeRS, as a transformative strategy for developing resistance-refractory antimicrobials.

Although cGAS-STING signaling is a well-established regulator of antitumor immunity, its spatiotemporal regulation in biofilm-infected microenvironments remains enigmatic, with limited evidence linking STING activation to biofilm clearance [56,57]. Our findings demonstrate that HMPF + NIR achieves dual therapeutic outcomes: (i) effective eradication of biofilm-embedded bacteria through SaeRS-mediated virulence silencing; (ii) BIME remodeling via cGAS-STING/PRR pathways co-activation, manifested as the activation enhancement of APCs, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and plasma cells, and decrease of the Treg cells and MDSCs populations in vivo. Notably, HMPF establishes pathogen-specific immunological memory, effectively preventing infection recurrence, and surpassing the efficacy of vancomycin. This aligns with emerging evidence that cGAS-STING activation induces memory T/B cell formation through IRF3-mediated epigenetic reprogramming [58]. Our study establishes the critical role of the cGAS-STING pathway in combating IAIs and preventing infection recurrence, while also highlighting the significant potential of manganese-based biomaterials in this process. Collectively, we delineate a Mn2+-enabled therapeutic paradigm where cGAS-STING activation synergizes with virulence reprogramming to disrupt the biofilm-immune evasion cycle, offering a blueprint for next-generation anti-infective biomaterials.

While our findings highlight the therapeutic promise of HMPF, several limitations merit discussion to guide future research and clinical translation. First, while virulence-targeting strategies inherently reduce resistance selection pressure, evidenced by maintained HMPF susceptibility 10 clinical isolates (MRSA and MSSA) over 6 weeks, extended monitoring across global epidemic clones is still required to exclude delayed resistance emergence prior to clinical translation. Second, while HMPF confers 6-week protection in murine models, the durability of immune memory requires validation in non-human primates, with longitudinal tracking of memory B and T cell frequencies and pathogen-specific antibody titers over longer periods.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we present a manganese-based pathogen-host dual-engineering nanotherapy (HMPF) for S. aureus-triggered IAIs, which coordinately silences S. aureus virulence through SaeRS TCS inhibition and reprograms BIME via cGAS-STING/PRR pathways co-activation, effectively breaking the self-reinforcing cycle of biofilm persistence and immune evasion. HMPF demonstrates pan-strain antibacterial efficacy against clinical isolates (including MSSA/MRSA and VRSA) with no detectable resistance development over 6 weeks, while surpassing vancomycin in biofilm clearance and recurrence prevention, positioning it as a transformative solution for recalcitrant IAIs. This study establishes a new paradigm in anti-infective nanomedicine by integrating virulence disarmament and host immunity reboot, with excellent implant sterilization rate in murine IAIs models, underscoring its clinical translation potential.

Materials

After euthanizing the mice, bone marrow cells were harvested and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and M-CSF (50 ng/mL, Invitrogen). On day 7, BMDMs were collected for in vitro analysis.

Bacterial strains

The bacterial strains used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 3. Clinical strain S. aureus ST8 (MRSA) were isolated from patients with IAIs and stored in our laboratory [60]. The hla mutants in S. aureus ST8 (Δhla), bioluminescent strain ST8-Lux (MRSA-Lux) and fluorescence-labeled S. aureus ST8 were constructed and stored in our laboratory. Clinical isolates of methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) (strain 73,547, strain 77,939, strain 82,237, strain 73,156, and strain 73,574) and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) (strain 84,822, strain 82,300, strain 85,116, strain 82,855, and strain 81,682) were obtained from the Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine [18]. Antibiotic-resistant S. aureus strain ATCC 43300 (MRSA) and ATCC 700699 (VRSA) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. Antibiotic-resistant S. aureus strain USA300 (MRSA) was kindly provided by Dr. B. Diep. All strains were stored at −80 °C in our laboratory. Before the experiment, a single colony of bacteria was selected and inoculated into tryptic soy broth (TSB, Haibo, Qingdao, China) or TSBg (TSB with 0.5% glucose, for biofilm experiments). The bacterial suspension was diluted to the appropriate concentration according to the experimental requirements.

Animals

Male C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks old) were purchased from JSJ Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Sting−/− C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old) were originally purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). Mice were grouped in cages containing 5 individuals per cage, each equipped with an individually ventilated system. Mice were kept under a 12-h light-dark cycle (08:00-20:00 light; 20:00-08:00 dark), with the room temperature maintained at 22 ± 1 °C and relative humidity maintained between 40 and 70%.

Preparation of HMPF nanoparticles

Hollow manganese dioxide nanoparticles (H-MnO2) were prepared through template-etching method. Briefly, solid silicon spheres (sSiO2) were first synthesized and pre-prepared KMnO4 solution was added drop by drop into the fully dispersed sSiO2 solution under ultrasonic condition. Then, the above solution was collected and centrifuged under 12,000 rpm/min for 30 min to obtain sSiO2 coated with manganese dioxide (MnO2@SiO2). Subsequently, MnO2@SiO2 nanoparticles were dispersed into 2 mol/L Na2CO3 solution and stirred at 60 °C for 12 h to etch sSiO2 core away to obtain hollow manganese dioxide (H-MnO2, termed as M) nanoparticles. Before the next modifications, these nanoparticles were washed three times using ultrapure water and ethanol respectively. Fenoprofen (Top Science, Shanghai, China, 99.29% purity) loading was achieved via mixing dispersed H-MnO2 and fenoprofen solution under ultrasound and agitation condition, to acquire the drug-carrying nanoparticles (HMnO2-Fen, termed as MF). Then, HMnO2-Fen (MF) was dispersed into dopamine solution (dissolved in pH 8.0 Tris-HCl solution) and stirred for another 4 h to obtain HMnO2@PDA-Fen (MPF). Finally, the hybrid cell membrane (HM) was prepared by macrophage cell membrane (MφM) and red blood cell membrane (RBCM) at the ratio of 1:1. HM and the PMF nanoparticles (PDA@HMnO2-F) were extruded for 20 times by reciprocating extruder, and the resulting nanoparticles named Hybrid membrane@HMnO2@PDA-Fen (HMPF).

Characterization of HMPF nanoparticles

The morphology and corresponding element mapping of the nanoparticles obtained at each step were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100F, Japan). Dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern, UK) was used to determine the hydrodynamic diameters and zeta potentials of different nanoparticles. The expression of membrane proteins in HMPF nanoparticles was analyzed by SDS-PAGE. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out to obtain the composition of HMPF nanoparticles. The release of fenoprofen from the nanoparticles was measured using a Calcium Ion Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The release of Mn ions was measured using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES; Thermo Scientific, USA). Photothermal performance of different kinds of nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF, and HMPF) was tested using 808 nm laser irradiation and recorded by the Fotric X Thermal Camera (Fotric 285, USA).

In vitro cytotoxicity assays

Macrophages (Raw 264.7) and fibroblasts (L-929) were used to assess the cytotoxicity of nanoparticles in vitro. Briefly, 100 μL of cell suspension (1 × 104 cells per well) was seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated for 12 h. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) and incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator. After 24 h, the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Cell viability in each group was assessed using a CCK-8 assay (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Furthermore, we used Cell Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to qualitatively assess the cytotoxicity of nanoparticles. The NPs-treatment cells were stained with Calcein and PI in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescence images of live and dead cells were captured using a confocal microscope (CLSM, Leica TCS SP8, Germany).

Hemolysis tests

1 mL of fresh blood was collected from the mice and immediately added to the anticoagulant vessels. The fresh blood was centrifuged at 2500 rpm/min 4 °C for 15 min and red blood cells were collected and re-suspended in 4 mL PBS buffer, and then washed with PBS buffer 5 times. 200 μL of the red blood cell suspension was mixed with various nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles, final concentration 100 μg/mL) in 1.5 mL EP tubes, and sterile PBS was added to adjust the total volume to 1000 μL. Next, nanoparticles and red blood cells were incubated at room temperature for 4 h. Of note, PBS buffer was used as a negative control while TritonX-100 was used as a positive control. After incubation, these mixtures were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was then taken and transferred to another new 96-well plate. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 541 nm with a microplate reader (BIO-TEK, ELX 800).

S. aureus-targeting capability characterization of HMPF

To evaluate the S. aureus-targeting properties of HMPF in vitro, different nanoparticles (MF, MPF, RMPF, and HMPF nanoparticles, the final concentration 100 μg/mL) were added to the S. aureus suspension (ST8 strain, MRSA, 1 × 107 CFU/mL) and co-cultured at 37 °C for 1 h. Next, the culture was centrifuged at 6500 rpm/min for 5 min, and the supernatant with non-adsorbed nanoparticles were discarded. The precipitation was washed three times with PBS, and then re-suspended using 1 mL sterile PBS. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM-7800F, Japan) was utilized to observe and record the in vitro S. aureus-targeting characterization of HMPF.

To evaluate the S. aureus-targeting properties of HMPF in vivo, Male BALB/C mice (6-8 weeks old) were divided into the MPF and HMPF groups. After anesthesia with 1% pentobarbital sodium, the back hair of the mice was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. 100 μL sterile PBS (left) or S. aureus suspension (right) (ST8 strain, MRSA, 1 × 107 CFU/mL) was injected subcutaneously on each side of the back to establish a mouse model of subcutaneous abscess. For the tail vein injection experiment, the MPF group mice were injected with 100 μL Cy5.5-labeled MPF nanoparticles (100 μg/mL), and the HMPF group mice were injected with 100 μL Cy5.5-labeled HMPF nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) via the tail vein. At 6 h, 12 h, 1 day, 3 d, and 5 d post-injection, the distribution of nanoparticles in the mice was monitored using the IVIS system (Tanon ABL X6, Shanghai, China). On the day 5 post-injection, the major organs of the mice were collected to assess bioluminescent intensity. For the local injection experiment, 100 μL Cy5.5-labeled MPF nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) were injected subcutaneously on both sides of the back of the MPF group mice, while the HMPF group mice received 100 μL Cy5.5-labeled HMPF nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) in the same manner. The distribution of nanoparticles in the mice was monitored using the IVIS system at 0 h, 12 h, 1 d, 3 d, and 5 d post-injection. On day 5 post-injection, major organs were harvested for bioluminescent intensity evaluation.

In vitro antimicrobial efficacy assessments

The pre-prepared S. aureus suspension (ST8 strain, MRSA, 1 × 107 CFU/mL) was co-cultured with different nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles, final concentration 100 μg/mL) followed by 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min). Next, the S. aureus suspension from each group was collected, and the bacterial count in each sample was determined using the spread plate method (SPM). Besides, S. aureus treated with nanoparticles and NIR was collected and stained with SYTO9 and PI (Invitrogen, USA). Confocal microscopy (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) was used to observe live/dead fluorescence images of the bacterial samples in each group. The K+ and ATP concentrations in the supernatants of S. aureus treated with nanoparticles and NIR in each group were measured according to the manufacturer's instructions (Nanjing jiancheng, Nanjing, China). S. aureus treated with nanoparticles and NIR in each group was collected and fixed overnight in 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Ultrathin sections were prepared, and the membrane disruption of the bacteria in each group after treatment was observed using TEM (JEM-2100F, Japan).

Toxin-responsive characterization of HMPF

HMPF nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) were mixed and incubated with α-toxin (100 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) or the supernatant of S. aureus (Δhla strain and wild type strain). Samples from each group were collected at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, and 48 h to measure the fenoprofen content and analyze the effect of α-toxin on the drug release rate from HMPF nanoparticles. Additionally, after 6 h of co-incubation, HMPF nanoparticles from each group were collected, and TEM (JEM-2100F, Japan) was used to observe the morphological changes of HMPF nanoparticles.

Anti-virulence characterization of HMPF

The prepared S. aureus suspension (ST8 strain, MRSA, 1 × 107 CFU/mL) was co-incubated with different nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles, final concentration 100 μg/mL) and irradiated with 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. For qPCR analysis of S. aureus, bacteria from each group were collected, and RNA was extracted using Ezscript Reverse Transcription Kit (EZBiosciebce, USA). Then, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the SYBR PrimerScript RT-PCR kit (EZBiosciebce, USA), and qPCR was performed according to the amplification protocol. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 4. The gyrB gene was used as the reference gene, and the relative RNA levels of each gene were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

SaeRS GFP-reporting S. aureus strain was constructed in our laboratory. The 48-h extracts from different nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles) were collected and added to the preparation of chloramphenicol selection plates (CM10 plates, the concentration of chloramphenicol was 10 μg/mL). 100 μL GFP-reporting S. aureus suspension was spread on CM10 plates using the SPM method and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The fluorescence images of GFP-reporting S. aureus on the CM10 plates were captured using a bioluminescence system (Tanon ABL X6, Shanghai, China).

For flow cytometry analysis of GFP-reporting S. aureus, the GFP-reporting S. aureus suspensions (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was co-incubated with different nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles, final concentration 100 μg/mL) and exposed to NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, GFP-reporting S. aureus suspension from each group were collected, washed twice, and resuspended in fresh PBS, with the OD600 adjusted to 0.8. The GFP fluorescence intensity of the reporter strain was analyzed using the FITC channel of a Fortessa flow cytometer (Beckton Dickinson, USA).

Biofilm assays

S. aureus ST8 (MRSA) was prepared in TSBg to a concentration of 107 CFU/mL, followed by the addition of different nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles, final concentration 100 μg/mL) and NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min). The cultures were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to form biofilm. The supernatants were removed, and the biofilms were washed twice with sterile PBS. SYTO9 and PI were added for staining under dark conditions for 30 min. CLSM (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) was used to observe and perform 3D reconstruction of the biofilms in each group.

To measure the biofilm biomass, after co-incubation with different nanoparticles for 24 h, biofilms from each group were collected and washed three times with sterile PBS. 500 μL methanol was added to fix the biofilms for 15 min. After removing the methanol, the biofilms were washed three times with sterile PBS and stained with 500 μL of 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 min. The crystal violet solution was then removed, and the biofilms were washed three times with sterile PBS to remove excess dye. Subsequently, 500 μL of 33% acetic acid was added to dissolve the crystal violet in each group. 100 μL L solution from each group was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 590 nm to analyze the biofilm biomass in each group.

To observe macrophage infiltration into the biofilm, S. aureus was co-incubation with different nanoparticles for 24 h to form biofilms. Then, the supernatants were removed, and the biofilms were washed three times with sterile PBS and stained using Live/Dead BacLight bacteria viability kits (L13152, Invitrogen, USA). Macrophages Raw 264.7 were stained using CellTrace™ Violet Cell Proliferation Kit (C34571, Invitrogen, USA). 1 × 105 macrophages were added to each biofilm and co-incubated for 3 h to allow macrophage infiltration into the biofilms. After 3 h, the biofilms were gently washed three times with sterile PBS to remove non-infiltrating macrophages. CLSM was used to observe the number and depth of macrophage infiltration into the biofilms.

S. aureus-NPs-immune cells co-culture system

Pre-prepared S. aureus suspensions (ST8 strain, MRSA, 1 × 107 CFU/mL) were co-incubated with different nanoparticles (M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles, the final concentration 100 μg/mL) in vitro and exposed to NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min). The samples were then added to immune cell culture medium and co-incubated for an additional 24 h to establish the S. aureus-NPs-immune cells co-culture system. After 24 h of co-incubation, the cells were collected for subsequent experimental analysis.

For flow cytometry analysis of macrophage polarization, the treated macrophages of each group were collected and pretreated with anti-CD16/32 antibody to block Fc receptors. The cells were then stained with PE/Cy7-F4/80, FITC-CD11b, APC-CCR7 and PE-CD206 antibodies, and the proportion of M1 phenotype macrophages in each group was analyzed using a flow cytometer (Beckton Dickinson, USA). To measure the levels of inflammatory cytokines, the cell culture supernatants from each group were collected. The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and IL-6 were quantified using ELISA kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For flow cytometry analysis of BMDCs maturation, the treated BMDCs from each group were collected and stained with PE/Cy7-CD11c, FITC-MHC II, APC-CD80 and PE-CD86 antibodies. Flow cytometer (Beckton Dickinson, USA) was used to assess the maturation status of BMDCs in each group. Besides, mouse TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, IL-6, and IL-12p70 ELISA kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), as well as IFN-β and CXCL10 ELISA kits (R&D Systems), were used to measure cytokine levels in the supernatants from each group.

RNA-sequencing assays

The pre-prepared S. aureus suspension (ST8 strain, MRSA, 1 × 107 CFU/mL) was divided into two groups: the control group and the HMPF group. HMPF nanoparticles were added to the HMPF group (final concentration 100 μg/mL) and sterile PBS was added to the control group. Both groups were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by NIR laser irradiation for 15 min (1.0 W/cm2). BMDCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 106 cells per well. The treated S. aureus samples were then added to the BMDCs culture medium in each group and co-incubated for 24 h. After 24 h, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with sterile PBS. Subsequently, RNA was extracted from the BMDCs by adding 1 ml of Trizol (Invitrogen, USA) to each well and submitted to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology for RNA-sequencing analysis.

Western blot analysis

As previously described, S. aureus suspension (ST8 strain, MRSA, 1 × 107 CFU ml−1) was divided into two groups: the control group (treated with sterile PBS) and the HMPF group (treated with 100 μg/mL HMPF nanoparticles). Both groups were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by 15 min of NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2). BMDCs or Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (106 cells/well), and the treated S. aureus samples were added to the culture medium for co-incubation for 24 h. Then, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with sterile PBS. RIPA lysis buffer, protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China) were added to extract cellular proteins. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein quantification assay kit (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE based on their molecular weights and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked at room temperature for 1 h and washed three times with TBST (5 min each). Primary antibodies specific to the target proteins were incubated with the membranes overnight at 4 °C, including P-IRF3, IRF3, P-TBK1, TBK1, P-STING, STING, P-p65 and p65 (CST, USA). After three additional washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by three more washes with TBST. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). The antibody information is shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Primary implant-associated infections murine model

Healthy 8-week-old BALB/c mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium. The back hair of each mouse was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. A sterile round titanium implant (diameter: 10 mm, thickness: 1 mm) was surgically inserted subcutaneously on the back of each mouse, and the incision was sutured. S. aureus ST8-Lux (MRSA-Lux) was cultured in TSBg at a density of 1 × 107 CFU/mL. On day −2, 100 μL of the MRSA-Lux bacterial suspension was injected onto the surface of the titanium implant in mice to establish a primary implant-associated infections model. Then, the infected mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 8 biologically independent animals per group). On day 0, 100 μL of PBS, M, MF, MPF and HMPF nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) were subcutaneously injected at the S. aureus infection site of the implant in each mouse, followed by exposure to NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min). These infected mice with different treatment were maintained under appropriate conditions until day 14 to evaluate the treatment efficacy.

To assess infection progression and treatment efficacy of mice with IAIs in each group, the infected area of the mice was measured and quantified on days −2, 2, 6, 10, and 14. Besides, the bioluminescence intensity of S. aureus at the infected region was imaged and quantified using the IVIS system on days −2, 2, 6, 10, and 14. On day 14, the infected mice from each group were euthanized, the titanium implants and the surrounding soft tissues were harvested for analysis. The bacterial burden of the implants and the surrounding soft tissues were quantified by CFU counting. The implant surrounding soft tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for pathological analysis, including H&E staining, Giemsa staining and immunofluorescence staining of CD31 and α-SMA (abcam, USA) for infection assessment. The titanium implants were washed twice with sterile PBS, stained using Live/Dead BacLight bacteria viability kits and analyzed with CLSM to visualize and reconstruct the biofilms on the implant surface. Additionally, the titanium implants were fixed overnight in glutaraldehyde at 4 °C, dehydrated with ethanol (50, 70, 80, 90, 95, 99 and 100% v/v), and examined using SEM to observe the biofilm morphology on the implant surface.

To evaluate the antimicrobial immune response induced by HMPF nanoparticles treatment in vivo, the infected mice from each group were euthanized on day 14, and the infection-associated draining lymph nodes (dLNs) were immediately harvested. The dLNs were dissected into small pieces and gently ground using a 70 μm cell strainer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to prepare a single-cell suspension. Red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma, USA) was added to the single-cell suspension to lyse red blood cells. The single-cell suspension was then incubated with anti-CD16/32 for 10 min to block Fc receptors. Subsequently, different flow cytometry antibodies were added to the single-cell suspension of dLNs and incubated to analyze the proportions of immune cells in mice of each group: M1-phenotype macrophages (PE/Cy7-F4/80, FITC-CD11b, APC-CD80 and PE-CD206), mature DCs (PE/Cy7-CD11c, FITC-I-A/I-E, APC-CD80 and PE-CD86), CD4+ T cells (APC-CD3, FITC-CD4 and PE/Cy7-CD8), CD8+ T cells (APC-CD3, FITC-CD4 and PE/Cy7-CD8), Treg cells (APC-CD3, FITC-CD4, PE/Cy7-CD8 and BV421-Foxp3), NK cells (APC-CD45 and PE-NK1.1), plasma cells (APC-CD45, FITC-CD19 and PE-CD138), MDSC cells (FITC-CD11b, APC-Ly6C and PE-Ly6G). Additionally, the dLNs were fixed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for immunofluorescence staining of CD80 (Thermo Fisher, USA), CD86, CD4, and CD8 (abcam, USA). The antibody information is shown in Supplementary Table 1 and Table 2. Furthermore, on day 14, serum samples of infected mice were collected, and the levels of IgM and IgG were measured using ELISA kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

To verify the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway by HMPF nanoparticles in vivo, the soft tissues surrounding the implants were harvested from infected mice on day 5 and cut into small pieces. RIPA lysis buffer, protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China) were added, and the tissues were homogenized at high speed to prepare tissue lysates. The protein concentration of the lysates was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). Subsequent Western blotting procedures were performed as previously described, using primary antibodies against P-IRF3, IRF3, P-TBK1, TBK1, P-STING and STING (CST, USA). The antibody information is shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Periprosthetic joint infection murine model study

Healthy 8-week-old BALB/c mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium on day 2. The hair around the knee joint was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. An insulin needle was used as an implant and inserted into the tibia of the mice. Pre-prepared S. aureus ST8-Lux suspension cultured in TSBg (1 × 107 CFU/mL, 50 μL) was injected into the joint cavity of the mouse knee to establish a PJI model. The PJI mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 8 biologically independent animals per group). On day 0, 50 μL of PBS, M, MF, MPF, or HMPF nanoparticles were injected into the joint cavity, followed by NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min). On days −2, 2, 6, 10, and 14, the swelling degree and the circumference of the knee joints in each group of PJI mice were observed and measured. The bioluminescence intensity of the infected knee joints was assessed using the IVIS system. On day 14, the mice were euthanized, the implants and the surrounding infected tissues were collected for CFU counting to quantify the bacterial burden. To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of HMPF nanoparticles against S. aureus-induced osteolysis, X-ray imaging (XRD; Rigaku Ultima IV) was performed on PJI mice on day 28, followed by micro-CT scanning (Skyscan 1172, Bruker Micro-CT, Germany) and quantitative analysis of the tibiae.

To assess the walking ability of the PJI mice in each group, the VisuGait gait analysis system (Xin Run Tech, Shanghai, China) was employed. On day 14, mice from each group were placed on a runway and allowed to move from the starting point to the endpoint, while high-speed, high-definition cameras recorded and captured various walking-related data, including footprints, supporting time, stride length, intensity of the runway and average speed. The walking-related data were quantified and analyzed using the VisuGait software (Xin Run Tech, Shanghai, China) to assess the walking ability of the PJI mice.

Recurrent implant-associated infections murine model

Healthy 8-week-old BALB/C mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium. The back hair was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. A sterile round titanium implant (10 mm in diameter, 1 mm in thickness) was surgically inserted subcutaneously, and the incision was sutured. S. aureus ST8-Lux (MRSA-Lux) was cultured in TSBg at 1 × 107 CFU/mL. On day −30, 100 μL of the MRSA-Lux was injected onto the implant surface to establish the IAIs mice model. Then, these infected mice were randomly assigned to five treatment groups (n = 8 per group). On day −28, 100 μL of PBS, M, MF, MPF, or HMPF nanoparticles (100 μg/mL) were injected subcutaneously at the infection site, followed by NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 15 min). Mice were then maintained under standard conditions for 4 weeks until day 0. On day 0, the mice were anesthetized again, the old implant was removed, and a new implant was inserted with 100 μL MRSA-Lux bacterial suspension to establish the recurrent IAIs mice model. Infected mice were kept under appropriate conditions until day 14 to assess the efficacy of different treatments on recurrent implant-associated infections.

To evaluate the infection severity in mice with recurrent IAIs of different groups, the infected area on the back of each mouse was measured on days 0, 2, 6, 10, and 14. The bacterial bioluminescence intensity in the infected regions was quantified using the IVIS system. On day 14, the implants and surrounding soft tissues were harvested for bacterial CFU counting to quantify the bacterial burden in the infection site. The surrounding soft tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde and subjected to histopathological analysis, including H&E and Giemsa staining. The implants were stained using Live/Dead BacLight bacteria viability kits, and CLSM was used to observe and reconstruct the S. aureus biofilm on the implants surface. Additionally, the implants were fixed with glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 °C and SEM was employed to observe and capture images of the biofilms.

To assess immune memory in mice with recurrent IAIs in each group, the dLNs were collected on day 14 and processed into single-cell suspensions as described previously, followed by treatment with red blood cell lysis buffer to lyse red blood cells. After Fc receptor blocking with anti-CD16/32, different antibodies were added to the single-cell suspension for incubation, and flow cytometry was conducted to analyze the percentage of various immune memory cells in mice of each group: memory B cells (APC-B220, PE-IgD and FITC-IgG), CD4+ memory T cells (APC-CD3, FITC-CD4, PE/Cy7-CD8, PE-CD44 and BV421-CD62L) and CD8+ memory T cells (APC-CD3, FITC-CD4, PE/Cy7-CD8, PE-CD44 and BV421-CD62L). The antibody information is shown in Supplementary Table 1. Additionally, on day 14, serum from the infected mice was collected, and IgG levels were measured using the mouse IgG ELISA kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Resistance induction assays

Pre-prepared S. aureus ST8 suspension cultured in TSB (1 × 107 CFU/mL) and divided into two groups: control and HMPF groups. The control group was treated with PBS, while the HMPF group treated with HMPF nanoparticles (final concentration: 100 μg/mL) for 24 h. After that, 10 μL of HMPF-treatment S. aureus was added to 5 mL of TSB in two groups: one group received HMPF nanoparticles (final concentration: 100 μg/mL) and the other PBS, which were marked as HMPF-treated group and control group at day 2. This procedure was continued for 42 days, and the RNA of S. aureus was extracted on each day to detect the expression of SaeP and hla. The fold change in gene expression was calculated as the ratio between the HMPF-treated and control groups. Additionally, on days 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42, bacterial DNA was extracted, and PCR sequencing of the SaeR DNA fragment was performed to assess potential site mutations.

Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as mean ± s.d. All experiments in this study, including various quantitative analyses, TEM, SEM, Western blot, immunofluorescence staining, histopathological imaging, micro-CT, and X-ray analysis, were performed with at least three biological replicates. Two-tailed unpaired t-test was used for statistical comparisons between two groups. One-way and two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons tests were employed for multiple group comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
