Enablers and barriers for climate resilient health systems: Lessons from a qualitative study in Nepal
Authors: Venkat S. Reddy, Helen Ross, Yibeltal Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change poses escalating threats to health systems, particularly in low-resource settings like Nepal, where extreme weather events and chronic stressors challenge service delivery. Despite Nepal’s high vulnerability, little is known about the enablers and barriers that underpin its health system’s resilience to climate change.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with 26 key informants across Nepal’s health system, including government officials, researchers, provincial workers and non-governmental organization (NGO) representatives. The World Health Organization Operational Framework for Building Climate-Resilient and Low-Carbon Health Systems guided thematic analysis to identify "enablers" - that is, facilitating factors - and barriers.ResultsBarriers significantly outweighed enablers. Enablers were sparse and largely not climate-specific. Notable climate-specific enablers included improved early warning systems and a Ministry of Health climate change focal point. Barriers included policymakers’ limited climate literacy and prioritization; poor policy implementation; lack of managerial capacity and climate-health expertise; and inadequate infrastructure. Most enablers and barriers originated at governmental and health system levels.ConclusionThis study offers rare, empirically grounded insights into the factors that shape climate resilience of health systems in low-income settings such as Nepal, where climate change amplifies entrenched leadership, governance and capacity deficits. Strengthening everyday resilience, political will to tackle climate change and health challenges, and embedding climate considerations into health policy, workforce and infrastructure are critical. These are constructive insights not only for the federalizing Nepal health system but for global health systems confronting the dual challenges of climate change and systemic fragility, underscoring the urgent need for strong, clear leadership and governance.

Introduction

Climate change is already having myriad direct and indirect adverse impacts on human health, such as increasing the burden of vector-borne, waterborne, airborne, and foodborne diseases, nutrition-related diseases, heat-related conditions, injuries and mental illness [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. The burden of disease is shaped by acute (e.g., extreme weather events (EWEs)) and long-term climate forces with the former also damaging health system infrastructure [8]. Furthermore, climate change synergizes the adverse effects of other health determinants such as food insecurity and the cost of living (e.g., energy) [7].

To retain control over their overall structure and functions, maintain good health outcomes and safeguard human life, health systems must be resilient [6,[9], [10], [11], [12]]. That is, they must absorb shocks from climate change and other sources such as COVID-19 and adapt to them, or transform as required. It has been argued that “understanding health system resilience has never been more essential” [13]. And in low-resource settings, “the need for enhanced health system resilience (is) particularly urgent” due to the frequent occurrence of both natural and man-made shocks such as recent United States (US) policy changes to international development [14,15].

The challenges of climate change are amplified in low-resource settings [16,17]. Therefore, it is necessary to examine the enablers (that is, facilitating factors) and barriers to climate resilience of low-resource national health systems [6]. Identifying enablers informs the development of strategies to leverage existing strengths, while understanding barriers is essential for crafting policy responses that mitigate institutional and operational constraints [18].

While there is limited literature from middle-high income countries [19], evidence from low-resource settings is lacking. Studies of low-resource health system resilience to climate change have been limited to specific climate change-mediated diseases, natural disasters or EWEs, or to one level -- community or provincial, for example -- or specific segment of the health system, such as emergency departments [[20], [21], [22]]. Studies through an overall health system lens do not exist. Qualitative or mixed methods studies rather than quantitative research of health system resilience arguably capture a broader range of resilience-shaping factors, but the former are few [23,24]. Specifically, the lived experience of diverse stakeholders of the factors shaping health system resilience to climate change has not been examined for a low-resource country.

Nepal is a landlocked country in South Asia bordered by China and India. Its diverse geography comprises fertile plains, subalpine forested hills, and mountains, making it vulnerable to earthquakes and floods occuring nearly every year in the Himalayan river systems coursing through Nepal. The country suffers catastrophic monsoon floods and landslides with resultant disease outbreaks from inadequate sanitation, as occurred in 2017 [25]. According to the Columbia Climate School’s Climate Finance Vulnerability Index, Nepal occupies the bottom quartile of countries in their capacity to respond to climate change risks [26]. Nepal’s climate risks include disasters such as hastening glacial melting and more severe and unpredictable rains [27].

Economically, Nepal ranks 165th in the world for gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, making it a low-income country [28]. Nepal’s health system has underdeveloped infrastructure, a fragmented private sector, and poor retention of human resources -- particularly in rural areas -- leading to inequity in health services across demography and geography [27]. This results in poor delivery of health services, particularly in primary care [29].

Nepal moved from a central, unitary government to a three-tier federated system of governance in 2017, with goals to improve equity, access and efficiency across the spectrum of healthcare from acute to preventive services and health promotion. Against this backdrop, the COVID-19 pandemic added stress to the already weak health infrastructure, which was recovering from the two major shocks of the 2015 earthquake and 2017 floods [27]. In addition, ongoing and repeated shocks due to climate change perpetually test health system resilience.

Despite this, the factors shaping Nepal’s health system resilience to climate change have not been examined. To address this gap, this study asked the question: What are the enablers and barriers to climate resilience of the Nepal health system?

Materials and methods

Design

We conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews of key informants from the Nepal health system to identify enablers and barriers to building health system resilience to climate change.

Theoretical framework

The World Health Organization (WHO) Operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems [6] is a comprehensive, wide-ranging and multi-layered tool to help build climate resilience of health systems. It represents the health system as ten functional components (Fig. 1). Sampling and study participants

A combination of purposive and snowball sampling based on expertise, seniority of role, working knowledge of English, and experience enabled recruitment of 26 participants representing a diverse cross-section of the Nepal health system: Division of Health Services (D), Ministry of Health (M), non-governmental organizations (NGOs) (N), provinces (P), and researchers (R). Participants were emailed, then followed up by phone and messaging apps where necessary. All participants gave signed informed consent.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted face-to-face in English in February 2019. The semi-structured interview guide was pre-tested with Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) staff to ensure clarity and included the question: In your experience, what helps/hinders the building of resilience in the Nepal health system to climate change? The interviewer (VR) had no prior relationship with participants. No participants declined to participate. There were no dropouts from the study.

Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by paid Nepalese transcribers proficient in English. Each transcript was checked for accuracy against its original audio file, and discrepancies were resolved with the transcriber. Transcripts were anonymized, their identities known only to the lead investigator (VR).

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using NVivo 12 (QSR International, Doncaster, Australia). Themes were based on the ten components of the WHO framework [6]. Three additional themes were suggested by the raw data, producing a total of 13 themes (Appendix A). Thus, the WHO framework was the guiding framework, but we remained open to other themes suggested by participants [30]. Each theme was ascribed a code. Subsequently, sub-themes and concepts were identified.

Positionality

The interviewer and first author [VR] was medically trained at The University of Queensland. He has worked in Fiji, a low-and-middle income country (LMIC). He is of Indian ethnicity and shares aspects of culture and religion with participants. The second and third authors are, respectively, a European-Australian interdisciplinary social scientist and specialist in resilience, and an African-born global health systems specialist. Both are senior researchers with extensive experience in LMICs. No author had any position of power or influence with respect to Nepal’s health system. Managing for positionality was approached as part of the wider approach to deep interpretation and mitigating possible biases. VR maintained detailed reflexive field notes and a self-reflection journal daily. This was supported by regular debriefing with the other authors in order to offer a balanced interpretation and to mitigate bias throughout data collection, analysis and reporting.

Ethics: Ethics approvals were obtained from the Australian National University Human Research Ethics Committee (exempted from The University of Queensland Human Research Ethics Review) and the NHRC.

Enablers

Enablers are scant, spanning six of the ten components, with LGN providing the most. However, not all enablers are current and actual. Two enablers are historical –- the extensive network of local female volunteer health workers pre-federalization, and the good public health surveillance at district and national levels pre-federalization.; oOne is a future hope - that federalized local governments will become more responsive. The societal/natural enabler– natural assets promoting health - is serendipitous. Of the current and actual enablers, only the few examples discussed below apply specifically to climate change; the remainder apply to general preparedness for disasters including earthquakes, to which Nepal is prone.

There are no external enablers (i.e., external to Nepal). Governmental enablers typically require coordination across non-health sectors, e.g., meteorology. Improved health information systems saving lives during EWEs is a striking example:We know how much of that rain will be there in the upper part of the mountains. And [we know] the speed of the water going to the stream, and then [going] down to the plain. So, we can also calculate [the time] … it will take. So … the people … residing in the plain area … can be … evacuated. That's [why] the casualties in flood season are going down. ∼ M1, Policymaker

Barriers

The myriad barriers span eight of the ten framework components, with LGN followed by EMP accounting for the majority (Fig. 3, Appendix A). Most barriers originate in the health system and government, with few barriers at the societal and external levels.

Non-climate change-specific barriers

External. Two external barriers were identified: the bulk of health funding coming from external sources, and non-user-friendly (nonspecific or unclear) Western advice:So-called ‘consultant‘ put text like this way. But not explained what the real thing is. ∼ D1, Senior health administrator

So-called ‘consultant‘ put text like this way. But not explained what the real thing is. ∼ D1, Senior health administrator

Societal/ natural. Societal attitudes and priorities can result in resistance to official advice, as noted in comments below.If the people are resistant to be relocated [from a danger zone] then that also hinders....Community opinions and community cultural practices and sociological factors - that also can hinder sometimes. ∼ M2, Senior health administrator

If the people are resistant to be relocated [from a danger zone] then that also hinders.

...Community opinions and community cultural practices and sociological factors - that also can hinder sometimes. ∼ M2, Senior health administrator

Destruction of nature and a preoccupation with treatment services over the social and environmental determinants of health were also noted.

Government. Numerous barriers originate at the governmental level, predominantly in LGN and sustainable climate financing. Examples were: a lack of policy implementation and monitoring leading to policy failure; lack of clear federalized management structure; and key health system appointments being political, and not competence-based:One Minister comes, he changes the head of all major organizations … based on political bias … The competent person has been displaced by the political-biased person … ∼ P1, Provincial director

One Minister comes, he changes the head of all major organizations … based on political bias … The competent person has been displaced by the political-biased person … ∼ P1, Provincial director

Government funding priorities can adversely impact the health system, as articulated by a participant from a large non-government organization:This is a poor country, and … the priorities might be very different then. So, not much comes into health … the bulk of it will go towards … payment of salaries … etc. So then, to maintain infrastructure, to renew infrastructure, to buy equipment … the Government might be strapped. ∼ N1, Team leader – health emergencies

So, not much comes into health … the bulk of it will go towards … payment of salaries … etc. So then, to maintain infrastructure, to renew infrastructure, to buy equipment … the Government might be strapped. ∼ N1, Team leader – health emergencies

This suggests that barriers to climate resilient health systems at governmental and health system levels can overlap. This is also exemplified by the lack of value given to “resultoriented work … right from the top [government]” undermining service delivery, and the infrastructure examples discussed below.

Health system. Most barriers are situated within the health system, especially in LGN. These likely stem from a narrow vision manifesting as preoccupation with job tenure; focusing on a specific time of the year e.g., flood season rather than taking an overall view; and maintaining the status quo, “doing things … in the usual pattern ... [on an] ad hoc basis,” rather than pursuing evidence-based best practices or recent global developments. There is also a lack of will to drive change at all levels of the health system, possibly due to pervasive inertia, or a “poor attitude.” Consequently, there is lack of urgency and direction for policymakers and the health workforce, with policymakers’ public stances often at odds with their actual policy priorities.

This is accompanied by a lack of managerial capacity in planning, organization, implementation, monitoring and evaluation from the local to the system level. Furthermore, “[not] enough people … appreciate this [these deficiencies],” noted a participant from a key NGO based in Nepal.

The experienced leader of a provincial hospital encapsulates the impact of poor health system leadership on service delivery and functioning:We have seen … say if one District Health Office chief is good, then the District Health Office is functioning [well], and the result is good. The other year [when] leadership changes, the personnel are the same, but the result goes down. ∼ P1, Provincial director

We have seen … say if one District Health Office chief is good, then the District Health Office is functioning [well], and the result is good. The other year [when] leadership changes, the personnel are the same, but the result goes down. ∼ P1, Provincial director

After LGN, most health system barriers are experienced within EMP, notably in design processes and principles; location and construction of health facilities; choice of building materials; and non-compliance with existing construction standards. Equipment and access to water supply also were concerns. Disasters exacerbate these barriers, with some health facilities considered old and unsafe:Our health buildings are not safe… And even space is also a big issue during disaster. ∼ P2, Provincial director

Our health buildings are not safe… And even space is also a big issue during disaster. ∼ P2, Provincial director

These barriers pre-exist and are thrown into sharp relief by climate change. Few barriers apply specifically to climate change.

Climate change-specific barriers

Of the climate change-specific barriers, two are societal: (1) the challenge of reaching marginalzsed, climate-affected people in rural and urban areas and (2) the lack of public awareness about climate change and health. At the governmental level, the choice of building materials and the lack of mandated standards for climate-resilient and sustainable construction result in poor design and increased running costs:Now we all change to cement and concrete [from traditional materials] … So, in the summer we use too much fan [and] … AC. In winters we use lot of energy for heater. So, we are now paying that cost … ∼ N2, NGO director

Now we all change to cement and concrete [from traditional materials] … So, in the summer we use too much fan [and] … AC. In winters we use lot of energy for heater. So, we are now paying that cost … ∼ N2, NGO director

Poor design of healthcare facilities and the tendency to retro-fit rather than design for emergencies, such as floods, are also important EMP examples.

At the health system level, climate change-specific barriers lie within LGN and HWF. Numerous participants emphasized that health system leaders generally have limited awareness of the impacts of climate change on human health and have not made the link between EWEs and climate change, compounding the failure to learn from past disasters. Consequently, climate change and health is relegated to a lower policy priority. With regards to HWF, there is a lack of expertise on climate change and health at the individual and collective levels:If you asked me to name … a health expert from the climate change perspective, I can't give a name. ∼ N3, Climate change and health focal pointThe dedicated workforce working on the climate change and health … there are very few people. ∼ N3, Climate change and health focal point

If you asked me to name … a health expert from the climate change perspective, I can't give a name. ∼ N3, Climate change and health focal point

The dedicated workforce working on the climate change and health … there are very few people. ∼ N3, Climate change and health focal point

This produces gaps in knowledge and practice at the frontline. For example, those working at higher altitudes are inadequately trained to diagnose diseases whose prevalence has shifted due to climate change, such as malaria and kalazar.

Discussion

These data provide four key findings. Firstly, there are far more barriers than enablers to the development of climate-resilient health systems in Nepal. Secondly, LGN is the most significant driver of enablers and barriers, followed by Service Delivery, EMP and HWF. Thirdly, most enablers and barriers originate at the governmental and health system levels, with some overlap. Finally, few enablers and barriers occur within climate change concerns specifically: most current enablers are associated with general disaster preparedness, and most barriers are pre-existing.

Federalization is transmuting some barriers we identified into enablers. For example, local infrastructure, financing, information systems, equipment, delivery of services and essential medicines, and leadership and governance is gradually improving [31]. However, other barriers identified in our study remain, such as limited understanding of health by local governments; coordination with federal government; poor data quality; community leaders prioritizing curative services over public health; and health workforce shortages [31].

Significantly more shortcomings than assets were unearthed in the Nepal health system’s response to COVID-19, with the former including poor coordination between the layers of the federalized health system; lack of preparedness measures; poor implementation of guidelines; lack of trained frontline personnel; inadequate budget to upskill the health workforce; and inadequate supply of essential medical products [32]. That our data also indicated similar shortcomings across numerous health system building blocks suggests underlying systemic issues in the Nepal health system and government.

A large proportion of the enablers and barriers in our study were associated with EWEs and disasters. Pre-made disaster management plans, international support, and clear and decentralized response pathways [33] provide lessons for climate change-induced EWEs and could complement the enablers identified in our study. On the barriers side, two our findings -- (1) staff turnover and skills shortages in the field undermining organizational and health system intellectual capital and (2) well-meaning external agents not tailoring their input to local needs -- were mirrored by Lee’s examination of disaster management in Nepal [34]. In particular, the narrow vision among health system leaders found in our study reinforces Lee’s conclusion that “much of the focus seemed to be short term and on ‘disaster response’ with less attention paid to disaster mitigation and recovery” [34].

Our finding that learning from past shocks, if done at all, is ad hoc rather than system-wide and systematic, reflects similar conclusions in the contexts of disasters [34] and COVID-19 in Nepal [32]. In considering the health information systems building block, Lee’s data support our finding that past experience supersedes evidence-based best practices; that evidence is used to justify positions rather than inform choices; and that evidence is sometimes considered noise and therefore, eschewed [34].

Furthermore, Lee’s thoughtful examination of the barriers to the uptake of evidence-based disaster management in Nepal enables similar reflections on some of our other findings. For example, the paucity of evidence (in ISC, ISI, ISO, ISR) may be due to challenges in generating the evidence base; confusion on what constitutes evidence; and disagreement on best practices [34]. Our finding of a reluctance to be relocated to safer areas before or during disasters probably derives from a culture of “living with risk” rooted in socio-economic circumstances, lessened risk perception, and a degree of fatalism [34]. The latter may afflict policymakers and the health workforce, explaining why they become a “barrier to engagement” [34], as suggested by our data. The inertia we found may actually be an innate allegiance to “existing processes … and how the system is set up” [34]. To break the mold and create up-to-date, evidence-based, fit-for-purpose priorities, policies and processes requires strong leadership.

The lack of political will, clear prioritization, governance, vision, and awareness of leaders in response to the 2015 earthquake [33,35], other disasters [34], COVID-19 [32], and shocks such as fuel and power supply disruptions [35], corroborate a key finding of our study: that leadership and governance are critical to building health system response and resilience to shocks. For example, a “use and throw” attitude towards healthcare workers and unwillingness to collaborate across the health sector and with other sectors were concerning manifestations of leadership deficits in the COVID-19 setting [32]. This presages our findings of health workforce number and expertise shortfalls, the importance of cross-sectoral collaboration in the climate change context, and that most barriers overarch the government and health system.

As in our study, Lee identified leadership and governance deficits on numerous fronts, including “insufficient political ownership, commitment and policy direction”; agendas unaligned with the national agenda; political instability; political priorities elsewhere; and “inadequate government capacity” [34]. Lee’s recommendation that policy frameworks be founded on acts of parliament that are “fit for purpose” [34] is an important piece of the health system’s climate resilience puzzle missing from our data. The critical trait is what Lee calls “political ownership,” for it innervates and sustains sound policymaking, implementation, monitoring, and improvements, and drives “the framework or its enforcement on key aspects such as building regulations and codes” [34]. An important example from our study is the absence of mandatory building codes and a concomitant lack of climate-resilient and sustainable infrastructure. Our study underlines Lee’s “striking finding [of] the demand … for strong policy direction and leadership, as well as a supportive legislative framework that is implemented and enforced” [34].

We found that most current enablers underpin resilience to disasters in general, while most barriers are endemic, not confined to climate change or disasters. This is echoed by Rawat et al.’s recommendation that resilience to shocks is founded on a “strong, prepared, equitable, and well-resourced” health system [33]. Furthermore, Schwerdtle et al. suggest that “the activity that builds the most climate resilience is installing a water system or ensuring a reliable supply chain and energy supply” [36]. It is likely that latent barriers in EMP and other building blocks are exposed and amplified by climate change as they were in the COVID-19 context, leading Shrestha et al. to reflect, “The background deficit in HCWs suddenly appeared as a major foreground hindering” [32]. All this is unsurprising, for resilience to routine and chronic stress, or “everyday resilience,” is considered a precondition to resilience to shocks such as climate change-related events [32,35,37].

In this study, the combination of purposive and snowball recruitment generated participants with rich lived experience and well-considered reflections on resilience. They represented a cross section of different levels of the Nepal health system. All interviews were conducted in person at participants’ workplaces (except for provincial participants who traveled to Kathmandu) allowing observation of workflows in situ in real time. The study would have been enhanced by the inclusion of more high-level officers, frontline healthcare workers -- especially from local communities -- and representation from the private sector. However, access and time constraints proved prohibitive. The study was limited to participants with a working knowledge of English, so may have excluded some potential participants with lived experience. (English is commonly spoken in Nepal, particularly by those in administrative positions, though possibly less so among frontline workers and in rural areas.) Despite these limitations, deep and nuanced insights on enablers and barriers were discovered.

These issues may be addressed in a future study which could include comparisons of different groups -- frontline workers versus policymakers, for example -- to deepen the understanding of resilience at different levels of the health system. While this study considered each WHO framework component separately, in reality, they are interconnected. Further research could investigate how enablers and barriers interconnect in shaping climate resilience of a low-resource health system and, what strategies stakeholders suggest to consolidate enablers and address barriers. This study identified themes that fell outside the WHO framework, raising questions about the efficacy of the WHO framework as an analytical tool. Finally, it would be helpful to compare the Nepal experience with other LMICs. These are all potential areas for future research.

Conclusion

This study offers numerous novel contributions to the discourse on climate change and health system resilience in low-resource settings. First, it provides an empirically-grounded, experience-based, multi-level examination of underlying factors. This enables a holistic understanding of the Nepal health system’s resilience to climate change. Second, this study offers evidence-based guidance for Nepal’s ongoing health system reforms, such as federalization, thereby providing valuable lessons for other low-resource health systems considering reform. Crucially, our study emphasizes that everyday resilience and leadership and governance are the substrata on which health system resilience is founded [31,33–35]. Third, the use of the WHO Operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems as an analytical lens grounds our findings solidly in theory and enhances generalizability to other low-resource nations facing similar climate-related challenges to health systems. At time when climate change threats are heightened by sluggish, patchy or absent global efforts, such information is timely and important.
