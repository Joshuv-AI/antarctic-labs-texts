Investigating the performance of asphalt pavements modified with reclaimed asphalt and crumb rubber
Authors: Hamza Shams, Hanif Ullah, Yanjun Qiu, Hamid Abdrhman, Jianfeng Fu, Enhui Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research provides useful insights into sustainable and cost-effective pavement rehabilitation by evaluating the combined effects of both Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) and Crumb Rubber (CR) modification on flexible pavement performance using actual motorway sections. Pavement rehabilitation and maintenance can enhance the design and serviceable life of the pavement. Additionally, modification of asphalt with Crumb Rubber (CR) and Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) not only proves to be economical but can also increase the resistance of flexible pavement concerning rutting, fatigue, and moisture damage. Four different pavement sections were selected, which were rehabilitated and modified with Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP), Crumb Rubber (CR), and a combination of both, along the Islamabad-Lahore motorway (M-2), Pakistan. The first pavement section consists of Asphalt Concrete Wearing Course (ACWC) with 60/70 grade bitumen as a binder (RAP 0%, CR 0%), the second pavement section was a mixture of asphalt concrete with crumb rubber modified bitumen as a binder (RAP0%, CR7%), the third pavement section was a blend of 15% RAP with 60/70 grade bitumen as a binder (RAP15%, CR 0%), the fourth section was a mixture of 15% RAP and 7% crumb rubber modified bitumen (RAP 15%, CR 7%). Pavement cores were extracted from the selected four pavement sections, which were experimentally explored in the laboratory to find the impact on the performance of highway pavement employing Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) and Crumb Rubber (CR), partially replacing bituminous binder in the asphalt. The results show notable improvements in rutting resistance, tensile strength, and resilience. It was concluded that the performance of the section employing either RAP, CR or combined RAP and CR modified bitumen better enhanced the performance of pavement in terms of rut depth, indirect tensile strength and modulus of resilience. For instance, rutting depth was reduced by 41.35% and indirect tensile strength of pavement was increased by 17.93% by employing 15% RAP and 7% CR in modified bitumen binder for asphaltic mix. Introduction

Over the past few decades, reusing waste materials for infrastructure projects has become more popular1,2. Reusing RAP in new pavement construction is now common in many parts of the world because of its environmental and economic benefits3–6. Building and repairing roads require large amounts of non-renewable resources like asphalt binder4,7. Pakistan also has a large and growing road network. According to Pakistan’s economic survey of 2016–2017, the road network covers 265,000 km, mostly made of hot mix asphalt (HMA), with an asset value exceeding 2,500 billion. About 20% of the GDP is invested in highway infrastructure, making the road network one of the most affordable transportation options in Pakistan8. Despite this significant investment, many highway pavements fail earlier than expected, often due to fatigue, cracking, rutting, moisture damage, potholes, and other issues. The leading causes include poor initial pavement design, increased commercial vehicle traffic, and reliance on traditional mix design methods, which can lead to pavement reconstruction and significant financial losses for the country. To address these issues, various preventive and rehabilitative pavement techniques—not frequent reconstruction—have been adopted. These methods help developing countries like Pakistan extend pavement life and cut costs. Techniques such as overlaying flexible pavements and modifying virgin asphalt with Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) and Crumb Rubber (CR) are affordable, environmentally friendly, and effective at preventing further pavement damage.

Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) and Crumb Rubber (CR) have been used in asphalt paving since the mid-1970s9. Using them to overlay flexible pavement has proven to be both cost-effective and successful in improving rutting resistance, resilient modulus, and other common pavement issues like fatigue, cracking, moisture damage, and potholes10,11. Most research has focused on adding different amounts of RAP and crumb rubber to virgin asphalt binder to create HMA mixtures in laboratory settings. However, few studies have analyzed pavement cores taken from actual roads. Past research shows that HMA mixtures with RAP and crumb rubber as binders perform as well as or better than those with virgin asphalt12–14. After recognizing the potential benefits and impacts of RAP and crumb rubber, researchers began exploring various performance measures, including rutting, resilient modulus, fatigue, cracking, and moisture damage in modified asphalt mixtures15.

Moghaddam et al.16 and Farhan et al.8 studied that recycling HMA is an old idea recognized as far back as 1915, although its demand increased due to the oil embargo of the mid-1970s. Insufficient availability of suitable aggregates for specific applications and higher costs of asphalt have increased the demand for RAP, which also offers numerous environmental and economic benefits. Since the mid-1930s, approximately 10 million tons of RAP have been used, demonstrating comparable performance and significant cost savings compared to virgin HMA mixes4. Decker et al.9 and Ibrahim et al.17 have shown that adding crumb rubber improves the properties of pure bitumen. The wet process of crumb rubber modification can enhance rutting resistance, resilient modulus, and fatigue cracking resistance of asphalt mixes.

Daryaee et al.10 and Paje et al.18 studied pavement rehabilitation using a higher content of crumb rubber-modified binder. In their study, the authors examined the increased use of rubber modified by the wet process on the surface of HMA. Crumb rubber, making up 20% by weight of the asphalt binder, was added to a gap-graded mix, and they concluded that the noise emitted by tires is reduced when using a higher content of rubber-modified binder. Yousefi et al.11 and Solanki et al.19 explored the effect of high RAP content in hot mix asphalt (HMA) for base and surface courses. Four Superpave mixes with different RAP percentages—namely, 25% RAP and 40% RAP for the base course, and 0% RAP and 10% RAP for the surface course—were designed, built, and tested. The results indicated that increasing RAP content decreased rutting susceptibility but led to more moisture damage in both the base and surface courses.

Fakhri et al.12 and Al-Qadi et al.20 conducted research showing that increasing RAP content reduces rutting potential. Mohammed et al.13 and Kim et al.21 studied the rheology of crumb rubber-modified asphalt binder. The research results indicate that using crumb rubber as a modifier appears to enhance the penetration resistance of the asphalt binder because of the increased viscosity at 60 °C. They also suggested that the asphalt binder containing higher crumb rubber content shows better resistance to permanent deformation at higher temperatures and greater elasticity at lower temperatures. Xiao et al.22 investigated that adding crumb rubber improves the rutting resistance of the mixture regardless of rubber size and type. Crumb rubber also extends the fatigue life of the modified mixtures.

Al-Qadi et al.23 studied that RAP materials may offer stronger moisture resistance than virgin HMA because the aggregate is already covered and protected with a binder. They also evaluated that the rutting performance of asphalt mixes has been improved using RAP.

Shen et al.24 examined the laboratory-prepared crumb rubber modified RAP. Six mixtures were designed and tested—three recycled mixtures containing laboratory-prepared crumb rubber modified RAP and three control virgin mixtures. The recycled mixture used 15%. The results showed that it is feasible to incorporate 15% crumb rubber modified RAP into hot-mix asphalt using standard recycling methods, and there was no significant difference in the values of indirect tensile strength and resilient modulus between the virgin and recycled mixtures.

Although much research has been conducted on the use of RAP and crumb rubber in laboratory-prepared asphalt mixtures for flexible pavements, very little has been done on field-extracted pavement cores. In this study, pavement cores were taken from four selected sections of the M-2 Motorway in Pakistan, and pavement performance tests were conducted. Nowadays, innovative and modified rehabilitation techniques are used that are economical, effective, and capable of resisting all types of pavement distress for many years. In Pakistan, most of these advanced techniques were applied during the overlay of the Islamabad to Lahore Motorway (M-2). The asphalt mixture was modified with RAP, crumb rubber, and a combination of both at different percentages to enhance resistance to rutting, fatigue, moisture damage, and to improve the resilient modulus of ACWC.

Materials and methods

Four rehabilitated pavement sections along the Islamabad–Lahore Motorway (M-2) were the subject of the study; each section included varying amounts of Crumb Rubber (CR) and Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) in asphalt mixtures. As the control, Sect. 1 had RAP 0% and CR 0%; Sect. 2 had crumb rubber modified bitumen (RAP 0% and CR 7%); Sect. 3 had 15% RAP mixed with 60/70 grade bitumen (RAP 15% and CR 0%); and Sect. 4 had 15% RAP mixed with 7% crumb rubber modified bitumen (RAP 15% and CR 7%). To ascertain the modulus of resilience, indirect tensile strength, and rutting depth, pavement cores were taken out of each piece and examined in a lab using established protocols. The outcomes were examined to see how RAP, CR, and their combination affected the performance of flexible pavement. A detailed flowchart of the proposed methodology is depicted in Fig. 1.

Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP) consists of the waste asphalt aggregate mix that is being excavated or milled during the construction of an existing road to make a new road or during the re-carpeting process. RAP can be recycled not just for the asphalt but also for the aggregate that is to be mixed in the central mixing plant with virgin aggregate and new binding material is formed as recycled Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA)25. The purpose of using RAP in different road-related projects is to obtain a sustainable way to use materials that are advantageous for the environment and are also economical. According to National Asphalt Pavement Association (NAPA), U.S. produces almost 60 million tons of RAP that are recycled and utilized in new asphalt pavements. According to an estimated majority of the states in the U.S., utilize on average 10–20% of RAP in the mix design of Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA). According to the UK Specification for Highway Works, the maximum RAP material to be used in the production of the asphaltic wearing course is 10% and 50% in all other layers.

Crumb rubber

Waste tire rubber has been used, for decades, as an additive for constructing asphalt pavement. The use of crumb rubber in asphalt has been started in the 1960s when it becomes an interest of the paving industry due to its elastic properties which can improve skid resistance and durability of asphalt mixture. The addition of crumb rubber also enhances the stability and strength of the asphalt mix. The process of adding crumb rubber has been modified and innovated over the years and has become popular across the world. The growth of rubberized bitumen usage happened in the 1980s. Since then, the use of rubberized bitumen has been established in continents like Asia, North America, Europe, and Australia.

Crumb rubber is made of tires, which is formed by combining natural and synthetic rubber and carbon black27. The study used crumb rubber that came from a private manufacturer in Peshawar, Pakistan. The first step was to shred the discarded tires into smaller pieces to remove the fabric and wire reinforcements. As advised by the literature, the material was next sieved through a No. 8 to eliminate the larger sizes. The different sizes of crumb rubber particles made for recycling are shown in Fig. 3.

Core extractions

Four different sections were selected on Islamabad to Lahore Motorway M-2, Pakistan having virgin asphalt and modified with RAP, crumb rubber, and the combination of both at different percentages i.e. the first pavement section was asphalt concrete wearing course with 60/70 grade bitumen as a binder (RAP 0%, CR 0%), the second pavement section was asphalt concrete with crumb rubber modified bitumen as a binder (RAP 0%, CR 7%), the third pavement section was made of 15% RAP with 60/70 grade bitumen as a binder (RAP 15%, CR 0%), the fourth section was blended of 15% RAP and 7% crumb rubber (RAP 15%, CR 7%). Pavement cores were extracted from the selected pavement sections of M-2 with the collaboration of Frontier Works Organization (FWO), Thalian camp. Pavement cores were extracted and performance testing i.e. Hamburg Wheel Tracking Test (HWT), Indirect Tensile Strength Test (IDT), and Resilient Modulus (MR) Test were conducted on the extracted cores according to the standards defines by AASHTO (T 324 − 17)29 and ASTM (D7369-07, D7369-22)30,31 standards. In this research, the demolished and grinded pavement of the same motorway was utilized in the production of RAP in the rehabilitation of the Islamabad-Lahore Motorway (M-2). Detailed map of the pavement, where cores were extracted is shown in Fig. 4. Pavement performance testing was executed on the extracted pavement cores. Afterward, analysis of the result was done, and the four different pavement sections were compared concerning rutting, Indirect Tensile Strength, and Resilient Modulus.

Hamburg wheel tracking test (AASHTO T 324 − 17)

HWT is used to measure the rut depth against the number of passes. HWT is an electrical device that can move a steel wheel having 203.2 mm diameter and 47 mm of width over a test specimen. The test was executed with reference (AASHTO T 324 − 17) to investigate the mixing of RAP into virgin HMA mixtures for one type of sieved RAP The load on the steel wheel is 158 + 0.1 lb and the normal contact stress delivered by the contact of the wheel is approximately 0.73 MPa with a contact area around 971 mm2. The speed of the wheel was set to 60 ppm (passes per minute). The numbers of passes were adjusted to 20,000 as specified for the determination of the rutting potential of the asphalt mixture. The wet mode of the wheel tracker device was selected for the determination of rutting potential at 60 °C temperature of the water. The stirrer was used to attain a uniform temperature of 60 °C at every point.

Indirect tensile strength test (ASTM D6931-17)

This test was performed according to ASTM standards30. The tensile strength of the HMA sample was calculated with the help of this test. According to the standards, temperature of 25 °C was used for conducting the test. The specimens were placed in the lower loading strip and slowly lower the top-loading strip to bring it into light contact with the test specimen. The loading rate of 50 + 5 mm was applied. The IDT strength was calculated as follows:

The tensile strength of each specimen is calculated by the equation.

St = 2000 × P / Π × t× D.

Where:

St = IDT strength, KPa.

P = Maximum Load, N.

T = Specified height immediately before testing, mm.

D = Specimen diameter, mm.

Resilient modulus test (ASTM D7369-11)

Resilient Modulus test was performed after the determination of indirect tensile strength test according to ASTM standard31. Only 20% of indirect tensile strength is used as a peak loading force as an input parameter during the test. The reason for applying 20% load is due to the non- elastic behavior of mixture as they would experience permanent deformation when a greater load is applied. To avoid this failure the applied load is always kept smaller than the failure load of the specimen and it will make the deformation almost recoverable. A resilient modulus test was performed on the specimen having a 4-inch diameter and 1.5-inch thickness and was carried out at a temperature of 25 °C. Results and discussions

The results obtained from the Hamburg wheel tracker test, indirect tensile strength test, and resilient modulus test were compiled to investigate the effect of Reclaimed Asphalt Concrete (RAP), Crumb Rubber (CR), and the combination of both at different percentages on rutting and resilient modulus of asphalt mixtures. The screened data was further analyzed to find the effectiveness of RAP, crumb rubber, and both as an anti-rutting agent to reduce permanent deformation and increase the resilient modulus of Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA) pavement as discussed later in detail.

The improved performance of the mixture stems from mechanisms involving crumb rubber (CR) and Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement (RAP). Adding CR boosts the binder’s elasticity by creating a polymer-like network that helps it recover under strain. However, some studies suggest that CR particles might retain their elastic properties individually due to interfacial detachment issues32. Meanwhile, RAP enhances bonding and stiffness because its aged binder interacts with virgin binder during mixing, improving cohesion and durability. Rejuvenators added during blending help restore the properties of aged binder, increasing their strength and crack resistance33,34. Nazari et al. (2023) also noted that rutting resistance and fatigue life could be improved through modification with chloroprene rubber. Still, concerns remain about the effects of individual particles rather than network formation35.

Conclusion

The main conclusions drawn from this research are as follows:

The modified asphalt pavement section has higher rutting resistance as it has a lower rut depth value in mm than the unmodified pavement section. The pavement section having CRMB and RAP shows greater resistance against rutting than unmodified, and the sections modified with RAP and crumb rubber separately. Furthermore, the pavement section with RAP has greater rutting potential than the pavement section with CRMB.

The modified asphalt pavement section has higher rutting resistance as it has a lower rut depth value in mm than the unmodified pavement section. The pavement section having CRMB and RAP shows greater resistance against rutting than unmodified, and the sections modified with RAP and crumb rubber separately. Furthermore, the pavement section with RAP has greater rutting potential than the pavement section with CRMB.

The trend shows that the modified asphalt pavement section has a relatively higher IDT value than the unmodified asphalt pavement section. The section having a combination of RAP and CRMB shows greater tensile strength compared to the pavement section modified with RAP and crumb rubber separately. Furthermore, the pavement section with CRMB has greater tensile strength than the pavement section with RAP as a modifier.

The trend shows that the modified asphalt pavement section has a relatively higher IDT value than the unmodified asphalt pavement section. The section having a combination of RAP and CRMB shows greater tensile strength compared to the pavement section modified with RAP and crumb rubber separately. Furthermore, the pavement section with CRMB has greater tensile strength than the pavement section with RAP as a modifier.

The result shows that the modified asphalt pavement section has a relatively higher Resilience Modulus (MR) value than the unmodified asphalt pavement section. The section having a combination of RAP and CRMB shows greater resilience compared to the pavement section modified with RAP and crumb rubber separately. Furthermore, the pavement section having CRMB has a greater resilient modulus than the pavement section having RAP as a modifier.

The result shows that the modified asphalt pavement section has a relatively higher Resilience Modulus (MR) value than the unmodified asphalt pavement section. The section having a combination of RAP and CRMB shows greater resilience compared to the pavement section modified with RAP and crumb rubber separately. Furthermore, the pavement section having CRMB has a greater resilient modulus than the pavement section having RAP as a modifier.

The findings of this study offer several important practical implications for pavement rehabilitation in areas with similar climate and traffic conditions. Using RAP and CR, both alone and combined, can significantly improve pavement performance in terms of rutting resistance, tensile strength, and resilience. This method not only boosts the sustainability of pavement construction by reducing waste and utilizing recycled materials but also proves to be cost-effective.

Furthermore, the application of this technology could be scaled up for use on highways, urban roads, and rural road networks, contributing to the broader goal of creating sustainable and resilient infrastructure. For policymakers and road maintenance authorities, adopting these modified pavement systems could lead to more economical and environmentally friendly practices, ensuring improved road performance over an extended period.
