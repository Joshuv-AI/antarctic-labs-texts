Conjugated biopolymer assisted separation of nanoplastics from aqueous systems: a step towards sustainability
Authors: Radhika Varshney, Praveen C. Ramamurthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanoplastics pose a significant environmental concern as they enter aquatic systems through various anthropogenic pathways. Consequently, the removal of nanoplastics from water, particularly polystyrene, is of paramount importance. This work focuses on the separation of polystyrene nanoplastics from aqueous systems, utilising polydopamine (PDA), a conjugated biopolymer, to induce flocculation. The synthesised conjugated biopolymer demonstrated a significant removal efficiency of approximately 75% for polystyrene nanoplastics within 24 hours. This was achieved by adding PDA to simulated water containing polystyrene nanoparticles (20 mg L−1) and adjusting the pH value to 2, with a PDA dosage of approximately 1 mg per 8 mL of water. The successful flocculation of polystyrene nanoplastics was confirmed through SEM and FTIR analysis. Quantification of polystyrene removal was accomplished using UV-vis spectroscopy. Additionally, regeneration studies of PDA from the formed flocs were conducted to maintain the sustainability of the removal process. Overall, this study highlights the efficacy of using the conjugated biopolymer PDA in the flocculation-based removal of polystyrene nanoplastics. The findings underscore the importance of exploring more sustainable biopolymers for the efficient removal of nanoplastics from aqueous environments.

Introduction

Water pollution, particularly by plastics, poses a significant threat to global ecosystems and organisms. Plastics are the predominant source of pollutants in marine environments, accounting for over 80% of the pollution.1 Over time, the plastic particles entering the water systems break down into smaller and smaller particles, forming microplastics (size <5 mm) or nanoplastics (size <1 µm).2 Apart from their formation by breaking down from larger plastic debris, which leads to the formation of secondary micro or nanoplastics (MNPs), MNPs can also be released directly into the aqueous systems via cosmetics, chemicals, and marine coatings (on boat hulls) that constitute primary MNP sources.3 Once introduced into water systems, these particles readily enter the tissues of marine organisms and can bioaccumulate.4 Further, they can enter the bodies of humans or animals who consume fish and other seafood. Microplastics such as polystyrene, polyethylene terephthalate, and polyethylene have been found in human blood.5 MNPs pose a health hazard to living organisms via three major pathways: (i) leaching toxic chemicals such as bisphenol A, a well-known endocrine disruptor; (ii) adsorbing toxic pollutants such as cadmium, a human carcinogen; (iii) causing direct damage in the body, such as oxidative stress, abnormal immune response, inflammation, intestinal damage, and metabolic disorders.6 Consequently, it is crucial to address this emerging issue and develop effective methodologies for removing MNPs from aqueous systems.

Polystyrene (PS), made from the monomeric unit styrene, finds extensive applications in the packaging,7 refrigeration,8 cosmetics,9 medical,8 and electronics10 industries. PS is also among the major types of microplastics detected in human blood.5 PS nanoparticles have already been associated with various toxicities in animals (such as developmental defects, altered gene expression, neurotoxicity, and oxidative stress) and in soil (such as reduced enzyme activity and reduced microorganism biomass).7 Various methods have been explored for the remediation of MNPs, including adsorption,11 flocculation,12 biodegradation,13 and photodegradation.14 Among these methods, flocculation is widely employed in water treatment processes worldwide owing to its lower cost compared with other techniques, such as membrane filtration.15 Flocculation involves the addition of flocculants to induce the aggregation of colloidal particles, leading to the formation of flocs that can settle. Certain studies on the flocculation or hetero-aggregation of nanoplastics are available in the literature.12,16 For instance, pectin, a polysaccharide, has recently been utilised to flocculate PS nanoplastics in the presence of Fe(iii) ions.12 Nevertheless, the subsequent treatment of flocs enriched in Fe(iii) ions remain unexplored. The regeneration of flocculants is vital to maintain the overall sustainability of the remediation process, but has been overlooked in many studies performing MNP separation. Additionally, the biocompatibility of flocculants is crucial. Shifting from chemical-based flocculants to biomaterial-based alternatives offers benefits such as biocompatibility, cost-efficiency, and safety.17

Polydopamine (PDA) is a conjugated biopolymer derived from dopamine, a neurotransmitter found in the brain. Existing literature reveals extensive research on PDA–PS composites or nanostructures.18–20 PDA exhibits a tendency to interact with other molecules through π–π stacking, electrostatic, and hydrogen bonding interactions.20 Previous studies have also reported interfacial interactions between PDA and PS.19 Notably, PDA demonstrates biocompatibility and hydrophobicity, facilitating its separation after the remediation process. While previous studies have primarily focused on PDA-PS interactions in material synthesis and interface engineering, the potential of PDA to capture, aggregate, and remove dispersed PS nanoplastic pollutants from water has remained largely unexplored. Therefore, the novelty of this work lies in the use of PDA as a flocculation-based remediation agent for the removal of polystyrene nanoplastics from aqueous systems, rather than as a surface modification or coating material. Hence, we propose utilising PDA as a flocculant for the flocculation-based removal of PS nanoplastics.

In this work, a conjugated biopolymer, PDA, was synthesised and characterised employing a range of techniques. The synthesised PDA has been employed for the removal of PS nanoplastics utilising the flocculation methodology. The quantification of PS nanoplastics removal has been carried out using UV-vis spectroscopy. Various parameters governing the flocculation process, such as pH, time, and flocculant dosage, have been systematically optimised. Furthermore, the influence of different chemical species on the flocculation of PS nanoplastics has been thoroughly examined. Moreover, a comprehensive investigation into the regeneration of the flocculant from the flocs has been conducted, emphasising the importance of maintaining the sustainability of the remediation process. Molecular docking simulations have also been conducted to predict interactions between PDA and PS, considering various possible PDA structures. Through this investigation, it has been found that PDA can enhance flocculation of PS nanoplastics under strongly acidic conditions by neutralising charges and hydrophobic interactions, thereby assisting in their removal without the need for additional coagulants or modification agents.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Polystyrene particles (5% w/v, size ∼70 nm) were purchased from Spherotech Inc., dopamine hydrochloride (DA-HCl), ammonia solution (∼30%), and absolute ethanol were procured from Sigma-Aldrich, SDFCL, and CSS, respectively. All the experiments were conducted using deionised water unless explicitly stated.

Polydopamine synthesis

Polydopamine spheres were synthesised utilising a previously reported protocol,21 with slight modification. In brief, 100 mg of DA-HCl was dissolved in 2 mL of deionised water to form a DA-HCl solution. A 26 mL solution of water and ethanol was prepared in a beaker with a volumetric ratio of 9 : 4. Then, 200 µL of ammonia solution was added to the beaker containing the above mixture. They were stirred at room temperature for 20 minutes at ∼300 rpm. Then, the DA-HCl solution (2 mL) was added to the beaker dropwise while stirring continuously. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 48 hours, and brown-black PDA was observed (Fig. 1).

Synthesis of PDA from DA-HCl.

Synthesised PDA was separated by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 15 min with a copious amount of deionised water, then once with ethanol. After that, the separated PDA was dried in a hot air oven overnight at 60 °C. The synthesis process was repeated several times to obtain sufficient amounts of PDA.

Material characterisation

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis were carried out using the Ultra55 FE-SEM Carl Zeiss EDS instrument to quantify the elemental composition and visualise the structural characteristics of PS nanoplastics, DA-HCl, synthesised PDA, and PS–PDA flocs resulting from flocculation experiments. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was conducted utilising the Bruker Tensor II in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode to analyse the synthesis of PDA from DA-HCl and elucidate the changes in PDA functionalities pre- and post-PS nanoplastic flocculation experiments. X-ray diffraction (XRD) using the Bruker D8 instrument was employed to investigate the structural features of PDA. The Malvern zeta potential instrument was utilised to examine the surface charges of PS nanoplastics and PDA at various pH values. The PerkinElmer UV/VIS spectrometer (Lambda 35) was used to measure the absorbance of PDA, PS, and PS–PDA samples, enabling the investigation of possible interactions and the quantitative analysis of the results.

Design of experiments for flocculation

The flocculation experiments primarily utilised borosilicate vials and were conducted as depicted in Fig. 2. The removal efficiency of PS nanoplastics was quantified using UV-vis spectroscopy, with PS nanoplastics concentration calibrated using the same technique. Parameters, including pH, flocculation time, and flocculant dosage, were optimised during the experimental process. The influence of various chemical species on the flocculation of PS nanoplastics using PDA was investigated. Additionally, to evaluate flocculant regeneration, a fixed amount of generated flocs was treated with solvents such as deionised water, acetone, ethanol, and chloroform individually, followed by drying the resulting materials in a hot-air oven at 60 °C.

Interaction between PDA and PS using molecular docking

To simulate interactions between PDA and PS, various possible PDA structures were identified from the literature (Table 1). Using ChemDraw 18.1, the PDA structures were drawn (checked for errors and cleaned up), then loaded into Chem3D 18.1 and saved as Protein Data Bank (PDB) files after structure cleanup. Then, using Avogadro software22,23 and BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (DSV),24 the geometries of PDB files were further optimised several times. Similar steps were followed to generate the PS PDB file. PDA was assigned as a macromolecule, and PS was assigned as a ligand for docking. Blind docking was performed using AutoDock Tools 1.5.725 in a grid box of dimensions 60 × 60 × 60 with a spacing of 0.375 units. The genetic algorithm was utilised for docking as the search parameter, with a population size of 300 and 500 runs. After docking, the topmost conformation (ranked by energy) was analysed using DSV to determine possible interactions.

Characterisation

Morphology and elemental analysis

The surface morphologies of DA-HCl and synthesised PDA are presented in Fig. 3a and b, respectively. It can be observed that the morphology of DA-HCl is flaky, whereas the morphology of PDA is spherical particles, mostly <1 µm. These results are consistent with previous studies and suggest the synthesis of PDA from DA-HCl.21 The EDS spectra of DA-HCl, PS, and PDA–PS complex are presented in the SI file (Fig. S1). The EDS spectrum of DA-HCl indicates the presence of elements such as C, N, O, and Cl. In comparison, the EDS spectrum of PDA (Fig. 3c) indicates the presence of elements such as C, N, and O, with weight percentages of ∼61.5, 13, and 25.5, respectively. This endorses the synthesis of PDA from DA-HCl. Furthermore, the EDS spectrum of PS (Fig. S1) indicates the presence of C and O elements with weight percentages of ∼97% and ∼3%, respectively. While the EDS spectrum of the PDA–PS flocs (Fig. S1) formed after flocculation indicates the presence of C, N, and O elements having weight percentages of ∼83, 6, and 11, respectively. The amount of carbon content present in PDA increased ∼1.3 times in PDA–PS flocs, and the amount of O and N content present in PDA decreased ∼2.3 and ∼2.2 times, respectively, in the PDA–PS flocs, suggesting the formation of a PDA–PS complex. Fig. 3d and e represent the SEM images of PDA and PS samples at 35 K× magnification. Fig. 3f represents the SEM images of PDA–PS flocs obtained after the flocculation experiments and indicates the presence of both PDA and PS in the flocs. This suggests successful flocculation of PS after adding PDA to the sample.

Surface charge analysis

Fig. 4a and b show the zeta potential vs. pH for PS (20 mg L−1) and PDA (0.6 mg/6 mL) samples, respectively. The zeta potential values of PS at all pH values studied were negative, consistent with previous reports.28 On the other hand, the zeta potential of PDA showed a point of zero charge (PZC) at a pH of ∼3.5 (pHPZC). Ma et al. also obtained positive zeta potential values for PDA in acidic pH conditions.29 From zeta potential studies of PS and PDA at pH values ranging from 2 to 9, it is suggested that PDA can interact with and assist in the charge neutralisation of PS at acidic pH conditions (pH 2 and 3).

Structural analysis

The XRD spectra of any material assist in determining the degree of crystallinity of the material, as endorsed by the nature of the spectra obtained. The XRD data for the synthesised PDA were collected using Cu Kα as the source of radiation. The XRD spectrum of PDA obtained in the range 2θ = 5° to 80° is broad (Fig. 5a), demonstrating a broad peak at ∼23°, which is consistent with the previous studies,30 and suggests that the synthesised PDA spheres are amorphous structures.

(a) XRD spectra of PDA, (b) UV-vis spectrum of PDA, PS, and PDA–PS after flocculation.

Spectroscopic analysis

The UV-vis spectra of PDA, PS, and PDA–PS samples were obtained using the supernatant collected after the completion of flocculation experiments. In these experiments, all three samples were allowed to settle for one day before measurement (Fig. 5b). The results from Fig. 5b provided insights into potential interactions between PS and PDA. Notably, when PDA was added to the PS sample at pH 2 (PDA–PS sample), the absorbance at 214 nm decreased by 1.39 units compared to the PS sample at pH 2 (it is important to note that all the samples were recorded after one day), indicating flocculation of PS resulting from its interaction with PDA. Furthermore, a peak appeared in the PDA–PS sample at approximately 308 nm, suggesting an interaction between PDA and PS molecules. The appearance of a peak (at 308 nm) and a significant reduction in the absorbance (200 nm–235 nm) suggest the presence of π–π interactions31,32 between PDA and PS, thereby supporting the insights obtained from molecular docking simulations.

PS nanoplastic removal efficiency quantification

The quantification of nanoplastics has been a challenge owing to the lack of efficient and robust detection of nanoplastics in the environment.39 We employed UV-vis spectroscopy to quantify the removal of PS nanoplastics. For this, the UV-vis spectrum of PS nanoplastics at concentrations ranging from 0.1 mg L−1 to 20 mg L−1 (prepared by diluting from the same stock solution) was recorded. It is noteworthy that PS nanoplastics do not exhibit a significant peak in the UV-vis spectrum, which prevents the concentration calibration curve from being plotted. Therefore, a Python code was developed that performs linear fitting at all wavelengths and returns the wavelengths with an R2 value >0.99. Based on the results obtained, the wavelength 214 nm was selected for the quantification of flocculation efficiencies, as the R2 value at 214 nm obtained was 0.998 (Fig. 7) after setting the intercept as 0, and PDA's own absorbance obtained (Fig. 5b) in this region was low. Influence of various parameters on flocculation

The pH of the PS nanoplastic sample (20 mg L−1) was varied in the range of 2–9 in order to study the influence of pH on the flocculation of PS nanoplastics using PDA as a flocculant. In a typical flocculation experiment, a constant amount of PDA (∼2 mg) was added to vials containing 8 mL of a PS nanoplastic sample (20 mg L−1) at varying pH values. UV-vis spectra of all the samples were taken before and one day after the addition of PDA (Fig. S2a and b). The UV-vis spectra of PS nanoplastic samples at all pH values before PDA addition were similar (Fig. S2a). After one day of the addition of PDA, significant flocculation was observed only in the sample with a pH of 2, and slight flocculation was observed in the sample with a pH value of 3 (Fig. 8a and S2b). While samples having pH values of 4–9 did not show any flocculation. The flocculation of PS nanoplastics occurring at highly acidic pH values can be explained by analysing the zeta potential values of PS nanoplastics measured at different pH values. As reported previously, the zeta potential of PS nanoplastic is negative at all pH values (Fig. 4a). Moreover, the zeta potential values of PDA are positive only in the pH values 2 and 3 (Fig. 4b). Therefore, the charge neutralisation mechanism often required for flocculation to take place only occurs at the acidic pH values (2 and 3). Nevertheless, pH 2 may restrict the practical applicability of PDA for nanoplastic removal in most actual water systems; yet, certain real-world scenarios, such as acid mine drainage,41 or textile wastewater streams,42 do have acidic water streams, wherein the current developed method can be adopted. Therefore, pH 2 was selected as the optimum pH value for further experiments.

Optimisations of parameters such as (a) pH, (b) time (N = 3), (c) dosage of PDA (N = 2), (d) influence of other chemical species.

The flocculation time was optimised at pH 2. A constant amount of PDA (∼2 mg) was added in the 8 mL of PS nanoplastic sample (20 mg L−1), and UV-vis spectra were measured before adding PDA and after the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th day of adding PDA (Note that the samples after recording UV-vis spectra were carefully added back to the same vial to check the effect on the following day on UV-vis spectra). It was observed that the flocculation efficiency became almost constant for the sample with a pH of ∼2 after the first day, suggesting that the optimal flocculation time was 1 day (Fig. 8b). In order to determine the optimum dosage of PDA required to maximise the flocculation of PS nanoplastics, the amount of PDA added was varied in a fixed volume (8 mL) of a 20 mg L−1 PS sample at pH 2. Results indicate that the optimum PDA dosage for the flocculation of PS nanoplastics (20 mg L−1) after 1 day was ∼1 mg (Fig. 8c), yielding a removal efficiency of ∼75%.

Influence of other chemical species

The impact of other chemical species on the flocculation of PS nanoplastics by using PDA was studied, wherein various types of chemical species were considered, such as cations (Fe(iii), Al(iii), Cr(vi), Zn(ii), Cu(ii), Na(i), K(i)), common organic solvents (ethanol, acetone and isopropanol), organic molecules (catechol, resorcinol, methylene blue). Herein, known concentrations of each chemical species (as tabulated in Table S1) were added to a separate vial containing 20 mg L−1 PS nanoplastic, with the pH adjusted to 2. The dosage of PDA added to induce flocculation was ∼0.8 mg/8 mL. Results obtained after one day of flocculation were analysed with respect to the control experiment (relative to the control). Here, control refers to the flocculation experiment of PS using PDA in the absence of any other chemical species, and the individual removal efficiencies were also calculated using AI of the control experiment. It was observed that, in the presence of all the chemical species considered, flocculation of PS nanoplastics occurred. In most cases, the flocculation efficiency obtained in the presence of chemical species was around or more than 100% compared to the control (relative to the control). Further, the flocculation efficiency obtained in the presence of cationic species having charges >2, such as Fe(iii), Al(iii), and Cr(vi), was approximately 9–17% higher than that of the control (Fig. 8d). This suggests that the addition of Fe(iii), Al(iii), or Cr(vi) cationic species to the samples will further enhance removal efficiency by assisting charge neutralisation. However, the addition of these cationic species was prevented as it would result in the formation of flocs containing these cationic species, posing a challenge in PDA regeneration, and causing the generation of secondary waste containing these metal ions.

It should be noted that the interfering species investigated in the present study were selected as representative inorganic and organic compounds and do not constitute an exhaustive list of the constituents that may be encountered in natural or industrial water matrices. Real-world waters contain a much broader range of competing species, including natural organic matter, surfactants, suspended particulates, pharmaceuticals, dyes, and other organic and inorganic contaminants that may influence the performance of PDA-mediated nanoplastic removal. Therefore, further studies involving more complex water matrices are necessary to comprehensively evaluate the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed remediation strategy.

Flocculant regeneration

Regeneration of flocculants is crucial in maintaining the overall sustainability of the remediation process. Therefore, we studied the possibility of regenerating the PDA by separating the PS from the PDA–PS complex floc. We identified a few solvents that tend to dissolve PS but not PDA, such as acetone, ethanol, and chloroform. We also considered deionised water as a regeneration solvent. Chloroform, being a hazardous solvent, is used here for exploratory purposes as it tends to dissolve PS. However, for any practical or scalable application, usage of such a solvent is neither environmentally acceptable nor aligned with green chemistry principles. Therefore, four solvents, namely, water (W), acetone (A), ethanol (E), and chloroform (C), were utilised in the regeneration studies. Solvents (1 mL solvent/1 mg floc) were added to a fixed amount of PDA–PS flocs in parts and were washed thoroughly using a vortex shaker, and the supernatant was discarded. The remaining material was dried in a hot-air oven at 60 °C and labelled as W, A, E, and C. Regenerated material was observed under SEM, and its FTIR spectrum was recorded. The FTIR spectrum recorded (Fig. 9) depicts that all the materials resemble the FTIR spectra of PDA (Fig. 6) to a great extent.

FTIR spectrum of regenerated PDA using chloroform (C), acetone (A), water (W) and ethanol (E).

However, it has been observed that in the sample regenerated using W, some peaks corresponding to PS are still present. Moreover, it is observed that regeneration with A and C resembles the FTIR spectra of PDA the most. From SEM images also, it can be observed that the morphology of the regenerated PDA in all 4 cases (W, A, E, and C) has remained spherical with a size <1 µm (Fig. 10). Further, it has been noticed that PS nanoplastics are not present in samples regenerated using A, E and C. Regeneration of PDA with original morphology could be attributed towards the chemical stability of PDA due to the presence of conjugations. The EDS data of regenerated PDA is present in Fig. S3 of the SI file, which depicts similarity to the as-synthesised PDA's EDS (Fig. 3c).

Overall, the FTIR spectra of regenerated PDA retained the characteristic functional groups of the pristine material, while SEM and EDS analysis indicated that the morphology and elemental composition were largely preserved after regeneration. These results suggest that the structural integrity of PDA remains substantially intact following use and recovery.

Molecular docking simulations indicate favourable aromatic interactions (π–π interactions) between the PDA and PS. This interpretation is further supported by the appearance of an absorption peak at 308 nm in the UV-vis spectra and by the observed FTIR peak shifts following PDA–PS interaction. Zeta potential measurements further provide evidence for a charge neutralisation mechanism. Therefore, the flocculation of PS nanoplastics by PDA is proposed to involve a combination of π–π interactions and charge neutralisation.

The PS nanoplastic particles are negatively charged in water under all pH conditions. When HCl is added to the sample to adjust its pH to 2, H3O+ ions help reduce the magnitude of negative charge on the PS surface to some extent. Then, when PDA is added to a sample containing PS at pH 2, it accomplishes the remaining task of further neutralising the negative charges present on PS. This reduction in electrostatic repulsion between PDA and PS promotes initial particle association and destabilisation of the colloidal suspension. Subsequently, π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions between the aromatic structures of PDA and PS strengthen the PDA–PS complexes and facilitate aggregate growth, ultimately leading to floc formation and sedimentation (Fig. 11).

Proposed mechanism of flocculation.

It is worth mentioning that the efficiency of PDA-mediated flocculation may also be influenced by the physicochemical properties of PDA particles, including particle size. Changes in PDA particle size can alter surface charge characteristics and the availability of protonated functional groups, thereby affecting zeta potential and the extent of charge neutralisation of negatively charged nanoplastics. However, as PDA was synthesised under identical conditions throughout this study, particle-size-dependent effects were beyond the scope of the present work.

Conclusion and future prospects

In conclusion, this study successfully synthesised and utilised a conjugated biopolymer, PDA, for the removal of PS nanoplastics. Characterisation techniques, including FTIR, SEM, EDS, and XRD, confirmed the successful synthesis of PDA. The flocculation technique using PDA as the flocculant effectively removed PS nanoplastics, with optimal performance observed under acidic conditions (pH 2) at a dosage of ∼1 mg/8 mL. Additionally, the study investigated the influence of other chemical species on PS nanoplastics flocculation, which showed no significant negative effect. Furthermore, PDA regeneration from PDA–PS flocs was carried out to ensure overall process sustainability. This study offers valuable insights into the potential utilisation of conjugated biopolymers for nanoplastic removal. Nonetheless, the limitations of operating at such a low pH value are recognised, and consequently, there is a need for further work to improve nanoplastic removal efficiencies under near neutral or environmentally relevant pH conditions. PDA is flexible for further modification owing to its abundant catechol and amine functionalities. Therefore, future studies may explore coupling PDA with metal ions or natural positively charged biopolymers (e.g., chitosan) to enhance surface charge interactions and flocculation efficiency at environmentally relevant pH values.

Henceforth, the future prospects include the design of bio-based materials suitable for removing PS nanoplastics near neutral pH conditions; addressing challenges related to improved repeatability in experiments involving biopolymers and difficult-to-detect nanoplastics; enhancing removal efficiencies; reducing contact time; evaluating material performance in the presence of natural organic matter (NOM), humic acids, surfactants, divalent cations (e.g., Ca2+ and Mg2+); real environmental water samples (e.g., tap water, river water, and lake water spiked with nanoplastics) analysis; a comprehensive evaluation of material (flocculant) regeneration efficiency, including quantitative recovery percentages and cycle-dependent removal efficiencies; studies involving the influence of nanoplastic or remediating materials’ particle size on the removal efficiencies; long-term stability of remediating material under realistic wastewater conditions, including multiple contaminants, variable ionic strengths, and fluctuating pH values; and exploring the removal of other nanoplastic types such as polyethylene terephthalate, polypropylene, and polyethene.
