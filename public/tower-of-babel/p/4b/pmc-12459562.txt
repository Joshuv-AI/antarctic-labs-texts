Current status of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in older patients with hip fragility fractures: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Tingting Dai, Dazhuang Li, Zhou Su, Huiwu Qu, Qiyun Zhen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to provide evidence for the development of targeted interventions by investigating the current status of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors and their impact factors in older patients with hip fragility fractures. This cross-sectional study employed a convenience sampling method. The participants included 160 older adults who had sustained their first hip fragility fracture and were recruited from the Department of Joint Surgery at a tertiary general hospital in Zhenjiang City, Jiangsu Province, China, between October 2023 and June 2024. Data were collected via questionnaires administered within the information-motivation-behavioral skills model framework, including: a general information questionnaire, the osteoporosis knowledge test (OKT), the osteoporosis self-efficacy scale (OSES), the social support rating scale (SSRS), the osteoporosis self-management behavioral scale, and the Healthy Behavioral Scale for the prevention of osteoporosis questionnaires. Factors influencing osteoporosis prevention health behaviors were identified via univariate analysis and multiple linear regression. A total of 154 older patients with hip fractures were included, and the valid return rate of the questionnaire was 96.25%. The score of the osteoporosis prevention health behavior scale was 6.64 ± 4.32. The results of multiple linear regression analysis revealed that sex, age, BMI, primary caregiver, whether they had fallen within one year, whether they had received health education on osteoporosis, knowledge, self-efficacy, social support, and self-management were the influencing factors of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in older patients with hip fragility fractures (P < .05), accounting for 82.8% of the total variance. Our study revealed that suboptimal osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in older patients with hip fragility fractures are influenced by sociodemographic factors, clinical history, and psychosocial determinants. These findings indicate that targeted interventions – enhancing health education, improving self-efficacy, and strengthening social support – may reduce the incidence of secondary fracture in this population.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a chronic skeletal disorder characterized by diminished bone mass and microstructural deterioration, substantially increasing the risk of fragility fractures among older populations.[1] Hip fractures, which represent the most severe fracture subtype, have a 1-year mortality rate ranging from 24% to 37.8%.[2] Following a hip fracture, progressive bone loss frequently leads to osteoporosis and increases the risk of secondary fractures.[3] In the absence of preventive interventions, continued bone loss and recurrent fractures are highly probable. Patients with prior fractures exhibit a 2 to 5-fold increased risk of secondary fractures compared with the general population, and without appropriate intervention, this cascade of events severely compromises both health status and quality of life.[4] The evidence indicates that osteoporosis prevention health behaviors associated with osteoporosis can improve fracture prognosis, decelerate disease progression, and diminish fracture recurrence rates,[5] thereby establishing their critical importance for older patients with hip fragility fractures.

Despite the established significance of these health behaviors and international consensus guidelines advocating for comprehensive osteoporosis management during fracture treatment, its implementation in clinical practice remains inadequate. Within the Chinese context, osteoporosis diagnosis and awareness rates among older patients with hip fragility fractures are markedly low, at 6.4% and 7.4%, respectively.[6] Concurrently, only 21.8% of patients received postoperative anti-osteoporotic medication.[7] These findings underscore significant deficiencies in patient care.[8] While 59.1% of patients utilize calcium and vitamin D supplementation,[9] these interventions are implemented inconsistently without standardized protocols. Furthermore, rehabilitation participation rates are substantially lower than those reported in comparable Western demographic cohorts.[10] Although fracture liaison services (FLSs) have been implemented globally to ameliorate these deficiencies, challenges remain in enhancing the uptake and adherence rates of health behaviors, as well as in demonstrating sustained long-term efficacy in fracture reduction.[11] Studies have shown that timely screening, assessment, and intervention for osteoporosis can reduce patients’ fracture risk by 10% to 60%[12,13]; consequently, proactive health promotion for patients with osteoporosis is crucial. However, the efficacy of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors is contingent upon multiple interrelated factors, including cognitive understanding, self-efficacy, and social support systems.[14]

Previous investigations examining these health behaviors have been characterized by methodological limitations, including the use of limited conceptual frameworks and a failure to systematically integrate the multifactorial determinants influencing behavioral adoption. The Information–Motivation–Behavioral (IMB) skills Model provides a robust theoretical framework to address these limitations, suggesting that sustained health behavior implementation necessitates three fundamental components: comprehensive knowledge, intrinsic motivation, and practical behavioral competencies.[15] Thus, the IMB model was used in this study to investigate the factors influencing preventive health behaviors for osteoporosis in older patients with hip fragility fractures. This study aims to provide evidence for the development of targeted interventions, thereby reducing patients’ secondary fracture incidence and optimizing their long-term quality of life.

2. Methods

2.1. Survey participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Joint Surgery at a tertiary general hospital in Zhenjiang City, Jiangsu Province, China, between October 2023 and June 2024. The study enrolled 160 older patients admitted for primary hip fragility fractures. Inclusion criteria: Aged ≥60 years old with a confirmed diagnosis of osteoporosis the basis of bone mineral density measurement; First occurrence of a hip fragility fracture; Provided voluntary informed consent to participate; and Cognitively conscious, without language barriers, and able to independently complete questionnaires (verbally or in writing). Exclusion criteria: Major preexisting comorbidities (e.g., severe cardiac, hepatic, pulmonary, renal, or neurological disease, or malignancy). Elimination criteria: Incomplete questionnaires (>5% missing data); Logically inconsistent responses within questionnaires; and questionnaires with repeated and regular answers. This study used multiple linear regression analysis with 18 independent variables. On the basis of the sample size calculation formula[16] (requiring 5–10 observations per independent variable) and considering a 20% buffer to account for invalid questionnaires, the calculated sample size ranged from 108 to 216 cases. Ultimately, 160 older patients with hip fragility fractures were consecutively enrolled. This study was approved by the Hospital Academic Ethics Review Committee of the Affiliated People’s Hospital of Jiangsu University (approval number: SQK-20220189-Y), and informed consent was obtained from the participants.

2.2. Survey instrument

2.2.1. General information questionnaire

On the basis of the literature review and research team consultation, the questionnaire included demographic and sociological information (age, sex, education, BMI, marital status, primary caregiver, monthly household income, medical payment method, whether the participants had fallen within one year, and whether they had received any health education about osteoporosis) and information related to the characteristics of the disease (fracture site, surgical procedure, comorbidities) in 2 sections.

2.2.2. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: informational factors

The level of knowledge about osteoporosis prevention among older patients with hip fragility fractures in this survey study was assessed via the osteoporosis knowledge test (OKT). The questionnaire was developed by scholar Kim[17] and translated into Chinese by researchers such as Chen Yuping[18] and it is highly useful. The questionnaire has 3 dimensions, including risk factors, exercise knowledge, and calcium knowledge. The total number of questionnaire items was 26, all of which were multiple-choice questions. One point was awarded for correct completion of the questionnaire, and 0 points were awarded for incorrect completion of the questionnaire or choosing the option of “do not know.” The total score of the questionnaire was 26 points, and the higher the score of the OKT of the patients was, the greater the level of knowledge of osteoporosis was. The Cronbach alpha coefficient of this questionnaire ranged from 0.83 to 0.87.

2.2.3. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: personal motivational factors

The level of osteoporosis self-efficacy of older patients with hip fragility fractures in this survey was assessed via the osteoporosis self-efficacy scale (OSES). The questionnaire was modified from the scale of Yuping Chen,[19] which contains twelve items in 2 areas, namely, calcium efficacy (6 items) and exercise efficacy (6 items), with each being scored from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates that the patient has no confidence in his/her condition, and 10 indicates that he/she is very confident, and the higher the score is, the stronger the patient’s self-confidence in the health behaviors of osteoporosis. The Cronbach alpha coefficients of the scale ranged from 0.90 to 0.94.

2.2.4. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: social motivational factors

The level of social support of older patients with hip fragility fractures in this survey was assessed via the social support rating scale (SSRS) developed by Xiao Shuiyuan.[20] The questionnaire consisted of 10 items. The 1st to 4th and 8th to 10th questions were scored as 4 options scored as 1, 2, 3, and 4 points, respectively; for the 6th and 7th items, no source option was scored as 0 points, there were several final scores for the sources, and the scale was scored as several points in total, ranging from 12 to 66 points. The higher the score is, the greater the level of social support received. 12 to 22 indicate a low level of social support, 23 to 44 indicate a medium level, and 45 to 66 indicate a high level. The Cronbach alpha coefficient for this scale was 0.92.

2.2.5. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: behavioral skills factor

The level of self-management behaviors of osteoporosis patients in older patients with hip fragility fractures in this research study was assessed via the self-management behavioral scale for osteoporosis patients compiled by Shen Qiuming.[21] The scale consists of 3 dimensions, namely, nutrition, exercise, and self-management behaviors, with a total of 11 items. The frequency of completing the following behaviors in the past 30 days was recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, with 0 representing “never” and 5 representing “always.” The scale was scored from 0 to 55, with higher scores indicating better osteoporosis self-management behaviors. The Cronbach alpha values for the total and subdimensions of the scale ranged from 0.812 to 0.928.

2.2.6. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: patient health behavior

The level of osteoporosis prevention health behavior of older patients with hip fragility fractures in this survey study was measured via the osteoporosis prevention health behavior scale, which is a questionnaire on osteoporosis prevention health behavior compiled by Liu Yuzhen[22] on her own and is based on a review of the literature and consultation with experts in response to the requirements of the relevant guidelines and clinical experience to investigate the osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in low bone mass populations. The questionnaire consists of 5 dimensions and 13 items: dietary habits (items 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5), regular exercise (item 6), living habits (items 7, 8, 9, and 10), outdoor sunshine (item 11), and regular monitoring (items 12 and 13). The scoring method is as follows: total score of 13 points; each question is assigned 1 point for correct implementation and 0 points for incorrect implementation; score range, minimum 0 points, maximum 13 points; and the higher the score is, the higher the level of health behaviors for osteoporosis prevention. The Cronbach alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.725.

2.3. Data collection and quality control methods

The case data of older patients with hip fragility fractures were reviewed in advance to ensure that the participants met the inclusion criteria, after which the general information of the patients was collected. During the questionnaire survey, 2 uniformly trained researchers introduced the purpose, significance, and precautions of the survey to the survey participants and collected the questionnaires after providing informed consent. Given that the participants were all older patients, paper questionnaires were used for the survey, and the researchers explained the content of the questionnaires with unified guidelines and assisted the patients in completing the questionnaires in a one-to-one manner. The completed questionnaires were collected and checked on the spot, and if there were any missing items or omissions, they were added on the spot. For patients with low literacy and poor eyesight, and those who could not fill out the questionnaire on their own, the investigator assisted them in completing the questionnaire.

2.4. Statistical methods

Excel software was used to enter the data, and SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Armonk) was used to analyze the data. Normally distributed data are described as the mean ± standard deviation, count data are described as the number of cases and percentage, and independent samples t-tests, ANOVA, or Pearson correlation analysis were used for single-factor analysis. A multifactorial analysis was performed via multiple linear regression analysis for factors affecting osteoporosis prevention health behavior. P < .05 was used to indicate that the difference was statistically significant.

3.2. Current status and results of univariate analysis of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in older patients with hip fragility fractures

Among the 154 older patients with hip fragility fractures in this study, the mean osteoporosis prevention health behavior scale score was 6.64 ± 4.32. The dimension-specific scores were as follows: dietary habits (2.35 ± 0.80), regular exercise (0.47 ± 0.25), lifestyle habits (2.06 ± 0.76), outdoor sunlight exposure (0.86 ± 0.12), and regular monitoring (0.89 ± 0.45). Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences (P < .05) in total scores across the following variables: age, sex, insurance type, BMI, primary caregiver, monthly household income, comorbidities, history of falls within one year, and osteoporosis health education status (Table 1).

4. Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we observed that the level of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in older patients with hip fragility fractures was moderately low and is influenced by age, sex, BMI, fall history, health education, self-efficacy, social support, and self-management. These findings provide a theoretical basis for healthcare professionals to develop interventions aimed at improving osteoporosis prevention health behaviors, thereby reducing the risk of secondary fractures and increasing patients’ quality of life.

4.1. The incidence of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors is moderately low in older patients with hip fragility fractures

The study revealed that older patients with hip fragility fractures scored 6.64 ± 4.32 on the osteoporosis prevention health behavior scale, with only 15.6% demonstrating high behavioral awareness, which is consistent with the findings of James.[23] The score for regular exercise was the lowest, possibly for the following reasons. On the one hand, China’s research on the health behavior of this type of patient started late, and healthcare professionals understand its relevant theoretical knowledge more one-sidedly, often ignoring the importance of guiding regular exercise in their health behavior. Additionally, older patients with hip fragility fractures typically present with multiple comorbidities. Fracture-related mobility restrictions coupled with fear of reinjury further discourage physical activity. Exercise can regulate bone metabolism in osteoporosis patients and effectively promote the secretion of sex hormones such as estrogen and testosterone and the absorption of nutritive tissues.[24] It is recommended that the healthcare system carry out training for healthcare professionals in osteoporosis prevention health behaviors and develop individualized intervention programs for exercise behaviors in this patient population to promote health behaviors.

4.2. Factors influencing osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in older patients with hip fragility fractures

4.2.1. Sociodemographic factors

Multiple linear regression analyses revealed that advanced age, female sex, and low body mass index are independent risk factors for poor adherence to osteoporosis prevention health behaviors, corroborating the findings of Li Junjie’s research.[25] With increasing age, the secretion of growth hormone gradually decreases, but the secretion of parathyroid hormone gradually increases, which weakens the activity of osteoblasts and leads to bone loss.[26] Moreover, during the perimenopausal stage, the precipitous decrease in estrogen levels promotes osteoclastogenesis, leading to increased bone resorption and reduced bone formation.[27] Some studies have confirmed that a low body mass index is an important risk factor for reduced bone mineral density.[25] Individuals with greater adipose tissue mass in the hip region may benefit from a mechanical cushioning effect, which reduces the impact force during a fall, thus preventing fractures or even secondary fractures from occurring. Therefore, healthcare professionals should pay attention to interindividual differences when providing health behavior guidance to older patients with hip fragility fractures and develop personalized and stratified health behavior interventions according to different ages, sexes and body mass indices when necessary.

4.2.2. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: information dimension

With respect to knowledge acquisition, only 10% of patients achieved adequate osteoporosis knowledge test (OKT) scores. This knowledge deficit significantly impedes behavioral adoption, aligning with findings by Kindra.[28] Scientifically sound medical knowledge is the basis for the formation of beliefs and a source of motivation to change an individual’s behavior. In a cohort study by James, only 68.75% of patients with vertebral fragility fractures reported that they were aware that they had a fracture after undergoing imaging, highlighting a substantial gap between diagnostic findings and patient perception.[23] Ahmed reported that reinforcing osteoporosis prevention knowledge can improve residents’ awareness and improve their behavioral habits.[29] Osteoporosis is characterized by a high degree of invisibility, and older patients often lack the awareness and means to actively acquire health knowledge. Moreover, this may be due to the incidental nature of the osteoporosis examination; because patients with other diseases choose to seek medical treatment, health care personnel do not fully explain the seriousness of osteoporosis and high risk of fracture potential to them, resulting in cognitive deficiencies. Therefore, it is imperative for healthcare professionals to identify and address patient knowledge gaps promptly and to strengthen communication between healthcare professionals and patients to increase patients’ knowledge of hip fragility fractures and their impact on long-term osteoporosis prevention health behaviors.

4.2.3. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: motivation dimension

In the motivation dimension, the levels of calcium intake and exercise self-efficacy of older patients with hip fragility fractures were lower than those patients without fractures. Higher self-efficacy was associated with stronger awareness of health behaviors, consistent with the results of the study by Wang Yichen.[30] This difference likely stems from motor function limitations inherent to hip fracture recovery. Dietary and exercise-based osteoporosis prevention health behaviors not only improve the outcome of osteoporosis but also reduce fall risk, thereby lowering the incidence of secondary fractures and improving long-term outcomes. Older patients with hip fragility fractures and greater self-efficacy were more adept at self-attribution and adjustment and had greater confidence in maintaining and promoting their health, resulting in higher levels of subjective motivation to improve health-promoting behaviors. This suggests that healthcare professionals conduct motivational interviews and experience-sharing sessions to guide patients in expressing their ambivalence and stimulating their motivation to change their behavior and cooperation. This study revealed that living alone and a lack of social support are barrier factors for older patients with hip fragility fractures. The possible reason for this is the lack of moral support from relatives, friends and all aspects of society, which does not create confidence in changing their undesirable behaviors. Studies have shown that adults who live alone and lack support from family and friends may underestimate their risk of fracture, and this state of isolation reduces bone density by affecting their cortisol levels.[31] These findings suggest that clinical practitioners could enhance departmental resourcing and encourage patients’ primary caregivers to be actively involved in intervention programs. Such efforts contribute to improving patients’ engagement in preventive behaviors and providing emotional support to foster a more supportive internal and external environment.

4.2.4. Information–motivation–behavioral skills model: behavioral skills dimension

Consistent with Kang’s chronic disease research, analysis of behavioral skills revealed that effective self-management positively influences osteoporosis prevention health behaviors[32] Effective self-management is the foundation of chronic disease prevention and treatment.[24] Old patients with hip fragility fractures often experience a range of negative effects, such as pain, activity limitations, and decreased physical function, so it is particularly important to help these patients develop good self-management behaviors. When developing health behavioral nursing interventions for osteoporosis prevention, healthcare professionals should not only focus on dietary modifications, enhancement of functional exercise, and appropriate medication guidance but also integrate an informative continuity of care model to strengthen disease awareness and establish behavioral change beliefs after discharge so that they can empower patients to take an active role in improving their self-management ability.

5. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participant recruitment was limited to a single tertiary hospital, potentially affecting its generalizability. Future research should use multicenter longitudinal designs to better elucidate the determinants of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors in older patients with fragility fractures. Second, reliance on self-report questionnaire data may lead to potential recall bias. Third, despite adjustment for several important covariates, residual confounding factors, such as clinical biomarkers may influence the results. Nevertheless, this study provides valuable insights into the determinants of osteoporosis prevention health behaviors, although the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

6. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study of older patients with fragility fractures revealed that suboptimal osteoporosis prevention health behaviors are influenced by sociodemographic factors (age, sex, BMI, health education), clinical history (fall history), and psychosocial determinants (self-efficacy, social support, and self-management). These findings suggest that enhancing health education, improving self-efficacy, and strengthening social support may reduce the incidence of secondary fractures in older patients with fragility fractures, particularly among high-risk subgroups including women, advanced-aged patients, and those with prior falls.
