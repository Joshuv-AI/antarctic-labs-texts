Phytochemical Profiling, Antiplatelet Potential, and Cytotoxic Assessment of Thymelaea tartonraira Aerial Part Essential Oil: In Vitro and In Silico Studies
Authors: Siwar Soltani, Asma Haffouz, Imed Koubaa, Ali Gargouri, Amira Jerbi, Ikram Ben Amor, Jalel Gargouri, Basma HadjKacem, Noureddine Allouche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study evaluates the antiplatelet potential of Thymelaea tartonraira essential oil, obtained from aerial parts by hydrodistillation. GC–MS analysis identified 43 compounds, with phenethyl alcohol (25.69%), eugenol (12.42%), and carvacrol (6.41%) as major constituents. In vitro platelet aggregation assays showed significant inhibition, with IC50 values of 6.37, 10.92, and 15.32 µL/mL against aggregation induced by ADP, collagen, and arachidonic acid, respectively. Molecular modeling (in silico docking) against P2Y12, cyclooxygenase‐1, and the glycoprotein GPVI revealed strong interactions of eugenol and carvacrol with key residues, suggesting mechanisms of action. Toxicity tests confirmed the absence of cytotoxicity of the oil on HEK293 cells, erythrocytes, and platelets. These results indicate that T. tartonraira essential oil is a safe and effective natural source of antiplatelet agents with therapeutic potential for cardiovascular diseases.Keywords: anti‐platelet activity, carvacrol, eugenol, phenethyl alcohol, Thymelaea tartonraira

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, largely due to complications such as thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and stroke [1, 2]. Atherosclerosis and platelet hyper‐aggregation are common factors in the pathogenesis of these diseases. Atherosclerosis promotes platelet activation after plaque rupture, resulting in thrombogenesis and the onset of CVD [2]. This phenomenon begins with the accumulation of lipids, coagulation factors, and cellular components beneath the endothelial lining, forming atheromatous plaques. These plaques may become unstable due to biomechanical and physical stresses, increasing their susceptibility to damage. Plaque rupture triggers platelet activation and aggregation, leading to thrombus formation that can block blood flow and cause cardiovascular events [2]. Thus, platelet aggregation plays a central role in the pathogenesis of serious cardiovascular events. Accordingly, antiplatelet agents like aspirin and clopidogrel are widely used in clinical practice to prevent thrombotic events. However, the long‐term use of synthetic antiplatelet drugs is often associated with side effects, including gastrointestinal bleeding and drug resistance [3]. Ongoing investigations into novel antithrombotic compounds with minimal side effects have led to a growing interest in herbal treatments as viable therapeutic options. This shift is becoming a significant focus for the pharmaceutical sector. In this regard, essential oils derived from plants have attracted significant interest due to their diverse biological properties, which encompass antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti‐inflammatory activities [4, 5]. Notably, various species exhibit important antiplatelet effects, such as Levisticum officinale [6], Goniothalamus species [7], Artemisia campestris L. [8], Croton gratissimus [9], Hedychium coronarium [10], various Salvia species, and Thymus vulgaris [11].

For this study, Thymelaea tartonraira (T. tartonraira) was selected. This evergreen shrub belongs to the Thymelaea genus within the Thymelaeaceae family. It is a Mediterranean plant traditionally utilized in North African folk medicine for its antidiabetic effects. Previous research has emphasized the plant's remarkable abundance of phenolic compounds, including phenolic acids and flavonoids, which are recognized for their powerful therapeutic properties. Extracts from this species have demonstrated significant antidiabetic, antioxidant, and antileishmanial activities while exhibiting low cytotoxicity [12, 13].

Despite its phytochemical significance, the chemical composition and pharmacological properties of the essential oil of T. tartonraira remain largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated the potential of T. tartonraira essential oil. Therefore, we hypothesized that the essential oil of T. tartonraira aerial parts contains bioactive compounds with significant antiplatelet activity and low cytotoxicity, potentially targeting key platelet activation pathways including P2Y12, COX‐1, and GPVI. Accordingly, this study was designed with four specific objectives: to extract and chemically characterize the essential oil from T. tartonraira aerial parts using GC‑MS; to evaluate its antiplatelet activity in vitro against ADP‑, collagen‑, and arachidonic acid‑induced aggregation; to investigate, through in silico molecular docking, how the major compounds interact with platelet receptors; and to assess the oil's toxicity profile on human platelets, erythrocytes, and HEK293 cells.

Chemical composition of T. tartonraira aerial part essential oil.

The essential oil derived from the aerial part of T. tartonraira exhibits a complex and diverse chemical composition, characterized by various phytochemicals that contribute to its aromatic profile and potential therapeutic properties. This analysis led to the identification of 43 compounds divided into more than five functional groups, including, alcohols, aldehydes, esters, ketones, acids, and other compounds.

Among the detected compounds, alcohols represent the most prevalent functional group, accounting for approximately 60% of the essential oil composition. Notably, Phenethyl alcohol stands out with a remarkable relative abundance of 25.69%. This compound is well‐known for its pleasant floral aroma and is often utilized in the fragrance industry [14], and it was reported for its antimicrobial activity [15]. Other significant alcohols include Eugenol at 12.42%, which is recognized for its potent therapeutic activities. Eugenol has been identified as a powerful inhibitor of rabbit platelet thromboxane synthesis and aggregation [16]. Additionally, it has been found to have an inhibitory effect on acetylcholinesterase [17] as well as antimicrobial and antioxidant activities. Following this, carvacrol is present with an abundance percentage of 6.41%. This volatile phytochemical exhibits a wide range of therapeutic effects, including antimicrobial, antitumor, and antimutagenic properties [18]. It also provides analgesic, antispasmodic, and anti‐inflammatory benefits, along with notable antiplatelet activities [19].

Concerning the aldehydes, a total of 11 compounds were identified. Among these, furfural was the most abundant, with a relative abundance of 4.55%, known for its antityrosinase and antimicrobial activities [20]. It was followed by Heptadienal at 3.09% and E‐2‐hexenal at 2.27%. T. tartonraira essential oil also contains several esters, with (E)‐9‐octadecenoic acid methyl ester (2.64%) and methyl palmitate (1.32%) being the most prominent. In terms of ketones, β‐damascenone (2.47%) is the major ketone, recognized for its antioxidant potency [21]. The essential oil also contained trace levels of carboxylic acids, with concentrations varying from 0.14% to 0.56%. It's essential to note that the presence of certain fatty acids, such as linoleic acid and oleic acid, is associated with both nutritional benefits and potential applications in cosmetics and skincare formulations [22]. Lastly, among the other compounds, γ‐Cadinene is notably present with 3.71%. This compound is well‐known for its antioxidant and insecticidal activities [23]. The essential oil of T. tartonraira contains a diverse range of phytochemicals, particularly alcohols and aldehydes, which contribute to its potential therapeutic effects. Therefore, the present investigation aims to explore the possible applications of this rich oil, with a specific focus on its antiplatelet aggregation activity.

Biological Activities

Antiplatelet Activity of T. tartonraira Essential Oil

The effect of T. tartonraira essential oil (TEO) on human platelet aggregation was assessed in the presence of three platelet activators (ADP, AA, and collagen). The curves presented in Figure 1 demonstrate that TEO (20 µL/mL) significantly inhibits collagen, ADP, and AA‐induced platelet aggregation with inhibition percentages of 100, 98.5, and 97.5%, respectively. Additionally, we included an ethanol‐only control to ensure that any antiplatelet effects were attributable to the TEO itself and not to the solvent. The results showed that ethanol alone had no significant effect on ADP, AA, or collagen‐induced aggregation, confirming that the antiplatelet activity was due to TEO itself. The concentration required for 50% inhibition of platelet aggregation (IC50 values) was determined by varying the TEO volume tested. We found that the IC50 values of TEO were 6.37, 10.92, and 15.32 µL/mL for the ADP, collagen, and AA pathways, respectively. The IC50 values were expressed in µL/mL due to the complex and non‐standardized composition of the essential oil, which precludes conversion into molar concentrations and limits direct comparison with pure antiplatelet compounds. This unit is widely used for essential oil‐based studies as it reflects the actual tested volume of the whole bioactive mixture. Further studies involving the isolation, identification, and evaluation of the individual active constituents will allow a more precise dose‐response characterization and enable more accurate pharmacological comparison with conventional antiplatelet drugs.

Representative curves of the inhibitory effect of TEO (20 µL/mL), indomethacin (0.5 mM), and ethanol (20 µL/mL) on collagen, ADP, and AA‐induced platelet aggregation.

Phytochemical characterization of the essential oil obtained from the aerial part of T. tartonraira revealed more than 40 compounds. The TEO was rich in alcohols (60.14%) and aldehydes (17.85%), while ketones (6.95%), esters (5.72%), and acids (1.44%) were detected in lower concentrations. The antiplatelet activity of the TEO may be attributed to its rich content of bioactive compounds, particularly alcohols and aldehydes. In the case of aldehydes, the total content (17.85%) was distributed among a large number of compounds, each present in relatively low concentrations, which may limit the individual contribution of each aldehyde to the observed antiplatelet activity. In contrast, while alcohol derivatives (60.14%) were also distributed into several different compounds, three major molecules, including phenethyl alcohol, eugenol, and carvacrol, were predominant, accounting together for approximately 45% of the total extract (25%, 12% and 6%, respectively). The higher proportion of these compounds could explain their stronger contribution to the antiplatelet activity of the TEO, possibly through a synergistic mechanism. These findings suggest that both the overall abundance and the proportion of individual bioactive constituents within each family play a key role in the biological potential of the essential oil of T. tartonraira.

Among the identified compounds, phenethyl alcohol was the most abundant, representing approximately 25% of the total extract. It is a naturally occurring aromatic compound present in several essential oils such as geranium, rose, orange blossom, and neroli [24]. Given this high concentration, it is reasonable to hypothesize that this compound may play a key role in the antiplatelet effect observed. Although this molecule has not been previously studied in the context of antiplatelet activity, it has been reported to exhibit various biological activities, including antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties [25], which are often associated with modulation of platelet aggregation. Indeed, oxidative stress and inflammation are key factors in platelet activation and thrombus formation [26, 27]. Its potential effect on the antiplatelet activity needs further investigation. In contrast, eugenol and carvacrol, although present in lower concentrations, have already been demonstrated to possess antiplatelet activity in the literature [28]. Therefore, the antiplatelet activity of the T. tartonraira essential oil could result from a synergetic effect of known bioactive compounds (such as eugenol and carvacrol) and the possible contribution of phenethyl alcohol as a novel active molecule. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting the antiplatelet activity of phenolic‐rich essential oils. For example, essential oils from Artemisia campestris [8] and Thymus vulgaris [11] have demonstrated significant inhibition of platelet aggregation, mainly attributed to phenylpropanoid compounds capable of interfering with thromboxane formation and platelet activation pathways.

Molecular Docking Study

The molecular docking study investigates the binding mode between the major phytochemicals found in the essential oil of T. tartonraira: carvacrol, eugenol, and phenethyl alcohol, and the target receptors COX‐1, P2Y12, and GPVI (Figure 2). Given the potential of the T. tartonraira essential oil to inhibit ADP‐induced platelet aggregation (IC50 = 6.37 µL/mL), we conducted an in silico molecular docking study to evaluate the interaction of its main compounds with the P2Y12 receptor of ADP (Figure 2B). This receptor is involved in both hemostasis and thrombosis [29]. The active site of P2Y12 consists of several residues such as Arg19, Arg93, Cys97, Tyr105, Phe106, Leu155, Asn159, Lys179, His187, Arg256, Tyr259, and Lys280 [30]. The docking study revealed that eugenol exhibited the most stable binding affinity with a binding energy of −6.8 kcal/mol (Ki = 10.3 µM). This was followed by carvacrol and phenethyl alcohol, which showed binding energies of −6.6 kcal/mol (Ki = 14.8 µM) and −5.6 kcal/mol (Ki = 81.5 µM), respectively (Table 2).

The docking results obtained in this study are consistent with previous reports describing the interaction of phenolic compounds, such as eugenol and carvacrol, with platelet‐related targets. These compounds have been shown to interact with key enzymes and receptors involved in platelet activation, including COX‐1, mainly through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions [16, 28]. Such consistency supports the reliability of the docking predictions and highlights their biological relevance.

As indicated in Figure 3, the compounds tested bind in the active site of P2Y12 through various interactions. Eugenol forms a conventional hydrogen bond with the Asn191 residue via its hydroxyl group and establishes a cationic bond interaction with the Cys194 residue, in addition to four hydrophobic interactions with Val190, Leu155, and Tyr105. Carvacrol is primarily stabilized by hydrophobic bonds, including one π–π stacking interaction with the Tyr105 residue and nine alkyl‐π interactions with Cys194, Val102, His187, and Tyr109 residues. Finally, phenethyl alcohol interacts with the amino acid residues Val102, Val190, and Tyr105 through hydrophobic interactions and forms one hydrogen bond with the His187 amino acid. These findings suggest that the observed inhibition of ADP‐induced platelet aggregation is likely due to the combined action of carvacrol, eugenol, and phenethyl alcohol, which may exert their effects by modulating the activity of the P2Y12 receptor through direct interactions and interference with its function.

Since the extract also inhibited the AA‐induced aggregation, we performed molecular docking to assess its interaction with the COX‐1 receptor of AA. COX‐1 plays a crucial role in platelet activation and aggregation. It catalyzes the conversion of AA into prostaglandin H2 (PGH2), which is further converted into thromboxane A2 (TXA2) by thromboxane synthase. The formed TXA2 presents a potent promoter of platelet activation and aggregation [31]. Therefore, COX‐1 is an interesting drug target for the prevention of cardiovascular disease. Docking study with the COX‐1 receptor demonstrates that carvacrol had the strongest binding affinity with an energy of −7.2 kcal/mol, followed by eugenol (−6.3 kcal/mol). However, phenethyl alcohol had the weakest interaction at −4.5 kcal/mol (Figure 2A and Table 2). The interactions of these molecules within the active site of COX‐1 should be visualized to confirm binding modes and key residue contacts (Figure 3).

As visualized in Figure 3, the molecules tested bind within the active site of the COX‐1. Carvacrol binds to the target protein with various interaction types. Most of the binding is driven by hydrophobic interactions with key amino acids, such as Leu352, Ile523, Val349, as well as aromatic residues like Phe381, Phe518, Tyr385, Trp387, and Gly526, which create a lipophilic pocket that envelops the molecule. One significant interaction is a hydrogen bond between carvacrol and Met522, which helps anchor the compound in place. The docking analysis of eugenol with COX‐1 shows that it interacts stably with the enzyme's active site. One of the most important interactions is a hydrogen bond between eugenol and the Trp387 residue. In addition, eugenol is surrounded by several hydrophobic amino acids, such as His388, His386, and Ala202, which create a favorable nonpolar environment for its aromatic and alkyl groups. However, phenethyl alcohol reveals a relatively weak interaction profile compared to carvacrol and eugenol. The diagram shows only one hydrophobic contact, notably with Ala202, indicating minimal engagement with the active site. These findings suggest that the in vitro inhibition of the AA‐pathway is primarily attributable to carvacrol and eugenol, which demonstrate strong binding affinities and interactions with key residues of the active site.

The interaction between the major compounds of the TEO and the collagen receptor, GPVI, was also studied in silico to understand the mechanism of inhibition of collagen‐induced platelet aggregation. The interaction of collagen with the GPVI receptor initiates a signaling cascade, leading to stable platelet adhesion, granule release, and glycoprotein activation and is therefore a key element in hemostasis and thrombosis [32, 33, 34]. This glycoprotein is formed by two active sites: S1, defined by residues Glu40 to Arg60, and S2, formed by Ser43, Arg60, Ala63, Arg65, and Arg166 to Lys170 as reported previously [35]. As shown in Figure 1, the tested compounds bind to both S1 and S2 sites, while phenethyl alcohol does not interact with the amino acid residues of the S1 site.

As reported in Table 2, carvacrol and eugenol were found to be more stable than phenethyl alcohol, with binding energies ranging from −5.2 to −4.6 Kcal/mol.

As illustrated in Figure 4, Carvacrol (A) formed seven interaction bonds: one conventional hydrogen bond with Arg46 and several hydrophobic interactions with Leu41 and Leu53 residues, indicating good stabilization within a hydrophobic pocket of site S1 of the receptor. Concerning eugenol (Figure 4B), it exhibited a favorable binding pattern characterized by the formation of a hydrogen bond with Ser69, a π–anion interaction with Glu40, and multiple van der Waals and π–alkyl interactions with residues such as Arg67, Cys68, and Trp76. These diverse interactions suggest that eugenol fits well within the active site of the collagen receptor, achieving a stable and energetically favorable complex.

2D interaction diagrams of carvacrol (A), eugenol (B), and ethyl phenyl alcohol (C) within both active sites of the collagen receptor (PDB ID: 2gi7).

For the second site, S2, the docking interaction profiles of the three ligands reveal distinct binding interactions within this active site. Eugenol exhibited a strong interaction pattern, characterized by multiple hydrophobic contacts with residues such as Trp140, Trp171, Ala124, and Arg142, which collectively enhance its binding affinity and stability. Carvacrol also formed nine hydrophobic interaction bonds with the same amino acids, including Trp140, Trp171, Ala124, and Arg142 residues. Lastly, 2D interaction diagrams of phenethyl alcohol (Figure 4C) showed a simpler interaction profile, forming a hydrogen bond with Glu138 and a π‐alkyl interaction with Trp140. Phenethyl alcohol may have a moderate affinity to the GPVI receptor, or the interaction with S2 may be responsible for the inhibition. Indeed, in our previous work, we showed that compounds interacting with S2 exhibited an antiplatelet activity [30]. Overall, eugenol demonstrated the strongest binding affinity toward the collagen receptor, likely due to its ability to engage in both electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, followed by carvacrol and phenethyl alcohol.

Toxicity Assessment

After assessing the antiplatelet activity of T. tartonraira essential oil, toxicity assessment is necessary to guarantee its safety and support its potential as a therapeutic agent before further development. Therefore, we tested its toxicity on platelets, erythrocytes, and HEK293 cells.

We first tested the toxicity of the TEO on platelets, as the observed activity specifically targeted these cells, to ensure that the observed effect was due to true antiplatelet activity and not a result of platelet damage. Results shown in Figure 5 demonstrate that TEO did not promote significant reductions in platelet viability percentage, even at a high volume of 50 µL (93% of platelets are viable) compared with the control. These results suggest that the essential oil of the aerial part of T. tartonraira is not toxic to human platelets and may be explored as a potential therapeutic agent.

Conclusion

The current study investigates the phytochemical composition and biological potential of the essential oil of T. tartonraira (TEO), extracted from its aerial part. GC–MS analysis revealed that TEO is predominantly composed of alcohols and aldehydes, with phenethyl alcohol (25.69%), eugenol (12.42%), and carvacrol (6.41%) as the major constituents. Functionally, TEO exhibited potent antiplatelet activity, targeting key receptors such as P2Y12, COX‐1, and GPVI. These inhibitory effects were supported by molecular docking studies, which highlighted multiple hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions between the major compounds and the active sites of these receptors. Additionally, toxicity assessments confirmed that TEO is non‐toxic to platelets, erythrocytes, and HEK293 cells, supporting its potential as a safe and effective candidate for further pharmacological development.

Methods

Plant Material

The T. tartonraira aerial part was collected in March 2024 from Foussana, located in Central‐Western Tunisia. The species was identified by Pr. Mohamed Chaieb, and a voucher specimen was deposited in the herbarium of the Natural Substances Unit at the University of Sfax under the reference code LSCN 151.

Essential Oil Extraction

Approximately 500 g of powdered, dried T. tartonraira aerial parts were mixed with 4 L of distilled water and subjected to hydrodistillation for 4 h. The essential oil was extracted by liquid–liquid extraction using diethyl ether as the solvent. The organic phase was then dried under vacuum pressure, and the resulting oil was stored in amber‐colored glass vials in the dark until further use.

GC‐MS Analysis

For the phytochemical analysis, the essential oil was first diluted in diethyl ether, and 1 µL of the resulting solution was injected into the GC–MS system. The separation was performed using an HP‐5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 µm film thickness; 5% phenyl, 95% methyl‐poly‐siloxane) connected to a mass selective detector (MSD 5975B, 70 eV ionization energy). Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate. The injector and detector temperatures were maintained at 250°C and 280°C, respectively. The oven temperature was initially set at 100°C for 1 min, then gradually increased to 260°C at a rate of 4°C/min, and held for 10 min. Compound identification was achieved by comparing the mass spectra of the detected components with those in the Wiley 9th edition and NIST 08 mass spectral libraries. Biological Activity of T. tartonraira Aerial Part Essential Oil (TEO)

Isolation of Human Platelets

Human venous blood samples were collected into trisodium citrate (3.2 % w/v; 1:9 dilution) from five healthy volunteers who signed informed consents before donation. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Blood Transfusion Centre of Sfax in Tunisia (Approval number 0374/2021). Platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) and platelet‐poor plasma (PPP) were obtained by centrifugation of whole blood at 1200 and 3500 rpm, respectively, for 10 min, as previously described [30, 36].

Antiplatelet Activity

The antiplatelet aggregation activity of T. tartonraira essential oil (TEO) was evaluated by a turbidimetric method [37] using an aggregometer (Chrono‐Log). TEO was diluted in ethanol (v/v) to enhance miscibility. Samples of PRP (270 µL) were incubated with various volumes of TEO dissolved in ethanol (5–20 µL/mL), or ethanol (20 µL/mL) at 37°C for 15 min in glass cuvettes. Indomethacin was tested as a standard drug. Thereafter, 30 µL of agonist (ADP (20 µM), arachidonic acid (1 mM), or collagen (5 µg/mL)) was added, and aggregation was recorded during 5 minutes under stirring at 1100 rpm [30, 36].

Molecular Docking Studies

The molecular docking study of the main phytochemicals from the essential oil of T. tartonraira aerial part was conducted using AutoDock Vina, Viewer Lite, and Discovery Studio Visualizer software. The selected targets included the P2Y12 receptor, Cyclooxygenase‐1 (COX‐1), and glycoprotein VI (GPVI). The crystal structures of these targets were obtained from the Protein Data Bank, identified by the PDB ID codes 4pxz [38], 3n8w [39], and 2gi7 [32], respectively. In preparation for docking, water molecules and co‐crystallized ligands were removed from the structures, and Kollman charges were added to ensure accurate interactions. For the COX‐1, the grid box was fixed with dimensions of x = 40, y = 40, and z = 40, with the corresponding center coordinates of x = 30.708, y = −44.632, and z = −1.416. The active site of the GPVI receptor was set to x = 60, y = 42, and z = 62 with center coordinates of x = 15.639, y = 36.500, and z = 39.389. Regarding the P2Y12 target, the grid box was set to dimensions of x = 40, y = 40, and z = 40, with the corresponding center coordinates of x = 15.434, y = 0.381, and z = 52.034.

The inhibition constant (Ki) values were calculated from the docking binding energies at 298 K using the standard thermodynamic relationship implemented in the docking software.

Platelet Viability

Platelet viability was assessed by measuring the activity of the acid phosphatase released exclusively from damaged platelets, as described by previously [40]. Briefly, the PRP was treated with various volumes of TEO (5–50 µL) or 1% Triton for 30 min in a 96‐well microplate. Then, 5 mM of the substrate, p‐nitrophenyl phosphate (PNPP), was added to the wells, and the plate was incubated in the dark for 1 h at 37°C. The reaction was stopped with NaOH (2N), and the amount of the formed p‐nitrophenol was measured at 405 nm. Viability was calculated, considering 1% Triton‐treated platelets and untreated platelets to be 0% and 100% viable, respectively.

Hemolysis Test

Red blood cells were obtained by centrifuging whole blood at 2400 rpm for 10 min and prepared by washing the pellets three times with PBS. Then, erythrocytes were diluted to 1/10 of their volume with PBS solution and treated with the TEO (5–50 µL/mL). Distilled water and ethanol were set as positive and negative controls, respectively. The samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The amount of released haemoglobin in the supernatant is detected at 540 nm [30, 40].

Cytotoxicity Evaluation

Human embryonic kidney 293 cells (HEK293, RRID: CVCL_0045) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were grown in 25 cm2 cell culture flasks in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum, 1% L‐glutamine, and 50 µg/mL of gentamycin and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. After they reached confluence, the cells were seeded in a 96‐well microplate at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Thereafter, increasing volumes of the extracts (5–50 µL) were added, and the plaque was incubated for an additional 24 h. After incubation, 5mg/mL of MTT (3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2, 5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide) solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 4 h. Next, the upper solution was removed, and the formed formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a Varioskan flash plate reader (Thermo Scientific) [41].

Statistical Analysis

Data from this study are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS‐22 software (SPSS‐IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) using the unpaired t‐test and ANOVA tests. The p‐values < 0.05 (*) are considered statistically significant, p‐values < 0.01 (**) very significant, and p‐values < 0.001 (***) highly significant.
