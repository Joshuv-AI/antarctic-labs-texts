Genitopelvic pain in racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples in Canada and the United States: a scoping review
Authors: Caroline F Pukall, Olivia R Adams, Melody Garas, Trinda Penniston, Gillian Akenson, Sophie Bergeron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVery little research exists on genitopelvic pain in racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples despite its high prevalence among these groups. Existing frameworks of genitopelvic pain are based largely on White, heterosexual, cisgender women samples; applying these frameworks to minoritized samples may be prone to inaccuracies, as they do not account for minority stress and strength, social safety, and broader social determinants of health that structure exposure to pain, access to care, and health outcomes among marginalized populations.ObjectiveThe main purpose of this scoping review was to synthesize findings of genitopelvic pain research published on racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples in Canada and the United States.MethodsAfter removal of duplicates, the abstracts of 1330 articles were independently screened for eligibility for full-text review, of which 974 were excluded. Of the remaining 356, 59 studies were included for data extraction and synthesis. Results were organized according to the underrepresented sample of interest (racially/ethnically, sexually, or gender/sex minoritized) and subcategorized according to study aims focusing on prevalence, pain characteristics and experiences, minority stressors and strengths (eg, stigma, coping, resilience), social safety (eg, relational and healthcare contexts), and social determinants of health.ResultsResults indicated that genitopelvic pain is prevalent, there are similarities and differences in pain characteristics and experiences, and variables related to minority stress and strength, social safety, and social determinants of health—when examined—were associated with pain prevalence, severity, coping, and care trajectories in racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples.ConclusionGenitopelvic pain is common in racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized individuals and is meaningfully shaped by minority stress processes, minority strength adaptations, social safety, and social determinants of health, underscoring the need for theory-informed and equity-oriented pain frameworks.Keywords: genitopelvic pain, racialized groups, sexually minoritized groups, gender/sex minoritized groups, minority stress and strength, review

Introduction

The term genitopelvic pain includes a variety of conditions that can affect people of all genders involving pain in the genital and/or pelvic regions.1 Conditions include vulvodynia, anodyspareunia, and chronic pelvic pain (CPP), and etiologies vary from known to idiopathic causes. Genitopelvic pain is common, and condition-specific prevalence rates indicate wide ranges in cisgender women, cisgender men, and gender diverse samples. For example, prevalence rates for vulvodynia in cisgender women, anodyspareunia in cisgender men, and intercourse-related genitopelvic pain in transmasculine samples are 16%,2 14%,3 and 61.5%,4 respectively. To date, most research on genitopelvic pain has focused on White, heterosexual, cisgender samples,2 and this work has informed the development of clinical guidelines for assessment and diagnosis.5 However, emerging research has indicated that genitopelvic pain also affects racially/ethnically,6 sexually,7 and gender/sex minoritized individuals4,8,9 and that the experiences of these groups may differ from what has been established in the literature based on majoritized samples.1

According to minority stress theory (MST),10,11 individuals from underrepresented social groups experience unique, chronic, and cumulative stress related to stigma because of their minority status. Stressors consist of objective events (eg, discrimination) and the ensuing psychological responses to these events (eg, internalized shame); repeated experiences to stressors compound existing distress, worsen social inequalities, and affect health outcomes. Although originally conceptualized for sexually minoritized populations, MST has been expanded by some researchers to include racially/ethnically and/or gender/sex minoritized groups independently or simultaneously,12 termed by some as intersectional minority stress13,14 or multiple minority stress.15,16 The compounding effects of stressors put multiply marginalized groups at increased risk of negative mental health outcomes, like anxiety and depression,16 which themselves are risk factors for the development of genitopelvic pain.17 Within genitopelvic pain research, MST provides a framework for understanding how stigma-related stressors may contribute not only to psychological distress but also to pain onset, persistence, interpretation, and barriers to care.

Previous research on minority and multiple minority stressors have largely focused on deficit-based factors and associated negative health outcomes. Perrin et al.18 developed the Minority Strengths Model to theorize how personal and collective strengths can help mitigate negative health outcomes and/or promote health outcomes, specifically among sexually minoritized groups. Applying the Minority Strengths Model to genitopelvic pain allows for examination of how coping strategies, relational resources, and community knowledge may buffer the impacts of minority stress on pain experiences and outcomes.

In addition, Diamond and Alley12 added the concept of social safety to MST, defined as “reliable social connection, social belongingness, social inclusion and social protection”.12 It is important to note that minority stressors and strengths and social safety exist within a larger set of social determinants of health (ie, the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age that are impacted by access to power, money, and resources as well as policies, norms, and political systems19); these determinants have been shown to play a critical role in health outcomes, including sexual wellbeing, and they may shape the availability of social safety across interpersonal, community, and healthcare contexts.

Research on genitopelvic pain in racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples is limited.6,7,9 Two scoping reviews—one examining genitopelvic pain in racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples1 and another focused on vulvovaginal pain in racialized women20—documented high prevalence rates, misalignment between pain descriptors used by racialized samples and diagnostic guidelines, and a clear need for more intentional, in-depth research of these populations. In their global review, Pukall et al.1 synthesized 227 studies (5% [11/227] from Canada; 40% [91/227] from the United States; 55% [125/227] from other countries) categorizing findings based on how minoritized samples were positioned within each study (eg, studies published exclusively on genitopelvic pain in underrepresented samples or in which genitopelvic pain results were reported separately for at least one of the samples of interest). Although most studies centered on prevalence among heterosexual cisgender women, prevalence of estimates over samples ranged widely (2.4%-90.4%) and included studies on sexually minoritized women, women in same-sex relationships, painful receptive anal intercourse (RAI, also referred to as anodyspareunia) in men who have sex with men (MSM), and genitopelvic pain following gender affirming surgery in trans men. Most studies involving Arab/Middle Eastern women focused on vaginismus and unconsummated marriage, with many defining successful intercourse as the primary outcome, reflecting culturally specific priorities. Only 3% (7/227) of the articles included multiply marginalized samples.1

Garas et al.20 reviewed studies on vulvovaginal pain in racialized women across Canada, the United States, and the United Kingdom. All 17 included studies were conducted in the United States, and most of them focused on exclusively Black (47%, 8/17) or Hispanic (6%, 1/17) women. Their findings indicated elevated risk and greater pain severity among racialized samples, as well as associations between medical mistrust and healthcare-seeking behaviors, such as appointment cancelations and altered pain communicating to avoid reinforcing racial stereotypes.20

Although both reviews referenced MST and social determinants of health in their interpretations, neither explicitly extracted or synthesized findings within these frameworks. Social safety was briefly noted by Pukall et al.1 as future research direction, yet neither review incorporated social safety or minority strength into their synthesis. Therefore, the present scoping review aimed to synthesize evidence on the prevalence, characteristics, and experiences of genitopelvic pain among racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples in Canada and the United States, explicitly situating findings within with minority stress and strength frameworks, social safety processes, and social determinants of health as organizing and interpretive lenses. A scoping review methodology was chosen because it is most suitable for summarizing and synthesizing evidence in the literature, for identifying knowledge gaps that should be addressed in future research, and for drawing conclusions about the existing literature.21

Methods

Ethical approval was not required given that the review was conducted on published literature. The scoping review was planned and conducted in accordance with guidelines from the Joanna Briggs Institute22 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR23). The protocol was not registered.

The present scoping review represents an analysis of a subset of articles retrieved from a previous search conducted for a much broader and inclusive scoping review on genitopelvic pain in racially/ethnically, sexually, and sex/gender minoritized samples.1 Briefly, the previous scoping review was international in scope and inclusive of many formats of publications outside of peer-reviewed articles (eg, abstracts, dissertations, case studies), and it organized results according to how well represented the underrepresented groups were in the studies (eg, fully, partially). The present scoping review differs significantly from the previous one in many ways: it has been reduced in scope to focus only on peer-reviewed articles (not abstracts or dissertations); it includes research conducted in Canada and the United States only; it excludes case studies and case series; and the findings are organized according to each underrepresented group of interest and further thematically organized according to research findings focusing on the prevalence, characteristics, and experiences of genitopelvic pain themes explicitly mapped onto minority stressors and strengths, social safety, and social determinants of health when reported. The result is a more focused scoping review with a deeper emphasis on themes extracted from the reviewed literature, which are discussed within the frameworks of minority stressors and strength, social safety, and social determinants of health.

A PCC (population, concept, context) table provided the framework for the study. For population, all racially/ethnically minoritized (eg, Black, people of color, mixed race), sexually minoritized (eg, lesbian, gay, bisexual, asexual), and gender/sex minoritized (eg, trans, non-binary, intersex), groups were defined as the underrepresented groups of interest. We included only peer-reviewed studies featuring adult samples (no upper age limit), and all research designs (eg, quantitative, qualitative, and mixed method studies) were included. We included studies that focused on genitopelvic pain only in the underrepresented samples of interest, studies of any kind in which at least one genitopelvic pain outcome was the primary objective and in which results were reported separately for one or more of the underrepresented groups of interest, and studies of any kind in which genitopelvic pain was not a primary objective and in which results were reported separately for one or more of the underrepresented groups of interest. We excluded grey literature, published abstracts, dissertations, case studies or series, reviews, essays, monographs, and opinion pieces. We also excluded studies in which the results were not related to the participants themselves (eg, questionnaire development studies involving sexually minoritized samples). For concept examined, we focused on genitopelvic pain, sexual pain, dyspareunia, vulvodynia, vulvovaginal pain, and vaginismus. For context, our search was limited to studies from Canada and the United States, with no date or language restrictions. We included studies from all research settings (eg, community, university, hospital/clinic).

The search strategy was developed with the aim of being comprehensive (Supplemental Table A). Databases searched included Medline (1946-), Embase (1947-), and PsycINFO (1806-). The strategy was developed by co-author (initials withheld for blind review), an academic librarian who works with the (name of department withheld) department as well as the lead author (initials withheld), and a health sciences librarian was also consulted. The search strategy was fully developed in Medline and then translated into Embase and PsycINFO, adapting to the variations in controlled vocabulary. The challenge to developing the strategy was that the databases do not adequately or consistently index the concepts that are the focus of the present review and therefore the extensive use of keywords to supplement available subject headings was necessary. The resulting keywords were searched in the title, abstract and keyword fields. The only limit applied to the search results was to exclude children to maximize retrieval.

Articles retrieved from the search were imported into Covidence. The first author, who has expertise with scoping and systematic reviews, developed the PCC table with input from all co-authors, and she trained and supervised three reviewers on all aspects of the review process. The three reviewers independently conducted title and abstract screening and full-text review. Results were cross-checked on a regular basis, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. For each article, publication information (author name, date of publication, setting) and information about the study (eg, design, aim[s], methodology, sample, type of genitopelvic pain, main findings) were extracted.

Results

The search resulted in 1594 results, and 264 duplicates were removed. The abstracts of the remaining 1330 titles were independently screened for eligibility for full-text review, of which 974 were excluded. In total, 356 manuscripts were evaluated via full-text review and 116 were not retrieved because they did not meet inclusion criteria. The remaining 240 studies were assessed for extraction, where 165 additional studies were excluded. Because of the geographical restrictions specific to this study (ie, Canada and the US only), 22 articles were also later excluded for being outside of the geographical region of interest. Six studies were extracted from manual citation searches. Thus, 59 studies were included for data synthesis (Figure 1). Articles were organized into tables based on the underrepresented group of interest (racially/ethnically minoritized, Supplementary Table B; sexually minoritized, Supplementary Table C; and gender/sex minoritized, Supplementary Table D). Articles that featured more than one underrepresented group of interest were listed in the most relevant table based on the majority sample; no articles identified intersectional underrepresented samples (eg, people of color who were also simultaneously sexually and/or gender/sex minoritized in research based in Canada or the United States) as a sole or majority sample. As with Supplementary Tables B-D, the results section presents articles organized according to the underrepresented group of interest with subcategories reflecting general themes that best represent the results of the extracted literature. In the results and discussion sections, we use the terms utilized by authors of the articles when referring to genitopelvic pain conditions, underrepresented groups, and other information (eg, surgery techniques).

Studies focusing on racially/ethnically minoritized samples

Thirty-nine studies focused on racially/ethnically minoritized samples (Supplementary Table B). These studies varied in terms of design, with the majority being quantitative (77%, 30/39; mixed method: 5%, 2/39; qualitative: 18%, 7/39), and they ranged widely with respect to their primary aims. Most assessed the prevalence or incidence of genitopelvic pain as a focus2,24,25 or as a subdomain of sexual function26,27 in racially/ethnically diverse samples, with fewer focusing on pain characteristics, cognitive-affective correlates of the pain, and social factors—including those related to minority stressors and strengths—in exclusively racially/ethnically minoritized samples.6,28–30

Prevalence of genitopelvic pain

Twenty-one studies compared frequency of pain (eg, prevalence, incidence) of various forms of genitopelvic pain in diverse samples. More studies indicated higher (38%, 8/21)2,24,27,28,31–34 than lower frequency of pain in racially/ethnically minoritized samples (19%, 4/21).25,35–37 Ten percent (2/21) of studies reported different patterns of findings depending on the racially/ethnically minoritized groups included in the sample: Avis et al.26 found higher frequency of pain in the African American (vs White) sample and lower rates in the Chinese/Japanese (vs White) sample, and Reed et al.38 found higher rates in the Hispanic vs White sample and lower rates in the Black vs White sample. In addition, 24% (5/21) of studies found no differences in frequency of pain based on race.39–43 Finally, 10% (2/21) of studies focused on different aspects of sexual wellbeing in racially/ethnically minoritized samples and reported on pain frequency. One study investigated sexual dysfunction in a non-clinical sample of Black women in their 30s and found that very few (2%, 1/59) reported dyspareunia.44 Another study examined sexual wellbeing in menopause in older African American and Latina women living with HIV. Results indicated that painful intercourse due to vaginal dryness was a common symptom.45

Pain characteristics

Three studies delved into specific genitopelvic pain subtypes and symptoms in racially/ethnically minoritized participants and White participants. Nguyen et al.46 in their investigation of chronic vulvar pain subtypes and symptoms in Hispanic and non-Hispanic White participants, found that Hispanic women reported more primary vulvodynia (pain at first intercourse or tampon use) and were more likely to describe their pain as burning, an established symptom of vulvodynia.47 Brown et al.48 compared vulvar pain characteristics in Black and White women with clinically confirmed provoked vestibulodynia and reported that Black women described their pain more often as aching and less often as burning, stinging, and itching. Similarly, Azim et al.28 found that pain symptom descriptors used by participants of color were less aligned with traditional descriptors of genitopelvic pain (eg, burning, stinging, cramping, pinching) as compared to those used by White participants. In addition, one study compared pain descriptors of vulvovaginal pain in women of color (Hispanic/Latinx non-Black, Black Hispanic/Latinx, Black non-Hispanic/Latinx, Asian (non-Hispanic/Latinx), and Native American/American Indian (non-Hispanic/Latinx)). Results indicated an association between participants who reported communicating about their pain differently to healthcare providers as compared with close others and utilizing more healthcare resources before diagnosis and significant variation in pain descriptors by racial/ethnic group and context (eg, pain during pelvic exam or sexual activity involving penetration).49

In addition, three studies indicated that racially/ethnically minoritized samples have poorer pain outcomes than White samples. One found that Black women (vs White) reported more severe vulvar pain,50 another reported that Black race was associated with lower pain improvement in a trial examining the effectiveness of gabapentin for the treatment of vulvodynia,51 and still another reported that Asian women were more likely (than Euro-Canadian women) to report higher scores on a vaginismus subscale.52 The latter study also reported that in Asian women with high levels of mainstream acculturation, a later age of sexual debut was related to more complaints of vaginismus.

Correlates of the pain

Studies focusing on the impact of genitopelvic pain provide some understanding of how racially/ethnically minorized samples navigate and respond to their genitopelvic pain experiences, with some outcomes characterized by potential vulnerability factors (eg, sadness, anxiety) and others by resilience (eg, proactive coping). Three studies focused on cognitions, emotions, and coping in Black women.30,53,54 Results indicated that Black women experience a range of emotions in response to their sexual pain (eg, self-judgment, anger, sadness, anxiety)54 and tend to cope with it proactively (eg, engaging in non-penetrative sexual activities and sexual communication),53 with some endorsing or resisting the Superwoman Schema (ie, obligation to manifest strength, suppress emotion, and help others; resistance to being vulnerable or dependent, and determination to succeed despite limited resources).30 In addition, in a study examining sexual self-consciousness (defined as spectatoring, with sexual embarrassment and sexual self-focus as components) and sexual wellbeing outcomes in Black women,29 sexual pain was positively correlated with sexual embarrassment and sexual anxiety and negatively correlated with age. Finally, in a qualitative study of Black women with sexual anxiety, sexual pain was a common cause of sexual anxiety.55

Social and minority stress/strength factors and social safety

Many studies emphasized the importance of social safety12 within, for example, one’s community, healthcare settings, and peer and intimate relationships, as it relates to genitopelvic pain experiences. For example, West et al.56 reported that Black women who were sexually abused in childhood and who were revictimized in adulthood were more likely to report painful intercourse compared to those who were abused in childhood only.

Two studies examined disclosure of pain symptoms to a partner.57,58 Dogan et al.57 examined barriers to disclosing sexual pain to partners among Black women in a qualitative study. Five common themes emerged: distressing emotions associated with disclosure (eg, embarrassment, shame), limited knowledge and communication skills (eg, not knowing the cause of the pain), protecting partner’s feelings and ego (eg, to ensure their partner’s psychological comfort and sexual pleasure), concerns about privacy (eg, too personal to share), and taking sole responsibility for managing sexual pain (eg, the pain is not serious enough). In a quantitative study focusing on perceived intimate partner support in women of color (most of whom were African American or Black) with chronic vulvovaginal pain, Bennett-Brown et al.58 found that most partners were rated as supportive. This study also reported that increased partner support was associated with lower levels of sexual dissatisfaction and sexual distress. These results relate to the findings of another qualitative study of Black women with sexual difficulties, which found the theme “Talk that Talk”. This theme described participants’ desire for safe spaces in which to discuss sex with their peers and normalize conversations about sexual pain and pleasure.59

In addition, several studies captured social safety in terms of healthcare experiences. Two studies focused on healthcare-related experiences (medical mistrust and perceived discrimination in women of color, specifically Asian, Black, and/or Hispanic/Latinx,6 and patient-provider communication in a sample of Black women60), and one reported briefly on medical professionals as a helpful source of emotional support for women of color with chronic vulvovaginal pain.58 Despite this latter study indicating that about half of the sample listed medical professionals as being the most helpful source of emotional support for their pain, another study found that racially/ethnically minoritized women reported medical mistrust (in the form of suspicion) as common during their healthcare journeys.6 In this study on medical mistrust, Asian women reported higher suspicion scores than non-Hispanic/Latinx Black women, non-Black Hispanic/Latinx women reported higher scores than both non-Hispanic/Latinx Black women and Black/Hispanic Latinx women, and non-Black Hispanic/Latinx women reported the highest scores. Independent of race, higher suspicion scores were associated with longer time to treatment-seeking and diagnosis, visiting more medical professionals before receiving a diagnosis, higher likelihood of canceling a follow-up appointment, and lower likelihood of avoiding healthcare appointments due to anticipated pain. Higher perceived discrimination scores were associated with shorter time and visiting more medical professionals before receiving a diagnosis, greater likelihood of leaving an appointment early, and lower likelihood of canceling a follow-up appointment.6

In a model of patient-provider communication based on lived experiences of Black women with sexual pain,60 components consisted of provider preferences (eg, specific demographics, with Black women physicians being preferred), healthcare experiences (eg, self-advocacy and the pressure to be knowledgeable in an effort to prevent being dismissed and to combat anti-Blackness in the healthcare system), reasons for non-disclosure (which included the provider failing to initiate the topic and participants’ own dismissal and minimizing of their pain), provider responses to disclosure of sexual pain (which ranged from concern to dismissal), and treatment for the pain (which ranged from no treatment to hysterectomy). This model accounts for racial-gendered stereotypes and provider implicit bias as part of the larger context in which discussions of sexual pain occur. It is likely that traditional health care settings do not provide culturally sensitive care to members of racially/ethnically minoritized groups; indeed, Cherrington et al.61 examined the use of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) for symptoms of menopause (including pelvic pain) in a racially/ethnically diverse sample. Results indicated that racial/ethnic minorities used meditation, relaxation, and prayer more frequently than non-Hispanic White participants, who used non-CAM approaches more frequently than African Americans.

Summary of main findings

Genitopelvic pain was found to be highly prevalent and associated with poorer outcomes (eg, higher pain severity, sexual anxiety) in racially/ethnically minoritized samples, and sexual violence revictimization was associated with greater odds of reporting pain during intercourse. In addition, participants’ pain descriptors were either aligned with traditional descriptors of genitopelvic pain or not, depending on the groups studied. Although there was a desire for safe spaces in which to discuss the pain, communicating about the pain was challenging in interpersonal and healthcare contexts, the latter of which were characterized by medical mistrust and perceived discrimination, which were associated with more negative outcomes. Collectively, these findings illustrate how minority stressors (eg, discrimination, revictimization), strength-based coping, and compromised social safety within healthcare and relationships intersect to influence pain severity, communication, and care-seeking.

Studies focusing on sexually minoritized samples

Fourteen studies focused on sexually minoritized samples (Supplementary Table C). Studies varied in terms of sample (eg, sexually minoritized and majoritized, only sexually minoritized, including one to more than one genders/sexes), design (93%, 13/14 quantitative; 7%, 1/14 qualitative), and aim, with many focusing on prevalence of pain in their samples. Compared to the articles on racially/ethnically minoritized samples, there were none examining healthcare-seeking experiences.

For ease of synthesis, the remainder of this section is divided into studies that focused on sexually minoritized women and men (none focused on simultaneously sexually and gender/sex minoritized participants as a sole or majority sample). However, two studies included results that were not separated by gender, and they both reported on childhood adversity,62,63 which is related to social safety. First, Shirsat et al.62 examined childhood adversity in a sample of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer individuals who reported chronic pain (only percentages reported). Chronic pain in the pelvis was reported by 35.3% of the sample. History of any sexual trauma was found in 36.7% of the full sample and was associated with chronic pain in the pelvis, and CPP was associated with being touched sexually and being forced to have sex. Second, in a study of functional pain (defined as pain without known pathology) in the head, abdomen, and genitals in young adults of various sexual orientations (completely or mostly heterosexual, bisexual, mostly or completely homosexual) and sexual partnerships (males, females, both, none), Roberts et al.63 found that the higher risk of functional pains in sexual minority versus heterosexual youth was partially explained by higher exposure to childhood abuse, particularly during adolescence.

Sexually minoritized women

Prevalence of pain

Six studies examined the prevalence of various forms of genitopelvic pain in sexually minoritized women, taking differences between identities into account. Although Breyer et al.64—who examined sexual function in North American medical students of various sexual orientations (ie, homosexual, bisexual, heterosexual)—reported that homosexual and bisexual women had a lower prevalence of sexual pain compared to heterosexual women, numerous other studies have reported different patterns of results. Indeed, most studies focusing on the prevalence of genitopelvic pain in sexually minoritized women indicate that genitopelvic pain is a common complaint.

Blair et al.7 in their investigation of chronic vulvar and pelvic pain in women, found that bisexual women were more likely to report pain than heterosexual and lesbian women. Flynn et al.65 examined sexual function and satisfaction among sexual minority (ie, gay or lesbian, bisexual) and majority (ie, heterosexual) adults. In their study, gay or lesbian women reported lower vaginal discomfort than other women, and bisexual women reported higher vulvar (labial) discomfort and higher anal discomfort with sexual activity than other women.

In addition, Tabaac et al.66 examined the prevalence of CPP in a large (more than 6000 participants) sexually diverse sample of young women in the United States. Participants were categorized as completely heterosexual, mostly heterosexual, bisexual, and lesbian in terms of their sexual identity, and past sexual partner gender (only men, only women, both men and women, no partners) was also used for grouping (results discussed in the Social and minority stress/strength factors and social safety section). Results indicated that 44.3% (2726/6150) of participants reported ever experiencing CPP, with bisexual women reporting the greatest proportion of lifetime CPP, followed by completely heterosexual and lesbian women. In terms of differences in lifetime primary CPP distributions, compared to completely heterosexual women, there was greater lifetime risk of CPP among mostly heterosexual, bisexual, and lesbian women. Among women who had ever experienced CPP, mostly heterosexual and lesbian women did not differ from completely heterosexual women in experiencing pain in the last year. Furthermore, Roberts et al.63 found that prevalence rates of female genital pain were highest in bisexual, followed by heterosexual with same-sex contact, mostly heterosexual, gay/lesbian, and heterosexual respondents.

Pain characteristics

Few studies examined pain characteristics (eg, symptom frequency, intensity) and no studies examined pain descriptors in sexually minoritized women. Tabaac et al.66 reported that bisexual women more frequently endorsed “always” experiencing CPP as compared to other sexual identity groups. Although Blair et al.’s study7 did not reveal significant differences in pain intensity as a function of sexual identity, one study focused on sexual pain severity in sexual minority women with and without breast cancer after completion of active cancer treatment found that women with breast cancer reported higher pain intensity than women without breast cancer.67

Correlates of the pain

Two studies, both quantitative,7,66 examined pain correlates, specifically the impact of genitopelvic pain in sexually minoritized women. First, Blair et al.7 focused on relationship variables and found no significant group differences in the frequency of negative impact of the pain on participants’ relationships or on ending their relationship because of the pain. However, there was a significant effect of sexual identity with respect to the effect of the pain on their sexual relationships, with heterosexual women being more likely to report that the pain had an effect, followed by bisexual women and lesbian women. In addition, varying patterns of associations with communication and love were found for different sexual identities. In sexually minoritized women, higher communication scores were associated with lower levels of perceived effect of pain on sexual and overall relationship quality. In heterosexual women, greater love was associated with lower levels of perceived effect of pain on sexual and overall relationship quality. Second, Tabaac et al.66 focused on health-related quality of life. Results indicated differences by partner gender in health-related quality of life variables: women who had partnered with both men and women were more likely to report that the pain interfered with their social, work and school, and sexual activities as compared to women with only men as sexual partners.

Social and minority stress/strength factors and social safety

Two studies accounted for relationship factors (eg, relationship status, partner gender) in addition to identity. The results of Blair et al.’s study7 focusing on relationship status (same-sex or mixed-sex relationships, single) indicated that women in same-sex relationships were least likely to report pain as compared to women in mixed-sex relationships and single women. Within the group of bisexual women, those in mixed-sex relationships were most likely to experience pain than those who were single or in same-sex relationships, and when comparing bisexual and heterosexual women in mixed-sex relationships, bisexual women were more likely to report pain. In addition, the results of the study of CPP conducted by Tabaac et al.66 specific to past sexual partner gender (only men, only women, both men and women, no partners) indicated that women with both men and women sexual partners had the greatest proportion of lifetime CPP, followed by participants with only men partners, only women partners, and no partners. In terms of differences in lifetime primary CPP distributions, compared to women with only men partners, women with no partners were less likely to ever experience CPP, whereas women with both men and women partners were more likely to ever experience CPP. Among women who had ever experienced CPP, partner gender did not play a significant role in experiencing pain in the last year.

Summary of main findings

Genitopelvic pain was found to be common in sexually minoritized women and there were some unique associations with orientation/identity and social factors, including partner gender and relationship status. The overall pattern of results indicated that bisexual women and bisexual women in mixed-sex relationships (taking identity and partner gender into account) report more frequent pain and that bisexual women in mixed-sex relationships report more chronic pain and higher relational impact of the pain within comparisons with other sexually minoritized women. These patterns are consistent with minority stress and social safety frameworks, suggesting that relational context and identity-partner congruence may shape perceived safety, stress exposure, and pain impact.

Sexually minoritized men

The most common type of genitopelvic pain examined in studies of sexually minoritized men was painful RAI, also referred to as anodyspareunia; however, some studies assessed anal discomfort with sexual activity or genital pain more broadly. Most studies included information on prevalence of pain in their samples, with several also including information on a variety of factors (eg, social) associated with the pain. One study that examined overall sexual function in sexually minoritized men within their sexually diverse sample did not report on pain due to the lack of questions on anodyspareunia; this study did, however, report on sexual pain in sexually minoritized women (described above).64 Few studies examined pain characteristics in depth, and only one study was qualitative.68

Prevalence of pain

Roberts et al.63 reported that prevalence rates of genital pain for males were highest in bisexual, followed by heterosexual with same-sex contact, mostly heterosexual, heterosexual, and gay respondents. However, “genital pain” can refer to pain associated with and/or independent of sexual activity; thus, it is not clear what proportion of this pain would be attributable to sexual activity. In addition, some studies examined the prevalence of pain during sexual intercourse, pain during sex, and pain during sexual activity; however, it is not clear what specific sexual activities (eg, receptive or insertive anal sex) were of interest. For example, Hirschfield et al.69 in their study of pain during sex in MSM, reported a prevalence rate of 14%. Using more specific wording, Flynn et al.65 in their investigation of sexual function among sexual minority (ie, gay or lesbian, bisexual) and majority (ie, heterosexual) adults, found that bisexual and gay men reported a higher prevalence of anal discomfort during sexual activity than heterosexual men (60.1%, 59.4%, and 50.1%, respectively).

Damon and Rosser3 examined the prevalence of anodyspareunia in a sample of 404 MSM. Based on participants who indicated frequent and severe pain (“behavioral” criteria), 14% (55/404) were classified as having anodyspareunia. Using a different set of criteria (“clinical” criteria: participants who reported anodyspareunia during sexual activities more often than not, who were distressed or experiencing interpersonal difficulty due to the pain, and who reported that their pain was not exclusively due to involuntary tensing of the anus, lack of lubrication, substance or medication use, or a general medical condition), Damon and Rosser3 found that 10% (40/404) of the sample was identified as having anodyspareunia. This rate was similar to that reported in earlier studies by the same group. In one study, Rosser et al.70 examined anodyspareunia in men who engage in same-sex anal intercourse and found that 24% always or nearly always experienced pain and 11% reported severe or extreme pain. Taking these two variables (frequency and severity of pain) together, 12.5% experienced frequent and severe pain.70 In addition, Rosser et al.71 examined sexual difficulties, concerns, and satisfaction in homosexual men (defined as men who were attracted to other men). Participants had a lifetime and current painful RAI prevalence of 61% and 16%, respectively, and for painful insertive sex, lifetime and current prevalence was 14% and 3%, respectively.

The prevalence of painful sex has also been examined in sexually minoritized men with prostate cancer. Wheldon et al.72 examined the prevalence of anodyspareunia in gay, bisexual, and other MSM following treatment for prostate cancer. Results indicated that 42.1% (82/195) of participants experienced some pain following treatment. Although more than half (52.6%, 40/195) did not attribute the pain to their treatment, 33.4% (25/195) reported that their pain worsened since their treatment. When defining anodyspareunia as moderate to severe pain during intercourse lasting six months or more with mild to severe distress, 15.4% (30/195) of the total sample met this definition and 36.6% (30/82) of those who experienced painful receptive intercourse since their treatment met this definition.

Pain characteristics

Three studies reported on a variety of pain characteristics (eg, onset, location, descriptors) of painful RAI. Wheldon et al. in their study of anodyspareunia in gay, bisexual, and other MSM following treatment for prostate cancer, found that 35.4% (29/82) reported lifetime anodyspareunia, with 37% (30/82) reporting that they experienced pain frequently or always.72 Also, Yessick et al.73 conducted two studies, the first of which focused on pain characteristics of individuals with male-typical genitals who experienced anodyspareunia. Participants in both samples were of diverse genders and sexual orientations. The first study investigated characteristics of anodyspareunia and will be discussed here. Results indicated that the pain was experienced 44% of the time, with average pain intensity and unpleasantness ratings in the moderate range (about 4 on a scale from 0 to 10). The location of the pain was primarily at the anal opening and inside the anus, and the pain most often occurred during entry or during entry and thrusting as compared to after exit. More than half the sample never or rarely stopped anal sex due to pain. When asked to choose descriptors of the pain from a list, words spanned sensory (most common: sharp, stinging, and stabbing), miscellaneous (most common: tight, piercing, tearing), and evaluative (most common: annoying) categories of descriptors. Most participants reported that the pain started with their first experience of anal sex. Similarly, Damon and Rosser, in their sample of MSM, reported that most identified their pain as lifelong.3 In addition, 25% (14/55) of participants with anodyspareunia rated their distress due to the pain as extreme.

Perceived causes and impact of pain

Several studies examined perceived causes of pain. The most common reasons were inadequate lubrication,3,70 lack of oral or digitoproctal stimulation before penetration,3,70 penis size,3,70,72 depth and rate of thrusting (Rosser et al. 1998), roughness or duration of sex,72 level of sexual arousal,70 anal health problems (eg, hemorrhoids3,72), prostate cancer treatment,72 and psychological factors (unspecified,3,70 anxiety72). To better understand psychological factors in painful RAI, Yessick et al.73 examined the role of control cognitions (thoughts regarding the lack of control of one’s own body and situation during penetration) and sexual flexibility (how flexible people are with alternative sexual interactions when faced with challenges) on pain intensity and sexual distress in participants with anodyspareunia. Results indicated that control cognitions positively predicted intensity of anodyspareunia and sexual distress; in addition, sexual flexibility negatively predicted sexual distress.

In addition, Hirshfield et al.’s latent class analysis examining sexual dysfunction in a sample of MSM revealed four groups: no/low sexual dysfunction, erection problems/performance anxiety, low desire/pleasure, and high sexual dysfunction/sexual pain.69 The latter class was distinguished from the others by use of prescription and non-prescription erectile dysfunction medication before sex in the past year and use of club drugs. Participants associated with this class were significantly younger, single, more likely to report poorer physical and mental health, and more likely to have been diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection in the last year than participants in the no/low sexual dysfunction class.

Based on studies that reported on the impact of the pain, it appears that it can be significant. In Damon and Rosser’s study, many participants rated the interpersonal difficulty due to the anodyspareunia as moderate or high.3 In an exploratory analysis, men with anodyspareunia reported significantly more dissatisfaction with their most recent sexual relationship than men without anodyspareunia. In addition, most avoided anal sex for periods of time, with some reporting that it interfered with a sexual relationship and with developing new relationships. Wheldon et al.72 in their sample of gay, bisexual, and other MSM following treatment for prostate cancer, found that participants who met classification for anodyspareunia had higher odds of avoiding RAI because of the pain. Avoiding RAI because of the pain was associated with lower sexual satisfaction and lower sexual self-esteem; as well, sexual self-esteem was lower among participants with anodyspareunia.

Social and minority stress/strength factors and social safety

Some studies examined the associations between sociodemographic variables and the presence of pain. Age (lower prevalence and/or severity of anodyspareunia among older participants69) and education level (college graduates reported less lifetime painful insertive sex and less current painful RAI than nongraduates71) were associated with painful sex. The only qualitative study of sexually minoritized men focused on young MSM who had a history of childhood sexual abuse. Interviews focused on understanding components of physical sexual health (eg, age of first consensual sexual activity, perceptions of sexual health, routine medical examinations, sexual function, sexual pleasure) in this sample. Using interpretive phenomenological analysis, four themes resulted, one of which was recurring anal pain during sexual intercourse: most participants reported that this was a longstanding and continuous problem during RAI.68

Two studies examined the association of pain with minority stress (eg, internalized homophobia) measures. Rosser et al. found that social internalized homophobia (ie, social discomfort with other gay men) was positively associated with greater pain severity and a greater combined score of pain frequency and severity.70 However, in another study, men with anodyspareunia did not report significantly more social internalized homonegativity than men without, and they were not less out.3

Summary of main findings

Despite the lack of specificity surrounding the concept of “pain during sex” in some studies, pain during sexual activity—specifically painful RAI—appears to be a common, chronic, distressing, and impactful experience reported by sexually minoritized men. Sexual, psychological, and medical factors have been implicated as perceived causes of pain; sexual violence was associated with pain; and internalized homophobia as a component of minority stress has been studied in men, yielding mixed results. Although inconsistently measured, minority stress processes (eg, internalized homophobia) and disruptions to social safety within sexual and relational contexts appear relevant to pain severity, distress, and avoidance behaviors.

Studies focusing on gender/sex minoritized samples

Six studies included solely or primarily gender/sex minoritized samples (Supplementary Table D). Eighty-three percent (5/6) of studies examined genitopelvic pain as an outcome of a variety of gender affirming medical treatments in gender/sex minoritized individuals (eg, trans women, trans men, trans masculine individuals), with three focusing on surgery74–76 and two on hormone therapy (testosterone4,77). Seventeen percent (1/6) of studies focused on genitopelvic pain in intersex individuals (which also included a small number of trans people), and it examined surgical outcomes (vaginoplasty).78 Results will be discussed based on sample: gender/sex minoritized or intersex.

Gender/sex minoritized samples

Gender affirming surgery and other trajectories including surgery

All studies examining surgical outcomes in gender/sex minoritized samples focused on vaginoplasty.74–76 Most studies reported genitopelvic pain as a long-term outcome. Potter et al.74 reviewed the charts of 80 patients (most of whom were within two years of surgery, 82.5%, 66/80) who underwent vaginoplasty (via penile inversion, sigmoid colon procedure, other procedures, or unknown procedures) and found that 33.8% (27/80) of patients reported sexual function concerns, which included dyspareunia (33.3%, 9/27). In addition, Rehman et al.75 examined outcomes up to 3-year follow-up in 28 individuals who underwent vaginoplasty; although general (eg, satisfaction with esthetics) and sexual (eg, orgasm) outcomes were positive, one of the most common sexual complaints was dyspareunia. However, Stein et al.76 did not report significant pain post-surgery. They interviewed 10 individuals who underwent gender transforming vaginoplasty about their post-surgical function (ie, economic, social, sexual), and relevant medical chart data were extracted for an additional four individuals. With respect to dyspareunia outcomes, only 7% (1/14) of patients experienced frequent intercourse pain and 29% (4/14) reported infrequent pain.

Hormone therapy

Two studies focused on hormone therapy outcomes, specifically testosterone. Abern et al.4 examined the prevalence of vulvar pain and dyspareunia in trans masculine individuals who completed an online survey. At the time of the survey, 59.8% (468/782) were on testosterone. Results indicated that 61.5% (372/605) of participants reported pain with intercourse and 9.6% (68/710) reported itching, burning, or knife-like pain in the vulvovaginal area for at least three consecutive months. Dyspareunia was experienced by 59.1% (236/399) of individuals on testosterone as compared with 66.0% (136/206) of those not on testosterone. In addition, of those experiencing dyspareunia, most (63.4%, 236/372) were on testosterone. Itching, burning, or knife-like pain in the vulvovaginal area for at least three consecutive months was reported by 9.3% (42/452) of individuals on testosterone as compared with 10.1% (26/258) who were not. Of all participants who reported this symptom, 61.8% (42/68) were on testosterone. For participants who had ever been sexually active, there was no difference in the percentage of those reporting itching, burning, or knife-like pain and testosterone use. Finally, of all sexually active individuals with this symptom, 64.8% (35/54) were on testosterone.

One study examined pain characteristics within gender/sex minoritized samples undergoing hormone therapy. Goestch and Ribbink77 surveyed 100 trans men with a vagina who were using testosterone on their experiences with penetrative genital pain. Most respondents who engaged in vaginal penetration reported engaging in finger penetration (90%, 61/68), with few reporting penile (7%, 5/68) and object (3%, 2/68) penetration. Results indicated that 11.7% (11/94) reported avoiding vaginal penetration because of the pain, 51% (48/94) reported pain with vaginal penetration, with moderate penetration pain ratings (average of 53 on a scale from 0-100). Sixty-three percent of the sample reported that the penetration pain began after having started testosterone. In addition, 37% (36/98) of the sample reported pain with speculum insertion; 48% (48/100) reported avoiding delaying healthcare visits due to fear of speculum exams, with 50% (24/48) reporting that their fears related to previous pain experiences. Only 25% (21/83) of the sample asked their doctors about their penetration pain or pain with speculum exams. Of these participants, a variety of reasons were given to them, ranging from lack of lubrication to dryness and small vagina. Some participants reported that their physician gave them medications to help with the speculum exam (eg, oral anxiolytic, lidocaine) and a few (n = 8) reported using them at least “sometimes”.

Intersex samples

Hensle et al.78 examined outcomes of 57 patients who underwent bowel replacement vaginoplasty over a 23-year period, most of whom had Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser (MRKH) syndrome, a condition characterized by the absence or underdevelopment of the uterus and upper vagina. Results indicated that 8% (3/36) reported dyspareunia.

Summary of main findings

Vaginoplasty and testosterone therapy were associated with genitopelvic pain in gender/sex minoritized samples, and vaginoplasty was associated with pain in a predominantly MRKH patient sample. These findings point to healthcare systems as critical social determinants of health that can either exacerbate or mitigate genitopelvic pain through mechanisms of safety, validation, and access to appropriate care.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesized 59 peer-reviewed studies conducted in Canada and the United States examining genitopelvic pain among racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples, with explicit attention to MST, minority strength, social safety, and social determinants of health. These frameworks provide a coherent structure for understanding how social marginalization becomes embedded in genitopelvic pain experiences. Across these samples, genitopelvic pain was common, often chronic, and frequently associated with social and structural contexts that depart from dominant frameworks developed largely in White, heterosexual, cisgender samples. However, the depth and consistency with which minority stress, strength, social safety, and social determinants of health were examined varied substantially by group, revealing important conceptual and methodological gaps.

Findings from this review provide partial empirical support for MST10,11 as a useful framework for understanding genitopelvic pain, particularly among racially/ethnically minoritized individuals and sexually minoritized men. In racially/ethnically minoritized samples, minority stressors were most clearly reflected in studies documenting perceived discrimination,6 medical mistrust,6 and strained healthcare interactions,60 which were associated with delayed diagnosis, disrupted care trajectories, and poorer pain-related outcomes.6,60 These findings align with MST’s emphasis on chronic, socially produced stressors that operate upstream of health outcomes, and they extend MST application to genitopelvic pain.

Among sexually minoritized men, particularly MSM, components of minority stress (eg, internalized homophobia) were explicitly examined in relation to painful RAI, though findings were mixed. One study demonstrated positive associations between internalized homophobia and pain severity and distress, whereas another did not.3,70 This variability may reflect heterogeneity in how minority stress is operationalized, as well as the tendency for studies to prioritize psychological stressors while under-examining structural or relational contexts. Notably, sexually minoritized women and gender/sex minoritized samples showed a relative absence of explicit MST-informed analyses, despite high prevalence of genitopelvic pain.

Although deficit-based minority stressors dominated much of the literature, several studies identified strength-based responses to genitopelvic pain, particularly among racially/ethnically minoritized women. Black women, in particular, frequently described proactive coping strategies such as sexual communication, adaptation of sexual activities, and perseverance despite pain.53,59 These findings are consistent with the Minority Strengths Model18 and suggest that minoritized individuals actively mobilize personal and relational resources to manage pain in contexts where structural support is limited. Importantly, strength-based responses often arose in contexts of limited social safety and structural support, highlighting how resilience may coexist with unmet healthcare needs. At the same time, this strength was often embedded within culturally embedded gendered expectations, such as the Superwoman Schema, which simultaneously facilitated perseverance and inhibited disclosure of pain to healthcare providers and partners.56,60 Thus, strength functioned as a double-edged construct; it was protective in some contexts, but it reinforced silence and delayed care in others. Few studies conceptualized minority strength as a dynamic moderator of stress-pain pathways, highlighting an important direction for future research.

Social safety, defined as reliable social connection, inclusion, and protection,12 emerged as a unifying but often implicit theme across groups. In racially/ethnically minoritized women, social safety was most clearly compromised within healthcare settings, where mistrust, perceived discrimination, and anticipation of dismissal shaped patient–provider interactions and pain communication.6,60 These findings suggest that reduced social safety may amplify the impact of genitopelvic pain by limiting access to validation, diagnosis, and effective treatment. In sexually minoritized women, social safety manifested primarily through relationship context, including partner gender and relationship structure. Bisexual women (particularly those in mixed-sex relationships) consistently reported higher prevalence and greater impact of pain,7 suggesting that relational environments may differentially confer or restrict safety depending on partner dynamics and identity congruence. Among sexually minoritized men, avoidance of RAI due to pain and its association with reduced sexual self-esteem and relationship satisfaction3,72 further underscore the relational consequences of diminished social safety. Across groups, diminished social safety functioned as a mechanism linking minority stress exposure to prolonged pain, distress, and disengagement from care. Despite its relevance, social safety was rarely operationalized or measured directly, limiting the ability to test it as a mechanistic contributor to genitopelvic pain outcomes. This gap is particularly striking given that many pain experiences documented in the review unfolded in interpersonal or institutional contexts where safety is negotiated rather than assumed.

Social determinants of health were unevenly addressed across studies but consistently shaped genitopelvic pain experiences when examined. Structural factors such as access to affirming healthcare, education, socioeconomic status, and exposure to violence influenced both pain prevalence and care trajectories. For example, fear of speculum exams led many transmasculine individuals to delay or avoid gynecological care,77 illustrating how healthcare environments function as structural determinants of pain persistence and exacerbation. Similarly, associations between childhood sexual abuse and genitopelvic pain were documented across racially/ethnically and sexually minoritized samples, linking early-life adversity to adult pain outcomes.56,63,68 However, most studies treated these determinants as background variables rather than integrating them into conceptual models of pain, limiting causal inference and theoretical advancement. Treating these factors as core components of genitopelvic pain is essential for advancing equity-focused pain models.

Collectively, the findings of this scoping review underscore that genitopelvic pain in minoritized populations cannot be fully understood without accounting for minority stress processes, strength-based adaptations, social safety, and structural determinants of health. Current dominant models of genitopelvic pain risk obscuring these influences, particularly when diagnostic criteria and pain descriptors fail to align with minoritized individuals’ lived experiences.28,46,48,49 Future research should prioritize theory-driven measurement of minority stress and strength, direct operationalization of social safety, and structural analyses of healthcare systems as social determinants that actively shape genitopelvic pain trajectories. Methodologically, qualitative and mixed methods designs remain essential for capturing dimensions of pain that are shaped by marginalization yet remain invisible in standardized instruments. Clinically, the findings highlight the need for culturally responsive, affirming approaches to assessment and care that recognize both vulnerability and resilience in minoritized individuals living with genitopelvic pain.

Implications

Across studies of sexually minoritized men, the type and phenomenology of genitopelvic pain were often poorly specified, with most work focusing on painful RAI and only one study explicitly examining pain during insertive intercourse.71 This narrow and often vague operationalization limits the field’s ability to characterize the full spectrum of genitopelvic pain experiences in sexually minoritized men and constrains comparison across studies. Measurement practices further reflect this gap. One study of sexually diverse adults employed the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF),79 a widely used sexual function measure that unfortunately does not assess genitopelvic pain, in contrast to the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI),85 which includes a pain/discomfort subscale.80 This omission may have inadvertently hindered research trajectories addressing genitopelvic pain as a core dimension of sexual wellbeing in sexually minoritized men. Importantly, efforts to address this gap are emerging. For example, the Gay Male Sexual Difficulties Scale includes subscales assessing difficulties with both receptive and insertive anal intercourse,81 signaling progress toward measurement approaches that reflect lived sexual contexts.

More broadly, most self-report sexual function questionnaires are normed on majoritized populations and may fail to capture constructs salient to underrepresented groups, contributing to delays in recognition, diagnosis, and treatment of genitopelvic pain. Encouragingly, recent work has identified measures normed for gender minoritized populations,82 supported cross-cultural validation efforts (eg, the International Sex Survey),83,84 and introduced more inclusive tools such as the Inclusive Sexual Function Index, which includes items assessing genital discomfort and pain.85 However, instruments that comprehensively integrate genitopelvic pain with minority stress processes, social safety, and intersectional lived experience remain limited.

Future directions

Findings from this scoping review underscore the need for theory-informed, inclusive approaches to assessing sexual wellbeing (including genitopelvic pain) across racially/ethnically, sexually, and gender/sex minoritized samples. Future research should prioritize clear specification of pain type, location, and sexual context (eg, receptive vs insertive sexual activities), the development and validation of inclusive measures that explicitly assess genitopelvic pain alongside sexual function, and greater integration of minority stress and strengths, social safety, and social determinants of health into analytic models of pain. Given persistent measurement gaps, qualitative and mixed methods research that centers lived experience is essential,1 particularly as existing genitopelvic pain frameworks may obscure or mischaracterize the influences of discrimination, stigma, and structural exclusion on pain experiences in minoritized groups.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this scoping review. First, risk of bias was not assessed, meaning that lower-quality studies may have influenced conclusions. Second, the inclusive search strategy encompassed diverse methodologies and multiple forms of chronic genitopelvic pain, resulting in some imprecision regarding specific pain types and experiences. Third, the review was limited to studies conducted in Canada and the United States, restricting insights into genitopelvic pain within other Western and non-Western contexts.

Finally, we could not account for every possible minoritized social location. Although studies included multiply marginalized samples by race and gender (eg, women of color), none of the reviewed articles included multiple marginalized samples beyond race or gender (eg, queer or trans racialized women) as a sole or majority sample. As a result, little is known about how intersecting systems of oppression related to race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, and gender/sex identity shape genitopelvic pain experiences, presentation, or intervention.

Conclusions

This scoping review identified higher prevalence and severity of genitopelvic pain among underrepresented groups, differences in pain descriptor endorsement, and substantial gaps in knowledge regarding the roles of minority stress and strength, social safety, and social determinants of health in shaping pain experiences and outcomes. By synthesizing an otherwise fragmented literature, this review highlights critical conceptual and measurement limitations in current genitopelvic pain frameworks. Addressing these gaps will require inclusive, theory-driven research that captures sociosexual and structural factors insufficiently represented in existing models, ultimately supporting more equitable assessment and care for minoritized populations.
