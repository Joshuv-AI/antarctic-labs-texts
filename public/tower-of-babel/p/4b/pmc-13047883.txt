A Novel Mouse Model of Sympathoadrenal Catecholamine Deficiency by Ablation of the Tyrosine Hydroxylase Gene With Slc6a4‐Cre
Authors: Ryohei Ogawa, Ryo Fukumoto, Satoshi Hara, Nobuhiro Hayashi, Hiroshi Ichinose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCatecholamines (CAs) play important roles not only in the central nervous system but also in the periphery. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) is the rate‐limiting enzyme for CA synthesis and selective Th ablation in tissues of interest is useful to investigate the physiological function of locally produced CAs. Slc6a4 encodes serotonin transporter (SERT), a membrane transporter for serotonin reuptake, which is reported to be expressed in several peripheral tissues as well as serotonin‐producing cells. Here, we report a novel Th conditional knockout mouse line generated by Slc6a4‐Cre. The conditional knockout mice (cKO mice) exhibited predominant depletion of adrenaline in the adrenal glands, while noradrenaline contents were preserved at approximately 70% of control levels. TH expression was largely lost, although sparsely retained, in the adrenal medulla of cKO mice contrary to unaltered expression of TH in the brain. In addition to the adrenal gland, TH expression was lost in approximately 80% of sympathetic neurons in the superior mesenteric ganglion of cKO mice. Plasma noradrenaline levels were significantly reduced, although plasma dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) levels were comparable to those of controls. Bradycardia and changes in heart rate variability in cKO mice suggested impaired sympathoadrenal catecholaminergic regulation of the heart. The present data highlight the value of these cKO mice as a useful tool for elucidating the properties of the sympathoadrenal catecholaminergic system independently of the brain noradrenergic system. Keywords: catecholamine, conditional knockout mouse, serotonin transporter, sympathoadrenal system, tyrosine hydroxylase

Introduction

Catecholamines (CAs) play important roles not only in the central nervous system but also in the periphery. Noradrenaline (NA) and adrenaline (AD) are the primary signaling molecules of the sympathoadrenal system: sympathetic neurons release NA onto peripheral organs as a neurotransmitter, and chromaffin cells in the adrenal medulla secrete NA and AD into the bloodstream as hormones. Dopamine (DA) also plays several physiological roles in the periphery. For instance, DA inhibits insulin secretion from pancreatic β‐cells (Farino et al. 2020; Aslanoglou et al. 2021; Simpson et al. 2012). Sympathoadrenal catecholaminergic regulation is necessary to orchestrate peripheral organ activity. Although studies on the sympathoadrenal system have been performed for a long time, many issues remain unresolved and warrant reexamination using modern experimental techniques (Martinez‐Sanchez et al. 2022).

DA, NA, and AD are products of a series of enzymatic reactions from tyrosine. Tyrosine is converted to AD via dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA), DA, and NA through enzymatic reactions by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), aromatic L‐amino acid decarboxylase (AADC), dopamine β‐hydroxylase (DBH), and phenylethanolamine N‐methyltransferase (PNMT), respectively. Because TH is the rate‐limiting enzyme in this pathway (Nagatsu et al. 1964), we targeted Th for genetic modification and generated Th‐flox mice harboring a Th gene flanked by loxP sites (Tokuoka et al. 2011). Previously, we crossed Th‐flox mice with DBH promoter‐driven CreERT2 mice to disrupt the Th gene in NA‐ and AD‐producing cells (Miyajima et al. 2021). Analysis of this mouse model revealed the contribution of the sympathoadrenal system to CA content in peripheral organs, demonstrating the utility of a genetic animal model (Miyajima et al. 2021).

In the above study, we found TH‐ and AADC‐immunoreactive cells in the corpus mucosa of the stomach and the epithelial cells of the lung, suggesting local production of DA in the cells of peripheral tissues (Miyajima et al. 2021). Tschaffon‐Müller et al. also reported that local DA signaling was involved in negative stress effects on bone growth through enhanced expression of TH in neutrophils (Tschaffon‐Müller et al. 2023). Because cell‐ or tissue‐selective destruction of Th is an ideal tool to analyze the function of locally produced DA, we have generated several Th conditional knockout mouse lines by crossing Th‐flox mice with Cre‐driver mice to ablate Th in peripheral tissues.

Here, we report a line crossed with Slc6a4‐Cre as a novel mouse model exhibiting sympathoadrenal catecholamine deficiency. Slc6a4 encodes serotonin transporter (SERT), a membrane transporter for serotonin (5‐HT) reuptake, which is reported to be expressed in the peripheral tissues as well as 5‐HT‐producing cells including enterochromaffin cells of the gastrointestinal system. To our surprise, TH‐immunoreactive cells greatly decreased in the adrenal medulla of the conditional knockout mice (cKO mice) whereas TH expression in the brain was not altered. Moreover, we observed loss of TH in the large population of sympathetic neurons. Electrocardiography revealed bradycardia and alteration of heart rate variability (HRV), suggesting impairments of sympathoadrenal catecholaminergic regulation on the heart. These results indicate that cKO mice can be a useful animal model to explore the nature of CA signals in the sympathoadrenal system independent of the control from the brain.

Materials and Methods

Animals

The Slc6a4‐Cre mouse, a BAC transgenic mouse in which Cre recombinase is under the control of the Slc6a4 promoter (Arakawa et al. 2014), was provided by RIKEN BRC through the National BioResource Project of the MEXT/AMED, Japan (RBRC10598). Slc6a4‐Cre/Th fl/fl mice (cKO mice) were generated by crossing Slc6a4‐Cre mice with Th‐flox mice (Tokuoka et al. 2011). Th fl/fl mice (Ctrl mice) served as the control group. Totally, 31 mice (Ctrl, 15 mice; cKO, 16 mice) were subjected to the experiments in this paper. Both Slc6a4‐Cre and Th‐flox mouse strains share the same genetic background as the C57BL/6J strain. Genotyping was conducted by polymerase chain reactions using 5′‐GTCTCCATAACACTGAGAGGAG‐3′ and 5′‐TTGGTCGTGGCAGCCCGGAC‐3′ for the Slc6a4‐Cre allele and 5′‐CATTTGCCCAGTTCTCCCAG‐3′ and 5′‐AGAGATGCAAGTCCAATGTC‐3′ for the Th‐flox allele. Mice were maintained in temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled cages (2–4 mice/cage) under a 12‐h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. All experiments were conducted during the light cycle. This study was approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the Institute of Science Tokyo (D2019008, D2022013) and performed in accordance with the Regulations on Animal Experimentation at the Institute of Science Tokyo.

Biochemical Analyses

Male mice aged 14–16 weeks were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane anesthesia (4% in air, induction; 2% in air, maintenance). Blood was collected from the right ventricle with a heparinized syringe and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min at 4°C to obtain plasma. Mice were euthanized by blood removal followed by decapitation under isoflurane inhalation. The whole brain, pancreas, spleen, bilateral adrenal glands, stomach, duodenum (2 cm long from the stomach), colon (2 cm long from the appendix), heart, and bilateral lungs were also collected. Dissected samples, including plasma, were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until use.

CA Analysis

CA levels in peripheral organs were measured as previously described (Miyajima et al. 2021). Briefly, dissected organs were homogenized with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.25 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM pargyline, 1 μg/mL pepstatin, 1 μg/mL leupeptin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. After deproteinization with 0.2 M perchloric acid containing 0.1 mM EDTA and neutralization, the CA in the homogenates was purified with aluminum oxide (cat. no. Art1078; Merck). Separation and detection of CA were performed using HPLC–ECD (ECD700, Eicom) with a reverse‐phase ODS column (SC5‐ODS, Eicom). In addition to CA, 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC) was measured as a metabolite of DA at the same time. Protein concentrations were measured by the Bradford method. CA levels in the brain and adrenal glands were measured without aluminum oxide purification.

Western Blotting

Western blotting was also performed as previously described (Miyajima et al. 2021). Homogenization of the organs and quantification of protein concentrations were performed according to the methods described in CA analysis section. Thirty micrograms of total protein (3.75 μg for adrenal glands) from tissue homogenates were subjected to 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk in Tris buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS‐T), the membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies diluted with 5% skim milk in TBS‐T: rabbit anti‐TH (1:5000; made in our laboratory), mouse anti‐β‐actin (1:10 000; RRID: AB_476744; Sigma–Aldrich), and rabbit anti‐glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) antibody (1:10 000; RRID: AB_1080976; GeneTex). The following horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies were used: anti‐rabbit IgG (1:5000; RRID: AB_772191; GE Healthcare) or anti‐mouse IgG (1:10 000; RRID: AB_772193; GE Healthcare). The peroxidase reactions with Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (cat. no. WBKLS0500; Merck Millipore) were detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Ez‐Capture MG, ATTO). Analysis of acquired images were conducted with Fiji version 1.53 t (Schindelin et al. 2012). Uncropped images of membranes were shown in Figure S1.

Anti‐TH antibody was produced by immunization of the purified GST‐fusion protein consisting of N‐terminal 82 amino acids of human TH. Rabbit antisera were affinity‐purified with the antigen and used as anti‐TH antibody (1 mg/mL).

Immunohistochemical Analysis

Whole‐Mount Staining

TH in sympathetic axons innervating the heart was visualized using a previously reported method (Jungen et al. 2017) with some modifications. Briefly, 12–16‐week‐old female mice were perfused with 0.9% NaCl followed by 4% paraformaldehyde through the left ventricle under inhalation of isoflurane anesthesia (4% in air, induction; 2% in air, maintenance). The heart was dissected and postfixed overnight with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C. After incubation in Dent's bleach (methanol: hydrogen peroxide: DMSO = 4:1:1) at 4°C for 1 week, the tissue was rehydrated in PBS, and the heart was incubated overnight with PBS containing 5% normal goat serum (NGS) and 1% Triton X‐100 (blocking buffer) at 4°C. The heart was then incubated with rabbit anti‐TH antibody (1:2000; made in our laboratory) diluted in blocking buffer, followed by biotinylated goat anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (1:2000; RRID: AB_2313606, Vector Laboratories) diluted in blocking buffer. After incubation with avidin and biotinylated peroxidase complex (Vectastain Elite ABC kit; RRID: AB_2336827; Vector Laboratories) for 3 h, 0.2 mg/mL 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride with 0.002% hydrogen peroxide was applied as a peroxidase substrate to detect the complex. Images were acquired with a stereo microscope (M125C with MC190HD, Leica).

To stain the superior mesenteric ganglion (SMG), 6‐month‐old mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. SMGs were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C and then stored in PBS containing 0.02% sodium azide at 4°C until use. After blocking, samples were incubated with mouse anti‐TH antibody (1:2000; RRID: AB_2201526; Merck Millipore) at 4°C for 4 days and then with rabbit anti‐AADC antibody (1:2000; made in our laboratory) at 4°C for 4 days. Subsequently, SMGs were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated anti‐mouse IgG antibody (1:2000; RRID: AB_2534088; Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 633‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (1:2000; RRID: AB_2535733; Invitrogen) at 4°C for 4 days. Images were acquired with a confocal microscope (LSM780, Carl Zeiss). In each mouse, TH‐immunoreactivity of 40–90 AADC‐positive neurons was examined for the quantification of the ratio of TH‐positive neurons.

Cryosection Staining

Under inhalation of isoflurane anesthesia (4% in air, induction; 2% in air, maintenance), 6‐month‐old male mice were perfused with 0.9% NaCl followed by 4% paraformaldehyde through the left ventricle. The left adrenal gland was dissected and postfixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C. After cryoprotection with 30% sucrose, the adrenal gland was embedded first in 10% gelatin and then in OCT compound (cat. no. 45833; Sakura Finetek). Frozen sections (40 μm thick) were prepared and mounted on APS‐coated slides. Sections were blocked with 5% NGS in PBS for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with rabbit anti‐TH antibody (1:2000; made in our laboratory) diluted in PBS containing 1% NGS and 0.1% Triton X‐100, followed by biotinylated goat anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (1:2000; RRID: AB_2313606; Vector Laboratories). After incubation with avidin and biotinylated peroxidase complex (Vectastain Elite ABC kit; RRID: AB_2336827; Vector Laboratories) for 30 min, 0.2 mg/mL 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride with 0.002% hydrogen peroxide was applied as a peroxidase substrate to detect the complex. Images were acquired with a microscope (APX100, EVIDENT).

Electrocardiography (ECG)

Twenty‐week‐old mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 3% isoflurane in air (1.2 L/min) for 1.5 min and fixed in the supine position while anesthesia (1.5% isoflurane in air) was maintained. Noninvasive electrodes were placed on the dorsal paws in the lead II configuration using CardioCream (Z‐101BC, Nihon Kohden). ECG signals were amplified with a Memory Oscilloscope (VC‐11, Nihon Kohden) equipped with a hum filter (50 Hz) and a bandpass filter. Amplified ECG signals were recorded for 3 min at a 10‐kHz sampling rate by WaveForms (Digilent). R, S, and J peaks were detected automatically with MATLAB, and the mean heart rate over 3 min was calculated. To perform frequency analysis on HRV, R–R intervals were resampled at 100 Hz with spline interpolation. Power spectra of HRV were obtained by fast Fourier transform. Low frequency components (LF; 0.15–1.5 Hz) and high frequency components (HF; 1.5–5 Hz) were calculated and normalized to the total frequency component (TF; 0.15–50 Hz), as a marker of both sympathetic and parasympathetic neuronal activity and parasympathetic neuronal activity, respectively (Thireau et al. 2008). All ECG analyses were performed with MATLAB version R2023b.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as individual data and their mean. All statistical analyses were conducted with R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team 2020), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No blinding, randomization, sample size calculation, and test for outliers were performed. No exclusion criteria were predetermined. Sample size was determined based on the previous study (Miyajima et al. 2021). Normality and homoscedasticity were not tested due to the small sample size, and a two‐tailed Welch's t‐test was used to compare the mean values of the two groups as the robust test for small samples (Ruxton 2006; Fagerland and Sandvik 2009). Full statistical reports were shown in Table S1.

Results

Predominant AD Depletion in the Adrenal Gland of Slc6a4‐Cre/Th fl/fl Mice

We generated Slc6a4‐Cre/Th fl/fl mice (cKO mice) by crossing Slc6a4‐Cre mice (Arakawa et al. 2014) with Th‐flox mice (Tokuoka et al. 2011). The cKO mice were born to adults and appeared normal. We performed biochemical analyses of the cKO mice.

We found a marked reduction of TH protein in the adrenal glands of cKO mice compared with that of Ctrl mice (Th fl/fl mice) (Figure 1a,b). Immunohistochemical staining also revealed loss of TH signals in a large proportion of the adrenal medulla (Figure 1c). However, some adrenal chromaffin cells in cKO mice retained TH expression (Figure 1c). CA analysis revealed that AD content in the adrenal gland of cKO mice drastically decreased to ~2% of that in the Ctrl mice, whereas NA content remained at ~70% of Ctrl (Figure 1d,e). The intermediate product, DA, in the adrenal glands of the cKO mice severely decreased (Figure 1f).

Alteration of Plasma CA Contents

The adrenal medulla secretes NA and AD into the bloodstream. Therefore, we next measured circulating plasma CA levels in cKO mice to evaluate the effect of TH loss in the adrenal medulla. Plasma NA content in cKO mice reduced to 13% of Ctrl (Figure 2a). Plasma AD levels tended to decline, but the difference did not reach statistical significance due to the high variability of Ctrl mice (Figure 2b). These reductions in NA and AD suggest impaired hormonal catecholaminergic regulation on peripheral organs in cKO mice. DOPA content in the plasma was not changed obviously (Figure 2c), suggesting that the main source of plasma DOPA was different from that of plasma NA or AD. Although plasma DA levels were comparable between genotypes, its metabolite, 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC), was significantly decreased (Figure 2d,e).

Horizontal bar represents the mean for each experimental group. Control, n = 4 mice; conditional knockout, n = 5 mice. *p < 0.05; ***p < 0.001; Welch's t test.

Th Ablation in Sympathetic Neurons and Other Tissues

We next investigated TH expression in sympathetic neurons, the other major source of peripheral NA. TH expression in the SMG was examined by immunohistochemistry (Figure 3). AADC was used as a marker of sympathetic neurons. In the SMG of cKO mice, ~80% of AADC‐positive sympathetic ganglion cells were TH negative, suggesting that TH ablation occurred in sympathetic neurons. Notably, TH expression was maintained in ~20% of sympathetic neurons of cKO mice.

Loss of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression in sympathetic neurons. Immunohistochemical staining for TH (green) and aromatic amino acid decarboxylase (AADC; magenta) in the superior mesenteric ganglion. Scale bars, 50 μm. n = 3 mice.

Additionally, TH in sympathetic nerve terminals on the heart was visualized by whole‐mount staining (Figure 4a). TH‐positive fibers were observed on both the anterior and posterior sides of the heart in Ctrl mice. In contrast, only a few very weak TH‐positive fibers were detected in the hearts of cKO mice. TH reduction in the heart was confirmed by western blot analysis (Figure 4b,c). These results demonstrated that TH expression in cKO mice was lost not only in the adrenal gland but also in a large population of sympathetic neurons. As expected, the NA levels in the hearts of the cKO mice significantly decreased to 5% of Ctrl mice (Figure 4d), suggesting that sympathetic noradrenergic regulation of the heart was impaired in the cKO mice.

Consistent with the loss of CA in the heart, NA contents in other peripheral organs were relatively lower in cKO mice than in Ctrl mice, significantly in the pancreas, spleen, and stomach (Figure 5). DA contents also showed a decreasing trend, significantly in the pancreas, spleen, lung, and duodenum (Figure 5). These results suggest that a deficiency of CA signals from sympathetic neurons is common in the peripheral organs of cKO mice.

We next examined whether TH expression and CA content were affected in the brains of cKO mice. Western blotting revealed comparable TH expression in the brain (Figure 6a,b). Immunohistochemistry for TH and AADC also confirmed preservation of TH expression in the mesencephalic DA neurons and NA neurons in the locus coeruleus (Figure S2). In line with TH expression, brain NA and DA levels were not altered in cKO mice (Figure 6c,d). 5‐HT levels also did not change as expected (Figure 6e). Because the serotonin transporter gene is expressed mainly in serotonin‐producing neurons in the brain, these results suggest that Th is destroyed in the serotonergic neurons but not in the catecholaminergic neurons.

Dysfunction of the Sympathoadrenal System

To assess the function of the sympathoadrenal system in cKO mice, heart rate (HR) was measured by ECG. HR under isoflurane anesthesia was significantly lower in cKO mice than in Ctrl mice (Figure 7a,b). Additionally, frequency analysis of heart rate variability (HRV) was performed to assess the activity of sympathetic neurons in cKO mice. The normalized LF of HRV, which is affected by both sympathetic and parasympathetic neurons, was significantly increased in cKO mice (Figure 7c). In contrast, the normalized HF, which is proposed as a marker of parasympathetic neuronal activity, decreased (Figure 7d). Therefore, the LF/HF ratio, proposed as a marker of sympathetic neuron activity, increased significantly in cKO mice (Figure 7e). These ECG results indicate an impaired balance between sympathetic neurons and parasympathetic neurons in cKO mice.

Horizontal bar represents the mean for each experimental group. (b–d) n = 4 mice. *p < 0.05; Welch's t test.

Discussion

Here, we report the generation of Th cKO mice by crossing Th‐flox mice with Slc6a4‐Cre mice. Contrary to mid‐gestational lethality of global Th knockout mice (Zhou et al. 1995), our cKO mice can survive to adults without any abnormal appearance under normal conditions. TH expression was lost in a large population of adrenal chromaffin cells and sympathetic neurons. We also found AD depletion in the adrenal gland. We revealed that cKO mice showed bradycardia. These results indicated that sympathoadrenal CA signals were downregulated in the periphery of cKO mice. On the other hand, TH expression and CA contents in the brains of cKO mice were comparable to those of Ctrl mice (Figure 6 and Figure S2).

Previous studies established and used several animal models exhibiting suppression of sympathoadrenal CA signals, such as adrenalectomized and chemical sympathectomized models (Ahrén and Lundquist 1981), global DBH‐null mice (Ste Marie et al. 2005; Ste Marie and Palmiter 2003; Alaniz et al. 1999), and mice with genetically ablated sympathetic innervation (Borden et al. 2013). However, this cKO mice line is unique to those models in that cKO mice showed severe CA deficiency (including dopamine) in both sympathetic neurons and adrenal chromaffin cells, whereas neither sympathetic innervation nor central catecholaminergic system were modulated. Therefore, our cKO mice can be a useful animal model to explore the nature of sympathoadrenal catecholaminergic regulation on the peripheral tissues.

We observed dispersed TH loss in the adrenal medulla (Figure 1c). CA measurements revealed predominant depletion of AD (~2% of Ctrl) in the adrenal glands (Figure 1e). Although expression of Cre driven by the Slc6a4 promoter may not be identical to intrinsic SERT protein, previous studies demonstrated that SERT is immunohistochemically colocalized with PNMT or AD (Schroeter et al. 1997; Verhofstad and Jonsson 1983), and 5HT immunoreactivity was observed in adrenal PNMT‐positive cells (Verhofstad and Jonsson 1983), suggesting that Slc6a4 is specifically expressed in the adrenal AD‐producing cells. The selective Slc6a4 expression may be a cause of predominant AD depletion in the adrenal gland of cKO mice.

In addition to the adrenal gland, Th expression was greatly decreased in the sympathetic neurons in SMG (Figure 3). Notably, TH‐positive sympathetic neurons remained in the SMG of cKO mice. A recent study revealed the molecular heterogeneity of sympathetic neurons, which was relevant to projection targets (Wang et al. 2024). Previous in situ hybridization analysis of the rat superior cervical ganglion suggested that approximately 30% of cells in the superior cervical ganglion express Slc6a4 (Nishimura et al. 1999). Our result might reflect the heterogeneity of gene expression pattern in sympathetic neurons in the SMG. Dr. Currie's group proposed that SERT modulates serotonergic regulation on the adrenal chromaffin cells (Brindley et al. 2017). Our results also implied the serotonergic control of not only adrenal chromaffin cells but also sympathetic neurons, although it should be noted that TH ablation in cKO mice does not fully reflect the current expression of Slc6a4.

Adrenal chromaffin cells release NA and AD into the bloodstream as hormones. We found that plasma NA levels in cKO mice decreased to 13% of Ctrl levels, even though adrenal NA levels in cKO mice were maintained at ~70% of Ctrl (Figures 1d and 2a). The contribution of adrenal medulla to circulating NA was estimated to be small because plasma NA levels did not decrease in patients who underwent adrenalectomy, and the difference in venous and arterial NA levels across the adrenal gland was much smaller than that of AD levels (Goldstein et al. 1983; Toleikis and Godin 1995; Brown et al. 1981). Our data were in good agreement with those reports and confirmed the limited contribution of adrenal NA‐producing cells to plasma NA levels. Because TH is lost in a large proportion of sympathetic neurons, sympathetic neurons should be a main source of plasma NA.

Plasma DOPA, which can be taken up by cells via amino acid transporters, is an essential source of DA in some peripheral tissues expressing AADC, such as the proximal tubules in the kidney (Harris and Zhang 2012; Soares‐da‐Silva 1994). Sympathetic neurons have been proposed to supply DOPA to the circulation (Goldstein et al. 1987; Kvetnanský et al. 1992). However, we previously reported that plasma DOPA levels under pentobarbital anesthesia were not decreased in mice with tamoxifen‐induced Th ablation controlled by the Dbh promoter (Miyajima et al. 2021). In line with this finding, plasma DOPA levels did not significantly change in cKO mice under isoflurane anesthesia (Figure 2c). These results indicate that sympathetic neurons are not the primary source of basal plasma DOPA. Since the genetic background of cKO mice is of C57BL/6J mice, the plasma DOPA detected may be derived from tyrosinase (Rios et al. 1999).

To evaluate sympathetic neuronal function, we conducted ECG analysis. Heart rate decreased significantly in cKO mice (Figure 7a,b). PNMT‐null mice did not exhibit any changes in heart rate under light isoflurane anesthesia (Bao et al. 2007), suggesting that bradycardia in cKO mice was caused by a reduced NA signaling from sympathetic neurons. However, HRV frequency analysis revealed a higher LF/HF ratio, which is commonly used as a marker of sympathetic neuron activity (Thireau et al. 2008) (Figure 7e). Similar results were obtained from ECG analysis in Spr‐KO mice, which showed systemic NA deficiency due to the lack of tetrahydrobiopterin, an essential cofactor of TH (Sumi‐Ichinose et al. 2017). These results suggest that congenital deficits in CA synthesis in sympathetic neurons might induce dominant sympathetic states due to some compensatory mechanism(s).

This study has several limitations. First, it should be noted that TH ablation in cKO mice does not reflect the current expression of Slc6a4. In a separately established mouse line harboring Slc6a4‐Cre, prenatal recombination was widely observed (Narboux‐Nême et al. 2008), suggesting that the Th gene is congenitally absent in tissues that do not express SERT in adulthood in the cKO mice. Further analyses are necessary to identify the timing of Th ablation. Second, we did not assess TH expression in all tissues. There may be some Th‐ablated cells in peripheral tissues other than sympathetic neurons and the adrenal medulla, although the contribution of those TH‐positive cells to CA contents in peripheral organs or the bloodstream would be small.

The sympathoadrenal catecholaminergic system is a key regulator of metabolic homeostasis, especially under stressed situations, and its physiological functions are undergoing reevaluation. Modulation of the sympathoadrenal system can be a therapeutic strategy for metabolic abnormalities such as obesity (Martinez‐Sanchez et al. 2022). This cKO mice can be utilized to assess the effect of low sympathetic CA signals on the activity of peripheral tissues, which enable us to discuss the physiological role of sympathoadrenal system and potential of sympathoadrenal catecholamine synthesis as a therapeutic target for metabolic abnormality of interest.
