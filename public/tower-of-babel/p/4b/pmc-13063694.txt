Day + 30 detection of minimal residual FLT3-ITD by high-sensitivity PCR-NGS predicts relapse risk and guides post-transplant maintenance in AML

AbstractBackgroundAllogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) has improved outcomes in patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) harboring FLT3-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) mutations. Background

FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) is a class III receptor tyrosine kinase that functions as a cytokine receptor for FLT3 ligand [1]. FLT3-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) mutations are identified in approximately 30% of patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [2, 3] and are recognized as key drivers of leukemogenesis, strongly associated with poor prognosis [4, 5]. Currently, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains the most effective post-remission therapy for these patients. Despite a 3-year overall survival (OS) of approximately 70% in this population [6–10], disease relapse occurs in 15–35% of patients following transplantation [11–13]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to optimize therapeutic strategies and post-transplant disease monitoring in AML patients with FLT3-ITD mutations.

The Morpho trial [14] was the first to apply ultra-sensitive polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-next-generation sequencing (NGS) for detection of FLT3-ITD mutations in AML patients undergoing allo-HSCT, and it demonstrated that minimal FLT3-ITD clones detected during the peri-HSCT period were significantly associated with an increased risk of relapse, while the prognostic value of the detection at a fixed early time point post-HSCT has not been independently validated. Given that the majority of patients receiving myeloablative conditioning have completed engraftment and achieved complete donor chimerism (CDC) at day + 30 post-transplantation, we aim to perform deep detection of minimal FLT3-ITD clones at this time point to identify patients at higher risk of relapse. Currently, several investigations have highlighted the prognostic utility of measurable residual disease (MRD) assessment at day + 30 post-HSCT [15, 16], but it remains unclear whether minimal FLT3-ITD mutations can be reliably detected at day + 30 and how such findings might inform post-transplant maintenance therapy.

To address these gaps, we performed the first study using an ultra-sensitive PCR-NGS assay to detect FLT3-ITD in bone marrow (BM) samples collected at day + 30 after allo-HSCT. This study aimed to clarify the prognostic impact of minimal FLT3-ITD clones at this fixed early time point and to explore their potential utility in guiding maintenance therapy strategies.

Methods

Patients

A total of 2711 patients with malignant hematologic diseases who underwent HSCT between September 2017 and September 2024 were enrolled in the National Longitudinal Cohort of Hematological Diseases in China cohort (NICHE). Adult patients with a de novo diagnosis of FLT3-ITD–mutated AML who underwent first allo-HSCT and were alive at day + 30 post-transplant were included. The inclusion and exclusion flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. Among the 186 eligible patients, day + 30 BM samples from 50 patients were unavailable for PCR-NGS analysis (Additional file 1: Table 1. A for detailed reasons). No statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the included patients and those with unavailable samples (Additional file 1: Table 1B). Ultimately, 136 eligible patients were included, all were observed to have CDC at day + 30 post-transplantation, and their BM samples obtained at this time point were analyzed for minimal FLT3-ITD clones using PCR-NGS.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

FLT3-ITD detection and MFC-MRD assessment

The method for detecting FLT3-ITD mutations using PCR-NGS was as follows [17, 18]. Genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) was extracted from the BM samples using the Lab-Aid® 896 automated nucleic acid extraction system. Subsequently, multiplex PCR amplification was performed targeting the FLT3 exon 14 and intron 14 regions using specific primers (Additional file 1: Table 2); a mean input of 1069.37 ng DNA was required for a 22-cycle amplification. After purification of the PCR products, sequencing libraries were prepared using the Union Precision in-house system. A total amount of 0.5 nmol library was required for sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform (Illumina, USA), to ensure a final data output of at least 2 GB. Sequencing data analysis was conducted using the proprietary software ITDhunter (Copyright ID: 2025SR0446606). The limit of detection (LoD) for newly identified FLT3-ITD sequences was 5 × 10−6, defined as the lowest variant allele frequency (VAF) consistently detected with ≥ 95% probability in replicate dilution experiments. For FLT3-ITD sequences identical to those reported at diagnosis, variants detected at a level ≥ 1 × 10−6 (≥ 3 variant read) were classified as positive. This ultra-low-level positivity criterion was applied under strict conditions: the sequence must match 100% with diagnostic ITD, and no identical ITD signal was observed in any of the negative control or blank control samples processed concurrently across all sequencing batches (n = 7), thereby minimizing the risk of false-positive calls. The detailed methodology for capillary electrophoresis (CE)-based fragment analysis, MRD detection by multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC), and chimerism assessed can be found in the methods of Additional file 1.

Maintenance therapy

Maintenance therapy was initiated after allo-HSCT in patients with successful engraftment and in the absence of grade II–IV acute graft-versus-host disease, according to institutional practice. Gilteritinib was initially prescribed at 40–80 mg qd, with gradual escalation to 120 mg qd according to patient tolerance. Sorafenib was administered at 400 mg bid, with dose adjustments within the range of 200–800 mg/day. Maintenance therapy was generally continued for at least 1 year post-transplantation.

Definitions

The cumulative incidence of post-HSCT MFC-MRD relapse was defined as the first event of MRD positivity by MFC after transplantation. MFC-MRD–free survival is defined as the survival without MFC-MRD relapse after transplantation. The definitions of cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) and OS were based on the 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) guidelines [19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the collected patient data was performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.2.3. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to calculate OS and MFC-MRD–free survival, and the Cox proportional hazards model was used to analyze factors influencing them. CIR and cumulative incidence of post-HSCT MFC-MRD relapse were calculated using the competing risks model and Fine–Gray test, with competing risks regression used to analyze factors influencing them. Death without relapse was considered a competing event for CIR, while death without post-transplant MFC-MRD relapse was considered a competing event for the cumulative incidence of post-HSCT MFC-MRD relapse. In the analysis of maintenance therapy, maintenance therapy is considered a time-dependent covariate, and the related results are visualized using the Simon–Makuch plot. To further reduce potential selection bias in this real-world setting, a landmark analysis was additionally performed. All statistical analyses were two-sided, with a p value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 136 patients were included in this study, with a median age of 39 years (interquartile range (IQR), 29–49) at the time of transplantation. The median follow-up duration for surviving patients was 24.9 months (IQR, 14.5–37.6). All patients received a myeloablative conditioning regimen primarily based on busulfan and cyclophosphamide [20], with dose adjustments made according to individual clinical conditions. Routine CE-based fragment analysis for FLT3-ITD was performed on day + 30 post-transplantation, and no FLT3-ITD mutations were detected. Subsequently, minimal residual FLT3-ITD mutations were detected using ultra-sensitive PCR-NGS on BM samples collected at a median of 29 days post-transplantation (range, 23–42 days). The patients were stratified based on the presence or absence of minimal FLT3-ITD clones.

Among them, 37 patients (27.2%) tested positive for minimal FLT3-ITD clones, with 48 FLT3-ITD clones identified. The VAF distribution is shown in Fig. 2A. As presented in Table 1., there were no significant differences between the FLT3-ITD–positive and FLT3-ITD–negative groups regarding age at transplantation, 2022 ELN risk classification, disease status at HSCT, and the use of FLT3 inhibitors pre-transplantation. Notably, NPM1 mutation was more frequent in the FLT3-ITD–negative group, although the difference was not statistically significant (46.5% vs. 32.4%, p = 0.141). In contrast, a significantly higher proportion of relapsed/refractory AML (R/R AML) (40.5% vs. 17.2%, p = 0.004) and pre-transplantation MFC-MRD positivity (32.4% vs. 16.2%, p = 0.037) was observed in the FLT3-ITD–positive group (Fig. 2B and C).

These findings demonstrate that ultra-sensitive PCR-NGS can detect low-level FLT3-ITD clones missed by CE-based fragment analysis, allowing for the identification of patients misclassified as false negative using conventional methods. Moreover, patients with R/R AML and pre-transplantation MFC-MRD positivity were more likely to harbor minimal FLT3-ITD clones at day + 30 post-transplantation.

FLT3-ITD positivity at day + 30 is associated with an increased risk of MFC-MRD relapse

To evaluate the impact of minimal FLT3-ITD clones at day + 30 on transplant outcomes, we compared the follow-up results between the FLT3-ITD–positive and FLT3-ITD–negative groups. The median follow-up durations for surviving patients were 27.9 months (IQR, 18.4–34.8) in the positive group and 23.2 months (IQR, 13.7–37.9) in the negative group. When assessing the MFC-MRD relapse after transplant, 14 of 37 patients (37.8%) in the FLT3-ITD–positive group experienced MFC-MRD relapse at a median of 126 days, whereas 17 of 99 patients (17.2%) in the negative group experienced MFC-MRD relapsed at a median of 170 days. Thus, day + 30 PCR-NGS detection of minimal FLT3-ITD clones enabled identification of MFC-MRD relapse a median of 96 days earlier post-HSCT and increased relapse-detection sensitivity by 37.8%, with 78.6%, 64.3%, and 50.0% of patients being identified at least 30, 60, and 90 days before relapse, respectively. Competing risks analysis further showed that the cumulative incidence of post-HSCT MFC-MRD relapse was significantly higher in the FLT3-ITD–positive group (p = 0.010). The 1-year cumulative incidence of post-HSCT MFC-MRD relapse was 29.8% (95% confidence interval [CI], 28.7–31.0) in the positive group versus 14.5% (95% CI, 14.2–14.7) in the negative group; at 2 years, these rates increased to 40.3% (95% CI, 38.8–41.7) and 18.8% (95% CI, 18.4–19.1), respectively (Fig. 3A). Patients who developed MFC-MRD relapse showed significantly worse OS and higher CIR (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

Regarding other transplant outcomes, FLT3-ITD–positive patients showed a trend toward increased CIR (2-year CIR: 22.8% [95% CI, 21.8–23.9] vs. 15.2% [95% CI, 14.8–15.5], p = 0.233), as well as inferior OS (2-year OS: 70.9% [95% CI, 57.0–88.0] vs. 76.0% [95% CI, 67.3–85.8], p = 0.399); however, none of these differences reached statistical significance (Fig. 3B and C). To further investigate these findings, we retrospectively reviewed clinical records and found that preemptive therapy was promptly initiated following MFC-MRD relapse, which likely mitigated the risk of progression to morphologic relapse. Among FLT3-ITD–positive patients, six patients developed limited MFC-MRD relapse. Similarly, in the negative group, 4 patients had limited MFC-MRD relapse and were able to maintain CR after preemptive intervention. This timely clinical management likely contributed to the absence of significant differences in the CIR and OS between the two groups.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that ultra-sensitive PCR-NGS detection of FLT3-ITD at day + 30 post-HSCT can identify AML patients at increased risk of MFC-MRD relapse, enabling timely interventions that may improve clinical outcomes.

No relapse observed in day +30 FLT3-ITD positivity patients on FLT3 inhibitor maintenance after allo-HSCT

The early initiation of maintenance therapy following transplantation is a critical strategy for preventing MFC-MRD relapse. To specifically evaluate the role of FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy, we excluded 28 patients who received maintenance therapy with non-FLT3 inhibitor agents. Among the remaining 108 patients, 55 (50.9%) received FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy post-transplantation, whereas the remaining 53 patients did not due to personal preference or insurance constraints. The median time to initiation of FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy was 3.3 months (range, 2.3–4.6). Among the 55 patients who received FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy, 15 patients (27.3%) discontinued treatment due to thrombocytopenia (n = 10), relapse (n = 4), liver dysfunction (n = 1), and pulmonary infection (n = 1). The median duration of maintenance therapy was 12 months (range: 0.2–27.9 months).

Initially, to reduce potential selection bias inherent to maintenance therapy in real-world studies, we performed a landmark analysis at 4 months post-transplant. Patients who died or experienced MFC-MRD relapse within the first 4 months post-transplant (n = 12) were excluded. The remaining patients were grouped according to whether maintenance therapy had been started by the 4-month landmark. The landmark analysis showed a significant improvement in OS among patients receiving maintenance therapy (2-year OS: 91.9% [95% CI, 83.5–100.0] vs. 70.5% [95% CI, 58.7–84.6], p = 0.029). MFC-MRD–free survival showed a favorable trend in the maintenance group, although this did not reach statistical significance (2-year MFC-MRD–free survival: 85.5% [95% CI, 74.4–98.3] vs. 68.7% [95% CI, 57.3–82.4], p = 0.051). Similarly, the maintenance group showed a lower CIR, but the difference was not statistically significant (2-year CIR: 6.7% [95% CI, 6.3–7.1] vs. 17.7% [95% CI, 17.1–18.3], p = 0.116) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

Then we explored whether minimal residual FLT3-ITD positivity detected at day + 30 could serve as a practical marker for guiding maintenance therapy decisions. Based on maintenance therapy administration, FLT3-ITD–positive patients were divided into two groups. Among patients who did not receive maintenance therapy, six patients experienced morphological relapse at various time points post-HSCT, whereas no relapse occurred in the maintenance group (Fig. 4A). As maintenance therapy was time-dependent, subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) for CIR could not be reliably estimated (Fig. 4B). Nevertheless, these real-world data remain informative. Considering the early predictive value of MFC-MRD relapse for disease progression, MFC-MRD–free survival was analyzed as an additional endpoint to complement the primary analysis. Patients receiving FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy showed a trend toward lower risk of MFC-MRD relapse and death compared with those not receiving maintenance (MFC-MRD–free survival: HR = 0.31, p = 0.162; OS: HR = 0.32, p = 0.171), although differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 4C and D).

Taken together, our results suggest that the identification of minimal residual FLT3-ITD on day + 30 post-HSCT may provide valuable insight for guiding personalized maintenance therapy. Although statistical significance was not reached, no relapses were observed in the FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy group, indicating that maintenance therapy may play a positive role in improving outcomes for these patients.

Limited benefit of maintenance in day + 30 FLT3-ITD–negative patients without high-risk factors

Multiple guidelines [19, 21] recommend initiating FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy approximately 3 months post-HSCT in AML patients with FLT3-ITD mutations. Although this approach is widely accepted, several studies [14, 22, 23] have indicated that its benefits may be limited in patients who have achieved deep remission during the peri-transplant period. Moreover, it remains unclear whether patients who are FLT3-ITD–negative at day + 30 derive meaningful benefits from maintenance therapy, or whether they are unnecessarily exposed to potential toxicities. To investigate this, we performed a Cox proportional hazards regression analysis on the negative group and found that the 2022 ELN adverse-risk, R/R AML, and MFC-MRD positivity pre-HSCT were the main risk factors affecting post-transplant outcomes (Additional file 1: Table 3). Therefore, patients with any of these three factors were defined as the high-risk group, and then stratified based on the presence of high-risk factors and whether maintenance therapy was administered.

Among FLT3-ITD–negative patients with high-risk factors, FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy was associated with a significantly reduced risk of morphological relapse, with an 81% reduction in relapse risk (sHR = 0.19, p = 0.023; Fig. 5A). In addition, maintenance therapy was associated with improved MFC-MRD–free survival (HR = 0.28, p = 0.039; Fig. 5B), with the benefit extending to OS, as evidenced by a reduced risk of death (HR = 0.15, p = 0.014; Fig. 5C).

In contrast, among FLT3-ITD–negative patients without high-risk factors, no morphological relapse was observed in patients who received FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy, whereas only one of the 24 patients who did not receive maintenance therapy experienced morphological relapse after transplantation (Fig. 5A). For other transplant outcomes, maintenance therapy did not demonstrate a clear protective effect on MFC-MRD–free survival (HR = 0.25, p = 0.204) or OS (HR = 0.20, p = 0.142) (Fig. 5B and C).

These findings suggest that patients who are FLT3-ITD positivity on day + 30, maintenance therapy should be administered. Conversely, FLT3-ITD–negative patients without high-risk factors may safely omit maintenance therapy, potentially avoiding unnecessary drug-related toxicity and financial burdens.

Continued surveillance remains essential despite day + 30 FLT3-ITD negativity

To assess whether patients who were FLT3-ITD–negative on day + 30 should continue concurrent MFC-MRD and FLT3-ITD monitoring, we analyzed 99 negative patients and found that 17 (17.2%) developed MFC-MRD relapse after transplant, with a median time of 4.7 months. These patients exhibited significantly higher CIR compared to FLT3-ITD–positive patients at day + 30 (2-year CIR: 52.9% vs. 22.8%, p < 0.001, Fig. 6A), as well as inferior OS (2-year OS: 14.7% vs. 70.8%, p = 0.001; Fig. 6B). In addition, according to available CE-based fragment analysis results at the time of relapse, 10 of the 17 patients who developed MFC-MRD relapse were FLT3-ITD–positive. As PCR-NGS validation was not performed on relapse samples, the biological characteristics of relapse cannot be fully characterized. Based on the information currently available, molecular relapse was still observed in a subset of patients who were FLT3-ITD–negative at day + 30, supporting the need for continued dynamic FLT3-ITD surveillance.

To determine whether day + 30 FLT3-ITD status at a single post-transplant time point could independently predict transplant outcomes, Cox proportional hazards regression and competing risks regression was performed. In univariable analysis, FLT3-ITD positivity at day + 30 was associated with a significantly increased risk of MFC-MRD relapse (sHR = 2.53, p = 0.010); however, this association was not statistically significant in the multivariable model (sHR = 2.01, p = 0.081), indicating that FLT3-ITD status at this single post-transplant time point is insufficient to independently predict transplant outcomes (Table 2).

Based on these results, a non-negligible risk of relapse remains even among patients who are FLT3-ITD–negative at day + 30, highlighting the need for continued post-transplant surveillance with both MFC-MRD and FLT3-ITD monitoring to allow early detection of relapse and timely intervention.

Intronic FLT3-ITD mutations are associated with increased CIR

Further analysis of the clinical significance of FLT3-ITD mutations was performed by characterizing the mutation profiles of FLT3-ITD–positive patients (Additional file 1: Table 4). Among the 37 patients analyzed, nine (24.3%) harbored multiple FLT3-ITD clones; however, this was not associated with adverse transplant outcomes. The median length of the FLT3-ITD clone was 33 bp (range, 18–168 bp), with all mutations in-frame, and the median VAF was 19.5 (range, 3.5–1029.0) × 10−6. Among the 48 FLT3-ITD mutations, 23 were insertions and 25 were duplications. Patients with FLT3-ITD mutations in the intronic region had an increased CIR compared to those with mutations only in the exon region (2-year CIR: 58.3% vs. 14.6%, p = 0.020) and worse MFC-MRD–free survival (2-year MFC-MRD–free survival: 25.0% vs. 67.2%, p = 0.044). No statistically significant differences were observed in the OS (Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

Discussion

Our study is the first to apply ultra-sensitive PCR-NGS for detecting minimal FLT3-ITD clones at day + 30 post-transplantation. We found that patients harboring minimal FLT3-ITD clones at this early time point had a significantly higher risk of MFC-MRD relapse, and that early initiation of FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy was associated with no relapse post-transplantation in this subgroup. Among FLT3-ITD–negative patients, maintenance therapy markedly reduced relapse risk in those with high-risk factors (2022 ELN adverse-risk, R/R AML, or MFC-MRD positivity pre-HSCT), whereas its benefit appeared limited in patients without such risk factors. Collectively, these findings highlight the value of ultra-sensitivity PCR-NGS–based FLT3-ITD detection at day + 30 post-HSCT for refining relapse risk stratification and informing personalized maintenance strategies.

In this study, we found that among 136 patients who tested negative for FLT3-ITD by CE-based fragment analysis on day + 30 post-transplantation, nearly one-third (27.2%) exhibited minimal FLT3-ITD clones detectable using an ultra-sensitive PCR-NGS. This finding is consistent with the results of Shota Yokoyama et al. [24], who reported subclinical minimal FLT3-ITD clones in 25.7% of newly diagnosed AML patients that were negative for FLT3-ITD by gel electrophoresis but positive by NGS at a sensitivity of 10−5.

To investigate whether the presence of minimal FLT3-ITD clones at day + 30 impacts transplant outcomes in AML patients, we found that FLT3-ITD–positive patients had a significantly increased cumulative incidence of post-HSCT MFC-MRD relapse (40.3% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.001). However, in multivariate regression analysis, day + 30 FLT3-ITD positivity was not identified as an independent adverse prognostic factor (Table 2 and Additional file 1: Table 5). This may be attributed to the strong predictive value of the pre-transplant MFC-MRD status, which likely diminished the independent effect of FLT3-ITD positivity in the statistical model. Nonetheless, FLT3-ITD–positive patients tended to have higher CIR and poorer OS, although the differences were not statistically significant. This may reflect the early initiation of preemptive therapy following MFC-MRD relapse, potentially influencing long-term outcomes. These findings are consistent with those of Emily C. Liang et al. [25], who reported a trend toward reduced progression-free survival but no significant difference in OS in patients identified as FLT3-ITD–positive by NGS at a sensitivity of 10−5 within 3 months post-transplantation in a prospective study. In contrast, a study by Mark J. Levis et al. [14] demonstrated that the detection of minimal FLT3-ITD clones at 10−6 levels during the peri-HSCT period (prior to or within 3 months after HSCT) was significantly associated with decreased relapse-free survival (p ≤ 0.001) and OS (p = 0.0025). Notably, in their cohort, more than two-thirds of the patients who were FLT3-ITD–positive during the peri-HSCT period had detectable mutations prior to transplantation, while fewer than one-third were positive within 3 months post-transplant. Multiple studies [18, 26–31] have confirmed that pre-transplant MRD is a strong negative prognostic factor, and it is likely that the adverse outcomes observed in that study were primarily driven by pre-transplant FLT3-ITD clones. Importantly, most previous studies [14, 32] have assessed FLT3-ITD using samples collected within the first 3 months after transplantation. By comparison, our study focused on BM samples obtained on day + 30, allowing for an earlier evaluation of residual clones. Theoretically, FLT3-ITD clones detected later in the post-transplant period may have a greater impact on long-term outcomes. However, early detection on day + 30 may facilitate the timely identification of high-risk patients and provide a critical window for initiating maintenance strategies to prevent relapse.

In terms of post-transplant maintenance therapy, we found that none of the patients who received FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy relapsed in the FLT3-ITD–positive group. For FLT3-ITD–negative patients, FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy significantly reduced relapse rates in patients with high-risk factors (p = 0.023), while no significant impact was observed in those without high-risk factors. Previous clinical trials [11, 12, 23, 33] have suggested that AML patients with FLT3-ITD mutations benefit from post-transplant maintenance therapy. However, a study by Mark J. Levis et al. [14, 22] indicated that FLT3-ITD–negative patients detected by PCR-NGS during the peri-HSCT period did not derive significant benefit from maintenance therapy with gilteritinib. Therefore, for FLT3-ITD–negative patients at day + 30 without high-risk factors, avoiding maintenance therapy can reduce the associated toxicities and financial burden, allowing for more rational resource allocation and personalized treatment strategies.

In addition, our study found that among the 99 patients with FLT3-ITD negativity on day + 30, 17 patients developed MFC-MRD relapse, and their prognosis was significantly worse than that of the 37 patients who remained FLT3-ITD–positive on day + 30. This discrepancy may be partly explained by the higher use of FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy among FLT3-ITD–positive patients (53.6%) compared with those who were FLT3-ITD–negative on day + 30 but subsequently developed MFC-MRD relapse (20.0%). But we should mention that among the 17 patients with MFC-MRD relapse, 7 patients were FLT3-ITD–negative at relapse by CE-based fragment analysis. The CE-negative relapses may reflect either undetected low-frequency FLT3-ITD clones or the expansion of true FLT3 wild-type clones, highlighting that the biological mechanisms driving relapse in these patients remain unclear. In this context, PCR-NGS can serve as a complementary assay alongside MFC-MRD for detecting FLT3-ITD clones, potentially enabling earlier identification of patients at higher risk. Nevertheless, until the characteristics of these CE-negative relapse clones are further clarified, any clinical recommendations derived from these cases should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, we observed that patients with intronic FLT3-ITD mutations exhibited a higher CIR. However, our study did not include functional experiments or transcriptome-level analyses, and therefore the underlying mechanisms cannot be determined. Consequently, this finding should be considered exploratory. Regarding potential mechanisms, intronic FLT3-ITD mutations may be indirectly associated with regulatory processes, such as ribonucleic acid (RNA) stability or transcriptional regulatory networks, which could in turn influence gene expression patterns or related signaling pathways. Previous studies [34, 35] have shown that a small fraction of introns in AML is retained in RNA, and that the expression of these retained introns is linked with mutations in epigenetic regulators such as DNMT3A, suggesting potential associations with other molecular features. Similarly, Salima Benbarche et al. [36] identified trans-acting factors involved in splicing regulation, indicating that introns and splicing networks in leukemia cells are highly plastic and modifiable. Taken together, intronic FLT3-ITD mutations may serve as exploratory molecular markers that reflect broader genomic or regulatory contexts, rather than as direct drivers of poor prognosis. Linkage phenomena of this kind have also been reported in the molecular evolution of leukemia [37, 38].

To optimize post-transplant management for FLT3-ITD AML patients, we established a decision-making flow based on FLT3-ITD results at + 30 days post-transplant (Fig. 7). If FLT3-ITD clones are detected at + 30 days, the prompt initiation of FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy is recommended. If FLT3-ITD is negative at + 30 days, it is essential to further evaluate pre-transplant high-risk factors, including the 2022 ELN high-risk classification, R/R AML, and MFC-MRD positivity pre-transplant. Patients with high-risk factors should promptly receive FLT3 inhibitor maintenance therapy, whereas those without high-risk factors may not benefit from it. Nonetheless, all FLT3-ITD AML patients should routinely undergo post-transplant monitoring of FLT3-ITD using ultra-sensitivity PCR-NGS.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective real-world study, it is subject to inherent limitations, including the inability to perform PCR-NGS testing in some patients due to sample-related constraints and potential indication-related selection bias in the initiation of maintenance therapy. Accordingly, all findings, including relapse outcomes observed in the maintenance therapy group, should be interpreted with caution, as they may partly reflect bias introduced by treatment decisions in a real-world context. In addition, incomplete baseline molecular data in some patients limited the assessment of comparability between groups. Moreover, dynamic monitoring of FLT3‑ITD mutations at multiple time points before and after transplantation was not performed, and relapse samples were not analyzed to determine whether relapse was driven by FLT3‑ITD clones. Finally, the conclusions are primarily based on patients receiving myeloablative conditioning, and their applicability to patients receiving reduced-intensity conditioning or non-myeloablative conditioning remains to be validated in future multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusions

This is the first study to evaluate the clinical value of ultra-sensitivity PCR-NGS for detecting minimal FLT3-ITD clones at + 30 days post-HSCT in AML patients. Our study demonstrates that these minimal FLT3-ITD clones on day + 30 post-HSCT effectively identify high-risk patients who may experience post-transplant MFC-MRD relapse, providing a strong rationale for personalized post-transplant maintenance therapy.
