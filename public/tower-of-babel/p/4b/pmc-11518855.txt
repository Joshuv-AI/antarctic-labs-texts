Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome in a Patient With Cutaneous Leishmaniasis and HIV: A Diagnostic Challenge for Clinicians Caring for a Migrant Population in the United States
Authors: Alejandro De La Hoz, Nirupa Gadi, Christina G Lopez, Alejandro Barrera-Godinez, Nancy S Miller, Daniel L Bourque, Candice Brem, Ekin Ozluk, Geetika Seth, Sarah Kimball, Jessica L Taylor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMigration routes determine exposure to endemic infections. We present a case of complex cutaneous leishmaniasis in a man with HIV infection who migrated to the United States from Haiti, where Leishmania is uncommon, acquiring leishmaniasis while on his journey via South America and Central America. His presentation included hyperpigmented, nonulcerated plaques and nodules on his extremities, chest, back, face, palms, and soles; initially he had no mucosal lesions. Infection with Leishmania panamensis was confirmed via polymerase chain reaction. He was prescribed bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine with rapid suppression of HIV and liposomal amphotericin B for diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis with a limited initial response. He subsequently developed mucosal lesions in the setting of immune reconstitution and was retreated with amphotericin dosed for mucocutaneous disease. A thorough migration history was critical to diagnosis. This case highlights the different presentations of leishmaniasis in people with HIV and the elevated risk for treatment failure.Keywords: AIDS, HIV, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, leishmaniasis, migration

CASE

A 39-year-old male originally from Haiti presented to a primary care clinic shortly after his arrival to the United States (day 1). His chief concern was dry skin. A physical examination revealed a body mass index (BMI) of 18.6 kg/m2 and hyperpigmented small papules on his shins, which were treated as eczema with topical steroids. On week 20 of follow-up, he returned reporting progression of the cutaneous lesions, and physical examination demonstrated a BMI of 17.6 kg/m2 and numerous nonpruritic hyperpigmented plaques and nodules involving his back and upper extremities (Figure 1A and 1B). His vital signs were normal, and he was diagnosed presumptively with pityriasis rosea. He did not undergo laboratory testing during these initial encounters.

On week 23, he returned with ongoing rash. The provider ordered laboratory testing, including an HIV 1/2 antigen/antibody screening test that resulted positive, and reflex HIV 1/HIV 2 RNA qualitative testing confirmed HIV-1 infection. Screening serologies for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and syphilis were negative. A complete blood count was remarkable for a white blood cell count of 3.6 × 103 cells/µL (reference range, 4–10 × 103 cells/µL), lymphocytes of 0.8 × 103 cells/µL (1.1–3.5 × 103 cells/µL), and hemoglobin of 11.7 g/dL (13.5–15.5 g/dL). He was referred to the infectious diseases clinic, where intake laboratory testing demonstrated a CD4 count of 10 cells/µL (430–1185 cells/µL) and an HIV-1 viral load of 25 500 copies/mL (<25 copies/mL), as well as negative QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus, Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii serum antigen, and Toxoplasma IgG/IgM serum antibodies. He was prescribed bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine for antiretroviral therapy (ART) and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis.

Additional migration history revealed that he was born in Haiti and moved to Chile 2 years prior to his arrival to the United States. From Chile he migrated to Mexico by land, traveling via bus and sleeping inside, except for a 2-day journey through the jungle between Colombia and Panama when he slept outside. He flew from Mexico to Boston, Massachusetts. The rash had progressively worsened since the time of his initial visit, spreading to his trunk, arms, legs, and face and remaining painless and nonpruritic. Other than a 20-lb weight loss, he had no systemic symptoms. He reported no tobacco, alcohol, or other substance use; he had prior female sexual partners, most recently 3 years prior.

A physical examination revealed a young man with a BMI of 16.6 kg/m2 and nontender hyperpigmented papulosquamous plaques with targetoid morphology and nodules involving his trunk, limbs, face, palms, and soles (Figure 1C–1G). No ulcers, lesional anesthesia, or mucosal lesions were observed. There was no lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly. The dermatology department was urgently consulted, given the broad differential diagnosis, which included secondary syphilis (with initial false-negative test result), fungal infections (eg, histoplasmosis and paracoccidioidomycosis), Kaposi sarcoma, leprosy, tuberculosis, nontuberculous mycobacterial infection, and other opportunistic infections. A representative punch biopsy of the lesions was nondiagnostic for bacterial, mycobacterial, and fungal etiologies. Urine histoplasma antigen was negative, and a peripheral smear showed no blood parasites. Histopathology sections showed a mild superficial and deep, perivascular, and periappendageal lymphohistiocytic infiltrate, as well as numerous intrahistiocytic small ovoid structures with eccentric nuclei. These were weakly Giemsa positive but periodic acid–Schiff negative (Figure 2A–2C) and highly suspicious for an infectious etiology, probably representing tissue amastigotes of Leishmania species.

A second biopsy was obtained, and polymerase chain reaction performed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) detected the presence of DNA for Leishmania panamensis, confirming the diagnosis of complex cutaneous leishmaniasis (CCL). The patient's lesions continued to progress, appearing more confluent and infiltrated (Figure 1H and 1J). Given the risk of unrecognized mucosal involvement in the setting of advanced immunosuppression, a nasolaryngoscopy was performed showing normal mucosa.

On week 28, after having started ART, the patient's HIV-1 viral load decreased to 332 copies/mL, and his CD4 count increased to 41 cells/µL (4%). He developed tenderness to palpation of the skin lesions overlying his bilateral lower extremities. There also was 1+ pitting edema and pain with active and passive ankle range of motion (Figure 1I–1L). Inflammatory markers were elevated, including C reactive protein (98.5 mg/dL; reference range, <5 mg/dL) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (>130 mm/h; reference range, <15 mm/h). The patient was admitted to the hospital for initiation of intravenous liposomal amphotericin B (LAmB) and monitoring for immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS).

LAmB was prescribed at a dose of 3 mg/kg/d, and the patient continued ART and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis. After 10 days, he was discharged home to continue LAmB once weekly to complete 42 mg/kg. On week 32, he reported resolution of lower extremity pain. However, the hyperpigmented lesions persisted (Figure 1M–1P), and on week 38 he was found to have epistaxis and had ulcerated lesions on his nares and lips (Figure 1Q). A biopsy performed on week 40 showed ulceration of the squamous mucosa and histiocytes with numerous 1- to 2-µm nucleated organisms consistent with amastigotes of Leishmania species (Figure 2D–2F). He was readmitted to the hospital on week 42 (Figure 1R–1T) for a new cycle of LAmB, with a plan to complete 42 mg/kg, and then every 2 weeks until immune reconstitution was achieved (CD4 cell count, 150–200 cells/µL).

DISCUSSION

We describe a case of CCL caused by L panamensis in a patient with a relevant migration history and advanced undiagnosed HIV who later developed mucosal lesions in the setting of IRIS. Our patient's case highlights key learning points related to HIV screening and the relevance of a migration history, as well as the clinical presentation, evaluation, and treatment of severe leishmaniasis.

Migration through Central America is reaching unprecedented numbers, with an estimate of >250 000 people who crossed the Darien Gap in 2022 [1]. Many of these people make an on-foot journey through the Darien Jungle in southern Panama, where they are exposed to infectious diseases, trauma, and exacerbation of chronic disease [2]. As increasing numbers of US health care professionals are treating these patients, it is important to understand these risks for infection [3]. We believe that lack of awareness for infections associated with migration and missed opportunities for HIV screening will contribute to a delay for both diagnoses for patients such as this one.

Currently there is no mandate to screen the migrant population for HIV to reduce stigma. However, a panel of tests for initial health screening is recommended in this population [4], including HIV screening, following the CDC and US Preventive Services Task Force guidelines [5, 6]. There is evidence that factors such as conflict, poverty, violence, discrimination, and exploitation may increase the transmission of HIV [5]. Furthermore, some individuals might not have access to HIV testing at their home countries due to social and economic reasons [5]. It is also important to highlight that the CDC/US Preventive Services Task Force recommendations have not even been fully implemented in the general US population; for instance, there was no increase in HIV testing during physician office visits between 2009 and 2016 [7, 8]. Therefore, it is important for clinicians to offer screening to all patients, particularly those with a history of migration. To achieve this, appropriate counseling to avoid stigma and maximize acceptance is important, as well as putting in place automated support systems to help clinicians identify patients who have never been tested or who are more vulnerable to HIV [7].

Leishmaniasis occurs as a result of infection from an intracellular protozoan of the genus Leishmania [9]. Infection often develops 2 to 8 weeks after inoculation from a bite of the vector: the female phlebotomine sand fly [10]. The severity of clinical presentation varies by the virulence of distinct species [11] and the host's immunity [12, 13]. There are 3 main manifestations of leishmaniasis: cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), mucocutaneous leishmaniasis, and visceral leishmaniasis [14]. “New World” leishmaniasis refers to infection caused by Leishmania species that are found in North America and South America, whereas “Old World” species are typically isolated in southern Europe, the Middle East, Africa, and South Asia [15]. In a 20-year analysis of surveillance data by GeoSentinel, L braziliensis is the most common, causing 42% of infections across the Americas, and L panamensis is the second most common, causing 14% [15].

In typical reports of CL, the initial infection manifests with the development of a small papule, pustule, or nodule that later progresses to a painless shallow ulcer [12, 13, 16]. In contrast, this patient presented with diffuse hyperpigmented plaques and nodules and developed mucosal lesions after starting ART. We categorized this presentation as CCL [17]. Two principal manifestations of CCL include diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis (DCL) and disseminated cutaneous leishmaniasis (DSL) [17]. DCL produces a Th2 immune response leading to plaque formation, no ulceration, and poor response to antimicrobial therapy [18]. Mucosal disease is infrequent, and histopathologic analysis of DCL shows multiple amastigotes in the dermal infiltrate with lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages [10, 19, 20]. Conversely, DSL involves a Th1 response and can present with nodules, ulcers, and mucosal involvement [10, 20]. Amastigotes are rarely seen in pathology, and patients may have a good response to therapy [10, 20]. From a clinical perspective, our patient seems best described as being on a spectrum between DCL and DSL, taking into account atypical manifestations in the context of immunosuppression and IRIS. Our patient initially presented with signs that fulfill some criteria for DCL. However, after ART and onset of apparent IRIS, the clinical presentation resembled an overlap between DCL and DSL. The diagnostic distinctions can help predict response to therapy. The species associated with CCL include L mexicana, L amazonensis, L infantum/chagasi, and L aethiopica, which are associated with DCL, whereas L braziliensis is mainly associated with DSL [10, 17]. L panamensis is known to cause DCL, DSL, and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis, though these manifestations are uncommon [18, 21]. The gold standard diagnosis is demonstration of the direct parasitologic presence of amastigotes on pathology [22], and polymerase chain reaction is recommended for confirmation [17].

CL has been reported in people traveling outside the United States, people with a history of migration, and rarely in people who reside in the southern United States, where there is some autochthonous transmission [14]. A study in Texas found 41 cases of endemic mild CL caused by L mexicana [23]. Yet, few cases of CCL [19, 24] or infections by L panamensis [12, 25, 26] have been reported, and there are no reports of CCL caused by L panamensis in the United States [10]. There is limited literature on leishmaniasis in the Caribbean islands. L waltoni is the etiologic agent of CL in the Dominican Republic [27], which may imply that this pathogen is also present in Haiti. We believe that this patient was exposed to Leishmania while traveling from Chile to Mexico by land while crossing the Darien Gap, since this was the only region where he was unsheltered and it is endemic for L panamensis.

The close relationship between HIV and leishmania is well described in the literature, particularly for visceral leishmaniasis in Europe and South Asia [28, 29]. Cases of CCL in people with HIV (PWH) have also been described [13, 24, 30, 31], but large epidemiologic studies are lacking [29]. The clinical presentation of CL in PWH varies from single lesions [30] to DCL, particularly with low CD4 counts [13, 28]. Frequently, PWH present with multiple lesions, and response to treatment is limited [29, 30]. IRIS is a rare syndrome in leishmaniasis, and it can present as a paradoxical reaction with worsening lesions and tissue involvement or as unmasking of the disease. A 2015 review of 34 cases, including all types of leishmaniasis, found only 1 case of IRIS in DCL, which manifested with mucosal lesions caused by L braziliensis [32]. There were 2 additional cases of CL in which IRIS manifested with mucosal lesions [33]. A fourth case involved unmasking of CL with nodular skin lesions after prescription of ART [19]. For all patients, the mean time from ART initiation to IRIS was 4 months, and CD4 counts ranged from 4 to 256 cells/µL. Treatment included pentavalent antimonials or amphotericin B in addition to ART [32]. The species causing IRIS in CCL or CL were L major, L braziliensis, L guyanensis, and L chagasi [32]. Another similar case described a young male with DCL who had new mucosal lesions after starting ART. No species identification was made. Treatment consisted of meglumine antimoniate but had a poor response [34]. Our patient had worsening of the cutaneous disease with new nodular lesions and worsening of the plaques, new mucosal lesions, low extremity pain located specifically on the lesions, edema, and ankle arthritis as a manifestation of IRIS.

There is no standardized treatment for CCL [17]. The main options are miltefosine, fluconazole, LAmB, and pentavalent antimonials [17]. In severe presentations, antimonials or LAmB is recommended, but LAmB is preferred for PWH [17]. In this case, the patient was treated initially with 42 mg/kg of LAmB. Treatment response in cases of CCL is low and patients often relapse [29, 30, 35]. This patient's skin lesions persisted after treatment, but resolution of his lower extremity pain was interpreted as a partial response. Nonetheless he developed mucosal disease, requiring additional treatment with LAmB until immune reconstitution was achieved.

CONCLUSION

Our patient's case highlights the relevance of migration history and specific risks for the individuals who traverse the Darien Gap each year, the importance of HIV screening, and the clinical challenges inherent in treating leishmaniasis in patients with immunosuppression. Systems interventions that support consistent use of laboratory screenings for migrants may facilitate timely care that improves outcomes for PWH and/or complex, migration-associated opportunistic infections.
