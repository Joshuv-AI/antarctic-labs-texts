Mycoviruses confer hypovirulence but enhance antifungal volatile organic compound production in a phytopathogenic fungus
Authors: Zhijun Liu, Qiangqiang Pan, Huang Huang, Lixia Gao, Zhi Zhou, Dan Zheng, Xueqiong Xiao, Yanping Fu, Jiasen Cheng, Qing Cai, Bo Li, Tao Chen, Xiao Yu, Yang Lin, Xueliang Lyu, Tom Hsiang, Jiatao Xie, Daohong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWe identified an evolutionary lineage of RNA viruses within the proposed family Shuviridae, revealing an unexpected connection to animal viruses. Using a transfection system, we demonstrated that SsAShV1 confers hypovirulence, attenuating fungal pathogenicity. Furthermore, we found that the infection by a community of mycoviruses acts synergistically within the fungal genetic background to activate an ecological strategy: the enhanced production of broad-spectrum antifungal volatiles. This compensation mechanism ensures the persistence of the mycoviral-fungal partnership and offers a paradigm for controlling plant diseases by engineering microbes that can suppress pathogens through multiple, sustainable mechanisms.

Mycoviruses are a phylogenetically diverse group of viruses found in all major fungal taxa (1). They are increasingly recognized as important modulators of fungal biology (2), with profound implications for ecology, evolution (3), and agricultural sustainability (4, 5). While often replicating asymptomatically, certain mycoviruses can strikingly alter host phenotypes, most notably by inducing hypovirulence, a significant reduction in fungal virulence (6, 7). This ability makes mycoviruses promising candidates for developing biological control agents against pathogenic fungi (8, 9), offering an alternative to chemical fungicides.

The discovery of novel mycoviruses is crucial for understanding viral diversity and evolution. Currently, mycoviruses are classified into 41 families by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), although many remain unclassified, such as the Botryosphaeria dothidea circular RNAs (BdcRNAs) (10). Recent studies have expanded mycovirus diversity by identifying single-stranded circular RNA viruses with ribozyme-flanked bidirectional open reading frame (ORFs) in fungi, leading to the establishment of new families (Unambiviridae and Dumbiviridae) within Ambiviricota (11). Phylogenetic analyses often reveal deep evolutionary connections between mycoviruses and viruses infecting other distant hosts. For instance, the ssRNA mycovirus Botrytis virus X shares RdRP similarity with plant viruses (12), while Mycoreovirus isolates from Cryphonectria parasitica and Rosellinia necatrix show structural and phylogenetic similarities to animal reoviruses (13, 14). Evidence of cross-kingdom viral similarities suggests that long-term fungal interactions with plants or animals may facilitate horizontal virus transfer (15, 16), supporting the hypothesis of a shared ancient origin among mycoviruses, plant viruses, and animal viruses (17).

Sclerotinia sclerotiorum is a devastating necrotrophic pathogen causing severe crop losses worldwide (18, 19). This fungus harbors a diverse range of mycoviruses, classified into 18 families recognized by the ICTV. Hypovirulent strains of S. sclerotiorum, which are often associated with mycovirus infections (20), are considered potential biocontrol agents. However, the mechanisms of mycovirus-induced hypovirulence are complex and may involve single viruses, synergistic interactions among multiple viruses, or interactions with the host (4, 21–23). Hypovirulence-associated mycoviruses reduce host fitness but persist in nature through efficient transmission or by altering host lifestyle. For instance, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum hypovirulence-associated DNA virus 1 (SsHADV-1) is transmitted by the mycophagous insect Lycoriella ingenua (24). Mycoviruses such as SsHADV-1 (9) and Pestalotiopsis theae chrysovirus-1 (25) can achieve long-term persistence by modifying the lifestyle of the host.

The discovery of highly divergent mycoviruses provides insights into viral diversity and evolution, revealing distinct genomic traits and unexpected evolutionary links to animal or plant viruses. Notably, some of these viruses confer hypovirulence to their fungal hosts. However, the mechanisms by which such mycovirus-mediated hypovirulent strains survive in complex environments remain unclear. In this study, we i) identified and characterized a mycovirus with a distinct evolutionary lineage in the hypovirulent S. sclerotiorum strain; ii) established a transfection system that confirmed mycovirus-mediated hypovirulence; and iii) demonstrated that antifungal VOC production is synergistically enhanced by mycoviruses infection and the genetic background of strain SCH767. This study provides evidence that mycoviruses not only can cause host hypovirulence, but also can trigger production of antifungal VOCs in a phytopathogenic fungus, potentially enhancing viral fitness and offering ecological compensation for reduced pathogenicity of the host. This work contributes valuable insight toward development of mycovirus-based biocontrol strategies.

Results

Strain SCH767 Coinfected with Six Mycoviruses Exhibits Hypovirulent Phenotypes.

Compared to wild-type S. sclerotiorum 1980, strain SCH767 exhibited slower growth, impaired sclerotial formation, and abnormal colony morphology on PDA (Fig. 1 A and E). Microscopic observation showed that SCH767 displayed more abundant and denser hyphal branching (Fig. 1B). Unlike the typical lesions induced by strain 1980 on detached rapeseed leaves, strain SCH767 failed to produce typical lesions (Fig. 1 C and F) probably due to delayed formation of infection cushions (Fig. 1D). These observations confirmed SCH767 as a hypovirulent strain. Additionally, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) released by SCH767 significantly inhibited mycelial growth of three phytopathogenic fungi, including S. sclerotiorum (25.3% inhibition), Rhizoctonia solani (38.3% inhibition), and Botrytis cinerea (52.9% inhibition) (Fig. 1 G and H).

The detection of multiple dsRNA segments in strain SCH767 suggested infection by several mycoviruses (Fig. 1I). Subsequent HTS analysis revealed the mycoviral composition of this strain and enabled the assembly of six virus-related contigs (contig7177, contig20, contig82, contig375, contig3356, and contig2), which were further confirmed by RT-PCR (Fig. 1J). These contigs corresponded to partial genomic sequences of six putative mycoviruses, including five +ssRNA viruses and one −ssRNA virus, and their complete genomes were obtained (SI Appendix, Table S1). These six mycoviruses were designated as follows: Sclerotinia sclerotiorum ascoshuvirus 1 (SsAShV1/SCH767), Sclerotinia sclerotiorum umbra-like virus 1 (SsULV1/SCH767), Sclerotinia sclerotiorum ourmia-like virus 14 (SsOLV14/SCH767), Sclerotinia sclerotiorum mitovirus 48 (SsMV48/SCH767), Sclerotinia sclerotiorum mitovirus 49 (SsMV49/SCH767), and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum negative-stranded RNA virus 2 (SsNSRV2/SCH767). Among these, the RdRP encoded by SsAShV1 shared 28.7% sequence identity with Bat calicivirus polyprotein (AIF74268.1; E-value: 0.007). Given this similarity to a vertebrate-infecting member of the Caliciviridae family, SsAShV1 was selected for further investigation.

SsAShV1 with Unique Genomic Structure Shares Homology with Viruses Infecting Animals.

The SsAShV1 genome comprised 10,749 nucleotides (nt) and lacked a poly(A) tail. It contained a single large ORF, flanked by a 530-nt 5’-untranslated region (UTR) and a 223-nt 3’-UTR (Fig. 2A). Notably, the 3’-UTR harbored three 27-nt repeat sequences (5’-CAAACAAUAACAAAUACAAACAAUUAA-3’). Secondary structure prediction indicated that the regions between these repeats form two stem-loop structures, with each stem-loop initiating one nucleotide after the end of a repeat sequence (Fig. 2A). The predicted free energy (ΔG) value of this repeat-containing structural region (RCSR) was −12.8 kcal/mol. Alignment of HTS data with the genome revealed comprehensive coverage of the SsAShV1 genome (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). A region spanning nt 228 to 543 within the 5’-terminal of SsAShV1 shared over 78% nucleotide identity with dsRNA mycoviruses, including Botrytis cinerea RNA virus 1 (BcRV1)/Antioquia (MN443879.1, E-value: 3e−74), Grapevine associated totivirus-2 (GaTV2)/Ctg341, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum dsRNA mycovirus-L (SsRVL), and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum fusagravirus 1 (SsFV1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C and Table S2). Secondary structure prediction indicated that this region forms a compact multi-stem-loop domain with stem-loop 1 (SL1) as a structural hub, connecting three primary stem-loop branches (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Notably, the start codon (AUG) was consistently located within the loop of SL1.

The SsAShV1 genome encoded a 3,331 amino acid (aa) polyprotein containing conserved protease and RdRP domains, along with ten putative transmembrane helices (Fig. 2A). The RdRP structure predicted by AlphaFold2 exhibited a canonical “right-hand” architecture (30), with seven conserved motifs A–G distributed across three characteristic subdomains: motifs A–D are embedded in the palm domain, motif E resides in the thumb domain, while motifs F and G are located within the fingers domain. Foldseek structural alignment showed similarities to RdRPs from Hepatitis C virus (Kitrinoviricota; TM-Score = 0.663, rmsd = 7.48 Å) and Norwalk virus (Pisuviricota; TM-Score = 0.659, rmsd = 7.52 Å) (Fig. 2B). HHpred and pLM-BLAST analyses identified 31% sequence identity between SsAShV1 residues 1345 to 1373 aa and the NS2B-NS3 protease of Zika virus (pdb_00005t1v; Kitrinoviricota) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). AlphaFold2 prediction and sequence alignment revealed a catalytic triad (His62, Asp76, and Ser160) in SsAShV1. Its protease domain exhibited structural similarity to Pisuviricota Norovirus Hu/1968/US 3C-like protease (TM-Score = 0.408, rmsd = 10.56 Å) and Kitrinoviricota Dengue virus NS2B-NS3 protease (TM-Score = 0.438, rmsd = 14.17 Å) (Fig. 2C). Notably, SsAShV1 retained a canonical His-Asp-Ser catalytic triad, consistent with typical serine proteases and identical to that of Dengue virus, suggesting functional conservation despite evolutionary divergence.

PSI-Search identified a sequence match with 23.7% identity between SsAShV1 residues 1,582 to 1,699 aa and the outer capsid protein VP4 of Rotavirus A (Q9WAK4, E-value = 1.7) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Given the low confidence of this match, we assessed whether SsAShV1 is encapsidated using an RNase A sensitivity assay. In mycelial homogenates, the encapsidated viruses Sclerotinia sclerotiorum reovirus 1 (SsReV1) (27) and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum botybirnavirus 3 (SsBV3) (28) remained resistant to RNase A after Triton X-100 treatment. In contrast, SsAShV1 was digested by RNase A under the same conditions, resembling the behavior of the capsidless virus Sclerotinia sclerotiorum hypovirus 1 (SsHV1) (29) and the naked viruses SsULV1 and SsOLV14. Notably, the naked viruses SsULV1 and SsOLV14 were sensitive to RNase A even without Triton X-100 treatment, whereas SsAShV1, just as with SsHV1, was only digested after Triton X-100 exposure (Fig. 2 D and E). These results indicated that SsAShV1 is likely a capsidless virus that may be protected by host-derived membranes, consistent with our bioinformatic prediction of multiple transmembrane helices in its ORF (Fig. 2A), suggesting membrane-binding properties of this virus.

SsAShV1 Reveals a Family with a Distinct Evolutionary Lineage.

Through comprehensive analysis of HTS data from S. sclerotiorum in our laboratory, we identified contigs with sequence similarity to SsAShV1. Assembly of these contigs yielded seven SsAShV1-related sequences (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A and Table S3). These viruses possessed diverse sequences (59.4 to 94.6% nucleotide identity) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B) but shared a conserved polyprotein (3,325 to 3,345 aa) containing RdRP and protease domains (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Using the RdRP of SsAShV1 and its related mycoviruses infecting S. sclerotiorum as queries, we conducted homology searches against public transcriptome databases. Homologous contigs were identified across diverse sources: eight from other phytopathogenic fungi, one from plant-symbiotic fungi, one from Asteraceae plant seeds, and seven from environmental samples, including soil, leaf litter, liquor fermentation, compost, and freshwater (SI Appendix, Table S3).

Based on the multiple alignment of the RdRP sequences from SsAShV1-associated viruses, we performed an HHblits comparison with the sequence clusters in the previously proposed supergroups (31), and identified supergroup054 as the most closely related to SsAShV1 (probability = 100%, E-value = 1.9e−35). Among other hits, supergroup001 belongs to Pisuviricota, while supergroup005, supergroup018, and supergroup021 are classified under Kitrinoviricota (SI Appendix, Table S4). Using maximum likelihood methods and RdRP sequences from Pisuviricota and Kitrinoviricota viruses (ICTV referenced sequences), the constructed phylogenetic tree revealed that SsAShV1 and its related viruses (n = 25) were clustered within supergroup054. This clade shares ancestors with Pisuviricota viruses (bootstrap support > 95%) but diverged early to form a distinct lineage (Fig. 3A). Phylogenetic analysis and pairwise alignment of RdRP sequences among viral contigs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C) supported the establishment of the family Shuviridae within Pisuviricota, with taxonomic thresholds defined by RdRP similarity: <45% for genus and <70% for species. This framework established five genera (Ascoshuvirus, Folishuvirus, Synshuvirus, Mycotashuvirus, and Solumshuvirus) and ten species within Shuviridae (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Table S3).

Comparative analysis of viruses from the phylum Pisuviricota with a focus on genomic architecture and conserved RdRP motifs revealed unique features for members in Shuviridae. Unlike members within other families belonging to Pisuviricota, Shuviridae exhibited distinct genome organization and a divergent catalytic triad (GDN) in the RdRP motif C, contrasting with the conserved GDD or SDD triads typical of RdRP of Pisuviricota viruses (Fig. 3B).

SsAShV1 Confers Hypovirulence on Two Sclerotiniaceae Fungi.

To elucidate the biological roles of SsAShV1 and its RCSR in 3’-UTR in S. sclerotiorum, we constructed infectious clones of SsAShV1 (full-genome) and SsAShV1L (RCSR-lacking mutant, in which the 3’-UTR RCSR was deleted). Full-length cDNA sequences of SsAShV1 and SsAShV1L were obtained (Fig. 4A) and subsequently used for in vitro transcription, yielding RNA products of expected sizes for both (Fig. 4B). These in vitro-transcribed RNAs were transfected into protoplasts of strain 1980, generating two transfectants each for SsAShV1 and SsAShV1L (Fig. 4C). After three subcultures, RT-PCR confirmed the SsAShV1L deletion was stably maintained in S. sclerotiorum (Fig. 4D). Compared to strain 1980, both SsAShV1 and SsAShV1L transfectants exhibited reduced growth and attenuated virulence, with SsAShV1 causing stronger growth inhibition than SsAShV1L (Fig. 4 E–G). Furthermore, the replication efficiency of SsAShV1 was significantly higher than SsAShV1L in S. sclerotiorum (Fig. 4H).

To determine whether SsAShV1 induces hypovirulence in B. cinerea, a pathogen closely related to Sclerotinia, we transfected protoplasts of B. cinerea B05.10 with in vitro-transcribed RNA of SsAShV1 or SsAShV1L. Only SsAShV1 was successfully introduced into strain B05.10 (Fig. 4I), with no SsAShV1L transfectants obtained after three independent transfection attempts. The SsAShV1 transfectant TBc2 exhibited reduced growth, attenuated virulence, and loss of sclerotial formation (Fig. 4 J–L). After three subcultures, SsAShV1 dsRNA remained detectable in TBc2 (Fig. 4M), with viral RNA accumulation increasing progressively at 48 h intervals (Fig. 4N). These results indicated that SsAShV1 confers hypovirulence in both S. sclerotiorum and B. cinerea, whereas SsAShV1L was unable to infect or persist in B. cinerea. The RCSR in the 3’-UTR of SsAShV1 may enhance viral replication efficiency in S. sclerotiorum and may contribute to its successful establishment in B. cinerea, thereby influencing the observed host-specific infectivity and hypovirulent effects.

To assess the ability of horizontal transmission of SsAShV1 and SsAShV1L progeny in S. sclerotiorum or B. cinerea, the transfectants (donor strains) were dual-cultured with strains 1980R or B05.10R (recipient strains) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A, C, and G). SsAShV1 or SsAShV1L could be detected in recipient strains (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B, D, and H), which exhibited colony morphology matching the donor transfectants (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E, F, and I). These results demonstrated that the progeny of SsAShV1 and SsAShV1L can be transmitted horizontally efficiently.

Genetic Background-Dependent Mycovirus Synergism Enhances Antifungal VOC Production.

Strain SCH767 was successfully cured of mycoviruses through protoplast regeneration, yielding the virus-free strain SCH767R22 (Fig. 5A). A coinfected strain 1980TR1 carrying six mycoviruses was generated by dual-culture of strain SCH767 (donor) with 1980R (recipient), and the presence of these mycoviruses was confirmed (Fig. 5B).

We compared the antifungal activity of VOCs between strains SCH767 and SCH767R22, as well as between strains 1980TR1 and 1980R. Compared to strain SCH767R22, VOCs produced by strain SCH767 significantly inhibited mycelial growth of S. sclerotiorum (14.4%), R. solani (29.7%), and B. cinerea (31.4%) (Fig. 5 C and D). In contrast, VOCs from 1980TR1 showed no significant antifungal activity against these pathogens, resulting in only 3.3%, 8.4%, and 7.7% growth inhibition, respectively (Fig. 5 E and F). These results indicated that mycovirus infection is necessary but insufficient alone to activate antifungal VOC production in S. sclerotiorum, and synergistic interactions with specific host genetic backgrounds are also required. The SCH767 genetic background enables synergistic enhancement of virus-induced VOC biosynthesis, whereas the 1980 background restricts this viral response, revealing a strain-dependent viral modulation of fungal metabolic pathways.

We generated strain SCH767R45, which was coinfected with the three mycoviruses SsULV1, SsMV48, and SsMV49, via single-protoplast isolation from strain SCH767 (Fig. 6A). Compared to its parental strain, SCH767R45 exhibited significantly improved growth (Fig. 6 B and C) and produced VOCs with stronger inhibitory effects against S. sclerotiorum than its parental strain (Fig. 6 D and E). Based on the observed plate-based inhibition of B. cinerea by VOCs produced from these two strains, we further evaluated the efficacy of VOCs from strain SCH767R45 and the mycovirus-free strain SCH767R22 on B. cinerea-inoculated strawberry fruits (Fig. 6F). VOCs from strain SCH767R45 reduced disease incidence by 22.2% and conferred 51.2% control efficacy (Fig. 6 G and H and SI Appendix, Table S5), thereby significantly delaying gray mold disease development.

We performed comparative metabolomic profiling of strains SCH767R45 and SCH767R22 to identify antifungal VOC (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Significant differences were observed in their metabolite profiles (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Compared to strain SCH767R22, strain SCH767R45 showed 77 up-regulated and 58 down-regulated differential metabolites (VIP ≥ 1.0 and |Log2FC| ≥ 1.0) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). The up-regulated metabolites were primarily esters and terpenoids, whereas the down-regulated ones were mainly heterocyclic compounds and hydrocarbons (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). To identify metabolites with biocontrol potential, we selected 17 commercially available standards from the 34 significantly up-regulated metabolites (Log2FC ≥ 5.0) in strain SCH767R45 (SI Appendix, Table S6) and evaluated their activity against S. sclerotiorum (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Seven compounds, including 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, 4-methyl-5-nonanone, β-pinene, 2-acetylthiazole, bis(4-chlorobutyl) ether, vinyl hexanoate, and benzyl nitrile, exhibited inhibitory effects exceeding 50.5% (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Among these, 2-ethyl-1-hexanol showed the highest Log2FC value (13.4) and was therefore selected as the key bioactive metabolite for further study.

We systematically evaluated the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of 2-ethyl-1-hexanol (Fig. 6I). It significantly inhibited the growth of diverse phytopathogenic fungi from Ascomycota, Mucoromycota, and Basidiomycota, with hyphal growth inhibition all exceeding 54.7% (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). 2-ethyl-1-hexanol also inhibited the germination of B. cinerea conidia at 12 hpi. Furthermore, this compound also exhibited remarkable antibacterial activity against Pseudomonas syringae, significantly reducing the number of bacterial colonies by up to 66.2% (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D).

Discussion

This study expands known mycovirus diversity through the characterization of SsAShV1 and supports the proposal of a virus family. We identified six mycoviruses coinfecting the hypovirulent S. sclerotiorum strain SCH767, including an RNA mycovirus, SsAShV1, which exhibits unique genomic features and reveals evolutionary links between fungal and animal viruses. Based on comprehensive molecular characterization, we propose the establishment of a virus family, Shuviridae (derived from “Shu” for Sichuan Province, China), within the phylum Pisuviricota, to accommodate SsAShV1 and related viruses. This classification is supported by several molecular hallmarks: i) SsAShV1 exhibits markedly low RdRP sequence identity with known viruses but shares significant structural homology with certain animal viruses in the Kitrinoviricota (Hepatitis C virus) and Pisuviricota (Norwalk virus), suggesting deep evolutionary connections. Phylogenetically, shuviruses form a distinct branch in the viral tree of life. ii) The RdRP catalytic motifs in shuviruses contain a divergent GDN catalytic triad within motif C, a pervasive and phylogenetically informative feature contrasting with the conserved GDD or SDD motifs typical of other Pisuviricota families (32). Although GDN has been sporadically reported in certain Pisuviricota viruses, such as Agaricus bisporus virus 10 (AQM49936.1; Fusariviridae) (33), it represents an exception within families largely utilizing GDD. iii) Notably, we identified a ~300 nt segment within the 5’-terminal region of SsAShV1 that shares >78% nucleotide identity with several dsRNA mycoviruses. This region forms a compact multi-stem-loop structure, with the start codon consistently located in the loop of a central stem-loop, suggesting a potentially conserved translation initiation mechanism.

Although studies on mycovirus diversity have advanced viral taxonomy and our understanding of viral evolution (3, 34), their ecological functions remain poorly understood. SsAShV1 confers hypovirulence not only in its native host S. sclerotiorum but also in the phylogenetically related pathogen B. cinerea. This cross-genus infectivity within the Sclerotiniaceae family underscores its potential as a broad-spectrum biocontrol agent, and aligns with evidence that interspecies transmission of mycoviruses occurs in nature and can induce phenotypic changes in new hosts (35). Furthermore, the shuvirus-related sequences identified in this study are predominantly distributed among plant pathogenic or symbiotic fungi. Notably, homologous sequences have also been occasionally detected in transcriptomic data from seeds of the Asteraceae plant Echinops ritro (36) and from environmental samples. These findings suggest that shuviruses exhibit broad ecological adaptability and display a fungal-centric distribution pattern, likely facilitated through interactions between fungal hosts and plants, as well as other environmental media.

The significant differences in VOC profiles and inhibitory activity between SCH767R45 (which harbors three mycoviruses) and its virus-cured derivative SCH767R22 indicate that mycoviruses can modulate host metabolic pathways to alter VOC synthesis. We identified 2-ethyl-1-hexanol as a key antifungal VOC with remarkably broad-spectrum activity, reinforcing its potential as a biocontrol agent. Its efficacy against B. cinerea aligns with reports in bacterial systems, where VOCs from Bacillus siamensis containing 2-ethyl-1-hexanol exhibited significant antifungal activity, supporting its potential as a biofumigant (37). This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that mycoviruses can remodel fungal secondary metabolism. Examples include enhanced methyl p-coumarate production in Trichoderma koningiopsis infected with Trichoderma harzianum Hypovirus 1 (38), and altered VOC-mediated insect attraction in hypovirulent strains of S. sclerotiorum infected with SsHADV-1 (via 1-octen-3-ol and 3-octanone) and C. parasitica infected with Cryphonectria hypovirus 1 (CHV1) (via terpenoids and phenylethyl alcohol) (24, 39).

We further found that mycovirus infection induces antifungal VOC production in both the native strain SCH767 and its derivative SCH767R45, but not in a heterologous strain, emphasizing that the host genetic background underpins mycovirus-mediated induction of antifungal VOC synthesis. Enhanced VOC production is inherent to SCH767 and may be triggered by viral infection. This host-mycovirus partnership may provide an ecological advantage: while reducing pathogenicity toward primary hosts such as rapeseed, increased broad-spectrum antifungal VOC production likely suppresses competitors, thereby improving niche occupancy and promoting mutual persistence. The induction of broad-spectrum antifungal VOCs represents an indirect mechanism whereby mycovirus infection not only attenuates fungal virulence but also suppresses co-occurring phytopathogens. This dual action—direct hypovirulence combined with VOC-mediated inhibition—enhances the ecological stability of mycovirus-based biocontrol agents in complex microbial environments. Thus, we reveal a survival strategy in which the mycovirus drives a host trade-off: loss of high virulence with improved chemical competition, thereby promoting its own continued survival and dissemination. From the mycovirus perspective, it robs the host of high virulence but improves the chemical arsenal of the host, thereby enhancing its own survival. Consequently, ideal mycovirus-mediated hypovirulent strains should be selected or engineered not only for hypovirulence but also for “mycovirus-enhanced” antimicrobial activity capable of suppressing other fungal diseases through multiple pathways.

Materials and Methods

Fungal Strains and Culture Conditions.

The S. sclerotiorum strain SCH767 was isolated from a sclerotium collected from diseased rapeseed stems in Sichuan Province, China. Hygromycin B-resistant derivatives (1980R and B05.10R) were generated from wild-type strains 1980 of S. sclerotiorum and B05.10 of B. cinerea, respectively, for mycovirus horizontal transmission assays. All tested strains (1980, 1980R, B05.10, and B05.10R) exhibited normal biological characteristics, with strong virulence to their plant hosts. Strains of S. sclerotiorum, B. cinerea, and Mucor hiemalis were cultured on PDA at 20 °C, while R. solani and Fusarium graminearum PH-1 were maintained on PDA at 25 °C. P. syringae Pst DC3000 was cultured on Luria agar at 25 °C.

Phenotypic Measurements.

The biological characteristics of S. sclerotiorum strains, including growth rate, hyphal tip morphology, and virulence, were evaluated following previously described methods (40) with slight modifications. Sclerotial production was quantified after 14 d of growth on PDA at 20 °C. Infection cushions formed by S. sclerotiorum on detached rapeseed leaves were examined using scanning electron microscopy at 12 h postinoculation (hpi) (41). Each treatment was performed with at least three replicates, and the entire experiment was repeated twice. Lesion areas were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software, while data analysis and visualization were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was assessed using Student’s t tests (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (for three or more groups).

Suppression of Fungal Mycelial Growth by VOCs.

Strain SCH767 or other mycovirus-infected strains were used as VOC sources in antifungal activity assays against S. sclerotiorum, R. solani, or B. cinerea using double-dish sets (DDSs) (26). Each DDS consisted of two lidless Petri dishes (9 cm diameter): one containing PDA (15 mL) inoculated with strain SCH767 (6 mm agar plug), and the other containing PDA inoculated with one of the three phytopathogenic fungi. For the control treatment (CK), one dish contained noninoculated PDA, while the other dish contained PDA inoculated with one of the three phytopathogenic fungi. The two dishes in each treatment were placed face-to-face (the upper dish containing the pathogenic fungus and the lower dish containing strain SCH767 or uninoculated PDA) and sealed with Parafilm. All DDSs were placed at 20 °C until the fungal colony in the control group fully covered the dish, and then the colony diameters of S. sclerotiorum, R. solani, or B. cinerea were measured. Each treatment was performed with at least three biological replicates, and the entire experiment was repeated twice. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8, with two-way ANOVA used to assess statistical significance (P < 0.05).

Total RNA Extraction, DsRNA Isolation, and Sequencing.

S. sclerotiorum strains were cultured on cellophane-covered PDA for 3 to 5 d. Approximately 1 g of mycelium was ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed via a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). rRNA was removed using the Ribo-Zero™ rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA), cDNA libraries were constructed by Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation (Shanghai, China) and were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw reads were filtered to exclude host RNA, genomic DNA, adapters, and low-quality sequences (42). Viral contigs were identified after de novo assembly using CLC Genomics Workbench (v6.0.4) and subsequent NCBI BLAST analysis.

The dsRNA extraction was performed following previously described methods (43). dsRNA was enriched via cellulose chromatography (44) and precipitated with 3 M sodium acetate and isopropanol. The precipitated dsRNA was subsequently purified using DNase I and S1 nuclease to remove contaminating DNA and ssRNA.

Verification, Quantification, and Full-Length Determination of Mycoiruses.

Mycovirus presence was verified via RT-PCR using cDNA synthesized with M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase and random hexamer primer (Takara, Dalian, China). Mycovirus-specific primers were designed based on the mycovirus contig sequences (SI Appendix, Table S7) to detect potential mycoviruses in each strain through RT-PCR. The 5’ and 3’ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) techniques were employed to obtain the terminal sequences of all mycoviruses infecting strain SCH767. Total RNA was extracted from SCH767 mycelia grown on PDA for 5 d. These RNA samples were subsequently utilized for both 5’ and 3’ RACE experiments, following previously described methods (45, 46). Specific primers designed for nested PCR to amplify the 5’ and 3’ terminal sequences of each mycovirus are listed in SI Appendix, Table S8. The full-length viral sequences obtained from metatranscriptomic data were confirmed by amplifying overlapping fragments with primers (SI Appendix, Table S9) and cloning into pMD18-T vectors for Sanger Sequencing.

For quantification, total RNA was reverse transcribed using the EasyScript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen, Beijing, China). qRT-PCR was performed using the TransStart® Tip Green qPCR SuperMix kit (TransGen, Beijing, China) with specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S10). The cycling conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 5 s and 56 °C for 30 s. Each qRT-PCR assay included three independent biological samples and three technical replicates per sample.

Mycovirus Genome Annotation, Multiple Alignment, and Phylogenetic Analysis.

The complete genomes of mycoviruses were analyzed using DNAMAN 7.0 software. ORFs were predicted using the ORF Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/), while the conserved motifs were identified using the MOTIF server (https://www.genome.jp/tools/motif/). The transmembrane helices were predicted using TMHMM 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/). The RNA secondary structure of viral 5’- and 3’-UTRs was predicted using mFold (https://www.unafold.org/mfold/applications/rna-folding-form-v2.php) with default parameters, and the minimum free energy structures were selected from multiple conformations based on thermodynamic stability.

For homologous sequence identification, BLASTp and Hmmsearch (HMMER 3.3.2) were employed to search against both the nr database and the clustered nr database to obtain homologous protein sequences. The protein identity matrix was generated using BioAider 1.03, and alignments were visualized in Jalview 2.11.1.0 (47). SsAShV1-related sequences from the selected sequence read archive (SRA) database were assembled using previously described methods (48). Notably, some candidate sequences were initially identified through searches of the comprehensive RdRP palmprint dataset (49, 50), using the SsAShV1 RdRP sequence as the query.

Phylogenetic trees were constructed using MAFFT 7.0 with the L-INS-i method to align viral protein sequences (51), and all analyzed mycoviral sequences are provided in figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.30024784.v2). The aligned sequences were subsequently trimmed using trimAl 1.2 (52). Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed using IQ-TREE 2.2.6 (53), with branch support values calculated based on 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated using the Evolview online platform (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/#/treeview) (54).

3D Structure Prediction and Comparative Analysis.

The 3D structure of SsAShV1 protein was predicted using AlphaFold 2 (55). Subsequently, the predicted viral protein structure was searched against both the Big Fantastic Virus Database (56) and PDB database using the 3Di/AA alignment mode in Foldseek (https://search.foldseek.com/search) (57). The top-scoring alignment results were then compared with the SsAShV1 protein structure through multiple structural alignment and visualization using UCSF ChimeraX software (58).

RNase A Susceptibility Test for Viral Capsid Verification.

Following a previously reported RNase A treatment protocol (59) with minor modifications, the sensitivity of viral RNA to RNase A was assessed at 37 °C. Fungal mycelia were cultured on PDA overlaid with cellophane at 20 °C for 3 to 5 d. Approximately 0.4 g of mycelia was ground into powder in liquid nitrogen and suspended in 4 mL of 0.1 M PBS buffer (pH 7.0). After centrifugation at 4,000 r/min for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered through Miracloth to remove cellular debris. The filtered supernatant was then aliquoted into three tubes. Two aliquots were treated with RNase A (Tiangen Biotech Co. LTD, Beijing, China) at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in the absence or presence of 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (60). The dsRNA was extracted by cellulose chromatography and examined by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Total RNA was then extracted and analyzed by RT-PCR with virus-specific primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S7.

Infectious Clone Construction and In Vitro Transcription.

Due to the substantial length of the viral sequence, we employed a dual-vector strategy to construct infectious clones of SsAShV1 and its 3’-UTR RCSR deletion mutant SsAShV1L. Initially, the viral sequences were cloned into the yeast-E. coli-Agrobacterium shuttle vector pCB301-2µ-HDV via yeast homologous recombination (61) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–E). To investigate the function of the RCSR within the 3’-UTR, the deletion mutant SsAShV1L was generated using reverse primers designed upstream of the 3’-UTR RCSR. Subsequently, both SsAShV1 and mutant SsAShV1L were independently cloned into T7 promoter-containing pUC19 plasmids through in vitro homologous recombination using the ClonExpress II (C112) or MultiS (C113) One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and F–I). The accuracy of the viral vector was confirmed by PCR using primers listed in SI Appendix, Table S11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 J and K).

For in vitro transcription, full-length viral sequences containing the T7 promoter were amplified from the respective viral vector using KOD FX high-fidelity DNA polymerase (TOYOBO, Shanghai, China), followed by RNA synthesis with the HiScribe® T7 ARCA mRNA Kit (NEB, USA) (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). The resulting transcripts were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis to verify their integrity and size.

Protoplast Preparation, Mycovirus Transfection, and Elimination.

Protoplasts were prepared following previously described methods (62) with minor modifications, using 0.7 M NaCl as the osmotic stabilizer. Subsequently, RNA transfection experiments were conducted by mixing 10 μL of protoplasts (108/mL) with in vitro-transcribed mycoviral RNA (5 μg) of mycoviruses, spermidine (50 mM), and heparin (2.5 mg/mL), followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. The mixture was then treated with KCl-Tris-CaCl2 buffer (KTC) and PEG4000 (60%, w/v) at 20 °C for 20 min in darkness. The transfected protoplasts were plated on regeneration medium (RM) and incubated at 20 °C for 48 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Individual hyphae were subsequently transferred to PDA for subculture. Transfectants were verified via RT-PCR using virus-specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S7).

To obtain mycovirus-free strains, the prepared protoplasts (105/mL) were serially diluted in Sorbitol-Tris-CaCl2 (STC) buffer (10×, 100×, 500×, and 1,000×), and 100 μL of each dilution was spread on RM, which provides osmotic stability and essential nutrients to support the regeneration of protoplasts into single colonies. After 48 h of incubation at 20 °C, the tips of single-protoplast isolation hyphae were transferred to the fresh PDA supplemented with 100 μg/mL ribavirin. Total RNA was subsequently extracted for mycovirus detection by RT-PCR.

Horizontal Transmission of Mycoviruses.

To assess horizontal transmission of mycoviruses between fungal strains, we dual-cultured a virus-infected donor strain with a virus-free recipient strain (usually marked with the hygromycin resistance gene) on PDA at 20 °C. After 3 to 5 d of hyphal contact between strains, we collected recipient strain mycelial plugs (>1 cm away from the contact interface), and transferred them to PDA containing 50 μg/mL hygromycin. After three serial subcultures on selective medium, viral infections in recipient strains were confirmed by RT-PCR with virus-specific primers. Each treatment included three biological replicates.

Control Efficacy of Strains VOCs against B. cinerea on Strawberries.

Mycelia of strains SCH767R45 and SCH767R22 (2 g each) collected from cellophane plates were homogenized in 10 mL sterile water. Then, 2 mL of the homogenate was inoculated into 500 mL of sterile sorghum grains and incubated at 20 °C for 25 d to prepare solid cultures. Fresh, healthy, and uniformly sized strawberry fruits were washed by rinsing twice with distilled water and air-dried. Fruits were inoculated with 6-mm-diameter hyphal plugs of B. cinerea. Solid cultures were placed at the bottom of a desiccator, with a porcelain plate separating them from the fruits. Pathogen-inoculated strawberries were immediately placed on the plate. The desiccator lid was sealed with petroleum jelly and incubated at 20 °C for 7 d. Disease incidence, disease index, and relative control efficacy were calculated. The experiment was repeated three times independently. Data analysis and visualization were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8. Untargeted Metabolomic Profiling Workflow for Fungal Analysis.

The mycelia of strains SCH767RR45 and SCH767R22 were collected with four biological replicates per strain and sent to Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for untargeted metabolomic analysis. After grinding in liquid nitrogen, 500 mg of each sample was weighed into a headspace vial, followed by the addition of a saturated NaCl solution and 10 μL of internal standard (50 μg/mL). Automated headspace solid-phase microextraction was employed for sample extraction prior to GC–MS analysis. Metabolites were identified using the MetwareBio in-house database, authentic standards, and retention indices. Qualification was based on retention time and characteristic ions, with one quantifier and two to three qualifier ions selected for each compound. Quantification was performed via internal standard calibration.

Data were preprocessed using log-transformation and mean-centering prior to multivariate statistical analysis in R. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the prcomp function. Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), implemented with the MetaboAnalystR package, was applied for model construction and variable importance in projection (VIP) calculation. The model was validated with 200 permutation tests. Differential metabolites were screened using thresholds of VIP > 1 and |log2FC| ≥ 1. Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) was conducted on unit variance-scaled data and visualized using the ComplexHeatmap package.

Fumigation Inhibitory Activity Assay of Standards Using DDSs or Two-Compartment Sets.

DDSs were used with sterilized filter papers as volatile carriers instead of medium. Solid standards were dissolved in methanol and adjusted to a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The filter paper disc containing the standard was placed in one Petri dish, while the other dish was inoculated with the pathogenic fungus. Both dishes were uncovered and assembled face-to-face (with the filter paper disc at the bottom and the pathogen on top), with a blank filter paper disc as the control. After sealing with Parafilm, the colony diameter of the treatment group was measured when the control colony fully covered the dish, and the growth inhibition was calculated. Additionally, P. syringae Pst DC3000 was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 102 cfu/mL and spread on LA. These were then paired with dishes containing filter paper discs infused with 50 μL of 2-ethyl-1-hexanol for cocultivation. The number of colonies on each plate was counted after 3 d to calculate the inhibition.

A two-compartment dish fumigation assay was employed to evaluate the effects of 2-ethyl-1-hexanol on the conidial germination of B. cinerea. Conidia of B. cinerea were collected from PDA, washed with PDB, filtered through Miracloth, and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 102 cfu/mL. A 50 μL conidial suspension was applied to a glass slide placed in one compartment of the dish, while a filter paper impregnated with 200 μL of 2-ethyl-1-hexanol was placed in the opposite compartment. The dish was sealed with parafilm and incubated at 20 °C for 12 h, after which conidial germination was assessed microscopically. All experiments were performed with at least three replicates. Data were analyzed using Student’s t test in GraphPad Prism 8 (P < 0.05).
