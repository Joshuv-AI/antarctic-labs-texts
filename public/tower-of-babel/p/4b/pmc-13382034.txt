The AcGγ gene regulates cleistothecium development and flavor metabolism in dark tea
Authors: Zhenglin Fei, Jie Wang, Yan Jiang, Changfa Yang, Rui Zhang, Yaya Yuan, Caiyan Tang, Qilan Hu, Junren Xu, Chao Huang, Zhonghua Liu, Mingzhi Zhu, Yongyi Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While the presence of Aspergillus cristatus cleistothecia (commonly known as “golden flowers”) is a hallmark of Fuzhuan brick tea (FBT) quality, their specific role in flavor metabolism remains poorly understood. We hypothesized that the AcGγ gene acts as a core genetic regulator of cleistothecium development, and that the physical and enzymatic presence of these reproductive structures is associated with shifts in the secondary metabolite profiles related to dark tea flavor. To test this hypothesis, we compared the wild-type A. cristatus with AcGγ knockout (ΔAcGγ) and overexpression (OE::AcGγ) mutants using a controlled solid-state fermentation model and non-targeted LC-QTOF-MS metabolomics. Phenotypic analysis confirmed that AcGγ is strictly required for the formation of cleistothecia and ascospores, while also regulating colony morphology and growth rate. Metabolomic profiling revealed that the failure to form cleistothecia in the ΔAcGγ strain significantly altered the mycelial metabolome, particularly altering the abundance profiles of organic acids, organoheterocyclic compounds, and benzenoids. Furthermore, in the fermented tea matrix, AcGγ-mediated cleistothecium development was strongly associated with the bioconversion of key putative flavor-active components, markedly shifting the profiles of amino acids, flavonoids, and lipid-like molecules known to be associated with sweetness, bitterness, umami, and aroma. These findings support our hypothesis that AcGγ provides a genetic link between sexual development and altered flavor-related metabolic fluxes. Ultimately, this work provides a preliminary theoretical genetic and biochemical basis for future strain engineering and quality modulation of dark tea fermentation.

Introduction

Dark tea encompasses a wide range of post-fermented products, including Fuzhuan, Pu-erh, and Liubao teas, characterized by a microorganism-driven solid-state fermentation process (Lu et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2015). Fuzhuan brick tea (FBT) is produced from mature tea leaves rich in amino acids, polysaccharides and other nutrients (Al-Mahdi et al., 2020). Uniquely, the Chinese national standards (GB/T 9833.1-2013) stipulate that Aspergillus cristatus must be present as an indicator fungal species in qualified FBT (All China Federation of Supply and Marketing Cooperatives, 2013). During fermentation, A. cristatus undergoes sexual reproduction to form abundant cleistothecia, commonly referred to as “golden flowers” (Rui et al., 2019). The abundance and maturity of these cleistothecia are generally regarded as key critical indicators for evaluating FBT quality.

Cleistothecium development is accompanied by abundant secretion of extracellular enzymes and secondary metabolites that modify the tea matrix, and thus may improve overall tea quality (Xiao et al., 2021a; Li et al., 2017). For instance, (Li et al., 2020) demonstrated that keto-aldehydes and heterocyclic compounds produced during cleistothecium formation serve as characteristic aromatic components associated with the unique fungal-floral note of FBT. Secreted polyphenol oxidase primarily catalyzes the oxidation of phenolic substrates to quinones, which then undergo non-enzymatic and/or microbially mediated polymerization to form theabrownins (Wang et al., 2021). This process greatly reduces the astringency and bitterness of the raw tea leaves, conferring a mellower taste profile. Simultaneously, secreted glycosidases hydrolyze complex polysaccharides into soluble monosaccharides, and extracellular proteases break down insoluble proteins into free amino acids. This enzymatic bioconversion is thought to enhance umami and sweetness in profiles of the tea matrix (Wang et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2021b). Collectively, this microbial activity is a major contributor to the distinctive organoleptic properties and putative functional metabolites of high-quality FBT (Chen et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2020). Many of these resulting components have been previously reported to exhibit beneficial bioactivities, such as immunomodulatory properties and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis (Xie et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2022). However, while cleistothecia are widely acknowledged as an important factor for FBT quality, the specific genetic mechanisms regulating their formation and how this developmental process correlates to flavor-related metabolite fluxes remain largely unexplored.

Cleistothecium development in A. cristatus is a complex physiological process tightly controlled by dedicated genetic networks. Currently, only a handful genes regulating cleistothecia formation in A. cristatus have been identified. For instance, the deletion of mating-type genes (AcMAT1-1-1 or AcMAT1-2-1) or Acsko1 results in abnormal cleistothecia development and failure to produce ascospores (Ge et al., 2019; Shao et al., 2025). Building upon our previous research, which identified the AcGγ gene via genome-wide association analysis and RNA sequencing (Ge et al., 2019), we selected this gene for functional characterization. AcGγ encodes a Gγ subunit of the heterotrimeric GTP-binding protein (G protein) complex, which plays a critical role in signal transduction, vegetative growth, and sexual reproduction in fungi (El-Defrawy & Hesham, 2020; Song et al., 2017). While heterotrimeric G-proteins are well-known to regulate fungal development, their specific downstream impact on secondary metabolism during solid-state food fermentation remains largely unclear. We hypothesize that the AcGγ gene is a core regulator of cleistothecium development, and that the physical and enzymatic presence of cleistothecia is associated with the translocation and bioconversion of flavor-related metabolites into the tea matrix. This hypothesis provides a framework to explore the linkage between fungal development and tea metabolite turnover.

This study addresses the current gap between fundamental fungal genetics and applied food chemistry by exploring the potential association between the genetic regulation of A. cristatus (via AcGγ) and tea matrix bioconversion, as well as the formation of key flavor-active metabolites. To test our hypothesis, we constructed gene knockout (ΔAcGγ) and overexpression (OE::AcGγ) mutant strains, and performed non-targeted LC-QTOF-MS metabolite profiling of both the fungal mycelia and the fermented dark teas inoculated with each strain. By comparing these metabolomic profiles, this study aims to characterize AcGγ-mediated alterations in flavor-related metabolite biosynthesis, thereby providing preliminary mechanistic insights into the potential biochemical contribution of cleistothecia to the sensory quality of dark tea.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

The wild-type (WT) A. cristatus strain (CGMCC 7.193) used in this study was isolated from Fuzhuan tea produced in tea gardens of Yiyang, Hunan Province, China. Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 and Escherichia coli DH5α were purchased from Shanghai WeiDi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), with all vectors maintained in the Escherichia coli DH5α strain. The knockout vector was constructed using the pDHt/sk plasmid backbone, which contains a hygromycin B resistance cassette (hph) as the selectable marker. For overexpression, the AcGγ CDS was driven by the constitutive PgpdA promoter and terminated by the TtrpC terminator.

The strains were cultured on malt yeast agar (MYA) consisting of 20 g/L malt extract, 5 g/L yeast extract, 30 g/L sucrose, and 15 g/L agar, at 28 °C. Sexual development was induced on MYA supplemented with 1 M NaCl at 28 °C. The strains were then cultured on MYA supplemented with 3 M NaCl at 37 °C for conidium collection. Transformation induction was carried out using IM (Induction Medium) solid medium (2.05 g K2HPO4, 1.45 g KH2PO4, 0.15 g NaCl, 0.5 g MgSO4.7H2O, 0.1 g CaCl2.6H2O, 0.0025 g FeSO4.7H2O, 0.5 g (NH4)2SO4, 2 g glucose, ddH2O was added and fixed to 1000 mL, 0.5% (w/v) glycerol, 40 mM 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid, and 200 μM acetosyringone; pH = 5.3).

Construction of AcGγ mutants (ΔAcGγ and OE::AcGγ)

The ΔAcGγ knockout strain was constructed using homologous recombination methodology (Shao et al., 2024). Briefly, genomic DNA of A. cristatus was used as template, specific primer pairs (Table S1) were employed to amplify the upstream and downstream homologous arms of the AcGγ. Subsequently, these fragments were ligated and transformed to construct the AcGγ knockout vector (AcGγ-L-pDHt/sk-hyg-AcGγ-R). To construct the AcGγ gene overexpression mutant, the CDS fragment of AcGγ was amplified from A. cristatus cDNA using specific primer pairs (Table S1). The amplified product was subsequently ligated and transformed to construct the AcGγ overexpression vector (pDHt/sk-hyg-PgpdA-AcGγ-TtrpC). The transformation of A. cristatus was performed using the Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation (ATMT) method, as previously described (Wang et al., 2021a). Putative transformants were primarily screened on MYA solid medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL hygromycin B, followed by confirmation of transformation events through PCR amplification.

Following the initial PCR screening of genomic DNA, successful homologous recombination and targeted insertion/overexpression were further validated by RT-qPCR to confirm the abolition or significant elevation of AcGγ transcript levels, respectively.

Preparation of mycelium and tea samples

Spore suspensions (1 × 106 CFU/mL) of WT, ΔAcGγ, and OE::AcGγ strains were separately inoculated onto MYA agar medium without NaCl (100 μL per strain). The cultures were incubated at 28 °C in complete darkness for 7 days. To quantify phenotypic differences, the diameter of cleistothecia and the number of ascospores per fixed area (cm2) were measured using a hemocytometer under the microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., Wetzlar, Germany) across at least three independent biological replicates. Subsequently, the samples were collected and designated as WT1, WT2, WT3; Δ1, Δ2, Δ3; OE1, OE2, OE3, then lyophilized for subsequent analyses.

1 mL of spore suspensions (1 × 106 CFU/mL) of WT, ΔAcGγ, and OE::AcGγ strains were separately inoculated onto sterilized tea leaves that had been cooled to room temperature (20 g of primary dark tea leaves were mixed with 6 mL of deionized water and then sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min) (Liu et al., 2023). The samples were cultured in the dark at 28 °C for 10 days. Subsequently, a microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., Wetzlar, Germany) was used to observe the fermented samples. The fermented teas were collected and labeled as WT-T1, WT-T2, WT-T3; Δ-T1, Δ-T2, Δ-T3; OE-T1, OE-T2, OE-T3, then lyophilized for further testing. (WT1-WT3, Δ1-Δ3, and OE1-OE3 represent three completely independent biological replicates). While autoclaving (121 °C, 20 min) alters the native chemical composition and eliminates the natural microbiome of the tea matrix, this reductionist in vitro solid-state fermentation model was deliberately chosen. It ensures that any observed metabolomic shifts are strictly and solely attributable to the genetic manipulation of A. cristatus (AcGγ mutants), free from background microbial interference.

All experiments were conducted using three independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate was analyzed using three technical replicates. Samples were randomized during extraction and LC-MS/MS acquisition to minimize batch effects.

Nontargeted analysis based on LC-MS/MS

Sample extraction

After grinding, 100 mg of ground mycelial or fermented tea sample from the WT, ΔAcGγ, and OE::AcGγ strains were respectively extracted with 300 μL of methanol. Then, 20 μL of the internal standard substance (2-chloro-L-phenylalanine, CAS No.185030–83-5, purity ≥98%, 1 mg/mL in methanol, purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was added to the mixture. The mixture was vortexed for 30s, followed by a 10-min ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath (frequency: 40 kHz, power: 200 W). Subsequently, the samples were incubated at −20 °C for 1 h to precipitate proteins and further quench enzymatic activity. After centrifugation at 15,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C using a 5424R centrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), the supernatant was filtered through a 0.2-μm filter. Finally, 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 2-mL LC-MS glass vial. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots of all biological samples, and injected every 10 samples throughout the analytical run to monitor instrument stability and correct for signal drift.

LC-QTOF-MS analysis

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (B), with a gradient elution program set as follows: 0–1.0 min, 5% B; 1.0–9.0 min, 5%–95% B; 9.0–12.0 min, 95% B; 12.0–12.1 min, 95%–5% B; 12.1–15.0 min, 5% B for column re-equilibration. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2.0 μL. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes with a TOF scan range of m/z 50–1200. The key ion source parameters were configured as: ion spray voltage of 5500 V (positive) and − 4500 V (negative), source temperature of 550 °C, nebulizer gas (Gas 1) and heater gas (Gas 2) at 50 psi, curtain gas (CUR) at 35 psi, declustering potential of 80 V, and collision energy of 10 eV for full-scan acquisition.

Data acquisition was supported by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Metabolite identification and structural annotation were performed by matching mass spectrometry data against the highly curated, proprietary in-house MS2 database (MWDB, Metware Biotechnology). Following the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines, all reported differential metabolites achieve MSI Level 2 identification confidence (putatively annotated compounds); annotation was based on accurate precursor ion mass (MS1, mass tolerance <10 ppm), isotopic distribution patterns, and characteristic MS2 fragmentation matching, with a spectral matching score threshold above 0.80. However, the complete raw MS2 fragmentation spectra are subject to the proprietary restrictions of the commercial database. All metabolite annotations follow the MSI Level 2 confidence standard, consistent with common practice in exploratory untargeted metabolomics studies.

Data analysis

The raw MS data (.d files) file was converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard and analyzed with the R package XCMS (version 3.2). The processed data produced a matrix with retention time (RT), mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity. Subsequent peak annotation was performed using the R package CAMERA after XCMS analysis, and metabolite identification was executed using the internal MS2 database. A predefined method was used for data normalization to correct and eliminate systemic errors.

Prior to multivariate analysis, the raw MS peak areas were normalized to the internal standard and total ion current (TIC) to correct for systematic analytical errors. Subsequently, log-transformation and Pareto scaling were performed using SIMCA software (V14.1, Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umeå, Sweden) to reduce the influence of noise and stabilize variance. Principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were applied to visualize global metabolic differences among experimental groups. The OPLS-DA models were validated through a 7-fold cross-validation strategy. Moreover, a 200-iteration permutation test was carried out to ensure that the models were not overfitted, resulting in robust negative Q2 intercept values. Given the untargeted, exploratory nature of this study (designed for hypothesis generation rather than definitive biomarker verification), we adopted a stringent multi-criteria joint filtering strategy for differential metabolite identification, without applying false discovery rate (FDR) correction. A metabolite was defined as significantly differential only when it simultaneously satisfied all three criteria: (1) Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1.0 from the OPLS-DA model; (2) absolute fold change (FC) ≥ 2.0 or ≤ 0.5; (3) p < 0.05 from pairwise Student's t-test. This integrated approach combines multivariate model contribution, effect size magnitude, and univariate statistical significance, which is a widely accepted practice to balance discovery sensitivity and false positive control in exploratory untargeted metabolomics (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025).

All metabolomic analyses were performed with three independent biological replicates, each analyzed in three technical replicates to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the data. The final results are presented on the basis of biological replicates, with each biological replicate value representing the mean of its three corresponding technical replicates. Given the highly controlled laboratory solid-state fermentation conditions employed in this study, sample matrices exhibit high homogeneity and substantially lower biological variability than ecological or clinical cohorts. The stringent statistical validation and differential filtering protocols described above were applied to minimize false positive identifications and ensure analytical robustness. All metabolomic interpretations in this work are framed as exploratory and associative rather than definitive causal conclusions. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), and OPLS-DA were performed using the SIMCA-P software (V14.1, Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umeå, Sweden) and heatmaps were performed using the Metware Cloud. Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test to assess differences between groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using the Metware Cloud platform.

Results and discussion

AcGγ affects colony color and morphology

The AcGγ gene spans 387 bp in length and contains three exons. Its CDS is 273 bp, encoding a 90-amino-acid protein with a conserved G-gamma superfamily domain. The ΔAcGγ and OE::AcGγ strains were screened and validated by PCR (Fig. S1 A, S1B, S2A, S2B). RT-qPCR validation confirmed the complete ablation of AcGγ gene expression in ΔAcGγ strain (Fig. S1C); the relative expression level of AcGγ in three OE::AcGγ strains was 8.3-, 9.2- and 13.6-fold that of WT (Fig. S2C). This indicates that the ΔAcGγ and OE::AcGγ have been obtained. Subsequent experiments will be carried out with OE::AcGγ strain whose relative expression level of AcGγ is 13.6 times that of WT strain.

On NaCl-free MYA agar medium, no discernible morphological differences were observed between WT and OE::AcGγ strains. Both exhibited concentric ring patterns with dark brown pigmentation at the colony center transitioning to yellowish peripheries, while maintaining comparable colony diameters (Fig. 1A-a, i; Fig. 1B). In contrast, ΔAcGγ colonies displayed distinct phenotypic characteristics, featuring light gray central regions surrounded by off-white margins, accompanied by a significant 23.0% reduction in colony diameter compared to WT controls (Fig. 1A-a, e; Fig. 1B). On MYA agar medium containing 1.0 M NaCl, the ΔAcGγ strain exhibited distinct morphological alterations compared to the WT strain, including fluffier colony texture with sponge-like architecture, paler coloration (pale yellow phenotype), irregular hyphal extension patterns at colony margins, and a 6.2% reduction in colony diameter (Fig. 1A-b, f; Fig. 1C). In contrast, the OE::AcGγ strain showed an 8.1% increase in colony diameter while maintaining comparable morphology and pigmentation to the WT (Fig. 1A-b, j; Fig. 1C). These results demonstrate that deletion of the AcGγ gene resulted in phenotypic alterations in A. cristatus, including colony color lightening, fluffy morphology with irregular margins, and reduced growth rate. In contrast, overexpression of AcGγ led to accelerated fungal growth. The research found that gene mutations can cause changes in colony pigmentation, morphology, and growth rate of filamentous fungi. For example, the deletion of the alb1, ayg1, arp1, and abr1 in Penicillium roqueforti leads to the colonies turning white, yellow-green, pink, and brown respectively (Cleere et al., 2024). In Yarrowia lipolytica, the overexpression of the YH1 results in the formation of fluffy mycelium in the colonies (Li et al., 2014). The overexpression of the TVPHSP70 in Trichoderma viride significantly increases the colony diameter, accelerates the growth rate, leads to more branching, and affects the colony morphology (Huang et al., 2024). AcGγ is required for the development of the cleistothecium and ascospore

Microstructural analysis revealed that the ΔAcGγ strain failed to produce cleistothecia and ascospores, while the OE::AcGγ strain exhibited a 17.0% increase in cleistothecium diameter compared to the WT (Fig. 1A-c, g, h, k; Fig. 1C; Fig. 1E and Fig. 1F). Notably, under both 0 M and 1 M NaCl conditions on MYA agar medium, OE::AcGγ demonstrated a 14.0% and 12.0% increase in ascospore density per unit area, respectively, relative to the WT (Fig. 1A-d, l; Fig. 1E and Fig. 1F). The cleistothecium is an essential structure for the normal sexual reproduction of A. cristatus and a necessary condition for the formation of ascospores. Impaired development of the cleistothecium can affect sexual development. For example, the deletion of AcMAT1–1-1 or AcMAT1–2-1 results in abnormal development of the cleistothecium in A. cristatus, preventing the production of ascospores and hindering sexual development (Ge et al., 2019). The deletion of Acsko1 leads to a reduction in both cleistothecium and ascospores of A. cristatus weakening its sexual reproduction ability (Shao et al., 2025). The overexpression of AcrpnR causes the growth and development of the cleistothecium in A. cristatus to slow down, delaying sexual development (Wang et al., 2021b). This indicates that AcGγ is involved in regulating sexual development and controlling the formation of the cleistothecium and ascospores in A. cristatus.

Characteristics of post-fermented teas

The fermentation outcomes of WT, ΔAcGγ, and OE::AcGγ strains in dark teas are shown in Fig. 2. Compared to the WT strain, tea fermented with the ΔAcGγ strain exhibited a distinct pale yellowish-white coloration (Fig. 2-a, d and g). In contrast, the OE::AcGγ strain showed no significant alterations in tea pigmentation relative to the control group. Microstructural analysis revealed that dark tea fermented by both WT and OE::AcGγ strains exhibited no evident mycelium and displayed golden-yellow. Notably, the OE::AcGγ strain samples showed a higher abundance of cleistothecia. In contrast, tea samples fermented by the ΔAcGγ strain were characterized by extensive mycelial networks adhered to the surface, complete absence of cleistothecia, and pale-yellow pigmentation (Fig. 2-b, e and h). The tea infusions of dark tea fermented by ΔAcGγ, WT, and OE::AcGγ strains all exhibited a reddish-brown coloration, with progressively deeper hues observed in the order of WT < ΔAcGγ < OE::AcGγ (Fig. 2-c, f and i). The study revealed that A. cristatus synthesizes polyphenol oxidase during cleistothecia maturation, which catalyzes the oxidation of catechin derivatives into water-soluble pigments (theaflavins, thearubigins, and theabrownins). This enzymatic process serves as the biochemical basis for the distinctive yellow-brown pigmentation of fungal structures and the development of orange-hued infusions (Jiang et al., 2022). Based on qualitative visual observations, the paler yellowish-white appearance of tea fermented with the ΔAcGγ strain is primarily attributed to the absence of cleistothecia. These cleistothecia are the “golden flowers” produced during the sexual reproduction of A. cristatus—the dominant microorganism in Fuzhuan tea fermentation. Their quantity and quality serve as critical criteria for evaluating tea quality and health benefits (Li et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2021a). The abundance of cleistothecia plays an important role in aroma, taste and health benefits of dark tea. During the developmental phase of cleistothecia, A. cristatus produce multiple beneficial metabolites that not only participate in the oxidation of tea polyphenols and the formation of volatile aromatic compounds, but also demonstrate multidimensional health benefits including lipid metabolism regulation, blood pressure homeostasis, and glucose metabolism modulation. Dark teas containing the cleistothecia of A. cristatus (e.g., Fuzhuan tea) exhibit remarkable advantages in aroma complexity, flavor profile, and health benefits (Chen et al., 2021, 2024; Lin et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019). This study employed ΔAcGγ strain (non-cleistothecia-producing), WT and OE::AcGγ strains (cleistothecia-producing) for primary dark tea fermentation. Non-targeted metabolomics analysis of the mycelia and teas between WT and AcGγ mutants

To investigate the effects of AcGγ on A. cristatus physiology and flavor metabolism in fermented dark tea, we performed non-targeted LC-MS analysis on two sets of samples: mycelia of WT, ΔAcGγ and OE::AcGγ strains cultured on 0 M NaCl MYA solid medium for 7 days, and primary dark tea fermented with each strain for 10 days. PCA analysis revealed distinct separation among the metabolites of WT, ΔAcGγ, and OE::AcGγ mycelia, with tight intra-group clusters (Fig. 3A). This indicates significant differences in the metabolites of mycelia in the three strains alongside high reproducibility within biological replicates. Similarly, there were significant differences and high reproducibility within biological replicates in the metabolites of fermented dark tea by the three strains (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, HCA of the mycelial metabolites from WT, ΔAcGγ, and OE::AcGγ strains revealed tight clustering patterns among triplicate biological replicates (Fig. 3B), demonstrating high inter-replicate consistency. The results of OPLS-DA analysis showed that the WT, ΔAcGγ, and OE::AcGγ strains could be well separated with good reproducibility (Fig. 3C). In this model, the R2X, R2Y, and Q2 scores were 0.909, 1.000, and 0.998 respectively. A 200-iteration permutation test was performed to verify model reliability, with intercept values of R2 = (0.00, 0.22) and Q2 = (0.00, −0.31), confirming the absence of overfitting. Similarly, for the metabolites of dark tea fermented by the three strains, the R2X, R2Y, and Q2 scores in the OPLS-DA model were 0.798, 1.000, and 0.987 respectively (Fig. 3F). The corresponding permutation test yielded intercepts of R2 = (0.00, 0.25) and Q2 = (0.00, −0.28), which also verified no model overfitting. This indicates that the OPLS-DA model is suitable for further screening of differential metabolites (Li et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2021a). Changes in differential metabolites during solid-state fermentation of the mycelia between WT and AcGγ mutants

A comparative analysis of mycelial metabolites between WT and ΔAcGγ strains identified 261 differential metabolites, 122 upregulated and 139 downregulated (Table S2). The heatmap revealed distinct clustering patterns of these metabolites into two separate color regions (Fig. 4A), supporting the pronounced metabolic divergence between the two strains. The differential metabolites were categorized into 12 classes, predominantly falling into organic acids and derivatives, organoheterocyclic compounds, benzenoids, and organic oxygen compounds (Table S2). Similarly, comparative analysis between WT and OE::AcGγ strains identified 175 differential metabolites, comprising 90 upregulated and 85 downregulated (Table S2). The heatmap depicted the relative abundance variations of differential metabolites between the two groups, which were notably clustered into two distinct color regions (Fig. 5A), indicating significant metabolic divergence. These differential metabolites were categorized into nine classes, with predominant enrichment in organic acids and derivatives, organoheterocyclic compounds, benzenoids, and organic oxygen compounds (Table S2). These differential metabolites, as critical components of A. cristatus metabolites, exhibit diverse bioactivities and demonstrate potential applications in food science and pharmaceutical development (Jiang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2024).

Organic acids and derivatives

Organic acids and their derivatives play a crucial functional role in the pharmaceutical and food industries due to their versatile properties (Chuchird et al., 2015). In this study, a total of 69 differential metabolites of organic acids and derivatives were identified in the WT and ΔAcGγ strains, with 32 upregulated and 37 downregulated (Table S2). Through non-targeted metabolomics, we identified differential metabolites such as S-carboxymethyl-L-cysteine and N2-fructopyranosylarginine, which have been previously reported in the literature to possess antioxidant properties in vitro (Khizar et al., 2020; Waters et al., 2024). However, since our current study is fundamentally based on metabolomic profiling, downstream in vitro or in vivo functional bioassays are strictly required to validate the actual health benefits and physiological bioaccessibility of the AcGγ-mediated fermentation matrix.

A total of 53 differential metabolites of organic acids and derivatives were identified in both the WT and OE::AcGγ strains, with 26 upregulated and 27 downregulated (Table S2). Many of these substances are widely used in the food industry. Among the upregulated metabolites, tryptophyl-lysine, a bioactive dipeptide, has been reported to enhance protein utilization and improve nutritional value (Fig. 5B) (Salyer et al., 2013). Among the downregulated metabolites, aspartyl-aspartate is a naturally occurring dipeptide formed by aspartic acid condensation, involved in fungal amino acid turnover and serving as a potential flavor precursor (Fig. 5B) (Hussein et al., 2022). These findings provide a preliminary metabolic basis for exploring the edible value of genetically modified A. cristatus.

Organoheterocyclic compounds

Organoheterocyclic compounds serve as vital sources of flavor-active components in numerous food products, while exhibiting significant biological activities including antimicrobial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties (Xu et al., 2019). A total of 68 differential metabolites of organoheterocyclic compounds were identified in both the WT and ΔAcGγ strains, with 32 upregulated and 36 downregulated (Table S2). Many of these substances are related to the flavor - active components in foods and possess health benefits such as preventing hypertension, providing neuroprotection, and improving mood. Metabolites such as DL-tryptophan were found to be upregulated. Although previous literature indicates that DL-tryptophan is a precursor to compounds related to sleep regulation (Stewart et al., 1973), its actual bio-efficacy in fermented dark tea would depend on its final concentration, extraction efficiency during brewing, and human bioavailability. This necessitates further quantitative and in vivo investigation. Among the downregulated metabolites, theophylline (1,3-dimethylxanthine), a naturally occurring xanthine alkaloid, has been reported to exert antioxidant and neuroprotective activities (Fig. 4C) (Mitro et al., 2024). These metabolic differences expand our understanding of the secondary metabolic profile of A. cristatus regulated by AcGγ.

A total of 35 differential metabolites of organoheterocyclic compounds were identified in both the WT and OE::AcGγ strains, with 21 upregulated and 14 downregulated (Table S2). Many of these substances possess health benefits such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. For instance, among the upregulated metabolites, the oxidized metabolites of 4-methylpyrrolo[1,2-a]pyrazine can inhibit cell apoptosis and have significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 5C) (Kang et al., 2012). Among the downregulated metabolites, kynurenic acid, an endogenous tryptophan metabolite, functions as a reactive oxygen species scavenger with demonstrated antioxidant and neuroprotective properties (Fig. 5C) (BratekGerej et al., 2021). These results characterize the impact of AcGγ overexpression on the organoheterocyclic metabolic profile of A. cristatus, laying a preliminary metabolic basis for exploring the potential health-associated properties of the fungus.

Benzenoids

Benzenoids are widely used as food additives, preservatives and flavoring agents, with well-documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anticancer activities in experimental studies (Bampidis et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2007). A total of 40 differential benzenoid metabolites were identified in the WT and ΔAcGγ strains, with 15 upregulated and 25 downregulated (Table S2). Many of these substances possess health benefits such as antioxidant, antiviral, and anticancer properties according to in vitro reports. Among the upregulated metabolites, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid exhibits antioxidant and antimicrobial activities and has various potential applications in the food and pharmaceutical industries (Fig. 4D) (Tian et al., 2018). Among the downregulated metabolites, protocatechuic acid, a common natural phenolic acid, has been reported to exert antioxidant and antiproliferative effects in vitro (Fig. 4D) (Ma et al., 2018). Therefore, the study of benzenoids in the ΔAcGγ strain can help us better understand and explore the potential applications of A. cristatus in the pharmaceutical field.

A total of 24 differential benzenoid metabolites were identified in the WT and OE::AcGγ strains, with 11 upregulated and 13 downregulated (Table S2). These metabolites are commonly used as food additives, flavor enhancers, antioxidants, preservatives, and antibacterial agents. For instance, among the upregulated metabolites, 2-propenyl phenylacetate, which has the aroma of bananas and honey, is used as a spice in the food industry to enhance food flavor (Fig. 5D) (Sax et al., 1989). Vanillic acid is widely present in fermented foods as a natural flavor enhancer with antioxidant and preservative properties (Fig. 5D) (Li et al., 2024). Therefore, the research on benzenoids in the OE::AcGγ strain is expected to significantly improve the quality and safety of food. These metabolic changes provide preliminary insight into how AcGγ overexpression may modulate the flavor-related benzenoid profile of A. cristatus, while the actual impact on food quality remains to be evaluated.

Organic oxygen compounds

Organic oxygen compounds, which exhibit antioxidant, preservative, and antimicrobial properties, have been widely applied in the food industry to enhance product safety, extend shelf life, and improve health benefits as documented in prior studies (Taban et al., 2021; Vital et al., 2016). A total of 24 differential metabolites of organic oxygen compounds were identified in both the WT and ΔAcGγ strains, with 13 upregulated and 11 downregulated (Table S2). Many of these substances possess beneficial effects such as antibacterial, anti-infective, immunomodulatory properties, and positive impacts on gut health. For instance, among the upregulated metabolites, 3-hydroxykynurenine exerts antibacterial and anti-infective effects by restricting bacterial expansion, inhibiting protein synthesis, or depriving bacteria of iron ions (Fig. 4E) (Yao et al., 2011). Among the downregulated metabolites, polyribophosphate plays a role in food health care through its antibacterial, immunomodulatory functions, and contributions to gut health (Fig. 4E) (Brehm-Stecher, 2016; Kashima et al., 2015). These data characterize the impact of AcGγ deletion on the organic oxygen metabolic profile of A. cristatus, providing a metabolic basis for exploring its potential application value.

A total of 13 differential metabolites of organic oxygen compounds were identified in the WT and OE::AcGγ strains, with 10 being upregulated and 3 downregulated (Table S2). Many of these metabolites possess anti-inflammatory and gut-regulating functions and are commonly used as food thickeners and stabilizers. For instance, among the upregulated metabolites, sedoheptulose 1-phosphate has anti-inflammatory properties and can alleviate diabetic nephropathy by modulating glucose metabolic pathways (Fig. 5E) (Katakura, 2022). Levan, widely used as a thickener and stabilizer in the food industry, can also regulate the gut microbial community and promote the production of short-chain fatty acids (Fig. 5E) (Yassunaka et al., 2023). Therefore, these findings provide preliminary metabolic evidence for exploring the quality improvement potential of AcGγ-overexpressing strains.

Changes in differential metabolites during solid-state fermentation of the dark tea between WT and AcGγ mutants

A total of 205 differential metabolites were identified between the dark tea metabolites fermented by the WT and ΔAcGγ strains, including 144 upregulated and 61 downregulated metabolites (Table S3). The heatmap depicts the changes in the relative abundances of these differential metabolites. The differential metabolites of the two strains significantly clustered into two distinct color regions (Fig. 6A), indicating a significant difference between them. These differential metabolites can be classified into 12 categories, mainly concentrated in substances such as organic acids and derivatives, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, and lipids and lipid-like molecules (Table S3). Among them, amino acids and derivatives, flavonoids, and lipids and lipid-like molecules account for 77.0% of the major differential metabolites (Table S4). Similarly, a total of 117 differential metabolites were identified between the dark tea metabolites fermented by the WT and OE::AcGγ strains, including 67 upregulated and 50 downregulated metabolites (Table S3). The heatmap depicts the changes in the relative abundances of these differential metabolites. The differential metabolites of the two strains are significantly clustered into two distinct color regions (Fig. 7A), indicating a significant difference between them. These differential metabolites can be classified into 12 categories, mainly concentrated in substances such as organic acids and derivatives, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, and lipids and lipid-like molecules (Table S3). Among them, amino acids and derivatives, flavonoids, and lipids and lipid-like molecules account for 84.0% of the major differential metabolites (Table S5). These substances play a crucial role in the formation of the dark tea flavor (Liu et al., 2023), suggesting that AcGγ-mediated cleistothecium development is associated with the remodeling of putative flavor-related metabolite profiles during dark tea fermentation.

Amino acids and derivatives

Amino acids and their derivatives are putatively associated with the sweetness, bitterness, and umami of Fuzhuan tea (Ma et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2020). In the dark tea fermented by the WT and ΔAcGγ strains, there were 44 differential metabolites of amino acids and their derivatives, accounting for 75.0% of organic acids and their derivatives. Among them, 36 were upregulated and 8 were downregulated (Table S4). In the dark tea fermented by the WT and OE::AcGγ strains, there were 21 differential metabolites of amino acids and their derivatives, accounting for 70.0% of organic acids and their derivatives. Among them, 14 were upregulated and 7 were downregulated (Table S5). Glycine, threonine, alanine, and proline are associated with sweetness (Yu et al., 2023). In the dark tea fermented by the ΔAcGγ strain, the amino acids and their derivatives related to sweetness, namely tyrosylhydroxyproline, L-threonine, threonyltyrosine, N,N′-bis(g-glutamyl)-3,3′-(1,2-propylenedithio)dialanine, gamma-glutamylhydroxyproline, and prolylproline were significantly upregulated; while L-hydroxyproline, furylacryloylalanyllysine, and glycylglycine were significantly downregulated (Fig. 6B-a; Table S4). In the dark tea fermented by the OE::AcGγ strain, the amino acids and their derivatives related to sweetness, such as L-proline,1-(N-((3-((propylamino)carbonyl)oxiranyl)carbonyl)-L-isoleucyl)-,(2S-trans)-, threonyltyrosine were significantly upregulated; whereas furylacryloylalanyllysine and gamma-glutamyl-beta-(isoxazolin-5-on-2-yl)alanine were significantly downregulated (Fig. 7B-a; Table S5). This suggests that AcGγ is involved in amino acid metabolism during dark tea fermentation and may alter the profile of sweet-taste related metabolites.

Histidine, arginine, valine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, and tryptophan are associated with bitterness (Yu et al., 2023). In the dark tea fermented by the ΔAcGγ strain, the bitterness-related amino acids and their derivatives, such as lysyltryptophan, L-isoleucine, polylysine, gamma-glutamylarginine, tryptophyl-tryptophan were significantly upregulated, while 5-hydroxy-4-oxo-L-norvaline was significantly downregulated (Fig. 6B-b; Table S4). In the dark tea fermented by the OE::AcGγ strain, the bitterness-related amino acids and their derivatives, such as histidinyl-lysine, L-alanyl-L-leucine, L-proline,1-(N-((3-((propylamino)carbonyl)oxiranyl)carbonyl)-L-isoleucyl)-,(2S-trans)-, L-isoleucine were significantly upregulated, while glutamylhistidine and 5-hydroxy-4-oxo-L-norvaline were significantly downregulated (Fig. 7B-b; Table S5). This indicates that the AcGγ gene is associated with the metabolism of bitter-related amino acids during dark tea fermentation.

Glutamate and tyrosine are associated with umami (Yu et al., 2023). In the dark tea fermented by the ΔAcGγ strain, the umami-related amino acids and their derivatives, namely urea glutamate, glucose-6-glutamate, alanyl-tyrosine, threonyltyrosine, L-tyrosine were significantly upregulated, and there were no significantly downregulated metabolites (Fig. 6B-c; Table S4). In contrast, in the dark tea fermented by the OE::AcGγ strain, there were no differential metabolites among the umami-related amino acids and their derivatives. Collectively, AcGγ-mediated cleistothecium development is associated with the remodeling of amino acid profiles during dark tea fermentation, which is predicted to influence umami taste properties.

Flavonoids

Flavonoids serve as the primary contributors to the bitterness, astringency, and distinctive aroma characteristics of Fuzhuan tea, while also exhibiting multiple health benefits as reported in the literature (Zheng et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2020). In the dark tea fermented by WT and ΔAcGγ strains, there were 13 flavonoids and 1 isoflavonoid among the differential metabolites. Among them, 9 were upregulated and 5 were downregulated (Table S4). Flavonoid glycosides were the main types, which had an impact on the bitterness, astringency, antioxidant capacity, and anti-cancer properties of tea. For example, quercetin 3-(3″-acetyl-alpha-L-arabinofuranoside) affects the bitterness, astringency, and astringent taste of tea (Fig. 6C) (Chen et al., 2022). Myricetin 3-alpha-L-arabinofuranoside enhances the bitterness and astringency (Fig. 6C) (Xiao et al., 2023). Quercetin 3-rhamnosyl-(1- > 2)-glucosyl-(1- > 6)-galactoside has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-cancer effects (Fig. 6C) (Zhao et al., 2022). In the dark tea fermented by WT and OE::AcGγ strains, there were 10 differential metabolites of flavonoids and 1 isoflavonoids. Among them, 7 were upregulated and 4 were downregulated (Table S5). Flavonoid glycosides were predominant, which have an impact on balancing the taste, aroma of tea and immune regulation. For example, the differential metabolite quercitrin can increase the absorption of catechins (such as EGCG) in tea, enhance its bioavailability and reduce methylation, and potentially strengthen the antioxidant, neuroprotective effects and immune regulation of EGCG (Fig. 7C) (Wang et al., 2012). (2S)-4,5,7-trihydroxy-2-(3,4,5-trihydroxyphenyl)-4H-chromen-3-one belongs to polyphenolic compounds, which has a structure of multiple hydroxyl groups and aromatic rings. It can balance the bitterness, astringency and sweetness of tea and has a positive effect on the aroma of tea (Fig. 7C) (Ponmurugan et al., 2019). This indicates that AcGγ-mediated cleistothecium development is associated with flavonoid metabolic remodeling during dark tea fermentation, which may influence taste, aroma and bioactive potential.

Lipids and lipid-like molecules

Lipids and lipid-like molecules have been identified as key flavor compounds in tea. These compounds are associated with the taste, fruity taste, oiliness and fatty flower fragrance of tea (Zhang et al., 2022a). In the dark tea fermented by the WT and ΔAcGγ strains, there were 18 differential metabolites of lipids and lipid - like molecules, with 12 upregulated and 6 downregulated (Table S4). These metabolites are related to the floral, creamy, and fruity flavors of tea. For example, glyceryl 5-hydroxydecanoate as a hydroxyl fatty acid glyceride metabolite, is putatively considered to contribute creamy, fatty, and fruity flavors based on its structural properties and the common flavor characteristics of similar lipids (Fig. 6D). (3S,7S)-jasmonic acid, an important component of the aroma of dark tea, has a sweet jasmine-like fragrance (Fig. 6D) (Xu et al., 2019). In the dark tea fermented by WT and OE::AcGγ strains, there were 10 differential metabolites of lipids and lipid-like molecules, among which 6 were upregulated and 4 were down-regulated (Table S5). These metabolites can endow the tea with unique aromas such as tea cream and caramel. For example, caproleic acid has a sweet flavor of cheese and cream (Fig. 7D) (Zhang et al., 2022b). During the heating process, 4-methyl-5-carboxyethyl-2-furanpentanoic acid degrades to produce 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furancarboxylic acid, forming unique aromas and flavors such as caramel and cream (Fig. 7D) (Reghizzi, 2016). Taken together, AcGγ-mediated cleistothecium development is associated with the remodeling of lipid and lipid-like metabolite profiles during dark tea fermentation, which may contribute to the aroma and mouthfeel of the product.

Conclusion

This study investigated the function of AcGγ and its effects on metabolite biosynthesis in A. cristatus, with a further exploration of its regulatory role during dark tea fermentation. The results revealed that AcGγ plays a key role in regulating the sexual development of A. cristatus, significantly influencing colony morphology, colony color, and governing the formation of cleistothecia and ascospores. Furthermore, AcGγ-mediated cleistothecium development is closely associated with the remodeling of both putative flavor-related metabolites and bioactive compound profiles. Notably, it is involved in the metabolic remodeling of amino acids and derivatives, flavonoids, and lipid-like molecules during dark tea fermentation. These metabolic shifts are predicted to alter the profiles of sweet, bitter, and umami taste-related metabolites, as well as putative aroma-associated compounds. Many of these metabolites have been reported to exhibit multifaceted bioactivities including antioxidant, anticancer, immunoregulatory, and neuroprotective properties in prior studies. While the opposing phenotypes of the knockout and overexpression strains in this study strongly implicate AcGγ in cleistothecium formation, the inherent pleiotropic effects of G-protein signaling pathways cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies utilizing complementation strains will be necessary to definitively isolate these specific genetic effects.

In conclusion, AcGγ serves as a core genetic regulator of cleistothecia formation in A. cristatus. The presence of AcGγ and the resulting cleistothecia modulates the solid-state fermentation matrix, shaping the biosynthesis and release of metabolites in key flavor-associated pathways, specifically amino acids putatively linked to sweet and umami taste attributes, flavonoids, and short-chain lipids. These specific metabolic fluxes highlight the structural and biochemical importance of cleistothecia in metabolic quality modulation of dark tea.
