FoCUS on Lazarus: autoresuscitation confirmed by focused cardiac ultrasound
Authors: Christophe Bianchi, Ludovic Maudet, Alexandra Schneider, Elena Garcia, Mathieu Pasquier
Published: 2025

To the editor,

Autoresuscitation after termination of resuscitation − also known as “Lazarus phenomenon”, is characterized by the return on spontaneous circulation (ROSC) following the cessation of cardio-pulmonary resuscitation (CPR) efforts. It remains an underreported condition, notably due to legal liability concerns, potential emotional distress among healthcare professionals, bystanders and relatives, and fear of being perceived as having terminated resuscitation prematurely.1, 2, 3 Supporting physicians in these situations is therefore warranted. For this purpose, focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) may represent a valuable adjunct, not only to objectify ROSC by identifying mechanical cardiac contractions associated with clinical signs of autoresuscitation, but also to reinforce diagnostic confidence among healthcare professionals confronted with such cases.

A 78-year-old man with a known history of coronary heart disease sustained an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) while shoveling snow in front of his house. The cardiac arrest (CA) was witnessed by his son, who initiated CPR. Upon EMS arrival, ventricular fibrillation (VF) was identified. Despite multiple defibrillation attempts and sequential administration of epinephrine and amiodarone, VF persisted. A FoCUS using a subxiphoid view confirmed the absence of organized ventricular contractility, pericardial effusion, or signs of ascending aortic dissection. Resuscitation efforts were terminated 40 minutes after the collapse.

Three minutes after cessation of CPR, a paramedic noticed spontaneous breathing at a rate of 10 breaths per minute and detected a carotid pulse. End-tidal CO2 raised from 10 mmHg to 35 mmHg. A repeated FoCUS demonstrated organized ventricular contractions, confirming ROSC following spontaneous defibrillation. Non-invasive blood pressure was 190/140 mmHg with a sinus rhythm on electrocardiogram.

A shared decision was made with the patient’s family to transport him to the hospital but to withhold any new resuscitation attempt, should CA occur again. Five minutes after ROSC, ventricular fibrillation recurred, and FoCUS showed the absence of organized cardiac activity. Death was pronounced ten minutes later, in accordance with local current recommendations.

We presented a case of autoresuscitation (Lazarus phenomenon), confirmed by FoCUS and due to spontaneous defibrillation.

FoCUS is now included as a supplementary tool in international guidelines.4 It has been studied in multiple clinical settings, including the development of image quality rating scales.5, 6 Furthermore, it can assist in identifying reversible causes of CA, such as cardiac tamponade or tension pneumothorax. Although its utility in evaluating cardiac contraction needs to be further evaluated, FoCUS represents an useful adjunct in helping clinicians managing CA. In our case, FoCUS was employed at three key moments during this refractory VF: initially to rule out cardiac tamponade, and subsequently to confirm delayed ROSC 4 min after termination of resuscitation. This technique may assist healthcare teams in recognizing the spontaneous appearance of vital signs by directly visualizing mechanical cardiac activity associated with an electrical activity, a palpable pulse, and/or a measurable blood pressure.

In summary, our case highlights the potential utility of FoCUS in managing autoresuscitations cases, including in the prehospital setting. However, the specific role of FoCUS in such situations remain unclear and should be further defined in future researches.
