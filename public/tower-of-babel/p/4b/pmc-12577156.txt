Ventricular septum curvature ratio: a novel imaging marker to predict clinical deterioration in normotensive acute pulmonary embolism
Authors: Yizhuo Gao, Zhichun Gu, Xuejiao Wei, Shibo Wei, Yuchen Liu, Shanshan Zhan, Jing Yang, Chuanxin Qi, Shougang Qi, Minggang Wang, Dong Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying high-probability deterioration individuals at the time of admission remains a challenge in normotensive patients with acute pulmonary embolism (PE). Ventricular septum (VS) deviation, as observed on computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), has been associated with poor prognosis in acute PE patients. However, the quantitative relationship between the VS curvature ratio and clinical deterioration in normotensive acute PE patients has not been fully explored. This study aimed to investigate the role of the VS curvature ratio derived from CTPA as an image marker for deterioration in normotensive acute PE patients.MethodsThis prospective multicenter cohort study enrolled normotensive patients with acute PE across five centers in China between January 2021 and June 2024. The VS and left ventricle (LV) free-wall curvature radii were measured using CTPA, and the VS curvature ratio was calculated and categorized. The endpoint was the occurrence of clinical deterioration event within 30 days after admission. The relationship between the VS curvature ratio and deterioration was analyzed using hierarchical logistic regression. The efficacy of identifying deterioration was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsOf 3,310 patients, 272 (8.2%) experienced deterioration. In 3,030 patients from three centres, VS were categorized into four groups: VS bowing to the RV with its curvature ratio as < 1.60, 1.60-<1.90, ≥ 1.90, respectively; and the fourth group with VS straight or bowing to the LV. Using a VS curvature ratio < 1.60 as the reference, patients with higher VS curvature ratios had an increased risk of deterioration (odds ratio: 22.94 for ≥ 1.90 and 22.95 for VS straight & bowing to the LV, both p < 0.05). The VS curvature ratio demonstrated good discrimination for deterioration, with an AUC of 0.83 in the three local centers and 0.84 in the two non-local centers (280 patients).ConclusionsThe VS curvature ratio, derived from CTPA images, is a promising image marker for predicting deterioration in normotensive acute PE patients. This study highlights the potential clinical impact of VS curvature ratio to identify normotensive PE patients at increased risk of deterioration and to adjust management strategies accordingly.Clinical trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05098769, October 16,2021, retrospectively registered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05708-w.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, Ventricular septum, Computed tomography pulmonary angiography, Clinical deterioration

Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is the third leading cause of cardiovascular mortality after myocardial infarction and stroke [1]. Approximately 90% of acute PE patients are normotensive upon admission, yet they account for over 80% of acute PE-related early deaths [2]. Early identification of deterioration is critical for timely therapeutic intervention [3, 4]. However, reliable markers for predicting deterioration in normotensive patients remain elusive as current risk stratification methods are insufficient [5].

The primary cause of deterioration in acute PE is right ventricular (RV) overload, which leads to acute RV failure due to thrombus-induced blockage of pulmonary circulation [6]. In acute RV failure, ventricular interdependence reduces left ventricular (LV) filling and decreases the LV stroke volume. This ultimately results in low cardiac output and cardiogenic shock, as the RV and LV share the same pericardial space, which restricts the motion of both ventricles [7]. An increase in ventricular septum (VS) curvature may indicate RV overload and its negative impact on LV function, reflecting transmural pressure gradients between the RV and LV [8]. Thus, the VS curvature could be a useful marker for evaluating the deterioration risk associated with acute PE.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the most widely used diagnostic tool for acute PE [9], providing in addition valuable information about the morphology of the heart chambers. Previous studies have suggested that VS deviation observed in CTPA may be a potential predictor of poor prognosis in normotensive acute PE patients [10, 11]. However, the ability of VS deviation to reliably predict deterioration remains debatable [12], primarily because the direction of VS deviation in the transverse plane on CTPA may not adequately reflect ventricular interdependence. Therefore, measuring the VS curvature ratio, which is the ratio of the VS curvature radius to the LV free wall curvature radius, could provide a more accurate assessment of the deterioration risk in acute PE patients. This prospective multicenter cohort study aimed to investigate the relationship between the VS curvature ratio and clinical deterioration in normotensive acute PE patients, and to evaluate its potential as a biomarker for identifying high-risk individuals.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter observational study was conducted between January 2021 and June 2024 as a part of a prospective ongoing acute PE cohort, named the Adverse Outcomes in Acute Pulmonary Embolism patients using Computed Tomography pulmonary angiography (AOAPECT) cohort (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05098769). This study included hospitalized patients with acute PE from five large tertiary care hospitals across China. These institutions are regional medical centers with integrated medical-system databases.

Three local centers were used to analyze the correlation between the VS curvature ratio and deterioration, and to assess its discriminatory power for deterioration. Data from two nonlocal centers were used to validate the predictive capacity of the VS curvature ratio. Additionally, a subgroup of patients diagnosed with acute PE via electrocardiogram-gated computed tomography angiography (ECG-gated CTA) was included to examine the effect of cardiac cycle on the measurement of the VS curvature ratio.

This study was approved by the hospital’s Institutional Ethics Committee (No. 2021PS783K), and informed consent was obtained in accordance with the requirements of each local committee. The cohort was established according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

Patients with acute symptomatic PE who were normotensive at the time of admission were enrolled in this study. Normotension was defined as the absence of PE-related hemodynamic instability, such as cardiac arrest, obstructive shock, or persistent hypotension, based on the 2019 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines and as documented in the admission medical records. Details of hemodynamic instability are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) diagnosis of acute PE confirmed by CTPA, and (3) normotensive status. Exclusion criteria were: (1) receipt of reperfusion therapy; (2) pregnancy; (3) missing CTPA data for reconstruction; (4) absence of cardiac troponin I (c-Tn I) or N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-pro BNP) data; (5) absence of echocardiography within 24 h of admission; (6) incomplete medical records; and (7) loss to follow-up. The details of inclusion and exclusion criteria were described in Supplementary Figure S1. Demographic information, vital signs, laboratory data, medical history, and CTPA data were collected from the electronic medical records and documented in a unified case report form.

Measurement of VS curvature ratio and VS deviation to LV in CTPA

The CTPA imaging protocols are detailed in Supplementary Method S1. CTPA reconstructions were performed using the Mimics Medical software (version 19.0, Mimics Medical Software, Leuven, Belgium). The short-axis view of the RV was reconstructed using double-oblique multiplanar reformation [13]. For VS bowing to the RV, the VS curvature ratio was calculated as the ratio of the VS curvature radius to the LV free wall curvature radius (Fig. 1A). VS curvature ratio was segmented according to its curve-fitting relationship with deterioration risk. As VS curvature radius increasing until a point where no curvature radius can be calculated, VS straight & bowing to the LV was defined (Fig. 1B). For VS bowing to the RV, the following measurements were taken: (1) locating the two intersection points between the VS and the LV free wall, and use them as common points for measuring the curvature radii of the VS and the LV free wall. (2) finding the third point, which can be combined with the first two intersection points to create a circle that best fits the VS, and then calculate the VS curvature radius. (3) finding the fourth point, which can be combined with the first two intersection points to create a circle that best fits the LV free wall, and then calculate the LV free wall radius (Fig. 1C).

Supplementary Figure S2 and Supplementary Appendix outline a simplified method for measuring the three side lengths of the triangle inscribed in the LV to calculate the VS curvature. This method allows the VS and LV free wall curvature radius to be measured using Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS), which are commonly available in most hospitals (Supplementary Video).

The four-chamber view was reconstructed to calculate the right-to-left ventricular (RV/LV) four-chamber diameter ratio [14]. The maximal diameters of the RV and LV were measured from the endocardial border to the VS using a line perpendicular to the long axis of the heart [11]. The RV/LV diameter ratio was also calculated (Supplementary Figure S3).

In the transverse section of CTPA, if the VS bowing direction was towards the RV, VS deviation to LV was not defined (Supplementary Figure S4A); however, if the VS was straight or bowing towards the LV, VS deviation to LV was defined (Supplementary Figures S4B and S4C) [10].

Risk stratification calculation

The 2019 ESC risk stratification, Bova score, and simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (sPESI) were applied based on patients’ characteristics at the time of acute PE diagnosis [9, 15]. For the 2019 ESC risk stratification, the patients were classified into low-, intermediate-low-, and intermediate-high-risk groups. For the Bova score, the patients were classified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. For sPESI, patients were classified as low- or high-risk groups (Supplementary Tables S2, S3, and S4). The RV function was evaluated by echocardiography. Tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRIV) and systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) were measured (Supplementary Method S2).

Study outcomes

Deterioration was defined as any PE-related clinical adverse event occurring within 7 days and 30 days after admission, including death, cardiac arrest, hemodynamic instability (Supplementary Table S1), unplanned endotracheal intubation, unexpected requirement for mechanical ventilation endotracheal intubation, or mechanical ventilation, as documented in the medical records or follow-up. Investigators evaluating outcomes were blinded to the clinical and laboratory data collection and CTPA parameter measurements [16].

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables are expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and percentages (%), respectively. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A random-effects model with inverse variance weighting and the DerSimonian and Laird approach was applied, with a sensitivity analysis performed using fixed effects (random effects) [17]. Heterogeneity among the three centers was assessed using forest plots and I² statistic [18]. Inter-observer variability was evaluated by randomly selecting 20% of the patients’ CTPA images, which were independently measured by two investigators. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Cohen’s κ coefficient were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model, with interpretation thresholds as follows: <0.5 (poor), 0.5–0.75 (moderate), 0.75–0.9 (good); and >0.9 (excellent) [19].

Curve-fitting analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between VS bowing to RV and deterioration, with VS curvature ratios partitioned into different intervals based on dose-response effects [20]. A log-likelihood ratio test comparing the one-line model to the segmented regression model was used to determine the presence of thresholds [21]. Based on the curve-fitting relationships, VS curvature ratios were stratified, and other important covariates related to PE-related prognosis, such as age [9], sex [22], heart rate (HR) [23], systolic blood pressure (SBP) [24], and RV/LV diameter ratio [25], were also categorized according to their relationship with deterioration. Trend analysis across different VS curvature ratio strata was performed using the Cochran-Armitage method. Hierarchical logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between VS curvature strata and deterioration, with odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to determine the independent association with deterioration, adjusting for age, sex, hospital site, HR, SBP, and the RV/LV diameter ratio. Besides, we conducted a spearman’s correlation analysis between echocardiography-related parameters and VS curvature ratio.

To evaluate whether the VS curvature ratio improved the discrimination power for deterioration, its performance was compared with traditional risk stratification methods (2019 ESC risk stratification, Bova score, and sPESI) using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. Differences in AUCs were compared using the DeLong test [26]. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA) to estimate the net benefit across different threshold probabilities, and calibration was evaluated using a calibration curve to assess the consistency between actual and predicted deterioration events. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.0; R Foundation, https://www.r-project.org) with statistical significance as p < 0.05.​

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 3,310 patients with acute PE were enrolled in the cohort, including 1,640 males and 1,670 females, with a median age of 65 years (IQR: 55–73 years). The overall 30-day deterioration rate in the cohort was 8.2% (272 of 3,310 patients), including 76 patients who died, 48 who required resuscitation following cardiac arrest, and 172 who experienced hemodynamic instability. Additionally, 63 patients required endotracheal intubation or mechanical ventilation. One patient experienced multiple deterioration events.

VS curvature ratio strata

Across the three centers, no significant heterogeneity was observed in patients with VS straight & bowing to the LV (I² = 0%) (Supplementary Figure S5). This group was considered an independent category because of a clear change in the direction of VS curvature. In the patients with VS bowing to the RV representing the majority of patients, VS curvature ratios exhibited a positively skewed distribution (Fig. 2A). A curved relationship between the VS curvature ratio and deterioration was identified, with threshold effects of 1.60 and 1.90 (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table S5). When the VS curvature ratio was < 1.60, the risk of deterioration remained low. However, between 1.60 and 1.90, the VS curvature ratio showed a positive correlation with an increasing deterioration risk. For ratios > 1.90, the risk remained consistently high. Based on these observations, the VS curvature ratios were categorized into three strata: <1.60, 1.60-<1.90, and ≥ 1.90 together with VS straight & bowing to the LV. The variation of VS curvature ratio with cardiac cycle was evaluated in a group of PE patients diagnosed by ECG-gated CTA. Paired t-test showed that there was a slight difference in the curvature radius of the VS and LV free wall during systole and diastole, but no statistically significant difference in the VS curvature ratio (Supplementary Method S3, Supplementary Figures S6, Supplementary Figures S7A, S7B, and S7C).

Patient characteristics among different VS curvature ratio strata

To facilitate the evaluation of VS curvature ratio trends in relation to deterioration, HR, SBP, and RV/LV diameter ratio were stratified based on their association with deterioration. The HR was categorized as < 100 beats/min, 100-<110 beats/min, and ≥ 110 beats/min. SBP was categorized as ≥ 120, >100-<120, and 90–100 mmHg. The RV/LV diameter ratio was categorized as ≤ 1.0, > 1.0–1.2, and > 1.2. As the VS curvature ratio increases, the rate of deterioration also increases. Patients in the higher VS curvature ratio strata exhibited more clinical signs of severe acute PE, including tachycardia, lower SBP, and increased RV/LV diameter ratio. They also had higher classification rates into intermediate- and high-risk groups according to the 2019 ESC risk stratification, Bova score, and sPESI (all p for trend < 0.001) (Table 1). Further details on the interobserver agreement for measuring the CTPA parameters are provided in Table 2.

Association between VS curvature ratio strata and deterioration

In the patients with VS straight & bowing to the LV, the risk of acute PE-related deterioration was significantly higher than in those with VS bowing to the RV (OR: 67.03, [95% CI: 47.24–95.13], p < 0.001). This association remained significant after adjusting for age, sex, hospital site, HR, SBP, and RV/LV diameter ratio (adjusted OR: 19.57, [95% CI: 12.10–31.63], p < 0.001).

The deterioration risk was low in patients with VS curvature ratios of < 1.60 (2.5%). When considering VS curvature ratios < 1.60 in patients with VS bowing to the RV as the reference, no significantly increased risk for deterioration was observed in patients with VS curvature ratios between 1.60 and < 1.90 (adjusted OR: 1.80, [95% CI: 0.79–4.11], p = 0.16). However, patients with VS curvature ratios ≥ 1.90 and those with VS straight & bowing to the LV had a higher risk of deterioration (adjusted OR: 22.94, [95% CI: 13.17–39.97], p < 0.001). The adjusted OR for patients with VS straight & bowing to the LV was 22.95, ([95% CI: 11.88–44.32], p < 0.001), indicating a similarly increased risk of deterioration (Table 3).

The predictive ability of VS curvature ratio was also evaluated at 7-day follow-up, with 93% of patients experiencing deterioration during this time (252 patients). Comparison of discrimination power between VS curvature ratio and VS deviation to LV

In both local and nonlocal centers, the VS curvature ratio exhibited higher AUC values than VS deviation to LV (0.83 vs. 0.71 and 0.84 vs. 0.72, respectively, both p < 0.01) (Table 4). The VS curvature ratio was found to reflect ventricular interdependence and acute PE-related deterioration more accurately than the VS deviation to LV, as measured using CTPA (Supplementary Figures S4A, S4B, and S4C).

Identification of VS curvature ratio for deterioration

Demographic data, vital signs, laboratory results, CTPA parameters, and risk stratification were summarized and compared between the three local and two nonlocal centers (Supplementary Table S9). In the three local centers, the VS curvature ratio (≥ 1.90 and VS straight & bowing to the LV) demonstrated a high AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.80–0.85), and in the two nonlocal centers, the AUC was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.74–0.93) (Table 4).

In the three local centers, the VS curvature ratio showed a higher AUC compared with Bova score (0.83 vs. 0.64, p < 0.01), the 2019 ESC risk stratification (0.83 vs. 0.66, p < 0.01), and sPESI (0.83 vs. 0.73, p < 0.01) for predicting deterioration risk (Table 4, Supplementary Figure S8A). In the two non-local centers, the VS curvature ratio also outperformed Bova score (0.84 vs. 0.65, p < 0.01), the 2019 ESC risk stratification (0.84 vs. 0.62, p < 0.01) and sPESI (0.84 vs. 0.61, p < 0.01) (Table 4, Supplementary Figure S8B). DCA indicated that when the threshold probability for deterioration exceeded 5.0%, the VS curvature ratio provided a superior predictive advantage over the 2019 ESC risk stratification, Bova score, and sPESI in both local and nonlocal centers (Supplementary Figures S8C and S8D).

Discussion

The symptoms and signs of deterioration in normotensive acute PE patients are often insidious and non-specific, yet the onset is sudden and potentially fatal [4]. Early detection of high-risk deterioration patients is critical for timely intervention [3, 27], however current tools remined remain insufficiently accurate deterioration identification [3, 5]. This study demonstrated that an increased VS curvature ratio on CTPA is strongly associated with a higher risk of deterioration. Specifically, the risk of deterioration increases significantly when the VS curvature ratio exceeds 1.90 or when the VS appears straight or bows to the LV. Increased VS curvature ratio as an indicator, can quantify the upper limit of cardiac function tolerance for deterioration. Therefore, this radiological marker demonstrates better discrimination power for predicting deterioration. Additionally, it provides a rapid method for identifying deteriorative patients at the time of diagnosis.

To meet needs of timely clinical decision-making, this study focused on deterioration prior to death, not just death, as the endpoint [3]. Consequently, incidence rate of our endpoints was higher. The relationship between VS curvature and prognosis in acute PE has rarely been mentioned and discussed. This study, based on a large cohort, quantifies VS curvature in PE using the curvature ratio, thereby effectively elaborating on the relationship between VS curvature changes and prognosis, but the patients with VS curvature ratio exceeding 1.90 or straight & bowing to the LV are still rare. This study quantified the VS curvature ratio and demonstrated its strong predictive value for clinical deterioration within 30 days and even 7 days. Furthermore, the VS curvature ratio showed moderate correlations with established PE-related echocardiographic parameters, such as sPAP and TRIV, which to some extent reflect its value in deterioration prediction. The echocardiography holds significant value in the dynamic assessment of VS deviation, however our study aimed to assess deterioration risk on admission. Using CTPA did not added extra burden to patients and could acquire VS morphology rapidly without need for the ultrasound specialist. In our study, most of the patients were divided into low-risk group by Bova score, yet some patients also showed an increased VS curvature ratio. This reason can be explained as that Bova score includes HR, SBP, c-Tn I and RV dysfunction [28], patients with none or one of the four items would be considered as low risk. Therefore, low-risk group in Bova score was somehow a relative wide range and not so good to discriminate low-risk patients.

In pathophysiology, these findings indicate a relatively higher overall deterioration risk when VS straight or bowing to the LV. This configuration suggests that pulmonary circulation pressure nearly matches or exceeds systemic pressure, significantly reducing the LV volume and cardiac output [29]. The severe increase in pulmonary vascular resistance delays RV contraction while the LV is already in diastole, resulting in desynchronization between the RV and LV systole, which can cause VS straightness or leftward shift. This exacerbates acute PE by impairing oxygen exchange and reducing blood return to the LV [30]. Due to pericardial restriction, a marked increase in RV afterload leads to RV overload, transmitting pressure to the LV via the VS, thereby shifting the VS towards the left and altering LV geometry [8]. Thus, a VS curvature ratio exceeding 1.90 in patients with VS bowing to the RV indicates the inability of RV to accommodate the overload. Additionally, reduced right coronary perfusion and myocardial ischemia worsened RV performance [31]. Consequently, normotensive patients with acute PE may rapidly progress to deterioration. Sometimes the patients with acute PE combined with chronic cardiopulmonary disease or chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension are indeed difficult to identify, which may interfere with the association between VS curvature ratio and deterioration, although the incidence is low and there is usually not necessary to separate this group of patients in clinical. Besides, we examined whether an increased VS curvature ratio was an independent risk factor for deterioration. As the RV/LV diameter ratio is currently a prognostic indicator reflecting ventricular interdependence [32]. HR and SBP were also important predictors for acute PE-related poor prognosis [23, 24]. We adjusted these confounding to assess the independence of the VS curvature ratio in predicting deterioration. Our results indicate that the increased risk associated with a higher VS curvature ratio persisted.

This study has several limitations. This measurement method for VS curvature requires a simple training. Our study did not separately explore the discrimination power of VS curvature in whether acute PE is associated with cancer and chronic cardiopulmonary disease or not, although VS curvature comprehensively reflects the cardiac function of these patients. We included some patients with acute PE who underwent ECG-gated CTA, but the potential influence of the cardiac cycle on VS curvature measurement cannot be completely excluded. Although the predictive ability of the VS curvature ratio for deterioration was validated in this multicenter study, the higher VS curvature ratio subgroups had a small patient. Our validation set was still small, further validation in larger cohorts is warranted.

Conclusion

An increased VS curvature ratio on CTPA is associated with a higher risk of deterioration in hospitalized patients with normotensive acute PE. The measurement of the VS curvature ratio can aid in assessing the risk of deterioration at the time of acute PE diagnosis, providing a valuable tool for identifying high-risk deterioration individuals and guiding clinical decision-making.
