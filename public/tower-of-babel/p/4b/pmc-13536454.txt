Efficacy of cryotherapy for oral leukoplakia: A systematic review
Authors: Chuan-Hang Yu, Tzu-Chen Chan, Chun-Pin Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral leukoplakia (OL) is the most common oral potentially malignant disorder and carries a risk of malignant transformation. Cryotherapy is a minimally invasive treatment option for OL, but its efficacy and optimal dose–response remain inconsistent among various studies. This systematic review evaluated the clinical efficacy and safety of cryotherapy for OL lesions and explored whether the characteristics of the lesions and treatment parameters influenced clinical outcomes. Electronic searches were performed in Embase, PubMed, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). Seven studies reporting original clinical outcomes of cryotherapy for OL lesions were included. Data on cryotherapy dose, number and frequency of sessions, complete regression rates, and recurrence rates were extracted. Seven studies comprised 357 patients and approximately 360 OL lesions. Complete regression rates ranged from 77.8% to 100%. Cryogun cryotherapy needed a mean of 3.1 ± 1.3 treatment sessions to achieve complete regression of the OL lesions, compared to 6.3 sessions by cotton-swab cryotherapy. OL lesions smaller than 2 cm2, located outside the tongue, with epithelial dysplasia, or with a surface keratin layer <55 μm required significantly fewer cryotherapy sessions to achieve a complete regression. Reported recurrence rates were 8–34%, and continued tobacco use was the most consistent risk factor for recurrence. Malignant transformation was observed in 6.6% of OL patients in a long-term cohort. We conclude that cryotherapy is a simple, safe, and effective treatment for OL. Cryogun cryotherapy is more efficient than cotton-swab cryotherapy.

Introduction

Oral leukoplakia (OL) is the most common oral potentially malignant disorder (OPMD) and is associated with a recognized risk of malignant transformation to oral squamous cell carcinoma.1,2 In regions, such as Taiwan, where betel quid chewing is prevalent, the incidence of OL and oral cancer is substantially higher than those in nearly all other regions, and oral cancer has been ranked among the leading cancers nationwide for decades.3 National health expenditure on oral cancer in Taiwan exceeded 10 billion New Taiwan Dollars in 2022, placing a heavy burden on the health care system.4 Early and precise intervention for precursor lesions is therefore a key strategy for reducing malignant transformation to oral cancer and the cost for treatment of oral cancer.

Current treatment options for OL include surgical excision, carbon dioxide laser ablation, photodynamic therapy, and cryotherapy. Cryotherapy delivers extreme cold to induce irreversible cellular necrosis and remove the OL lesion, with the advantages of minimal invasiveness, low cost, and few complications. However, the reported results, particularly the complete response rate, local recurrence, and the influence of lesion size, site, and cryotherapy dose on prognosis, vary considerably across different studies, and quantitative consensus is lacking.

The objective of this systematic review was to synthesize the available clinical evidence to rigorously evaluate the efficacy and safety of cryotherapy for OL lesions. In addition to pooling clinical response and recurrence data, we also explored how the characteristics of the lesions and treatment parameters influence treatment outcomes of OL lesions, to provide an evidence-based foundation for clinical decision making and subsequent translational research.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement.5 The review question was developed using the PICO framework: P (Population), patients with OL; I (Intervention), cryotherapy; C (Comparison), conventional treatments or no treatment; O (Outcome), efficacy (complete regression), recurrence, and treatment parameters (dose, number, and frequency of treatment sessions).

Search strategy

Electronic searches were carried out in Embase, PubMed/MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials up to Jan 13, 2024. In each database, controlled vocabulary (Emtree or Medical Subject Headings) and free text synonyms for “oral leukoplakia” and “cryotherapy” were combined using the Boolean operator OR within each concept and between the two concepts. The initial searches retrieved 61, 8, and 407 records from Embase, PubMed, and Cochrane, respectively.

Eligibility criteria

Studies reporting original clinical outcomes of cryotherapy for OL lesions in humans were included. Exclusion criteria were: 1) topic unrelated to cryotherapy for OL; 2) evidence of lower level, such as individual case reports; and 3) reports not published in English and for which no full English text was available.

Study selection and data extraction

Records were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA), and duplicates were removed in three successive passes (by author-title-year, then title-year, then secondary-title-column-page). Two reviewers independently screened the relevance of the titles and abstracts and then assessed full texts against the eligibility criteria; disagreements were resolved by discussion. Data extracted from each included study comprised first author and year; study design; number of patients and lesions; cryotherapy dose (temperature and freeze-thaw duration); number and frequency of treatment sessions; complete regression rate; recurrence rate; and, where available, lesion characteristics (site, size, dysplasia, thickness of the keratin layer). Results

Study selection

The searches yielded 460 records in the three databases. After the removal of 28 duplicates, 432 records were screened for title and abstract, and 392 were excluded as off-topic. Of the 40 full-text reports requested for retrieval, 11 could not be retrieved; 22 of the remaining 29 were excluded in full-text review, leaving seven studies for inclusion in the final synthesis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The seven included studies were published between 1972 and 2015 and comprised six clinical studies6–11 and one retrospective case series.12 Together, they reported on 357 patients and approximately 360 OL lesions. Sako et al.6 treated 60 patients with single or multiple OL lesions by cryotherapy. Bekke and Baart12 reported a single-center experience of six years with cryosurgery for treatment of 90 patients undergoing 121 procedures for a range of benign and malignant oral lesions, of them 24 had OL lesions. Yu et al.7 treated 60 OL lesions in 47 patients with cotton-swab cryotherapy. Kawczyk-Krupka et al.8 compared the clinical outcomes of photodynamic therapy for treatment of 48 OL lesions with those of cryotherapy for treatment of 37 OL lesions. Lin et al.9 treated 60 OL lesions in 54 patients with cryogun cryotherapy. Chen et al.10 used cryogun cryotherapy for treatment of 72 patients with 72 OL lesions and adjacent smoking-induced melanosis. Starzyńska et al.11 reviewed 204 OL patients diagnosed between 1999 and 2009 and treated with topical 0.05% tretinoin (n = 74), cryosurgery (n = 63), or conventional surgery (n = 41). Cryotherapy techniques and dose

Three delivery methods were represented in all included studies: closed-probe (or contact) cryosurgery,6,12 cottonswab cryotherapy,7 and cryogun cryotherapy.9,10 The temperatures of the nadir tissue reported ranged from −160 °C to −80 °C, and the freeze durations ranged from 7 s to more than 120 s per cycle. Sako et al.6 used −120 °C with the freeze durations −30 s; Bekke and Baart12 used −160 °C or −80 °C with the freeze durations of 30–120 s; and Kawczyk- Krupka et al.8 used −89 °C with prolonged freeze durations of 120–160 s. The cryogun-based regimens used by Lin et al.9 and Chen et al.10 employed shorter freeze-thaw cycles of 7–10 s and 4 to 5 repeated sprays per session. Starzynska et al.11 used 40-sec cycles and repeated twice. The treatment frequencies were once every 2 weeks for cotton-swab and cryogun protocols,7,9,10 once every 2–3 weeks in the study of Starzynska et al.,11 and once every 3 weeks in the studies of Bekke and Baart12 and Kawczyk- Krupka et al.8

Number of treatment sessions

The mean number of treatment sessions till complete regression of OL lesion varied substantially with technique. Cryogun cryotherapy required a mean of 3.1 ± 1.3 sessions9 and 3.3 ± 1.3 sessions10 to achieve complete regression of OL lesions, which were significantly fewer than the mean of 6.3 sessions reported for cotton-swab cryotherapy.7 Closed-probe cryosurgery required 1 to 5 sessions per OL lesion.6,12 Mean treatment sessions were not reported by Kawczyk- Krupka et al.8 and Starzynska et al.11

Complete regression

Complete regression rates ranged from 77.8% to 100%. Five studies reported a complete regression rate of 100%: 60 of 60 OL lesions in the study of Sako et al.,6 all leukoplakia lesions in the study of Bekke and Baart,12 60 of 60 OL lesions in the study of Yu et al.,7 60 of 60 OL lesions in the study of Lin et al.,9 and 72 of 72 OL lesions in the study of Chen et al.10 The larger comparative cohorts reported a complete response rate of 89.2% in the cryotherapy arm of Kawczyk-Krupka et al.8 and 77.8% in the cryosurgery subgroup of Starzynska et al.11

Recurrence

Recurrence rates were reported in all seven studies and ranged from 8% to 34%. The lowest recurrence rates were reported by Lin et al. (8%),9 Yu et al. (8.3%),7 and Chen et al. (11%).10 Intermediate recurrence rates were reported by Starzynska et al. (22.2%),11 Kawczyk-Krupka et al. (24.3%),8 and Bekke and Baart (25%).12 The highest recurrence rate (34%) was reported by Sako et al.6 Continued tobacco use was the most consistent risk factor associated with recurrence; in the study of Chen et al., all recurrences occurred in patients who continued smoking after obtaining the complete response of the OL lesions.10

Influence of lesion characteristics on treatment response

Multivariate analyses by Lin et al.9 and Yu et al.7 identified four characteristics of the OL lesions significantly associated with the number of treatment sessions required: lesion size, anatomical site, presence of epithelial dysplasia, and thickness of the surface keratin layer of the OL lesions. OL lesions located outside the tongue, those <2 cm2, those with epithelial dysplasia, and those with a surface keratin layer <55 μm required significantly fewer sessions to achieve complete regression of the OL lesions. In multivariate analysis by Yu et al.,7 only the anatomical site and size of the OL lesion remained as the independent predictors. Starzynska et al.11 similarly concluded that the location of the OL lesion and the stage of progression influenced the treatment outcome of the OL lesions.

Malignant transformation

Malignant transformation after cryotherapy was reported in only one cohort. Sako et al.6 observed squamous cell carcinoma occurring at or adjacent to the treated sites of the OL lesions in 4 of 60 (6.6%) patients during 2.5–4.5 years of follow-up. Malignant transformation events were not explicitly reported in the other six included studies, although the follow-up durations were generally shorter than 12 months.

Discussion

This systematic review demonstrates that cryotherapy is a consistently effective treatment for the OL lesions, with complete regression rates ranging from 77.8% to 100% in various settings, techniques, and follow-up periods.6–12 The minimally invasive nature of the procedure, the low cost of the equipment, and the high patient acceptability6 support the inclusion of cryotherapy as a viable first-line option for the OL lesions, especially when surgical excision is impractical or contraindicated.

The most clinically significant difference between studies was the choice of cryotherapy delivery method. Cryogun cryotherapy9,10 required less than half of the number of sessions needed for cotton-swab cryotherapy (mean approximately 3.1 vs. 6.3 treatment sessions),7,9 probably because the spray device delivers a relatively higher amount of liquid nitrogen to maintain a lower and more stable tissue temperature and produces deeper intracellular ice formation in a shorter period of time. Although the nadir temperatures varied widely (−160 °C to −80 °C),6,8,12 the most efficient regimens used the short (7–10 s) freeze–thaw cycles that repeated four to five times per treatment,9,10 suggesting that the delivery method and thermal stability, rather than a single freeze duration or peak temperature, are the dominant determinants of treatment efficacy.

The biology and anatomy modulated treatment in a reproducible manner. Multivariate analyses showed that the OL lesions with the larger size (>2 cm2), tongue location, and thicker surface keratin layer (>55 μm) required more treatment sessions to achieve complete regression.7,9 The thick keratin layer and high vascular perfusion of the tongue likely limit the depth of freezing, while the OL lesions with epithelial dysplasia characterized by the looser intercellular adhesion and higher proliferative activity or thinner keratin layer are more susceptible to cold-induced injury and respond more effectively. Therefore, pre-treatment evaluation of the size, site, and histology of the OL lesion can help to set realistic expectations of the number of treatment sessions required and guide the choice of using either cryogun or cotton-swab cryotherapy.

The long-term recurrence remained an important limitation. The lowest recurrence rates (8%–11%) were observed in the cohorts treated with cryogun cryotherapy 9,10 or cotton-swab cryotherapy by the frequency of once per 2 weeks,7 while the highest recurrence rate (34%) was observed in the first series of the OLlesions.6 Continued tobacco use emerged as the most consistent risk factor. In the study of Chen et al.,10 all recurrent cases occurred in patients who continued smoking. Because cryotherapy ablates the visible OL lesion but does not eliminate the underlying etiologic risk factor, it is necessary to let the patients stop the risk habits to avoid the recurrence of the OL lesions. Therefore, tobacco and betel quid cessation counseling should be regarded as an integral part of OL management.

Although cryotherapy effectively removes visible OL lesions, it does not remove their malignant potential. Sako et al.6 observed a 6.6% rate of malignant transformation into squamous cell carcinoma during a follow-up period of 2.5–4.5 years. Although the malignant transformation rate fell within the natural history range of OL, this rate is still clinically significant. Most of the other included studies had the follow-up durations of less than 12 months,7–11 which limits inferences about the long-term cancer risk and may underestimate the malignant transformation rate. Sustained surveillance with structured re-examination intervals and biopsy of suspicious lesional changes should therefore form an integral part of any cryotherapy protocol.

Strengths of this review include a pre-specified PICO question, a structured multi-database search, and reporting in accordance with PRISMA 2020.5 Limitations include the small number of eligible studies, methodological heterogeneity in cryotherapy dose, technique, and follow-up period, the absence of a formal risk-of-bias assessment, limited long-term outcome data, and the exclusion of non- English studies for which no full English text was available. Only one included study compared cryotherapy directly with another modality (photodynamic therapy),8 so inferences about relative efficacy versus alternative treatments are limited.

Cryotherapy appears to be particularly suitable for patients with superficial, multifocal, or extensive OL lesions which are poor candidates for surgery or laser ablation. Treatment should be individualized according to the size, thickness, site, and histology of the OL lesions. Cryogun cryotherapy should be preferred to cotton-swab cryotherapy where equipment is available, given its substantially lower number of treatment sessions to achieve complete regression of the OL lesions.7,9,10 Long-term clinical follow-up and behavioral risk habits control are essential complements to management of the OL lesions.

In conclusion, Cryotherapy is an effective, safe, and relatively simple treatment option for the OL lesions and achieves high complete regression rates in various clinical settings. Cryogun cryotherapy is more efficient than cottonswab cryotherapy, and the size, site, epithelial dysplasia, and thickness of the surface keratin layer are important predictors of the number of treatment sessions required to achieve complete regression of the OL lesions. Despite favorable short-term efficacy, recurrence rates of 8%–34%, and a lower but non-negligible risk of malignant transformation rate highlights the need for sustained follow-up and control of etiologic risk factors. Larger, standardized prospective studies with longer follow-up period are required to define the optimal role of cryotherapy in the treatment of OL lesions.
