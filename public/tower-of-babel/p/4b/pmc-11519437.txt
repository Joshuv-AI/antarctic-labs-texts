Global pattern of persistent human papillomavirus infection in female genital tract: An update system review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ming Zhao, Peipei Kang, Liling Zhu, Dan Zhou, Meimei Cui, Min Zhang, Jingjing Jia, Limei Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe analyzed the type-specific persistent infection of high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) across different continents, aiming to offer valuable insights into the implementation and development of HPV vaccines. There were 129 studies included 656,555 participants. The weighted mean time for any persistent HPV infection was 13.29 months. The single and multiple HPV infection were persistent for median of 12.27 and 18.99 months, respectively. HPV35 is the common genotype of persistent infection in Asia and Africa, as well as HPV53 in Europe. Individuals (>50 years old) were to be more prone to persistent infection. Our study revealed approximately 50% of persistent HPV infection resolved after two years. A 24-month interval for HPV testing is proposed as a way to identify persistent HPV infection. In addition, to develop region-specific guidelines for HPV vaccines can be utilized as references for the diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer.Subject areas: Virology

Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks as the fourth most malignancy among women globally.1 Persistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection is strongly associated with cervical cancer and invasive cervical cancer.2,3 It has been demonstrated that there are more than 200 genotypes of HPV, 54 of which can infect the genital tract mucosa.4,5 Generally, HPV infection is transient in nature, with more than half of them resolving without treatment in one year and 90% in two years,6,7 while some study revealed the clearance of HPV infection was 50% in two years,8,9 there was no consensus on the duration of persistent HPV infection.

The definition of persistent HPV infection typically bases on the duration of the infection specific to each HPV genotype.10 Given that aspects of the definition of persistence, such as the frequency and length of testing intervals, may affect the estimated risk of cervical precancer associated with HPV persistence.11 Some studies supported defining persistent HPV infection as positive HPV result for at least two time points,12 while other studies suggest requiring HPV-positive detection at least three time points.13 Additionally, most studies assert that two HPV tests conducted with a six-month interval with both yielding positive results constitute persistence.14 Trottier et al. concluded that the relative risk of HR-HPV infection with squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs)/cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) lasting 6 or 12 months or longer was very high,14 and Rositch et al. argue that repeat HPV testing at 12-month intervals could identify women at increased risk of high-grade cervical precancer due to persistent HPV infections.10 Persistence and duration of HPV should be better understood to operationalize inclusion of repeat testing into future cervical cancer screening programs and for use as an endpoint in HPV vaccines trials and efficacy modeling.

To date, though some studies have investigated the risk factors associated with persistent HPV infection, there have been relatively limited investigations into the duration of the persistent infection.15,16 Some study found that specific genotype of HPV as well as single/multiple infection were strongly associated with persistent HR-HPV infection.17 A former meta-analysis in 2013 reported the prevalence and duration of 14 different genotypes of HR-HPV persistent infection.10 However, there was still no agreement on whether single or multiple infections are more likely to result in persistent infection, along with uncertainties surrounding the duration of these infection.18,19 Therefore, an up-to-date overview and meta-analysis of the global epidemiology of persistent HPV infection among women worldwide is necessary.

In this study, we investigated the prevalence of persistent infection at different ages, statue, and genera, as well as the prevalence of persistent HPV infection by follow-up time. Furthermore, we analyzed the prevalence patterns of type-specific persistent infection of HR-HPV across different continents, aiming to offer valuable insights into the implementation and development of HPV vaccines in different regions.

Results

Characteristics of qualified studies

A total of 51,992 relevant studies were retrieved according to the search strategy, 18,519 duplicates were excluded, and 680 studies were read in full text after examining their titles and abstracts. After re-screening according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, 129 studies were selected (Figure 1). Most studies were conducted in Europe (36%), North America (23%), and Asia (23%), followed by South America (9%) and Africa (8%). The publications spanned from 1994 to 2023. The prevalence of HPV infection

The pooled infection prevalence of HPV was 19.22% (95% confidence interval CI: 16.32%–22.49%), while the pooled persistent HPV infection was 28.38% (95% CI: 24.01%–32.97%), and that of HR-HPV was 35.41% (95% CI: 31.03%–39.78%). The prevalence of persistent infection in SILs was 43.48% (95% CI: 33.28%–56.81%). The persistent infection prevalence of incident infection was 39.32% (95% CI: 23.98–55.80) and the persistent HPV infection prevalence of prevalent infection was 31.27% (95% CI: 28.14–34.40).

In our study, the prevalence of persistent infection remained essentially unchanged over two years (from 38.93% to 31.78%). Discussion

Currently, there is no consensus on the definition of persistent HPV infection. In this study, persistent HPV infection was defined as the infection of the same/different genotypes for follow-up time ≥6 months. Consistent with previous studies, the persistent HR-HPV infection prevalence was 35.41%.20,21,22 Pirtea et al. reported persistent HPV infection prevalence of 40.9%, 20%, and 11.8% at 6, 12, and 18 months, respectively,23 Kim et al. reported persistent HPV infection prevalence of 14.3%, 2.2%, and 1.1% at 6, 12, and 18 months, respectively.24 Our findings indicate a gradual decline in the prevalence of persistent infection as the follow-up duration extends, with significant regional differences.10,23,25,26,27 Furthermore, our study found that approximately 50% of persistent HPV infection resolved after two years, a finding that contrasts with previous studies that over 50% of HPV infection resolve on their own within one year and up to 90% within two years.6,7 Previous studies have suggested that the high persistence of HPV infection after two years could be attributed to subsequent acquisition of occasional infections,9 However, our study leans more toward the hypothesis that the initial prevalent infection develops into persistent infection. Our result will have a significant impact on the diagnosis and treatment of persistent HPV infection.

Our study showed that HPV16 was the most prevalent HPV genotype, followed by HPV52, 31, 35, and 53. It is noteworthy that HPV16 is widely acknowledged as the most common genotype in cervical cancer,28,29,30,31 with the highest rate of persistent infection.15,32 In 2010, a comprehensive meta-analysis involving 1 million women across five continents revealed that HPV52 and 31 ranked as the third and fourth most prevalent genotypes, which was consistent with our study.33 Numerous studies have consistently highlighted the prevalence of HPV52 in Asia,34,35,36 with evidence suggesting that this genotype is more likely to cause persistent infection.37 Moreover, a meta-analysis focusing on the distribution of cervical cancer in East Asia also demonstrated that HPV52 and HPV58 exhibited 2.5–2.8 times and 3.7–4.9 times higher representation in CIN and invasive cervical cancer, respectively, compared to other regions.38 In Europe, HPV31 is the second most common genotype in CIN2 and CIN3,39 coupled with the second-highest persistent infection prevalence.40 Our findings revealed that HPV35 was the fifth most common genotype susceptible to persistent HPV infection. Clifford et al. reported that HPV35 is the fourth most prevalent genotype with invasive cervical cancer in Africa and is more prevalent in the general population in sub-Saharan Africa,41,42,43,44 particularly concerning CIN3.45 Therefore, the inclusion of HPV35 in HPV vaccines has been recommended, raising the prospect of effective prevention against CIN and cervical cancer.45 As for HPV53, it contributed to the fourth highest prevalence of persistent infection, with a much higher prevalence (6.4%–9.1%)46,47 in Europe than in Asia (5.11%).48 Wang et al. further confirmed that the most common non-nine-valent vaccine-related genotypes in SILs were HPV53, 68, and 39.49 HPV53 was associated with CIN2/3 and invasive squamous cervical cancer,50,51,52 and HPV53 was found to be the more common genotype among the 14 non-nine-valent vaccine-related genotypes,53 therefore, we suggest that HPV53 should be included in new vaccine development programs, particularly for Europe.54 We found that HPV26 had a higher prevalence of persistent infection in North America, while HPV82 is the third most common genotype in Africa, with an incidence of 15%.55 HPV26 and 82 are significantly more common in CC than in women with normal cervical cytology, and may be considered upgraded carcinogenic genotypes.56 However, given their rarity in cervical cancer, they may not be included in HPV vaccine, but attention should be paid to these areas of incidence and strengthened management.

We found that the median duration of HPV infection in women with normal cytology was 13.29 months. The median duration of HR-HPV infection was about half a year longer than that of LR-HPV infection, which is consistent with previous studies.57,58 The longer duration of HR-HPV infection may be due to a lack of or weaker immune response to these genotypes, or a higher intra-type diversity that makes it easier for these genotypes to evade immune surveillance.59 Previous studies have shown that HR-HPV infection lasting more than 12 or 18 months can serve as an indicator of existing or potential SILs.60 It has been demonstrated that HPV16 and related genotypes, such as HPV31, often have longer durations than other genotypes, which is consistent with our study.59,61,62 Furthermore, HPV70 demonstrated a longer persistence (11.62 months) compared to HPV6 and 11. However, HPV70 was mainly present in genital warty lesions and displayed a relatively insignificant oncogenic effect, warranting intensive follow-up.63 By contrast, HPV82 was considered a rare genotype, accounting for a small proportion of genital infection and SILs.64,65 Our study revealed that multiple infections tend to persist for a longer duration than single infection. One possible explanation is that the synergistic effect between multiple HPV infections contributes to prolonged HPV infection.66,67 Immune status changes caused by multiple infections may also affect the duration of HPV infection.68 The increased viral load caused by multiple HPV infections was another possible reason for prolonged HPV infection.69 Persistent HPV infection lasting more than a year is more likely to develop into CIN or cervical cancer.11 Therefore, we should strengthen the follow-up and treatment of multiple HPV infections.

HPV16 (α-9) and 18 (α-7) are the most common HR-HPV genotypes, causing 70% of invasive cervical cancer worldwide, while the genotypes of LR-HPV, such as HPV6 and 11 (α-10), are associated with proliferative lesions.43 Our study found that α-9 had the longest duration (12.9 months) similar to previous studies,18 followed by α-6 and α-7. All genotypes in α-9 were high-risk and associated with malignant lesions of the mucosa. The onset and progression of malignant lesions such as oral cancer, laryngeal cancer, esophageal cancer, cervical cancer, and vulvar cancer are inextricably linked to the infection of these HPV genotypes. The genotypes of within the α-6 group were also high-risk, which can cause benign or malignant lesions of the skin and mucosa. However, relatively little attention has been given to this genus.70

In conclusion, strengthening follow-up and timely treatment for women with multiple HPV infections, particularly those over 50 years old is a proactive approach for cervical cancer prevention and control. The development of region-specific screening guidelines is advised to furnish clinical practitioners with valuable references for diagnosing and treating cervical cancer, and to inform HPV vaccination strategies. Additionally, incorporating HPV35 into vaccines for Africa and Asia, and HPV53 for Europe, is suggested as a targeted approach to prevent cervical cancer. Approximately 50% of persistent HPV infection resolved after two years. For incident-infected populations, HPV testing at 24-month intervals is recommended as an indicator to define persistent infection.

Limitations of the study

This study also has certain limitations. Firstly, although the heterogeneity is often unavoidable when conducting meta-analysis of observational studies, it does not necessarily invalidate the findings. Secondly, due to limited data on the persistent infection prevalence for specific genotypes in South America, we were unable to explore the distribution of genotypes. In addition, the limited number of studies on LR-HPV hindered a comprehensive across-continental analysis. Lastly, the dataset concerning the relationship between persistent infection and SILs was scarce, preventing robust analysis within a large pooled sample. Therefore, our forthcoming study will emphasize the relationship between persistent infection genotypes and the grading of SILs.

Method details

Search strategy

For this study, literatures on the persistent HPV infection in the female genital tract were collected from major databases including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane from inception to July 2023. For publication retrieval in PubMed, both subject headings and free words were used, including "human papillomavirus" and "persistent infection" as the subject headings. The complete search strategy was shown in Data S1.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two co-authors (MZ and PPK) independently reviewed the literature and obtained data. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or involving a third author (LML). The following information was retrieved: (1) basic information about the study, such as author, year of publication, region, follow-up time, and baseline cytology status; (2) baseline characteristics of the participants, including the number of HPV-positive cases, the number of positive cases for different HPV genotypes, the number of HPV-positive cases in different age groups, and the number of positive cases for different levels of SILs; (3) outcome indicators, including the prevalence of persistent HPV infection, including different HPV genotypes and different age groups, as well as the median duration of overall HPV/HR HPV/LR HPV/single/multiple HPV genotypes and genus.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The software package of R 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used to execute a single group rate meta-analysis. The combined effect was represented by the efficiency value, and the p value and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. Before pooling data, Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to stabilize variance and improve their statistical properties.71

For the I2 index, values of 25% or lower corresponded to low degrees of heterogeneity, 26%–50% to moderate degrees of heterogeneity, and greater than 50% to high degrees of heterogeneity. Because of high heterogeneity, a random-effects meta-analysis was used to calculate the pooled prevalence of persistent infection with 95% CIs throughout this study. To explore the sources of heterogeneity, a meta-regression analysis was conducted, considering type specificity, year of publication, sample size, follow-up time, and sample source. Literature bias was analyzed by funnel plot and Egger’s test. SPSS26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) was used to conduct χ2 test to compare the prevalence of persistent HPV infection across different age groups, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To calculate the prevalence of persistent HPV infection, the number of persistent infection was divided by the total number of positive cases.15,16,72. The median duration of HPV infection was calculated by averaging the duration of each HPV group and genotype, while also considering it according to the number of women represented in each group.
