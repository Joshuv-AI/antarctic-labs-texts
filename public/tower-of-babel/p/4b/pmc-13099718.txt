Tribological and antibacterial behavior of Cu doped DLC coatings for biomedical implants
Authors: Ayowolemiwa Victor Towobola, Amal George, Tonye Alaso Jack, Jenny-Lee Thomassin, Qiaoqin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCu-DLC coatings were deposited on CoCrMo alloys and silicon wafers using magnetron sputtering. Increasing the atomic percentage (at%) of Cu incorporated into the DLC coatings did not significantly alter their overall structure. However, X-ray diffraction results revealed structural differences with increasing Cu content, including larger crystallite sizes and a transition from a predominantly amorphous structure to a nanocrystalline phase. Significant changes in chemical bonding states were observed through Raman and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Nanoindentation analysis showed reductions in both hardness and elastic modulus, which were inversely correlated with increasing Cu content. Moreover, the wear rate of Cu-DLC showed a greater decrease at 11.8 at% Cu compared to the other coating. Corrosion analysis further indicated that higher Cu content moderated the corrosion inhibition rate. Time course bacterial killing assays demonstrated that Cu contents between 11 and 15.2 at% in the DLC coatings effectively killed both Staphylococcus aureus and multi-drug resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, with the highest Cu at% resulting in complete bacterial killing at all time points. Overall, incorporation of Cu into DLC coatings enhances corrosion and antibacterial activity with reduced mechanical integrity. An optimal balance between mechanical integrity and antibacterial function can be achieved by carefully controlling the amount of Cu incorporated into the DLC coatings.Keywords: Diamond-like carbon, Copper, CoCrMo, Corrosion, Adhesion, antibacterial coatings

Introduction

Bacterial infections at sites of biomedical implants, including periprosthetic joint infections (PJI), can lead to implant failure, the need for revision surgery, prolonged hospitalization, and high medical costs. These infections can result from direct bacterial colonization on the surface of implants. The tissue surrounding the implants can also serve to sequester bacteria, which makes it more challenging to prevent, identify and treat infections [1]. In addition to tissue specific challenges, treatment is further complicated when infections are caused by multi-drug resistant bacteria including Klebsiella pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus that are becoming more complex and expensive to treat due to their increased resistance to various antibiotics [2–9]. Current strategies to prevent and treat biomedical implant associated infections include adding antibiotics to bone cement or fitting patients with percutaneous catheters to be able to infuse the joint space with antibiotics, however neither strategy will be effective if bacteria are resistant to the antibiotics used [10–12]. Another strategy is to develop antibacterial coatings that repel or kill bacteria. To date one of the most common strategies described has been to incorporate silver into nanocoatings [13–20]. These silver-doped nanocoatings were shown to kill both Escherichia coli and S. aureus [16–19, 21]. Some useful modifications have also been explored, such as using silver-doped diamond-like carbon (DLC) films to improve film adhesion, corrosion and wear resistance [22, 23], and the incorporation of titanium dioxide into the silver-doped nanocoatings also makes these coatings anticorrosive [13, 24]. The need for modifications highlight a challenge faced by researchers when developing antibacterial coatings for implants, particularly for joint implants, which requires the coatings with both high corrosion and wear resistance and antibacterial performance.

Cobalt-chromium-molybdenum (CoCrMo) alloys, characterized by their composition of approximately 58–65% cobalt, 26–30% chromium, and 4–7% molybdenum, have long been the material of choice for high-performance applications in the aerospace, industrial, and biomedical fields [25, 26]. In particular, CoCrMo alloys are used extensively in orthopaedic implants, such as hip and knee prosthetics, due to their remarkable mechanical properties, including high hardness, excellent wear resistance, and corrosion resistance. The ability of CoCrMo alloys to withstand the mechanical and corrosive challenges of the human body makes them ideal for long-term implant applications, where durability and biocompatibility are essential. The microstructure of CoCrMo alloys typically includes an α phase (a Co solid solution), β or β′ phases (martensitic phases), and κ phases enriched with nickel and iron. This specific microstructure enhances the durability and biocompatibility of the alloy, ensuring a long-term reliability for use in implants [27].

Diamond-like carbon thin coatings are known for their excellent low friction coefficient, wear resistance and superior corrosion resistance [22, 23]. These coatings also have intrinsic antibacterial activity, as they limit bacterial adhesion [28, 29]. However, DLC also faces challenges related to adhesion to metal substrates, particularly in CoCrMo alloys, due to the high internal stress within DLC layer and the variation in thermal expansion between DLC and the metal substrate [30]. To overcome these challenges, researchers have investigated doping DLC coatings with metals or non-metals. When comparing metal doped DLC and pure DLC; copper (Cu) doped DLC (Cu-DLC) coatings offer various advantages as Cu enhances adhesion to metal substrates, reduces internal stress, and improves overall performance [31].

In addition to the Cu’s corrosion resistance, Cu is known to have antibacterial properties [32]. These properties make doping DLC with Cu suitable for reducing the risk of bacterial infections around implants [33–36]. Recent research studies have shown that Cu-DLC thin coatings have some antibacterial activity against S. aureus [37]. Additionally, Cu has shown effectiveness against K. pneumoniae [38], which are increasingly resistant to carbapenems and other antibiotics of last resort [39]. Hence the antibacterial and mechanical properties of Cu-DLC make it an attractive option for preventing infections and improving the long term use of orthopaedic implants and other biomedical devices [40].

Compared to metal doped hydroxyapatite and ceramic based antibacterial coatings, which exhibit excellent bioactivity but limited load bearing capability [41–44]. Cu-DLC coating offer superior mechanical robustness and wear resistance while ceramic systems rely primarily on ion release for antibacterial functionality, Cu-DLC coatings enable a combination of contact mediated killing and controlled ion release, making them suitable for orthopaedic implant applications where mechanical durability and antibacterial protection must coexist.

Previous research on Cu-DLC and Ag-DLC reports antibacterial or mechanical behaviours in isolation [23]. The present study established a systematic structure- property relation in Cu-DLC coatings deposited on CoCrMo alloy. By correlating Cu concentration with microstructure, tribological and corrosion behaviour, and antibacterial activity against clinically relevant S. aureus and multi drug resistant K. pneumoniae. This work identifies a multifunctional optimization window capable of simultaneously enhancing tribological performance and antibacterial activity.

While the benefits of Cu-DLC coatings are well-documented, there has been limited research on the specific effects of Cu doping concentrations on the properties of Cu-DLC coatings, particularly when applied on CoCrMo alloys. The concentration of Cu in the DLC layer can influence the coating’s mechanical, tribological, corrosion, and antibacterial properties, as well as its adhesion to the substrate. In this study, we aim to explore the effects of varying Cu concentrations on the performance of non-hydrogenated Cu-DLC coatings on CoCrMo alloy substrates. Specifically, we will investigate the stress, hardness, adhesion, tribological, corrosion, and antibacterial behaviour of these coatings, with a focus on their potential applications in biomedical implants. By examining the impact of Cu concentrations on the structure and properties of Cu-DLC coatings, we aim to find the desirable Cu doping concentration for orthopaedic implants with high hardness, high wear and corrosion resistance, sufficient adhesion to CoCrMo alloy substrate, and antibacterial effectiveness. Furthermore, this study will deepen the understanding of the relationships between Cu doping and surface roughness, offering valuable insights for the development of advanced biomedical coatings.

Materials and methods

Cu-DLC coating deposition on CoCrMo alloy and silicon substrates

Cu-DLC coatings were deposited onto ASTM F1537 CoCrMo alloy sheets and silicon wafers using magnetron sputtering [45]. Prior to deposition, the CoCrMo alloy samples were ground and polished to achieve a mirror-like finish, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in acetone for 30 min. Additionally, the samples were subjected to argon (Ar) cleaning in the deposition chamber for one hour to remove any residual surface contaminants. The deposition process parameters are detailed in Table 1. During the deposition, the substrate temperature was maintained at ~ 24 °C using an integrated cooling system. The deposition was carried out for a duration of 3 h for all samples. Parameters such as the working distance (60 mm), substrate rotation speed (3 rpm), and Ar flow rate (30 sccm) were maintained consistently throughout the deposition of all the coatings.

Microstructural characterization

The crystal structure and phase composition of the coatings was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using CuKα radiation with a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer. A Cu target with a wavelength of 1.54 Å was used at 40 kV and 44 mA. Scans were conducted at 2θ angles ranging from 20° to 80°, with a step size of 0.02º. The chemical composition and bonding state of the coatings was evaluated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with a Kratos AXIS Supra instrument, employing a 500 mm Rowland circle monochromatic Al K-α source (1486.6 eV) and a spot size of 300 × 700 μm. The bonding states of carbon were analysed using a Renishaw InVia Raman spectrometer. High-resolution imaging of both the top and cross-sectional views of the samples was obtained with a Hitachi SU8010 scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector for further elemental analysis.

Mechanical characterization

The hardness of the coatings was measured using nanoindentation with a Berkovich indenter at 3 mN load on a Universal Materials Tester (UMT; Center for Tribology (CETR)). The load was selected to ensure the that the indention depth remained well below 10% of the coating thickness, thereby minimizing substrate influence and ensuring reliable mechanical property evaluation. The adhesion quality of the coatings on CoCrMo alloy was evaluated by Daimler-Benz Rockwell-C adhesion tests using Verein-Deutscher-Ingenieure (VDI) 3198 as the reference standard [46]. The indentations were observed using the SEM (JSM-6010LV, Jeol Corp). The indentation area (crater edge) obtained from SEM was used to identify its states as excellent (HF1) to poor adhesion (HF6), depending on the number of cracks and delamination. For wet wear testing, a UMT in a ball-on-disk configuration according to ASTM F7232-17 standard was used [47]. The counter surface was a flat-ended 4.5 mm diameter pin made of ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE). Tests were conducted in a saline solution (0.9% [w/v] NaCl) to simulate bodily fluids. A constant normal load of 10 N was applied through the UHMWPE pin with a 4 mm displacement length against both coated and uncoated CoCrMo alloy disks at room temperature for 10,000 s. The UHMWPE pins and coated samples were weighed and measured before and after testing to evaluate the wear rate by weight loss. A NewView 8000 model surface profilometer (Zygo) was also used to measure the volume loss of the coated and uncoated CoCrMo alloy disks.

Electrochemical corrosion experiments

The electrochemical corrosion resistance of the coatings were evaluated using potentiodynamic polarization technique in accordance with the G59 ASTM standard using a Gamry potentiostat [48]. The electrochemical setup comprised a Saturated Calomel Electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode, CoCrMo discs (both bare and coated with Cu-DLC coatings) as the working electrodes, and a graphite rod as the counter electrode. These electrodes were immersed in a saline solution (0.9% [w/v] NaCl) at room temperature. Prior to the potentiodynamic polarization tests, the open-circuit potential (OCP) was allowed to stabilize for 2 h. The polarization was performed by sweeping the potential ± 350 mV relative to the OCP at a scan rate of 0.5 mV.s-1. The corrosion current density (icorr) and corrosion potential (Ecorr) were determined from the Tafel curves using Gamry Echem Analyst software, and the experiments were repeated 3 times for each condition.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Here, K. pneumoniae ST258 [49] and S. aureus ATCC 9144 were used. Unless otherwise stated, bacteria were routinely cultured in lysogeny broth (LB; 1% [w/v] tryptone, 0.5% [w/v] yeast extract, 1% [w/v] NaCl) or solid LB (LBA; LB with 1.5% [w/v] agar). Bacteria were routinely cultured at 37 °C with or without aeration (220 rpm).

Antibacterial assays

The antibacterial properties of the Cu-DLC coatings with varying Cu concentrations were evaluated against S. aureus and K. pneumoniae using a modified time-course survival assay [50]. Cu-DLC coated substrates were sterilized with 70% ethanol, dried in a laminar flow hood and placed in a 24 well plate. Bacterial strains were cultured, collected by centrifugation (16000 g), normalized to an optical density at 595 nm of 0.5 in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 4.3 mM Na2HPO4, 1.4 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) and diluted to a concentration of 105 colony forming units (CFU)/ml in PBS. Aliquots of diluted bacteria were deposited onto the sterile Cu-DLC coatings and incubated at 37 °C for 0, 20, 40, and 60 min. At each time point, the bacteria were collected, serially diluted, plated on LBA, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The next day, the bacterial colonies were enumerated and the CFU/ml were calculated. The antibacterial assays were performed in three times independantly, and the results are presented as mean values with corresponding standard deviation.

Raman spectra of Cu-DLC coatings are shown in Fig. 5. As expected, the Raman spectra displayed typical D and G peaks of DLC coatings. In DLC coatings, the G peak is observed at around 1550 cm⁻¹ due to the vibration of sp2 bonded carbon atoms, while the D peak at 1350 cm⁻¹ indicates disorder induced modes in graphite-like sp2 bonding [55]. The positions of these peaks displays small shifts depending on the sp3/sp2 ratio in the coatings. A summary of the G-position, ID/IG ratios, and full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the G peak of the coatings is provided in Table 5. Overall, increasing Cu incorporation into DLC coatings results in a shift of the broad D peak towards lower wavenumbers (Raman shift) and the G peak towards a higher wavenumbers as illustrated Fig. 5; Table 5. Additionally, there is an increase in the ratio of the peak intensity of D to G (ID/IG ratios) when increasing Cu-incorporation into the coatings. Together these results suggest that increased Cu-incorporation into the coatings results in a reduction of the sp3 carbon fraction (diamond like) and increased sp2 (graphitic) carbon in the coatings, which is in consistence with those reported in a previous work [56].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the Cu-DLC coatings are shown in Figs. 6 and 7. As shown in Fig. 6a the surface of the DLC coatings with 8.1 at% Cu was smooth and featureless, which is characteristic of amorphous DLC coatings. This indicates that the carbon matrix remains amorphous and the Cu is embedded within the DLC and that the grain size of the incorporated Cu particles are below the SEM resolution limit of 10 nm. In contrast, the surface of the Cu-DLC with 15.2 at% Cu is relatively rough, revealing a closely packed cluster structure composed of crystallites averaging 10 nm in size (Fig. 7a). This shift in the microstructure of the coatings is in line with previous reports, where increasing Cu incorporation can change the DLC form from a featureless amorphous like to a nanocrystalline clustered surface [29, 30]. This was supported by cross-sectional SEM images that showed that the coatings with 8.1 at% Cu have a dense and uniform structure, whereas those with 15.2 at% Cu are less tightly packed with a nanocrystalline (Figs. 6b and 7b) structure. Increased Cu incorporation has little impact on the average thin layer thickness which is 1.1 ± 0.1 μm as displayed in Figs. 6b and 7b). This might be due to the similar deposition parameters maintained for all the coatings except the sputtering power of Cu target. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images are illustrated in Figs. 6c and 7c. The images show that the doped Cu is uniformly dispersed throughout the DLC matrix. These results indicate that Cu incorporation alters the coating morphology and microstructure, from amorphous DLC to nanocrystalline composite Cu-DLC.

Mechanical analysis of Cu-DLC coatings

Both hardness and elastic modulus are important properties for biomedical implant applications, as they impact the quality and lifespan of implants [57]. The hardness and elastic modulus of Cu-DLC coatings are summarized in Table 6. Cu-DLC coatings with 2.4 at% of Cu exhibits a high hardness value of approximately 21.3 GPa. Increasing the Cu content in the DLC coatings leads to decrease in hardness, with higher Cu concentrations resulted in greater reductions. Specifically, the hardness drops from 21.3 to 8.3 GPa and the elastic modulus decreases from 214.8 to 165.6 GPa when the Cu concentration increases from 2.4 to 15.2 at% (Table 6). The observed increase in elastic modulus at higher Cu content is attributed to the formation of nanocrystalline Cu clusters, which locally reinforce the coating and increase stiffness. This suggests the presence of microstructural heterogeneity at elevated Cu concentrations. This reduction of mechanical properties is consistent with the change in sp³/sp² carbon ratio of the coatings (Figs. 4 and 5). This is because metallic Cu is very soft. In particular, coatings with Cu content above 11.8 at% shows a more than 50% reduction in hardness. These results are consistent with the observations obtained from XRD, SEM, EDS mapping, Raman, and XPS analyses.

Hardness and Young’s modulus of the Cu-DLC coatings

In addition to hardness, Young’s modulus plays a crucial role in determining the wear performance of Cu-DLC coatings. Especially the H/E ratio, which represents the elastic strain to failure, provides a more accurate indicator of wear resistance than hardness alone. Wear in coatings is typically caused by plastic deformation and fracture during mechanical loading. A higher H/E ratio suggests that the material can sustain greater elastic deformation before yielding, which improves resistance to wear and damage [31–33]. It is also evident that increasing Cu content enhances the toughness of the coating, defined as the ability to absorb energy during plastic deformation and fracture. Therefore, precise control over both the Cu content and the sp³ carbon fraction in Cu-DLC coatings is essential for reducing internal stress and optimizing mechanical performance.

Another property of biomedical implants is its ability to resist compression stress, however, too much resistance can negatively impact coating adhesion and potentially lead to delamination of the coating from the biomedical implant. Studies have shown that incorporation of metal into DLC coatings can effectively reduce compression stress in the coatings to improve coating adhesion [34]. Therefore, we determined the variation in internal (compressive) stress of Cu-DLC coatings with increasing Cu concentration (Fig. 8). The internal stress was significantly reduced from 0.62 GPa to 0.04 GPa as the Cu concentration increased from 2.4 at% to 15.2 at%. This reduction is likely attributed to the incorporation of soft Cu, a decrease in sp³-bonded carbon, and changes in carbon bond lengths and angles caused by Cu incorporation.

Residual stress in Cu-DLC coatings

As the coatings adhere well to the substrate is critical for many practical applications, we assessed the adhesion of the Cu-DLC coatings on CoCrMo alloy substrates. Rockwell C indentation tests were conducted using a 1471 N load followed by SEM to analyse the indentation sites and surrounding areas for signs of cracking or delamination (Fig. 9). Spallation and radial cracks were not observed for any of the Cu-DLC coatings (Fig. 9), indicating excellent adhesion. Based on Rockwell C indentation tests evaluated according to the VDI 3198 standard, the adhesion quality of the coatings was classified using HF levels. All Cu-DLC coatings exhibited HF1-HF2 adhesion behaviour, indicating good coating to substrate adhesion suitable for biomedical applications [46]. This marks a significant improvement in adhesion compared to previously reported results for undoped DLC coatings [35].

Adhesion quality of Cu-DLC coatings

To further assess the tribological performance of the coatings, pin-on-disc wear tests were conducted using ultra high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) pins sliding against the coated samples. The results demonstrated negligible wear for the Cu-DLC coatings with Cu content up to 11.8 at%. The wear of UHMWPE pins and the coefficient of friction (COF) are shown in Fig. 10. Notably, Cu incorporation reduced the wear rate of the pins compared to undoped DLC coatings. Lowest wear rate of pins and lowest COF were observed for Cu-DLC coatings with Cu content at 11.8 at%, although COF for all the coatings are low and very close.

Friction coefficient and wear rate of Cu-DLC coatings

As Cu content increased to 11.8 at%, the COF progressively decreased with the lowest COF value of 0.15 observed for the Cu-DLC coating containing 11.8 at% Cu (Fig. 10). This can be attributed to Cu-induced modifications in the surface chemistry of the DLC coatings that reduce adhesive interactions between the sliding surfaces [36, 37]. These findings indicate that Cu doping at low to moderate concentrations significantly enhances the tribological performance of DLC coatings on CoCrMo, a material widely used as joint implants [40]. Corrosion of the biomedical implants can trigger inflammation and loss of antimicrobial properties [58, 59]. To assess corrosion resistance, we performed open-circuit potential (OCP) measurements over time. OCP is a useful indicator of corrosion susceptibility, with higher values generally suggesting lower tendency to corrode. The Cu-DLC coatings exhibited behaviour similar to that of undoped DLC, with the potential initially increasing and then stabilizing after approximately 3000 s (Fig. 11a).

(a) Open-circuit potential vs. time plots of Cu-DLC films and CoCrMo alloy, (b) Potentiodynamic polarization curves of the CoCrMo substrate and Cu-DLC coatings

Notably, the 11.8 at% Cu-DLC coating showed a brief initial drop in OCP, followed by a steady rise and eventual stabilization, while the 15.2 at% Cu-DLC coating displayed the most stable passivation behaviour and achieved the highest OCP value among all samples. This suggests lower likelihood for higher Cu concentrations to corrode. These findings are in agreement with previous reports indicating that increased Cu content can promote surface passivation [34]. Although OCP measurements alone do not fully capture the kinetics of corrosion processes, the results demonstrate that Cu-DLC coatings offer improved corrosion resistance compared to the bare CoCrMo alloy, making them promising candidates for protective surface applications, particularly in biomedical environments [39].

To further assess the corrosion potential of the coatings, potentiodynamic polarization analysis was performed and the corrosion potential and corrosion current were determined from the potentiodynamic polarization curves (Fig. 11b; Table 7). The results indicate that Cu-DLC coatings significantly enhanced the corrosion resistance of the CoCrMo substrate.

Overall, the polarization results indicate that Cu-DLC coatings, particularly at moderate Cu concentrations, significantly enhance the electrochemical stability of the CoCrMo alloy in chloride-containing environments.

Antibacterial analysis of Cu-DLC coatings

Bacterial adherence to and survival on biomedical implants is a major source of post-surgical infections [3]. To test whether incorporating Cu into DLC coatings is an effective approach to preventing bacterial survival on biomedical implants, the antibacterial properties of Cu-DLC coatings were analyzed. S. aureus and K. pneumoniae were selected, as both are associated with post-surgical and prosthetic joint infections that are becoming more difficult to treat due to increased antimicrobial resistance [60]. We assessed the survival of S. aureus and K. pneumoniae at different exposure times, using pure DLC as the control. As shown in Fig. 12, neither S. aureus nor K. pneumoniae showed a reduction in CFU after incubation in PBS alone or substrate at any time point. K. pneumoniae was unaffected by DLC incorporated with a Cu concentration of up to 8.1 at% (Fig. 12a). In contrast, after 60 min exposure there was a 2 log decrease in the amount of S. aureus recovered from surfaces coated with DLC incorporated with a Cu concentration of up to 8.1 at% (Fig. 12b; Cu-DLC I and Cu-DLC II). DLC incorporated with a Cu concentration of 11.8 at% showed killing of both K. pneumoniae and S. aureus, with complete killing after 60 and 40 min, respectively (Fig. 12; Cu-DLC III). The most rapid killing was observed when 15.2 at% Cu was incorporated into the DLC coatings with no K. pneumoniae or S. aureus recovered at any time points (Fig. 12). Together these results suggest that Cu-DLC coatings, particularly those with relatively high amounts of Cu-incorporated show biologically relevant antibacterial activity against both Gram positive and Gram negative bacteria.

Increased Cu-concentration in DLC-coatings results in K. pneumoniae and S. aureus killing

Discussion

The rise of antibiotic resistance has further complicated the treatment of bacterial infections at sites of biomedical implants. This has led to the exploration of new strategies, including the development of antibacterial surface coatings that do not impact the mechanical and anticorrosive properties of biomedical implants. In this study, we aimed to systematically define the structural, mechanical, chemical, tribological, and antibacterial properties of Cu-DLC coatings. We also specifically tested the impact of increased Cu-incorporation into the coatings to determine whether there was a potential trade-off between antibacterial activity and function of the implant.

In this study, we used physical vapour deposition with increasing Cu target power to incorporate increasing concentrations of Cu into DLC coatings. Our microstructural analyses confirmed the lack of crystalline structure in DLC coatings, the amorphous nature of DLC partially explains its low coefficient of friction [61]. The XPS results indicated that by increasing Cu target power, Cu concentration ranging from 2.4 at% to 15.2 at% were uniformly dispersed within the amorphous DLC matrix. Furthermore, incorporating increased concentrations of Cu into the DLC coatings gradually increased crystallinity, which was indicated by the XRD results [62, 63]. It was also observed that lower concentrations of Cu (≤ 11.8 at%) in Cu-DLC demonstrated various advantages over pure DLC, which includes the enhanced adhesion of the coatings on the substrate as well as a 21% decrease in wear rate of the pins. This improvement in wear resistance can be attributed to the presence of Cu, which enhances the lubricity and reduces friction at the contact interface, thereby minimizing the material loss [64]. Electrochemical corrosion analysis further revealed that increasing the Cu concentration in the Cu-DLC coatings moderated the corrosion inhibition rate. Although higher Cu incorporation improves OCP due to increased surface nobility excessive Cu content leads to microstructural heterogeneity and localized galvanic coupling between Cu-rich regions and the carbon matrix. These effects facilitate localized anodic dissolution, resulting in increased corrosion current density and reduces corrosion inhibition efficiency. This suggests that Cu plays a dual role by improving tribological performance and contributing to corrosion resistance by forming a more stable and protective passive film on the coating surface. However, high Cu concentrations in DLC coatings compromise coating’s mechanical integrity.

Antimicrobial action of the Cu-DLC coatings was evaluated using S. aureus and a multi-drug-resistant high-risk clone of K. pneumoniae. A multi-drug-resistant K. pneumoniae clone was selected for this study because metal resistance, including Cu-resistance, is often co-selected for with antibiotic resistance [65–68]. Consistent with these reports, an initial pilot experiment showed that the multi-drug-resistant clinical isolate of K. pneumoniae used in this study was less susceptible to Cu-toxicity than an antibiotic sensitive ATCC K. pneumoniae strain (Data not shown). Cu-resistance has been reported for drug-resistant isolates of S. aureus; however, previous studies have reported that antibiotic resistant S. aureus are susceptible to Cu-killing and that Cu-treatment can resensitize strains to antibiotics [69, 70]. We showed that contact with coatings containing 11.8 at% of Cu completely killed (~ 5 log decrease) S. aureus and K. pneumoniae after 40 and 60 min, respectively (Fig. 12). As expected, increasing the Cu content in DLC coating accelerated killing; Cu-DLC coatings containing 15.2 at% Cu killed both S. aureus and K. pneumoniae within 20 min. This is consistent with both time and concentration dependent antibacterial activity of Cu reported in other studies [71, 72]. The enhanced bacterial activity observed is likely attributed to the increased release of Cu ions at higher concentrations. High Cu concentrations have been proposed to act by disrupting bacterial membranes, interfering with essential metabolic functions and/or causing accumulation of toxic reactive oxygen species [72–77]. While our results are promising, future studies are needed to test toxicity in the host, tissue compatibility as well as antimicrobial activity in the presence of a functional host immune system. Beyond Cu related antibacterial mechanisms, variations in the sp3/sp2 bonding ratio may also influence bacterial adhesion and membrane integrity by modifying surface electronic properties and charge distribution, thereby contributing to contact mediated antibacterial activity [78].

Overall, this study highlights the multifunctional advantages of Cu-DLC coatings synthesized using a physical vapor deposition technique. These coatings not only exhibit mechanical robustness and surface stability but also provide antibacterial protection, which is considered a desirable feature for reducing post-surgical infections linked to biomedical implants. We also show that there is a trade-off between maintaining desirable mechanical and surface properties of Cu-doped DLC coatings and maximal antimicrobial activity. In the context of rising antibiotic resistance and the growing demand for infection resistant medical surfaces, our findings offer valuable insights into the rational design of bio-functional coatings.

Conclusion

Cu-doped DLC coatings were successfully synthesized using physical vapor deposition, achieving uniform Cu dispersion within the amorphous DLC matrix at concentrations ranging from 2.4 at% to 15.2 at%. Lowr concentrations of Cu (≤ 11.8 at%) enhanced adhesion and, tribological performance, including a 21% reduction in wear rate of the counter part of the pins compared to pure DLC, due to improved lubricity. Electrochemical corrosion analysis indicated improved corrosion resistance with moderate Cu doping (8.1 at%) compared to the undoped DLC film, likely due to the formation of a stable passive film; however, high Cu concentrations led to reductions in corrosion performance. Antibacterial performance was significantly improved with increasing Cu content, achieving complete killing of S. aureus and multi-drug-resistant K. pneumoniae strains within 20 to 60 min depending on Cu concentration. Antibacterial efficacy was both time- and concentration-dependent, with higher Cu levels accelerating bacterial killing. A trade-off exists between mechanical integrity and antibacterial potency, as high Cu content improves antibacterial performance but reduce the mechanical properties of the coating, such as hardness and elastic modulus.
