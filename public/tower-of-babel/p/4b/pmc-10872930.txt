Reciprocal transmission of activating and inhibitory signals and cell fate in regenerating T cells
Authors: Peter H. Wang, Robert S. Washburn, Dylan L. Mariuzza, Wen-Hsuan W. Lin, Amanda L. Gill, Rafi Ahmed, Steven L. Reiner
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe ability of activated progenitor T cells to self-renew while producing differentiated effector cell descendants may underlie immunological memory and persistent responses to ongoing infection. The nature of stem-like T cells responding to cancer and during treatment with immunotherapy is not clear. The subcellular organization of dividing progenitor CD8+ T cells from mice challenged with syngeneic tumors is examined here. Three-dimensional microscopy reveals an activating hub composed of polarized CD3, CD28, and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) activity at the putative immunological synapse with an inhibitory hub composed of polarized PD-1 and CD73 at the opposite pole of mitotic blasts. Progenitor T cells from untreated and inhibitory checkpoint blockade-treated mice yield a differentiated TCF1− daughter cell, which inherits the PI3K activation hub, alongside a discordantly fated, self-renewing TCF1+ sister cell. Dynamic organization of opposite activating and inhibitory signaling poles in mitotic lymphocytes may account for the enigmatic durability of specific immunity.

INTRODUCTION

Consumption of most mature blood lineages is replenished through constitutive and facultative hematopoiesis from long-term, self-renewing hematopoietic stem cells and their more down-stream stem-like progenitor cells.1 For lymphocytes recruited into immune responses, however, the unique antigen receptor specificity demands its own intra-clonal mechanism of self-renewal, as proposed more than 65 years ago.2 Clonal selection of lymphocytes into the immune response must avoid wholesale terminal differentiation of all cell descendants and, instead, must ensure that some cellular descendants retain the ability to self-renew with an ongoing ability to produce more differentiated cell progeny.

A stem-cell-like capacity of lymphocytes could be regarded as the potential explanation for immune memory after acute infection and immunization as well as the ability of immunocompetent hosts to exert control over lifelong, opportunistic infections such as cytomegalovirus and Toxoplasma gondii.3–6 A critical role of stem cell-like lymphocyte behavior has also been suggested in the T cell response to chronic active viral infections and cancer.7–14 Progress in understanding the mechanism of action of therapeutic PD-1-blockade has ensued from the appreciation that such therapeutic disinhibition of T cells boosts the acute output of freshly differentiated effector-like cells from self-renewing, stem cell-like progenitor T cells rather than reversing dysfunction of terminally exhausted T cells.7,8,10–12,15,16

Despite the emergence of a stem-like model of lymphocytes, there has been controversy concerning the cellular and molecular mechanisms underpinning regulated intra-clonal heterogeneity of cell fate.4,17 Antigen, costimulatory, and inhibitory receptors of T cells have all been implicated in the cell fate decision to differentiate or self-renew.18–21 Those same receptors can also undergo dynamic subcellular repositioning in activated and dividing T cells.22–24 We therefore examined the localization of key signaling molecules in mitotic T cells as well as the incipient cell fate of daughter T cells responding to tumor challenge and in the context of therapeutic PD-1 blockade.

RESULTS

Complementary activating and inhibitory hubs in anti-tumor T cells poised to divide

C57BL/6 mice were injected in the flank with B16F10 melanoma expressing the SIINFEKL epitope of ovalbumin (B16-OVA). After 10 days of tumor growth, CD8+ T cells from draining lymph nodes (LNs) and tumors were sorted and prepared for confocal imaging (Figure S1A). A large fraction of CD8+ T cells were SIINFEKL/Kb specific based on tetramer staining of the LN (Figure S1A) and tumor.13 We first examined the fluorescence staining pattern of PD-1 (labeled “PD1” in figures), which is expressed in a substantial fraction of LN and tumor T cells (Figure S1B). From lower-power microscopy views, there was evidence of heterogeneity in the pattern of PD-1 staining, with some cells having concentric PD-1 and others having more crescentic or polarized PD-1 (Figure 1A).

Like other signaling molecules, PD-1 undergoes dynamic rearrangement into the immune synapse upon in vitro activation.24 Higher-power analysis of interphase blasts (containing a single microtubule-organizing center [MTOC]) from LNs and tumors of challenged mice revealed a substantial proportion (more than half) of T cells with polarized rather than concentric distribution of PD-1 (Figures 1B, 1C, and S1C). In mitotic blasts (two oppositely facing microtubule arrays with DNA and cell body morphologies ranging from pro-metaphase through telophase), the frequency of PD-1 polarity became significantly more common than observed in interphase in the LN and tumor (Figures 1B and 1C). PD-1 staining appeared to concentrate at the cell membrane in polarized and non-polarized cells.

In interphase blasts with PD-1 polarity, PD-1 co-localized to the same side of the cell as the MTOC in the majority of cells, but in more than 25% of cells with interphase PD-1 polarity, PD-1 concentrated on the opposite side of the cell from the MTOC (Figures 1B and 1D). In mitotic blasts, the bipolar pattern of tubulin staining does not permit assignment of synaptic and distal poles. We therefore examined candidate transmembrane and membrane-proximal activating molecules that might indicate whether the high incidence of PD-1 polarity observed in mitotic blasts (Figures 1B and 1C) was more likely to be on the same or opposite side from the cell’s activation hub.

Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) activity, as detected by staining of its enzymatic by-product PIP3, polarizes and colocalizes with antigen receptors in activated T and B cell blasts.22 PI3K activation is responsible for silencing TCF1 in T cells and Pax5 in B cells, creating irreversibly determined effector blasts and plasmablasts, respectively.22,25 Optimal PI3K activity in T cells requires signaling through CD3 and CD28.26 Accordingly, our current examination of CD28 polarity revealed its uniform colocalization with focal PIP3 staining in interphase and mitotic blasts (Figure 2A).

Using the constellation of CD3, CD28, and PIP3 to mark the activation hub of T cells, we found that the polarity of PD-1 in mitotic blasts was typically at the distal pole from the activating hub in cells close to division (Figures 2B and 2C). Interphase blasts were characterized by a smaller fraction of PD-1 polarity distal to the activating hub. The trend of less frequent distal polarity earlier in the cell cycle is compatible with a prior description of initial proximal polarity of PD-1 along with antigen receptors at the immune synapse of acutely activated T cells.24 In contrast to the common localization of PD-1 at the pole opposite from the activation hub, we found that the inhibitory receptors LAG-3 and CTLA-4, which also frequently polarize, are more often colocalized to the same side as the activation hub in interphase and in mitotic T cells (Figures S1D and S1E).

Insofar as PD-1 may play a causal role in maintaining self-renewal of TCF1+ T cells,18–21 we took a candidate approach of examining the positioning of CD73, an ectonucleotidase that may also mark and maintain self-renewing T cells.27 As observed for polarity of PD-1, the polarity of CD73 in interphase LN T cell blasts is frequently localized opposite the activating hub (Figures 2D and 2E). In mitotic LN blasts, CD73 polarity becomes significantly more likely to localize opposite the activating hub (Figures 2D and 2E). Combined analysis of activating (CD3, CD28, and PIP3) and inhibitory (PD-1 and CD73) markers suggests that activated progenitor T cells committed to division (mitotic blasts) are more likely to organize polar-opposite signaling domains associated with differentiation versus self-renewal (Figure 2F).

Independent model and methodology supporting reciprocal T cell asymmetry

C57BL/6 mice were next challenged with lung carcinoma CMT167 cells. After 14 days, imaging cytometry of gated, progenitor PD1+TCF1+CD8+ T cells from draining LNs and tumors was used to examine subcellular localization of PD-1 in interphase cells with 2N DNA content versus G2/M cells with 4N DNA content (Figure S2A). There was substantial polarity of PD-1 in interphase T cell progenitors, which became significantly more frequent in G2/M progenitors (Figures 3A, 3B, and S2B), as was observed using the first model and methodology. Interphase cells with PD-1 polarity contained many with PD-1 concentrated at the MTOC and some with PD-1 concentrated at the distal pole (Figure 3C). Using CD3 polarity as a frame of reference for the activating hub, PD-1 polarity opposite from CD3 was evident in a substantial fraction of interphase cells (Figures 3D and 3E). There was also a non-significant trend toward elevated frequency of PD-1 polarity opposite from CD3 polarity in G2/M blasts (Figures 3D and 3E). The observed receptor polarity using imaging cytometry did not appear to be an artifact of 2-dimensional imaging. Using this method, we readily detected many cells with concentric PD-1 distribution among progenitor blasts (Figure S2B) and routinely concentric CD3 distribution among small, resting T cells (Figure S2C).

Discordant daughter cell fates linked to reciprocal signal transmission

To examine the fate of incipient daughter cells arising from mitotic TCF1+ progenitor T cells 10 days after challenge with B16-OVA, we examined cells undergoing cytokinesis using confocal microscopy. Conjoined sibling cells were identified by the presence of a tubulin bridge (based on bright-field morphology and tubulin fluorescence) connecting two adjacent cell bodies, each with a discrete nucleus (based on DAPI staining). In LNs and tumors, the major pattern of TCF1 staining in cell doublets meeting the criteria of conjoined siblings and arising from TCF1+ progenitor T cells was one of discordant expression, with one sister cell TCF1+ and the other sister cell TCF1+ (Figures 4A–4C). Less frequently, we detected sibling pairs where both cells concordantly expressed TCF1 (Figures 4B and 4C).

In sibling cell pairs with discordant TCF1 expression, the majority of the pairs also exhibited greater PIP3 staining in the daughter cell with silenced TCF1 expression (Figure 4D), which is consistent with the established role of PI3K activation in driving irreversible effector differentiation.4,22,25,28 Examination of sibling cell pairs from mice treated with anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-1 plus anti-LAG-3 revealed a similar ability of progenitor T cells to yield one daughter cell that self-renews (TCF1+) alongside a sibling cell that undergoes effector commitment (TCF1−) (Figures 4B and 4C). Despite the clear capacity of sibling cell pairs from immunotherapy-treated mice to undergo divisions with discordant cell fate outcomes, we found a lower frequency of discordant divisions that exhibited asymmetric PIP3 abundance in the daughter cell with silenced TCF1 expression (Figure 4D). This finding may be consistent with the proposition that loss of inhibitory signals lowers the threshold for activating T cells and silencing TCF1.18–21,29

Results similar to the confocal analyses were obtained using CMT167 challenge and imaging cytometry. We readily detected sibling cell pairs with discordant expression of TCF1 and some sibling cell pairs with concordant expression of TCF1 in all treatment arms (Figures S3A–S3C). In some instances, more so from tumors than LNs, sibling cell pairs where both cells were TCF1− were detected using confocal imaging and imaging cytometry (Figure S3D), probably reflecting a higher intra-tumoral incidence of irreversibly committed TCF1− cells, which can only give rise to two TCF1− daughter cells4,7 (Figure S1B).

DISCUSSION

The present results offer a hypothetical model for how dividing lymphocytes could perform the paradoxical task of differentiation alongside non-differentiation; i.e., self-renewal.2 Rather than invoking a stochastic mechanism, the proposed model is grounded in the regulated, facultative polarity lymphocytes and their cell descendants undergo as a result of activation in vivo.25,30 In addition to the concentration of activating molecules at the immune synapse, other molecules, often inhibitory in nature, become excluded from the synapse to form a complementary distal pole complex.31 Such distal counter-regulation in lymphocytes is dynamic, based on the activation state, differentiation fate, and stage of the cell cycle.32

The present model of progenitor CD8+ T cells suggests formation of a stereotyped arrangement of activating receptors and downstream PI3K activity that drives differentiation4,22,25,28,33,34 at one pole of the mitotic spindle, with inhibitory receptors that curb differentiation and guard self-renewal18–21,27 positioned at the opposite pole of the cell. Accordingly, we observe that a daughter cell that inherits stronger PI3K tends to silence TCF1, the gateway event for irreversible differentiation, while her sibling cell, which arises from the inhibitory pole, tends to maintain the status quo of TCF1 expression and self-renewal. Together with recent findings from acute and chronic infection models,4,14,22,25,28,35 the present results concerning the response to cancer suggest that the ability of an activated T cell progenitor to allocate opposite fates to its two daughter cells may underpin a robust clonal selection theory of lymphocytes.2

Although consolidation of an activation hub may occur early, in interphase, it is probably not surprising to find that consolidation of the putative inhibitory hub of PD-1 and CD73 tends to occur more frequently in mitosis, as cells prepare for division. Silencing of TCF1 in CD8+ T cells has been linked to the act of cell division, with non-dividing cells only able to maintain TCF1 expression.4 The stem-like behavior of progenitor T cells thus appears to coordinate two separable requirements: (1) the necessity to divide in order to differentiate4,36 and (2) the ability to transmit unequal information during such division, allowing one daughter cell to undergo irreversible differentiation while inhibiting change in the sister cell.22,25

Distal PD-1 and CD73 polarity in anaphase and telophase could simultaneously support self-renewal of the daughter inheriting more inhibitory receptors while favoring differentiation of the sister cell inheriting fewer inhibitory receptors by lowering its activation threshold. Such a premise is compatible with the observed roles of PD-1 and CD73 in curbing effector differentiation as well as promoting T cell self-renewal and memory.18–21,27,37 It is also likely that redundancy in this robust process is conferred by the reciprocal inheritance of disparity in activating and inhibitory information. Because PD-1 blockade does not disrupt cell divisions with discordant cell fate outcomes, it is possible that imbalance of PI3K activity itself can foster birth of one TCF1+ sister cell alongside a TCF1− sibling cell.

The ability to detect divisions with discordant cell fate between sibling cells in mice treated with inhibitory checkpoint blockade implies that this stem-like mechanism may be the primary pathway for clonal lymphocyte renewal under physiological and pharmacological challenges. In contrast to the durability of T cell responses in acute infection or low-level persistent infection, responses to cancer and induced by inhibitory checkpoint blockade are often finite, which is often ascribed to dysfunction of differentiated T cells, known as exhaustion.38 The generality of this stem-like mechanism in the contexts of acute infection through cancer and immunotherapy suggests that impairment of T cell self-renewal may be critical and rate limiting for the durability of high-intensity immune responses.

Limitations of the study

The number of mitotic events recovered for confocal microscopy was relatively small, although the findings supported significance of the trend that PD-1 more frequently localizes opposite the activating hub as cells approach division. The present findings focused on the role of discordant T cell division for regeneration in cancer but did not fully address the fate of T cells under pressure from immunotherapy to intensify production of differentiated daughter cells. Blockade of PD-1 or combination blockade of PD-1 and LAG-3 did not seem to eliminate the self-renewal of a TCF1+ sister cell alongside generation of a TCF1− differentiated cell despite genetic evidence that PD-1 and LAG-3 cooperatively safeguard some TCF1+ T cell populations.19 We do not know whether a more intensive blockade regimen or blockade of a different combination of inhibitory receptors would result in TCF1+ progenitors that give rise to two TCF1− differentiated sister cells instead of undergoing some self-renewal.

METHOD DETAILS

Tumor challenges

Animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility accredited by Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care, and experiments were performed in accordance with Columbia University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratories) aged 8–12 weeks were used for tumor challenges with B16F10 melanoma expressing SIINFEKL epitope of chicken ovalbumin fused to GFP (provided by Andrea Schietinger, MSKCC) or CMT167 Kras mutant lung carcinoma cells (Sigma-Aldrich), as described.13 Tumor cells (2.5 × 106) were inoculated subcutaneously in the right flank. On d10 (B16-OVA) or d14 (CMT16), draining lymph nodes and tumors were obtained from euthanized mice. For some experiments, mice were randomized to i.p. treatment every three days with isotype control (Armenian Hamster IgG; BioXcell), anti–PD-1 (200 μg [female]; 250 μg [male]) (J43 clone; BioXcell), or combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-LAG-3 (200 μg [female]; 250 μg [male]) (C9B7W clone; BioXcell). CMT167 mice were treated on days 4, 7, 10, and 13. B16-OVA mice were treated on days 3, 6, and 9.

Confocal microscopy

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and draining lymph node cell preparations were performed as described.13,28 For cell sorting prior to microscopy, Fc receptors were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32. Subsequently, cells were stained with either CD8a PE/Cy7 (QA17A07; BioLegend) or CD8b percpcy5.5 (YTS156.7.7; BioLegend) for sorting on BD FACS Aria II with a 100-μm nozzle. Sorted cells were washed once with PBS and reconstituted at a concentration of ~1 × 10^6 cells/mL and transferred to coverslips (Thermo Fisher) coated with poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich) at a density of 50,000 cells per coverslip. Cells were allowed to adhere at 37°C before 4% paraformaldehyde fixation (Electron Microscopy Sciences); quenching with 50mM NH4CL (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich); blocking overnight in buffer comprised of 0.25% fish skin gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.01% saponin (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS.

The day following blocking, cells were stained with different, three-antibody combinations of rat anti-beta-tubulin (YOL1/34; Thermo Fisher supplier Novus Biologicals), rabbit anti-beta tubulin (ab6046; Abcam), mouse anti-beta-tubulin (AA2; Abcam), mouse anti-PD-1 (RMP 1–30; BioLegend), rabbit anti-TCF1 (C63D9; Cell Signaling Technology), syrian hamster anti-CD28 (37.51; BioLegend), mouse anti-PIP3 (RC6F8; Thermo Fisher), syrian hamster anti-CD3 (500A2; Biolegend), rat anti-CD73 (TY/11.8; BioLegend), rat anti-LAG-3 (C9B7W; BioLegend), and armenian hamster anti-CTLA4 (UC10-4B9) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by blocking buffer washes. Subsequently, secondary antibody staining with different combinations of goat anti-rat 488 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-rabbit 488 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-mouse IgG 488 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-rat 568 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-mouse IgM 568 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-syrian hamster 647 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-armenian hamster 647 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-rat 647 (Thermo Fisher), goat anti-rabbit 647 (Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at room temperature, was followed by blocking buffer washes, and water rinses.

ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher) was used to mount stained cells to frosted microscope slides (Thermo Fisher) overnight. Nail polish was used to seal coverslips to microscope slides the following day. Images were collected at the Confocal and Specialized Microscopy Shared Resource of the Herbert Irving Comprehensive Cancer Center at Columbia University. Z-stacked images were acquired on a Nikon Ti Eclipse laser-scanning inverted confocal microscope 60×/1.49 Apo TIRF oil lens (Nikon) with 405nm, 488nm, 561nm, and 638nm lasers. Transmitted light (brightfield) and some tubulin images were from a single-z plane. Other images were displayed by creating a maximum projection image from a z stack summary.

Imaging cytometry

Single-cell suspensions from draining lymph nodes or tumor cell suspensions were incubated with anti-mouse CD16/32 (clone 13; BioLegend) to block Fc receptors for 10 min on ice and fixed for 20 min at room temperature, followed by washing with permeabilization buffer (Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Kit; TONBO). Cells were incubated with fluorescent antibodies in a permeabilization buffer for either 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4°C. FACS Abs were purchased from BioLegend, Cell Signaling Technology, or Santa Cruz Biotechnology and included CD3 (145-2C11), Tubulin (YOL 1/34), CD8b (YTS156.7.7), PD-1 (RMP-1-30), and TCF1 (C63D9). Following staining, cells were washed with permeabilization buffer and stained in 0.01 mg/ML DAPI (Thermo Fisher) for 5 min before suspending in PBS for analysis using the Amnis ImageStreamX MkII imaging cytometer. Images were collected at 60× magnification, compensated using single stain controls from the spleen of tumor-challenged mice, and gated and processed using the IDEAS V6.2 software.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Image analysis

All images from confocal microscopy and imaging cytometry were exported for quantitation using Fiji V2.3.0/1.53f software (National Institutes of Health). Interphase blasts in confocal analysis were identified by enlarged, round shape in brightfield, DAPI staining of nucleus, and one microtubule organizing center (MTOC). Mitotic cells in confocal analyses were characterized by having two MTOCs, manifest as arrays of microtubules emanating from two separate poles of the cells. In mitosis, DAPI staining pattern could be round or more elongated in prometaphase, condensed into a metaphase plate, or elongated with furrowing in early anaphase. Cell body and DNA furrowing of mitotic cells was distinguished from a uropod structure by the presence of DAPI stain within both lobes and microtubules emanating from both poles of the cells. Conjoined sibling cell pairs undergoing cytokinesis were specifically identified based on the extended cytoplasmic cleft between cell bodies in brightfield images, a defined tubulin bridge within the cleft, and dual-nuclei DAPI staining.25 Assessment of sibling status was performed blinded to the staining of molecules-of-interest.

For imaging cytometry analyses, interphase cells were defined as those within the singlet brightfield gate (area vs. aspect ratio) and containing 2N DNA content. For comparative analyses, singlet G2/M cells were defined as those with 4N DNA content and enriched from the lower aspect ratio within singlet brightfield gate (Figure S2A). Cytokinetic, conjoined sibling cell doublets were enriched by cytometry gating on events with high brightfield area and low brightfield aspect ratio, along with imaging evidence of tubulin staining within the joint between the two cell bodies (Figure S2A).

Quantitation of polarity was performed as previously described.22 Briefly, using Fiji software, interphase and mitotic cells were bifurcated with an equator into halves, with respect to the MTOC or spindle location, as well the positioning of the DAPI stain (Figure S1C). Relative amounts of molecules-of-interest and DAPI staining (as a control for symmetry) for each half was calculated by taking the (fluorescence of hemisphere 1 – fluorescence of hemisphere 2) and dividing by the (fluorescence of hemisphere 1 + fluorescence of hemisphere 2), with values >0.2 and < −0.2 considered polarized. If both molecules-of-interest were polarized, the enrichment being on the same versus opposite sides is quantitated. Comparative quantitation of TCF1 and PIP3 intensity between sibling cells was calculated by taking the (fluorescence of sibling 1 – fluorescence of sibling 2) and dividing by the (fluorescence of sibling 1 + fluorescence of sibling 2), with values >0.2 and < −0.2 considered asymmetric. For imaging cytometry, asymmetry quantitation was performed in the same manner.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with Prism V9 software. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison tests were used to compare calculated asymmetry of molecules-of-interest to distribution of DNA in interphase and mitotic cells. For conjoined sibling cells, two-way ANOVA with Šidák multiple comparison tests were used to compare calculated asymmetry of TCF1 and PIP3 between sibling cells to the abundance of DNA between cells. Binomial tests were used to compare frequency of reciprocal asymmetry in interphase versus mitotic cells, as well for comparing frequencies of cell fate and PIP3 disparity in treated versus untreated animals.
