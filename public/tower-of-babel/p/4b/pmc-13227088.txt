Opportunistic Screening of High-Risk Breast Cancer Variants in Hospital Biobank Setting
Authors: Minja Pehrsson, Eveliina Jakkula, Kimmo Ala-Kulju, Anu Loukola, Janita Niemelä, Katja Löfman, Anna-Kaisa Anttonen, Tuomo Meretoja, Johanna Mattson, Maarit Lappalainen, Minna Pöyhönen, Olli Carpén
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Genetic data accumulating in biobanks provide novel possibilities for personalized medicine through opportunistic screening. In this pilot study, we evaluated the applicability and impact of screening pathogenic variants (PVs) of BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 genes predisposing to hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC), from biobank samples.Methods:PVs of BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 were screened from array-based genotyping data produced in the FinnGen study and returned to Helsinki Biobank (HBB). Samples with suspected variants were validated by sequencing, and novel findings were disclosed to participants who had consented to return of results (RoR). Post-disclosure surveys were conducted to examine participants’ experiences and perceptions of receiving genetic results.Results:Of 103 donors contacted, 71% (n = 73) consented to receiving HBOC risk results. In the cohort of approximately 11,000 HBB donors, 73 carriers of PVs in BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2 were identified, representing 0.6% of screened samples. Only 26% (n = 19) of these PVs had been previously identified in healthcare. Among newly identified families, 53% (17/32) met the National Comprehensive Cancer Network criteria for genetic testing. Return of biobank-based screening results led to changes in clinical management in 89% (17/19) of newly identified female PV carriers, with 35% opting for risk-reducing surgery. Survey responses indicated high satisfaction with RoR and perceived personal utility of the information.Conclusions:Returning actionable genetic biobank findings provides clear clinical benefit and is supported by donors.Impact:Our results demonstrate that array-based genotyping data produced from biobank samples can serve in personalized disease-risk screening and preventive medicine.

Introduction

Biobanks collect biological samples and associated health data to support medical research. When genetic data produced from biobank samples in research projects are returned to biobanks for utilization in further studies, biobanks become a notable source of genetic information. Biobanks can significantly contribute to personalized and preventive medicine, if clinically actionable research results can be efficiently utilized in healthcare.

Biobanking in Finland is regulated by the Biobank Act, which mandates the return of individual health information determined from biobank samples, such as genetic information, upon request by the donor (1). Sample donors are asked in the biobank consent whether they opt-in to be contacted concerning findings in the sample that are significant to their health, and 99% of Helsinki Biobank (HBB) donors have given their consent. As biobank sample donors have the right to be informed about findings relevant to their health, the impact of reporting genetic biobank results needs to be addressed.

Several biobanks have implemented a genotype-first approach to identify carriers of clinically significant variants (2–5), and this opportunistic screening has been suggested as a possible model for population-wide genetic testing (5). Biobank participants generally express positive attitudes toward the return of results (RoR), with most indicating a preference to receive genetic findings (6). When genetic information is available in biobanks, it should be screened for hereditary cancer-predisposing variants, and systematic RoR should be planned (7). The European Society of Human Genetics recommends that opportunistic genomic screening should be offered through pilots to generate data for effect evaluation (8).

Currently, no specific recommendations exist on which genetic variants should be screened from biobank samples. However, screened genetic variants should be of clinical relevance regarding their pathogenicity and penetrance and actionable offering options for treatment or prevention (6). FinnGen, a large biobank research project, has produced array-based genotyping data from more than 500,000 biobank donors (9). This study was designed to pilot screening of selected high-risk pathogenic variants (PVs) in BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 genes from the data returned from FinnGen to HBB. These three genes are included in the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics recommendation for reporting of secondary findings in clinical exome and genome sequencing (10). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention screening group has categorized BRCA1 and BRCA2 to Tier 1 as the most appropriate genes for screening, and PALB2 to the Tier 2 group of possible screening candidates (11).

PVs in BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 substantially increase the lifetime risk of cancer. Female BRCA1/2 carriers have up to ∼70% risk of breast cancer and up to 45% (BRCA1) or 20% (BRCA2) risk of ovarian cancer (12, 13), whereas female PALB2 carriers face 40% to 60% breast cancer and ∼5% ovarian cancer risks with family history and polygenic risk scores (PRS) contributing to risk estimates (14, 15). Male BRCA2 carriers have an up to 60% prostate cancer risk (16), and pancreatic cancer risk ranges from 2% to 10% across these genes (17, 18). The Finnish founder variant PALB2 c.1592del, with a heterozygous carrier frequency of 0.35% (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/; RRID: SCR_014964), represents the most common single high-risk Finnish breast cancer susceptibility variant, accounting for about 0.7% of breast cancer cases in Finland (19, 20), and was therefore regarded as important to include in the pilot. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and European Society for Medical Oncology guidelines for the management of individuals at high hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) risk include breast-imaging screening and prophylactic interventions such as risk-reducing mastectomy (RRM) and risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO; refs. 17, 18). Genetic testing for HBOC is well accepted, and recipients of HBOC risk information report low levels of distress (5, 7, 21).

Genotyping with single-nucleotide polymorphism arrays has been commonly used for producing genetic information from biobank samples. As array-based genotyping data are not optimal for reliable detection of very rare variants, such data should not be used to guide clinical decisions without further confirmation (22). However, with a validation step, array-based genotyping data produced from biobank samples could serve in screening individuals for selected rare variants when sequencing data are not available. This pilot study aimed at (i) evaluating the applicability of array-based genotyping data for screening of selected rare variants, (ii) investigating the preferences and attitudes of biobank sample donors to receive genetic results, and (iii) examining the clinical impact of opportunistic screening and RoR of BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 variants to biobank donors.

Materials and Methods

Study cohort and ethics approval

Material for this study consists of a subcohort [n = 11,622, 63.2% females, data freeze (DF) 2] of the altogether ∼120,000 donors from HBB genotyped as part of the FinnGen research project (https://www.finngen.fi/en; ref. 9). Each participant had given a biobank consent, and each individual receiving genetic results provided a study-specific written informed consent. This study was approved by the Helsinki University Hospital (HUS) Regional Committee on Medical Research Ethics (HUS/1990/2021) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. More information about HBB is available at the biobank’s website (https://helsinginbiopankki.fi/en/front-page).

Variant detection

Genotyping of blood-derived DNA was performed using FinnGen Thermo Fisher Axiom custom array v1. Genotyping, quality control (QC), and genotype calling have been described elsewhere (9). Genotyping data returned to HBB were screened for a total of 52 recurrent PVs previously identified in Finnish patients with HBOC in BRCA1 (33 variants), BRCA2 (18 variants), and PALB2 (c.1592del, founder variant; see Supplementary Table S1). The reference sequences for variant nomenclature are BRCA1 NM_007294.4, BRCA2 NM_000059.4, and PALB2 NM_024675.4, and ClinVar classification for the screened variants was queried in November 2024 from the ClinVar website (RRID: SCR_006169). Variants were filtered from variant call format (VCF) genotyping DF2 (called based on a total of 96,499 samples) and recalled DF8 (called based on a total of 342,499 samples) data, as indicated in the results. The positive predictive value (PPV), defined as the proportion of confirmed findings of variants identified from VCF data, was calculated for all detected variants and per gene. Cluster plot analysis of intensity data CEL files were performed for a subset of DF2 genotype data by Axiom Analysis Suite v4.0.3 software, and putative heterozygotes were manually determined from the cluster plots. Putative PV carriers identified from array-based genotyping data were eligible for further analysis if they were aged ≥18 years and had indicated in their biobank consent their willingness to receive significant findings related to health from analyses of their biobank samples. Electronic health records (EHRs) of these biobank donors were reviewed by text search for the gene names (BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2) and their possible spelling variations (e.g., BRCA 1, BRCA-1, BRCA 2) and were manually revised. Samples with putative PVs based on genotyping data but with no mention of the PV in EHRs were validated from existing HBB DNA samples with next-generation sequencing (NGS) in the accredited genetic diagnostic laboratory of HUS Diagnostic Center with a gene panel of 16 cancer-associated genes (BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDH1, CHEK2, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PALB2, PMS2, PTEN, RAD51C, RAD51D, STK11, and TP53). Possible PVs detected in other genes (secondary findings) in the validation were not disclosed to the participants.

Registry data, survey, and statistical analysis

Sample donors were classified as PV carriers (NGS validated) or as non-carriers (array false-positive cases confirmed as negative for PVs in BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 by NGS). Demographics of the participants were collected from EHRs, and ages are reported as age at initial contact. Personal history of breast, ovarian/fallopian tube, prostate, and pancreatic cancers was investigated by use of EHR-extracted International Classification of Diseases-10 codes: C50 (malignant neoplasm of breast), C56 (malignant neoplasm of ovary), C57 (malignant neoplasm of other and unspecified female genital organs), C61 (malignant neoplasm of prostate), and C25 (malignant neoplasm of pancreas). These codes do not include in situ neoplasms.

Feedback on the reception of genetic results from biobank samples was gathered using a two-stage survey. The first questionnaire was mailed immediately after the disclosure of the preliminary biobank result, and the second shorter questionnaire ≥6 months after receiving results when PV carriers had received their confirmatory testing and in-depth genetic counseling. Also participants who had not returned the first survey received the second survey. Survey responses were collected on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree).

For hypothesis testing, Fisher's exact test served for categorical data, Welch's t-test for continuous data, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test for survey data. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SciPy Python library version 1.7.3 (RRID: SCR_008058; ref. 23).

Result disclosure

Participant consent status and eligibility for RoR were verified prior to contact. All individuals (both confirmed PV carriers and non-carriers) who in their biobank consent had indicated their interest in receiving findings significant to health without an EHR record of the investigated gene/variant, and whose samples were sent for validation, were contacted by mailing an invitation letter. The invitation letter contained information about the study, the genes investigated, and the cancer risk the variants confer but no information about the results. The recalled individuals were asked either to opt-in or to opt-out of receiving genetic biobank results with a study-specific consent form. The material was sent via mail, with a maximum of two reminder mails sent. Consent disposition for each participant was documented as either active consent (returned written consent), active decline (returned written refusal), or passive decline (no response). If consent to receive results was acquired, normal results (array false-positives validated by sequencing as non-carriers) were disclosed by mail. Participants with NGS-verified PVs in BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2 received a scheduled telecounseling call either by a genetics nurse (K. Löfman) or by a clinical geneticist (E. Jakkula or M. Pöyhönen) disclosing the biobank result and offering a referral to the HUS Department of Clinical Genetics for confirmatory testing and in-depth genetic counseling. Confirmatory genetic testing from a new blood sample was performed in the accredited genetics laboratory of HUS Diagnostic Center. After confirmatory diagnostic genetic testing, participants had either an in-person genetic counseling visit or telecounseling where also detailed family history was collected and results were recorded to the participant’s EHR. Participants were encouraged to inform their at-risk family members about the availability of cascade testing, and, when appropriate, were referred—following confirmatory testing and genetic counseling—to surveillance or risk-reducing surgeries. On average, contact for RoR occurred about 4.3 years after biobank enrollment.

Cancer history and medical impact

Personal and familial cancer history was assessed from documented medical history supplemented with questionnaire data for personal and family histories of HBOC-related cancers available for 32 individuals in the clinical cohort referred to HUS Department of Clinical Genetics. Medical records were reviewed for evidence of previous clinical genetic testing either in the participant or in family. Based on the data of personal and family history of relevant cancers, eligibility for HBOC genetic testing based on NCCN criteria (18) and Finnish national guidelines by the Finnish Breast Cancer group (2022 criteria; https://rintasyoparyhma.yhdistysavain.fi) was assessed. The participants’ ongoing medical management was reviewed via EHR, and depending on the time of result disclosure, follow-up periods for PV carriers ranged from 19 to 23 months (7/2022–5/2024). To ensure participant anonymity, ages at initial contact were reported as age ranges.

Results

Identification and validation of pathogenic BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 variants

VCF data–based genotyping data analysis for the 52 selected PVs in BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 genes (see Supplementary Table S1) identified altogether 117 heterozygous calls in 116 of the 11,622 (1%) biobank samples (Table 1). Of 52 screened variants, heterozygous calls were observed for 27. Of the detected PV calls, 104 passed QC, whereas 13 variants (1 BRCA1, 4 BRCA2, and 8 PALB2) failed QC in array-based genotyping. EHR review revealed that of the 116 sample donors, 13 had a record of prior positive diagnostic genetic testing for the variant. For the remaining 103 samples, we performed internal validation by NGS. Based on EHR review and validation, 59% (69/117) of the DF2 VCF data–based putative variants were confirmed as true-positives. 30.6% (11/36) and 28% (7/25) of the QC-passed BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV calls, respectively, were true-positives, and all QC-failed BRCA1 and BRCA2 calls were negative. All 51 suspected PALB2 PV carriers, including eight which had failed QC in array-based genotyping, were confirmed as true-positives (PPV 100%) in NGS. (Table 1). When diagnostic testing results were compared with VCF data, the sensitivity of detecting PV carriers from array-based genotyping data was 83.3% for BRCA1, 80.4% for BRCA2, and 100% for the PALB2 c.1592del variant (see Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Results S1).

Additional post hoc analyses were performed to evaluate the relevance and utility of cluster plot review and genotype calling in an expanded dataset accumulating during this study. Of the 43 QC-passed false-positive BRCA1/2 variant calls in VCF data, 39.5% (17/43) were negative based on the cluster plot review (Table 1; see Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Results S1). The initial false-positive rate of 41% (48/117) in VCF data fell to 20.9% (24/115) when cluster plot review was combined in the analysis. As the calling accuracy of rare variants is expected to improve with increased sample size, initial DF2 calls for the 116 samples were compared with recalled DF8 genotypes (Table 1). For all 69 samples with confirmed BRCA1/2 and PALB2 variants, DF8 data contained the correct heterozygous genotype. Of the 43 QC-passed false-positive BRCA1/2 samples in DF2 data, only 9.3% (4/43) had a false-positive heterozygous genotype for the variant in the recalled DF8 data. By using the DF8 data, 90% of the false-positives could have been excluded from validation. Thus, the larger recalled dataset significantly improved the accuracy of the genotype calls of rare BRCA1/2 variants. Based on VCF data and cluster plot review, a total of 73 confirmed PV carriers (0.6%) were identified in the HBB DF2 cohort.

Response rate and return of results

A total of 103 sample donors with a consent to recall, and whose sample had been validated, were eligible for RoR (Fig. 1). To enable comparison of opinions regarding disclosure of genetic results between PV carriers and non-carriers, we included, in addition to all 46 (45.1%) carriers of BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2 PVs, also 56 (54.9%) non-carriers in the recall cohort. Between PV carriers and non-carriers in the recall cohort, no significant difference appeared in either age, sex, or prevalence of cancer diagnoses. A significant difference (P = 0.017) did appear in the prevalence of HBOC-associated cancers, with 21.7% of PV carriers having a history of breast cancer, ovarian cancer, or prostate cancer, compared with 5.4% in the non-carrier group (Table 2). No participant had a documented diagnosis of pancreatic cancer in the EHR.

Participants in the recall cohort were contacted by letter a maximum of three times, with a request to return their consent to either opt-in or to opt-out from receiving genetic HBOC risk results. Cumulative response rates were 53.4%, 59.2%, and 72.8% after the first, second, and third contact attempts. Of the responders, 70.9% (73/103) gave a consent to receive genetic results (active opt-in), 1.9% (2/103) declined (active opt-out), and 27.2% (28/103) failed to respond (passive opt-out). Comparing consenting sample donors (disclosure cohort) with active or passive decliners, the opt-in participants were more often female and slightly younger, although these differences lacked statistical significance (Table 2). A statistically significant difference (P = 0.014) appeared in willingness to receive genetic risk information depending on cancer history, with a cancer diagnosis in 35.6% of the consenting participants compared with 10.3% in decliners and non-respondents. All 13 sample donors in the recall cohort with a history of HBOC-associated cancer consented to receive genetic risk information (Table 2). The results were returned to the 73 consenting participants; the non-carriers received their negative result by mail, and for the 33 PV carriers (45.2%), the result was disclosed in a counseling call.

Participant feedback on RoR

A two-stage feedback survey evaluated the attitudes of biobank donors regarding their expectations of and experiences with RoR. Of participants who received results, 82.2% (60/73) responded to the first survey, of whom, 85% (51/60) completed also the second survey six months after receiving their results (Table 3). 91% of PV carriers (30/33) and 75% of non-carriers (30/40) responded to the first survey.

The great majority of respondents assumed that they would be contacted if information relevant to their health was detected in their biobank sample (Table 3; Fig. 2). The study participants found it easy to decide about participating in the study, and they felt that written information provided beforehand was adequate (Table 3; Fig. 2). Respondents felt that they benefited from the genetic information received (Table 3; Fig. 2). Three quarters of the participants experienced no increase in anxiety after receiving the results, although PV carriers reported more anxiety at that point and a need for counseling compared with non-carriers (Fig. 2; Table 3). Of PV carriers, 20% (6/30) reported feeling anxiety (a score of 4 or 5) after receiving the results (Q6) and 10% (3/30) were still experiencing anxiety (Q7) six months after receiving the results. The PV carriers mostly felt that the telecounseling they received at result disclosure was adequate, but they expressed a slightly higher need for additional counseling than did non-carriers (Table 3). Respondents generally felt that their decision to participate in the study was right (Fig. 2), and after receiving comprehensive genetic counseling, all 30 PV carriers shared this same opinion. Respondents also felt that biobank samples should be more widely used in risk screening (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Clinical implications of result disclosure

Of the 33 clinical cohort participants, 22 (67%) were female, and of those, 6 (27.3%) were below the breast cancer national screening age of 50 years. Of the participants without prior knowledge of the PV, only one (ID28) did not participate in the confirmatory clinical testing. Diagnostic genetic testing confirmed the variant in all cases, and a secondary finding (CHEK2 c.1100del) was detected in ID3.

Of participants, 42.4% (14/33) had a personal history of cancer, 30.3% (10/33) with HBOC-related cancer (Table 4). Of the females, 31.8% (7/22) had been previously diagnosed with breast cancer or ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), and 3 of 6 of breast cancer cases had occurred before the national screening age of 50 years. Two females (2/22, 9.1%) were diagnosed with ovarian cancer, and 9.1% (1/11) of the males had a diagnosis of prostate cancer. All participants except for ID5 had a family history of cancer, and 60.6% (20/33) had a family history of breast cancer. In 18.2% (6/33) of the cases, the participant or a close family member had been previously evaluated for HBOC or undergone genetic testing for cancer risk genes. The variant of ID1 had been previously diagnosed in a family member, but the information had not been passed to the biobank participant. Of the 27 individuals carrying the PALB2 founder PV, breast (including DCIS), ovarian, or prostate cancer was in the history of 33.3% (9/27), and 59.3% (16/27) had a family history of breast cancer.

The currently used clinical criteria for HBOC evaluation was not fulfilled for many of the PV carriers, as only 17/32 (53.1%) of the families met NCCN criteria for HBOC genetic assessment (Table 4). Of the individuals without HBOC-related cancer themselves, 39.1% (9/23) had family history that fulfilled the NCCN testing criteria. Only 28.1% (9/32) of participants’ families fulfilled the national criteria for HBOC testing at the time of the study.

The individuals, for whom family history was obtained, had on average 3.7 first-degree relatives who were alive and older than 18 years of age, thus eligible for genetic counseling and cascade screening (Table 4). However, on average only 2.3 first- or second-degree family members per index case contacted the Clinical Genetics Unit for cascade testing during the approximately 1.5 years follow-up time.

To explore the clinical implications of RoR to biobank donors, we studied the 19 newly identified female PV carriers in more detail. The average age at contact was 61.8 years (range 41–81 years; Table 4). Seventeen women were referred to intensified breast screening, and one (ID8) was already in follow-up due to a recent DCIS diagnosis. The intensified breast-imaging screening detected one recurrence of a previously diagnosed breast cancer. RoR from biobank samples resulted in risk-reducing interventions; 35.3% (6/17) of the women chose RRM, three of whom were also referred for RRSO. Participant ID21 had a malignant finding in mastectomy. Three women had undergone salpingo-oophorectomy for other reasons.

Discussion

This pilot study conducted to investigate the feasibility of opportunistic screening of HBOC-related genetic variants from hospital biobank samples provides several important results. Some can be generalized, whereas others depend on the context in which biobanks operate. In Finland, legislation allows recontacting of biobank donors to report results significant for their health, and hospital-integrated biobanks can collect large numbers of samples during clinical routines. The results show that (i) genetic screening using genotyping information is feasible and impactful, (ii) PV findings can be informed to sample donors and to clinical care after appropriate validation, and (iii) attitudes of biobank sample donors toward this approach are highly positive.

Confirmed PVs in BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 were detected in 0.6% of screened samples. All genotype-based PALB2 c.1592del variant calls were true-positive findings, demonstrating that prevalent PVs are detectable by genotyping arrays with high accuracy. False-positive calls for rare variants are typical in array-based genotyping data where the likelihood of true-positives declines with decreasing variant frequency (22, 24). The reliability in our study was highly dependent on the variant, with an average PPV of 30% for BRCA1/2 variants. Although the PPV of BRCA1/2 variants was quite low, it was significantly higher than the 4% reported for BRCA1/2 variants in UK Biobank data (22). This likely reflects the accumulation of certain founder variants as a result of the bottleneck effect of the Finnish population leading to higher variant frequencies (9). However, because our study screened only a selection of PVs, PPVs are not directly comparable between studies. By using the largest possible genotype-calling dataset and combining VCF data with cluster plot analysis of intensity data, the reliability of rare variant calling was increased. As validation of possible variant calls is costly, reducing the false-positive rate is essential for cost-effectiveness of the approach.

One of the key aspects of this study was evaluation of the medical impact of opportunistic genetic screening of biobank samples. Only 26% (19/73) of individuals identified through this screening had been previously diagnosed for their PV in healthcare, demonstrating that current clinical guidelines had been unable to identify a significant number of HBOC variant carriers and their families. The higher rate of previous genetic diagnoses in BRCA1/2 carriers (59%) compared with PALB2 carriers (12%) in this study probably results from the more recent addition of PALB2 to diagnostic genetic tests. These findings are concordant with previous ones in which 18% to 50% of BRCA1/2 variant carriers had been previously identified in healthcare (2, 21, 25, 26). Only half of our study participants would have met the NCCN eligibility criteria for HBOC testing. Similarly, NCCN testing criteria were fulfilled for 33% to 59% of identified PV carriers in other studies (2, 4, 5, 7). Screening of biobank samples can identify individuals who do not meet current testing criteria but who may nevertheless benefit from intensified medical management. Among the recalled and consenting PV carriers, approximately one quarter were younger than the national breast cancer screening program starting age of 50 years, and notably, one third of the newly identified female PV carriers were under this age threshold. Half of the breast cancer cases in female PV carriers in our cohort and 75% of the cases in an Estonian cohort (7) had occurred before the age of 50 years. Thus, opportunistic genetic screening of biobank samples enabled identification of female PV carriers who could benefit from intensified screening or risk-reducing surgeries that might otherwise have remained unrecognized by standard clinical approaches. In addition to PVs and familial history, use of breast cancer PRS has also been suggested to be included in breast cancer risk stratification (27), which might lead to more personalized risk estimation and screening suggestions. There are still uncertainties in penetrance estimates even for these well-known genes. UK Biobank–based analyses have highlighted that penetrance estimates for BRCA1 and BRCA2 are dependent on family history of relevant cancers (28) and PRS (29). Thus, there is a chance for over/underestimation of the cancer risk if the context and PRS are not considered. In our cohort, 9 of 15 (60%) of PV carriers who did not meet the NCCN testing criteria reported no personal or family history of breast cancer. Receiving genetic risk information can also cause anxiety and lead to potential risk-reducing surgeries with possible complications; this needs to be balanced with the benefits of returning genetic results (30).

In this pilot, 71% of the biobank donors with prior biobank consent to receive clinically relevant findings gave a study-specific consent to receive genetic HBOC risk results. This is consistent with previous studies in which 55% to 80% of contacted individuals consented through a separate opt-in to receive genetic cancer risk results (4, 5, 7, 31, 32). Individuals with a history of cancer, especially HBOC-related malignancies, understandably have the greatest incentive to obtain genetic information. However, because the response rate in our study required three contact attempts, nonresponse to initial contact should not be automatically interpreted as refusal to receive results, as concluded also by others (33). To reduce the passive opt-out rate, additional contact modalities beyond mailed letters—such as direct telephone calls or digital communication tools—could be considered.

Biobank donors expect to be notified if clinically relevant findings are detected in their sample. HBB donors reacted positively to RoR concerning their cancer risk and there was a high level of satisfaction with participating in the study. These results confirm previous findings that HBOC genetic testing is well accepted (21). No evidence for significant enduring psychological harm resulting from RoR has been demonstrated (6). Our study participants reported low levels of anxiety, which is in line with the emotional responses of Estonian biobank participants receiving HBOC risk information (5, 7). Biobank donors also feel that biobank samples should be more widely used for genetic risk estimation. Based on these findings, actionable cancer risk results should be offered to biobank donors when available.

Despite the small sample size in this pilot, breast cancer was diagnosed in one PV carrier following result disclosure. Additionally, several women were referred for enhanced breast screening or risk-reducing surgeries, and many family members participated in cascade screening. No information is available on how many of the participants informed their relatives about the findings, but on average 2.3 family members per index case requested cascade testing. This rate of cascade testing is lower than the 3.3 reported by Rowley and colleagues (21), suggesting that information on the cancer-risk variant was ineffectively communicated to family members. A higher proportion of female PV carriers (35%) chose to undergo RRM, compared with only 0% to 17% in other studies (5, 7, 21, 25). The differences in RRM uptake among cohorts are probably dependent on various factors such as age distribution, clinical guidelines, counseling, and reimbursement policies (5).

This study has some limitations. As array-based genotyping data was used, not all PVs of BRCA1/2 and PALB2 could be identified. To reduce uncertainties related to variant interpretation, we also restricted the analysis to variants previously detected and classified as pathogenic in the Finnish population. This approach, however, raises important considerations regarding potential inequities: screening only a limited set of population-specific variants may fail to identify PVs that are more prevalent and relevant in minority or immigrant groups. Consequently, opportunistic screening of selected PVs from biobank-derived genotyping data should not be viewed as a substitute for comprehensive genetic testing when clinically warranted. Due to cost constraints, it was not possible to validate a sample of adequate size to generate a reliable estimate of the negative predictive value, given the rarity of the screened variants. In this regional study, only a portion of the HBB samples were analyzed, so the findings may not apply to the whole biobank cohort or at the national level. We believe, however, that the positive feedback of participants would reflect the general preference of biobank donors. A larger national study should confirm the general acceptance of RoR. Extending screening analysis to the whole HBB cohort and on national level is now ongoing, as is the cost-effectiveness assessment of the clinical utility.

In summary, opportunistic genetic screening of biobank samples enables identification of previously unrecognized individuals at increased genetic risk of cancer. This can significantly impact both the individual biobank donor and the healthcare system as enhanced risk management and prophylactic interventions can be offered. However, only variants with clear clinical relevance should be returned, and, when interpreting results, additional factors such as family history should be considered in the context of the individual participant. The high acceptance of biobank donors to receive genetic findings further supports the return of biobank results. This pilot provides a basis for further research needed before this approach can be widely implemented.
