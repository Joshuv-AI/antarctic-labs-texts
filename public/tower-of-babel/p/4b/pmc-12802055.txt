Impact of COVID-19 on Invasive Pneumococcal Disease: A Comparative Analysis of Clinical Features and Antibiotic Resistance across Pandemic Periods
Authors: Enes Kasım Tekgül, Güzin Cinel, Saliha Kanık-Yüksek, Tuğba Erat, Seval Özen, Aslınur Özkaya Parlakay, Aysun Yahşi, Bedia Dinç, Belgin Gülhan, Gülsüm İclal Bayhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic and associated infection control measures (lockdowns, masking) dramatically altered infectious disease epidemiology, including reduced invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) incidence. This study aimed to assess the pandemic effects on IPD clinical features, outcomes, and antibiotic resistance in children.Materials and MethodsThis study included pediatric patients (1 month to 18 years) diagnosed with IPD, comparing two distinct periods: the pre-pandemic era (March 2014–February 2020) and pandemic/post-pandemic era (March 2020–March 2024). Clinical characteristics and antibiotic resistance patterns of IPD patients were compared between the pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic periods.ResultsAmong 61 cases (27 pre-pandemic, 34 pandemic/post-pandemic), age distribution remained similar (median 46 vs. 49 months, P=0.799). The distribution of IPD manifestations and vaccine failure rates (70.4% vs. 76.5%) were consistent between periods. Comparative analysis revealed significantly reduced susceptibility rates during the pandemic/post-pandemic period versus pre-pandemic for penicillin, ceftriaxone, and fluoroquinolones.ConclusionIPD remains a significant pediatric health concern. IPD continues to disproportionately affect children under five years of age, with no significant pandemic-related changes in age distribution, disease manifestations, or clinical outcomes. The concerning rise in resistance to first-line antibiotics during the pandemic underscores the urgent need for robust antimicrobial stewardship programs.

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, responsible for a spectrum of diseases ranging from non-invasive respiratory infections to life-threatening invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD). The inclusion of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) in routine immunization programs has led to a significant reduction in IPD worldwide. As vaccination coverage has expanded, a marked decline in vaccine-serotype-associated diseases has been observed. However, there has also been a reported increase in the relative prevalence of non-vaccine serotypes, highlighting the phenomenon of serotype replacement [1]. In Türkiye, a multicenter study conducting 2008–2014 demonstrated the proportion of non-vaccine serotypes increased from 27.2% in 2008–2010 to 37.6% in 2011–2014, indicating a growing circulation of non-vaccine strains [2]. A subsequent study conducted between 2015–2018 revealed that PCV13 serotypes accounting for 56.2% of isolates [3]. These findings demonstrate that while vaccine-serotype infections persist, non-vaccine serotypes are also emerging as a significant concern.

In Türkiye, PCVs were first introduced into the national immunization program in 2008 with the 7-valent vaccine (PCV7). PCV7 was replaced by the 13-valent vaccine (PCV13) in 2011, which has since been routinely administered as part of the national schedule. Initially given in a 3+1 schedule at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months of age, the regimen was revised in April 2019, with infants born after January 1, 2019, receiving PCV13 in a 2+1 schedule at 2, 4, and 12 months, and this schedule has continued since then [4].

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic introduced unprecedented changes in infectious disease transmission patterns. Strict non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) including lockdowns, mask mandates, and social distancing were associated with a remarkable global reduction in IPD cases. In the UK, IPD incidence dropped sharply during 2020–21. However, following the lifting of restrictions on July 19, 2021, cases resurged in 2021–22. Pediatric cases rebounded rapidly to pre-pandemic levels, whereas adult cases increased more gradually, remaining below pre-pandemic rates in 2022–23. Although both PCV13 and non-PCV13 serotypes contributed to rising IPD trends in young children and adults during 2022–2023, overall incidence remained 14% lower than in 2019–2020 [5]. A study conducted in Peru reported a notable decrease in pneumococcal carriage during the COVID-19 pandemic compared to the period before the pandemic, with the most pronounced decline seen in children under the age of five [6]. These observations suggest that pandemic measures temporarily suppressed pneumococcal transmission, but the post-restriction resurgence underscores the need for sustained surveillance. The post-pandemic era presents critical questions regarding the long-term dynamics of pneumococcal serotype distribution and disease burden.

This fluctuating epidemiological landscape raises critical questions about how the pandemic may have altered the clinical presentation and outcomes of IPD. Reduced pneumococcal exposure during lockdowns may have diminished population immunity, potentially predisposing to more severe clinical presentations during the resurgence period. Simultaneously, dramatic changes in healthcare utilization patterns and increased empirical antibiotic use for COVID-19-related respiratory infections may have driven significant shifts in antimicrobial resistance profiles among pneumococcal strains.

In light of these pandemic-related epidemiological shifts, this study aimed to assess the impact of the pandemic on the clinical course of IPD by comparing the clinical characteristics, outcomes, and antibiotic resistance profiles of infections during the pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic periods.

Materials and Methods

1. Study setting and patient population

This retrospective study was conducted at Ankara Children's Health and Diseases Hematology Oncology Training and Research Hospital, with continued patient enrollment at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Children's Hospital following the hospital's relocation in March 2019. This study included pediatric patients (1 month to 18 years) diagnosed with IPD, comparing two distinct periods: the pre-pandemic era (March 2014–February 2020) and pandemic/post-pandemic era (March 2020–March 2024).

IPD cases were confirmed either by isolation of S. pneumoniae from normally sterile sites (such as blood, cerebrospinal fluid, or pleural fluid) or by detection of pneumococcal DNA through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in cerebrospinal fluid or blood samples. All microbiological analyses were performed according to standardized laboratory protocols. Only cases of pneumonia with concomitant bacteremia were classified as IPD. Cases with parapneumonic effusion that were confirmed by isolation of S. pneumoniae from pleural fluid were categorized as empyema. Mastoiditis cases were considered IPD and included in the study only if S. pneumoniae was cultured from a surgical specimen obtained from the mastoid bone (e.g., during mastoidectomy). In contrast, cases where pneumococcus was detected solely in ear canal swabs or in middle ear fluid obtained via tympanocentesis were not classified as IPD and were therefore excluded. Similarly, patients with perforated acute otitis media who grew pneumococcus from middle ear effusion with purulent discharge, as well as parotitis cases in which pneumococcus was isolated from exudate draining through Stensen’s duct, were not regarded as IPD and were not included in the study.

The demographic characteristics, comorbid conditions, pneumococcal vaccination status, clinical presentation types, and treatment outcomes were evaluated. Vaccine failure was defined as the occurrence of invasive pneumococcal disease following complete vaccination according to national immunization schedules [7]. Cases of vaccine failure were evaluated for the presence and frequency of underlying comorbidities

All pneumococcal isolates cultured during both study periods underwent antibiotic susceptibility testing. Antibiotic resistance patterns were compared between the pre-pandemic, pandemic/post-pandemic periods using standardized antimicrobial susceptibility testing methods.

Patient outcomes were categorized into two groups: those who developed sequelae or died (adverse outcomes), and those who achieved full recovery. Cases with adverse outcomes underwent comparative analysis to identify risk factors associated with poor prognosis.

2. Ethics statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee of Ankara City Hospital (Number: E2-22-2765, date: 09 November 2022). As this was a retrospective study, obtaining informed consent from patients or their parents was not possible, and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

3. Microbiological methods

The identification of bacteremia/sepsis-causing pathogens and associated antibiotic resistance genes was performed using the Bio-Speedy Sepsis qPCR MX-30S Panel (Bioeksen, İstanbul, Türkiye) on the, CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Additionally, the Meningitis/encephalitis RT-qPCR Kit (Bio-Speedy, Bioeksen, İstanbul, Türkiye) was used for the meningitis panel analysis on the CFX96 Touch platform (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Mannheim, Germany).

At Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Gram staining of clinical specimens was performed using an automated Gram staining device (Previ Color Gram, bioMérieux, Marcy l'Étoile, France). Prepared slides were examined under a 100X objective for the presence of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNLs) and Gram-positive diplococci. Suspected S. pneumoniae samples were cultured on 5% sheep blood agar and incubated in the WASPLab® (Walk Away Specimen Processor, Copan, Italy) under conditions appropriate for the specimen type.

Blood samples collected in BACT/ALERT blood culture bottles (bioMérieux, France) were incubated in the BACT/ALERT 3D system (bioMérieux, France) for up to 120 hours. Bottles signaling positive were subjected to Gram staining, and subcultures were incubated for 24–48 hours. Suspected S. pneumoniae colonies were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (VITEK® MS, BioMérieux, France). Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the VITEK 2 automated system (bioMérieux, France), with results interpreted according to EUCAST guidelines. At Ankara Children's Health and Diseases Hematology Oncology Training and Research Hospital, gram staining followed the same protocol, assessing samples for PMNLs and Gram-positive diplococci. Suspected pneumococcal isolates were cultured on 5% sheep blood agar and incubated for 24–48 hours. Blood cultures were processed similarly in the BACT/ALERT 3D system (bioMérieux, Marcy l'Étoile, France), with positive bottles undergoing Gram staining and subculture. Identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing were conducted using the VITEK 2 system (bioMérieux, France), with EUCAST criteria applied for interpretation. According to EUCAST criteria, for meningitis, penicillin minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) >0.06 mg/L and ceftriaxone MIC >0.5 mg/L are considered resistant, whereas for non-meningitis infections, penicillin MIC values between >0.06 and 1 mg/L and ceftriaxone MIC values between >0.5 and 2 mg/L indicate intermediate susceptibility [8].

4. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) with 95% confidence intervals. Descriptive statistics are presented as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables and frequencies/percentages for categorical data. Normality was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests (for sample sizes >50) complemented by graphical evaluation. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed for comparisons between two non-normally distributed groups. Categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. A two-tailed P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 61 IPD cases were identified, comprising 27 pre-pandemic and 34 pandemic/post-pandemic cases. S. pneumoniae was isolated by culture in 57 patients (93.4%). Among culture-positive cases, one patient additionally showed positivity on meningitis PCR panel. Three culture-negative cases were diagnosed solely by PCR (1 meningitis panel, 2 sepsis panels).

Underlying comorbidities were present in 26 patients (42.6%), with detailed distributions presented in Table 1. Comparative analysis revealed no significant differences between pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic groups regarding age, sex, comorbidity prevalence, and pneumococcal vaccination status. The distribution of IPD clinical presentations and outcomes showed no significant variation between pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic periods (Table 2).

Four patients (6.6%) developed long-term sequelae following IPD, three of whom were previously healthy and one with underlying combined immunodeficiency. The first patient exhibited severe motor and cognitive impairment secondary to cerebral atrophy confirmed on magnetic resonance imaging. The second patient developed global cerebral dysfunction requiring chronic antiepileptic therapy and remained dependent on nasogastric tube feeding. The third patient, an infant with meningitis and septic shock, was discharged with tracheostomy-dependent respiratory failure and required home mechanical ventilation. The fourth patient underwent prosthetic valve replacement due to pneumococcal endocarditis involving native cardiac valves.

Five patients (8.2%) died during hospitalization. Only one deceased patient had been previously healthy. Mortality cases included patients with cerebral palsy, multiple cardiac anomalies, severe combined immunodeficiency, and a post-hematopoietic stem cell transplant AML patient (6 months post-transplant). Discussion

This study revealed that the consistent comorbidity rates across both periods demonstrate that underlying health conditions remain a major risk factor for IPD, regardless of pandemic-related changes in behavior or healthcare access. Of particular concern, vaccine failure cases were equally common in otherwise healthy children and those with comorbidities, suggesting current vaccines may not adequately protect against all IPD forms. While the types and severity of IPD cases stayed remarkably stable - with similar proportions of meningitis, bacteremia, and other presentations - the concurrent decline in antibiotic susceptibility profiles raises important concerns about the growing threat of treatment-resistant pneumococcal infections.

A systematic review of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine failures identified underlying comorbidities in 33 of 77 cases (42.9%) [9]. Similarly, a study of 155 breakthrough IPD cases found 31% had comorbid conditions. While 72% of breakthrough cases had received ≥1 vaccine dose per schedule, 32% received only a single dose by age ≤6 months. Multivariable analysis revealed children with comorbidities had 2.8-fold higher odds of breakthrough infection. Notably, incomplete vaccination alone showed no significant association with breakthrough IPD [7]. The presented study’s findings align with this evidence, demonstrating comparable comorbidity rates. The consistent association between comorbidities and breakthrough infections across studies suggests that comorbid children remain vulnerable to IPD despite vaccination and strict adherence to vaccination protocols is critical for these patients. While comorbidities are established risk factors for IPD, studies have reported that most vaccine breakthrough cases involving vaccine-targeted serotypes occur in children without underlying conditions [7]. This study's findings align with this observation - in both pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic cohorts, over half of IPD cases developed in previously healthy children. This consistent pattern across periods suggests that breakthrough infections may not necessarily indicate an underlying immune deficiency or serious health condition, even in fully vaccinated children.

The annual incidence rate of IPD demonstrated a clear inverse relationship with age, reported as 36.5, 25.3, 8.5, and 2.4 cases per 100,000 children in the 0–11 month, 12–23 month, 24–59 month, and 5–18 year age groups, respectively [10]. A study examining S. pneumoniae detection rates in children before and after the COVID-19 pandemic found that the highest positivity rates occurred in children under 2 years old, representing more than 50% of all positive cases. Additionally, children under 5 years accounted for over 80% of positive samples [11]. The current study shows remarkably consistent age distributions both pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic period: approximately one-third of cases occur in children under 2 years of age, while half of all cases are found in children under 5 years. Together, these findings reveal that the characteristic concentration of pneumococcal infections in children under 5 years, particularly under 2 years, represents an intrinsic epidemiological feature that remained unaffected by pandemic-related disruptions.

A large-scale United States study examining 7,605 pediatric cases across 219 hospitals (January 2011–February 2020) reported resistance rates of 40% to macrolides and penicillin, 11% to tetracyclines, 6.8% to extended-spectrum cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or cefepime), and 0.4% to fluoroquinolones. The study highlighted a progressive annual increase in resistance, with particularly alarming trends in macrolide resistance and multidrug resistance (MDR) [12]. Similarly, Turkish surveillance data from adults (1996–2011 and 2014–2018) demonstrated the highest resistance to tetracycline (63.5%), followed by trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (48.0%), penicillin (30.4%), erythromycin (21.7%), clindamycin (15.8%), fluoroquinolones (5.9%), cefotaxime (2.2%), and rifampicin (1.8%). No vancomycin resistance was observed, but rising trends were noted for penicillin, cefotaxime, erythromycin, and clindamycin [13]. A retrospective analysis of the United Arab Emirates’s national antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance program (2010–2021) further documented escalating resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and quinolones, alongside a pronounced rise in MDR rates among both invasive and non-invasive isolates [14]. The presented study revealed a concerning rise in pneumococcal antibiotic resistance rates both before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite low bacterial co-infection rates in COVID-19 patients (<5%), excessive broad-spectrum antibiotic use was widespread. Clinical overlap between COVID-19 and bacterial pneumonia likely drove unnecessary prescribing [15]. The pandemic may have exacerbated the situation, further accelerating the already worsening trend of pneumococcal antibiotic resistance. However, not all countries reported similar patterns. In Japan, a nationwide AMR study covering hundreds of hospitals found no significant changes in overall antibiotic susceptibility trends for S. pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae during the COVID-19 pandemic, despite reduced incidence rates and some level shifts in specific antibiotics. Specifically, S. pneumoniae showed no significant trend changes in susceptibility throughout the pandemic period [16]. These contrasting findings highlight the potential influence of local antibiotic stewardship practices, pandemic response measures, and baseline resistance levels on the trajectory of antimicrobial resistance.

Although COVID-19 is a viral illness, the empirical use of antibacterial agents is widespread [1718]. A retrospective cohort study from Türkiye evaluating antibiotic use in hospitalized patients with mild-to-severe COVID-19 found that 80% of patients were prescribed antibiotics [18]. In a multicenter study involving 1500 adult patients diagnosed with COVID-19 across 13 hospitals in Türkiye, it was reported that 74.5% of patients received antibiotics, despite 71.2% having neither clinical nor microbiological justification for antibiotic therapy [17]. Another study conducted in Türkiye compared the consumption of intravenous antibacterial drugs one year before and during the COVID-19 pandemic among hospitalized patients. The results revealed a substantial increase in the use of several intravenous antibiotics during the pandemic compared to the previous year: moxifloxacin by 350%, ciprofloxacin by 111%, vancomycin by 90%, meropenem by 83%, and piperacillin-tazobactam by 68% [19]. These findings highlight the prevalent unnecessary use of antibiotics in COVID-19 cases in Türkiye and the increased overall antibiotic consumption during the pandemic. The observed increase in pneumococcal resistance to first-line antibiotics during pandemic/post-pandemic period, as presented in this study, is very likely a reflection of this heightened antibiotic usage.

Certain pneumococcal serotypes are associated with antibiotic resistance. Among the serotypes included in PCV13, 19A and 19F demonstrated the highest prevalence of penicillin non-susceptibility, whereas serotypes 14 and 6B exhibited the highest prevalence of macrolide non-susceptibility. Among non-PCV13 serotypes, 17F and 35B showed the highest rates of penicillin non-susceptibility, while 9A, 15A, 24F, and 33F were frequently associated with macrolide non-susceptibility [20]. In the study presented here, the increased antibiotic resistance observed during pandemic/post-pandemic period may also be attributed to shifts in pneumococcal seroepidemiology influenced by the pandemic. However, a significant limitation of this study is the inability to perform serotyping, which precludes definitive conclusions regarding these associations.

The determination of antimicrobial susceptibility breakpoints for S. pneumoniae presents unique challenges compared to many other bacterial pathogens. While most microorganisms can be adequately assessed using a single breakpoint criterion, S. pneumoniae requires distinct interpretive criteria for meningitis versus non-meningitis infections [821]. For meningitis, penicillin MIC >0.06 mg/L and ceftriaxone MIC >0.5 mg/L are considered resistant, whereas for non-meningitis infections, penicillin MIC values between >0.06 and 2 mg/L and ceftriaxone MIC values between >0.5 and 2 mg/L indicate intermediate susceptibility [8]. These critical breakpoint differences become particularly significant given recent findings that automated systems like Vitek2 may underestimate or overestimate MIC values for S. pneumoniae. Such underestimations could lead to false susceptibility interpretations, especially concerning for meningitis cases where stricter breakpoints apply. These methodological limitations emphasize the need for cautious interpretation of susceptibility results and, when possible, confirmation with reference broth microdilution methods (BMD) for isolates with borderline MIC values [22]. While BMD remains the gold standard for resolving borderline MIC values, it's important to note that the current study did not employ BMD methodology. Another important consideration in assessing antimicrobial resistance is the non-absolute nature of susceptibility breakpoints. Clinical evidence shows that high-dose amoxicillin/clavulanate (2,000/125 mg) remains effective against penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae strains with MIC values of 4-16 mg/L in community-acquired pneumonia. This finding indicates that pharmacodynamic optimization through high-dose administration and prolonged time above MIC can overcome conventional breakpoint limitations in clinical practice [23]. This evidence particularly challenges traditional susceptibility interpretations for non-meningeal infections and highlights the potential discrepancy between automated susceptibility testing results and clinical outcomes. Clinicians must consider the infection site, pharmacokinetic properties, and dosing regimen.

Although this study did not reveal statistically significant differences in age distribution, clinical presentations, or outcomes between the pre-pandemic and pandemic/post-pandemic periods, the absolute numbers of deaths and long-term sequelae were higher in the latter group, even though this difference did not reach statistical significance. This observed trend could be attributed to waning host immunity from reduced pathogen exposure during lockdowns or shifts in circulating pneumococcal serotypes due to altered transmission dynamics. The fact that this increase was observed in a country like Türkiye, which has a widespread healthcare network encompassing public hospitals, family health centers, and private institutions in both urban and rural areas, high population coverage under its universal health insurance system enabling low-cost access to care, and notably high pneumococcal vaccination rates, is particularly remarkable. It raises the possibility that in countries with lower vaccine coverage and more limited or expensive access to healthcare, the negative impact of the pandemic on invasive pneumococcal disease outcomes could be even more pronounced. Consequently, from a public health standpoint, vigilant monitoring of these trends is crucial, as they may become significant in larger cohorts. Therefore, further studies with extended surveillance are recommended to confirm these findings and elucidate the underlying causes.

This study has several important limitations. First, it is likely that some cases of IPD were missed during the study period, particularly among patients who had already received antibiotics prior to culture sampling, as this can hinder bacterial growth and detection. A second and significant limitation is the absence of pneumococcal serotype data. This gap critically hinders our analysis in two key areas: it prevents the assessment of potential shifts in pneumococcal serotype distribution following the pandemic, and it limits our ability to evaluate serotype-specific vaccine effectiveness. Furthermore, the lack of serotype information impairs our understanding of local antimicrobial resistance patterns, as resistance profiles are often intrinsically linked to specific serotypes. Notably, a retrospective study conducted in a tertiary pediatric center among children with empyema reported no significant changes in pneumococcal serotype distribution between the pre- and post-pandemic periods (2017–2023), with serotype 3 remaining the most common, highlighting the value of such data in understanding IPD trends [24]. However, as that study was limited to children with empyema, broader conclusions about pandemic-related changes in serotype distribution across all forms of IPD remain uncertain. Finally, as a single-center study, our findings may not be fully generalizable to other settings. Future multicenter studies incorporating advanced molecular diagnostics (e.g., PCR-based serotyping) would help address these limitations and provide more comprehensive insights into the evolving epidemiology of IPD.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that IPD remains a significant pediatric health concern. IPD continues to disproportionately affect children under five years of age, with no significant pandemic-related changes in age distribution, disease manifestations, or clinical outcomes. The concerning rise in resistance to first-line antibiotics during the pandemic underscores the urgent need for robust antimicrobial stewardship programs.
