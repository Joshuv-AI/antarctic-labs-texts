Left-hand-dominant subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy: a case series
Authors: Haodan Shi, Yixiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft-handed surgeons face systemic ergonomic challenges in conventional right-oriented surgical environments. This study proposes a standardized left-dominant protocol for subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy, integrating dual perspectives as a left-handed surgeon and surgical educator.MethodsThis study describes a case series of 82 consecutive thymic mass resections, performed using the left-dominant subxiphoid thoracoscopic approach at Dalian Medical University.ResultsWe analyzed 82 patients, comprising 35 males (42.7%) and 47 females (57.3%). Introduction

Approximately 10.6% of the global population is left-handed [1] with lower prevalence reported in China historically [2]. Left-handed surgeons face systemic ergonomic challenges in conventional right-oriented surgical environments encompassing instrument design, training paradigms, and procedural ergonomics [3, 4]. For instance, standardized liver palpation techniques requiring right-handed maneuvers contradict natural left-handed motor patterns, potentially compromising examination quality and risking academic penalties for noncompliance. Targeted medical education and training programs are essential. These programs include specialized ergonomic accommodations and simulation modules designed for left-handed individuals to tackle the unique psychomotor challenges these trainees face during skill acquisition and clinical practice [5, 6].

Predominantly right-handed–configured clinical curricula and instrumentation compel left-handed trainees to acquire procedural skills through non-dominant hand adaptation [3, 7, 8]. As a left-handed thoracic surgeon, I developed an optimized subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy protocol to address this educational gap. The subxiphoid thoracoscopic approach has gained international acceptance [9–11], offering advantages over unilateral techniques including bilateral visualization and rapid recovery [9, 12]. The restricted mediastinal space fundamentally constrains subxiphoid surgical maneuvers, where optimizing the anterior pericardial-sternal distance becomes critical for procedural enhancement. The conventional operative technique requiring crossed manipulation of instruments both obstructs the field of vision and necessitates greater longitudinal operative space, consequently compounding the difficulty of dissecting the superior pole of the thymus.

We present a modified left-hand-dominant subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy, summarizing our experience to modularize the surgical workflow and achieving true ambidextrous operation. This approach offers detailed technical guidance for left-handed early-career surgeons and provides a practical solution for right-handed surgeons to navigate challenges related to operative angles and instrument collisions.

Materials and methods

Patients were included based on the surgeon’s assessment that R0 resection could be achieved via a subxiphoid minimally invasive approach. Exclusion criteria included patients with a history of sternotomy or those with severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction deemed unfit for the surgical procedure. We conducted a retrospective case series of 82 patients undergoing left-dominant subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy for thymic masses at The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. This study was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee. Clinicopathological data were systematically analyzed to evaluate the safety and feasibility of this approach.

Incision design

Patients underwent general anesthesia with single-lumen endotracheal tube intubation. With the patient placed supine in a head-elevated position, the assistant takes position on the right side of the patient, and the primary surgeon stands between the patient’s legs. A 3 cm skin incision along the right xiphoid margin (observation port), preserving linea alba and xiphoid integrity (Fig. 1A, B). Thoracic access was established above the sternal diaphragmatic attachments to avoid entry into the abdomen. Under palpation guidance, 5-mm trocars were inserted at bilateral midclavicular line-costal margin junctions (future drainage sites). (Fig. 1C–F) The observation port was secured with figure-of-eight sutures to ensure air-tight thoracic cavity closure. For left brachiocephalic vein (LBCV) invasions, we reconfigured the trocar from 5-mm to 12-mm to allow for the use of endoscopic staplers.

Establishment of retrosternal operative space

After securing the trocars and adjusting CO₂ insufflation to 15 mmHg to further expand the retrosternal space, we access the pleural cavity under thoracoscopic guidance using concurrent bimanual manipulation to prevent hemorrhage and accidental injury. Given the relatively high placement of our incisions, the distance required for accessing both pleural cavities is notably short. Pressure was reduced to 6- 8mmHg after mediastinal pleura incision to minimize cardiopulmonary interference. Dissection was confined to the retrosternal space terminating at manubrium sterni superior border. Kept close contact with the posterior sternum during dissection to ensure a more direct entry into the thorax. Prolonged unsuccessful dissection suggests prepericardial fat space misentry or pleural adhesions, requiring immediate correction. Preserve bilateral internal thoracic vasculature throughout the procedure to prevent iatrogenic injury.

Localization of left brachiocephalic vein -superior vena cava confluence

The 30° angled thoracoscope provides left -to- right visualization to circumvent tumor obstruction. Initiated 5 mm anterior to the right phrenic nerve complex, the dissection progresses along the superior vena cava (SVC) localizes the LBCV. The phrenic nerve complex—comprising phrenic nerve, vasculature, and overlying pleura—is preserved as an integrated anatomical unit through meticulous blunt dissection of surrounding mediastinal fat. (Fig. 2A, B) The natural suspension of the phrenic nerve by the mediastinal pleura is utilized as a protective anatomical barrier during dissection. (Fig. 2C) The ultrasonic scalpel in the left hand executes blunt dissection of thymic and fatty tissues while the right-hand graspers maintain optimal traction, enabling precise identification of the LBCV-SVC confluence. In obese individuals, posterior thymic capsule-oriented blunt dissection demonstrates enhanced safety. Direct ultrasonic transection is deferred until definitive vascular visualization ensures procedural safety.

Thymectomy via non-crossing instrument technique

The non-crossing instrument technique utilizes synchronized bimanual manipulation, where the left hand performs ultrasonic scalpel dissection while the right hand applies dynamic traction, resulting in elevation of the thymus from the LBCV plane. The LBCV-axis-oriented bidirectional dissection is conducted along the cephalocaudal dimension, enhanced by LBCV clamping process that enable prompt hemorrhage containment in bleeding scenarios. The arrangement yields greater longitudinal spatial efficiency. Since the ultrasonic scalpel, atraumatic grasper, and thoracoscope are aligned in the same plane without crossover, instrumental interference and the “chopstick effect” are avoided. (Fig. 3)

Superior thymic pole dissection

The upper pole of the thymus exhibits morphological variability, presenting as either discrete structures or adherent triangular formations. Division of the thyrothymic ligament ensures en bloc resection integrity (Fig. 2D). Identifiable inferior parathyroid glands adjacent to the pole require meticulous preservation. A coordinated bimanual technique employs right-handed endoscopic graspers for inferolateral traction while the left-hand ultrasonic scalpel executes tangential dissection along the superior plane, achieving collision-free instrument navigation throughout the procedure.

Thymic venous dissection

The anatomical dissection between the posterior thymic capsule and the LBCV constitutes the cornerstone of complete thymic resection. Guided by the posterior thymic capsule, the ultrasonic scalpel executes precise dissection to preserve the LBCV integrity while simultaneously exposing 1–2 thymic venous trunks emerging from the mid-posterior region. These vessels are transected at their capsular exit points, preserving 2–3 mm stumps to prevent vessel retraction.

Results

This study included 82 patients (35 males, 47 females; mean age 53.3 ± 12.8 years) with maximum tumor diameter 4.0 ± 2.2 cm. Postoperative pathology confirmed: thymoma (58.5%, 48/82), thymic carcinoma (8.5%, 7/82), thymic cysts (23.2%, 19/82), hyperplasia (3.7%, 3/82), seminoma (1.2%,1/82), mediastinal teratoma (2.4%,2/82), nodular goiter (1.2%,1/82),and granulomatous inflammation(1.2%,1/82)(Table 1). Five patients (6.1%, 5/82) had myasthenia gravis (3 thymoma-associated, 2 hyperplastic). Thymoma classification by WHO/Masaoka/TNM criteria is detailed in Table 2. Representative specimens are shown in Fig. 4. An uncomplicated recovery was observed in 98.8% of patients, equating to 81 out of 82 participants. One myasthenia gravis patient developed postoperative day 3 respiratory failure requiring transient ICU care. Notably, two cases requiring LBCV resection maintained normal cephalocranial and upper extremity venous return postoperatively without edema or complications. Transient intraoperative hypotension occurred, which resolved promptly after reducing the CO₂ insufflation pressure. No bleeding or arrhythmia was observed. The cohort demonstrated zero perioperative mortality or major complications.

Conclusions

Our results suggest that This left-dominant approach is feasible, safe, and reproducible.

The methodology introduces a standardized procedural framework for ambidextrous skill development, offering distinct ergonomic advantages for left-handed surgeons while maintaining compatibility with conventional right-handed techniques.

Discussion

Surgical management remains the cornerstone of thymic tumor treatment supplemented by radiotherapy, chemotherapy, glucocorticoids, immunotherapy, and targeted therapies. While traditional approaches such as median sternotomy and lateral thoracotomy provide excellent surgical exposure, their significant trauma and postoperative morbidity have led to the widespread adoption of minimally invasive thoracoscopic techniques. The subxiphoid thoracoscopic thymectomy demonstrates unique advantages over unilateral approaches, including enhanced visualization of bilateral phrenic nerves and the LBCV.

Research by Adusumilli et al. indicates that only 3% of left-handed surgeons received guidance related to left-handedness during medical school [13]. Targeted procedural guidance for left-handed medical students is essential. This study details a left-hand dominant subxiphoid three-port thoracoscopic thymectomy technique. The patients in this case series were meticulously selected by the operating surgeon after careful consideration, as minimally invasive thymic tumor surgery is highly dependent on the surgeon’s experience.

The developed " non-crossing instrument technique” strategy enables systematic thymic separation from the LBCV through left -to- right dissection, achieving 100% resection rates.

Expanding the operative space, which is fundamentally limited by the sternum-pericardium distance, is crucial in subxiphoid surgery, with current methods relying mainly on the sternal retraction technique. Our modification of the operative technique, which involves solely a change in the surgical maneuver, increases spatial utilization efficiency within the existing anatomical confines. It accomplishes this without introducing additional procedural steps or causing any patient injury. Holding the harmonic scalpel in the left hand positions the blade tangentially to the thymic tissue, which simplifies cutting, particularly during dissection of the superior pole. The modified incision facilitates access to both thoracic cavities while also avoiding injury to the retrosternal attachments of the diaphragm. This technique consistently supports combined procedures such as thymectomy with partial pericardectomy, pulmonary wedge resection, and LBCV resection, showcasing its adaptability in complex mediastinal dissections.

However, its application remains contraindicated for tumors involving the superior vena cava, right brachiocephalic vein, or bilateral hilar regions. As an initial experiential summary from our team, this technical report currently lacks both systematic comparison with established minimally invasive thymectomy modalities and long-term follow-up data on patient outcomes.
