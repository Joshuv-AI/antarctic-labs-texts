Pharmaceutical effluent degradation using hydrogen peroxide-supported zerovalent iron nanoparticles catalyst
Authors: Favour Abumere Imohiosen, Edwin Andrew Ofudje, Khairia Mohammed Al-Ahmary, Saedah R. Al-Mhyawi, Ibtehaj F. Alshdoukhi, Mazen R. Alrahili, Ahad Amer Alsaiari, Salah Ud Din
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pharmaceutical effluents generated during drugs production and application are often times released into the water systems with little or no treatment, which could pose potential danger to the ecosystem. Advanced oxidation processes for organic pollutants treatment have gained wide consideration due to their effectiveness. In this work, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydrogen peroxide-supported nano zerovalent iron (H2O2@nZVIs) were deployed to study pharmaceutical effluents (PE) degradation via batch experiments, under various reaction time, (H2O2) and (H2O2@nZVIs) concentrations, pH, PE concentration, and temperature. The nZVIs was prepared from the green synthesis of Vernonia amygdalina leaf extract and characterized using different analytical tools such as Fourier Transform-Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR), Gas Chromatography Mass Spectroscopy (GC-MS), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and X-Ray Diffraction Spectroscopy (XRD). The FT-IR results showed the presence of -C = O, -NH, -OH, -C = C and, -C-O functional groups, SEM report showed that the morphology of the nZVIs is round in shape, while GC-MS revealed the presence of several phytochemicals. When the concentration of the effluent was increased from 10 to 30 ml, the percentage decolourization decreased from 74.74 to 51.96% and from 80.36 to 54.38% for H2O2 and H2O2@nZVI respectively, whereas when the contact time was increased from 10 to 60 min, the percentage decolourization rose from 70.39 to 83.49% for H2O2 and from 85.19 to 89.73% when H2O2@nZVI was used. When the effect of pH was assessed, it was observed that on increasing the pH from 2 to 10, the percentage decolourization rose from 74.5 to 80.25% for H2O2, however, with H2O2@nZVI, the percentage decolourization decreased from 81.50 to 68%. Maximum percentage decolourization of 57.10% and 94.56% for H2O2 and H2O2@nZVI was achieved at catalyst volume of 25 ml. For all the parameters tested, the H2O2@nZVIs performed much better indicating that the nZVIs enhanced the decolourization ability of the H2O2. The kinetic results showed that the decolorization of pharmaceutical effluent by both catalysts fitted very well with the second-order model, while thermodynamic properties of enthalpy change were found to be 10.025 and 27.005 kJ/mol/K for H2O2 and H2O2@nZVIs respectively suggesting that the oxidation process is endothermic in nature. This technique employed in using hydrogen peroxide-supported zero valent iron, proved to be highly efficient not only for pharmaceutical effluent degradation but also in the elimination of lead from the effluent.

Introduction

When the levels of organic pollutants surpass the allowable limits in the environment, they become toxic and these toxic molecules can lead to several diseases in people, animals and plants1. These organic pollutants are primarily obtained from industrial products like detergents, plastics, organic solvents, pesticides, dyes and in a variety of settings2. Organic pollutants are widely present in industrial wastewater (effluents), pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and other chemical products. They are harmful to the environment and health of humans due to their toxic nature, and they can cause cancer, birth defects, and other health problems1,3,4. These organic pollutants are substances that can withstand chemical, biological, and photolytic processes without degrading.

Pharmaceutical effluents are wastewater discharges that contain pharmaceutical by-products and their metabolite residues as well as other chemicals used in the preparation of medications and treatments5. These effluents have detrimental consequences on public health and on the environment if they are not properly handled before release into the environment. The availability of these pharmaceutical wastes in the environment modifies the properties and structures of microbial organisms in soil and water environments2. The primary risks posed by these developing pollutants are the eco-toxicological effects on human health that emerge from the appearance and build-up of these contaminants in water sources. Drug remnants and their derivatives have the potential to contaminate aquifers, posing a risk to aquatic life, plants, and even people6. Although there are a variety of ways to treat wastewater, including coagulation, ion exchange, chemical precipitation, membrane processes, solvent extraction, filtering, sedimentation, and adsorption7,8, AOPs are one of the most effective treatment processes often deployed in removing organic pollutants. AOPs use powerful oxidizing agents to degrade the pollutants into simpler substances which are less dangerous to the environment9–13.

The AOPs methods are very effective at breaking down and getting rid of pollutants like organic compounds, dyes, and medicines from wastewater and other types of polluted water. The generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (OH−), that are incredibly reactive and can rapidly react with and degrade a variety of pollutants, is the mechanism behind AOPs2,9. AOPs come in a variety of forms, including those that use ozone, hydrogen peroxide, and photocatalytic processes that make use of ultraviolet (UV) light and photocatalysts like titanium dioxide (TiO2)2,7.

Concerning ozone-based AOPs, ozone reacts with water to produce hydroxyl radicals. If organic pollutants are present, the hydroxyl radicals can combine with them and convert them into less dangerous, simpler compounds7. The same is true for hydrogen peroxide-based AOPs, which depend on generation of hydroxyl radicals due to the reaction between hydrogen peroxide and a catalyst like iron or copper. With the help of UV light, photocatalytic AOPs can create hydroxyl radicals by causing the electrons in a photocatalyst like TiO2 to become excited and once exposed to organic pollutants, these radicals can respond and degrade them. Similarly, Oluwole et al.9 research work looked into the use of a Fenton-like method to remove drugs from wastewater. With removal rates varying from 74 to 99%, the researchers discovered that the procedure successfully eliminated a variety of medicines, including antibiotics and antihypertensive medications. In wastewater treatment, research on AOPs has produced encouraging findings demonstrating their efficiency in removing a variety of pollutants. Zawadzki et al.4 researched a photocatalytic AOP’s capacity to purge pollutants from wastewater. According to the study, the AOPs could be tailored to various types of wastewaters and was very efficient at removing pollutants like bisphenol A and ibuprofen4.

The utilization of an ozone-based AOPs in the treatment of pharmaceuticals wastewater was the subject of research by Kanakaraju et al.5. According to the research, the AOPs was very efficient at getting rid of a variety of drugs, including antibiotics, antiepileptics, and analgesics, and it could be tailored to work with various kinds of wastewater. Among the most frequently employed advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) for treating water are those utilizing a Fenton catalyst, hydrogen peroxide, and photo-Fenton processes. These procedures depend on oxidizing and removing contaminants from water by highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. Hydrogen peroxide and iron (Fe2+ or Fe3+) combine in an acidic environment to produce hydroxyl radicals9,14,15. For Ozone based AOPs, ozone (O3) preferentially combines with the ionized molecules and dissociated form of organic molecules rather than the neutral form in a selective reaction called direct oxidation16–18.

Despite the efficacy of AOPs in the elimination of organic pollutants, their application in real-world scenarios is hindered by the limited availability of suitable catalysts to enhance the oxidative process. There is a need therefore to explore green synthesized iron nanoparticles to serve as efficient and cost-effective catalysts for the AOPs in dealing with organic pollutants. Nanomaterials has gained considerable interest as a result of their diverse applications in several fields and the fact that they are cost-effective with unique features such as large surface area, nanometers in size., quantum effects, electrochemical, and possess unexpected optical, physical, chemical, and thermal properties that can be easily modified through their synthesis route19. Among the different types of nanomaterials, metallic nanoparticles (NPs) are the most widely utilized for various purposes such as in the detection and removal of contaminants, electronic chips and biomedical applications such as diagnostics, tissue treatment, wound healing, immunotherapy, detection of ailment, dentistry, regenerative medicine, and drug delivery19,20. The most promising method for the synthesis of NPs is the green approach which is due to the use of cheap, readily available and eco-friendly materials19. Vernonia amygdalina, which is known as bitter leaf, is widely utilized which is traditionally used in the treatment of ailments such as fever, diarrhea, malaria, hepatitis, dysentery, cough, and many more21,22. It is reported to be rich in phytochemical compositions, that includes the likes of phenolic, flavonoids, polyphenols, and saponins compounds which could assist in the reduction of metal salts to form nanoparticles, offering a non-toxic and environmentally friendly method when compared with traditional chemical reduction methods22–24. These phytochemicals play a crucial role in the green synthesis of nanoparticles by acting as reducing agents and stabilizers Oluwaseun et al.22 They can interact with iron precursors by reducing metal salts to form nanoparticles while simultaneously preventing agglomeration through steric stabilization [25 L]. This interaction enhances the control over the size and shape of the nanoparticles. The antioxidant properties of these phytochemicals also contribute to the synthesis process, by improving the stability and functionality of the resulting nanoparticles and they can chelate metal ions, facilitating the reduction process and preventing aggregation of produced nanoparticles25. The process may result in nanoparticles with unique properties influenced by the specific phytochemicals used. Although, there are little reports on the use of V. amygdalina for the synthesis of nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVIs), literature reports had it that V. amygdalina had been widely utilized for the synthesis of other nanoparticles which was attributed to its rich phytochemical compositions24,26. For instance, the use of green synthesized selenium nanoparticles has been documented for it’s activity in catalysis, antioxidant, photocopiers, anticancer, solar cells, rectifers, and xerography27. Matussin et al.28 used green approach to prepared stannic oxide nanoparticles which was demonstrated to have great antibacterial, photocatalytic, and antioxidant potency and which can be deployed in the environment and biological applications. In a similar vain, cerium oxide nanoparticles prepared using green synthesis was reported for its ability in the photocatalytic degradation of dye, antidiabetic, antioxidant activity, antifungal activity, and anticancer, and antibacterial properties29.The ability of silica-supported titania-magnesium catalysts in the photocatalytic degradation of imidazolium ionic liquids in water was reported by William et al.30. Rashtbari et al.31 used Pomegranate peel extract for the green synthesis of zero-valent iron nanoparticles and was engaged for furfural adsorption. The degradation of dexamethasone and guaifenesin under UV and visible radiation in the presence of a supported photocatalyst generated from petrochemical residue was investigated by William et al.32.

The novelty of using hydrogen peroxide supported by green synthesized zero-valent iron nanoparticles (ZVI NPs) in the degradation of pharmaceutical effluents lies in its environmentally friendly approach and enhanced efficacy. Hydrogen peroxide serves as a powerful oxidizing agent, while ZVI NPs, produced through eco-friendly synthesis methods, offer a sustainable catalyst that promotes the degradation of complex pharmaceutical compounds. This method not only reduces harmful waste but also minimizes the environmental footprint often associated with traditional chemical process. This research aimed at evaluating the efficiency of hydrogen peroxide supported green synthesized iron nanoparticles as efficient and cost-effective catalysts in the oxidation of pharmaceutical effluents (wastewater). The consideration of some parameters such as temperature, pH, and contact time will also be documented.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ferric chloride (FeCl3·6H2O), HCl, BaCl3, HNO3, AgNO3, were purchased from Sigma, India and they were all of good analytical grade and used without further purification.

Collection, preparation and physiochemical analysis of effluent

The effluent used was sampled from the effluent tank of a pharmaceutical company in Agbara Industrial Estate Phase III, Ogun State, Nigeria. The sample was taken in 2 separate bottles and kept refrigerated for preservation until it underwent analysis. The following parameters: pH, conductivity (EC), temperature, total dissolved solids (TDS), sulfates, chlorides, total suspended solids (TSS) and lead contents were measured by using the standard methods described by APHA33 and USEPA34.

Preparation of leaf extract

Vernonia amygdalina (V. amygdalina) leaves were collected in large quantities from Mountain Top University, Prayer City, Ogun State, Nigeria, and selected from around the same area to prevent different species or plants from contaminating the batch. The leaves were cleaned, dusted, and washed with water to eliminate impurities. The collected samples were air-dried in the laboratory and shredded into smaller pieces to reduce surface area and increase drying. They were then pulverized with a mortar and pestle and sieved with a mesh sieve to make the particles of the leaves finer into a powdered form. The most common ratio for extract extraction in earlier research studies is employing a mixture of roughly 5% dried leaves in distilled water. Therefore, 5 g of dried leaves were weighed and brought to a boil in 100 ml of distilled water using a 250 ml round bottom flask in a heating mantle for about 15 min under reflux. This mixture was cooled to room temperature and filtered and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 12 min to eliminate leaf microparticles. The clear solution obtained after this process was sealed in a polypropylene tube, refrigerated and kept for the production of the iron nanoparticles. Phytochemical screening and gas chromatography-mass spectroscopy (GCMS) of leaf extract

The common phytochemical techniques outlined by Radia et al.35 were used to do initial screening of primary and secondary metabolites, including proteins, alkaloids, sugar, polyphenols, flavonoids, and saponins. The GC-MS investigation was done with an 8860 A gas chromatograph coupled with a 5977 C inert mass spectrometer in an electron impact source (Agilent Technologies) to characterize the extract of Vernonia amygdalina.

Zero valent iron nanoparticles (nZVIs) preparation

The synthesis of nZVIs was carried out using 10 ml of distilled water and 18 ml of the leaf extract with 2 ml iron precursor (FeCl3.6H2O, 1 M) in 50 ml volumetric flask. After the leaf extract was prepared, 18 ml of the extract was taken into the 50 mL volumetric flask, thereafter, 10 ml of distilled water was introduced followed by 2 ml of 1 M iron precursor. The mixture (nZVIs) was stirred vigorously using a magnetic stirrer. Thereafter, certain amount of the solution was removed from the vessel at different time interval and analyzed using a UV- spectrophotometer (Shimadzu; UV-3600 UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer).

Characterization of nZVIs

FT-IR Spectroscopy was done as technique to analyze the chemical bonds of the nZVIs by mixing the samples of nZVIs with KBr and pulverized to form a homogenous powder which was compressed under a pressure of 7 tons. Pelkin Elmer 3000 MX spectrometer was used to record all the spectra from 4000 to 400 cm− 1. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out for crystal structure determination of nZVIs nanoparticles and confirm the presence of iron crystals using Rigaku D/Max-lllC X-ray diffractometer, Japan. The diffraction peaks founds were compared with standard data of minerals (JCPDS file no: 65- 3107). The scanning electron microscope (SEM, using a Zeiss evo 18 instrument) is a great tool for determining the size and shapes or morphology of nanoparticles since image capture and sample preparation are both very quick and easy processes. The measurement was done using Energy Dispersive X-ray analysis (SEM-EDX) using a Zeiss evo18 scanning electron microscope.

Effect of concentration of H2O2

About 30 ml of the effluent was taken into a conical flask with 20 ml of different concentrations of H2O2 between 0.1 M and 0.8 M. The reaction was observed at 60 min to note the impact of H2O2 concentration on the reaction process. At the end of each process, the solution was run through a spectrophotometer at 650 nm and the decolorization efficiency of the effluent was calculated using Eq. 2 above. Effect of temperature

About 30 ml of the effluent was taken into a conical flask with 20 ml of 0.4 M of H2O2 and placed in a water bath at different temperatures; 25ºC, 35ºC, 45ºC, 55ºC and 65ºC. The reaction was observed at 60 min to note the impact temperature has on the reaction process. At the end of each process, the solution was run through a spectrophotometer at 650 nm and the decolorization efficiency of the effluent was calculated using Eq. 2 above. The experiments were performed twice and the average value reported.

The experiments were performed twice and the average value reported.

Results and discussions

Physicochemical analysis of effluent

As depicted in the table, the pH for the raw effluent before treatment, after treatment with H2O2 and with H2O2@nZVIs are 5.2, 6.0 and 7.4 respectively. Olusegun et al.36 reported that the pH of pharmaceutical effluents to be in the range of 5.57 to 7.34 and they opined that at a low pH, there is availability of heavy metals in the solution due to their high solubility in this pH range and as such, they may be dangerous to living organisms, while at a high pH, the metal species become insoluble and could as well store up in slugs and sediments. From this present study, it was observed that the H2O2@nZVIs treatment of the pharmaceutical effluent ameliorate the pH to normal range which could be as a result of the removal of some acidic components from the PE.

The results for TSS are 0.122, 0.105 and 0.018 mg/L for the raw effluent before treatment, after treatment with H2O2 and after treatment with H2O2@nZVIs respectively with the catalyst also showing greater potential to eliminate the TSS from the PE. Both the TDS and EC increased after the treatment with both oxidizing agents which could be due to the introductions of ions by the hydrogen peroxides. Lead concentration in the PE sample before treatment is 0.30 mg/L which is higher than 0.1 mg/l recommended by WHO. However, upon treatment with H2O2, the Pb concentration reduced to 0.14 mg/l and after H2O2@nZVIs treatment, the Pb concentration was totally eliminated from the PE sample. The fact that Pb was completely eliminated after being treated with H2O2@nZVIs is significant since it shows that H2O2 combined with nZVIs may be used to remove Pb from pharmaceutical effluent. Lead removal using nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVIs) primarily involves a combination of adsorption and chemical reduction processes. The nZVIs can adsorb lead ions onto their surface, and simultaneously, the zero-valent iron can chemically reduce lead from its ionic form to a less toxic or less soluble form, often leading to the formation of lead nanoparticles. Complexation with iron species can also play a role but is generally secondary to these primary mechanisms30,31.

Chloride concentration in the PE sample before treatment is 60.22 mg/L. However, upon treatment with H2O2 and H2O2@nZVIs, the chloride values reduced to 43.17 mg/l and 34.26 mg/l. The presence of sulfates was reported to be 10.22 mg/l in the raw sample of PE. Upon treatment with H2O2 and H2O2@nZVIs, the value reduced to 8.16 and 5.82 mg/l respectively indicating that both catalysts effectively reduced the quantities of both chlorides and sulfates in the PE sample. Chloride and sulfate levels in effluent decrease after post-treatment with the H2O2@nZVIs (hydrogen peroxide supported on nanoscale zero-valent iron) system could be due to the oxidative degradation processes facilitated by this treatment30,31. The H2O2 acts as an oxidant, breaking down complex organic pollutants and potentially converting inorganic compounds like sulfates and chlorides into less harmful forms or facilitating their removal through precipitation or adsorption processes25,31. Thus, combined effects of oxidation, potential precipitation, and adsorption activity help reduce chloride and sulfate levels in effluent after treatment with H2O2@nZVIs.

Characterization of synthesized nZVIs

Figure 3 depicts (Fa) leaf extract of V. amygdalina, and (Fb) V. amygdalina leaf extract with Fe-solution, while Fig. 4 shows (a) UV absorption spectrum of nZVIs, and (b) particle size of the aqueous extract of V. amygdalina iron nanoparticles (nZVIs). When the extract of V. amygdalina leaf was added to the solution of Fe, it was observed that the color change from brown to pale yellow indicating the formation of Fe-nanoparticles (nZVIs). This change in colour was measured using UV-vis spec which was able to detect peaks wavelength at 300 and 400 nm inFe-solution, 300 nm for the V. amygdalina leaf extract and 250 nm for leaf extract of V. amygdalina with Fe solution (nZVIs). Nur et al.39 reported the existence of a strong peak around 242 nm in the UV analysis of neem leaf extract and this was attributed to the excitation of surface plasmon vibrations in the iron nanoparticles which is caused by the combined vibrations of free electrons of the iron nanoparticles in resonance with light wave. Similarly, Norazlina et al.38 identified three peaks 300 nm, 380 nm and 390 nm in their study of neem leaves. The particle size of the aqueous extract of V. amygdalina iron nanoparticles (nZVIs) was found to be in the range of 10 to 100 nm. Figure 5a shows the FT-IR analysis of the synthesized nZVIs. The band appearing at 3457.22 cm− 1 could be assigned to a stretching of O–H group40, bands observed at 2359.00 and 2925.81 cm− 1 corresponds to C-H stretching. The peak located at 1741.50 cm− 1 is due to the presence of C = O vibration in aldehyde and ketones41, the absorption at band 1652 cm− 1 corresponds C = C stretching, whereas, the peaks at 1541.50 and 1451.50 cm− 1 indicate C = C vibration of ring stretching. The corresponding absorption at 1165.00 and 1025.00 cm− 1 were assigned to C-O stretching. The elemental analysis of the synthesized nZVIs as revealed by EDS is shown in the Fig. 5b. It was noted that the nZVIs composed mainly of Fe (89.74%) and O (10.26%) as the only two elements present in the nanomaterial with iron showing the larger amount. The morphology of the nZVIs before the decolorization process (Fig. 5c) revealed that the particles of the nanoparticles are made up of round shapes with particles sizes in the range of 30 to 56 nm. Changes in the characteristics of surface morphology explained some structural changes of the nZVIs after the decolorization were observed and analyzed by scanning electron microscopy as evident in Fig. 5c. It was noted that, after the degradation process, a large number of particles adhered closely together revealing agglomerated nature. XRD analysis of the nZVIs (Fig. 5e) revealed various peaks indexed planes of (220), (311), (400), (422), (520) and others confirming the characteristics of the Fe-nanoparticles formed42.

Oxidation of pharmaceutical effluent

Effect of pollutant concentration

The effect of the pharmaceutical effluent (PE) concentration on the removal ability of the two catalysts tested was investigated and the results are shown in Fig. 6a and b respectively. From Fig. 6a, the absorbance of the hydrogen peroxide treated effluent increased from 0.077 to 0.159 when the concentration of the effluent was raised from 10 to 30 ml. Upon treatment with H2O2@nZVI, it was noticed that the absorbance increased from 0.065 to 0.151 which implied that the H2O2@nZVI treated sample is less coloured as a result of much decolorization. Similarly, from Fig. 6b, the percentage decolourization when H2O2was used decreased from 74.74 to 51.96% as the concentration of the effluent was increased from 10 ml to 30 ml. However, after treatment with H2O2@nZVI, it was noticed that the percentage decolourization decreased from 80.36 to 54.38% under the same effluent concentration as in the case of H2O2. At lower effluent concentration, the catalyst is able to perform better due to the presence of fewer PE molecules in solution which compete for the active sites of the catalysts. However, at higher PE concentration, the presence of higher PE molecules in solution which compete for the catalyst increase and since the quantity of the catalyst is constant with increase in PE concentration, the efficiency of the catalyst is reduced. Similar observation was made by Teng et al.43, that at low pollutants concentrations, the generated ratio of ·OH to that of the pollutant in the system was more than that at high pollutant concentrations. In a study conducted by Gao et al.44, it was observed that with an increase in initial concentration of Cr(VI) from 15 to 45 mg/L, the adsorption capacity of nZVI dropped from 50.0 to 27.8 mg/g and that the removal of Cr(VI) was obviously inhibited from 100 to 54%. This they attributed to the fact that as more of Cr(VI) approached nZVI particles, there is higher passivation layers formed by oxidation, which significantly reduces the removal ability of nZVI. In a related study conducted by Yohannes et al.25, it was noted that the rate of degradation of violet and methylene blue reduced with an increase in dye concentration because higher concentration of the pollutant was said to have inhibited the transfer of electron process on the nanoparticle surface, thus reducing the number of active sites on surface of the catalyst.

Effect of pH

The effect of the pH level on the degradation ability of the two catalysts tested was examined and the results are shown in Fig. 8a and b respectively. In Fig. 8a, it was observed that the absorbance of the hydrogen peroxide treated effluent decreased from 0.102 to 0.079 when the pH was raised from 2 to 10, but upon treatment with H2O2@nZVI, it was noticed that the absorbance increased from 0.074 to 0.128. Also, from Fig. 8b, the percentage decolourization when H2O2 alone was used, rose from 74.5 to 80.25%, however, with H2O2@nZVI, the percentage decolourization decreased from 81.5 to 68% on increasing the pH from 2 to 10. The reaction appears to be more effective in an acidic medium than in an alkaline medium due to the ability of iron to dissolve better in a lower pH which attack the effluent causing more decolourization to occur. Saeedeh45 in the study of Fenton-like oxidation of malachite green solutions observed that the extent of decolorization decreases with rise in pH, and that almost 95% color removal was observed at pH 3.0. It was concluded that the decrease in oxidation rate at pH > 3 could be due to the formation of Fe(OH)3, which shows lower decomposition catalytic activity. The catalytic activity, dominant Fe species, pollutant, oxidant and substrate activities, as well as the stability of H2O2 all depends of the solution pH and as such, pH plays a significantly role in advance oxidation processes18,33,34. In a study conducted by Teng et al.43. for the degradation of P-chlorophenol by NZVI/rectorite, it was observed that within a pH between 3 and 5, high P-chlorophenol degradation efficiency was achieved. They stressed further that at a pH above 5, the degradation efficiency of NZVI/rectorite decreased gradually. Teng et al.43. concluded that the presence of inactive FeO2+ ion, small OH oxidation tendency, H2O2 decomposition, and catalyst deactivation with Fe(OH)3 complex formation at high pH values is responsible for this behaviour. The decrease in decolorization efficiency at higher pH levels when using H2O2 and H2O2@nZVIs in the pharmaceutical compounds can be attributed to the fact that at higher pH levels, hydrogen peroxide can be more susceptible to decomposition, reducing its availability for generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as hydroxyl radicals (*OH) and this may lead to lower overall oxidation potential in the system42–44. Also, at higher pH values, the increased negative charge on the particle surface may hinder the adsorption of contaminants and reactants, thus reducing the efficiency of the redox reactions. Additionally, higher pH conditions might favor the formation of more stable iron oxides, limiting the availability of reactive Fe0 or Fe2+ species needed for activating H2O2. The rate of hydroxyl radical formation is generally higher in acidic or neutral conditions compared to alkaline conditions due to favorable reaction pathways with Fe2+ and H2O2 and the reaction kinetics may be hindered at higher pH42–44. Similarly, at elevated pH, competing reactions involving hydroxide ions (OH−) may also occur, leading to the formation of superoxide anions (O2−) or other species that may not effectively degrade the target compounds, whereas, in alkaline conditions, the concentration of reactive species generated might be diluted due to a higher pH, leading to a lower overall concentration of effective oxidizing agents in the solution. The practical applications of this discovery is that low pH enhances the production of hydroxyl radicals (•OH), which are highly reactive species responsible for degrading and decolorizing pollutants and also, in acidic environments, iron (Fe⁰) from nZVI more readily undergoes oxidation, releasing Fe² which reacts with H₂O₂ for generating hydroxyl radicals which boosts the decolorization and degradation of organic dyes and pollutants. However, in an environments with fluctuating pH, this requires careful control of pH in practical applications to maintaining an acidic pH (typically in the range of 3–5) which is essential for optimal performance of the H₂O₂@nZVI system in decolorization or pollutant degradation processes.

Mechanistic pathway for hydroxyl radical formation using nZVI/H₂O

The generation of hydroxyl radicals in water by H2O2 is most usually activated by iron metal in Fenton-related AOPs16. The production of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) through the interaction of nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) represents an advanced oxidation process. This approach significantly enhances the generation of reactive oxygen species, particularly hydroxyl radicals, which are crucial for pollutant degradation and environmental remediation. The method operates via a Fenton-like reaction, where the nanoscale size of nZVI particles boosts reactivity due to their increased surface area, leading to more efficient radical formation.

For instance, in addition to hydroxyl radicals (·OH), the Fenton reaction can generate other reactive oxygen species (ROS) that may lead to side reactions16–18. Also, the Fenton reaction often requires the presence of high concentrations of iron (Fe2+ or Fe3+). Over time, the iron can precipitate as iron oxides or iron hydroxides (such as Fe(OH)3), forming a solid waste or sludge that needs to be treated. This iron sludge can also trap organic pollutants or catalytic iron, reducing the efficiency of the reaction16–18. Similarly, unreacted hydrogen peroxide can remain in solution after the reaction if not properly controlled. The effective control, optimization of the reaction conditions (e.g., pH, H2O2 concentration, iron dosage) and the use of nZVIs employed in this study helped to mitigate some of these adverse effects and improve the selectivity of the Fenton reaction.

Reuseability study of nZVIs

Since some catalysts often deactivate after few cycles of operations, it’s important to investigate the reuseability of catalysts. In this study, the pharmaceutical effluents were engaged to examine the reuseability of nZVI under optimum experimental conditions and this was done five times. After each decolourization experiment, the effluent mixture containing the nZVI was centrifuge and the nZVI was recovered and washed with distilled water for the reuseability experiment. The results obtained are shown in Fig. 13 and as depicted, the percentage decolourization of nZVI was relatively stable even after five cycles of operations indicating that the synthesized nZVI could serve as effective catalysts with minimal reduction in the degradation ability even after being used five times. The study further demonstrate the renewable of nZVI since it was found that it could still retain its catalytic activity even after being recycled up to five times, indicating the economic viability and environmental sustainability of the process and also eliminating the possibility of releasing the catalyst into the environment after use. The results from this study are similar to literature reports of Rashtbari et al.31 and Yohannes et al.25.

Conclusion

The effectiveness of hydrogen peroxide supported zero-valent nanoparticle catalyst in treating pharmaceutical effluent was tested. The nZVIs was produced from the green synthesis using the leaf extract of V. amygdalina and characterized with different analytical techniques. Results from the characterization confirmed that nZVIs was successfully produced. The catalytic efficiency was shown to greatly depend on experimental conditions such as time, PE concentration, pH, temperature and the catalyst concentration. It was observed that the potency of H2O2 to degrade the effluent was influenced by the presence of the prepared nZVIs. Conclusively, advanced oxidation process using H2O2@nZVIs catalyst could be a promising method for treating pharmaceutical effluent before releasing them into the environment.
