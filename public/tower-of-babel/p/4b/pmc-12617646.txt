Impact of a decision-making support framework on nurse burnout and quality of end-of-life care: A before-and-after study in intensive care units
Authors: Yoshihiko Takahashi, Shu Utsumi, Kenji Fujizuka, Hiroyuki Suzuki, Yumiko Takadera, Mitsunobu Nakamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe high incidence of burnout syndrome among intensive care unit nurses highlights the need for effective prevention strategies. Poor decision-making in end-of-life care contributes to nurse burnout syndrome through a decline in the quality of end-of-life care; however, the effects of enhancing decision-making support remain unclear.ObjectivesTo examine the relationship between enhanced decision-making support in end-of-life care, nurse burnout syndrome, and the quality of end-of-life care.MethodsA before-and-after intervention study was conducted in a 24-bed mixed intensive care unit at a tertiary emergency medical center in Japan. In September 2022, a decision-making support framework, developed through an international Delphi meeting in 2020, was introduced. Pre- and post-intervention questionnaires (August 2022, 2023) assessed nurse burnout syndrome using the Maslach Burnout Inventory and nurses' evaluation of end-of-life care using the Quality of Dying and Death scale. Univariate analysis used t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Multivariate analysis used multiple regression, adjusting for nurses' age, sex, and intensive care unit experience.ResultsAmong the 70 nurses, 51 (73%) completed pre- and post-intervention surveys. While the Maslach Burnout Inventory showed no statistically significant change (15 vs. 11, p = 0.177), the subscales of emotional exhaustion (15 vs. 14, p = 0.089) and depersonalization (12 vs. 10, p = 0.087) showed trends toward improvement. No significant subgroup differences were observed based on sex, age, or intensive care unit experience. However, the Quality of Dying and Death scale showed a significant improvement (33 vs. 27, p = 0.002), which remained significant in the multivariate analysis (β = 5.015, p = 0.02). Subgroup analysis revealed more pronounced improvements in the Quality of Dying and Death scale among female nurses, those under 30, and nurses with less than five years of intensive care unit experience.ConclusionsThe decision-making support framework was associated with a non-significant reduction in burnout syndrome but a significant improvement in nurses' evaluation of end-of-life care. This suggests that factors contributing to burnout syndrome in end-of-life care may have improved. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are required to determine whether this intervention ultimately contributes to mitigating burnout syndrome.Keywords: Burnout, Decision-making, End-of-life, Intensive care unit, Nurse, Palliative care

Introduction

Burnout syndrome is a psychological condition caused by prolonged occupational stress, leading to emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment (Embriaco et al., 2007). It has been associated with declining staff well-being (Aiken et al., 2002), a decrease in care quality (Shanafelt et al., 2002), and increased absenteeism and turnover (Ackerman, 1993). Intensive care unit nurses are at a particularly high risk, with one in three experiencing severe burnout syndrome, a higher rate than that in other departments (Poncet et al., 2007). Therefore, strategies to prevent burnout syndrome among nurses are urgently needed (Chen and McMurray, 2001).

Previous reports have identified the quality of end-of-life care as one factor contributing to burnout among intensive care nurses (Embriaco et al., 2007; Lief et al., 2018). The quality of end-of-life care refers to how well care is provided during the final stages of life and how closely it aligns with the patients’ values and preferences, while ensuring their comfort and dignity (Hafifah et al., 2025). However, in intensive care settings, end-of-life care often faces challenges due to a lack of clarity in care goals and limited communication about treatment preferences (Effendy et al., 2022). As a result, interventions may be prolonged despite not reflecting the patient’s wishes (Hochendoner et al., 2021). Such misalignment between care and patients' values or preferences can increase emotional strain and dissatisfaction among nurses, thereby contributing substantially to burnout syndrome (Embriaco et al., 2007). Notably, one crucial factor influencing this alignment is the decision-making process in end-of-life care.

Decision-making support is essential in end-of-life care, as it determines how treatment goals are set and aligned with patients' values through collaborative discussions among patients, families, and healthcare professionals (Sinuff et al., 2015). However, such support has been reported to be often fragmented or lacking a clear structure (Kon et al., 2016). When poorly coordinated, it may result in prolonged or aggressive treatments that fail to reflect patients’ values, ultimately diminishing the quality of end-of-life care and increasing nurses’ psychological burden (Flannery et al., 2016). Therefore, systematically strengthening and organizing decision-making support may alleviate the ethical burden on nurses and improve the alignment of care with patients' values, potentially reducing burnout (Kon et al., 2016). Despite these potential benefits, few studies have directly examined whether enhancing decision-making support positively affects burnout among healthcare providers.

A previous study found that communication strategies in end-of-life care were associated with reduced burnout syndrome among healthcare staff (Quenot et al., 2012). However, it primarily focused on burnout syndrome and depression incidence, making it difficult to determine whether the improvement in the quality of end-of-life care through communication strategies contributed to burnout syndrome reduction. The study also included multiple interventions, such as workplace optimization, organizational support, and psychological coping, leaving the specific factors contributing to burnout syndrome reduction unclear. In this study, we aimed to determine whether a structured decision-making support framework—developed through an international Delphi consensus and implemented in daily intensive care units practice—could improve the quality of end-of-life care and reduce burnout syndrome among nurses (Kerckhoffs et al., 2020).

Methods

Design

We conducted a before-and-after intervention study in a single-center, 24-bed, closed-mixed intensive care unit at a tertiary emergency medical center in Japan. The Ethics Committee of Maebashi Red Cross Hospital approved the study procedures (approval no.: 2022–47), and all participants provided informed consent.

Participants and data collection

We administered a questionnaire to all registered nurses—full-time, part-time, or newly hired—who worked two shifts in the intensive care units during the study period and were involved in direct patient care (Fig. 1). Temporary or agency nurses and those in administrative roles without bedside duties were excluded. The first survey was conducted in August 2022, before the framework was implemented, and the second was conducted in August 2023, after its implementation. Nurses who provided consent received a sealed questionnaire and returned it anonymously to the principal investigator after completing it. Data from participants who declined to participate were not recorded.

Study flow.

ICU: intensive care unit.

The questionnaire collected information on age, sex, intensive care unit experience, Maslach Burnout Inventory, and Quality of Dying and Death in Intensive Care Units. Intensive care unit environment data were retrospectively collected from the electronic system between September 2021 and August 2023, including total intensive care unit admissions, admission types, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II scores at admission, in–intensive care unit and in-hospital mortality, and do-not-resuscitate order rates at the time of death in the intensive care unit and the hospital. Severity data were collected because of their correlation with mortality and their influence on nurses' assessment of the quality of end-of-life care (Merlani et al., 2011). Furthermore, although do-not-resuscitate orders at death do not always indicate better decision-making support for all patients, they have been linked to improved outcomes at the hospital level (Curtis et al., 2011).

Intervention

To enhance decision-making support, a structured framework was integrated into daily intensive care unit practice starting in September 2022, following a series of briefing sessions for intensive care unit staff (Fig. 2) (Kerckhoffs et al., 2020). The framework was developed in 2020 through an international Delphi consensus involving physicians, nurses, former intensive care unit patients, and surrogate decision-makers from 13 European countries, under the initiative of the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine. The intervention was delivered primarily by intensive care unit physicians and nurses and, when necessary, supported by a multidisciplinary team including pharmacists, nutritionists, physiotherapists, medical social workers, and palliative care specialists.

The European society of intensive care medicine proposed a framework supporting the decision-making process on life-sustaining treatments in intensive care units (ICUs).

ICU: intensive care unit.

The framework was incorporated into routine clinical practice through the following components: (i) intensive care unit admission and intensive care unit follow-up meetings: an informative collection sheet was created in the electronic medical records at the time of admission, and the physicians and nurses held regular meetings with the patients and families to consolidate key information to be identified (to ensure early understanding of the patient’s condition and wishes, and to align the care plan accordingly). (ii) Goal-of-care meeting: every Wednesday, doctors and nurses held a goal-of-care meeting referring to the information collection sheet (to align treatment goals with patient/family wishes and clarify any treatment limitations, while addressing identified family needs). (iii) Timeout meeting: when necessary, a timeout meeting was held with a doctor, nurse, pharmacist, nutritionist, physiotherapist, medical social worker, and palliative care staff (to reach consensus on complex care decisions, establish clear treatment goals, and confirm the appropriateness of ongoing interventions). The framework was embedded into routine intensive care units' workflows by incorporating information collection into electronic medical records, scheduling goal-of-care meetings during the weekly case conference, and convening timeout meetings when clinical or ethical complexity arose. The intervention was continuously applied over a 12-month period following its implementation.

Measurements

The primary outcome was the Japanese version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (Supplemental File 1). Given that the assessment of burnout syndrome varies by region and ethical considerations, we used the Japanese version. The Maslach Burnout Inventory is a 22-item questionnaire that comprehensively measures three aspects of burnout syndrome: (i) emotional exhaustion — the feeling of being overextended; (ii) depersonalization — a cynical and distant attitude toward one’s work and patients; and (iii) personal accomplishment — the tendency to evaluate one’s achievements at work negatively (Büssing and Perrar, 1994). Each item is rated on a six-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 5 (always). Higher emotional exhaustion and depersonalization scores and lower personal accomplishment scores indicate greater burnout syndrome severity. Accordingly, emotional exhaustion and depersonalization were scored as positive, whereas personal accomplishment was scored as negative. Owing to its widespread use in assessing burnout syndrome among healthcare providers (Embriaco et al., 2007), the Maslach Burnout Inventory was selected as the primary outcome in this study and previous research. The Japanese version, adapted to a 17-item format to better reflect Japan's working environment while referencing Maslach and Jackson’s (1981) original Maslach Burnout Inventory, has demonstrated high internal consistency, strong factor validity, and test–retest reliability (Kubo, 2014). The Japanese version, developed by Kubo and Tao (1994), requires no additional permission for non-commercial academic use when properly cited.

The secondary outcome was the Japanese version of the nurse assessment of the Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire (Supplemental File 2), which was selected for the same reasons as the Maslach Burnout Inventory. The original Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire is a 14-item instrument that evaluates the quality of end-of-life care, with each item rated on an eleven-point Likert scale from 0 (very bad) to 10 (very good). This tool is widely used to assess the quality of dying and death in intensive care unit settings. Nurse assessments are reportedly more objective and reliable than those of family members or clinicians, with a lower risk of emotional bias (Hodde et al., 2004). Given its widespread use in evaluating nurses’ assessment of end-of-life care and its relevance to burnout syndrome–related end-of-life factors, the nurse-assessed Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire was selected as the secondary outcome (Takahashi et al., 2024). The Japanese version was adapted into a six-item, two-domain instrument to better reflect Japanese values on life and death, demonstrating strong factor validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability (Kinoshita et al., 2018).

Statistical analysis

A power analysis was not performed because this was a single-center before-and-after study, including all eligible intensive care unit nurses during the study period. Continuous variables with a normal distribution, as determined by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, were presented as means and standard deviations, whereas non-normally distributed variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. To assess the effects of the intervention, univariate analyses were performed using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Outcomes were further stratified by age, sex, and intensive care unit experience to examine potential variation in intervention effects across subgroups. These subgroup analyses were pre-specified based on prior findings suggesting demographic influences on burnout symptoms (Ramírez-Elvira et al., 2021). Multivariate analysis using multiple regression was conducted to adjust for age, sex, and intensive care unit experience as continuous covariates. A complete-case analysis was performed. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using EZR (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) (Kanda, 2013).

Results

Nurse characteristics and medical record data

Among the 51 out of 70 nurses (73 %) who completed both the pre- and post-intervention questionnaires, data on age, sex, and intensive care unit experience were missing for four cases in the pre-intervention group and five in the post-intervention group. These data were missing because completing demographic items was voluntary in the anonymous survey, and some participants chose not to provide this information. No participants were excluded because of missing outcome data. There were minimal staffing changes during the study period: two nurses were reassigned to other departments, and two new nurses joined. The characteristics of the nurses, including sex, age, and intensive care unit experience, showed no significant differences between the pre-intervention and post-intervention groups, as illustrated in Table 1. Both groups had similar nurse demographics, with a median age of 30 years, approximately 60 % female, and most nurses having less than 10 years of experience in the intensive care unit. Table 2 presents the intensive care unit environment data, indicating no significant changes in intensive care unit admission type, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score, intensive care unit mortality, or in-hospital mortality. While do-not-resuscitate order rates at death in the intensive care unit remained unchanged (74 % vs. 85 %, p = 0.356), hospital-wide do-not-resuscitate order rates increased significantly (77 % vs. 90 %, p = 0.030).

Discussion

Overall, while the Maslach Burnout Inventory indicated reduced burnout syndrome, this association was not statistically significant. However, the intervention was significantly associated with an improvement in the Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire, which remained significant in the multivariate analysis. Subgroup analysis showed no significant associations for the Maslach Burnout Inventory but identified significant associations for the Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire in female nurses, those under 30, and those with less than five years of intensive care unit experience.

Overall analysis of the Maslach Burnout Inventory

In the overall analysis, although the intervention did not show a statistically significant association with the Maslach Burnout Inventory, a trend toward significance was noted, which might have reached statistical significance with a larger sample size. However, this finding is inconsistent with a previous study reporting that the intensive communication strategy regarding end-of-life care was significantly associated with improved burnout syndrome among healthcare staff (Poncet et al., 2007).

One possible reason for this discrepancy is that multiple factors beyond end-of-life issues influence burnout syndrome among intensive care unit nurses. These include personal characteristics such as age, organizational factors such as work schedules and participation in working groups, and workplace relationship quality, including conflicts with patients, families, or other staff (Poncet et al., 2007). In the previous study, the intervention was combined with other components, such as workplace optimization, organizational support, individual psychological coping, and mental health support, which may also have contributed (Quenot et al., 2012; Moss et al., 2016). In this context, despite focusing solely on end-of-life care, our study showed a trend toward improvement in the Maslach Burnout Inventory, suggesting a direct relationship between end-of-life care and burnout syndrome, and potentially providing valuable insights for future research.

In the Maslach Burnout Inventory subscales, the intervention showed a trend toward an association with emotional exhaustion and depersonalization; however, no significant association was observed for personal accomplishment. Emotional exhaustion has been reported to be linked to spending time and effort on care perceived as non-beneficial, which can lead to depersonalization—a detached and impersonal response to patients (Dall’Ora et al., 2020). In this study, the acquisition of do-not-resuscitate orders at the time of death without affecting patient mortality suggests that end-of-life treatment plans were appropriately aligned with the patients' prognoses. This may have reduced opportunities for nurses to spend excessive time and effort on care they perceived as non-beneficial, potentially contributing to lower levels of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization (Gama et al., 2014). Although no significant association was found for personal accomplishment, this result supports the perspective that personal accomplishment is less related to the other two subscales and represents a distinct dimension of burnout syndrome (Hetzel-Riggin et al., 2020).

Subgroup analysis of the Maslach Burnout Inventory

In the subgroup analysis, no significant association between the intervention and improvement in the Maslach Burnout Inventory was observed in any subgroup. However, as previously reported, younger and less experienced nurses tend to have higher Maslach Burnout Inventory scores (Ramírez-Elvira et al., 2021). They have also been found to be more empathetic toward patients and more sensitive to the stress of dealing with dying patients, while their coping skills remain underdeveloped, placing them at a higher risk for burnout syndrome (Sumi et al., 2018). This study did not find a significant association with improvement in the Maslach Burnout Inventory, suggesting that although the intervention may have influenced the end-of-life care provided by younger and less experienced nurses, the sample size may have been insufficient to detect a statistically significant effect on Maslach Burnout Inventory improvement. Moreover, compared to older and more experienced nurses, factors such as workplace optimization and organizational support may play a crucial role in burnout syndrome among younger and less experienced nurses (Moss et al., 2016).

Overall analysis of the quality of dying and death questionnaire

The overall analysis revealed a significant positive association between the decision-making support framework and improvements in the Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire. This finding is consistent with a previous study reporting that an intervention to improve the quality of palliative care was associated with higher nurse-assessed Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire scores in the intensive care unit.

This framework emphasizes prognosis prediction, communication, and patient- and family-centered decision-making—key elements also identified in recent comprehensive reviews on appropriate palliative care in intensive care units (Salins et al., 2024). Inaccurate prognosis predictions and disagreements have been reported to be associated with moral distress among intensive care unit staff and may hinder the care provided to patients and their families (Henrich et al., 2016). Multidisciplinary discussions on prognosis and the alignment of care goals may have helped reduce nurses' moral distress by ensuring that end-of-life care was appropriately aligned with patient prognoses (Poncet et al., 2007). However, communication barriers in end-of-life care have been reported not only between patients, families, and physicians but also between nurses and physicians (Puntillo and McAdam, 2006). These barriers arise from differences in perspectives and experiences between the nurses and physicians. As nurses spend more time directly with patients and their families, they frequently encounter the dying process and death. Therefore, regular care goal meetings are likely to improve communication between nurses and physicians, enabling nurses to act more effectively as mediators between physicians, patients, and families (Kirchhoff and Beckstrand, 2000). Additionally, patient- and family-centered decision-making often faces challenges, such as difficulties in eliciting the wishes of patients and families or contradictions with explicit directives (Nelson and Danis, 2001). Incorporating the values and preferences of patients and their families from an early stage could help provide care that is beneficial to patients, their families, and nurses who advocate for them. Lastly, intensive care nurses often experience frustration because of their lack of involvement in the decision-making process, and their continuous participation through this framework may have contributed to greater satisfaction with end-of-life care (Ferrand et al., 2003).

Subgroup analysis of the quality of dying and death questionnaire

The association with improving the Quality of Dying and Death questionnaire was more pronounced among female nurses, those under 30 years of age, and those with less than five years of intensive care unit experience. Female healthcare providers often show a stronger preference for patient-centered care than their male counterparts (Ward et al., 2012), and younger, less experienced nurses have been reported to exhibit greater empathy toward patients, which may explain their stronger association with patient- and family-centered decision-making support (Bertakis and Azari, 2012). However, the lack of significant associations among male, older, and more experienced nurses could be attributed to the small sample size of the study. Further research is needed to examine the relationship between intensive care nurses’ experience and their age.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, as this was a single-center study, the limited sample size may have affected the generalizability of the findings. Second, the survey response rate of 70 % may have introduced selection bias, as nurses with a greater interest in palliative care may have been more likely to participate. Third, the intensive care unit’s relatively low mortality rate—because of nearly half of the patients being admitted for scheduled postoperative care—may have influenced the assessment of end-of-life care quality. Finally, as a before-and-after intervention study without a control group, it is difficult to attribute the observed changes solely to the intervention, since external factors may have influenced the outcomes.

This study had several notable strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to apply a widely implementable framework and evaluate its effectiveness, particularly in Japan, where ethical values differ from those in Europe, where the framework was originally developed. Previous studies on decision-making support interventions in intensive care units have shown significant variability in their approaches (Pignatiello et al., 2018). This framework has the potential to standardize decision-making support in intensive care units and enhance its quality, thereby positioning it as a potentially innovative intervention. Furthermore, there is limited research evaluating the relationship between enhanced decision-making support in end-of-life care and burnout syndrome. The intervention was associated with improvements in nurses’ evaluation of end-of-life care and exhibited a trend toward a non-significant association with burnout syndrome among nurses. To confirm whether the trends observed in this study are statistically significant, multicenter collaborative studies and large-scale research are necessary.

Conclusion

The implementation of the decision-making support framework resulted in a non-significant reduction in burnout syndrome among nurses but was significantly associated with improvements in nurses' evaluation of end-of-life care. These findings suggest that enhancing communication, prognostication, and shared decision-making may improve nurses’ perceptions of end-of-life care and help mitigate burnout symptoms. This decision-making framework is also feasible in Japanese intensive care units and reaffirms the association between deficiencies in the decision-making process and moral distress among nurses. The lack of a significant reduction in burnout syndrome may be due to the limited sample size in this single-center study; therefore, further large-scale, multicenter studies are required to determine whether this intervention ultimately contributes to mitigating burnout syndrome among intensive care nurses.
