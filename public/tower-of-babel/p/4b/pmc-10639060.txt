Additional principles that govern the release of pre-ribosomes from the nucleolus into the nucleoplasm in yeast
Authors: Amber J LaPeruta, Jelena Micic, John L Woolford Jr.
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring eukaryotic ribosome biogenesis, pre-ribosomes travel from the nucleolus, where assembly is initiated, to the nucleoplasm and then are exported to the cytoplasm, where assembly concludes. Although nuclear export of pre-ribosomes has been extensively investigated, the release of pre-ribosomes from the nucleolus is an understudied phenomenon. Initial data indicate that unfolded rRNA interacts in trans with nucleolar components and that, when rRNA folds due to ribosomal protein (RP) binding, the number of trans interactions drops below the threshold necessary for nucleolar retention. To validate and expand on this idea, we performed a bioinformatic analysis of the protein components of the Saccharomyces cerevisiae ribosome assembly pathway. We found that ribosome biogenesis factors (RiBi factors) contain significantly more predicted trans interacting regions than RPs. We also analyzed cryo-EM structures of ribosome assembly intermediates to determine how nucleolar pre-ribosomes differ from post-nucleolar pre-ribosomes, specifically the capacity of RPs, RiBi factors, and rRNA components to interact in trans. We observed a significant decrease in the theoretical trans-interacting capability of pre-ribosomes between nucleolar and post-nucleolar stages of assembly due to the release of RiBi factors from particles and the folding of rRNA. Here, we provide a mechanism for the release of pre-ribosomes from the nucleolus.

Introduction

Eukaryotic ribosome assembly occurs in three subcellular compartments, beginning in the nucleolus, where the majority of subunit maturation occurs. Assembly continues in the nucleoplasm, then final steps occur in the cytoplasm. This complicated pathway involves transcription, modification, folding, and processing of the precursor-ribosomal RNA (pre-rRNA) together with the incorporation of 79 ribosomal proteins (RPs) to form the large and small ribosomal subunits (LSU and SSU). Approximately 200 ribosome biogenesis factors (RiBi factors) in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, mostly conserved in other eukaryotes, coordinate these steps (1–4).

The nucleolus consists of three subcompartments in eukaryotes: the fibrillar center (FC), the dense fibrillar center (DFC), and the granular component (GC). At the border between the FC and DFC, RNA polymerase I transcribes 35S pre-rRNA, which contains sequences for the 25S and 5.8S rRNAs of the LSU and 18S rRNA of the SSU. Nascent rRNA enters the DFC as it associates with RiBi factors and RPs. Meanwhile, the rRNA is modified, folded, and processed. rRNA cleavage at the A2 site separates the two subunits. Both precursors to the SSU (pre-SSU) and LSU (pre-SSU) travel from the DFC into the GC (Figure 1A) (5). Once the pre-SSU and pre-LSU reach a critical stage of maturation, pre-ribosomes are released into the nucleoplasm, and eventually complete assembly in the cytoplasm (6–8). The mechanisms of nuclear export of assembling subunits from the nucleoplasm into the cytoplasm have been studied in detail (9–13). However, how pre-ribosomes are released from the nucleolus into the nucleoplasm, including what factors enable their release from the nucleolus, remains an open question.

To understand how pre-ribosomes are released from the nucleolus, the biophysical properties of the nucleolus must be considered. The nucleolus is a membraneless organelle formed through the process of liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). Phase-separated membraneless organelles are created and maintained by interactions between the molecules within them. In that process, specific molecules spontaneously segregate away from the general population of biological molecules through thermodynamically advantageous trans interactions (14). The number of trans interactions that single molecules or molecular complexes can form simultaneously is referred to as their ‘valency’. Molecules or complexes that are capable of undergoing numerous simultaneous trans interactions are ‘multivalent’. The probability that a protein, RNA, or molecular complex can undergo LLPS with its interaction partners increases as the number of simultaneous trans interactions they are capable of forming with nearby molecules or complexes of molecules increases. These interactions are diverse in their strength and specificity, ranging from specific and long lasting to weak and transient. Generally, the network of interactions that make up a phase separated organelle influences its biophysical characteristics such as its fluidity, a measure of interaction dynamics and movement of molecules (15–17). The more transient the interactions are within the network, the more fluid and dynamic the phase is, which affects the function of the condensate. If interactions that make up the phase are too strong or permanent, the phase will lose fluidity and aggregate or form a gel.

The connection between the nucleolar stages of ribosome biogenesis and the principles of LLPS are evident upon examining the relationship between ribosome biogenesis and nucleolar morphology. Defects in ribosome biogenesis influence nucleolar structure, and vice versa, alterations of the biophysical characteristics of the nucleolus influence ribosome biogenesis (18–22). For example, both rapid inhibition of rRNA transcription and knockdowns of select RPs cause abnormalities in nucleolar morphology (22,23). Furthermore, when the biophysical properties of the nucleolus are altered using optogenetic protein clustering technology to make the organelle more gel-like, ribosome biogenesis is inhibited (16). Although it is unclear how and why the nucleolar stages of ribosome biogenesis are connected to nucleolar morphology, these results suggest that individual pre-ribosomes assembling in the nucleolus abide by the same principles as any molecule or complex in biological condensates. As such, pre-ribosomes are thought to be retained in the nucleolus by trans interactions with other nucleolar components (Figure 1B).

In the context of ribosome assembly, one major component of pre-ribosomes, rRNA, has been shown to have strong phase separation capabilities. RNA attains multivalency more effectively than proteins, due to the comparatively small binding sites it requires to interact with other molecules and because it often establishes promiscuous RNA–RNA interactions via base pairing (24,25). RNA often regulates and/or facilitates the formation of molecular condensates within cells (25–31).

Proteins can also interact with other proteins and RNAs in numerous ways to facilitate phase separation. They can do this through intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), conformationally heterogeneous protein regions that can interact non-specifically or with low specificity with other molecules (17, 32). Although IDRs are often present in proteins within biological condensates, their function is poorly understood. IDRs may contribute to the valency of a molecule or a complex of molecules by facilitating trans interactions. These domains could also contribute to the fluidity of a phase by competing with abundant, promiscuous RNA–RNA interactions, or they could serve a combination of both functions. Alternatively, IDRs may act as linkers between structured regions of a protein, enabling a highly structured region of a protein to interact in trans. Amino acid composition, post-translational modifications, size, location within a protein sequence, as well as the distribution of charged amino acids are shown to influence the function of IDRs (33–36).

Riback et al. recently suggested that rRNA folding may have a critical influence on the valency and nucleolar localization of pre-ribosomes and that RPs assist in structuring and compacting rRNA when they stabilize onto assembling pre-ribosomes (37). They concluded that the retention of pre-ribosomes with the nucleolus may become less and less favorable as assembly progresses because the compaction of rRNA due to RP stabilization decreases its capacity to interact in trans. However, this is the only experiment thus far to investigate how the valency of pre-ribosomes might decrease to enable their release from the nucleolus, and this experiment was performed in vitro. Thus, this idea needs to be further examined in the context of eukaryotic ribosome biogenesis in vivo. It is also important to consider the other protein components of pre-ribosomes, specifically the RiBi factors.

Work presented here provides a comprehensive model for how the interconnected functions of rRNA, RiBi factors and RPs during ribosome biogenesis are involved in the release of pre-ribosomes from the nucleolus. We performed a bioinformatic analysis to investigate the predicted changes in valency of pre-ribosomes throughout assembly and provide the first examination of the differences between nucleolar and post-nucleolar assembly intermediates in terms of theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes as a complex. To do this, we took advantage of the numerous cryo-EM models of ribosome biogenesis intermediates undergoing the nucleolar and post nucleolar stages of assembly (7, 8,38–51). We first analyzed 35 of these models to document the extent of rRNA compaction associated with each cryo-EM model (Table 1). Next, we created a dataset for predicted IDRs (pIDRs) within all known RiBi factors and RPs in S. cerevisiae. We assessed the characteristics of these pIDRs relative to all pIDRs within the yeast proteome. We then analyzed the cryo-EM models to document the number of pIDRs within RPs and RiBi factors associated with each structure that could potentially interact in trans (Table 1). Finally, we analyzed the protein sequences of RiBi factors and RPs of other species for pIDRs. Our results clearly show that the theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes decreases from the nucleolar to the post-nucleolar stages of assembly in yeast. This decrease in theoretical valency is primarily due to the release of pIDR-enriched RiBi factors, as well as the compaction and folding of rRNA, which is supported by the stabilization of RPs that structure rRNAs into their mature conformation.

Materials and methods

Datasets

All protein sequences from the S. cerevisiae proteome were retrieved from the Saccharomyces Genome Database (52). The amino acid sequences of Homo sapiens, Escherichia coli and Arabidopsis thaliana proteins were downloaded from the UNIPROT database (53).

Prediction of intrinsically disordered regions

Intrinsically disordered regions were identified using both IUPred2A and Metapredict (54,55). IUPred2A is a popular disorder prediction software that bases predictions based on sequence conservation and the physicochemical properties of the amino acids. Metapredict is a deep-learning based method of predicting disorder that has been trained on pre-computed consensus scores of eight different disorder prediction softwares.

Outputs were analyzed for regions predicted to be disordered using both tools with a minimum length of 30 amino acids that had a >50% probability of being disordered. The script used to identify pIDRs can be found at (https://github.com/alaperut/RiBo_IDR_Analysis).

Analysis of disorder in protein sequences

The total probability of protein disorder was calculated by first determining the minimum probability of disorder calculated using IUPred2A and Metapredict for the amino acid sequences of each protein (54, 55). The minimum probabilities were then totaled and normalized by the length of the respective protein.

The proportion of pIDRs within each protein sequence was calculated by dividing the total length of all pIDRs within the protein sequence by the length of the protein. The distribution of predicted disorder was determined by dividing each protein sequence into quartiles and calculating the proportion of predicted disorder within each quartile. This was calculated by dividing the number of amino acids within each quartile within pIDRs by the total size of the quartile. The net charge of pIDRs was calculated by totaling the charge of each amino acid at pH 7. Calculations accounted for the presence of an alpha-amino group or alpha-carboxyl group.

Identification of mixed charge regions

The sequences of all pIDRs were analyzed for the presence of MCRs using a script modified from Greig et al. (https://github.com/alaperut/RiBo_IDR_Analysis) (33). This modification allows for user input of FASTA files and variable specifications. MCRs were identified using the following specifications: the cutoff score for the fraction of charged residues to be considered an MCR must meet or exceed 0.7, the window size examined was set to 30, and minimum cutoff for kappa values was set to zero. The kappa values were recorded and used for later statistical analysis. A value of one indicates complete segregation of the positively changed amino acids from the negatively charged amino acids, while zero indicates complete interspersal of positive and negatively charged amino acids.

Statistical analysis of protein and IDR characteristics

Comparisons between two sets of data were conducted using a single factor ANOVA analysis followed by a two-tailed t-test. Statistical analyses comparing three or more datasets were conducted using single factor ANOVA analysis followed by a Tukey–Kramer test. A weighted linear regression analysis was performed on the data analyzed in Figure 7A–C. The minimum value required to determine significance was P < 0.05. More stringent cutoffs of P < 0.01 and P < 0.001 are indicated.

Structural analysis of PDB files

All structures were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) (56). The scripts used to annotate PyMOL files can be found here (https://github.com/alaperut/RiBo_IDR_Analysis). pIDRs were manually analyzed for structure within PyMOL for the presence of visualized amino acid sequences. The pIDR start sites and end sites were adjusted to eliminate amino acids that were modeled in the structures. pIDRs that require adjustment of the start or end sites were eliminated if the non-visualized region was shorter than 30 amino acids in length.

Selection of rRNA and snoRNAs within the cryo-EM models was extracted as .cif files. The visualized sequences were identified and were then used to determine non-visualized regions of rRNA using this script (https://github.com/TrainerRed151/PyMOL-AtomCharacterization?fbclid=IwAR3ysARRtDVgFm22LHIUaDahqP41xTkbqPGvYynxQtmIJYJyaiNA3gzdElA). The ratio of non-visualized nucleotides was calculated by dividing the number of the non-visualized nucleotides by the total number of nucleotides associated with the particles. The 5′ETS within SSU precursors that are associated with the exosome or are reported to have undergone cleavage at the A1 site (6LQV/Enp1 State C1, 6LQS/Enp1 State D, 6LQT/ENP1 State E, 6LQU/Enp1 State A1, 6ZQD/State Post-A1, 6ZQE/State Dis-A, 6ZQF/State Dis-B and 6ZQG/State Dis-C) were given careful consideration. In the analysis of these structures, regions of the 5′ETS that are not visualized were excluded from contributing to the total rRNA and the total of flexible rRNA nucleotides, because these regions of the 5′ETS may have been excised.

Statistical analysis of nucleolar and post-nucleolar cryo-EM models was performed in the manner described previously. Data collected from cryo-EM structures that are known nucleolar ribosome biogenesis intermediates were compared to data collected from cryo-EM models of known nucleoplasmic or cytoplasmic ribosome biogenesis intermediates. Data collected from cryo-EM models wherein the location of the intermediates is not known were not statistically analyzed.

Overall percent disorder of all proteins within each structure was calculated by dividing the total amino acid (aa) content within each non-visualized pIDR within a structure by the total amino acid content of all proteins within the structure.

Homology of pIDRs

The H. sapiens homologs ofS. cerevisiae RiBi factors and RPs have been identified previously. Statistical analysis of homologous pairs of proteins was performed using two-tailed, paired t tests.

Sequences of protein pairs were visualized using Jalview and were aligned using the T-Coffee server. The conservation of predicted disorder was confirmed if the pIDR(s) of each protein were predicted to be in the same location in the protein with a minimum overlap of one amino acid.

Results

A vast majority of rRNA compacts during nucleolar stages of ribosome biogenesis

To determine how nucleolar pre-ribosomes differ from post-nucleolar pre-ribosomes, we took advantage of available cryo-EM models. We examined 35 cryo-EM structures of ribosome assembly intermediates (Table 1) (7,8,38–51). These structures represent pre-ribosomes from the pre-LSU and pre-SSU at various stages of their assembly, from the nucleolus where the pathway begins, to the cytoplasm where ribosome biogenesis concludes. To assess the contribution of rRNA to the theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes and build on the idea presented by Riback et al., we analyzed these cryo-EM structures for non-compacted, non-visualized regions of rRNA (37). These regions could possibly facilitate trans interactions, while rRNA that has stably compacted into the body of pre-ribosomes is unlikely to do so. RNAs are flexible at helical junctions and other non-base paired regions (57). Flexible rRNA, which would not be visualized by cryo-EM, might arise due to rotation about helical junctions linking that portion of the rRNA to the remaining rRNA that is already stably folded within the body of a pre-ribosome. Of course, stable structures might exist within the non-compacted rRNA that cannot be resolved (47). Therefore, such measurements of rRNA structure serve as an indirect metric for the degree of rRNA compaction rather than rRNA flexibility or conformational heterogeneity. Our analysis showed that the amount of non-compacted rRNA associated with precursors of the LSU and SSU is significantly higher in nucleolar intermediates compared to post-nucleolar intermediates (Figure 2A and B). Indeed, by the time pre-ribosomes are released from the nucleolus, a majority of the rRNAs are compacted to form a stable structure that could be visualized by cryo-EM.

Since large RNAs phase separate more readily than small RNAs, we wanted to assess the number of large, non-compacted rRNA regions in pre-ribosomes that we defined as non-visualized regions of rRNA exceeding 100 nt in length (27,58,59). In addition, we determined the average size of these rRNA regions. We found that the nucleolar pre-SSU intermediates contain at least three of these large, non-compacted regions, while cytoplasmic intermediates contain none (Figure 2C). Likewise, the nucleolar LSU precursors are enriched in large regions of non-compacted rRNA compared to their more mature, post-nucleolar assembly intermediates (Figure 2D). Furthermore, non-compacted rRNAs in the nucleolar precursors of both subunits are significantly lengthier than those associated with post-nucleolar intermediates (Figure 2C and D). Therefore, release of pre-ribosomes from the nucleolus appears be enabled by compaction of rRNA at late nucleolar steps to significantly reduce the valency and strength of trans interactions that retain pre-ribosomes in the nucleolus, in agreement with the findings by Riback et al. (37).

The majority of pIDRs that may be involved in ribosome biogenesis are present within RiBi factors

To further assess the qualities that differentiate nucleolar assembly intermediates from post-nucleolar ones, we analyzed the proteome of S. cerevisiae for predicted disorder and compared these data to the probability of disorder within the 206 RiBi factors and 79 RPs involved in the ribosome biogenesis pathway. We found that RiBi factors have a higher overall probability of disorder relative to the general population of proteins in S. cerevisiae (Figure 3A).

We then examined all of the protein sequences for the presence of large IDRs (≥30 amino acids in length). We identified 2867 such pIDRs across the yeast proteome, 169 of these pIDRs are within RiBi factors, and four of these pIDRs are in RPs. Although RiBi factors and RPs are predicted to have a similar amount of overall disorder within their sequences, RiBi factors were found to contain significantly more pIDRs within their sequences, and pIDRs make up a greater proportion of amino acids in their sequences (Figure 3A–C). As the size of IDRs has been shown to influence their function, we next analyzed the sizes of the pIDRs identified in this analysis (33,60). We found that there is not a significant difference in the size of pIDRs between the proteome, RiBi factors, and RPs (Figure 3D). We observed no correlation between protein size and the size of pIDRs (Supplemental Figure S1a). However, large proteins were found to contain significantly more pIDRs than smaller proteins (Supplemental Figure S1b). RiBi factors are significantly larger than RPs, which may partially explain why they contain more pIDRs. 110/206 (53.4%) of the RiBi factors contain at least one pIDR while only 4/79 (5.0%) of RPs contain a single pIDR. It remains unclear whether the low number of pIDRs within RP sequences is solely due to their small size, or if this is related to their function.

Although there are fewer RPs associated with the SSU than the LSU (33 compared to 46), there are slightly more RiBi factors known to function in the assembly of the SSU compared to the LSU (107 compared to 91). However, we found that the assembly pathways of the LSU and SSU contain a similar number of pIDRs within their associated proteins. 89/173 (51.5%) of pIDRs are within proteins that function in the SSU assembly pathway, and 77/173 (44.5%) of pIDRs are present in proteins that are associated with LSU assembly. The remainder of pIDRs (7/173, 4.0%) are located within RiBi factors known to function in the biogenesis of both subunits.

RiBi factors contain the most disorder at their N termini

Often, the N and C termini of proteins contain more disorder than the protein core (61,62). To determine if such a trend persists in the sequences of RiBi factors, we assessed where pIDRs are located within protein sequences by analyzing the proportion of amino acids within pIDRs in each quartile of the protein sequences (Figure 3E). We found that over two-thirds of all residues predicted to be within pIDRs are proximal to either the N or C termini (37.1% near the N-terminus and 30.1% near the C-terminus), more than the two internal quartiles of the protein sequences (Figure 3E). Furthermore, the N termini of RiBi factors contain significantly more residues predicted to be within pIDRs than the two internal quartiles of the protein sequences (Figure 3E). IDRs near the N or C terminus of a protein may be able to interact in trans more easily, as they are less likely to be anchored onto the core of the pre-ribosome by a structured domain on either side. Alternatively, IDRs bounded by structured domains on either side may act as linkers that allow structured regions to interact in trans rather than the IDRs themselves.

pIDRs within RiBi factors are enriched in charged amino acids and mixed charge regions

Numerous studies indicate that the amino acid composition of IDRs influences their function by altering their cellular localization and interaction partners (33,63). We found that pIDRs in RiBi factors are enriched in lysine, glutamic acid, and aspartic acid, relative to all pIDRs within the yeast proteome (Figure 4A). pIDRs within RiBi factor sequences are primarily composed of lysine, glutamic acid, aspartic acid, and serine, which account for ∼50% of the amino acids within the sequences on average (Figure 4A). Since the pIDRs within RiBi factors are enriched in negatively charged amino acids, it follows that they have a significantly more negative net charge than the average pIDR within the S. cerevisiae proteome (Figure 4B).

Mixed charged regions (MCRs), motifs of amino acids enriched in positively and/or negatively charged amino acids within IDRs, can influence the localization and interaction partners of IDRs (33). The kappa value, a description of how segregated the positively charged amino acids are from the negatively charged ones, is used to describe such motifs. A value of one indicates complete segregation of the charged amino acids and zero indicates the complete interspersal of charged amino acids. pIDRs within RiBi factors are enriched in MCRs relative to the pIDRs within the proteome (Figure 4C). A total of 314 MCRs were identified within pIDRs of the proteome, while 72 were identified within pIDRs in RiBi factors. Over half (58/114) of the RiBi factors that contain pIDRs have at least one MCR within their pIDRs. The MCRs identified within the pIDRs of RiBi factors contain low kappa values compared to those found within pIDRs in the yeast proteome (Figure 4D). However, the molecular grammar of how IDR composition influences the function of IDRs remains undetermined (33).

Nucleolar RiBi factors are more abundant than shuttling and post-nucleolar RiBi factors

We further investigated pIDRs within RiBi factors by analyzing the subcellular location where RiBi factors function during ribosome biogenesis. RiBi factors were classified as those that only function in the nucleolus (strictly nucleolar), those that initially bind to pre-ribosomes in the nucleolus and are released in the nucleoplasm and cytoplasm (shuttling), and those that only associate with pre-ribosomes outside of the nucleolus (post-nucleolar). These classifications were based on the available data for each protein's cellular localization, function, and co-immunoprecipitation, as well as cryo-EM data for each RiBi factor. RiBi factors that localize exclusively to the nucleolus, only have defined nucleolar functions, and solely co-immunoprecipitated and/or are only visualized by cryo-EM with other known nucleolar RiBi factors were classified as strictly nucleolar RiBi factors. Nucleolar shuttling RiBi factors have defined functions in both the nucleolus and the nucleoplasm, localize in both the nucleolus and the nucleoplasm, and/or are associated with ribosome assembly intermediates of the nucleolus and the nucleoplasm. The remaining RiBi factors were classified as post-nucleolar RiBi factors.

Over half (64.5%) of the RiBi factors associated with ribosome biogenesis are strictly nucleolar proteins (Figure 5A). An additional 17.5% of RiBi factors are considered to be nucleolar shuttling RiBi factors. The remaining 18% of RiBi factors only associate with post-nucleolar intermediates. We found that a majority of pIDRs are within the sequences of nucleolar RiBi factors (Figure 5B). There is no difference between the size of nucleolar RiBi factor proteins nor the size of pIDRs within their sequences compared to the shuttling or post-nucleolar RiBi factors (Supplemental Figure S2a and b). Furthermore, we found that the nucleolar RiBi factors do not contain significantly more disorder than nucleolar shuttling or post-nucleolar RiBi factors (Figure 5C–E). Because nucleolar RiBi factors are the largest group of RiBi factors and contain the most disorder, a majority of pIDRs associated with the ribosome biogenesis pathway likely function during the nucleolar stages of ribosome biogenesis.

We next assessed how the distribution of disordered amino acids within a protein may differ between the three different classes of RiBi factors. We found that there was no difference in predicted disorder in other regions nor between the amount of predicted disorder at any location within the nucleolar shuttling proteins and the nucleolar or post-nucleolar RiBi factors (Figure 5F, Supplemental Figure S2c and d).

To determine how pIDRs differ between the three classes of RiBi factors, we compared the charge and amino acid composition of pIDRs within the proteins of each group. We found no difference with respect to the net charge of the pIDRs (Supplemental Figure S2e). Furthermore, the amino acid composition of the pIDRs within the different classes of RiBi factors is uniform, with two exceptions: (i) the proportion of serine is low within pIDR sequences of shuttling RiBi factors relative to the other classes; (ii) the pIDRs within post-nucleolar RiBi factors are enriched in asparagine relative to the other RiBi factors (Supplemental Figure S2f).

Finally, on average, all RiBi factors, regardless of class, contained a similar number of MCRs within their disordered regions (Supplemental Figure S2g). Since most pIDRs in the ribosome biogenesis pathway are in nucleolar RiBi factors, a majority of MCRs identified in RiBi factor pIDRs were found within the nucleolar proteins (Supplemental Figure S2h).

Nucleolar pre-ribosomal intermediates are enriched in pIDRs relative to post-nucleolar assembly intermediates

We next assessed the same set of 35 cryo-EM models that we used to analyze rRNA compaction for their pIDR composition by assessing which pIDRs were not visualized in the cryo-EM models (Table 1). Visualization of a pIDR indicates that it likely has a stable structure within that specific assembly intermediate. Since it is less likely that such stable structures interact in trans, those pIDRs were excluded as candidates for trans interactors that could contribute to the theoretical valency of the pre-ribosome (Figure 6A and B).

Importantly, almost half (91/206, 44.1%) of all RiBi factors have never been visualized on a pre-ribosome by cryo-EM. Therefore, the pIDRs within these proteins were excluded from this analysis. Of the 86 remaining pIDRs that we could analyze, 71 are possibly conformationally heterogeneous (i.e. not visualized) in at least one cryo-EM model of an assembly intermediate and, thus, may be capable of interacting in trans. We continued our analysis using only these possibly conformationally heterogeneous pIDRs.

RiBi factors are enriched in pIDRs, and nucleolar assembly intermediates contain more RiBi factors compared to post-nucleolar assembly intermediates (Figure 3B, Supplemental Figure S3). Therefore, it is not surprising that nucleolar intermediates of both subunits are associated with significantly more pIDRs and a higher proportion of disorder within their proteins than their post-nucleolar counterparts (Figure 6C and D). Structures representing pre-ribosomes undergoing the transition from the nucleolus into the nucleoplasm, such as 6YLX/State NE1 and 6YLY/State NE2 for the pre-LSU, have fewer pIDRs associated with them than nucleolar ribosome assembly intermediates but more than post-nucleolar assembly intermediates (Figure 6D). The nucleolar cryo-EM models of the pre-SSU contain more pIDRs than those of the pre-LSU (Figure 6C and D). Overall, nucleolar intermediates are associated with the highest number of pIDRs, and post-nucleolar intermediates contain the fewest pIDRs.

Many disordered regions within RiBi factors are conserved between yeast and humans

74.7% (154/206) of yeast RiBi factors and all yeast RPs have a human homolog. To examine how protein disorder in ribosome biogenesis compares between yeast and humans, we analyzed all of the human homologs of yeast RiBi factors and RPs for pIDRs. We found that human homologs contain significantly more pIDRs within their sequences than yeast proteins and contain a slightly higher overall probability of disorder (Figure 7A and B). This is in agreement with observations that more complex organisms tend to contain a higher proportion of IDR-containing proteins (64). However, the reason for this difference remains undetermined. It may be because human homologs are generally larger than the yeast proteins, and therefore more likely to contain IDRs (Figure 7C). To address this, we divided the homologous protein pairs into three groups: those where the human homolog contains fewer (34/233), the same (150/233) or more (49/233) pIDRs than the yeast homolog. We found that human proteins that contain more pIDRs than their yeast homologs are not significantly larger than the yeast homolog (Figure 7D). We next assessed how the proportion of pIDRs within the amino acid sequences of yeast and human homologs differs between pairs where the human homolog contains fewer, equivalent, or more pIDRs relative to the S. cerevisiae protein. We found that human proteins that contained more pIDRs also have a significantly higher proportion of predicted disorder in their sequence than their yeast homologs, while those that have equivalent or fewer pIDRs than the yeast homologs have a significantly lower proportion of disorder in their sequences (Figure 7E). Taken together, these data indicate that that some human RiBi factors have acquired additional pIDRs.

We next analyzed conservation of the location of pIDRs within yeast and human RiBi and RP sequences. 38/176 of pIDRs are within RiBi factors that have no human homolog. IDR analysis and sequence alignment of yeast proteins containing the remaining 138 pIDRs to their human homologs revealed that, in 56.9% (78/137) of cases, the human homolog contains a pIDR in the same location within its sequence as the yeast counterpart (Supplemental Table S1). The conservation of the locations of many pIDRs between these homologs indicates that these protein regions may serve important, fundamental functions in eukaryotic ribosome biogenesis.

RiBi factors from the prokaryotic and organellar ribosome assembly pathways contain few pIDRs

Because ribosome assembly is a fundamental cellular process, we analyzed the protein sequences of RPs and RiBi factors that function in prokaryotic and organellar ribosome biogenesis pathways for pIDRs: E. coli, S. cerevisiae mitochondria, H. sapiens mitochondria, and A. thaliana chloroplasts. These ribosomes contain similar numbers of RPs compared to S. cerevisiae (65–67). However, these ribosome biogenesis pathways are associated with fewer documented RiBi factors (68–70). We found that the RPs of prokaryotic and organellar assembly pathways contain a low number of pIDRs, similar to yeast RPs (Supplemental Figure S4a). Furthermore, we found that the prokaryotic and organellar RiBi factors have fewer pIDRs within their protein sequences than the yeast RiBi factors (Supplemental Figure S4b). Additionally, we observed that the RPs and RiBi factors that function in S. cerevisiae ribosome biogenesis tend to contain a higher total probability of disorder than proteins that function in the assembly of E. coli, mitochondria, or chloroplast ribosomes (Supplemental Figure S4c and d). Thus, it appears that pIDRs may serve a more prominent role in eukaryotic ribosome biogenesis pathways.

Discussion

Although the nucleolus is the largest and best-known molecular condensate in the cell, it remains unclear how nucleolar morphology relates to its major function, facilitating ribosome biogenesis. What features of pre-ribosomes retain them in the nucleolus and how are those features altered by the mechanisms of ribosome biogenesis to enable their release? Previously, an in vitro experiment indicated that the valency of pre-ribosomes decreases as ribosome maturation proceeds due to the folding of rRNA and the binding of RPs (37). Here, we expand on this idea by using bioinformatic analysis to connect concepts of LLPS with the mechanisms of ribosome assembly in more detail (4,14). We analyzed cryo-EM structures of eukaryotic ribosome assembly intermediates undergoing nucleolar and post-nucleolar stages of maturation in vivo. Nucleolar pre-ribosomes contain stretches of non-compacted pre-rRNA and are associated with numerous nucleolar RiBi factors that are enriched in pIDRs. Both the non-compacted pre-rRNA of nucleolar pre-ribosomes and the flexible pIDRs may undergo trans interactions with other nucleolar components, thus creating and maintaining nucleolar architecture. Specific changes in the composition of pre-ribosomes causes them to be released from the nucleolus: exit of a large group of RiBi factors that contain pIDRs, stabilization of RPs, and folding of RNA all decrease the theoretical valency of individual pre-ribosomes, which increases the probability that they will release from the nucleolus.

Our structural analysis of the features of nucleolar and post-nucleolar ribosome assembly intermediates allowed us to analyze the ribosome biogenesis pathway in terms of theoretical valency. From these data, we infer six general principles relating ribosome assembly mechanisms to the phase separation of assembling pre-ribosomal subunits. (i) RiBi factors are more likely to contain flexible pIDRs than RPs and may support the retention of pre-ribosomes in the nucleolus via these pIDRs. Thus, the release of pIDR-containing RiBi factors from the pre-ribosome decreases the theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes. For example, the release of Bud21, Nop1, Rrp5, Rrp9, Utp14, Upt18, Utp5, Utp7 and Utp9 (all of which contain pIDRs) between 6ZQE/State Disc-B and 6ZQF/State Disc-C during SSU maturation may decrease the valency of the pre-SSU (Figure 5B). (ii) Flexible pIDRs can stabilize onto the pre-ribosome during assembly and thus no longer contribute to the theoretical valency of the particle (see below). (iii) Non-compacted rRNA may be able to interact in trans directly and thus maintain interactions within the nucleolus (as suggested by Riback et al.) (37). Additionally, the processing of pre-rRNA during ribosome biogenesis, such as the removal of 5′ETS and ITS1 sequences likely decreases the number of trans interactions pre-rRNA can attain within the nucleolus by removing pre-rRNA nucleotides from the pre-ribosome. Alternatively, non-compacted rRNA may support the trans interactions of proteins associated with that rRNA. As rRNA compacts, trans interactions are lost. For example, rRNA domain III of the LSU is not compacted until pre-LSUs reach 6EM5/Nsa1 State D, and may support trans interactions (48). (iv) RPs and RiBi factors bind to rRNA at flexible helical junctions as they assemble onto the pre-ribosome, thereby stabilizing the rRNA into on-pathway conformations. For instance, eS4 stabilizes rRNA h16, h17 and h18 as it is accommodated into the pre-SSU by interacting with the helical junction connecting them. Therefore, the binding of eS4 supports the formation of mature, compacted rRNA structures (49,57,71–73). Furthermore, the binding of RiBi factors can support the formation of mature rRNA structures during downstream assembly events by binding to helical junctions, which prevents rRNA from forming kinetic traps (74). rRNA that is kinetically trapped may not be able to fold properly. Consequently, trans interactions that are normally lost due to rRNA folding would be maintained. (v) The presence of RiBi factors can sterically hinder the rearrangement of rRNA structures such that rRNA compaction can only occur once these RiBi factors are released. For example, the Kre33 module is bound to the 5′ domain of 18S rRNA within the pre-SSU during the early nucleolar stages of ribosome biogenesis. Following rRNA cleavage at the A1 site, the Kre33 module dissociates, which allows the 5′ domain of the 18S rRNA to compact and form a mature-like conformation with the central domain (44). (vi) The binding of late-associating RPs can facilitate the release of RiBi factors. For example, the association eL43 supports the release of the RiBi factors Loc1 and Puf6 (75). In our model, the theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes decreases throughout assembly due to all of these aforementioned mechanisms. It is clear from our structural analysis that the theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes decreases dramatically prior to the release of pre-ribosomes from the nucleolus (Figures 2, 6C and D). This is due to the release of whole groups of RiBi factors and the stabilization of rRNA. This suggests that there may be key steps in ribosome biogenesis during the late nucleolar stages of ribosome biogenesis that enable the release of pre-ribosomes from the nucleolus. When these steps are completed, the theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes drops below a critical threshold and, therefore, the probability of their release from the phase increases dramatically (Figure 8A) (37).

Pre-SSUs are thought to reach the threshold for release from the nucleolus at a point following A2 cleavage, during the processing of the 5′ETS, but little is known about the point in ribosome biogenesis when they are released from the nucleolus (Figure 1A) (5,76). More is known about the stage in ribosome biogenesis where pre-LSUs are released from the nucleolus. We found that mutants that inhibit the release of three specific RiBi factors, Nip7, Nop2 and Spb1, also inhibit the release of pre-LSUs from the nucleolus, which suggests that this is the key step that enables the nucleolar release of pre-LSUs (Figure 8B) (manuscript in preparation). In the late nucleolar stages of ribosome biogenesis, the RiBi factors Nip7 and Nop2 bind to the junction between rRNA H74 and H75 and prevent the pre-mature compaction of a majority of rRNA domain V, while Spb1 inhibits the stabilization of many rRNA helices of domain IV (Figure 8b). The release of these three RiBi factors coincides with compaction of rRNA domains IV and V and the stabilization of RPs uL2 and eL43. Furthermore, two pIDRs stabilize onto pre-LSUs following the release of Nip7, Nop2, and Spb1. rRNA H79ES31 co-stabilizes with the pIDR of eL8, and the pIDR within the C-terminus of Nog1 stabilizes within the nascent polypeptide exit tunnel (nPET), where it supports the proper formation of the nPET (Figure 8B) (47,50,77).

It is likely that the information presented in this manuscript underestimates the potential contribution of proteins to the theoretical valency of pre-ribosomes. We were not able to include a significant fraction of pIDRs in our structural analysis due to the lack of structural data for half of the proteins containing pIDRs. Furthermore, many other types of trans interactions that proteins can undergo were not explored in this analysis and could theoretically contribute to the valency of pre-ribosomes, such as RNA recognition motifs, dsRNA binding domains, and structured protein–protein interaction motifs. Additionally, although we have shown that RPs generally contain few pIDRs, RPs aggregate in the absence of rRNA and thus may be able to attain multivalency through other means (78). Finally, post-translational modifications can regulate the function of IDRs (34,35,79). It is likely that the function of some of the pIDRs analyzed in this manuscript are modulated in this way. Future studies are needed to examine whether individual pIDRs identified in this study function as predicted by our bioinformatic analysis. Some pIDRs may serve other functions, such as supporting nucleolar fluidity, as previously reported (80).

The data collected from cryo-EM structures about the contribution of rRNA to the valency of pre-ribosomes likely overestimates the amount of rRNA capable of interacting in trans with other RNAs and proteins within the nucleolus. rRNA that is not visualized by cryo-EM may have attained stable, cis interactions with itself or interactions with RiBi factors and RPs of the pre-ribosome (2,3,81). However, because these rRNAs are connected to the rest of the pre-ribosome by helical junctions that have not yet attained stable conformations, they are not visualized by cryo-EM (57). Thus, just because rRNA structures are not visualized by cryo-EM does not imply that they are necessarily capable of interacting in trans. Chemical probing of structural intermediates will indicate which helices have not yet attained stable conformations, and are thus capable of base pairing in trans with other rRNAs. However, because proteins are capable of interacting with single-stranded as well as double-stranded rRNAs, it will still be difficult to predict which non-visualized rRNAs are capable of interacting in trans with proteins. Furthermore, non-visualized regions of rRNAs may stably bind to RiBi factors and RPs, some of which may be capable of forming trans interactions themselves. Despite these limitations, our results are consistent with the idea that pre-ribosomes become more compact as they assemble, which naturally will limit the contribution of rRNA to the valency of pre-ribosomes (37).

Our bioinformatic analysis suggests that RiBi factors function in the nucleolar retention and release of pre-ribosomes. Consistent with this, we found that the amount of disorder associated with ribosome biogenesis increases with organism complexity and that many RPs and RiBi factor proteins contain pIDRs in the same location. We hypothesize that the additional pIDRs in some human RiBi factors and RPs are related to other functions of the human homologs that have evolved over time. The nucleoli of humans and yeast are vastly different environments; human nucleoli are much more complex in their content, structure and function than those of yeast. Therefore, the additional pIDRs may be necessary to circumvent the challenges faced by human ribosomes in the nucleolus.

Intriguingly, even though E. coli, mitochondria and chloroplasts do not contain nucleoli, there is some evidence that their ribosome assembly pathways also begin in phase-separated compartments (82–84). We found few pIDRs in prokaryotic and organellar ribosome assembly pathways, and their composition of pIDRs differs from that of eukaryotic ribosome biogenesis. However, their rRNA folding pathways are thought to be similar to those of eukaryotes (85). Thus, this indicates that pre-ribosomes in these organisms might undergo phase separation primarily due to non-compacted rRNA, which folds as RPs bind. In contrast, in eukaryotic ribosome biogenesis pathways, RiBi factors have a more prominent role in regulating rRNA folding and RP binding. However, the role of IDRs in prokaryotic and organellar ribosomes biogenesis may become more prevalent as new RiBi factors in these pathways are discovered.

This work suggests that both trans-interacting rRNA and IDRs function in ribosome biogenesis pathways in eukaryotes. Not all IDRs of proteins are sufficient to drive phase separation. The association of IDR-containing proteins with RNA significantly promotes LLPS (86). Thus, it is tempting to propose that non-stabilized rRNA likely serves a more significant role in directly facilitating phase transitions of pre-ribosomes, due to its multivalent nature (87). This leads to the question: What is the precise function of individual IDRs in ribosome biogenesis? How important is their function? IDRs may simply be an additional feature that retains pre-ribosomes in the nucleolus by contributing to their valency. Alternatively, some IDRs may have more specialized functions. For example, because so many pIDRs within RiBi factors are enriched in glutamic and aspartic acid, some of these IDRs might compete with the numerous and promiscuous trans rRNA interactions within the nucleolus. Since prolonged trans interactions of rRNA with other nucleolar components may inhibit rRNA stabilization, IDRs may be important for moving ribosome assembly forward by disrupting such interactions.

The models and concepts discussed in this manuscript raise many questions. Why does ribosome biogenesis take place in the nucleolus? Is it only to concentrate the components into one location? What function could trans interactions serve during ribosome biogenesis? Could trans interactions between pre-ribosomes and other components of the nucleolus contribute to the efficiency and/or accuracy of ribosome biogenesis? Trans interactions might be important for trafficking of nucleolar proteins into the nucleolus to assemble into pre-ribosomes, and to allow efficient radial diffusion of nascent ribosomes out of the nucleolus. Precisely which trans interactions are most important for enabling the retention of pre-ribosomes within the nucleolus? Finally, how do these principles relate to disease pathologies that alter the architecture of the nucleolus and inhibit ribosome biogenesis, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) in humans (15)? Ultimately, by investigating the ribosome biogenesis pathway in the context of the phase-separated nucleolar environment, we will develop novel insights into both the mechanisms of ribosome biogenesis as well as how this essential process influences, and is regulated by, the other functions of the nucleolus.
