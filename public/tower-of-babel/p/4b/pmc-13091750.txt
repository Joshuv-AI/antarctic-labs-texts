Fiber-degrading enzymes improved egg production, quality, and apparent retention of components in post-peak Lohmann Brown-Classic laying hens fed a high-fiber diet
Authors: Felix M. Njeri, Ahmed M. Amerah, Fernando Bravo de Laguna Ortega, Adebayo O. Sokale, Elijah G. Kiarie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dose response of fiber-degrading enzymes (FDE) on egg production, quality, and apparent retention (AR) of components was investigated in 48-week-old Lohmann Brown-Classic hens housed in enriched cages (4 hens/cage). A corn-soybean meal basal diet was formulated with 19 % wheat middlings and fed without or with 50, 100, or 200 g FDE/ton. The FDE was a blend of endo-1,4-β-xylanase and endo-1,4-β-glucanase (Natugrain® TS), at 5,600 and 2,500 U/g, respectively. The mash diets contained titanium dioxide (0.25 %) as an indigestible marker and phytase at 2,000 FTU/kg and were allocated in a completely randomized design (n = 10). Birds had free access to feed and water for 16 weeks. Hen-day egg production (HDEP), feed intake (FI), egg weight (EW), egg mass (EM), feed conversion ratio (FCR, FI/EM), eggshell thickness (EST), breaking strength (EBS), Haugh unit (HU), albumen height (AH), and yolk color (YC) were reported at 4-week periods. Excreta samples for AR determination were collected at 4 and 12 weeks. The FDE increased HDEP non-linearly (P < 0.001) with the highest value at FDE50. There were interactions (P < 0.01) between diet and period on EW; overall EW declined linearly and quadratically in response to FDE (P < 0.001). The EM and FCR exhibited a quadratic response to FDE (P ≤ 0.025), with FDE50 and FDE100 yielding the best outcomes. There were interactions (P < 0.01) between diet and period on egg quality traits; however, the overall FDE response was linear and non-linear. The EST peaked at FDE50, whereas HU and AH peaked at FDE100, and YC at FDE200. The AR of NDF increased (P < 0.001) linearly in response to FDE, whereas the AR of gross energy increased (P < 0.001) linearly and quadratically in response to FDE. Overall, supplementation with 50 to 100 g FDE/ton improved egg production, egg quality, and nutrient retention in post-peak laying hens fed a high-fiber diet.

Introduction

Feeding the human population that is projected to exceed 9 billion by 2050 demands more efficient and sustainable animal production systems (Dijk et al., 2021). As a key component of this system, egg production offers a cost-effective source of high-quality protein. A primary strategy for improving productivity and reducing environmental impact in egg production is extending the laying period in a single cycle, minimizing flock replacement, and resource use (Bain et al., 2016). However, hens entering the mid-to-late stages of production often exhibit physiological and metabolic challenges that compromise performance, egg quality, and health (Kiarie et al., 2025).

Lohmann Brown-Classic laying hens, one of the most widely used commercial breeds, typically reach peak production around 30 to 35 weeks of age (WOA) (Lohmann, 2021). Although these modern birds have genetic capacity for attaining a single-cycle production through to 100 WOA, age-related physiological changes, including decreased digestive enzyme secretion, impaired intestinal morphology, and reduced calcium absorption, can negatively impact nutrient utilization (Hanlon et al., 2022; Sinclair-Black et al., 2023). These changes lead to thinner eggshells, increased egg breakage, decreased albumen quality, and reduced overall laying performance. Moreover, aging hens are more susceptible to metabolic disorders such as Fatty Liver Hemorrhagic Syndrome (FLHS), often triggered by excessive energy intake, poor nutrient partitioning, and limited physical activity (Robinson and Kiarie, 2019; Choi et al., 2012; Li et al., 2023). Nutritional strategies that enhance feed efficiency and support metabolic function are thus essential for sustaining productivity and animal welfare in later production stages.

Dietary fiber plays a complex role in poultry nutrition. On one hand, fiber, particularly insoluble and fermentable types, can improve gut health by promoting gastrointestinal motility and serving as a substrate for microbial fermentation (Amerah et al., 2009; Leung et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2024). The fermentation of fiber by gut microbiota produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) such as butyrate and propionate, which play key roles in regulating lipid metabolism, reducing hepatic fat synthesis, and enhancing fat oxidation (Han et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021). These effects contribute to a leaner body composition and improved liver health, thereby mitigating the risk of FLHS and supporting better metabolic balance (Han et al., 2023). On the other hand, fiber-rich diets also contain non-starch polysaccharides (NSP), such as arabinoxylans and β-glucans, which can increase digesta viscosity and interfere with nutrient digestion and absorption, particularly in older hens with diminished digestive capacity (Sanchez et al., 2021; Njeri et al., 2023; Rho and Kiarie, 2026).

Fiber-degrading enzymes (FDE) such as xylanase and β-glucanase have been shown to mitigate the antinutritional effects of NSP by hydrolyzing complex polysaccharides, thereby improving nutrient release (Kiarie et al., 2014; Rho et al., 2020; Njeri et al., 2023). This not only enhances feed utilization but also supports better egg quality and gut health. Enzyme action also facilitates more effective fermentation of dietary fiber, resulting in beneficial shifts in gut microbiota composition. Studies have reported that xylanase supplementation improves protein retention and internal egg quality while also promoting microbial populations that contribute to lipid metabolism and immune function (Kiarie et al., 2013; Nguyen et al., 2021; Morgan et al., 2022). Despite these known benefits, limited data exist on the optimal inclusion levels of xylanase and β-glucanase in high-fiber diets for post-peak Lohmann Brown-Classic hens. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the effects of feeding a high fiber diet without or with different units of xylanase and β-glucanase on laying performance, egg quality, and apparent retention of dietary components in Lohmann Brown-Classic laying hens from 48 to 64 WOA.

Materials and methods

The use of animals and experimental protocol was approved by the University of Guelph Animal Care Committee, and birds were cared for following the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines (CCAC, 2009).

Birds and housing

A total of 160 Lohmann Brown-Classic layers hens were selected from the general production flock at the Arkell Poultry Research Station in Guelph, Ontario, Canada. These birds were initially obtained as day-old chicks from Archer’s Hatchery in Brighton, Ontario, and were raised following the guidelines outlined in the Lohmann management guide (Lohmann, 2021). Upon transfer from the general flock, birds underwent a 7-day adaptation period in the experimental facility to acclimate to the enriched cage system prior to allocation of experimental diets. The hens were at 48 WOA at the start of the study and were housed in enriched cages (4 birds/cage) (FDI Poultry Equipment, Mitchell, Ontario, Canada). Each cage measured 61 cm in width, 66 cm in depth, and 46 cm in height and was equipped with a perch, scratch pad, feeder, and two nipple drinkers (Kiarie et al., 2025). Environmental conditions in the room were maintained at 18–20°C, with 16 h of incandescent lighting at 15 lux.

Experimental diets

A basal corn, soybean meal, and wheat middlings (Table 1) was formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements for Lohmann Brown-Classic layers hens (Lohmann, 2021) except for AME which was reduced by 150 kcal/kg. The specifications for feed ingredients were from Evonik AMINODat® 6.0 database. The FDE diets were created by adding 50, 100, and 200 g of product/ton. The product contained 5,600 units of xylanase and 2,500 units of β-glucanase/g (Natugrain® TS) and was substituted for corn in the basal diet. All diets contained phytase at 2,000 FTU/kg (Natuphos® E, 10,000 FTU/g). All enzymes, along with analytical support, were provided by BASF SE, Ludwigshafen, Germany. Additionally, all diets included titanium dioxide as an indigestible marker and were prepared in mash form at the Arkell Research Station Feed Mill. One batch of each treatment diet was produced throughout the study. Diet manufacturing followed a specific sequence: 0, 50, 100, and 200 FDE g/ton, with a flushing step using 500 kg of wheat between treatments containing the enzyme. Experimental procedures and sample collection

The diets were assigned in a completely randomized design (10 replicate cages per diet). The hens had unrestricted access to water and diets for the 16-week experimental period. Daily egg production was recorded at 10:00 h, and feed intake (FI) was measured weekly. At 4-week intervals, eggs collected on Monday were assessed for egg weight (EW), eggshell breaking strength (ESBS) and thickness (EST), Haugh unit (HU), albumen height (AH), and yolk color (YC) using the Egg Tester Ultimate™ (ORKA Food Technology LLC, Utah, USA) (Mwaniki et al., 2018). Fresh excreta samples were collected from Monday to Thursday during the 8th and 12th experimental weeks for the apparent retention (AR) of components (Neijat et al., 2019).

Sample processing and laboratory analyses

Excreta samples were dried at 60°C for four days, then finely ground along with the diet samples for subsequent analysis. All samples were analyzed for dry matter (DM), gross energy (GE), nitrogen (N), neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and titanium content. Diet samples were further analyzed for starch, crude fat, minerals, insoluble, soluble, and total dietary fiber, as well as phytase and xylanase activities. The DM content was determined using the AOAC method 930.15 (AOAC, 2005), while the N content was analyzed using the combustion method based on the AOAC method 2001.11 (AOAC, 2005). The CP was calculated by multiplying the N values by 6.25. A bomb calorimeter (IKA Calorimeter System C 5000; IKA Works, Wilmington, North Carolina, USA) was used to measure GE. The NDF content was analyzed according to Van Soest et al. (1991) using an Ankom 200 Fibre Analyzer (Ankom Technology, Fairport, NY). Titanium content was determined using the method described by Myers et al. (2004). Starch, crude fat, calcium, phosphorus, and sodium analyses were conducted at a commercial laboratory (SGS Canada Inc., Guelph, Ontario, Canada. Insoluble, soluble, and total dietary fiber were analyzed using the Ankom total dietary fiber analyzer (Ankom Technology, Fairport, NY) according to AOAC method 991.43 (AOAC, 2006), as further elaborated by Njeri et al. (2026). The xylanase and phytase activities were measured using ISO method (ISO, 2024). One xylanase unit (TXU) was defined as the quantity of enzyme that produces reducing carbohydrates equivalent to one μmol of xylose per minute under assay conditions of 50.0 ± 0.1°C and pH 3.5. Xylanase was analyzed as the indicator enzyme in the FDE product (Rychen et al., 2016). One phytase unit (FTU) was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 micromole of inorganic phosphorus per minute from 0.0051 mol/L sodium phytate at pH 5.5 and 37°C.

Calculations and statistical analyses

Feed intake (g/bird/day), HDEP (%), EM (g/bird/day), and feed conversion ratio (FCR; FI divided by EM) were computed as per the methods described by Mwaniki et al. (2018). Egg production and quality parameters were reported in four-week periods. The AR of dietary components and nitrogen-corrected values were calculated based on the method described by Kiarie et al. (2014). Data were analyzed using the Proc GLIMMIX in SAS Studio learning Edition (v9.4). The cage served as the experimental unit, and the model included diet, period/sampling time point, and their interactions as fixed factors. To further evaluate early-period responses, weekly feed intake (FI) and hen-day egg production (HDEP) data from the adaptation period and the first four experimental weeks were analyzed and presented graphically (supplementary material 1). Data were analyzed using PROC GLIMMIX in SAS, with treatment, week, and their interaction as fixed effects, and cage as the experimental unit. Pre-planned orthogonal contrasts were used to evaluate specific treatment effects, including comparisons among dietary treatments and responses to factor levels where applicable. Coefficients for FDE response were generated using PROC IML (SAS). When a significant diet × period interaction was detected (P ≤ 0.05), treatment means were separated within period using Tukey’s adjustment to facilitate interpretation of interaction effects. Tukey’s procedure was also applied for separation of period means where appropriate. Significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05 and trends at P ≤ 0.10.

There were interactions between FDE and period (P < 0.001) that influenced EST, ESBS, HU, AH, and YC (Table 4). The FDE increased EST and ESBS linearly and cubically (P < 0.05) in periods 1 and 2, peaking at 50 FDE. However, in period 4, FDE had linear and quadratic effects on eggshell parameters, peaking at FDE100 and declining at FDE200. Overall, the EST and ESBS were greatest for FDE50. The HU increased linearly and non-linearly in period 4, peaking at FDE100. For AH, the response to FDE was cubic in period 1, with the lowest values observed for FDE50 and 200. The FDE had a linear decrease in AH periods 2 and 3. However, FDE increased AH linearly and non-linearly in period 4, peaking at FDE100. Discussion

The present study demonstrated that supplementing a high‑fiber diet with FDE, a blend of xylanase and β-glucanase, improved egg production, egg quality, and nutrient utilization in post‑peak Lohmann Brown‑Classic hens. These enhancements occurred despite age‑related physiological declines in digestive function typically expected between 48 and 64 WOA, indicating that exogenous carbohydrases can mitigate reductions in digestive capacity and prolong productivity during the late laying phase. Notably, the apparent retention of gross energy showed clear linear and quadratic improvements with increasing FDE dose, confirming that enhanced energy extraction is the primary mechanism driving the observed performance benefits.

The recovery of phytase and xylanase in the experimental diets exceeded the intended inclusion levels, likely due to endogenous enzymes present in ingredients such as wheat middlings. Wheat and its by-products contain significant levels of endogenous phytase and xylanase activity, which remain active in feed ingredients unless heat-treated (Amerah, 2015). The observed improvements in hen productivity are attributed to the enzyme's ability to degrade the NSP, such as arabinoxylans and β-glucans, which are abundant in cereal-based feed ingredients (Abdollahi et al., 2021; Pirgozliev et al., 2010). These NSP increase intestinal viscosity, impairing nutrient digestion and absorption (Njeri, et al., 2023). By hydrolyzing NSP, enzyme supplementation enhances nutrient uptake and energy utilization even in already optimized diets (Kiarie et al., 2014). Beyond viscosity reduction, xylanase-driven hydrolysis of arabinoxylans increases the generation of xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS), which can modulate cecal microbial activity and fermentation, thereby contributing to improved nutrient retention and sustained productive performance (Kiarie et al., 2013; 2017). Recent work in laying hens demonstrated that this microbiota-mediated mechanism complements the classical viscosity-reduction pathway and helps explain persistent responses to enzyme supplementation over time (Morgan et al., 2022). However, non-linear responses, in which performance initially improves with increasing enzyme inclusion but eventually plateaus or declines, reflect biological thresholds or nutrient-interaction limits.

According to Lohmann Brown Classic breeder guidelines, hens between 48 and 64 WOA are expected to show a decline in HDEP from 92 to 84 %, an increase in EW from 65.5 to 67.5 g, a decrease in EM from 60.2 to 56.8 g, and a FI ranging from 110 to 117 g/bird/day (Lohmann, 2021). In the present study, the HDEP remained consistently above the breeder targets throughout the 16 weeks, while the FI stayed within the breeders’ guidelines. These observations suggest that overall flock performance was stable and not indicative of systemic production depression during the study. However, it is important to acknowledge that egg production data were not recorded prior to dietary allocation. As such, although hens were randomly assigned following a standardized adaptation period, baseline equivalence among treatment groups cannot be empirically verified. This represents a limitation, particularly in mid-lay hens, where production dynamics are sensitive to prior history and physiological status. Consequently, early-period responses should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Supplementary analysis of weekly HDEP and FI during the early experimental period (Supplementary Fig. S1–S2) indicates that treatment differences were transient and not associated with a consistent divergence in production trajectories among treatments, supporting cautious interpretation of early-period responses.

Birds fed FDE showed reduced egg weight compared to the control group. This apparent divergence between improved feed efficiency and relatively stable egg weight likely reflects nutrient partitioning dynamics in mid-cycle layers, where additional absorbed nutrients are preferentially allocated to maintenance and sustaining the ovulatory rate rather than increasing individual egg mass. Such allocation patterns have been described in extended laying-cycle systems, where metabolic priorities favor production persistence over egg-size expansion (Bain et al., 2016). Typically, egg production peaks at around 30 WOA, after which productivity and egg quality gradually decline, making extended laying beyond 70 WOA less profitable (Arulnathan et al., 2024). Furthermore, aging hens face increased susceptibility to health issues such as reproductive disorders, fatty liver, and reduced disease resistance, contributing to higher mortality and economic loss (Alfonso‐carrillo et al., 2021; Bain et al., 2016).

Enzyme supplementation not only supports persistently high HDEP but also contributes to economic and environmental sustainability by improving feed efficiency and hen welfare. A major concern during extended laying cycles is the increase in EW, which can negatively affect skeletal and reproductive health. Heavier eggs require more calcium for shell formation, often mobilized from bones, thereby reducing bone mineral density and increasing the risk of fractures such as keel bone damage (Alfonso‐carrillo et al., 2021). Additionally, oversized eggs may strain the reproductive tract, leading to complications like egg binding or cloacal prolapse (Alfonso‐carrillo et al., 2021). In the present study, FDE supplementation helped to maintain stable EW compared to the initial 0–4 weeks, whereas the control diet led to increased EW and reduced EM. Despite the general trend of declining EM with age, enzyme-supplemented hens maintained stable EM between 48 and 64 WOA, contrary to expectations under breeder guidelines, highlighting a potential benefit of prolonging and sustaining both egg quantity and quality during the late-laying phase.

As indicated earlier, FDE inclusion enhances nutrient digestibility by reducing intestinal viscosity, thereby improving the absorption of critical nutrients, including calcium, phosphorus, amino acids, and carotenoids (Nguyen et al., 2021; Pirgozliev et al., 2010). These effects were time-dependent, reflecting adaptive changes in gut function and nutrient metabolism with age in hens. Temporal variation in enzyme efficacy suggests that nutrient transport mechanisms and gut microbiota composition evolve across the laying cycle, influencing how supplemental enzymes exert their benefits. Dai et al. (2022) reviewed the literature and found that gastrointestinal microorganisms mainly influence egg quality in older laying birds. Gao et al. (2025), confirmed that as hens age, their digestive systems undergo significant changes, including alterations in gut morphology, enzyme activity, and microbiota composition.

As previously noted, high enzyme doses may surpass biological thresholds, potentially disrupting nutrient homeostasis. On the one hand, the observed cubic and quadratic trends in egg quality responses suggested a nonlinear relationship between FDE dose and nutrient bioavailability. This implies that excessive FDE may have altered calcium-phosphorus balance or gut microbiota, resulting in reduced or negative effects. These findings support the hypothesis that enzyme supplementation should be optimized rather than maximized. On the other hand, the declining egg quality parameters, such as HU and albumin height over time, particularly in the absence of enzymes, reflect natural senescence processes, specifically, reduced protein metabolism efficiency, diminished calcium mobilization, and declining intestinal function. This aligns with Gu et al. (2021), who found that as hens aged, hepatic function was altered, including decreased antioxidant enzyme activities and increased oxidative stress markers. These physiological changes further reinforce that the digestive and metabolic processes in hens evolve with age, potentially influencing their response to exogenous enzyme supplementation. The enhanced yolk pigmentation in FDE-fed hens likely arises from improved carotenoid release and absorption, supported by lower gut viscosity. This observation aligns with previous research indicating that xylanase supplementation enhanced yolk pigmentation in Lohmann Brown laying hens (Pirgozliev et al., 2010). The non-linear response observed for eggshell quality suggests that the enzyme inclusion rate requires careful optimization to avoid disproportionate mineral release, which may disrupt calcium utilization and shell deposition. Overall, the mechanisms highlighted point to a complex interplay among enzyme activity, nutrient metabolism, gut health, and age-related physiological shifts, advocating for dynamic, adaptive enzyme feeding strategies tailored to the stage of lay. Moreover, the sustained improvements observed in post-peaks layers fed for over 16 weeks during the present study suggest that enzyme supplementation can mitigate the age-related decline in egg quality typically seen in laying hens.

The improvements in the AR of DM, CP, NDF, fat, and gross energy confirm enhanced nutrient digestibility due to FDE-induced cell wall breakdown, leading to more efficient nutrient release and absorption. These effects exhibit a strong dose-response, with linear and quadratic trends indicating that moderate enzyme levels optimize nutrient retention. At the same time, very high doses may reach a plateau or induce nutrient imbalances. The enhanced AR of CP and fat suggests that FDE facilitates proteolytic and lipolytic activity, likely by improving substrate exposure and enhancing transit conditions in the small intestine. An enhanced fat digestion in laying hens can significantly improve yolk color intensity. This is because carotenoids, the pigments responsible for yolk coloration, are fat-soluble compounds (Tyczkowski et al., 1989; Yao et al., 2023). Efficient fat digestion facilitates the absorption and deposition of these pigments into the egg yolk. In agreement with the present study, Abdollahi et al. (2021) investigated the effects of fiber source and xylanase supplementation on production, egg quality, digestibility, and intestinal morphology in aged laying hens. The researchers found that xylanase supplementation improved AR of components, including DM and CP, and enhanced intestinal morphology. These improvements were more pronounced at moderate levels of enzyme inclusion, supporting the concept of an optimal dosing strategy. The time effects, particularly for AR of CP and crude fat, are age-related impacts on endogenous digestive efficiency (Gu et al., 2021a). The decline in digestive enzyme activity was associated with reduced CP retention, indicating impaired nutrient absorption in older birds. Furthermore, Yuan et al. (2017) demonstrated that supplementation with multi-carbohydrase and proteases increased pancreatic enzyme activities, including trypsin, lipase, and amylase, in broilers. In the present study, the diet and sampling time interactions for NDF and fat retention further hint at evolving gut microflora dynamics, where enzymes not only release nutrients but also modulate microbial populations favorably for fiber fermentation and lipid utilization (Kiarie et al., 2017). Quadratic and cubic trends for fiber and fat retention highlight complex regulatory feedback, where excessive NSP hydrolysis could reduce structural fiber content needed for optimal gut health or trigger shifts in microbial metabolism.

This study demonstrated that supplementation with FDE at 50 to100 g/t in a high-fiber diet improved feed efficiency, selected egg quality parameters, and nutrient retention in post-peak-laying hens. The observed dose-dependent responses indicate that moderate inclusion optimizes nutrient utilization under practical feeding conditions. From a practical perspective, these findings support the strategic use of FDE at 50 to 100 g/t to enhance feed efficiency, stabilize egg quality, and potentially reduce environmental nutrient excretion, while confirming that higher inclusion rates confer no additional performance advantage. Several limitations warrant cautious interpretation. The study was conducted using a single genetic strain (Lohmann Brown-Classic), under enriched cage housing, and with a wheat middlings-based diet during the mid-cycle production period (48–63 WOA). Additionally, the 16-week duration does not fully capture responses across extended laying cycles (up to 100 weeks). Future research should evaluate enzyme efficacy across multiple strains, alternative fiber sources, later production stages, and diverse commercial housing systems. Integration of microbiome, metabolomic, and nutrient partitioning analyses, together with economic modeling, would further support the precision application of FDE technology for sustainable egg production systems.
