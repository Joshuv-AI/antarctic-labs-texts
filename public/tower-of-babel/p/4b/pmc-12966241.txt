Effectiveness of manual interventions on overall symptoms, pain and quality of life in patients with chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ying-xiang Chen, Wen-yan He, You Zhou, Bin-lian Yao, Xiao-lan Zhang, Qin Ye, Min Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe systematic review and meta-analysis aim to thoroughly assess the efficacy of manual interventions like massage therapy, reflexology, and acupressure techniques in relation to overall symptoms, pain, and quality of life (QOL) in patients suffering from CIPN.MethodsA total of eight Chinese and English databases were searched, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, ProQuest, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wan Fang Database. The searching lasted from the establishment of each database to July 2025. The study aims to include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies. The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool. The primary outcome measures were symptoms of CIPN, pain, and QOL. Standard Deviation (SMD) and 95% CI were calculated in a random-effects model. This study was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD420251112391).ResultsIn total, 11 RCTs with a total of 646 patients were included. The meta-analysis showed that manual interventions may improve primary outcomes, including quality of life (SMD = 1.05; 95% CI = 0.60, 1.50; P < 0.00001), sensory (SMD = −0.62; 95% CI = −0.88, −0.35; P < 0.00001), and motor (SMD = −0.42; 95% CI = −0.72, −0.12; P = 0.006) symptoms, with low to moderate levels of heterogeneity. Although the aggregated data for overall CIPN symptoms (SMD = −1.67; 95% CI = −3.03, −0.30; P = 0.02) and pain (SMD = −0.77; 95% CI = −1.31, −0.24; P = 0.006) were statistically significant, they showed high heterogeneity (I2 > 90%), which may be related to the limited number of existing studies and differences in intervention details.ConclusionsLow- to very low–certainty evidence suggests that manual interventions may reduce core symptoms and pain associated with CIPN, and potentially improve sleep quality and quality of life. However, due to high heterogeneity and risk of bias, these findings should be interpreted with caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00520-026-10530-3.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a common side effect observed in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy. The main reason for this side effect is considered to be a number of common chemotherapy drugs, mainly platinum and paclitaxel agents. Severe neuropathy has even been reported in as many as 16.9 to 89.4% cancer patients [1]. The symptoms associated with this neuropathy are multifaceted and can specifically be differentiated based on the presence of symmetrical glove sensory disturbance in patients. The symptoms not only affect patient’s activity patterns but can also affect patient’s sleep and emotional state to a substantial extent. Moreover, this side effect can cause lower motor nerves to develop in severe neuropathy. Weakness and deficits in fine motor skills and balance can significantly raise the risk of falling in patients [2, 3]. Moreover, this side effect can cause patients to stop or lower their intake of cancer medications. As a direct effect, this can significantly affect the final success of cancer treatment in many patients [4, 5].

As of now, the management of CIPN has actually continued to pose a serious concern in spite of a broad consensus on its significance in relation to its clinical status. Currently, there is not a gold standard drug that can really prevent and treat CIPN. The only guideline made available through the American Society of Clinical Oncology is that duloxetine is moderately beneficial in reducing pain associated with CIPN. However, this is still a less efficacious therapeutic choice for a large number of patients and is often underlain by potential side effects like fatigue and nausea. The need to resort to non-pharmacological treatments that are safe and efficacious and can easily and extensively be applied is imperative in this connection since its efficacy is actually confined to a large number of patients and is underlain by side effects like fatigue and nausea [6]. Manual interventions are a well-acknowledged non-invasive approach in relation to cancer support care. The process actually involves the systematic mechanical stimulation of the body’s soft tissues through bare hands or instruments [7–11]. In this context, it has become increasingly valuable.

In this systematic review, manual interventions are mainly related to techniques like acupressure, reflexology, and massage. The theory behind all these interventions is complex, as they can promote blood flow in the localized area and aid in repair through physical stimulation. In addition, they can inhibit pain conduction and promote endogenous opioid peptides secretion through stimulation of thick fiber nerves in gate control theory to produce an analgesic effect. The touching and relieving effect can effectively calm patients’ anxiety, which is more important [12–14]. In between, theories in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) provide a unique theory for acupressure. High-level evidence-based medicine has preliminarily validated the potential of its application [15]. A bibliometric analysis confirmed that manual interventions have become a research hotspot in the management of pain, anxiety, and quality of life in cancer patients, and a large-scale randomized controlled trial demonstrated that massage was no less effective than acupuncture in relieving pain in patients with advanced cancer [16]. This evidence collectively indicates that manual interventions are a highly promising direction in the field of oncology supportive care.

Nevertheless, the efficacy of manual interventions for CIPN is unclear, and the findings of the preliminary studies addressing this topic have significant inconsistencies. Kim and Wu et al. conducted studies that reported substantial beneficial effects of manual interventions on the improvement of symptoms and quality of life in CIPN [17, 18]. However, Kurt et al. [19] discovered that the effects were restricted to sensory function. In addition, Park et al. [20] demonstrated that manipulation interventions were not as effective as conventional footbaths and were associated with a reduced quality of life. Clinicians are at a loss when it comes to making judgments due to the significant discrepancy in conclusions. To the best of our knowledge, there is no systematic review of the multi-dimensional effects of manual interventions, including massage, reflexology, and acupressure, in patients with CIPN. Consequently, the aim of this study was to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the overall efficacy of manual interventions by systematically retrieving, evaluating, and integrating all related studies. This was done in order to resolve existing controversies and provide clear evidence for clinical practice.

Methods

Type of study

This investigation adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21]. Details are available in Supplementary Material 1. The study protocol has been registered in the PROSPERO (CRD420251112391).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study employed the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study Design) to provide explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria for the literature.

Criteria for inclusion: (1) Population: Adult patients (≥ 18 years) having a clinically confirmed diagnosis of any malignant tumor type. Patients undergoing or having completed chemotherapy exhibit symptoms of CIPN, as clinically diagnosed or evaluated using established scales. No limitations exist regarding cancer type, stage, or specific chemotherapy regimen. (2) Intervention: The intervention group employs a diverse array of manual interventions, encompassing massage, reflexology, and acupressure, performed by either a licensed professional or patients themselves. Interventions may be utilized independently or in conjunction with standard care. Justification for the Grouping: The three techniques, massage, reflexology, and acupressure, were grouped together under the common rubric “manual interventions.” This is because these three techniques share a common underlying theoretical basis and are already a part of a common practice in oncology, namely the use of non-invasive manual contact for the purpose of treating symptoms. The grouping was done in an effort to examine the net benefit that accrues from mechanical stimulation. By pooling them, we aimed to evaluate the collective benefit of mechanical stimulation, which is supported by shared physiological mechanisms such as the Gate Control Theory. (3) Comparators: Controls get standard treatment, sham interventions, and placebos. (4) Outcomes: The principal end measures were alterations in overall symptoms, pain, and QOL in CIPN. Secondary outcomes encompass sleep quality, as well as anxiety and depression. The study results pertain to at least one pre-specified outcome measure. The study design encompassed RCTs and quasi-experimental studies.

Exclusion criteria: (1) The intervention was a composite intervention from which the independent effects of the manual interventions could not be separated. (2) The studies did not report quantitative data (e.g., mean, SD) that could be used for meta-analysis, or could not be obtained after contacting the authors. (3) Repeated publications will be included only in the version with the most comprehensive data. (4) Studies comparing a manual intervention against another active non-pharmacological intervention (e.g., footbathing) were excluded to ensure a clear comparison against a non-active control. (5) Thesis, retrospective studies, cohort studies, case reports, reviews, and conference abstracts were excluded.

Search strategy

We performed a systematic search across eight Chinese and English databases, covering the period from the inception of each database until July 2025. The English databases comprised PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, and ProQuest, while the Chinese databases included CNKI and Wan Fang Database; the search languages were restricted to Chinese and English.

During the development of search strategies, preliminary searches revealed that using keywords such as “manual interventions,” “Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy,” “Randomized controlled trials,” and “quasi-experimental studies” yielded a very limited number of relevant documents. To guarantee the inclusion of all relevant studies and prevent omissions from restrictive search terms, a comprehensive search strategy was employed. Specifically, a systematic review of the literature on “non-pharmacological interventions in CIPN” was undertaken, followed by a refinement of the results to isolate all studies that satisfied the inclusion criteria of this research. The search was further narrowed to discover all intervention studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria for this research.

The search strategy combined subject terms (e.g., mesh terms) with free terms, including diseases such as “Peripheral Nervous System Diseases”[MeSH Terms], “CIPN,” and “Neurotoxicity Syndromes”; and interventions such as “Massage”[MeSH Terms], “Acupressure”[MeSH Terms], “Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine”[MeSH Terms], “Integrative Medicine”[MeSH Terms], “Complementary Therapies”[MeSH Terms], “Reflexology,” “Manual Therapy,” and “Non-pharmacologic Treatment.” The search terms are connected by “OR,” and “AND” is used between P and I. The search terms are not linked to each other. At the same time, to avoid omissions, we retrospectively searched the reference lists of the included literature. The search strategy was developed in collaboration with an experienced medical librarian to ensure its comprehensiveness and rigor. The detailed search results are shown in Supplementary Material 2.

Study selection

All collected literature was initially imported into EndNote X9 to remove duplicates. Subsequently, the titles and abstracts of the literature were independently reviewed in tandem by two researchers (YXC, WYH). For literature that successfully underwent the preliminary screening, the complete text was then acquired for further examination to confirm inclusion. Disputes arising during the screening process were addressed through dialogue and negotiation; if consensus was unattainable, a third-party researcher (YZ) intervened to arbitrate.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers using a pre-designed standardized form. Extracted content primarily included (1) basic study information (e.g., first author, publication year, country); (2) baseline characteristics of study subjects (sample size, cancer type, mean age and SD); (3) chemotherapy drugs; (4) timing of intervention; (5) intervention details, such as specific types of manual interventions, specific manipulation techniques/access points, stimulation tools/media, provider, pressure intensity, single intervention duration, frequency, and total length of the treatment; (6) control measures; and (7) outcome measurement tools. For studies that reported outcomes at multiple follow-up time points, we extracted data from the final assessment point to evaluate the overall effect of the intervention. To ensure the accuracy of all data in this study, two researchers (YXC, WYH) conducted independent, comprehensive cross-checks of all extracted outcome measures from the included studies to eliminate any potential data extraction or classification errors.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two investigators (YZ and WYH). The Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool, version 2 (RoB 2) [22, 23], was employed to evaluate five dimensions in RCTs: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. The risk of bias was classified as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” Upon encountering conflicts, the two reviewers engaged in discussions until a consensus was achieved. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were reconciled through dialogue, and a third reviewer (BLY) was solicited for additional evaluation.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

This study utilized Reviewer Manager (RevMan 5.4) software from the Cochrane Collaboration for data synthesis and meta-analysis. To calculate effect size for continuous variables, including pain scores and symptom scale scores, we employed the SMD and its 95% CI as the aggregated effect sizes to mitigate the influence of magnitude resulting from varying measuring instruments. Hedges’s g was utilized to compute the SMD as it adjusts for small sample studies. Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic were employed to evaluate heterogeneity among studies [24]. Given the potential clinical heterogeneity among included studies in terms of intervention details, patient populations, and implementation methods, we employed a random-effects model for data synthesis across all outcome measures to obtain more robust estimates. For outcomes where a lower score indicates improvement (e.g., pain, CIPN symptoms, sleep quality), a negative SMD favors the manual interventions group. Conversely, for outcomes where a higher score indicates improvement (e.g., quality of life), a positive SMD favors the manual interventions group.

Subgroup analyses were performed to determine if the effectiveness of manual interventions was affected by specific factors, including chemotherapy drugs, the type of intervention (acupressure, massage therapy, or reflexology), timing of intervention, single intervention duration, frequency, total length of the treatment, and the provider of the intervention (professional or self/family). Based on clinical practice and a review of the literature, these subgroups were defined as follows: intervention frequency was categorized as high frequency (≥ 2 times/week) and low frequency (< 2 times/week); total intervention duration was categorized as short term (≤ 4 weeks) and long term (> 4 weeks); and single intervention duration was categorized as short duration (< 30 min) and long duration (≥ 30 min). In the provider analysis, the intervention providers were grouped into two categories. The categories included a professional group, which consisted of nurses, licensed therapists, and researchers. The other category included a self-administered/family-led group.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias assessment

Sensitivity analysis and evaluation of publication bias were conducted using STATA 18.0 software and RevMan 5.4. To evaluate the robustness of the meta-analysis results, the leave-one-out approach was employed for the primary outcome indicators during the sensitivity analysis. We successively removed individual studies and recalculated the aggregate effect sizes to determine if the results were significantly altered [25]. For publication bias, when the number of studies included for a specific outcome indicator was greater than or equal to ten, the preliminary evaluation was intended to be conducted using a funnel plot, with statistical analysis performed through Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s linear regression test. If P > 0.05, it was deemed that there was no substantial publication bias.

Evidence quality assessment

This study employed the Grades of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to rigorously assess the overall quality of evidence for each outcome indicator [26]. The evidentiary quality was classified as high, moderate, low, or extremely low. This analysis established the first level of evidence for all outcome measures as high. Consequently, we reduced the quality of the evidence based on five criteria: (1) Risk of bias, (2) Inconsistency, (3) Indirectness, (4) Imprecision, (5) Publication bias. The evidence quality was assessed separately by two researchers (YXC, BLY), with any discrepancies handled by discussion; if consensus was unattainable, a third researcher (YZ) intervened to adjudicate.

Results

Study selection

The study first retrieved 1752 articles through a systematic search conducted in a total of eight databases. After eliminating 376 duplicated articles, a total of 1376 articles remained. The first level of screening entailed a review of titles and abstracts for each article. As a consequence, 106 articles were considered to fulfill the requirements. After excluding 8 articles for which full-text versions could not be retrieved, 98 articles remained. Among these, 87 articles were excluded for reasons including participant mismatch, interventions involving drug therapy combined with auricular acupressure, study type mismatch, and control groups receiving footbaths. Consequently, the final review exclusively comprises 11 RCTs. The process of study selection is shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram of study selection

Characteristics of included studies

Eleven studies considered were published from 2018 to 2025 and comprised a total of 646 participants. The studies exhibited geographical representativeness with a diverse array of sites encompassing South Korea (n = 3) [12, 18, 27], China (n = 2) [28, 29], Iran (n = 1) [30], Turkey (n = 4) [14, 19, 31, 32] and the USA (n = 1) [33]. Ten studies were performed in English [12, 14, 18, 19, 27, 29–33] and one study was conducted in Chinese [28]. The study participants primarily consisted of adult cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, encompassing various cancer types: breast cancer (n = 5) [12, 18, 29, 31, 32], colorectal cancer (n = 1) [30], gynecological tumors [27], non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (n = 1) [14], and mixed tumors (n = 3) [19, 28, 33]. Risk of bias and sensitivity analysis

This systematic review included 11 RCTs [12, 14, 18, 19, 27–33], all of which were evaluated for methodological quality utilizing the RoB 2. The methodological quality of the included studies exhibited variability: three studies [14, 27, 33] were classified as low risk of bias, whereas four studies [18, 28, 30, 32] were categorized as having some concerns. Four studies [12, 19, 29, 31] were classified as high risk of bias. This indicates that the overall methodological quality of the included studies was moderate, with certain biases potentially impacting the dependability of the conclusions. The detailed results of the assessment are shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Risk of bias assessment for randomized controlled trials

Risk of bias assessment for randomized controlled trials

The main causes of increased risk of bias are as follows: ① Bias in selection of the reported (D5): This is the most common area of concern. The vast majority of studies were rated as having “some concerns” in this area because they failed to provide a pre-registered study protocol or a detailed statistical analysis plan. This makes it impossible to completely rule out the possibility that researchers may selectively report favorable results from multiple outcome indicators or analytic methods. ② Deviations from intended interventions (D2) and bias in measurement of the outcome (D4): These two types of bias arise primarily from the challenges inherent in the difficulty of blinding in manual interventions studies. This can lead to implementation bias (Performance Bias) as the subgroups are usually clear to both participants and intervention implementers. Also, when outcome indicators (e.g., pain, quality of life) are dependent on subjective patient reports, measurement bias (Detection Bias) is highly likely to be introduced if outcome assessors are not blinded. In this assessment, the study by Şimşek et al. [31] was rated as high risk in the area of outcome measurement for this reason. ③ Bias due to missing outcome data (D3): Although most studies performed well in this domain, this issue is still a key factor contributing to the elevated risk of bias in individual studies. For example, Jung’s study [12] was rated as high risk in this area due to a high and potentially uneven rate of missing visits. ④ Bias arising from the randomization process (D1): Some studies had methodological concerns in this area, which affected the overall assessment of their risk of bias.

This study evaluated the potential risk of publication bias by constructing a funnel plot and doing Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s linear regression test for pain outcome indicators. The findings from this segment of the risk of bias assessment must be interpreted cautiously, as only 6 studies were considered for pain outcomes, which is below the 10 or more recommended by the Cochrane Handbook. The assessment of publication bias for other outcome markers, including CIPN symptoms and quality of life, was not conducted due to the insufficient number of studies included in the study.

The Egger test concluded that there are no publication biases that were statistically significant (P = 0.8753). The P-values for both tests are greater than 0.05. Sensitivity analysis of CIPN pain

In order to evaluate the robustness of our findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding studies that were at a high risk of bias. Four studies [12, 19, 29, 31] were excluded from the respective analyses due to their assessment as having a high or serious risk of bias. After excluding two high-risk studies, the pooled effect for the aggregate CIPN symptoms outcome remained statistically significant, and the conclusion remained unchanged (SMD = −0.97; 95% CI = −1.35, −0.59; P < 0.00001). The analysis continued to demonstrate a substantial analgesic effect (SMD = −1.71; 95% CI = −3.34, −0.08; P = 0.04) for the pain outcome, despite the exclusion of two high-risk studies. The quality-of-life outcome remained highly significant (SMD = 0.91; 95% CI = 0.27, 1.55; P = 0.005), despite the exclusion of one high-risk study, confirming a robust positive effect. These findings suggest that the primary conclusions of this meta-analysis regarding its primary outcomes are robust and not significantly influenced by studies with a higher risk of bias. The corresponding forest plots for these sensitivity analyses are included in Supplementary Material 6.

Characteristics of interventions

Reflexology

Four studies [19, 27, 31, 33] employed foot reflexology, which focuses on alleviating symptoms by stimulating certain reflex zones in the foot. Intervention sites targeted reflex zones of the foot associated with the brain, cervical spine, spinal column, and solar plexus. Massage techniques encompass “peristaltic” pressure used with the thumb and fingers, complemented with soothing strokes such as nudging and patting, typically escalating in intensity as tolerated by the patient. Participants comprise skilled researchers, certified massage therapists, and the patient or their family (self-administration), frequently use olive oil or a mixture of essential oils as a medium. The duration of an individual intervention ranged from 20 to 40 min. Frequency fluctuated significantly, ranging from bi-daily to weekly. The complete intervention cycle spans 4 to 6 weeks.

Acupressure

Three studies [12, 18, 29] employed acupressure techniques to modulate physiological activities through the application of pressure on designated acupoints, which can be classified into two types: auricular acupressure and body acupressure. Auricular acupressure involves the nurse putting Wang Bu Liu hang seeds or magnetic beads to stimulate the patient’s primary acupoints in the ear, including the Shenmen, liver, and spleen, along with corresponding reaction points on the fingers, toes, occiput, and jaw, chosen based on the symptoms presented. Body acupressure was primarily self-administered by patients or facilitated by family members. The chosen acupoints were exclusively situated in the extremities, including Hegu (LI4), Ashisanli (ST36), Qu chi (LI11), Baxi (Ex-UE9), Ba feng (Ex-LE10), Wai guan, and Sanyinjiao. The pressure intensity was suitable for the patient to experience comfort with a slight, bearable pain. Patients were directed to exert “comfortable, slightly cool and painful” pressure on these locations with their fingers. The practitioner encompasses the nurse, the patient (self-management), or their primary caregiver. The intervention frequency was elevated, typically occurring 3 to 5 times daily, with pressure exerted on each location for a duration of 10 s to 2 min. The overall number of interventions varied from 3 weeks to 6 cycles of chemotherapy.

Massage therapy

In four studies [14, 28, 32, 33], massage therapy that entails direct manipulation of soft tissues in the human body was used. The studies mainly involved traditional approaches that include effleurage, petrissage, and friction techniques especially in areas where high prevalence of CIPN is observed. The design of intervention programs has varied. In Lopez’s study [33], for instance, a comparison between a high-frequency approach and a standard-frequency approach of intervention programs is highlighted. In other studies, interventions were conducted either once to thrice a week for a duration of between 4 and 12 weeks. The length of individual interventions ranged between 20 and 30 min. The intervention medium was diverse and comprised combinations of essential oils (e.g., geranium, lavender, bergamot), liquid paraffin, or unscented hypoallergenic moisturizers. The practitioners comprised experts, including nurses, qualified researchers, and licensed oncology massage therapists.

Primary outcome: effect on CIPN symptoms

Four studies [12, 18, 27, 29] with a total of 246 participants reported on overall CIPN symptoms. The meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model due to high heterogeneity (I2 = 95%). The analysis showed a statistically significant result (SMD = −1.67; 95% CI = −3.03, −0.30; P = 0.020) (Fig. 5). However, this result must be interpreted with extreme caution. Secondary outcomes: effects on anxiety and depression

Two studies [27, 28] (n = 161) evaluated the effects of manual interventions on anxiety and depression. For anxiety, the analysis did not show a statistically significant result (SMD = −1.45; 95% CI = −2.92, 0.02; P = 0.05), with extremely high heterogeneity (I2 = 94%). Similarly, for depression, the effect was not significant (SMD = −1.30; 95% CI = −2.87, 0.27; P = 0.10), also with extremely high heterogeneity (I2 = 95%). Therefore, based on the currently available evidence, a definitive conclusion on the effectiveness of manual interventions for these psychological outcomes cannot be drawn. These results were based on a small number of studies (n = 2) and should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size (Supplementary Material 3.7, 3.8).

Subgroup analysis

In order to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses for the primary outcomes. We intended to conduct subgroup analyses based on chemotherapy drug. However, the inclusion of mixed tumor populations in certain studies and the diverse drug regimens across studies (e.g., taxanes, platinums, bortezomib) made it impossible to establish homogeneous subgroups with an adequate number of data points for comparison. Subsequently, this analysis was rendered impossible.

Overall CIPN symptoms

Meta-analysis of four studies (n = 246) found manual interventions to be effective for symptoms of CIPN (SMD = −1.67; P = 0.020). However, this needs to be understood in the context of very high heterogeneity (I2 = 95%). Furthermore, subgroup analysis showed there are no statistically significant differences between the groups for the subgroups (P > 0.05). These include timing of intervention (P = 0.10), provider (P = 0.46), total intervention time (P = 0.29), type of intervention (P = 0.32), and single session intervention time (P = 0.32). This result indicates that none of these factors is the statistical source of high heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis by provider revealed the sources of high heterogeneity. In the Sself-administered/family-led category (n = 2), the effect was highly significant and homogenous (P < 0.00001; I2 = 0%). In the Professional category (n = 2), the effect was not significant (P = 0.22), together with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 98%). Given the limited number of studies in this subgroup (n = 2), these results should be interpreted with caution. Detailed forest plots are available in Supplementary Material 4.1.

Pain

Manual interventions showed a statistically significant reduction in pain scores compared to the control group (SMD = −1.43; P = 0.006), but the heterogeneity between studies was extremely high (I2 = 93%). An exploratory subgroup analysis has been carried out. The tests for differences between the subgroups were not significant for any variable, so the factors were not found to be the statistical causes of the high heterogeneity. The results revealed that high heterogeneity was present only among certain subgroups. For example, the heterogeneity became 0% in the Low frequency category and 29% in the Intervention after chemotherapy category. However, given the limited number of studies in this subgroup (n = 2), these results should be interpreted with caution. The heterogeneity in the category of interventions of high frequency and during chemotherapy also remained extremely high (I2 = 95%). Detailed forest plots are available in Supplementary Material 4.2.

Quality of life

Three studies (n = 212) examined the effects of manual interventions on quality of life as the outcome measure. A significant difference was found in the quality of life for the manual interventions group (SMD = 1.05; P < 0.00001), which showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 58%). Subgroup analysis indicated total intervention time and providers as the essential determinants influencing the results. In the long-term subgroup, the results showed a significantly greater improvement in quality of life (SMD = 1.28; P < 0.00001), compared to the short-term subgroup (SMD = 0.58; P = 0.03). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (P = 0.03). In the professional subgroup, the result showed a substantially greater improvement in quality of life (SMD = 1.28; P < 0.00001), compared to the patient/family self-management subgroup (SMD = 0.58; P = 0.03). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (P = 0.03). In particular, the heterogeneity was effectively tested and eliminated in the long-term subgroup and the professional subgroup (I2 = 0%). Furthermore, for the intervention type subgroup analysis as well as the subgroup analysis for the intervention frequency, there were no statistically significant differences between the groups (P = 0.55). Detailed forest plots are available in Supplementary Material 4.3.

Grade of evidence

We conducted a GRADE evaluation of the quality of evidence for all outcome indicators, and the detailed results are shown in Table 3. Overall, the quality of the evidence included in this meta-analysis was rated as low to very low. Specifically, the quality of evidence for the effectiveness of manual interventions in improving pain, overall CIPN symptoms, and sleep quality was rated as “low.” The main reasons for the downgrading of the evidence included a high risk of bias in the included studies. For example, blinding was difficult due to the nature of the intervention; some studies had methodological flaws such as non-randomized subgroups, and significant statistical inconsistencies (generally high I2 values). However, for outcomes such as quality of life, anxiety, and depression, the quality of evidence was rated as “very low” or “low.” This was mainly due to very high imprecision in addition to risk of bias and high inconsistency, as evidenced by the small number of included studies, statistically insignificant combined effect sizes, and wide confidence intervals. These findings suggest that despite the positive trends observed, we have very limited confidence in the effect estimates for these outcomes, and future studies are likely to change the current conclusions (Table 3). Discussion

Main findings

Manual interventions, a long-established and extensively utilized non-pharmacological therapy, are being highlighted for their potential in supplementary alternative oncology treatments. This systematic review and meta-analysis thoroughly integrate for the first time the multifaceted effects of three forms of manual interventions: massage, reflexology, and acupressure, in the management of CIPN. The findings indicate that manual interventions may offer statistically significant relief for core CIPN symptoms and associated pain, as well as benefits in improving quality of life. As for secondary outcomes, there is low-certainty evidence suggesting manual interventions can improve sleep quality. The evidence remains unclear regarding the efficacy of these interventions for psychological distress (anxiety and depression). This may be associated with the small number of included studies (only 2) and limited sample size, requiring further verification by more studies in the future.

Our meta-analysis revealed that, although manual interventions reduced patients’ pain score (SMD = −1.43; P = 0.006) and overall CIPN symptoms (SMD = −1.67; P = 0.020), we need to view both overall results with extreme caution. Both overall results were accompanied by extremely high and statistically inexplicable heterogeneities. This condition reflects the existence of obvious clinical or methodological discrepancies between the studies, thus casting doubt on the validity of both overall effect sizes. Although the result on overall CIPN symptoms is highly unreliable, further overall result analysis based on symptom dimension offered more valid inferences. Our result revealed that the manual interventions brought significant improvements in both sensory dimension (SMD = −0.62; P−value < 0.00001) and the motor dimension (SMD = −0.42; P−value = 0.006). Most importantly, in both cases, the result was accompanied by extremely low heterogeneities (I2 = 0%; I2 = 23%). This result reveals that the positive effect of the manual interventions in terms of improvement in specific sensory and motor dysfunction is truthful, legitimate, and more clinical.

The conclusion that manual interventions may be effective for CIPN pain aligns with previous studies on acute and chronic pain management, demonstrating the efficacy of manual interventions for chronic ailments such as chronic neck pain [34] and knee osteoarthritis [35], in addition to acute postoperative pain [36]. The effectiveness of manual interventions in reducing symptoms in patients with CIPN can be understood in two contexts: contemporary scientific medicine and traditional Chinese medicine.

In contemporary scientific medicine, for instance, its efficacy is complex. The manual interventions induced an increase in blood flow to the tissues through the optimal microcirculation in this area. In all probability, this sped up the clearance process of neurotoxic substances and provided nerves with vital nutrients and oxygen for repair. Furthermore, this is vital for lessening ischemic hypoxic nerve injuries [37]. Izgu et al. [32] demonstrated that classical massage enhances sensory nerve action potentials and tibial nerve latencies in breast cancer patients through nerve conduction studies, serving as an objective indicator that provides direct physiological evidence of manual interventions role in facilitating neurological recovery. Secondly, as per the classical “gate control theory,” the physical stimulation of tissues through manipulation activates the rapidly transmitting Aβ nerve fibers, thereby inhibiting the transmission of pain signals at the spinal cord level [38]. Ultimately, manual interventions may also produce analgesic effects at the biochemical level by facilitating the release of endogenous opioid peptides, including endorphins [39].

TCM theories offer a distinctive theoretical framework for acupressure and reflexology. In accordance with TCM meridian theory, CIPN is classified as “paralysis” and “impotence,” primarily resulting from chemotherapy-induced damage to positive qi, leading to deficiencies in qi and blood, as well as stagnation in the veins and collaterals. By pressing specific acupoints, such as the Hegu point (LI4) in the Hand Yangming Large Intestine Meridian and the Foot Sanli point (ST36) in the Foot Yangming Stomach Meridian, manual interventions can stimulate the meridian qi, dredge the meridians, and regulate the qi and blood, thus moistening the limbs and bones and achieving the effect of “through the passage of the body, there will be no pain,” effectively relieving numbness and pain [29]. At a macro level, manual interventions are thought to alleviate “qi stagnation,” which induces symptoms, and restore its natural flow through the meridians, thereby recalibrating the body’s overall state and mitigating discomfort [40].

Our meta-analysis indicated that manual interventions exert a statistically significant and clinically relevant positive effect on the quality of life of patients with CIPN (SMD = 1.05; P < 0.00001). This conclusion is in line with a recent meta-analysis study on massage therapy [11], which notably confirmed that massage increases significantly the total quality of life of cancer patients. The study not only supports and reproduces the same finding but even widens its scope from a massage therapy to related but broader manual techniques like acupressure and reflexology. Thus, in one way, it increases our confidence level in making use of this therapy for cancer patients.

Unlike the high heterogeneity observed for pain and overall CIPN symptoms, subgroup analysis for QoL identified a key source of heterogeneity (I2 = 58%). Analysis revealed that total intervention duration (P = 0.03) and provider (P = 0.03) were key determinants of intervention effectiveness. Furthermore, subgroup analysis showed that the professional practitioner group (SMD = 1.28) demonstrated significantly superior outcomes compared to the patient/family self-management group (SMD = 0.58; the test for subgroup difference P = 0.03). We hypothesize three reasons: First, professionals possess distinct advantages in anatomical knowledge, acupoint localization, reflex zone stimulation, and precision of massage techniques. Simultaneously, professional intervention may ensure patient adherence to the treatment protocol. Second, QoL is inherently subjective. Professional interventions transcend technical execution; they foster greater trust and security through the act of care and touch. This is crucial for alleviating anxiety and building confidence, directly enhancing quality of life. Third, it must be acknowledged that the “self-management” model offers substantial advantages in cost-effectiveness, accessibility, and enhancing patient empowerment and family support [8]. It is also worth noting the difference in heterogeneity between the two groups. The reduced heterogeneity in the self-administered and family-led group can be accounted for by the fact that fairly standard protocols (like particular acupoints or standard procedures) are used in these interventions, which increases homogeneity among participants. The heterogeneity in the professional group is likely to be high because of greater variability in intervention technical details, such as the therapist’s experience, manipulation techniques, and personalized adjustments during treatment. Similarly, the long-term intervention group (SMD = 1.28) demonstrated significantly superior outcomes compared to the short-term intervention group (SMD = 0.58). This indicates that manual interventions exhibit a cumulative effect on QoL, potentially reflecting mechanisms such as promoting local circulation, clearing neurotoxins, and releasing endorphins. These processes require time to accumulate and produce stable physiological changes. Interventions shorter than 4 weeks may only provide transient comfort without inducing sustained neuro-reparative effects or altering pain thresholds.

Moreover, the findings of this meta-analysis indicated that manual interventions may enhance sleep quality in patients with CIPN. Sleep disturbance is a prevalent and significant concomitant symptom affecting the quality of life in cancer patients, creating a detrimental cycle with pain, fatigue, and mood disorders [41, 42]. Our analysis produced a combined effect size SMD of −1.08, indicating a large effect size per Cohen’s criterion [43], which implies that the enhancement in sleep resulting from massage therapy is both statistically significant and clinically meaningful. As a secondary outcome, the improvement in sleep quality may be clinically closely linked to the relief of primary symptoms. The mechanisms through which massage therapy enhances sleep may be complex. Crucially, the alleviation of neuropathic pain and sensory discomfort (our primary outcomes) directly removes major physical barriers to sleep onset and maintenance. Furthermore, massage induces relaxation of both the body and mind, diminishes sympathetic excitability, and facilitates the release of neurotransmitters like endorphins, thereby alleviating patients’ anxiety and enhancing sleep quality [44]. A network meta-analysis of sleep disorders in cancer patients found [45] that although both invasive acupuncture manipulation and non-invasive acupressure demonstrated marked superiority over standard care, direct comparisons between the two revealed no statistically significant differences. This evidence underscores the distinct advantages of non-invasive manual interventions, such as acupressure, which demonstrate comparable effectiveness to invasive treatments while significantly improving patient acceptance and safety, rendering them a more favorable clinical choice for oncology patients who are frail or apprehensive about invasive procedures.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of the study lies in the fact that it represents the first systematically conducted review for the combined effects of the three major manual interventions (massages, reflexology, and acupressure) for CIPN in a holistic manner for the first time in this area of research. Firstly, the results have reinforced the fact that manual interventions are capable of providing relief for both the sensory and the motor symptoms. However, the results of the pain and overall CIPN symptoms need some careful consideration regarding their pain-relieving aspect. Second, the importance of the total intervention time and the manual therapists is crucial for quality of life. The long-term manual interventions provided by professionals are capable of providing greater benefits for the quality of life in cancer patients. However, the home management approach appears valid and cost-effective.

There are a few limitations in our study. Firstly, the RCTs included in the analysis were characterized by a considerable risk of performance and detection bias, mainly because of the lack of blinding. Given the physical and interactive nature of these interventions, blinding therapists and participants is practically unfeasible. It is important to note that the predominance of trials with some concerns or high risk of bias limits our confidence in the magnitude of the observed benefits. The outcomes of interest in the study (pain, CIPN symptoms, quality of life) were all subjective in nature, so the lack of blinding increased the risk of performance and detection bias, which could result in the overestimation of the benefits of the treatments. To counter the effects of the aforementioned biases in the results, the GRADE approach was used to downgrade the certainty of the evidence in order to interpret the results in a conservative manner. Additionally, the presence of high clinical heterogeneity among the studies accounted for high statistical heterogeneity. In spite of the subgroup analysis, the high level of heterogeneity among the results of pain and CIPN symptoms remains unclear, which might be associated with the distinct intervention strategies and chemotherapy regimens used among the studies. The limitations in evidence quality are mainly related to the insufficient number of existing studies, heterogeneity in intervention types, and methodological constraints of some included studies, which are objective current status in the field.

Implications for clinical practice, future research, and conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, the following clinical practice recommendations are proposed: First, for patients whose primary goal is to improve quality of life, this study provides relatively clear evidence. Clinicians are recommended to prioritize intervention protocols with a duration of at least 4 weeks to achieve optimal quality of life improvements. Second, for patients whose primary goal is to alleviate core symptoms of CIPN, the patient/family self-management model demonstrates practical value. Our analysis revealed that the self-management group not only demonstrated significant efficacy but also exhibited the lowest heterogeneity across all subgroups. Therefore, the self-management model can be appropriately promoted as a cost-effective and evidence-consistent foundational strategy for managing CIPN symptoms. For instance, we can promote them through instructional videos and manuals. Finally, when recommending manual interventions for pain or overall CIPN symptoms, clinicians must recognize the limitations of the overall evidence. While positive trends were observed, extremely high and unexplained heterogeneity indicates that specific factors, such as frequency or timing, that consistently yield optimal analgesic effects remain unclear. Therefore, interventions targeting these outcomes should be considered exploratory and closely monitored for individual patient responses.

Implications for future research: (1) High-Quality Trials: There is a critical need for more rigorously designed large-scale multi-center RCTs that conform to reporting standards like those recommended in CONSORT. (2) Head-to-Head Comparison Trials and Standardized Protocols: Future trials need to focus on establishing and testing standardized intervention protocols to define techniques, duration, intensity, and frequency of interventions and actually pursue direct comparison trials to identify strengths for distinct doses. (3) Objective Outcome Measures and Long-Term Follow-up: In an effort to increase objectivity in finding manual interventions evidence, it is important for future investigations to assess improvements through more concrete physiological parameters (nerve conduction studies) and track long-term follow-up to actually confirm improvements.

Conclusion

Current low-certainty evidence suggests that manual interventions may be beneficial for patients with CIPN, especially in sensory and motor symptoms, quality of life, and sleep quality. Specifically, the benefit in quality of life is most evident in longer treatment duration (≥ 4 weeks) and provided by professionals. But in terms of overall pain reduction and overall symptoms, the evidence is unclear due to extreme heterogeneity. Large-scale, rigorously designed RCTs must be conducted in the future to validate such data.
