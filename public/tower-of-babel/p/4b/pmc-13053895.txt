Post-Marketing Surveillance Study of the Live Attenuated Varicella Vaccine (SKYVaricella Inj.) in Korean Children
Authors: Hyunjeong Kim, Kihyun Kim, Hye Young Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVaricella, caused by the varicella-zoster virus, is a highly contagious pediatric infection that can lead to severe complications. SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Incheon, Korea), a live-attenuated varicella vaccine developed by SK bioscience, was approved in Korea in 2018. This post-marketing surveillance study aimed to evaluate the safety profile of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) in children aged 12 months to 12 years in routine clinical practice.Materials and MethodsThis multicenter, prospective, non-interventional study was conducted across 25 sites in Korea from June 4, 2018, to June 3, 2022. A total of 623 subjects were included in the safety analysis. Adverse events (AEs), including those assessed as adverse drug reactions (ADRs), and serious adverse events (SAEs), were monitored for 42 days post-vaccination. Events were further categorized by causality and expectedness based on regulatory definitions. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize AE incidence and to explore potential associations with participant characteristics.ResultsAmong the 623 participants, 263 (42.2%) reported 514 AEs, and 56 (9.0%) experienced ADRs. The most frequently reported ADRs were injection site pain (4.0%), erythema (3.9%), and irritability (3.7%). Two SAEs (0.3%) - bronchitis and hand-foot-and-mouth disease - were reported and assessed as unrelated to vaccination. Varicella-like rashes occurred in 10 subjects (1.6%) and were mostly mild or moderate in severity. No unexpected serious adverse drug reactions were observed.ConclusionSKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) demonstrated an acceptable safety profile in pediatric use in Korea, with most AEs being mild and consistent with expected vaccine-related reactions. Continued pharmacovigilance and larger-scale studies are warranted to further support its long-term public health impact.

Introduction

Varicella, commonly known as chickenpox, is a highly contagious disease that primarily affects children and is caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV). Transmission occurs through direct contact with infected individuals or via airborne droplets [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), varicella accounts for approximately 140 million cases globally each year, including 4.2 million severe cases requiring hospitalization and about 4,200 deaths [2]. Although typically mild in children, varicella can lead to severe complications such as pneumonia, encephalitis, and secondary bacterial infections, particularly among immunocompromised individuals, thereby contributing substantially to the global healthcare burden [234].

The varicella vaccine, an attenuated live virus vaccine, effectively prevents VZV infections and their complications. In Korea, varicella has been included in the National Immunization Program (NIP) and designated a nationally notifiable infectious disease since 2005. Reported cases temporarily increased during the early phase of surveillance but declined sharply during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic and have since stabilized. In contrast, diagnosis-based data from the National Health Insurance Service indicate a steady long-term decrease in varicella cases, suggesting that high vaccine coverage under the NIP has contributed to a sustained reduction in disease burden [5678]. SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Incheon, Korea), based on the Oka/SK strain and developed by SK bioscience Co., Ltd., was approved by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) in 2018 for use in children aged 12 months to 12 years and acquired WHO pre-qualification in 2019.

While pre-licensure clinical trials are essential for evaluating initial safety and efficacy, a post-marketing surveillance (PMS) study is crucial to assess vaccine safety and reactogenicity in routine clinical settings, which may not be fully reflected in pre-licensure trials [9]. This PMS study was conducted in accordance with MFDS requirements to evaluate the safety of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) in routine clinical use. Here, we present findings on the frequency and severity of adverse events observed among vaccinated children.

Materials and Methods

1. Study design

This multicenter, prospective, non-interventional post-authorization study, conducted in accordance with MFDS regulations, aimed to evaluate the safety profile of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) in at least 600 subjects who received the vaccine over a four-year re-examination period (2018–2022).

The study was designed to assess all adverse events (AEs) occurring within the first 42 days post-vaccination with SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea). Alongside the safety evaluation, participants’ demographic characteristics, medical and vaccination histories, and concomitant medication use were recorded. Screening was performed before enrollment, and the history of all concomitant medications, including steroids and immunosuppressive therapies, was reviewed within 3 months prior to vaccination.

Medications that had been initiated more than 3 months earlier but were still ongoing at the time of screening were also documented. None of the subjects were under immunosuppressive treatment, indicating consistency with the predefined exclusion criteria.

Hepatic and renal disorders were captured as separate categories, reflecting their designation by the MFDS as populations of special interest for safety surveillance.

Eligible participants were children aged ≥12 months and ≤12 years who received SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) according to the approved indication and dosage, and whose legal guardians provided written informed consent. Participants with contraindications listed in the product information, particularly those with known hypersensitivity to vaccine components or with conditions associated with immunodeficiency or immunosuppressive therapy, were excluded due to the potential risk of vaccine-related complications from this live-attenuated vaccine. Individuals with acute febrile illness at the time of vaccination were also excluded.

2. Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the protocol approved by the MFDS. PMS studies are mandated under the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act and relevant MFDS guidelines as part of the re-examination process for newly approved products, to evaluate their safety in real-world clinical settings in Korea. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the independent ethics committee Busan St. Mary's Hospital (IRB No. BSM 2019-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects or their legal guardians.

3. Vaccination study population

Children aged 12 months to 12 years who were vaccinated with SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) in accordance with the approved indication and contraindications of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) and met the inclusion/exclusion criteria were enrolled from 25 institutions and followed up between June 4, 2018, and June 3, 2022. Only subjects who provided informed consent before participation were enrolled in the study. In PMS studies of this type, safety assessments in specialized populations, such as pregnant or lactating women, and individuals with impaired renal or hepatic function, subgroup analyses are generally performed. However, this study was conducted in children between 12 months and 12 years of age; therefore, pregnant or lactating women were not included in the study population.

4. Safety assessments

The dose of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) was administered by subcutaneous injection on the lateral side of the upper arm at the first visit (Day 1). Safety data were collected through diary cards, follow-up visits, or telephone interviews. Solicited local AEs, including pain/tenderness, erythema/redness, and induration/swelling, and systemic AEs, including fever (≥38°C), irritability, drowsiness/lethargy, headache, and fatigue/malaise (recorded only in participants aged ≥5 years), were collected for 7 days after vaccination using diary cards. Any solicited AEs that occurred beyond 7 days post-vaccination were classified as unsolicited. In addition, all AEs reported within 42 days following vaccination, regardless of causality, were collected and analyzed. AEs were recorded by system organ class (SOC) and preferred term (PT) using MedDRA 25.0. AEs for which a causal relationship with the vaccine could not be ruled out were classified as adverse drug reactions (ADRs). In the assessment, AEs categorized as Certain, Probable/Likely, Possible, Conditional/Unclassified, or Unassessable/Unclassifiable were conservatively considered to have a causal relationship with the drug. Conversely, events evaluated as unlikely or definitely not indicated no causal relationship. AEs are classified as either expected or unexpected based on their characteristics and whether they are described in the approved labeling. For subjects who did not attend a follow-up visit after vaccination, investigations were conducted to assess AEs, including causality assessment by phone or correspondence.

5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using descriptive and inferential methods. Two-tailed tests were conducted with a significance level of 5%. Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests were used for comparisons of each factor depending on the expected cell frequencies, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method for binomial proportions. Summary statistics were reported to two decimal places. The incidence rates of AEs, ADRs, and other event categories were calculated based on the number and percentage. The varicella-like rash was further evaluated to assess severity and causality in relation to AEs/ADRs and serious adverse events (SAEs)/serious adverse drug reactions (SADRs), and treatment status was described.

Results

Of 626 enrolled subjects, three subjects were excluded due to major protocol deviation, resulting in 623 subjects for the safety analysis. Among the excluded subjects, two received the vaccine prior to the contract date, and one did not undergo any safety assessment after vaccination. Consequently, the latter subject could not be evaluated for safety, while no SAEs were reported in the two subjects. The mean age was 2.5±2.3 years, and 317 subjects (50.9%) were female. Past medical history was reported in 163 subjects (26.2%), including 51 (8.2%) with a history of allergies (Table 1). Current medical conditions were present in 130 subjects (20.9%), most classified as Infections and Infestations (107 subjects, 17.2%), followed by Respiratory, Thoracic and Mediastinal Disorders (36 subjects, 5.8%) and Skin and Subcutaneous Tissue Disorders (8 subjects, 1.3%) by SOC classification. By PT classification, the most common medical history was bronchitis (40 subjects, 6.4%), followed by rhinitis allergy (34 subjects, 5.5%) and upper respiratory tract infection (30 subjects, 4.8%) (Table 1).

Within 6 weeks of SKYVaricella Inj. All injection site cases, and 5 generalized cases, were assessed as ADRs. No events were reported as SAEs or SADRs (Table 5).

In terms of severity, 8 events were classified as mild-including all 4 injection site cases and 4 generalized cases—while 2 generalized rashes were moderate (Supplementary Table 1). No events were considered severe, and all subjects fully recovered (Supplementary Table 2). Causality assessments determined 6 events as possible, 2 as probable/likely, 1 event as unlikely, and 1 event as conditional/unclassified. No events were classified as certain, definitely not, or unassessable/unclassifiable. An AE assessed as unlikely to be related to the study drug was reported in one subject (Supplementary Table 3). The event occurred on the day of vaccination in a participant whose family member had developed varicella two weeks earlier. All varicella-like rash cases (n=10) occurred within 14 days after vaccination, including eight within 7 days and two on days 9 and 11.

All events required no action related to the study drug. Pharmacologic treatment was administered in 5 cases, while the remaining 5 required no treatment. Among those with generalized rashes, five received pharmacological treatment, primarily antiviral agents and antihistamines, to manage symptoms or prevent secondary complications. No non-pharmacologic interventions were reported (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This PMS study was conducted to evaluate the safety profile of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) under real-world clinical conditions in children aged 12 months to 12 years over a four-year re-examination period in accordance with MFDS regulations. Safety data were collected through routine PMS activities and included reports of AEs, ADRs, SAEs, unexpected ADRs, and other relevant safety findings related to SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea).

Among the 623 enrolled subjects, a total of 514 AEs were reported in 263 subjects (42.2%), and 136 ADRs occurred in 56 subjects (9.0%). The most frequently reported ADRs were injection site pain (4.0%), erythema (3.9%), and irritability (3.7%), all of which are consistent with expected post-vaccination responses. Unexpected ADRs, including acute sinusitis and scratch, were mild in severity and resolved completely. Notably, only two SAEs were reported during the study period-bronchitis and hand-foot-and-mouth disease-both of which were assessed as not related to the vaccine. No SADRs or serious safety concerns were identified during the observation period.

The incidence of AEs varied by age group, with the highest rate observed in the ‘≥12 months to <24 months’ age group (49.5%), followed by the ‘≥4 years to <7 years’ group (34.2%), and the ‘≥7 to ≤12 years’ group (14.7%). This distribution reflects real-world vaccination practices in Korea, where the first dose of the varicella vaccine is administered according to the NIP at 12–15 months of age, and a second dose, though not included in the NIP schedule, is often administered between 4 and 6 years of age based on clinical practice.

The relatively higher frequency of AEs observed in the ‘≥ 12 months to <24 months’ age group compared to the ‘≥4 years to <7 years’ group may be attributed to the greater number of vaccines administered concurrently during this period as part of the NIP. While both age groups routinely receive vaccines such as MMR, DTaP, and IPV, children aged 12–15 months additionally receive several other vaccines, including the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, hepatitis A, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and Japanese encephalitis vaccines [10]. The concurrent administration of multiple vaccines during this period may have influenced the increased detection and reporting of AEs, either due to additive immune responses or heightened observation by caregivers during a developmental stage characterized by increased medical surveillance [111213]. In the present study, approximately half of all participants received concomitant vaccinations, further suggesting that simultaneous vaccine administration may have contributed to the higher AE rates observed in the younger age group. Notably, when the analysis was restricted to participants without concomitant vaccinations, the distribution of AEs across age groups changed: the difference between age groups became less pronounced, and the ≥12–24 month group no longer exhibited the highest incidence (Supplementary Table 5). Taken together, these findings indicate that the elevated AE frequency observed in younger children may have been partially influenced by concomitant vaccine administration, which is common at 12–15 months of age under the NIP schedule. This observation underscores the importance of considering co-administration effects when interpreting AE profiles in post-marketing safety evaluations of vaccines.

The varicella-like rash is considered a relevant safety endpoint in the evaluation of live attenuated varicella vaccines, as it may be associated with the degree of viral attenuation. Considering its potential clinical significance, this study evaluated the occurrence of varicella-like rashes as one of the key safety results. Varicella-like rashes occurred in 10 out of 623 subjects (1.6%), including both localized (n=4) and generalized (n=6) rashes. The incidence observed in this study (1.6%) is within the range of varicella-like rash previously reported (1.0% to 15.0%) in clinical trials and post-marketing surveillance studies [1415]. Further details regarding the varicella-like rash events observed in this study are outlined below. None of these events were classified as SAEs or SADRs. Causality assessment suggested a possible or probable relationship with the study vaccine in all but one case, where the association with the generalized rash was considered unlikely.

All varicella-like rashes (n=10) were reported within 14 days after vaccination. Most varicella-like rashes were assessed as possibly or probably related to vaccination, except for one case considered unlikely. This finding highlights the importance of interpreting post-vaccination rashes in the context of potential wild-type exposure and the incubation period of varicella. The incubation period of the wild-type varicella virus typically ranges from 10 to 21 days (average, 14–16 days), overlapping with the time window in which vaccine-related reactions may also occur. In particular, when rash onset occurs on the day of vaccination (as observed in the unlikely case), natural infection during the incubation period should be considered a more plausible explanation than a vaccine-related event. Therefore, rashes appearing within the first two weeks after vaccination should be interpreted with caution, as they may represent either early breakthrough infections or vaccine-associated responses. Laboratory confirmation (e.g., PCR or genotyping) and clinical–epidemiological assessment are essential to distinguish between these possibilities and ensure accurate case classification [161718].

In comparison, in the Phase 3 clinical trial of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea), a total of 8 varicella-like rash events (3.2%) were reported among 251 subjects [19], which is slightly higher than the incidence observed in this PMS. While the proportion of generalized rashes was comparable -2.0% in the Phase 3 vs. 1.0% in this study-variations in study design, population characteristics, and surveillance methodology may account for the differences. These findings suggest that the frequency of varicella-like rash observed in real-world settings remains within an expected and manageable range for live attenuated varicella vaccines, considering the rates reported in the Varivax (Merck Sharp & Dohme LLC, USA) product label (3.8% for generalized rash and 3.4% for injection site rash) [1920], although direct cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size may not be sufficient to detect rare AEs. For example, rare neurological events such as aseptic meningitis and encephalitis—previously reported with similar live attenuated varicella vaccines —remain a theoretical concern [21]. Second, the 42-day follow-up period may not have been adequate to capture such delayed events, particularly those that may occur this time window. Considering this limitation, extended longitudinal follow-up is warranted to more accurately characterize the long-term safety profile of the vaccine and to identify rare events that may manifest later in the post-vaccination period. Last, the virological etiology of rash events was not confirmed, as virologic testing was not routinely performed in the routine clinical setting.

In conclusion, this four-year post-marketing safety evaluation of SKYVaricella Inj. (SK bioscience Co., Ltd, Korea) conducted a study in Korean children aged 12 months to 12 years demonstrated an acceptable safety profile. These findings support ongoing confidence in its use within NIP strategies. Continued pharmacovigilance and larger-scale studies will be warranted for monitoring its long-term public health impact.
