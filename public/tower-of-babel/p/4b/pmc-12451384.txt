A long-term quasi-equivalent in vitro study of high-strength Zn alloy interference screws for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction
Authors: Bo-Yao Li, Zhang-Zhi Shi, Xiang-Min Li, Xiao-Long Liu, Yu Yan, Lu-Ning Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiodegradable Zn alloy interference screws are promising for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. However, previous in vitro studies not only predominantly focus on short-term (≤30 days) immersion, but also neglect influence of crevice corrosion in bone tunnel. This study develops a Zn-0.45Mn-0.2 Mg alloy with yield strength of 288.3 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 325.4 MPa and elongation of 26.3 %. The alloy is fabricated into screws, inserted into polyurethane bone blocks and immersed in Hank's balanced salt solution for 180 days. The alloy degraded at a corrosion rate of 0.07 mm/y, with its yield strength and elongation eventually decreasing to 229 MPa and 4.3 %, respectively. Second phase detachment during long-term corrosion reduces alloy's surface fracture toughness (KIC) and increases stress intensity (KI) at corrosion pits. This causes premature fracture of the alloy (KI > KIC) and a larger decrease in the alloy's ductility (70 %) than that in strength (30 %). Crevice corrosion of the screws originates deep within the bone tunnel and spreads outward over time. Accumulation of Cl− ions exacerbates corrosion severity in the narrow thread tops of the screws. This study provides a foundation for quasi-equivalent in vitro studies of Zn alloy implants, and highlights the necessity of developing ductility-attenuation-resistant Zn alloys.Keywords: Zn alloy, Interference screw, Quasi-equivalent, Crevice corrosion, In vitro

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tear is among the most common joint injuries in individuals with high levels of physical activity [1]. After ACL tear, surgical reconstruction using autograft or allograft tendons as substitutes can stabilize the knee [2]. These grafts are secured in place with interference screws within bone tunnels [3]. Among commercial screw materials, titanium and stainless steel can provide high initial fixation strength while facilitating early bone integration [4]. However, both the materials may tend to induce chronic inflammation and require secondary surgery for removal [5].

Bioabsorbable polymer materials such as poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) has also been applied clinically [6]. However, polymer screws’ strength degrades rapidly. This can lead to several complications, including absorption fragments, fractures during placement, loss of initial fixation, and aseptic inflammation such as osteolysis and cyst formation [7,8]. Moreover, the by-products of polymers may induce long-term inflammatory responses in the surrounding tissues of the implant [9,10]. Recent advances in biodegradable Zn alloys have demonstrated the potential to revolutionize implants for orthopedics [[11], [12], [13]]. Zn alloy has garnered attention due to its excellent mechanical properties and good biocompatibility [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Zn alloys typically exhibit a more moderate degradation rate compared to biodegradable Mg alloys. Additionally, Zn alloys do not release hydrogen gas, which avoids the interference with tissue healing [14,18]. Interference screws made of Zn alloys can promote graft healing in ligament reconstruction by reducing bone loss around the tunnels. Sun et al. [14] investigated interference screws made from Zn-0.8Mn-0.4 Mg alloy. Compared with Ti6Al4V alloy screws, the Zn alloy screws significantly accelerated new bone growth during ACL reconstruction in rabbits and promoted tendon-bone integration through partial tendon mineralization [19].

In vivo testing is an essential process for evaluating the performance of medical implants, providing crucial reference data for clinical applications. Compared to in vitro testing, in vivo testing in big animals (e.g., pigs and monkeys) more closely resembles the actual service environment in the human being. However, this process is time-consuming, very expensive, and conducting implantation experiments on big animals is very challenging. Therefore, the development of quasi-equivalent in vitro tests that can simulate the in vivo environment is of great significance. Take interference screws as an example. According to clinical studies, patients typically require a recovery period of several months, during which the screws must remain stable to support graft fixation and the growth of new ligaments [20]. The follow-up period after surgery is usually 6 months to 1 year, during which the screws must maintain good fixation effects [[21], [22], [23]]. A previous study [14] involving the implantation of Zn alloy interference screws into rabbits lasted for 16 weeks. To better simulate real service conditions, it is necessary to conduct long-term immersion tests. There have been numerous in vivo and in vitro tests conducted on Zn alloys, but most of these tests focus on immersion periods of around 30 days [15,24,25]. Test involving 180-day long-term immersion is rare [26]. The impact of long-term immersion on corrosion mechanism and mechanical properties of Zn alloy is worth paying attention to.

In vitro testing must account for both the solution environment surrounding the implant and the influence of adjacent tissues and organs on its performance. In ACL reconstruction surgery, interference screws function within bone tunnels. Despite the close contact between the screw and the bone, crevice is inevitable. Crevice corrosion is a critical factor affecting the service life of implants, as it can lead to accelerated material corrosion and even premature fracture [27,28]. Previous studies [29,30] on crevice corrosion of medical metallic materials have predominantly focused on non-degradable titanium alloys, cobalt alloys, and stainless steels. In contrast, research on biodegradable Zn alloys remains limited. These alloys are active, and their corrosion mechanisms should be differed from those of non-degradable metallic materials. Therefore, it is essential to construct an in vitro crevice environment to simulate in vivo service conditions. Polyurethane bone blocks, which have densities, strengths, and Young's moduli similar to those of human bone, are used to mimic the human skeletal system [31]. In studies of mandibular fracture fixation systems, polyurethane bone blocks have been employed to simulate fracture models and evaluate the biomechanical stability of different plate and screw designs [32]. For example, the initial stability and stability after fatigue of locking reconstruction plates have been assessed by simulating the fixation process following fractures [32].

In this study, we developed a high strength and ductile Zn-0.45Mn-0.2 Mg alloy and evaluated its corrosion behavior, antibacterial properties, and cytocompatibility. The alloy was then fabricated into interference screws. To construct a quasi-equivalent testing environment for long-term crevice corrosion of screws in bone tunnel in vitro, we drilled the screws into polyurethane bone blocks and immersed them in Hanks’ balanced solution salt (HBSS) for 180 days. As we know, this is the first time that a 300 MPa grade Zn alloy has undergone 180 days immersion.

Materials and methods

The fabrication process of the screw

Zn-0.45 wt%Mn-0.2 wt% Mg (ZMM) alloy ingots were prepared by melting high purity Zn (99.99 %) alloyed with Mn (99.99 %) and Mg (99.99 %). The alloy melts were kept at 700 °C for 5 min, before being cast into a graphite mold and naturally cooled down. Extruding blanks of Φ 40 mm × 50 mm were cut from the alloy ingots. They were homogenized first at 250 °C for 2 h and then at 350 °C for 2 h, followed by furnace cooling. Then, they were extruded at 250 °C with a speed of 3 mm min−1 and an extrusion ratio of 16. The screws were fabricated from the ZMM rods with diameter of 10 mm (Fig. 1(a)).

(a) ZMM screw sample morphology and (b) Appearances of the sample screwing into polyurethane bone block.

Characterization of microstructure

The microstructure was observed by using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Regulus 8100, HITACHI, Japan) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) detector, or by another SEM (SEM, Gemini 500, Zeiss, German) equipped with an electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) camera. Phases in the alloy were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Smart Lab 9 kW, Rigaku, Japan).

Mechanical property testing

The tensile samples were prepared parallel to the extrusion direction (ED) and designed according to the standard ISO 6892–1:2016. Room temperature tensile testing was performed on a Universal Testing Machine (CMT4105, Zwick/Roell Z20, Germany) with a astrain rate of 1.4 × 10−3/s. Three parallel samples were tested. Hardness was tested by using a Vickers hardness tester (HV1000, Leica, Germany). Ten locations were measured and then averaged for each group.

Immersion tests

The screw samples were divided into two groups and immersed in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). The composition of HBSS is shown in Table 1. To one group were screwed into polyurethane bone blocks to simulate a real in vitro implant environment (Fig. 1(b)). Performance parameters of different bone blocks are shown in Table 2. The procedure is as follows: Use an electric drill to make a hole in the bone block. The hole should be slightly larger in diameter and shallower in depth than the screw, and at a 10-degree angle to the normal line. Then use a screwdriver to insert the screw into the hole. Temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.1 °C, and the ratio of the solution volume (ml) to the sample surface area (cm2) was 20:1, according to ASTM-G31-72. Body fluids circulate continuously within the human body, degradation products from biodegradable Zn alloys are subsequently removed through fluid flushing [14]. To simulate this situation, the HBSS was refreshed every two days according to GB 10124-88. Four parallel samples were prepared at each time point. Corrosion products on their surface were removed with 100 g/L NH4Cl solution at 70 °C for weight loss measurement, according to ASTM-G1-90. Open circuit potential (OCP) measurements were recorded for 30 min immersion. Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) tests were conducted in a range of OCP ± 0.5V at a scanning rate of 1 mV/s. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data were analyzed by using ZSimpWin software. Antibacterial examination

Antibacterial properties of the alloy against Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC25922, China) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, CMCC26003, China) were evaluated using the agar plate diffusion method and the sample and bacteria co-culture method. Three parallel samples were used for each of the alloy as well as pure Zn as the control group.

The agar plate diffusion method entailed the uniform application of a bacterial suspension, with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/ml, onto the surface of an agar culture dish. Circular alloy samples were positioned at the dish's center and the setup was incubated at a temperature of 37 °C for a duration of 24 h. The inhibition zone diameter (IZD) was observed and measured.

The method of co-culture of samples and bacteria involved placing the alloy discs in a 24-well plate, with each well receiving a sample, followed by the addition of 2 ml of bacterial suspension at a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU/ml. Alloy samples were then incubated in a shaker at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were retrieved, and the absorbance of the bacterial suspension was quantified using a spectrophotometer (uni-7200, China). The antibacterial properties of the materials were evaluated according to the optical density (OD) value of the bacterial suspension.

The above alloy samples were taken out of the bacterial solution and washed with PBS buffer to remove non-adherent bacteria on the surface. Then, the remaining bacteria were fixed, dehydrated, dried, and sprayed with gold. Morphology of bacteria on sample surfaces were observed by SEM.

Cytotoxicity tests

Alloy discs (Φ 10 mm × 2 mm) were first immersed in 75 % sterilizing alcohol and then exposed to ultraviolet light before testing. Then they were immersed in α-MEM medium (89 % α-MEM, 10 % fetal bovine serum, 1 % penicillin/streptomycin) for 72 h at 37 °C, in a sterile atmosphere with 5 % CO2 and 95 % humidity to make an extract of 100 % concentration. The ratio of sample surface area to extraction medium volume was 1.25 cm2 ml−1. Then the concentration of extract was diluted to 75 %, 50 % and 25 %.

Mouse embryo osteoblast precursors (MC3T3-E1) were purchased from IMMOCELL (Xiamen, Fujian, China) and used for cytotoxicity testing and cytoskeletal morphology observation. The specific primer sequences used for RT-PCR are detailed in Ref. [14].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software for one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the statistical significance of differences between groups. The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The difference between ∗p < 0.05 and ∗p < 0.01 was considered statistically significant and highly significant, respectively.

Results

Microstructure and mechanical properties

For convenience, ZMM alloy in as-extruded state is designated as ‘HE’. Fig. 3(a) and (b) show XRD spectra and metallographs of the ZMM (HE) alloy. The alloy is basically composed of Zn, MnZn13 and Mg2Zn11 phases. The microstructure of ZMM (HE) analyzed by EBSD is shown in Fig. 3(c). Zn grains are arranged along the extrusion direction (ED). The alloy shows a texture of [0001]Zn normal to ED, which is a typical texture for extruded metal with hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structure. This is due to preferred activation of {0001}<2 1‾1‾ 0> basal dislocation slipping [35]. The average grain size of Zn grains is 2.81 ± 1.03 μm (Fig. 3(d)). A kernel average misorientation (KAM) value less than 1° indicates recrystallization [36]. More than 98 % of Zn grains have recrystallized after hot extrusion (Fig. 3(e–f)).

In vitro corrosion behaviors

Surface morphology and chemical composition

SEM observations (from screw head to valley) are taken after1, 15, 30, and 180 days of immersion. All the samples are screwed into polyurethane bone blocks. Morphology of the corrosion layer and element analysis results are shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 5. On the first day of immersion, both the corrosion products and corrosion pits on the surface of the samples are relatively few (Fig. 4). As the immersion time increases, amount of corrosion products on the samples’ surfaces gradually increased. After 15 days, surface of the screw starts to become rough and uneven. White granular corrosion products cover the surface (Fig. 5(a1) and (f1)). Cracks visible in the corrosion layer after 30 days, which is possibly resulted from internal stress or dehydration during sample drying (Fig. 5(c2) and (g2)). EDS results show that the corrosion layers on all the samples are composed mainly of O, P, Ca, C and Cl, whose concentrations increase inversely to that of Zn with prolonged immersion duration. At 30 days, concentration of Cl at the valley increases significantly from <0.5 at the screw head to 4.22 at.% at the valley, which may cause by the Cl− enrichment of the crevice environment [37]. Fig. 6 shows morphology and element distribution of corrosion layer in cross-section area. According to linear EDS scanning profiles, the corrosion layers consist of O, P, Ca and Zn, agreeing with the results in Fig. 5. The corrosion layer thickness increases from 3.4 ± 1.1 μm at day 15–16.2 ± 3.4 μm at day 180 (Fig. 6). Deposition of Ca and P becomes increasingly evident over time. Their concentrations increase and the distribution is uniform (Fig. 5), which is beneficial for osteogenesis [38].

Morphologies of the samples' surfaces after removal of the corrosion products are shown in Fig. 7. With the extension of immersion time, non-uniform corrosion becomes more obvious. After 15 days, corrosion pits are observed on both screw head and valley (Fig. 7(a1) ∼ (f1)), then the pits multiply and grow with time (Fig. 7(a4) ∼ (f4)). The formation of corrosion pits is due to two factors: expansion of intergranular corrosion and detachment of the second phase caused by galvanic corrosion. This section focuses only on intergranular corrosion, while galvanic corrosion will be discussed with AFM results in section 4. Observation at 20,000 magnification reveals that corrosion preferentially occurs at grain boundaries (GBs) (Fig. 7(c1) and (f1)), due to their higher energies than intragranular regions [39]. The GB corrosion expands with immersion time, consuming adjacent grains. Several adjacent GB areas can be merged into a large pit, as shown in the blue dotted line in Fig. 7(f2). Diameter of the pit >5 μm, while that of the pore <1 μm. The pores accelerate the corrosion process (Fig. 7(c2) and (f2)). After 180 days, obvious localized corrosion appeared on the samples’ surface. Corrosion pits increased in both number and size. Corrosion at the screw valley is more serious than the head, with deeper GB corrosion and larger pits.

Mechanical properties change with immersing time

Fig. 10(a) and (b) show mechanical properties of ZMM (HE) alloy after 180 days’ immersion in HBSS under unscrewed and screwed situations. The alloy exhibits yield strength (YS) of 288.3 ± 1.2 MPa, ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 325.4 ± 4.5 MPa and elongation to failure (EL) of 26.3 ± 1.1 % at day 0 with the corresponding data listed in Table 4. These mechanical properties of the alloy attenuate with the increase of immersion time. The attenuation is relatively fast within 15 days, slows down between 15 and 90 days, and then accelerates after 90 days. The YS, UTS and EL values of all the samples decrease by 22 %, 30 % and 70 %, respectively. The relative magnitude of EL decrease is the largest, which may be related to the appearance of local corrosion. Stress concentration causes crack initiation and leads to fracture. The mechanical properties of the samples screwed into bone block decrease faster. This is because a crevice environment is formed between the bone block and the sample, which aggravates the corrosion of the alloy.

Fig. 11(c) shows Nyquist plots, fitting results of which are listed in Table 6. A diffusion impedance character is observed in the low frequency region of the immersed samples after 15 days, evidenced by the rising slope of the curve in the low frequency region. So that the Warburg diffusion element ‘W’ [43] is used to fit the EIS data of all the samples. The diameters of capacitive loops increase with immersion time, which is consistent with the change trend of the impedance modulus |z| (Fig. 11(e)). This indicates that the barrier effect of the corrosion product layer increases, resulting in a slower electron loss rate in the anode region [44]. Two-time constants exist in the Bode curves of the samples (Fig. 11(d)), So that a two-time constants model with Warburg diffusion element ‘W’ is used to fit the EIS data of all the samples. Values of charge transfer resistance (Rct) of the alloy increase with immersion time, due to the increasing thickness of corrosion layer on the freshly surface. Corrosion product layer resistance (Rc) gradually increases which indicates the corrosion product layer has a good protective effect. Mechanism of mechanical property degradation due to long - term immersion

Mechanical properties should be concerned during the material design process. Limited mechanical properties are blamed for screw breakage when it is inserted into bone channel during surgery. This happens commonly for biodegradable Mg screws [45]. Generally, tensile mechanical properties of biodegradable metals for orthopedic implants are supposed to be YS > 230 MPa, UTS >300 MPa, EL > 15 % [15]. Toughness of a material can be evaluated by PSE (= UTS × EL) [46]. Accordingly, the benchmark of PSE should be higher than 5.4 GPa·% (=300 MPa × 18 %). Mechanical properties of ZMM (HE) alloy decrease with the prolonged time of immersion (Fig. 10). Reasons may include changes in grain size and effects caused by corrosion. Grain sizes measured after different immersion times are shown in Fig. 15(c)–(e). There is no significant difference in grain size between 3.06 ± 0.44 μm and 3.58 ± 0.72 μm (∗P > 0.05). This indicates that the alloy has sufficient thermal stability at 37 °C. Therefore, degradation of the mechanical properties is primarily due to corrosion pits and ion release from surface layer (Fig. 15). Mg and Mn additions in Zn matrix contribute to solid solution, second phase and fine grain strengthening effects [15]. Mg2Zn11 and MnZn13 s phase particles can impede dislocation movement and promote the formation of a fine-grain microstructure [47]. Corrosion causes the second phases to dissolve and detach from the alloy surface in ionic form (Fig. 9). Meanwhile, the Zn matrix is compromised, leading to the development of corrosion pits on the surface (Fig. 15(b)).

After 60 days immersion, the YS and UTS of ZMM (HE) alloy still meet the benchmark, while the EL has dropped below the benchmark (Fig. 10). This is likely due to premature fracture caused by corrosion pits. The pits can be considered as semi-elliptical in shape (Fig. 15(b)). Fracture occurs when KI > KIC [48]. Dissolution of Mg2Zn11 and MnZn13 particles leads to formation of the corrosion pits (Fig. 7). In these pitted regions, stress concentration significantly elevates KI. Stress concentration at the bottom of the pit is schematically illustrated in Fig. 15(b), with the peak stress (σmax) occurring at the bottom [49]. For a semi-elliptical pit characterized by a = 3b, σmax can reach 7σ according to Eq. (8). As a result, cracks are prone to initiate and propagate at the pit bottom. Upon long-term immersion, the pits deepen with increased values of a/b, resulting in intensified σmax and KI. Simultaneously, proliferation of corrosion pits occurs, thereby increasing the likelihood of crack nucleation. Dissolution and detachment of the second phase particles lead to a reduction in strength of the surface layer, Therefore, its ability to resist crack propagation is reduced, i.e., KIC of the surface layer decreases. After long-term immersion, the fracture condition (KI > KIC) is more readily satisfied in the surface layer. When the crack in the surface layer grows beyond the critical length in the interior region beneath the surface, the crack will become unstable and propagate through the material, leading to premature fracture of the alloy. This explains why EL decreases more severely than YS and UTS.

(12), (13), Zn tends to form a passive layer of Zn(OH)2 under neutral or slightly alkaline conditions, with some regions likely dehydrating to form ZnO [39]. Additionally, HBSS solution contains H2PO4− and HPO42− (Table 1), which, according to Eq. (10), promotes the formation of Zn3(PO4)2 in the corrosion products. These corrosion products form a protective layer that mitigates further corrosion (Fig. 5), thereby reducing the dissolution of the alloy and decreasing the release of Zn2+ ions (Fig. 9). With the accumulation of corrosion products on the of the alloy's surface, stress concentration of the corrosion product layer leads to the generation of cracks (Fig. 5). Cl− in HBSS disrupts the corrosion product layer, leading to a slight increase in Zn2+ release (Fig. 9). After 80 days, the corrosion layer is sufficient to protect Zn substrate, stabilizing the release of various ions. Qu [61] et al. implant Zn-2Ag alloy screws into mice. they find that the corrosion products would diffuse into the surrounding tissues of the implants, but do not trigger inflammatory responses, indicating their biocompatibility. The recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for zinc is approximately 10–15 mg/day. The Zn-rich corrosion products can be effectively removed through human body's inherent pathways. For example, they can diffuse into surrounding cells via tissue fluid and be taken up, transported by albumin, and eliminated through renal and gastrointestinal excretion [14]. Therefore, they are considered safe.

Crevice corrosion mechanism

Previous studies [39,65,66] have primarily focused on clarifying how solution environments influence the corrosion behavior of Zn alloys. However, tissues in human body, such as bones, can also affect their degradation. Few studies have comprehensively investigated the corrosion of Zn alloys when they are in contact with biological tissues. After implantation, screw and bone are in close contact, but crevices still inevitably form. Elucidating the corrosion mechanism is instructive for its clinical application. Fig. 18(a1) and (a2) illustrate the mechanism of crevice corrosion between ZMM (HE) alloy and polyurethane bone blocks. In initial stage, Eqs. (11), (12) occur both inside and outside the crevice. Once oxygen in the crevice is consumed without timely replenishment, Eq. (12) no longer occurs inside the crevice. This creates a ‘differential aeration cell’ [67] due to the difference in oxygen concentration between inside and outside of the crevice. Regions of Zn matrix outside the crevice act as the cathode of the cell, while regions inside the crevice serve as the anode and releases Zn2+. Enrichment of Zn2+ attracts Cl− from the solution into the crevice to maintain charge balance. High concentration of Cl− not only intensifies pitting corrosion but also lowers the alloy's surface potential by disrupting the passive film [68]. This makes the Zn matrix more susceptible to corrosion.

In aqueous solutions, Zn2+ exists in a form of hydrated ions (e.g. Zn(H2O)62+) [69]. When the local Zn2+ concentration is elevated, Zn(H2O)62+ undergoes desolvation, releasing free water molecules into the surrounding environment [69]. These free water molecules subsequently engage in hydrolysis reactions with Zn2+, yielding Zn(OH)2 and liberating H+ ions, thereby augmenting the acidity of the local solution. Acidic environment causes Zn to shift from oxygen absorption corrosion to hydrogen evolution corrosion. At this time, the alloy can undergo electrochemical corrosion without the need for O2. The localized acidic environment also inhibits the formation of passivation layer, thereby accelerating corrosion of the alloy. Reactions are shown as follows [67]:

Crevice in screw is irregular due to the tops and valleys of their threads. Intervals between top and valley of the screw thread and the bone block are approximately 0.1 mm and 0.6 mm, respectively (Fig. 18(b1)). Severe pitting corrosion appears earlier at top of the screw thread, while the valley is relatively less affected (Fig. 7). This is attributed to the smaller crevice width at the top, and the larger width and confined space volume at the bottom. Luo et al. [68] found that an increase in the volume of the confined space within the crevice markedly reduces the accumulation of Cl−, thereby extending the incubation period of crevice corrosion. Hu et al. [70] found that the thinner the crevice, the faster the rate of oxygen consumption by cathodic reactions, leading to a greater potential difference between the inside and outside of the crevice. After 120 days of immersion, no significant difference in corrosion severity is observed between the top and the bottom (Fig. 7, Fig. 18). This indicates that after long-term immersion, oxygen is depleted and Cl− concentrations in different regions become similar, so that the degree of corrosion is also similar. Previous studies [67,71] often focused on crevice corrosion under conditions of uniform crevice width, with limited research addressing the impact of periodically varying crevice widths on corrosion.

In this study, wire beam electrode (WBE) is used to explore the crevice corrosion behavior of ZMM (HE) alloy. After the WBE with a crevice width of 0.1 mm is immersed in HBSS for 7 days, an anode region (with a lower potential and a larger current) appears in deep inside (Row 9 and Row 10) of the crevice. The potential decreases 20 mV and the maximum current reaches 2.1 × 10−5 A/cm2, as shown in Fig. 18(c1∼3). After 14 days’ immersion, the anode region (i.e., the corroded region) inside the crevice expands significantly (Fig. 18(d1∼2)). The potential decreases by 40 mV and the maximum current reaches 4.2 × 10−5 A/cm2. The cathode region outside the crevice is located near the crevice mouth, and the maximum cathode current reaches −3.9 × 10−5 A/cm2 (Fig. 18(d1∼3)). The expansion of the oxygen-depleted region inside the crevice leads to an increase in the anodic area. Moreover, the further reduction of oxygen concentration in the deeper regions of the crevice results in a steeper oxygen concentration gradient, which in turn amplifies the anodic current.

Alleviation of cytotoxicity by released Mg2+ ions

Zn alloys have attracted attention as a new type of degradable medical implant material due to their excellent antibacterial properties and osteogenic effect. These properties are related to the release of Zn2+ [15]. The antibacterial properties of Zn2+ stems from their ability to induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in bacteria, which disrupts bacterial cell membranes and inhibits bacterial DNA replication [72]. Cell wall of S. aureus is more sensitive to Zn2+ [73]. In contrast, cell wall of E. coli is surrounded by an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide molecules, which effectively prevent the penetration of Zn2+ ions [74]. Therefore, the ZMM(HE) alloy exhibits better antibacterial properties against S. aureus (Fig. 12). Additionally, high concentration of Mg2+ can disrupt normal physiological functions by increasing bacterial cell membrane permeability and causing intracellular leakage [75]. The antibacterial rate of E. coli for ZMM (HE) alloy is collected under different concentrations of Zn2+ (Fig. 19 (a)). Since the concentration of Mg2+ detected in the co-culture solution (0.2 mg/L) is much lower than that of Zn2+ (4 mg/L), only the effects of Zn2+ are discussed. As the concentration of Zn2+ increases, the antibacterial rate rises significantly. The threshold appears at 0.67 mg/L. When the concentration exceeds this value, the antibacterial rate exceeds 90 %, which meets the antibacterial standard of SN/T 2399-2010. Subsequently, the antibacterial rate no longer changes significantly with further increases in the concentration of Zn2+.

The osteogenic effect of the alloy can be attributed to the synergistic interaction of Zn2+ and Mg2+ ions. This interaction facilitates the influx of Zn2+ and Mg2+ into Bone Marrow Stromal Cells (BMSCs) while simultaneously inhibiting the efflux of Zn2+, thereby enhancing the activation of Runx-2 and promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [76]. The addition of Mn also has a positive impact on osteoblast activity. A previous study [77] has found that Mn increases the expression levels of osteogenic marker genes (ALP, Col I, OCN, and Runx-2) in MC3T3-E1 cells undergoing induced differentiation. Additionally, Mn can regulate the interactions between cells and the extracellular matrix by affecting integrin activity, thereby promoting cell proliferation, adhesion, and spreading [62].

Compare to pure Zn, ZMM(HE) alloy exhibits lower cytotoxicity to MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. 13). Zn2+, Mg2+ and Mn2+ positively influence the proliferation, adhesion, and spreading of MC3T3-E1 cells. Similar conclusions have also been observed in Zn06Mn006Mg alloy [15]. Fig. 19(b) shows MC3T3-E1 cell viability under different ion concentrations. Previous study [62] have shown that low concentrations of Mn2+ can enhance the activity of MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts and reduce the apoptosis rate. However, Mn2+ are not included in the discussion due to their low concentration (3 mg/L). As Zn2+ concentration increases, cell viability first rises and then declines. This indicates that low concentrations of Zn2+ promote osteoblast proliferation, while high concentrations of Zn2+ exert toxicity that inhibits cell viability. Meanwhile, under low Zn2+ conditions, increasing Mg2+ concentration can enhance cell proliferation. However, under high Zn2+ conditions (over 15 mg/L), an increase in Mg2+ concentration correspondingly increases cell damage. Table 8 summarizes existing research results on biocompatibility and ions releasing of Zn-Mg-based and Zn-Mn-based alloys in different concentrations of extract solutions. Most Zn-Mg-based alloys demonstrate good biocompatibility. High concentrations of Zn2+ disrupt the redox balance within cells and inhibit the activity of key enzymes [77]. Mg2+ can mitigate the cytotoxic effects of Zn2+ through several mechanisms: 1. High concentrations of Zn2+ inhibit the activity of glutathione reductase (GSR) by binding to the cysteine residues (Cys102 and Cys107) in its active site, leading to increased oxidative stress within the cell [78]. Mg2+ can reduce the inhibitory effects by competing with Zn2+ for these sites; 2. Mg2+ activate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which helps osteoblasts better tolerate the toxicity of Zn2+ while enhancing metabolic functions of osteoblasts [79]; 3. Mg2+ have antioxidant properties that can reduce oxidative stress damage within cells [80]; 4. Mg2+ can improve the formation and remodeling of the extracellular matrix, stabilizing the extracellular environment. Mg2+ can also facilitate the uptake of Zn2+ into cells, reducing their accumulation outside the cell and thereby lowering their toxicity [80]. For Zn-Mn-based alloys, Mn2+ can also improve biocompatibility and reduce cytotoxicity, but their effects are not as significant as those of Mg2+. Therefore, controlling the release of Zn2+, Mg2+, and Mn2+ can help enhance biocompatibility of ZMM(HE) alloy, which is of guiding significance for alloy design.

Limitations and future directions

This study provides a foundation for quasi-equivalent in vitro studies of Zn alloy implants. However, the model has significant limitations due to its oversimplified environment:

(1)Monocomponent fluid: Real in vivo environments contain a rich array of bioactive molecules (e.g., proteins, lipids, growth factors, cytokines), cells (inflammatory cells, osteoblasts, osteoclasts), and metabolites. PBS cannot simulate these complex biochemical signals, potentially overestimating material stability in an inert environment or underestimating potential biological responses.

Monocomponent fluid: Real in vivo environments contain a rich array of bioactive molecules (e.g., proteins, lipids, growth factors, cytokines), cells (inflammatory cells, osteoblasts, osteoclasts), and metabolites. PBS cannot simulate these complex biochemical signals, potentially overestimating material stability in an inert environment or underestimating potential biological responses.

(2)Static mechanical environment: Static immersion fails to replicate dynamic physiological loads (tension, compression, shear, micromotion), crucial for predicting long-term interface stability, fretting corrosion, and osseointegration under actual biomechanics.

Static mechanical environment: Static immersion fails to replicate dynamic physiological loads (tension, compression, shear, micromotion), crucial for predicting long-term interface stability, fretting corrosion, and osseointegration under actual biomechanics.

(3)Absence of bone bioactivity: Simulated bone blocks lack the biological activity of living bone, specifically the dynamic remodeling processes (resorption/formation) and biological responses to degradation products that occur in vivo.

Absence of bone bioactivity: Simulated bone blocks lack the biological activity of living bone, specifically the dynamic remodeling processes (resorption/formation) and biological responses to degradation products that occur in vivo.

(4)Dynamic local microenvironment: The critical early post-implantation phase involving inflammation, vascularization, and healing is completely unrepresented in static PBS immersion.

Dynamic local microenvironment: The critical early post-implantation phase involving inflammation, vascularization, and healing is completely unrepresented in static PBS immersion.

Given these limitations, future studies should focus on developing in vitro models or validation systems that more accurately reflect the physiological complexity in vivo:

(1)Optimize culture medium: Supplement immersion media (e.g., with serum proteins or dynamic cell culture media) to better simulate biomolecular interactions.

Optimize culture medium: Supplement immersion media (e.g., with serum proteins or dynamic cell culture media) to better simulate biomolecular interactions.

(2)Integrate biomechanical stimuli: Develop models incorporating in vitro dynamic loading devices. Incorporate dynamic loading devices applying physiologically relevant forces/micromotion during/after immersion.

Integrate biomechanical stimuli: Develop models incorporating in vitro dynamic loading devices. Incorporate dynamic loading devices applying physiologically relevant forces/micromotion during/after immersion.

(3)Supplement with in vivo validation: Critical findings must be verified in long-term animal implant studies assessing actual tissue responses and osseointegration.

Supplement with in vivo validation: Critical findings must be verified in long-term animal implant studies assessing actual tissue responses and osseointegration.

(4)Consider personalization: Investigate effects of different physiological states (e.g., osteoporosis) or specific environments.

Conclusions

In this paper, a novel Zn-0.45Mn-0.2 Mg alloy with yield strength of 288.3 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 325.4 MPa and elongation of 26.3 % is developed. The alloy is fabricated into screws, inserted into polyurethane bone blocks and immersed in Hank's balanced salt solution for 180 days. Conclusions are as follows:

1)Corrosion-induced second phase detachment reduces fracture toughness (KIC) of the alloy's surface layer. Stress concentration at the bottom of corrosion pits increases stress intensity (KI). These factors make the alloy more prone to fracture from its surface (KI > KIC), resulting in a more severe decrease in ductility (70 %) compared to strength (30 %).

2)Low oxygen concentration causes crevice corrosion of the screws to initiate deep within the bone tunnel and spread outward with time. This leads to Zn2+ ions accumulation and hydrolysis, causing acidification that shifts the mode of Zn corrosion from oxygen absorption to hydrogen evolution.

3)Thread tops of the screws experience more severe crevice corrosion than their bottoms due to narrower crevices. This is due to Cl− ions accumulation and their penetration through the corrosion products layer.

4)The alloy exhibits antibacterial properties against E. coli and S. aureus of 96.2 % and 97.3 %, respectively. The alloy shows good biocompatibility due to alleviation of cytotoxicity by released Mg2+ ions. The alloy also shows good osteogenic differentiation ability in vitro, rendering the alloy screws potential for ACL reconstruction.
