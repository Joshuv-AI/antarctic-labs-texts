Expert Consensus on the Clinical Application of Poly ADP Ribose Polymerase Inhibitors in Breast Cancer (2025 Edition)
Authors: Fei Ma, Binghe Xu, China Anti‐Cancer Association Committee on Clinical Research of Anticancer Drugs, National Expert Committee on Monitoring Clinical Application of Antineoplastic Drugs, Breast Cancer Expert Committee of National Cancer Quality Control Center, Expert Committee on Chemotherapy Quality Control of Beijing Cancer Treatment Quality Control and Improvement Center
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBreast cancer represents a major threat to women's health in China. Its continuously rising incidence contributes to a growing disease burden, thereby creating a critical demand for novel and effective therapeutic strategies. In recent years, poly ADP‐ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, as a novel class of anti‐tumor drugs targeting the DNA damage repair pathway, have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in treating breast cancer with BRCA1/2 mutations. By selectively inhibiting DNA repair in tumor cells, these agents induce a “synthetic lethality” effect, offering a new option for precision therapy. Currently, PARP inhibitors such as Olaparib and Fluzoparib have been approved for clinical use. This consensus, based on the latest evidence‐based medical data, provides guidance on the standardized application and safety management of PARP inhibitors in breast cancer treatment, aiming to serve as a reference for clinical practice.

Background

Breast cancer has become one of the most common cancers and a leading cause of cancer death among women worldwide [1]. Identifying novel therapeutic targets and developing corresponding targeted therapies are pivotal to improving breast cancer care. Poly ADP‐ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors can inhibit DNA damage repair and induce death in tumor cells with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) [2]. The breast cancer susceptibility genes (BRCA) are important tumor suppressor genes involved in DNA damage repair and transcriptional regulation, playing a key role in maintaining genomic stability. Abnormalities in the structure and function of BRCA1/2 genes are closely related to the development of various malignancies, including breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and prostate cancer, and are more common in patients with a family history of breast or ovarian cancer, young breast cancer patients, and those with triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) [3]. Since the synthetic lethality effect of PARP inhibitors with BRCA mutations was confirmed in 2005 [4], targeted drugs for DNA repair pathways have gradually attracted attention. In recent years, PARP inhibitors have emerged as a research focus in precision oncology, with olaparib, talazoparib, and fluzoparib successively approved for the treatment of breast cancer harboring germline BRCA mutations. However, their clinical application in breast cancer needs further standardization. Therefore, China Anti‐Cancer Association Committee on Clinical Research of Anticancer Drugs, National Expert Committee on Monitoring Clinical Application of Antineoplastic Drugs, Breast Cancer Expert Committee of National Cancer Quality Control Center, Expert Committee on Chemotherapy Quality Control of Beijing Cancer Treatment Quality Control and Improvement Center jointly organized and invited domestic oncology experts with rich clinical experience and relevant interdisciplinary experts to co‐author the “Expert Consensus on the Clinical Application of PARP Inhibitors in Breast Cancer 2025 Edition.” This aims to better standardize the application of PARP inhibitors in breast cancer and strengthen their safety management.

Methodology for Consensus Development

Consensus Formation Process

The working group held consensus meetings to review and discuss existing evidence on PARP inhibitor treatment in breast cancer. Consensus recommendations were formed through discussion combining clinical experience. The initial draft was compiled by the writers and reviewed and revised by other members to form the final consensus document.

Literature Search Method

The literature search for this consensus primarily included foreign databases (PubMed, Embase) and Chinese databases (CNKI, CBM, Wanfang Data). The search was conducted up to June 30, 2025, including systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, and randomized controlled trials involving PARP inhibitors in breast cancer treatment.

PARP Inhibitors and Their Mechanisms of Action

PARP is a family of enzymes responsible for synthesizing poly (ADP‐ribose) (PAR), a polymer composed of ADP‐ribose units linked by ribose‐ribose glycosidic bonds. The PARP family has 18 members, categorized into five groups based on structure and function. PARP1 is the most typical member, with multiple biological roles, including DNA damage signaling, base excision repair (BER), gene transcription regulation, and apoptosis [5], accounting for over 90% of PARP family function and being the primary target of PARP inhibitors.

DNA damage mainly includes single‐strand breaks (SSBs) and double‐strand breaks (DSBs). Defective DNA damage repair is a key driver of tumorigenesis. Most tumors have varying degrees and forms of DNA damage repair defects. DNA homologous recombination repair (HRR) is the most important pathway for DNA DSBs repair, involving numerous genes, including BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, BARD1, and so forth. HRD, primarily in the form of BRCA mutations, is relatively common in breast and ovarian cancers. Individuals carrying germline BRCA1/2 mutations have about a 10‐fold higher risk of developing breast cancer compared to the general population [6]. PARP inhibitors are analogs of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) that inhibit PARP enzymatic activity by competitively binding to the catalytic domain of PARP in place of NAD+ [5]. PARP inhibitors competitively bind to PARP, leading to the accumulation of SSBs, which are converted into DSBs during DNA replication. In cells with intact homologous recombination (HR) function, these DSBs can be efficiently repaired through HRR. However, in HRD tumors, such as those with BRCA1/2 mutations, DSBs cannot be repaired, resulting in chromosomal instability, genomic mutational accumulation, and ultimately apoptosis. This cell death mechanism, based on defects in two complementary DNA repair pathways, is known as synthetic lethality [7] and represents the primary mechanism of action of PARP inhibitors.

BRCA Gene and Other HRR Gene Testing

Testing Population

A domestic study involving 8085 breast cancer patients revealed that the germline mutation rate of BRCA1/2 genes among Chinese breast cancer patients was 5.3%. Among these, the mutation prevalence in TNBC patients was approximately 11.2% [3]; the mutation rate was 8.7% in young patients (< 40 years) without a family history of breast or ovarian cancer, 18.1% in patients with a family history, and as high as 28.4% in young patients with a family history [7]. BRCA mutations are divided into germline (heritable) and somatic (non‐heritable) types. Currently, most PARP inhibitor research reports involve germline BRCA mutations. The classification standards established by the “Chinese Expert Consensus on BRCA1/2 Data Interpretation” [8] and the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics [9], germline mutations are classified into five types: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance, likely benign, and benign. BRCA germline mutations mentioned in this consensus specifically refer to pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations.

The Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Familial Hereditary Tumors (2021 Edition) [10] indicates that the appropriate population for BRCA1/2 testing falls into two categories: individuals with a personal history of breast cancer and individuals regardless of personal history. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines [11] primarily recommend BRCA genetic testing based on factors including young age at diagnosis, high‐risk family history, and TNBC. However, specific testing criteria continue to vary considerably across different domestic and international guidelines and consensus documents.

Recommendation 1: BRCA genetic testing is recommended for breast cancer patients with a hereditary high‐risk profile if they meet any of the following criteria: diagnosed of TNBC, age at diagnosis ≤ 50 years, ≥ 1 first‐degree relative with breast or ovarian cancer history, presence of bilateral or multiple primary breast tumors, male breast cancer; Germline BRCA mutation testing is recommended for breast cancer patients who meet any of the following criteria: (1) high‐risk, HER2‐negative early‐stage disease who are candidates for adjuvant intensive therapy (see Recommendation 6); or (2) HER2‐negative metastatic breast cancer requiring salvage therapy. (Recommendation Grade: 1).

Testing Methods

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) is currently the predominant method for detecting BRCA mutations. In contrast to Sanger sequencing, NGS enables accurate sequencing of all exonic and intronic regions of the BRCA genes in a single assay, providing high sensitivity, specificity, and cost‐effectiveness. The Chinese Expert Consensus on Germline BRCA Mutation Screening in the Chinese Population (2024 Edition) recommends NGS as the preferred method for germline BRCA mutation testing [12]. For NGS‐based BRCA1/2 testing procedures and quality control standards, reference can be made to the BRCA1/2 Gene Testing Guidelines Based on Next‐Generation Sequencing Technology (2019 Edition) [13] and the industry standard General Technical Requirements for BRCA Gene Mutation Detection Kits and Databases: YY/T 1865‐2022 [14]. Sanger sequencing is generally employed for the validation and confirmation of pathogenic variants initially identified by NGS.

However, NGS also has certain limitations, including relatively low sensitivity and accuracy in detecting large genomic rearrangements (LGRs) in BRCA genes. Currently, multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA) is the most widely adopted method for identifying LGRs, and it is primarily performed using blood samples for germline mutation testing [15]. Therefore, if no pathogenic point mutations or small insertions/deletions are identified by NGS, additional testing for LGRs is recommended—particularly in individuals with a relevant family history. Moreover, compared to family history‐based screening alone, combined germline testing of BRCA along with genes such as RAD51C, RAD51D, BRIP1, and PALB2 can detect nearly twice as many individuals at risk for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer in the population [16]. Accordingly, the Chinese Expert Consensus on Germline BRCA Mutation Screening in the Chinese Population (2024 Edition) [12] recommends that individuals with a strong family history and negative germline BRCA results via NGS should be considered for expanded genetic screening of other susceptibility genes.

Recommendation 2: BRCA1/2 gene testing or NGS panels including BRCA1/2 are recommended. Sanger sequencing may serve as a complementary approach, for instance, to validate NGS findings or for targeted analysis of specific known familial mutations. In cases with a significant family history where NGS results are negative but there remains a strong suspicion of hereditary breast cancer susceptibility, MLPA can be employed to detect large rearrangements in BRCA1/2, and additional susceptibility genes can be screened. (Recommendation Grade: 2A).

In China, four PARP inhibitors—olaparib, niraparib, fuzuloparib, and pamiparib—have been approved for ovarian and prostate cancers.

Olaparib

Olaparib, the first PARP inhibitor approved worldwide, exerts anti‐tumor effects through synthetic lethality by targeting tumors with defective DNA repair mechanisms. It is currently indicated for the treatment of ovarian, breast, and prostate cancers.

Advanced Breast Cancer

The Phase III OlympiAD trial (NCT02000622) [17] demonstrated that among patients with germline BRCA‐mutated, HER2‐negative metastatic breast cancer who had received ≤ 2 prior lines of chemotherapy, median progression‐free survival (PFS) was significantly longer in the olaparib group than in the standard‐therapy group (7.0 vs. 4.2 months; Hazard Ratio [HR] = 0.58, p < 0.001). The objective response rates (ORR) were 59.9% and 28.8% for the olaparib and standard‐therapy groups, respectively. Updated 2019 follow‐up data [18] showed no significant improvement in median overall survival (OS) for olaparib compared to standard therapy (19.3 vs. 17.1 months, HR = 0.90, p = 0.513). However, for patients who had not received prior chemotherapy, median OS was significantly longer in the olaparib group than in the standard‐therapy group (22.6 vs. 14.7 months, HR = 0.51, p = 0.02). Compared with standard therapy, olaparib was associated with a modest increase in all‐grade adverse events but a reduction in grade ≥ 3 adverse events (36.6% vs. 50.5%) [17]. The TBCRC 048 study evaluated the efficacy of olaparib in advanced breast cancer with somatic and germline mutations in HR‐related genes beyond germline BRCA mutations. The results demonstrated that the ORR for patients with PALB2 germline mutations and BRCA somatic mutations was 82% and 50%, respectively, with median PFS of 13.3 and 6.3 months. However, no significant efficacy was observed in patients harboring mutations in other HR genes [19].

Recommendation 3: Olaparib may be considered for patients with germline BRCA mutations, HER2‐negative advanced breast cancer who have received prior chemotherapy. (Recommendation Grade: 1).

Recommendation 4: Olaparib may be considered for patients with germline BRCA mutations, HR‐positive, HER2‐negative advanced breast cancer who have progressed after endocrine therapy and have not received prior chemotherapy. (Recommendation Grade: 2A).

Recommendation 5: For patients with HER2‐negative advanced breast cancer harboring somatic BRCA mutations or germline PALB2 mutations, olaparib may be considered as a later‐line treatment option. (Recommendation Grade: 2B).

Adjuvant and Neoadjuvant Therapy for Early Breast Cancer

Approximately 50% of BRCA mutation carriers develop TNBC, a subtype for which chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment. Prior to the availability of PARP inhibitors, these patients were managed primarily through routine follow‐up, without targeted adjuvant strategies to reduce the risk of recurrence in TNBC patients.

The Phase III OlympiA trial [20] enrolled patients with HER2‐negative, germline BRCA‐mutated, high‐risk early breast cancer. For TNBC patients with non‐pathological complete response (non‐pCR) after neoadjuvant therapy and either postoperative pathological tumor size > 2 cm or lymph node involvement, and for HR‐positive, HER2‐negative patients with non‐pCR after neoadjuvant therapy, CPS + EG score ≥ 3, and ≥ 4 positive lymph nodes postoperatively, 1 year of olaparib following completion of (neo)adjuvant and local therapy significantly improved invasive disease‐free survival (iDFS) and distant disease‐free survival (DDFS) compared with placebo. The 6‐year iDFS rates were 79.6% in the olaparib group versus 70.3% in the placebo group (HR = 0.65, 95% Confidence interval [CI]: 0.53–0.78), while the 6‐year DDFS rates were 83.5% versus 75.7% (HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.53–0.81). Furthermore, olaparib was associated with a 28% reduction in the risk of death, with 6‐year OS rates of 87.5% in the olaparib group compared to 83.2% in the placebo group (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.56–0.93).

The phase II GeparOLA trial [21] broadened the indication for PARP inhibitor therapy by including patients with HER2‐negative early breast cancer who had either somatic BRCA mutations or high HRD scores. Results demonstrated a higher pathological complete response (pCR) rate with neoadjuvant paclitaxel plus olaparib compared to paclitaxel plus platinum‐based chemotherapy (55.1% vs. 48.6%). The Olaparib group did not show statistically significant superiority but met the non‐inferiority hypothesis (15%). Subgroup analysis showed that the pCR rate in the olaparib group reached 76.2% in patients under 40 years of age and 52.6% in those with HR‐positive disease, both significantly higher than observed in the chemotherapy group. In the OlympiA trial, the 4‐year iDFS rates were 82.7% in the olaparib group compared to 75.4% in the chemotherapy‐alone group. The 4‐year OS rates were 89.8% and 86.4%, respectively. These results suggest that beyond germline BRCA mutation carriers, patients with somatic BRCA mutations or HRD positivity may also derive benefit from PARP inhibitors.

Recommendation 6: For patients with germline BRCA mutation, high‐risk, HER2‐negative breast cancer, olaparib maintenance therapy for 1 year following adjuvant chemotherapy is recommended if any of the following criteria are met: (1) TNBC patients: non‐pCR after neoadjuvant therapy, postoperative pathological tumor size > 2 cm, or lymph node involvement; (2) HR‐positive HER2‐negative patients: non‐pCR after neoadjuvant therapy with a CPS + EG score ≥ 3, or ≥ 4 positive lymph nodes postoperatively. (Recommendation Grade: 1).

Talazoparib

Talazoparib not only inhibits PARP enzyme activity—thereby disrupting DNA repair and leading to cell death—but also potently traps PARP onto DNA. Its PARP‐DNA trapping efficacy is nearly 100‐fold greater than that of other PARP inhibitors [22]. Preclinical studies indicate that trapping PARP on DNA is more effective at inducing cancer cell death than mere enzymatic inhibition. With its dual mechanism of action and high oral bioavailability, talazoparib represents a highly potent PARP inhibitor [23].

Advanced Breast Cancer

The phase III EMBRACA trial (NCT01945775) [24] enrolled patients with locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer who had received ≤ 3 prior lines of chemotherapy, including treatment with a taxane and/or an anthracycline. Talazoparib significantly improved median PFS by 3 months compared to standard chemotherapy (which included capecitabine, eribulin, gemcitabine, or vinorelbine), with a median of 8.6 versus 5.6 months (HR = 0.54, p < 0.0001). Median OS was 22.3 versus 19.5 months (HR = 0.76, p = 0.105), and ORR was 62.6% versus 27.2%. No significant difference was observed in the incidence of all‐grade or grade ≥ 3 adverse events between the treatment groups. The EMBRACA trial confirmed both the efficacy and safety of PARP inhibitors in patients with BRCA mutation, HER2‐negative breast cancer.

Recommendation 7: Talazoparib may be considered for patients with germline BRCA mutation, HER2‐negative, locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer who have received prior chemotherapy. (Recommendation Grade: 2B).

Early Breast Cancer

The phase II NEOTALA trial [25] evaluated the efficacy of talazoparib monotherapy as neoadjuvant treatment in patients with germline BRCA mutation, HER2‐negative early breast cancer. Enrolled patients with tumor size > 1.5 cm, stage N0‐3M0, underwent surgery after completing 24 weeks of neoadjuvant therapy with talazoparib. The pCR rate in the evaluable population was 45.8%. Single‐agent talazoparib demonstrated a pCR rate comparable to that of anthracycline‐taxane‐based regimens, along with a favorable tolerability profile. It is expected to become a novel neoadjuvant treatment option for patients with germline BRCA mutation, HER2‐negative breast cancer in the future.

Fuzuloparib

Advanced Breast Cancer

A Phase III randomized controlled trial (NCT04296370), presented at ESMO 2024, evaluated the efficacy of fuzuloparib monotherapy or in combination with apatinib versus the physician's choice of standard chemotherapy (including capecitabine, gemcitabine, or vinorelbine) in patients with germline BRCA1/2 mutations, HER2‐negative locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer [26]. The study results showed that fuzuloparib plus apatinib combination significantly extended median PFS compared to standard chemotherapy (11.0 vs. 3.0 months), reducing the risk of disease progression by 73%. The fuzuloparib monotherapy group also showed a significant improvement in median PFS (6.7 vs. 3.0 months), corresponding to a 51% reduction in risk. Regarding OS, the combination therapy achieved a median OS of 29.2 versus 21.5 months with standard chemotherapy, representing a 42% reduction in the risk of death. Meanwhile, monotherapy with fuzuloparib resulted in a median OS of 31.5 months, reducing mortality risk by 39% compared to chemotherapy. In terms of safety, both regimens were well‐tolerated overall, with no new adverse events identified (Table 3).

Other PARP Inhibitors

Niraparib

The phase III BRAVO trial [27] enrolled patients with germline BRCA mutation, HER2‐negative advanced breast cancer who had received ≤ 2 prior lines of chemotherapy for advanced disease, or whose disease recurred within 12 months after adjuvant chemotherapy. Results indicated no statistically significant difference in median PFS between niraparib and physician's choice chemotherapy (4.1 vs. 3.1 months; HR = 0.96, p = 0.86). Although niraparib has demonstrated efficacy both as first‐line maintenance and in later‐line treatment of advanced ovarian cancer [28, 29], its therapeutic role in breast cancer warrants further investigation.

Veliparib

Veliparib is a selective inhibitor of PARP1 and PARP2. In a phase I study, veliparib monotherapy achieved an ORR of 37% in patients with germline BRCA mutation advanced TNBC [30]. The phase III BROCADE3 trial compared veliparib versus placebo, both in combination with paclitaxel and carboplatin, as first‐line treatment for patients with germline BRCA mutation, HER2‐negative advanced breast cancer. The primary endpoint, PFS, was met: median PFS was 14.5 months in the veliparib group compared to 12.6 months in the placebo group (HR = 0.705, p = 0.002), corresponding to a 29% reduction in the risk of progression. OS data are not yet mature [31].

Veliparib combination therapy was explored in neoadjuvant treatment for early TNBC. The Phase III BrighTNess study showed that adding veliparib did not improve pCR rates compared to paclitaxel plus carboplatin alone (53% vs. 58%). Long‐term follow‐up also showed no improvement in event‐free survival or OS with the addition of veliparib [32, 33].

Rucaparib

Rucaparib is an oral small‐molecule inhibitor targeting PARP‐1, PARP‐2, and PARP‐3 and has demonstrated antitumor activity across multiple cancer types. The phase Ib COUPLET study is designed to evaluate the combination of rucaparib and atezolizumab in patients with advanced gynecological cancers or TNBC [34].

Recommendation 9: Patients with germline or somatic BRCA mutations, or other HRD‐positive advanced breast cancer, are recommended to actively participate in PARP inhibitor clinical trials. (Recommendation Grade: 1).

Consensus on PARP Inhibitor Safety Management

PARP inhibitors have demonstrated significant efficacy in breast cancer with germline BRCA mutations. Compared to chemotherapy, orally administered PARP inhibitors provide improved safety profiles and better health‐related quality of life, contributing to enhanced treatment experience and adherence. Variations in pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and mechanisms of action among different PARP inhibitors result in distinct adverse reaction profiles. Commonly reported reactions include myelosuppression (e.g., anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia), gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea), and fatigue. All adverse events are graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) (Table 4).

Monitoring and Managing Hematological Toxicity

Hematologic toxicity is a common class of adverse events associated with PARP inhibitors, manifesting primarily as anemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia. These events typically occur within the first 3 months of treatment and represent the majority of grade 3–4 toxicities. They are also the most frequent reason for dose modifications, interruptions, or discontinuation of therapy. A complete blood count should be obtained prior to initiating treatment with a PARP inhibitor. Caution is advised in patients whose baseline hematologic parameters fall below the required thresholds; treatment should only be started after blood counts have normalized, either spontaneously or with appropriate supportive measures. Regular monitoring and proactive management throughout therapy are essential (Table 5).

Anemia is the most frequently reported hematologic adverse event associated with PARP inhibitor therapy, which may be linked to PARP2 inhibition and its role in erythropoiesis [35]. A study demonstrated that PARP2 deletion impairs erythroid progenitor differentiation and shortens red blood cell survival in mice under conditions of elevated erythropoietin plasma levels, indicating that exogenous erythropoietin supplementation may not be an optimal strategy for anemia management [36]. Although human studies are lacking, transfusion is generally indicated for symptomatic anemia or when hemoglobin levels fall below 7 g/dL [35].

Thrombocytopenia has been more frequently reported with niraparib treatment. In general, PARP inhibitor therapy should be interrupted if platelet counts fall below 100 × 109/L, and weekly monitoring is recommended until platelets recover to ≥ 100 × 109/L. Treatment may be resumed at the same or a reduced dose upon recovery. However, dose reduction should be considered if platelet levels remain below 75 × 109/L or if thrombocytopenia recurs.

Neutropenia of any grade has been reported in approximately 20% of patients receiving maintenance PARP inhibitor therapy [37], with incidence rates exceeding 50% in those treated with niraparib [38]. Management generally involves interrupting treatment if the absolute neutrophil count falls below 1.0 × 109/L, accompanied by weekly blood monitoring. Therapy may be resumed once ANC recovers to above 1.5 × 109/L, potentially at a reduced dose. Alternatively, reinitiating treatment at a lower dose may also be considered once neutropenia improves to grade 2 (ANC ≥ 1.0 × 109/L).

Gastrointestinal Toxicity

Nausea and vomiting are recognized as class‐effect adverse events associated with PARP inhibitors. When used as maintenance therapy, niraparib, olaparib, rucaparib, and veliparib are classified as moderate emetic risk agents, with reported incidence of vomiting exceeding 30% of patients [39, 40]. A phase III trial evaluating talazoparib monotherapy in breast cancer classified it as having minimal to low emetic risk [24]. Nausea typically occurs early during treatment, most commonly in the first cycle. Administration of metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, or promethazine 30 min before each PARP inhibitor dose is a recommended management strategy, particularly during the initial treatment phase. Taking food 30–60 min prior to dosing may also help reduce emesis. For patients with anticipatory nausea and vomiting, benzodiazepines may be considered [41]. Although serotonin (5‐HT3) receptor antagonists such as ondansetron are effective antiemetics, they are not generally recommended for chronic use with PARP inhibitors due to the continuous dosing schedule and an associated increased risk of constipation [42].

Dyspepsia has been reported in approximately 10%–20% of patients receiving maintenance PARP inhibitor therapy [37]. Clinicians may recommend dietary modifications such as consuming smaller, more frequent meals and avoiding potential triggers (e.g., fatty foods, spicy items, and alcohol). If symptoms persist, proton pump inhibitors can be considered; for refractory cases, prokinetic agents may be appropriate.

Diarrhea and/or constipation occur in approximately one‐third of patients receiving maintenance PARP inhibitor therapy, with most cases being grade 1–2 in severity. Other commonly reported gastrointestinal adverse events include abdominal pain and decreased appetite. Loss of appetite has been observed in about 20% of patients on maintenance treatment, is generally mild, and is reported as severe in fewer than 1% of cases [37]. Regular monitoring of body weight may be advisable to assess nutritional status during treatment.

Hepatic and Renal Toxicity

Transient elevation of transaminases is a commonly observed adverse event during the initial treatment cycles with PARP inhibitors. It is generally self‐limiting, not accompanied by other signs of liver toxicity, and typically resolves within 3–4 cycles of therapy [43]. In cases of grade 4 ALT/AST elevation, PARP inhibitor treatment should be interrupted until levels recover to grade ≤ 2. Therapy may then be resumed at a reduced dose with weekly monitoring for at least 3 weeks. For grade 3 elevations, management should be individualized. Treatment may be continued under close monitoring if bilirubin remains normal and alkaline phosphatase is below three times the institutional upper limit of normal. If ALT/AST levels do not decrease within 2 weeks, treatment should be withheld until ALT/AST levels resolve to grade ≤ 2, after which it may be resumed at the same or a lower dose.

Elevated creatinine levels have been reported in approximately 10%–12% of patients treated with various PARP inhibitors [43, 44, 45]. In cases of significant elevation, other potential causes should be excluded and renal imaging may be considered. If glomerular filtration rate remains preserved, dose modification or treatment interruption may not be necessary.

Pulmonary Toxicity

Dyspnea and/or cough occur in approximately 10%–20% of patients receiving PARP inhibitor therapy, with the majority being grade 1–2 in severity [37]. A comprehensive symptom assessment should be conducted, and any reversible comorbid conditions should be addressed. If new or worsening respiratory symptoms develop, treatment should be interrupted and a diagnostic workup—including high‐resolution computed tomography—should be performed to rule out pneumonitis. If investigations reveal no abnormalities and symptoms resolve, resumption of therapy may be considered. For symptomatic patients with radiographic evidence of pneumonitis, PARP inhibitor treatment should be discontinued. Further evaluation—including pulmonary function tests and bronchoscopy—is recommended, along with multidisciplinary management. Initiation of corticosteroids and antibiotics, as clinically indicated, is advised.

Dermatological Toxicity

Dermatologic adverse events have been primarily reported with rucaparib and niraparib treatment [43]. Patients receiving PARP inhibitor therapy should be advised to practice sun protection, and referral to dermatology may be considered when indicated.

Fatigue Management

Fatigue is one of the most frequent class‐effect adverse events associated with PARP inhibitors, affecting 50%–70% of patients, with most cases being grade 1–2 in severity [37]. According to NCCN guidelines, physical activity is recommended for managing fatigue; however, the intensity should be carefully individualized, particularly in patients with concurrent treatment‐related anemia or thrombocytopenia. Adjunctive approaches such as massage therapy and psychosocial interventions may also be beneficial. Additionally, optimizing the management of sleep disturbances and nutritional deficiencies may help alleviate fatigue.

Other Safety Monitoring

Long‐term administration of PARP inhibitors necessitates close attention to their potential effects on vital organs, including the heart and liver, given the risk of adverse events such as cardiotoxicity and hepatic dysfunction. Therefore, regular cardiac function tests, such as electrocardiograms and echocardiograms, are recommended to monitor heart health. It is crucial for clinicians, patients, and their families to understand these potential adverse reactions and how to manage them. Additionally, educating them on the recognition of early symptoms is essential (e.g., palpitations, chest pain, nausea, and fatigue), which can help improve treatment adherence and overall outcomes. Close collaboration with doctors and nursing teams enables patients to better manage and prevent these potential side effects, thereby maintaining optimal health throughout long‐term treatment.

Treatment Management in Special Populations

The management of special populations (e.g., elderly patients, those with cardiac or hepatic dysfunction, and pregnant or lactating women) demands particular attention when administering PARP inhibitors for breast cancer treatment. Key points for the therapeutic management of special populations are shown as follows:

Elderly Patients

Elderly patients with breast cancer frequently present with comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, as well as declined organ function, necessitating cautious use of PARP inhibitors. These individuals often exhibit reduced tolerance to treatment‐related adverse events and are at increased risk of complications such as myelosuppression (including anemia and neutropenia) and gastrointestinal toxicities (e.g., nausea and vomiting). Management strategies include regular monitoring of full blood counts to evaluate myelosuppression; dose adjustment based on renal function to minimize drug accumulation and toxicity; and careful review of concomitant medications to avoid interactions that may exacerbate adverse effects.

Patients With Cardiac Dysfunction

PARP inhibitors may cause cardiotoxic effects, including electrocardiographic abnormalities and arrhythmias. For patients with a known history of cardiac disease, a baseline assessment of cardiac function—using electrocardiography and echocardiography—is recommended before initiating treatment to establish a reference for future comparisons. Management strategies include regular monitoring of cardiac function, especially if symptoms such as palpitations or chest pain develop; careful dose adjustment in high‐risk patients; and collaboration with cardiologists to optimize individualized treatment plans.

Patients With Hepatic Dysfunction

Hepatic impairment can alter the metabolism and excretion of PARP inhibitors, potentially leading to elevated systemic drug exposure and increased toxicity. Therefore, in patients with pre‐existing liver dysfunction, baseline liver function tests, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and total bilirubin, should be performed before initiating therapy. Dosage adjustments should be considered based on the degree of hepatic impairment. Management strategies include regular monitoring of liver function, and in cases of worsening hepatic parameters, dose reduction or temporary discontinuation of treatment should be considered.

Pregnant and Lactating Women

PARP inhibitors are contraindicated during pregnancy and lactation due to the risk of fetal harm and potential adverse effects in breastfed infants. Female patients of reproductive potential must use effective contraception throughout treatment and for at least 6 months after the last dose. A pregnancy test should be performed prior to initiating therapy in these individuals. Breastfeeding is not recommended during PARP inhibitor use, as these agents may be excreted in human milk. Management strategies include recommending the use of reliable contraceptive methods during treatment and after treatment completion; if a patient is pregnant or planning to become pregnant, the risks and benefits of treatment must be weighed, and a temporary suspension of treatment may be necessary.

Patients With Multiple Comorbidities

The management of patients with multiple comorbidities requires a comprehensive and coordinated approach. Individualized treatment plans should be developed based on a thorough evaluation of each condition and potential drug–drug interactions. A multidisciplinary team involving oncology, cardiology, hepatology, and other relevant specialties is recommended to optimize holistic care. Close monitoring of the patient's overall health status is essential, with timely adjustments to the treatment regimen to ensure both safety and efficacy.

PARP inhibitors hold broad prospects for application in breast cancer treatment; however, extra caution must be exercised in the treatment management of special populations. Strengthening the management and monitoring of relevant adverse reactions through regular organ function monitoring, dosage adjustments, and patient education can improve patient adherence and treatment outcomes, thereby enhancing prognosis.

Summary and Outlook

PARP inhibitors have demonstrated significant antitumor activity in the salvage treatment of germline BRCA mutation advanced breast cancer, as well as in the adjuvant and neoadjuvant settings for early‐stage disease, offering new therapeutic options for patients with tumors exhibiting HRD. The safety profile of PARP inhibitors is generally manageable; however, close monitoring during treatment is essential. Prompt intervention should be implemented upon the occurrence of adverse events to ensure safe and standardized use of PARP inhibitors.
