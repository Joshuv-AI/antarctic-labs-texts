An improved precision-cut lung slices technique for pulmonary vasoconstriction
Authors: Shengquan Luo, Pu Liao, Xinyu Song, Xiuchuan Yue, Xin Wu, Rui Xiao, Liping Zhu, Qinghua Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study presents an improved technique for precision-cut lung slices to overcome the challenge of identifying pulmonary arteries in hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction.MethodsThe innovation employs the connexin 40 immunofluorescence staining to precisely identify pulmonary arterial endothelium.ResultsThe results of the experiments demonstrated that the refined protocol successfully differentiated between pulmonary vessels, trachea or bronchus, and bronchial arteries in precision-cut lung slices. This differentiation was accomplished by administrating a cell tracer into the pulmonary circulation to aid in identifying specific structure. Furthermore, this study accurately distinguished pulmonary arteries from veins using CX40 immunostaining.ConclusionsThis study validates the accuracy and reliability of the improved precision-cut lung slices method in evaluating hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction particularly in pulmonary arteries.

Introduction

Tissue slicing has been employed in organ metabolism and toxicology studies since the 1920s. Stadie and Riggs developed a microtome with specialized blades in the 1940s [1], which improved the initial manual sectioning methods. This advancement significantly enhanced slice thickness uniformity and tissue viability, establishing critical groundwork for modern tissue-slicing applications. Vibrating microtomes can complete continuous tissue sections with thinner thickness and higher precision after gradual improvement and optimization, which are now termed precision cut tissue slices [2]. This standardized technique is currently applied across multiple tissue types including brain, liver, kidney, and lung [3, 4].

Traditional cell culture has been extensively used in pulmonary disease research, such as pulmonary hypertension and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. However, it still bears significant differences compared to the in vivo environment due to discrepancies in cellular interactions and tissue complexity. Precision-cut lung slices (PCLS) overcome these challenges by enabling the study of lung tissue structure and function within a natural three-dimensional context [5]. PCLS have emerged as a crucial tool in respiratory research for the assessment of pharmacological and toxicological impact on the tracheal and pulmonary vasculature systems.

Lung tissue consists of a bronchial tree branching into alveoli, forming a dense honeycomb-like structure to facilitate gas exchange. Therefore, obtaining PCLS from lung tissue is more challenging compared to solid organs. A critical breakthrough was achieved in the 1980s when Packle and Fisher introduced airway infusions in hamsters and rats with heated liquid agarose [6]. The solidified agarose maintains lung inflation below 25 °C, preventing the collapse of airway and alveoli during tissue sectioning. Nevertheless, the challenges faced by PCLS extend beyond these. Firstly, many standardized PCLS protocols lack viability verification steps, risking false-negative outcomes in experimental results. Secondly, the presence of various structures such as the trachea, blood vessels, and alveoli in lung tissue, as well as the distinction between veins and arteries, poses challenges to the accuracy and reliability of research and observational results using PCLS, especially in studies focusing on pulmonary hypertension and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction where pulmonary arteries are the primary subjects. PCLS typically range from 150 to 350 micrometers in thickness. The overlapping structures in PCLS obscure cellular details compared to thinner (< 10 μm) hematoxylin-eosin-stained sections. Various methods have been explored by researchers to assist in distinguishing blood vessels from the trachea. Most research used anatomical landmarks for identification, proposing that pulmonary arteries accompany airways, while pulmonary veins are located separately [7–9]. Acharya used α-SMA antibody staining to label smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and elastic staining to locate elastic fibers in vessel walls [10]. Desireddi injected cell tracer reagents into the pulmonary circulation to differentiate pulmonary vessels from the trachea [11]. The NG2-DsRed transgenic mice expressing red fluorescent protein in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) and pericytes has been employed to locate intra-alveolar arteries [12]. The bronchial and pulmonary arteries in a fresh, thick slice exhibit considerable structural homology under a microscope, rendering it more challenging to differentiate without meticulous histological examination.

This study introduces an optimized PCLS workflow that integrates viability assessment and arterial-specific identification for conducting further mechanistic studies on pulmonary hypertension and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (Fig. 1). Current challenges in applying the PCLS technique to pulmonary hypertension research primarily involve (1) distinguishing bronchi from pulmonary vessels within PCLS preparations.‌ (2) differentiating pulmonary arteries from bronchial arteries, and (3) distinguishing pulmonary arteries from pulmonary veins. Our approach combines three key innovations: (1) quantitative viability screening using the WST-1 Cell Proliferation & Cell Viability Detection Kit, (2) dynamic vascular tracing with Cell Tracker CM-DiI, and (3) CX40 (GJA5-encoded connexin protein) immunohistochemistry targeting arterial endothelium. CM-DiI, a vital fluorescent tracer, was introduced via right ventricular perfusion into the pulmonary circulation for vascular labeling. This approach not only enables precise delineation of the tracheobronchial tree from pulmonary vasculature but also facilitates differentiation between pulmonary arteries of pulmonary circulation and bronchial arteries of systemic circulation [13, 14]. The connexin 40 (CX40) encoded by the GJA5 gene, one gap junction consists of a cluster of closely packed pairs of transmembrane channels and plays a crucial role in arteries [15, 16]. CX40 is specifically expressed in arterial endothelium and is abundantly expressed in the lung [17, 18]. This improved protocol enhances reproducibility in pulmonary hypertension and hypoxic vasoconstriction research, offering significant improvements in artery identification accuracy and experimental efficiency compared to existing techniques.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

All procedures received ethical approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Medical College ([2024]IACUC number-4975), Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Animal

The study utilized ten C57BL/6 mice, aged 7–8 weeks, with an average weight of 20 g obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., LTD. (specific pathogen-free grade, SYXK2019-004). Animals were housed in groups of three per cage under controlled 12-hour light/dark cycles. Standard laboratory chow and water were provided ad libitum, with autoclaved wood-chip bedding replaced twice weekly to maintain hygienic conditions.

Reagents

Gelatin (porcine skin, type A, 300 g Bloom, Vetec), Cell Tracker CM-DiI (Yeasen Biotechnology, 40718ES50), low melting point agarose (CAS 9012-36-6), tissue perforator (GLOBALEBIO), Penicillin-Streptomycin (1%, Gibco), WST-1 Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Biyuntian, C0035), CX40 Polyclonal Antibody (BA1593, Boster, rabbit polyclonal antibody), FITC-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Servicebio, GB22303), α-SMA specific antibody (Proteintech, 67735-1-Ig, Clone: 1E9A11), Cy3-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Proteintech, SA00009-1), and Antifade Mounting Medium (Servicebio, G1401).

Preparation of precision cut mouse lung slices (PCLS)

Adult mice (C57, ~ 20 g) were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg). A 24G catheter was inserted into the trachea and connected to an animal ventilator. The respiratory rate was set to 80–120 breaths per minute, the tidal volume to 5–8 mL/kg, and the positive end-expiratory pressure to 5 cmH2O. Mice were placed on a heating plate set at 37 °C to maintain body temperature. The chest was opened along the lower edge of the ribs to expose the heart and lungs. The abdominal cavity was then opened, the intestinal loops were set aside, and the abdominal aorta was exposed. The lungs were perfused in situ with HEPES-Ringer buffer (10 mM HEPES, 136.4 mM NaCl, 5.6 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 2.2 mM CaCl2, 11 mM glucose, pH 7.4) containing 250 IU/mL heparin through the right ventricle, and the perfused blood was drained through an abdominal aortic incision. HEPES-Ringer buffer containing 6% gelatin was used to prepare the tracer perfusion solution with a Cell Tracker CM-DiI concentration of 2 µM [11]. The superior and inferior vena cava were ligated using No.4 surgical suture, and then 1 mL of tracer perfusion fluid was perfused through the right ventricle using a 1 mL syringe. The aorta and pulmonary artery were ligated to prevent the tracer from leaking out of the pulmonary circulation. During the procedure, a heating pad was maintained at 37 °C for 15 min to ensure stable core body temperature for optimal CM-Dil uptake. HEPES-Ringer buffer containing 1.5% low melting point agarose was pre-heated in a water bath to above 65 °C until the agarose was completely dissolved without any bubbles [19]. 1.5 mL of low melting point agarose perfusion solution was infused into the trachea through the tracheal intubation, and the trachea was ligated to prevent liquid leakage after disconnecting the ventilator. The heart and lung tissues were immediately removed and placed into pre-cooled HEPES-Ringer buffer at 4 °C to promote agarose coagulation [20]. Care was taken to avoid damaging the heart and lung tissues during removal to prevent liquid leakage from the lung. A piece of lung tissue was obtained using an 8 mm tissue perforator after cooling the lung tissue at 4 °C for 30 min. The lung tissue was fixed on an oscillating microtome or vibratome (Leica VT1000) and soaked in HEPES-Ringer buffer at 4 °C throughout the sectioning process. Section thickness was set at 200 μm, speed was set to 1.25 mm/s and the sectioning frequency to 80 Hz. Several previous studies indicated that PCLS thickness between 150 μm and 300 μm was optimal for monitoring mouse pulmonary arteries, resulting in better arterial activity and observability [21–23]. The parameters for slicing speed and frequency varied across previous reports, largely due to differences in vibratome models [22, 24, 25]. We examined a series of settings in our vibratome in pilot experiments and finalized the sectioning speed and frequency for this study. The lung slices were transferred to a sterile culture dish containing Penicillin-Streptomycin (1%, Gibco) supplemented culture medium and placed in a 36 °C cell culture incubator. PCLS were washed three times with PBS, and the culture medium was changed every half hour for the first two hours during cell culture on the first day [11, 26]. The washing step removed unbound dye, cell debris, residual agarose, and other impurities following CM-DiI perfusion. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium every day from the second day.

Activity detection of PCLS

The metabolic activity of PCLS was measured using the WST-1 Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit [27]. WST-1 can perform an NADH-dependent enzymatic cleavage reaction with mitochondrial dehydrogenase in the presence of electronic coupling reagents to generate the orange-yellow water-soluble formazan product. WST-1 can be used to detect cell proliferation and cytotoxicity by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm. The WST-1 powder in the kit was added to 5 mL of electron coupling reagent to dissolve and stored at -20 °C. The stock solution was diluted in the culture medium at a ratio of 1:10 to become WST-1 working solution with a final concentration of 2 µM. PCLSs were transferred to a 24-well plate, and the culture medium was removed and replaced with 250 µL of freshly prepared WST-1 working solution. 100 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well microtiter plate after 1 h of incubation at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 (Molecular Devices) with a reference wavelength of 595 nm.

Acute hypoxia experiment on PCLS

PCLS was transferred to a custom-made perfusion chamber to prevent the slices from floating [28]. The perfusion chamber was placed on an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus FV300) and perfused with HEPES-Ringer buffer heated to 37 °C at a rate of 2 mL/min. The pulmonary blood vessels were located based on the Cell Tracker CM-DiI which showed red fluorescence under 570 nm excitation wavelength, and the morphological changes of the pulmonary blood vessels on the lung slices were recorded every minute with a camera connected to the inverted fluorescence microscope.

The experiment was divided into a blank control group and a hypoxic group. The control group did not undergo any treatment during the experiment and only used HEPES-Ringer buffer (21% oxygen, 5% carbon dioxide, 74% nitrogen) for perfusion for 25 min. The hypoxic group was switched to hypoxic HEPES-Ringer (1% oxygen, 5% carbon dioxide, 94% nitrogen) after the fifth minute of perfusion under normoxic conditions [29]. A 40 mM high-potassium solution was added to the two groups for 10 min to observe the vasoconstriction activity after the perfusion of each lung precision slice. The inactive blood vessels were identified as those without a high-potassium contraction response, and only the active blood vessel areas in the slice were quantified.

Immunostaining and lung-slice confocal imaging

Fresh mouse (C57, WT, ~ 20 g) lung tissue was fixed with tissue fixative for 48 h, dehydrated, transparentized, and embedded in paraffin before being sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Two consecutive paraffin sections were used for hematoxylin-eosin staining and immunofluorescence staining to compare the tissue structure and staining at the same location. The first paraffin section was dewaxed, stained with hematoxylin and eosin for 5 min, dehydrated with anhydrous ethanol, and then mounted with neutral resin. The second paraffin section was dewaxed and then blocked with blocking buffer (5% goat serum, 0.5% Triton X100) for 1 h. CX40 Polyclonal Antibody (1:500) was added, overnight at 4 °C [20]. PCLS was washed three times in PBS and placed in FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L for 1 h. After the sections were blocked again, the above steps were repeated using α-SMA−specific antibody (1:300) and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG H&L, and the sections were finally sealed with antifade mounting medium. The hematoxylin-eosin staining sections and immunostaining sections were scanned using a fully automatic section scanning system (Olympus VS120).

PCLS was fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min in a 24-well plate [20] and washed three times in PBS buffer after the acute hypoxia experiment was completed. Then each PCLS was placed in blocking buffer (10% goat serum and 0.5% Triton X100) for 1 h. CX40 polyclonal antibody (1:500) was added, overnight at 4 °C [20]. PCLS was washed in PBS three times and placed in FITC-conjugated goat Anti-rabbit IgG H&L for 6 h. Finally, PCLS was mounted with an anti-fluorescence quenching agent. A Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (OLYMPUS FV3000) was used to evaluate the morphology of the pulmonary artery.

Repositioning of the pulmonary artery

After the PCLS hypoxia-perfusion experiment, the spatial relationships and morphological features of vasculature under brightfield were recorded using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus FV300, 10× objective). Subsequently immunofluorescence staining was performed as per the standard protocol. Finally, the identical vascular regions were relocated under brightfield using a confocal microscope (OLYMPUS FV3000, 10× objective) for subsequent fluorescence imaging. To ensure that the vascular regions for immunofluorescence staining and functional analysis originated from the same anatomical location, the images under bright field were recorded using a low power microscope (10×) after PCLS perfusion. The low power microscope held a field of view diameter of 2 mm, it determined the anatomical features around the observed blood vessels and their relative positions on an 8 mm diameter PCLS slice (center or edge). During immunofluorescence imaging, the vessels were firstly located under bright field using the low-power microscope. Because the number of morphologically intact and clearly observable blood vessel cross-sections was limited, the blood vessels were quickly located at the same anatomical location based on the anatomical features surrounding the blood vessel under low power lens and their relative position on the slice.

Statistical analysis

The pulmonary artery area was calculated using the software Pmage-Pro, and the data were expressed as mean ± SEM. The viability of PCLS was quantified using the dual-wavelength correction method (measurement at 450 nm and reference at 595 nm) to eliminate background interference from nonspecific absorbance. According to the WST-1 manufacturer’s protocol, a ΔOD value of 0.3 corresponds to a viable cell density of 104 cells. Based on this threshold, PCLS with ΔOD values below 0.4 (after dual-wavelength correction at 450/595 nm) were excluded to ensure consistent metabolic activity across experiments. Changes in the pulmonary vascular area were evaluated by pixel summation using grayscale threshold (range 20%, smooth 0), and reevaluated by lining the liner boundaries by hand in the software Image-Pro. Use two-way repeated measures ANOVA to compare groups. Statistical significance was determined using a probability level (P) of 0.05. The SigmaPlot software package was used for all statistical tests.

Results

The injected Cell Tracker CM-DiI cannot be metabolized after they enter the endothelial cells of the blood vessels and therefore serve as a tracer to distinguish blood vessels from bronchi and bronchial arteries [5]. Bronchi and bronchial arteries were not labeled by the cell tracer, while pulmonary vessels emitted red fluorescence under the excitation wavelength of 570 nm (Fig. 2A). Concurrently, hematoxylin-Eosin staining of consecutive sections validated the structural relationship between bronchi and arteries within the same anatomical region (Fig. 2B).

The cell viability of PCLS was assessed using the WST-1 assay kit. Compared to the blank control, for which only the WST-1 working solution was applied, the PCLS group showed a significant increase in activity (Fig. 3A). The PCLS samples from the same mouse with higher viability were selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3B). Several techniques have been successfully used to assess PCLS viability, including either WST or lactate dehydrogenase (L-LDH) alone [30, 31] or both [32]. Others used the WST-1 kit and live/dead staining ratio [25]. These methods generally yield consistent conclusions regarding PCLS viability. L-LDH is an enzyme commonly used to detect cytotoxicity, based on the release of L-LDH when cell membrane integrity is compromised, and therefore it is widely used in toxicology studies of PCLS. WST-1, on the other hand, mainly reacts with mitochondrial metabolites and primarily detects cell proliferation and activity. This study aimed to assess baseline metabolic activity without prior cytotoxic insult, the WST-1 assay was selected.

After the perfusion experiment, the CX40 antibody was used to verify whether the observed vessels were arteries [15, 16]. Following immunofluorescence staining of the same PCLS, the pulmonary artery labeled with CX40 was observed to show green fluorescence under the excitation wavelength of 570 nm, which verified that the blood vessel we selected for observation was the pulmonary artery.

Positive CX40 staining confirmed that the observed vessels were pulmonary arteries (Fig. 5A and B, right). Analysis using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant difference between the normoxic control and hypoxic groups starting in the 9th minute of hypoxia (Fig. 4C).

The smooth muscle cell marker α-SMA alone is insufficient to distinguish between pulmonary arteries and pulmonary veins because both types of vessels can contain smooth muscle cells. We verified the specific localization of CX40 in arterial endothelium through HE staining and immunofluorescence. The results showed that the pulmonary arteries were stained with both α-SMA (red) and CX40 (green), while pulmonary veins containing smooth muscle cells were only stained with α-SMA (red) (Fig. 5A). Small veins without smooth muscle were not stained. Similar to the bronchial-arterial analysis, HE staining of consecutive sections revealed the anatomical correlation between pulmonary arteries and veins in the same region (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The primary challenges in applying the Precision-Cut Lung Slice (PCLS) technique to the study of pulmonary hypertension include distinguishing bronchi from pulmonary vessels, pulmonary arteries from bronchial arteries, and accurately identifying pulmonary arteries versus veins. Our optimized PCLS protocol leveraged the dual-circulation characteristics of systemic and pulmonary circulation, using CM-Dil dye to clearly distinguish the trachea, bronchial arteries, and pulmonary vessels. Subsequently, CX40 labeling was employed to precisely differentiate pulmonary arteries from pulmonary veins within the pulmonary circulation. The main findings indicate that CX40 immunofluorescence staining along with regular PCLS procedures makes it easier to distinguish arteries from veins within pulmonary vessels, thereby excluding some vessels misidentified due to unclear structures and providing more accuracy (Fig. 4). Additionally, the inclusion of a step to verify PCLS viability eliminates results affected by viability issues (Fig. 3).

Current arterial identification techniques face three principal constraints. Most of these methods rely on subjective analysis and judgment based on anatomical structures and morphologies, while a few use α-SMA or transgenic mice with spontaneous fluorescence to locate smooth muscle cells [10, 12]. However, these methods have apparent limitations. First, structural analysis in thick (150–350 μm) PCLS specimens proves unreliable due to tissue overlap artifacts that obscure histological details visible in conventional thin HE staining sections. Second, α-SMA labeling shows inadequate specificity as venous smooth muscle cells generate false-positive signals (Fig. 5). Third, although transgenic mice with spontaneous fluorescence can theoretically accurately locate pulmonary arteries, their economic and time costs are much higher than those of regular immunofluorescence staining. Moreover, as mentioned earlier, spontaneous fluorescence in smooth muscle cells does not necessarily indicate that the vessel is a pulmonary artery. Our CM-Dil tracer/CX40 co-labeling strategy circumvents these limitations through an economical, objective workflow that maintains compatibility with downstream analyses.

This study adopts an improved sequential design for the PCLS protocol (Fig. 1). Before slicing, the lung vessels were perfused from the right ventricle with a gelatin perfusion solution containing the Cell Tracker CM-DiI, facilitating subsequent differentiation of pulmonary vessels from the trachea under a microscope, as shown in Fig. 1. The arterial vessels in the lungs include bronchial arteries and pulmonary arteries. The bronchial arteries typically arise from the proximal segment of the descending thoracic aorta [13, 14]. We performed simultaneous ligation of both the aortic root and main pulmonary artery after Cell Tracker CM-DiI perfusion. This surgical approach selectively restricted the fluorescent tracer to the pulmonary circulation while preventing its diffusion into the bronchial arteries, thus enabling clear differentiation of pulmonary arteries from bronchial arteries. After slicing, we used the WST-1 kit to assess cell viability in PCLS, excluding those with poor or no viability. Following observation and monitoring of the acute hypoxic perfusion, we fixed the PCLS with paraformaldehyde and performed immunofluorescence staining using a CX40 antibody to verify under a confocal microscope whether the hypoxic-treated vessels were pulmonary arteries. Additionally, observations indicated that bronchi can also be stained by CX40 (Fig. 6), which verified the necessary use of CM-DiI in early experimental steps to pre-differentiate bronchi from vessels.

The improved method proposed in this study objectively and accurately identified pulmonary arteries in PCLS studies, eliminating the subjectivity and uncertainty in observing vessels using traditional PCLS methods. It provides guidance for future use of PCLS in studying the pathophysiology of pulmonary arteries and lung diseases. It opens up the possibility of multi-marker co-staining, such as the CX40 together with α-SMA staining, and also possibly offers a new approach for distinguishing human pulmonary arteries from veins in human lung tissues. The human GJA5 gene also encodes the CX40 protein [33, 34].

A limitation of this study is that PCLS cannot remain viable for any subsequent experiment(s) after the CX40 immunofluorescence staining step. Preliminary differentiation relies solely on the Cell Tracker CM-DiI, which can accurately differentiate pulmonary vessels from the trachea before the acute hypoxic experiment. During the acute hypoxic perfusion, the only structure we can clearly record is the pulmonary vessel, followed by CX40 immunofluorescence staining to differentiate pulmonary arteries from veins.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that the improved precision lung slice method accurately identifies pulmonary arteries, providing a reliable histopathological assessment in studies related to pulmonary hypertension and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, offering valuable insights into disease mechanisms and potential translation.
