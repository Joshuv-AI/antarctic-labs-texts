Spatial epidemiology of malaria by bed net utilization in 19 sub-Saharan African countries: a DHS-based study (2013–2023)
Authors: Gelila Yitageasu, Lidetu Demoze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health concern in Sub-Saharan Africa, where prevention efforts such as insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) play a critical role in reducing transmission. However, malaria prevalence varies across geographic areas and household contexts, including differences in mosquito bed net utilization (treated, untreated, or absent), as well as other environmental and socioeconomic determinants. Understanding the spatial distribution of malaria and how it varies across households with different bed net utilization patterns can provide descriptive insights to support malaria control programs. This study aimed to describe and map the spatial distribution of malaria among households with different mosquito bed net utilization categories (no net, treated net, and untreated net) across 19 sub-Saharan African countries.MethodA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted using the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data from 19 sub-Saharan African countries. The analysis included 126,424 individuals who underwent malaria rapid diagnostic testing across 11,594 survey clusters. Malaria infection status was measured at the individual level, while spatial analyses were conducted at the cluster (enumeration area) level, the primary sampling unit used in DHS surveys. Data were cleaned, weighted, and analyzed using Stata version 17, accounting for the DHS complex sampling design (sampling weights, clustering, and stratification). Malaria prevalence and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated. Spatial patterns of malaria distribution were explored using ArcGIS version 10.7 and SaTScan™ version 10.0.2.ResultThe pooled malaria prevalence across 19 sub-Saharan African countries was 24.0% (95% CI 18–30%), with regional variation observed: Central Africa recorded the highest prevalence at 26% (95% CI 18–34%), followed by West Africa at 25% (95% CI 15–35%), and East Africa at 20% (95% CI 10–31%). Regarding bed net usage, 49.17% (95% CI 48.89–49.45%) reported no bed net, 48.06% (95% CI 47.78–48.34%) used insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), and 2.77% (95% CI 2.68–2.86%) used untreated bed nets. Malaria prevalence was highest among no bed net users with 32.75%, followed by 28.91% among untreated bed net users and 13.23% among ITN users. Spatial analysis indicated significant spatial clustering of malaria cases (Global Moran’s I = 0.159, Z-score = 239.06, p ≤ 0.001). Hotspot areas identified by the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic included Benin, Burkina Faso, Togo, Uganda, Rwanda, and parts of Republic of the Congo and Mozambique. SaTScan analysis detected 34 significant malaria clusters overall, with 23 clusters among no bed net users, 25 among ITN users, and 12 among untreated bed net users.ConclusionDespite ongoing malaria control efforts, malaria prevalence remains substantial across sub-Saharan Africa, with notable regional and spatial disparities observed across areas with different bed net utilization patterns. These descriptive spatial findings highlight geographic heterogeneity in malaria burden and may help inform geographically targeted malaria control strategies. Strengthening access to insecticide-treated nets, improving community awareness, and prioritizing identified hotspot areas may contribute to strengthening malaria control efforts toward achieving global malaria reduction targets by 2030.Keywords: Malaria, Spatial epidemiology, Insecticide-treated nets, DHS, Spatial clustering, Sub-Saharan Africa

Introduction

Malaria remains a major worldwide public health problem, particularly in Africa and other highly endemic regions [1]. The disease is transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes, which serve as vectors for the malaria parasite [2, 3]. Among the five parasite species that infect humans, Plasmodium falciparum is the most prevalent in Africa and is responsible for the majority of severe disease and malaria-related deaths [4, 5]. It predominantly occurs in tropical and subtropical regions [6]. Plasmodium vivax is also widely distributed and occurs in temperate, tropical, and subtropical regions [6]. Malaria imposes a substantial burden on population health and economic development in many endemic countries [7].

According to data from 85 countries where malaria is common, the number of malaria cases worldwide increased from 227 million in 2019 to 241 million in 2020, with most of the rise taking place in African nations [8]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there were approximately 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 deaths globally in 2023 [9]. The WHO African Region carried the largest burden of malaria in 2023, accounting for 95% (569,000) of all malaria fatalities and 94% (246 million) of all malaria cases globally [9]. Six countries in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) account for more than half (55%) of all malaria cases globally: Nigeria (27%), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (12%), Uganda (5%), Mozambique (4%), Angola (3.4%), and Burkina Faso (3.4%) [10]. These statistics highlight the substantial public health burden of malaria in sub-Saharan Africa. Malaria transmission remains high in SSA due to the widespread presence of efficient mosquito vectors (particularly the Anopheles gambiae complex), favorable climatic conditions, and environmental factors that support vector survival and reproduction [11, 12].

Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 3.3 aims to end the epidemics of malaria, tuberculosis, and other communicable diseases by 2030 [13]. In response to rising malaria incidence in the ten sub-Saharan African countries with the highest malaria burden between 2015 and 2016, the WHO launched the “High Burden to High Impact (HBHI)” or 10 + 1 initiative to accelerate progress toward global malaria control targets [14].

Over the past two decades, global malaria control programs have aimed to improve access to prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where the disease burden is highest [1, 15, 16]. Between 2005 and 2014, international funding for malaria prevention more than doubled, increasing from US$960 million to US$2.5 billion [16]. As a result of the scale-up of effective malaria control interventions, an estimated 10.6 million deaths and 1.7 billion malaria cases were averted globally between 2000 and 2020 [10]. These gains in malaria morbidity and mortality have been largely attributed to expanded coverage of proven interventions, including insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), and additional vector control approaches such as larval source management and environmental modification [17, 18]. However, according to the World Malaria Report 2024, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 deaths globally in 2023, with approximately 95% of deaths occurring in the WHO African Region, highlighting that progress has stalled and malaria remains a major public health challenge [19]. This evidence indicates that while significant reductions in malaria morbidity and mortality were achieved in earlier years, recent trends show stagnation or increases in burden in many settings, underscoring the need for sustained and strengthened control efforts.

Malaria transmission is influenced by a complex interaction of multiple factors. These include demographic factors (age and sex), environmental factors (presence or absence of bushes and forests, altitude, wall, floor, and roof material), climatic factors (temperature, humidity, and rainfall), and socioeconomic factors (education, occupation, bed net utilization, indoor residual spraying, and income), all of which have been documented to influence malaria transmission [20–26]. Parasite resistance to antimalarial drugs and mosquito resistance to insecticides have also been associated with persistent malaria transmission [27, 28]. Therefore, spatial patterns of malaria transmission across geographical areas reflect the combined influence of multiple determinants rather than a single factor.

The Global Technical Strategy for Malaria (2016–2030) aims to substantially reduce the global malaria burden, particularly in SSA, by reducing global incidence and mortality by at least 90% by 2030 compared to 2015 [28]. To align with this target, there is a need to generate and document adequate epidemiological evidence to guide malaria prevention and control efforts, particularly in SSA. Among the various malaria prevention strategies, insecticide-treated nets remain one of the most effective household-level malaria prevention interventions for reducing human–mosquito contact. However, malaria risk across geographical areas reflects the combined influence of multiple contextual determinants. Understanding how malaria prevalence varies spatially across households with different bed net utilization patterns may therefore provide useful descriptive insights for malaria control strategies.

Although previous studies have investigated malaria prevalence and its determinants in SSA, limited evidence exists on the large-scale spatial distribution and clustering of malaria across different bed net utilization categories using multi-country population-based data. Spatial epidemiological analysis provides an important approach for identifying geographical variations and clustering of malaria risk, which can support targeted malaria control interventions. Therefore, this study aims to describe and map the spatial distribution and clustering of malaria across households with different mosquito bed net utilization categories in 19 sub-Saharan African countries using Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data collected between 2013 and 2023. The findings of this study may provide valuable information for policymakers, researchers, and public health stakeholders to support strategic planning and resource allocation for malaria control in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

Study setting and period

This study was conducted in 19 sub-Saharan African countries using the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) Household Member Record dataset (PR file). The countries included are Angola, Benin, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, Republic of the Congo, Côte d'Ivoire, Gabon, Gambia, Ghana, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritania, Mozambique, Nigeria, Rwanda, Tanzania, Togo, and Uganda (Fig. 1). The region is commonly divided into four sub-regions West Africa, East Africa, Central Africa, and Southern Africa according to the United Nations geographical classification [29]. The region encompasses diverse ecological zones, including savannas, tropical rainforests, and mountainous areas.

Map of the 19 sub-Saharan African countries included in this study, with each country labeled and color-coded to indicate its inclusion in the analysis

Data source and study population

The data for this study were obtained from the DHS program (www.dhsprogram.com) following approval of the project concept notes. We used a standardized DHS dataset [30], which is harmonized across all participating countries with consistent questionnaires, variable definitions, and coding. The DHS surveys are designed to collect nationally representative samples of households and individuals, including both urban and rural areas. DHS surveys use standardized sampling procedures designed to produce nationally representative estimates for each participating country [30]. Large sample sizes and standardized procedures allow for reliable comparisons across countries and over time. The data used in this study cover the years 2013 to 2023. Because DHS surveys are conducted in different years across countries, pooling spatial data over time may introduce temporal variability that could influence observed spatial patterns. However, DHS surveys use standardized sampling designs and harmonized questionnaires, which allow for cross-country comparability. In this study, we used the most recent DHS available for each country to provide a regional spatial overview while minimizing duplication of surveys within countries, consistent with methodological considerations described for pooled spatial analyses by Dubé and Legros [31].

Only DHS surveys were included in this analysis. Although some countries conducted Malaria Indicator Surveys (MIS) during the study period, MIS datasets were excluded because they differ from DHS in survey design, scope, and available variables. Therefore, for each country we selected the most recent DHS conducted between 2013 and 2023 that included malaria testing and mosquito bed net utilization variables to ensure methodological consistency.

Sample size determination and sampling method

To assure national representativeness, the survey employed a two-stage sampling design. In the first stage, enumeration areas (clusters) were selected using each country’s most recent population and housing census as the sampling frame. In the second stage, a fixed number of households were systematically selected from each enumeration area [32]. In each selected household, a household questionnaire was first administered to collect information on household characteristics. In addition, individual questionnaires were administered to eligible household members, including women aged 15–49 years and men aged 15–59 years, depending on the country-specific survey design. The sample was weighted to improve representativeness and produce more precise estimates. Following DHS guidelines, we applied the DHS household member sampling weight (hv005). This weight adjusts for unequal probability of selection and non-response. The DHS-provided value (v005) was rescaled by dividing it by 1,000,000, as recommended in the DHS guidelines [33, 34]. These weights were applied to the existing DHS dataset; we did not conduct the surveys ourselves.

Our analysis included data from 11,594 clusters across the 19 sub-Saharan African countries. All spatial analyses were conducted at the cluster (enumeration area) level, which represents the primary sampling unit in DHS surveys. Malaria prevalence and mosquito bed net utilization categories were first aggregated within each cluster based on the individuals tested in that cluster, and the resulting cluster-level estimates were then linked to the DHS geographic coordinates for spatial analysis. For confidentiality purposes, DHS cluster coordinates are geographically displaced by up to 2 km in urban areas and up to 5 km in rural areas (with a small proportion displaced up to 10 km). Therefore, the spatial analyses represent approximate cluster locations rather than exact household positions.

Malaria testing in DHS surveys is primarily conducted among children aged 6–59 months using rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs). In some surveys, malaria testing may also include additional population groups such as older children or women of reproductive age depending on survey design. Therefore, the total of 126,424 individuals represents all eligible individuals who were tested for malaria in the included surveys, rather than the number of households surveyed (Table 1).

Variables of the study

Outcome variable: The outcome variable was malaria infection detected by a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) or microscopy as recorded in the DHS dataset. Malaria status was defined at the individual level based on the test result of each eligible participant. Individuals who tested positive for Plasmodium spp. were classified as malaria-positive, while those with negative test results were classified as malaria-negative. For analytical purposes, malaria status was coded as a binary variable. Malaria positive was coded as 1 and malaria negative was coded as 0.

Mosquito bed net utilization was derived from the DHS variable hml12, which records the type of mosquito bed net under which the individual slept the night before the survey. Bed net utilization was categorized into three mutually exclusive groups: no bed net use, treated bed net use, and untreated bed net use. For spatial analysis, malaria status and bed net utilization were combined to generate categorical variables representing the different exposure–outcome combinations. Each category was assigned a unique numeric code solely as an identifier, with no ordinal meaning. Specifically: no bed net and malaria-negative were coded as 2, no bed net and malaria-positive as 3; treated bed net and malaria-negative as 4, treated bed net and malaria-positive as 5; untreated bed net and malaria-negative as 8, and untreated bed net and malaria-positive as 9.

The non-sequential coding was intentionally used to ensure distinct classification of categories during data management and statistical modeling and does not affect estimation or interpretation of results.

Data management and analysis

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted using Stata version 17, while spatial visualization and mapping were performed using ArcGIS version 10.7. All analyses accounted for the DHS complex survey design by applying sampling weights, clustering, and stratification. Accordingly, DHS-provided sampling weights were applied in all analyses to adjust for unequal probabilities of selection, stratification, and non-response, ensuring nationally representative estimates.

Weighted proportions of malaria cases and mosquito bed net utilization categories (no bed net, treated bed net, and untreated bed net) were calculated in Stata and exported for spatial analysis. ArcGIS version 10.7 was used for spatial data processing, geographic visualization, and map production. Administrative boundary shapefiles for sub-Saharan Africa were obtained from the GADM database of Global Administrative Areas and used for academic and non-commercial purposes [35].

Spatial statistical analyses were conducted using multiple tools. Hotspot and cold spot analysis (Getis–Ord Gi*) and cluster and outlier analysis (Anselin Local Moran’s I) were performed within ArcGIS. In contrast, spatial scan statistics were conducted using SaTScan™ as a standalone software, independent of ArcGIS, to identify statistically significant spatial clusters of malaria. All cartographic elements, including layout, labeling, and projections, were created by the authors. All maps are original and comply with the Creative Commons Attribution License (CC BY 4.0).

Spatial analysis

Global spatial autocorrelation was assessed using Moran’s I statistic to evaluate the presence and strength of spatial clustering in malaria prevalence [36, 37]. Moran's I value ranges from −1 to 1. A Moran’s I value close to − 1 indicates spatial dispersion, values close to 1 indicate spatial clustering, and values near 0 indicate a random spatial pattern [38]. A statistically significant Moran's I (p < 0.05) confirms the existence of spatial autocorrelation [39].

Hot spot and outlier cluster analysis

Getis-Ord Gi* statistics were used to quantify the variation in spatial autocorrelation in 19 SSA countries [39]. It identifies “hot spots” in spatial data by comparing local estimates of spatial autocorrelation with global averages [37]. The Z-score was computed to confirm the statistical significance of malaria clustering at a p < 0.05 with 95% CI [40]. The high/low clustering analysis results were interpreted within the context of a null hypothesis, i.e., “there is no spatial clustering of malaria”. When the absolute value of the Z score was large and the p-value was very small, the null hypothesis was rejected. The sign of the Z score was considered when the null hypothesis was rejected. A positive z-score value indicated that there was high clustering, and a negative z-score value indicated that there was low clustering in the study area.

Spatial scan statistical analysis

The possible statistically significant high-rate clusters of malaria were examined using a spatial scan statistical analysis utilizing the Bernoulli model by Kulldorff, SaTScan version 10.0.2. Individuals who tested positive for malaria were considered cases, whereas individuals who tested negative were considered controls. To detect both large and small clusters, we set a higher boundary of 50% of the population for the spatial cluster size. It was possible to identify both the primary and secondary clusters. 999 Monte Carlo simulations were used to determine each cluster's log-likelihood ratio (LLR) and corresponding P-value; clusters with a p-value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant [41].

Ethics statement

This study was based on secondary data previously collected and published household survey data of the sub-Saharan DHS. Informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity, and privacy of study participants are already ethically handled by the DHS office. As such, Ethical approval was not required because this study used publicly available secondary DHS data. Review boards in each country provided ethical clearance for each included survey, with informed consent provided by all participants. Details of this process vary by survey. All methods were carried out following the Helsinki Declaration and guidelines and regulations.

Results

Pooled prevalence of malaria across 19 sub-Saharan African countries

The overall meta-analytic pooled prevalence of malaria across 19 sub-Saharan African countries was 24.0% (95% CI 18–30%) reflecting between-country variability. Among the included countries, Côte d’Ivoire had the highest malaria prevalence at 41% (95% CI 39%–42%), followed by Togo at 37% (95% CI 35%–38%). In contrast, the lowest prevalence was observed in Mauritania, at 4% (95% CI 4–5%) (Fig. 2). At the regional level, malaria prevalence was highest in Central Africa (26%, 95% CI 18%–34%), followed by West Africa (25%, 95% CI 15%–35%) and East Africa (20%, 95% CI 10%–31%) (Fig. 3).

Bed net utilization patterns in 19 sub-Saharan African countries

Among the surveyed individuals, 49.2% (95% CI 48.9%–49.5%) reported not sleeping under any bed net, 48.1% (95% CI 47.8%–48.3%) used treated bed nets, and 2.8% (95% CI 2.7%–2.9%) used untreated bed nets (Fig. 4). Nigeria (8.86%) and Rwanda (8.76%) contributed the largest proportions of the pooled analytic sample, whereas Togo contributed the smallest proportion (3.06%). The greatest use of treated bed nets was observed in Benin (76.40%) and Mali (73.53%), while the lowest was in Mauritania (13.27%). Gabon reported the highest proportion of untreated bed net users (11.67%), whereas Côte d’Ivoire had the lowest (0.10%) (Fig. 5).

Malaria prevalence by bed net usage type in 19 Sub-Saharan African countries

Malaria prevalence varied across bed net utilization categories. Individuals who reported no bed net use had the highest malaria prevalence (32.8%), followed by those using untreated bed nets (28.9%), while the lowest prevalence was observed among treated bed net users (13.2%) (Fig. 6). These descriptive differences should not be interpreted as causal effects, as malaria risk is influenced by multiple environmental, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors. At the country level, Benin had the highest malaria prevalence among no bed net users (71.40%), whereas Mauritania reported the lowest (5.11%). Among treated bed net users, Côte d’Ivoire recorded the highest malaria prevalence (32.38%), with Gambia having the lowest at 0.17%. Among untreated bed net users, Togo exhibited the highest malaria prevalence (87.18%), while Mauritania had the lowest prevalence at 4.82% (Table 3).

Spatial autocorrelation

The Global Moran’s I statistic was used to assess the spatial autocorrelation of malaria prevalence across 19 sub-Saharan African countries. The results indicate a statistically significant clustered pattern of malaria distribution rather than random dispersion. For overall malaria prevalence among all households, the Global Moran’s I value was 0.159, with an expected index of − 0.00084, a Z-score of 239.06, and a p-value < 0.0001 (Fig. 8A).

Spatial autocorrelation analysis of malaria prevalence in 19 sub-Saharan African countries using DHS data (2013–2023): A overall malaria prevalence; B malaria among no bed net users; C malaria among treated bed net users; and D malaria among untreated bed net users. Results are based on Global Moran’s I, indicating statistically significant clustering patterns in all cases

When stratified by bed net usage, similar clustering patterns were observed. Malaria among no bed net users showed a Moran’s I value of 0.107, Z-score of 161.83 (Fig. 8B); among treated bed net users, the Moran’s I was 0.156, with a Z-score of 234.70 (Fig. 8C); and among untreated bed net users, the Moran’s I was lower at 0.017, with a Z-score of 26.86 (Fig. 8D). In all cases, the clustering was statistically significant (p < 0.0001).

This spatial analysis was conducted using the Inverse Distance conceptualization (assigning greater influence to nearer features), the Euclidean distance method (measuring straight-line distances), and a fixed threshold distance of 1.95 km to define neighborhood relationships.

Because the analysis included a large number of spatial units (11,594 clusters), the resulting Z-scores were very large. In spatial statistics, particularly with large datasets, Z-scores can become inflated and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. As noted by Moeller, standardized scores may exaggerate the magnitude of group differences when sample sizes are large [42]. In this study, interpretation of spatial clustering was therefore based on the combined assessment of Moran’s I values, p-values, and the observed spatial distribution patterns rather than relying solely on Z-scores.

Discussion

In alignment with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, Target 3.3, which aims to end the malaria epidemic by 2030, malaria control has increasingly been integrated into broader national development strategies and multisectoral approaches addressing vector-borne diseases [43]. Estimates indicate that malaria interventions have reduced case numbers by up to 70% in sub-Saharan Africa [16]. This success is largely attributed to the scale-up of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), vector control programs, and preventive chemoprophylaxis measures [44, 45].

This study found a pooled malaria prevalence of 24.0% (95% CI 18%–30%) in 19 sub-Saharan African countries, indicating that malaria remains a significant public health challenge in the region. These estimates reflect pooled survey data collected across different countries and years and should therefore be interpreted as a regional overview rather than a single time-point estimate. This finding aligns with previous studies conducted in Ghana (22.3%) [46], across sub-Saharan Africa (18.8%) [47], 11 sub-Saharan African countries (24.2%) [17], the Republic of Congo (29.3%) [48], and Uganda (23.3%) [49]. However, it is lower than estimates from Ghana (39.1%) [50], Malaysia (33.6%) [51], and 16 sub-Saharan African countries (31.1%) [52]. The variation in findings may be due to differences in data sources, survey methods, geographic scope, and study periods. Environmental and socioeconomic factors such as climate, vector density [53], urbanization, healthcare access, and the effectiveness of malaria control programs may also contribute. Differences in cultural practices, population behaviors, and regional health system capacities further explain these discrepancies [54].

The present study demonstrated significant variation in malaria prevalence across sub-regions of sub-Saharan Africa. Central Africa reported the highest prevalence at 26% (95% CI 18–34%), followed by West Africa at 25% (95% CI 15–35%), while East Africa had the lowest burden at 20% (95% CI 10–31%). This regional disparity is likely influenced by variations in ecological characteristics, coverage, and effectiveness of malaria interventions, healthcare system capacity, and the broader socio-political context. Central Africa’s elevated malaria burden can be attributed to its favorable environmental conditions, including dense equatorial forests, high humidity, and persistent rainfall, all of which promote year-round mosquito breeding [55]. Furthermore, challenges such as weak health systems, poor infrastructure, and ongoing conflicts, particularly in countries like the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), undermine consistent delivery of malaria prevention and treatment services [56]. In West Africa, high malaria transmission remains a concern due to intense seasonal transmission, high population density, and incomplete implementation of indoor residual spraying (IRS), particularly in rural and remote areas [57]. Although widespread distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) has been achieved in many countries, usage remains inconsistent, and the nets' effectiveness declines over time due to physical degradation and increasing insecticide resistance among Anopheles mosquitoes [58]. In contrast, East Africa’s relatively lower prevalence may reflect the impact of improved public health interventions, especially in highland areas where IRS and ITNs are widely adopted [59]. Additionally, urbanization and enhanced housing conditions have likely reduced breeding sites in cities and peri-urban areas. Several East African countries, including Rwanda and Ethiopia, have demonstrated progress through comprehensive malaria control programs involving community health workers and strengthened case management [60, 61].

The results of this study revealed that among surveyed individuals, 49.17% (95% CI 48.89–49.45%) reported no bed net use, 48.06% (95% CI 47.78–48.34%) used treated bed nets, and only 2.77% (95% CI 2.68–2.86%) used untreated bed nets. These findings highlight substantial gaps in protective coverage, particularly for households with no or ineffective bed nets. The World Health Organization strongly advocates for the widespread use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) as a core strategy in malaria prevention and control efforts due to their proven effectiveness in reducing vector-human contact and malaria transmission [62]. This study aligns with prior research from sub-Saharan Africa, where ownership of at least one ITN per household increased from 47% in 2010 to 72% in 2017, but population-level access remains insufficient, reaching only 56% during the same period [63]. Barriers such as heat discomfort, perceptions of low mosquito density, difficulties in net hanging, chemical odor, cultural norms, and limited health knowledge have been identified as contributing to low ITN use, even when nets are available, as well as no bed net utilization [64, 65]. Therefore, national malaria programs should prioritize expanding both the availability and effective use of ITNs, with targeted outreach to households lacking nets or using untreated nets. Interventions should be tailored to overcome behavioral, educational, and logistical barriers, ensuring equitable and sustained protection across age groups and regions.

The prevalence of malaria differed markedly by bed net usage: no bed net users exhibited a high malaria prevalence of 32.75%, those using untreated net had a rate of 28.91% and those using treated bednets experienced the lowest prevalence at 13.23%. These differences represent descriptive associations and should not be interpreted as causal effects, as malaria risk is influenced by multiple environmental, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors. Bed nets serve as a physical barrier against malaria vectors, yet their lack of use significantly raises exposure risk. The high cost of bed nets, the absence of free nets (omitted during free distribution), and the anxiety about the discomfort and heat that come with sleeping under bed nets contribute to limited bed net use, increasing the malaria risk among these households [66, 67]. Even among households with treated nets, persistent malaria prevalence may reflect misuse or inconsistent use of ITNs, shaped by limited awareness, inadequate behavior change messaging, household environmental constraints, and socio-cultural beliefs [67–70]. In addition, the insecticidal qualities and physical integrity of mosquito bed nets determine their preventative impact [71]. Frequent washing, Soap type, and drying under direct sunlight can degrade both the physical and chemical integrity of these nets, reducing their effectiveness [72, 73]. While some long-lasting nets remain effective after up to 20 washes, improper handling can accelerate the loss of insecticidal activity [74]. Additionally, behavioral and structural barriers such as a limited number of nets relative to household size, outdoor sleeping, and net repurposing for other household needs undermine consistent use, despite ownership [75]. Collectively, these findings highlight that mere possession of ITNs does not guarantee protection. Efforts must focus on ensuring universal ITN access, reinforcing correct and consistent usage practices, providing community-based education tailored to user preferences, and counseling on net care and replacement schedules. These strategies are essential to reducing malaria prevalence effectively across all household types.

The spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed significant geographic variation in malaria clustering across 19 Sub-Saharan African countries. Based on the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic, distinct hotspot areas with significantly higher malaria prevalence were identified in Benin, Burkina Faso, Togo, Uganda, Rwanda, and parts of the Republic of Congo and Mozambique. Conversely, cold spots, regions with lower than expected malaria cases, were observed in Mauritania, Côte d'Ivoire, Madagascar, Tanzania, and certain regions of Angola and Mozambique. These spatial patterns are likely influenced by a complex interplay of environmental, socio-economic, and healthcare-related factors. Climatic conditions such as high temperatures and humidity, along with environmental factors like stagnant water bodies near households, and proximity to water bodies, facilitate mosquito breeding and increase malaria transmission. Limited access to healthcare services, economic barriers, low community awareness, and cultural practices can exacerbate vulnerability in hotspot regions [76–79]. Another major contributor to spatial heterogeneity could be disparities in the implementation and coverage of malaria interventions. Regions with lower insecticide-treated bed net (ITN) use or insufficient indoor residual spraying (IRS) tend to have higher malaria burdens [80]. Conversely, cold spot areas may benefit from successful malaria control programs, urbanization, improved housing, environmental management, and better socioeconomic conditions. Studies have shown that consistent and correct use of ITNs and IRS, along with early diagnosis and treatment, significantly reduces malaria transmission in both rural and urban settings [27, 53, 81]. Overall, the observed spatial heterogeneity in malaria burden emphasizes the need for targeted, context-specific interventions, taking into account local environmental, climatic, and socio-economic realities.

The SaTScan cluster analysis conducted across 126,424 individuals in 19 Sub-Saharan African countries revealed multiple significant spatial clusters. Specifically, 34 malaria clusters were identified among all individuals, 23 malaria clusters among no bed net users, 25 malaria clusters among treated bed net users, and 12 malaria clusters among untreated bed net users. The most statistically significant clusters in all cases, as indicated by the highest log-likelihood ratio (LLR) values, were located in Ghana, Burkina Faso, Togo, and Benin. These countries are known malaria-endemic zones with persistent transmission, and several factors may explain the concentration of clusters. A recent study in Ghana stated that malaria is an endemic disease and accounts for 38.0% of all outpatient attendance, with the most vulnerable groups being children under 5 years of age, and the study reported a malaria prevalence of 20.63% [82]. Approximately 85% of ITNs were freely received from the public and private sectors, according to the GDHS survey. However, based on budgetary expenses and technological viability, the government and non-governmental groups only implemented the IRS in a few districts of Ghana [76, 83]. As a result, ITN became the primary preventive strategy for a sizable percentage of homes that were not covered by the IRS. Consequently, reliance on ITNs as the sole preventive strategy may be insufficient, particularly in densely populated or ecologically favorable areas for mosquito breeding.

In Burkina Faso, malaria remains a major public health challenge. A nationally representative study reported a combined malaria prevalence of 44%, indicating widespread transmission [84]. According to the World Health Organization, in 2019, approximately 384,000 children under the age of five died of malaria across Sub-Saharan Africa, with Burkina Faso accounting for about 4% of these deaths, underscoring the country's disproportionate burden [85]. One of the key contributing factors is the high poverty rate: an estimated 43.8% of the population lives below the international poverty line of $1.90 per day [86, 87]. Poverty can directly limit access to healthcare and preventive measures, making both children and adults more vulnerable to malaria infections and complications. This socioeconomic constraint hinders the purchase, consistent use, or replacement of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and reduces access to timely diagnosis and treatment, particularly in remote or underserved regions. In Togo, malaria is also endemic, with over 2 million cases and 905 deaths reported in 2018, in a total population of around 7 million people [88]. Another study reported a prevalence of 32.1%, reflecting persistent transmission across various ecological zones [89]. Although indoor residual spraying (IRS) is included in Togo’s national strategic plan for malaria control, it has not yet been effectively implemented, likely due to resource limitations and operational challenges. As a result, ITNs remain the primary vector control tool. However, the effectiveness of ITNs depends heavily on their physical integrity and insecticidal potency [71].

Conclusions and implications of the study

This study highlights the persistent burden and spatial variability of malaria across 19 Sub-Saharan African countries. The overall pooled malaria prevalence was estimated at 24.0% (95% CI 18–30%). Regional differences were observed, with Central Africa bearing the highest burden (26%), followed by West Africa (25%), and a relatively lower rate in East Africa (20%). Bed net utilization was suboptimal, with nearly half of individuals (49.17%) reporting not sleeping under any bed net, while 48.06% used insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and only 2.77% relied on untreated mosquito nets. Malaria prevalence varied significantly across these categories: 32.75% among no bed net users, 28.91% among untreated net users and 13.23% among ITN users. The spatial analysis further revealed significant clustering of malaria cases, particularly in Ghana, Burkina Faso, Togo, and Benin, across different bed net utilization categories. These findings highlight the importance of regionally tailored malaria control strategies that consider geographic, behavioral, and systemic determinants of transmission.

To reduce the malaria burden, governments and stakeholders, including policymakers, researchers, NGOs, and community-based organizations, must collaborate to design and implement integrated malaria prevention programs. These should focus on improving equitable access to subsidized or freely distributed ITNs, especially for populations currently using untreated nets or not using nets at all. Complementary efforts should include community-based health education to promote consistent and correct ITN usage, address behavioral and cultural barriers, and raise awareness of other environmental and social contributors to malaria risk. Furthermore, priority should be given to high-burden geographic clusters through targeted surveillance, resource allocation, and intensified vector control efforts. Strengthening multi-sectoral partnerships and tailoring strategies to regional needs will be essential in accelerating progress toward malaria reduction and eventual elimination in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Strengths of the study

This study presents several notable strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is among the first multi-country studies to examine the spatial epidemiology of malaria in relation to bed net utilization across 19 sub-Saharan African countries. Second, the analysis is based on a large, nationally representative and weighted dataset from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), which enhances the validity and regional comparability of the findings. Third, by identifying geographic clustering of malaria cases, the study provides valuable insights that can inform targeted and region-specific malaria control interventions by policymakers, public health practitioners, and other stakeholders.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of cross-sectional Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data limits the ability to infer causal relationships between bed net utilization and malaria prevalence. Second, the analysis was restricted to 19 sub-Saharan African countries with available and recent DHS malaria data, and thus may not fully represent all sub-Saharan African countries. Third, the study did not incorporate other potentially influential factors such as demographic, socioeconomic, environmental, and meteorological variables, which could provide a more comprehensive understanding of malaria transmission dynamics. Fourth, because DHS surveys were conducted in different years across countries (2013–2023), temporal variation may influence the pooled spatial patterns observed in this study. Finally, DHS cluster coordinates are geographically displaced to protect participant confidentiality, which may reduce spatial precision and influence the magnitude of spatial statistics. In addition, because the dataset included a large number of clusters, very large Z-scores were observed and should be interpreted cautiously.
