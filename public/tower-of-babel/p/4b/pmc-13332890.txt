Regulating the Irregular: Phage Therapy and the Case for a Regulatory Sandbox Approach in Australia
Authors: Marco Rizzi, Stephen M Stick, David G Hancock, Anthony Kicic, Yuliya V Karpievitch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteriophages (phages) are viruses that selectively kill bacteria and offer a promising option to address the growing global pandemic of antimicrobial-resistant infections. However, phage therapy does not easily align with traditional regulatory pathways designed for fixed-composition chemical drugs or biologics with fixed non-evolving compositions. Globally, alternative models such as personalised pharmacy-based formulations (known as magistral preparations), compassionate use provisions and flexible licensing have emerged to accommodate the biological complexity and personalised nature of phage-based therapeutics. Australia is well positioned to take a leadership role and consider a regulatory sandbox approach to the therapeutic use of phages that balances innovation with safety. Being time limited, this controlled framework would allow regulators and innovators to test new approaches under provisional rules and inform potential reforms. A sandbox should include strict participation criteria, and lead to the development of regulatory-ready biobanks of well-characterised phages, accelerating clinical access and manufacturing capacity. This model is a pragmatic and proactive approach that would enable safe scalable phage therapy in response to rising antimicrobial resistance threats.

Key Points

Phage therapy has strong potential to help address antimicrobial resistance, but existing regulatory frameworks are not well suited to its unique and adaptable nature.Regulatory sandboxes offer a controlled setting in which innovative therapies can be tested, enabling developers to generate evidence while regulators gain experience and refine oversight approaches.A regulatory sandbox for phage therapy could support the adaptation of current regulatory pathways and the exploration of novel approaches that better reflect how phage therapies are developed and used.

Introduction

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that specifically infect and kill bacteria with promising evidence to support their use as a safe and effective alternative to antibiotics [1–3]. Phage therapy is gaining renewed momentum as a responsive measure to the escalating global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [4–7]. This resurgence is fuelled by increasing resistance patterns to conventional antibiotics, the drying pipeline of new antibacterial agents and the widespread retreat of pharmaceutical investment from the high-risk low-return antimicrobial space.

Unlike conventional, chemically defined small-molecule medicines, phage therapy calls for bespoke regulatory arrangements. Phages are biological medicinal products that combine the inherent variability typical of such products in general, with high levels of target selectivity and evolutionary patterns linked to those of bacterial populations. Many phages display a narrow “host-range”, infecting only specific bacterial species or strains, thus necessitating personalised formulations tailored to individual infections [8]. Phage cocktails, containing multiple broader-spectrum phages, can also be developed to cover a larger range of bacterial targets, but these too must evolve over time as bacterial strains and resistance patterns shift [9]. Phage therapy thus requires both precision and adaptability, with considerable challenges for established pharmaceutical regulatory frameworks, primarily designed for mass-produced stable medicinal products [10, 11]. This complexity calls for experimental regulatory approaches to simultaneously foster innovation, uphold public health needs, protect safety and ethical standards, and enable timely patient access to life-saving treatments.

Australia is yet to develop its approach to phage therapy regulation. This paper outlines why regulatory sandboxes could be a viable experimental option and explores what an effective conceptual implementation would entail. We first provide an overview of a range of regulatory experiences at a transnational level to highlight the specificity of phage regulation. We subsequently discuss the viability of regulatory sandboxes as a form of regulatory experimentalism. We then outline the main characteristics of a possible sandbox for phage therapy in Australia. Ultimately, embedding sandboxes into the regulatory framework as a modality to tackle challenging infections with innovative therapies will require legislative intervention. A conceptual implementation focused on phage therapy could facilitate this transition and provide Australia with the opportunity to align with global trends and spearhead the development of innovative regulatory pathways.

An Overview of Alternative Approaches to Phage Regulation Transnationally

Regulatory approaches to phage therapy vary across jurisdictions, reflecting differences in national health priorities, legislative frameworks and regulatory infrastructure. A variety of strategies exist to address challenges associated with phage characterisation, Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) compliance and clinical access pathways. What follows is an overview of select approaches that reveal the spectrum of regulatory mechanisms deployed.

In Georgia and Russia, over-the-counter bacteriophage preparations, such as the “Pyo” and “Intesti” bacteriophage cocktails, are available in pharmacies as over-the-counter medications, despite missing standard clinical trial data (Fig. 1). These products are authorised through national approval processes that focus on ensuring quality and safety using preclinical data, rather than mandating the standard medication development pipeline and requiring extensive clinical trials. This approach facilitates broader access to phage therapies while maintaining a measure of oversight over product standards [12, 13]. Periodic updating of these formulations against recently circulating bacterial isolates allows phage cocktails to remain responsive to evolving resistance patterns while maintaining timely patient access. By expanding access to targeted antibacterial treatment, this approach may also reduce dependence on conventional antibiotics. Furthermore, Georgia employs a flexible regulatory framework for personalised phage therapies to facilitate compounding of tailor-made phage preparations at the Eliava Institute [14, 15]. These personalised treatments are prepared based on individual patient needs and used under medical supervision, without the necessity for formal clinical trials or strict adherence to GMP [14]. This system utilises preclinical efficacy data where phages are tested against a patient’s isolate, thereby enabling phage therapy delivery.

In Belgium, a dedicated magistral (pharmacy-based compounded formulations) phage therapy framework enables physicians to prescribe personalised phage treatments without requiring per-patient regulatory approval from national authorities. Under this framework, phages are processed as active pharmaceutical ingredients in magistral preparations. These formulations can be prepared in pharmacies or hospitals under controlled conditions, facilitating patient access while allowing data collection on safety and efficacy. Good Manufacturing Practices compliance is not required, but manufacturers must ensure consistent quality and purity, and each batch must be certified by external laboratories against established quality criteria [16]. Portugal has also adopted a magistral framework for phage preparations, with the key distinction that quality-control certification may be performed by the manufacturer, rather than requiring assessment by an independent external laboratory [16]. The preparation of magistral phage therapies is inherently time consuming, which limits their applicability in time-critical settings such as sepsis.

In June 2025, the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency published its first official guidance on the development and use of phage therapies [17]. This comprehensive document specifies that phage therapies intended for human medicinal use are classified as biological medicines and are subject to the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 [17]. These therapies must therefore comply with GMP standards. In the USA, the Food and Drug Administration permits the use of phage therapies primarily under the Investigational New Drug pathway for single-patient Expanded Access and Emergency Use Authorizations [18]. While several phage-based clinical trials have been conducted or are ongoing [19–21], there is currently no dedicated regulatory pathway or formalised guidance specific to phage products. Developers must therefore navigate existing biologics regulations, with GMP requirements applying on a case-by-case basis depending on the intended clinical context.

In Canada, Health Canada regulates phage therapies under existing biologics and advanced therapeutic products frameworks. Through the Advanced Therapeutic Products initiative established under the Food and Drugs Act in June 2019, Health Canada has indicated that its traditional biologics pathways are sufficiently robust to accommodate novel biologic interventions [22]. For example, Cytophage Technologies Ltd recently secured a “new substance notification” approval from Health Canada for a phage therapy targeting Enterococci in prosthetic joint infections [23].

The ‘Regulatory Sandbox’: An Instrument to Navigate Innovation

The fragmentation of existing approaches to phage therapy regulation signals a growing need for adaptive regulatory mechanisms to improve patient access. Regulatory sandboxes represent one such approach. A sandbox is a controlled testing environment established and overseen by a regulatory authority. Within this defined ‘safe space’, innovators have the opportunity to test novel products or services that may not conform to existing regulatory requirements [24]. This aspect acknowledges that strict adherence to regulations can create structural obstacles in areas where established rules are not well suited to the characteristics of a new technology or process. Sandboxes provide reduced or bespoke regulatory requirements, limited waivers or derogations from specific rules, which would otherwise impede testing and development [25].

Sandboxes have been successfully explored and implemented in fast-moving fields such as financial technology [26], artificial intelligence, food [27] and Australian border control [28]. Sandboxes are also gaining traction in health [29, 30], and particularly in frontier innovation fields such as neurotechnology [31], orphan drugs [31, 32] and data science in medicine [33]. By facilitating the collection of experiential data, sandboxes enable the evaluation of effectiveness, safety and practical feasibility of interventions in actual clinical settings, while contemporaneously gauging appropriate guardrails.

A sandbox is typically structured in four phases [34], each designed to enable structured experimentation while ensuring oversight and accountability. In the health context, this would manifest as follows:

(i)an initial application stage where innovators propose their therapeutic product or service and outline the intended use and rationale for participation to the sandbox. Regulators assess whether the proposed innovation falls outside existing regulatory pathways and presents potential public health value. Crucially, scrutiny at the entry level is particularly stringent, and should encompass assessment of: novelty; prospected public benefit; planned risk management; and readiness and feasibility;

By observing innovation within a controlled setting, regulators gain valuable insights through ‘regulatory learning’ [35, 36], which can inform adjustments to the framework. The aim is to develop proportionate and evidence-based regulatory responses to emerging technologies through a proactive and iterative approach to regulation, rather than a reactive approach. This process also aligns with broader calls to shift the paradigm in the regulation of medical products towards regulators performing a facilitative function rather than a unilaterally directive function [32, 37]. For example, the European Union has included provisions for regulatory sandboxes as regulatory experimentation tools in its proposed new pharmaceutical legislation, signalling a move towards the adoption of this approach within health regulation at a transnational level [38].

Significance of the Sandbox Approach for Phage Therapy Regulation in Australia

A sandbox approach would give the opportunity to the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) to develop in-depth understanding of practical implications and risks associated with bespoke manufacturing processes involved in phage therapy, and evaluate different approaches to phage production, quality-control standards, and models for clinical deployment, from personalised phage selection to evolving broad-spectrum cocktails. Phage therapy does not fit neatly within existing TGA definitions of ‘therapeutic goods’, and presents challenges in aligning with standard GMP requirements designed for chemically defined products [39]. While GMP-compliant phage manufacturing is feasible, including in dedicated facilities such as Phage WA, it may be prohibitively expensive and difficult to replicate at scale. Laboratory-scale purification often relies on polyethylene glycol precipitation and caesium chloride density-gradient ultracentrifugation, yet caesium chloride is toxic to humans and must be fully removed before clinical use. The process is poorly suited to large-scale production [40, 41]. For this reason, rather than requiring full GMP compliance, jurisdictions with experience in bespoke phage therapy regulation have generally adopted risk-adapted quality frameworks, particularly in the context of compassionate use or personalised treatment settings [42].

A sandbox approach offers opportunities for regulatory learnings on several complementary fronts:

Regulatory flexibility for a biologically tailored assessment:Phage therapy requires evaluation tailored to its biological and clinical characteristics, without compromising core principles of patient safety and therapeutic efficacy. Experimental evidence would be drawn from complex clinical settings, whether involving patients with severe drug-resistant infections or those for whom phage therapy may offer a microbiome-sparing alternative to antibiotics. This type of evidence is critical for evaluating the therapy’s real-world effectiveness, safety profile, and logistical feasibility within Australia’s healthcare system. For example, it would allow assessment of the viability of N-of-1 or N-of-few clinical trials as alternative methods to large-scale controlled trials, which is particularly valuable in highly individualised therapeutics [43, 44].

Adaptability for evolving bacterial resistance and regulatory models:The dynamic nature of bacterial epidemiology and resistance patterns requires phage preparations to be frequently updated to remain effective. This calls for adaptive and modular regulatory pathways, with processes enabling the pre-approval of well-characterised phage biobanks, predefined manufacturing processes and purity levels, and implementation of streamlined procedures for updating phage cocktails based on evolving bacterial resistance patterns. Analogous frameworks exist for annual influenza vaccines, which are treated as platform technologies. Influenza vaccines receive a first approval based on a standard process involving pre-clinical and clinical data analysis of safety and efficacy. Subsequently, new strains can be added on a yearly or seasonal basis without having to undergo further clinical testing [45, 46]. These strain updates are not new products but minor variations to a well-established and previously authorised platform [47]. As such, lifecycle monitoring replaces repetitive trials and approvals. Notably, both the Food and Drug Administration and the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency are moving towards similarly flexible baseline approvals for rare disease therapies, reflecting a global trend towards adaptive regulation [48, 49]. A sandbox would be the ideal setting to trial the viability of platform approvals for phage therapy — in Australia specifically, but with a potential broader transnational impact.

While offering regulatory flexibility, sandboxes are designed with robust and carefully embedded mechanisms ensuring protection of public health and effective risk mitigation [51]. For phage therapy, this would include establishing carefully defined eligibility criteria, for both the phage products to be tested within the sandbox and the patient populations enrolled in trials. The inherently collaborative nature of a sandbox would facilitate the TGA working with sandbox participants to develop and implement clear and comprehensive risk mitigation strategies, while rigorously monitoring patient outcomes throughout the process.

Filling a Regulatory Gap

Currently in Australia, the Standardised Treatment and Monitoring Protocol for Adults and Paediatric Patients (STAMP) provides an avenue for the compassionate use of phage therapy [52]. STAMP facilitates access to phage products not included in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods on a case-by-case basis for patients with severe treatment-resistant infections. STAMP is designed to address immediate clinical needs and enable valuable safety and efficacy data collection of eligible individual cases. It is not however designed to support iterative testing of manufacturing models, scalable clinical deployment strategies or longitudinal data collection beyond individual outcomes. As such, while STAMP represents an important first step in enabling access to phage therapy in Australia, a regulatory sandbox model would complement it by providing a framework for controlled system-level innovation in phage development, regulation and delivery.

Conceptual Implementation: A Regulatory Sandbox for Phage Therapy

The ultimate goal is to transition phage therapy from its current niche and last resort use to an integrated and adaptable component of Australia’s antimicrobial arsenal. However, rather than proposing immediate legislative change to integrate sandboxes into the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989, one approach is to conduct a conceptual implementation through a regulatory sandbox project focused on phage therapy. This would allow the TGA to evaluate the feasibility of flexible regulatory mechanisms applied to emerging health technologies.

The sandbox should entail a TGA-established, time-limited, and scope-defined programme targeting both a compassionate use and an early-stage clinical evaluation of phage products. Operationally, the project should rely on a close collaboration between the TGA and selected university hospitals, phage biobanks and manufacturers, relevant patient representative bodies, biotechnology companies and microbiology laboratories, functioning as a learning ecosystem to evaluate experiential workflows.

Conclusions

Regulatory sandboxes, as a form of experimental regulatory governance, offer a promising and adaptable approach for the TGA as it navigates the complexities of novel therapies. By providing a controlled environment for flexible testing, facilitating the generation of experiential evidence, fostering collaboration and regulatory learning, and incorporating robust safeguards, sandboxes can help bridge the gap between rapid biomedical innovation and traditional regulatory processes.

Lessons from successful deployment of sandboxes in other sectors can provide valuable guidance. Prospectively, integrating sandboxes into the TGA’s framework would align with international trends as seen in the upcoming European Union legislation. While current laws on therapeutic goods do not yet contemplate sandboxes, a conceptual implementation focused on phage therapy should be an attractive proposition for Australian health authorities. It would indicate a proactive step towards adaptive, experiential and evidence-based regulatory pathways to effectively support life-saving innovations in the fight against AMR.
