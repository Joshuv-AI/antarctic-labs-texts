Profiling fear-extinction learning induced small ncRNAs landscape and identifying miR-29b-3p as a promising biomarker for PTSD
Authors: Jiazhi Jiang, Junhui Liu, Sha Liu, Wenjing Wang, Ziyue Xu, Yuliang Zhou, Shuzhen Ran, Zhipeng Xu, Wei Wei, Xiang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe pivotal role of small non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) in gene expression highlights their potential as therapeutic targets and biomarkers in neuropsychiatric disorders. However, their transcriptional dynamics during fear extinction (EXT) remain poorly understood, limiting insights into disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).Methods We profiled the small ncRNAs transcriptome in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) following EXT training using small RNA array and explored the potential biological functions of differentially expressed (DE) miRNAs. Publicly available miRNA expression data from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of PTSD patients was integrated to identify conserved miRNAs biomarkers. Validation of the expression of candidate miRNA in mouse mPFC, plasma and PTSD patients’ PBMCs were performed by RT-qPCR. Inhibitor of candidate miRNA was infused into the infralimbic prefrontal cortex (ILPFC) of mice to assess its effect on fear extinction memory. To assess the impact of Dnmt3a, a common candidate hsa- and mmu-miRNA target, on DNA methylation, publicly available whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data from Dnmt3a knockout or mutant mice across four brain regions were analyzed. Based on the small RNA data, we also analyzed DE tsRNAs, including their subtype distributions, tRNA origins, and potential functions.ResultsWe identified 63 DE miRNAs following EXT training, with predicted targets enriched in dendritic and synaptic function. In PTSD patient PBMCs, 250 DE miRNAs were identified, including three conserved extinction-induced miRNAs that were downregulated in PBMCs, which were further confirmed in our mouse model and PTSD human blood samples. We next demonstrated that miR-29b-3p facilitates the formation of fear extinction memory. Bioinformatic analysis suggests that miR-29b-3p targets the 3’ untranslated region of Dnmt3a mRNA, which encodes a DNA methyltransferase involved in de novo non-CG methylation in brain. For other small ncRNAs, we identified 75 DE tsRNAs, with 5′tiRNA and 3′tiRNA being the most abundant in the up-regulated and down-regulated groups, respectively. Gln-CTG, Lys-CTT and Lys-TTT was identified as the predominant tRNA contributing to the biogenesis of DE tsRNAs.ConclusionsOur findings emphasize the critical role of small ncRNA regulation in fear extinction learning and highlight miR-29b-3p as a promising functional biomarker for PTSD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07202-x.Keywords: Small non-coding RNA, microRNA, miR-29b-3p, Biomarker, PTSD

Introduction

Small non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), defined as RNA molecules shorter than 100 nucleotides, play essential roles in the development and various physiological processes of the central nervous system (CNS) [1–4]. These include transfer RNAs (tRNAs), tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs), small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs), which collectively regulate diverse aspects of gene expression and neuronal function [5–8]. SnoRNAs are involved in RNA modifications such as 2′-O-methylation and pseudouridylation of rRNAs and mRNAs, and have been implicated in stress-related process [4, 8]. Under stress conditions, tRNAs are enzymatically cleaved into tsRNAs, including tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) and tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs (tiRNAs), which have been associated with translation regulation and autophagy [9–11]. Notably, altered tsRNA profiles have been reported in the hippocampus of Alzheimer’s disease models [12], and age-related changes in sperm tsRNAs have been linked to the intergenerational transmission of anxiety-like behaviors [13].

Among small ncRNAs, miRNAs have received the most attention due to their well-established role as post-transcriptional “fine-tuners” of gene expression, particularly in relation to synaptic plasticity, memory formation, and behavior [14, 15]. The neuron-specific miRNA miR-134, which is enriched in dendrites, plays a critical role in regulating dendritic spine morphology and synaptic plasticity in hippocampal neurons by repressing brain-derived neurotrophic factor-induced Limk1 mRNA expression [16]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the synapse- and dendrite-specific localization of miRNAs, emphasizing their essential roles in shaping dendritic architecture and establishing synaptic connectivity during memory-related processes [17, 18].

In animal models, miRNAs have been shown to regulate fear learning and extinction, core processes that are often disrupted in psychiatric disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [19]. For example, stress-responsive miRNAs may facilitate fear extinction by modulating synaptic plasticity and immune-related signaling pathways following administration of D-Cycloserine [20]. Inhibition of miR-598-3p in the basolateral amygdala (BLA) attenuates stress-enhanced fear memory by targeting genes associated with cytoskeletal organization [21]. Similarly, Du et al. reported that chronic social defeat stress alters miRNA expression in the nucleus accumbens, potentially affecting both anxiety-like behaviors and fear memory formation [22]. Recent small RNA sequencing studies have identified miRNAs such as miR-135b, which is consistently dysregulated in the BLA of PTSD-like mice and in the serum of PTSD patients [23]. Notably, overexpression of miR-135b in the BLA of stress-resilient animals enhances the expression of remote fear memory [23].

In humans, dysregulated miRNA expression has been consistently associated with PTSD. Circulating miRNAs and exosome-associated miRNAs have emerged as promising biomarkers for PTSD diagnosis and prognosis [24, 25]. Previous studies have also revealed a strong association between altered miRNA expression and immune dysfunction in PTSD patients [26]. For instance, the downregulation of miR-7113-5p has been shown to induce the upregulation of proinflammatory gene WNT10B, suggesting that miRNAs may act as epigenetic regulators contributing to the inflammatory response observed in PTSD [27]. These findings underscore the involvement of miRNAs in the pathophysiology of PTSD and highlight their potential utility as both therapeutic targets and diagnostic biomarkers.

Despite significant progress, the role of small RNAs in the regulation of fear extinction remains incompletely understood. In particular, few studies have systematically characterized small RNA expression profiles in mouse models of extinction learning. It also remains unclear whether specific miRNAs not only modulate extinction processes but could simultaneously serve as biomarkers for PTSD. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted small RNA profiling in mice undergoing fear extinction training and integrated these data with miRNA expression profiles from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of PTSD patients, aiming to identify candidate miRNAs with dual functions in extinction regulation and PTSD biomarker potential.

Materials and methods

Overview of study design

Figure 1 outlines flowchart of this study. Small RNA microarray analysis was conducted on the mPFC of mice following fear extinction training to elucidate the expression and characteristics of small RNAs induced by extinction and assess their functional roles. We then integrated miRNA expression profiles from the PBMCs of PTSD patients to identify miRNAs that were upregulated in the mPFC during fear extinction in mice but downregulated in human PBMCs, focusing on species-conserved candidates with potential relevance as PTSD biomarkers. Finally, bioinformatics predictions of miRNA targets were performed, and publicly available whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data were utilized to analyze the role of Dnmt3a in DNA methylation, followed by experimental validation to confirm their role in fear extinction.

Flowchart of this study. CTX, context control; FC, fear conditioning; RC, retention control; EXT, fear extinction; PTSD: post-traumatic stress disorder; PBMCs: peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6 mice aged 8–12 weeks, were housed in groups of four per cage. Mice were kept with unrestricted access to food and water and in a controlled environment with temperature (21–22 °C) and humidity, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. This animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuhan University (ethical number: ZN2023027). While the importance of including both sexes in neuroscience research is widely recognized [28, 29], this study focused on establishing a stable molecular profile in male mice. Only male mice were used in this study to minimize biological variability associated with the estrous cycle in females, which can influence stress responses, fear extinction, and small RNA expression [30, 31]. This approach allowed us to reduce hormonal confounds and focus on establishing a stable molecular profile of small RNAs during extinction learning.

All behavioral experiments were conducted quietly between 8:00 a.m. and 6:00 p.m. to avoid disruption of the mice’s circadian rhythm. A total of 77 mice were used in this study. Specifically, for small RNA sequencing and validation, 11 mice were assigned to the extinction training (EXT) group and 11 to the retention control (RC) group, of which 6 mice (3 per group) were used for sequencing. For RT-qPCR experiments, 8 mice were assigned to the fear conditioning (FC) group and 8 to the context control (CTX) group. For behavioral experiments involving miRNA inhibition, 19 mice were allocated to the control group and 20 to the inhibition group. All behavioral experiments were initiated when the mice reached adulthood (postnatal day 63) (Supplementary Figure 1).

Behavioral training and test

FC, CTX, EXT and RC were performed as previously described [32–34]. All behavioral testing was executed during the diurnal light phase within environments illuminated by red light, utilizing two distinct contexts, labeled A and B. Both conditioning chambers (AniLab Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., China) featured two transparent and two stainless steel walls, equipped with a steel grid floor (3.2 mm in diameter, spaced at 8 mm centers). In Context B, the grid floors were masked by a flat, white plastic layer, and the area was illuminated by two white LED lights to reduce context generalization effectively. Ceiling-mounted digital cameras, linked to a quad processor, facilitated automated data collection and analysis through a freezing measurement program (LabState). In Context A, both FC and CTX procedures were conducted in the chambers with a lemon-alcohol spray (5% lemon and 10% alcohol). FC began with a 120-second pre-conditioning habituation phase. This phase preceded three couplings of a 120-second, 80 dB white noise conditioned stimulus (CS) with a 1-second, 0.7 mA foot shock unconditioned stimulus (US). CTX control mice were placed in the same context for 14 minutes without exposure to CS or US. For the EXT and RC experiments, mice first underwent FC, followed by either EXT training or RC exposure 24 hours later. During the extinction phase (Context B), which involved exposure to a vinegar spray, mice were given a 2-minute acclimatization period before undergoing extinction training consisting of 30 unreinforced presentations of the CS, each lasting 120 seconds. In contrast, mice in the RC group were placed in Context B for the same duration without exposure to either the CS or the US. For the retention test, all mice were returned to Context B. After a 2 minutes acclimation, freezing behavior during CS presentations was recorded and expressed as the percentage of time spent freezing, to evaluate memory retention (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Cannulation surgery and miRNA inhibitor infusion

Following deep anesthesia, the fur on the mice’s heads was shaved, and they were placed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Instruments). Using the bregma as the reference, 1 μL of miRNA inhibitor (200 nM) was infused into the ILPFC (AP: 1.78 mm, ML: ±0.2 mm, and DV: −2.85 mm). The infusion rate was maintained at 0.1 μL/min, and the needle was left in ILPFC for 10 minutes before being carefully withdrawn. Following the surgery, mice were then allowed to recover from anesthesia on a heated pad. For the miRNA inhibition experiments, inhibitors were infused into ILPFC 24 hours after mice underwent fear conditioning, followed by fear extinction training 7 days post-infusion. Memory was assessed based on the percentage of time spent freezing during the recall test conducted one day after extinction training. The 5‘end of the miRNA inhibitor was labeled with a FAM modification for visualization, while the 3’ end was modified with cholesterol, and the entire chain was methoxy-modified to improve its stability in vivo.

Open-field test

The open field test was utilized to evaluate generalized anxiety and spontaneous locomotion in mice following miRNA inhibition. The experiment was conducted in a sound-attenuated room with dim white lighting. After a 30 minutes acclimatization period, mice were positioned in the center of a white plastic open field (50 cm × 50 cm × 40 cm), and their movements were recorded for 5 minutes using an overhead camera. VisuTrack software was employed to analyze the total distance traveled, velocity, and cumulative time spent in the center area, defined as a 15 cm × 15 cm square concentric with the base of the open field.

mPFC and plasma sample collection

Mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation immediately after behavioral training (Supplementary Figure 1). The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) was rapidly dissected on ice, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Blood was harvested by retro-orbital puncture using EDTA-coated capillary tubes. Samples were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C to separate plasma, which was subsequently stored at −80 °C for RNA extraction.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RNA extraction was performed with Trizol reagent (Takara), followed by RNA quantification (Nanodrop). For miRNA quantification, 500 ng RNA was reverse transcribed using miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (by stem-loop) (Vazyme #MR101) according to manufacturer’s guidelines. Quantitative PCR was then performed for mature miRNAs, including miR-29b-3p, miR-494-3p, and miR-17b-5p, using the miRNA Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme #MQ101). For Dnmt3a mRNA quantification, 1000ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed using HiScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme #R423). The resulting cDNA was used for qPCR with SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme #Q712). Relative expression of Dnmt3a was normalized to the endogenous control Pgk1, and relative expression of miRNAs was normalized to U6. All expression levels were analyzed using the 2-DeltaDeltaCt method. All qPCR reactions were performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time System. The cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 seconds, 60 °C for 15 seconds, and 72 °C for 20 seconds. Each sample was run in technical duplicates. Data are presented as mean ± SEM from technical replicates. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Small ncRNA microarray and bioinformatic analysis

Briefly, for each sample, 3-OH end RNA were generated using 100 ng total RNA. The RNA was then denatured and labeled with Cy3. The labeled RNA was hybridized onto microarray (8x15K, Arraystar Mouse small RNA Microarray) and the array was scanned by an Agilent Scanner G2505C. Array images were analyzed using Agilent Feature Extraction software (version 11.0.1.1). Quantile normalization and subsequent data processing were performed using GeneSpring GX v12.1 software package (Agilent Technologies). After normalization, the probe signals having Present (P) or Marginal (M) QC flags in at least 3 out of 6 samples were retained. Differentially small ncRNAs analysis was performed in order to identify those small ncRNAs that were DE during EXT, with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 as cut-off criteria. Gene expression profiles of PBMCs from combat veterans were in public database ArrayExpress (Accession# E-MTAB-4880) [35]. tRFtarget 2.0 [36], a tool utilizing RNAhybrid [37] and IntaRNA [38] to provide binding sites predicted via two different mechanisms, was used to predict tsRNA potential targets. To identify potential miRNA-mRNA interactions, we used the miRWalk 3.0 database [39], which integrates predictions from the TarPmiR algorithm [40], TargetScan [41], and miRDB [42], as well as experimentally validated interactions curated in miRTarBase [43]. Candidate interactions were retained only if they met the following criteria: (1) predicted by the TarPmiR algorithm with a binding probability > 0.9; and additionally, either (2) co-predicted by both TargetScan and miRDB, or (3) supported by experimental evidence in miRTarBase. Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession numbers GSE147899 and GSE225372 [44, 45]. Dataset GSE147899 includes samples from wild-type and Dnmt3aKO/+ (heterozygous knockout) mice [45], while GSE225372 includes samples from wild-type, Dnmt3aP900L/+ and Dnmt3aR878H/+ mutant mice [44]. In both datasets, sequencing was conducted on four brain regions: the cortex, hippocampus, striatum, and cerebellum. The Dnmt3aP900L/+ and Dnmt3aR878H/+ mutant mice correspond to the P904L and R882H mutations identified in human patients with Tatton-Brown Rahman syndrome [44]. Each dataset includes two biological replicates per genotype for each brain region.

Human participant recruitment and sample collection

All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wuhan University (ethical number: 2,022,174). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to sample collection. Healthy controls and PTSD patients were recruited for this study. All participants were first clinically assessed by professionals for PTSD. The Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) [46] was used to determine the presence of PTSD. Diagnostic interviews in the study were carried out by two independent evaluators, who were graduate-level clinical psychology students trained to achieve 85% reliability alongside an experienced clinical psychologist. Importantly, all evaluators were blind to the group assignment of the participants.

In this study, mentally healthy controls consisted of age-matched healthy volunteers without active infection symptoms, psychiatric disorders, or immune compromise history such as chronic steroid therapy, cancer, or pregnancy. In total, 9 PTSD patients and 8 healthy controls were included. Peripheral blood samples (5 mL) were collected from all participants and immediately processed by centrifugation to isolate PBMCs.

Results

Fear extinction learning-induced miRnas expression in mPFC

To confirm the effectiveness of the behavioral model, we first verified that fear conditioning successfully induced cue-associated fear memory, as indicated by increased freezing behavior. In the subsequent extinction training, mice in the EXT group displayed a gradual reduction in freezing across trials, indicating successful extinction learning (Supplementary Figure 2). These results confirm the reliability of the behavioral paradigm and provide a solid foundation for analyzing extinction-induced changes in miRNA expression in the mPFC. Next, we employed a small non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) microarray to profile the expression of small ncRNAs in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) following fear extinction training. The array covered multiple classes of small RNAs, including miRNAs, pre-miRNAs, tRNAs, tsRNAs, and snoRNAs. Among these, a total of 1,152 miRNAs were detected, of which 27 were significantly upregulated and 36 were downregulated (Fig. 2A–B, Supplementary Table S2). Among the 63 differentially expressed (DE) miRNAs, several, including miR-182, miR-466o-3p, and miR-17-5p, have previously been implicated in fear memory or cognitive behavior [47–50]. Notably, the study by Malan-Muller et al. [20] also reported that miR-1839-5p, one of the upregulated miRNAs in our dataset, was elevated following D-Cycloserine administration in fear-conditioned mice that exhibited reduced freezing behavior during fear extinction. Although miR-144-3p [5], which our group has shown to play a crucial role in rescuing impaired fear extinction, did not meet the stringent false discovery rate (FDR) threshold, it was nominally significant with a p-value < 0.05 and thus qualifies as a DE miRNA under less stringent criteria. Overall, the DE miRNAs identified with FDR < 0.05 are considered the most robust. Beyond miRNAs, we also detected 35 pre-miRNAs expression and found no DE pre-miRNA (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S3).

We next predicted potential target mRNAs of 63 DE miRNAs using miRWalk 3.0 database and obtained a total of 1666 and 1579 mRNAs for up-regulated and down-regulated miRNAs, respectively. To clarify the biological functions of the predicted miRNAs targets, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. GO analysis of the targets of both up-regulated and down-regulated miRNAs unveiled significant gene clusters related to dendrites and synapses, including “axonogenesis”, “synapse organization”, “dendrite development” (Fig. 2D–E). These findings suggest that extinction-induced miRNAs may play crucial regulatory roles in modulating genes involved in dendritic and synaptic processes underlying learning and memory.

miR-29b-3p serve as a potential biomarker for PTSD

Traumatic memories in PTSD patients become resistant to extinction over time due to the failure of extinction-dependent mechanisms. To explore whether extinction-induced miRNAs could serve as potential biomarkers for PTSD, we analyzed publicly available miRNA expression profiles in PBMCs from PTSD patients and healthy controls [35], identifying 250 differentially expressed miRNAs (Fig. 3A). Notably, analysis of publicly available PBMC miRNA microarray datasets from PTSD patients revealed that three conserved extinction-induced miRNAs including miR-29b-3p, miR-17-5p, and miR-494-3p, were downregulated compared to healthy controls (Fig. 3B). We next assessed the expression of these miRNAs in the mPFC of mice using RT-qPCR and found that miR-29b-3p and miR-494-3p were significantly upregulated following extinction training (Fig. 3C). To further validate these findings in an independent human cohort, we performed RT-qPCR on PBMC samples collected from clinically diagnosed PTSD patients and matched healthy controls. Detailed information on the human participants is provided in Supplementary Table S4. Among the three candidates, only miR-29b-3p consistently showed significant downregulation in PTSD patients (Fig. 3D).

To further characterize the expression dynamics of miR-29b-3p, we measured its levels in mouse plasma and mPFC following fear conditioning (FC), as well as in plasma after extinction training. Behavioral data confirmed that FC was effective in inducing fear memory, as the FC group exhibited significantly higher freezing levels compared to the context control (CTX) group (Supplementary Figure 3). qPCR results revealed that miR-29b-3p was significantly downregulated in both plasma and mPFC after FC, but remained unchanged following extinction training (Fig. 3E). The reduction in plasma miR-29b-3p levels following FC is consistent with its decreased expression in the mPFC of fear conditioned mice and PBMCs of PTSD patients, suggesting that miR-29b-3p may serve as a promising prognostic biomarker for PTSD.

To ascertain that whether miR-29b-3p plays a role in the mechanisms underlying extinction memory and could serve as a functional biomarker for PTSD. Next, we infused a miR-29b-3p inhibitor, which was complementary to miR-29b-3p and specifically designed to inhibit its function, into the ILPFC and confirmed its transfection efficiency (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S5). No significant differences were observed among the four groups during fear acquisition (Fig. 4B). Inhibition of miR-29b-3p prior to extinction training did not affect fear memory expression or the acquisition of extinction learning, as indicated by comparable reductions in freezing behavior within the extinction session (Fig. 4C–D). However, miR-29b-3p inhibition significantly impaired the formation of extinction memory, as evidenced by elevated freezing levels during the recall test 24 hours later (Fig. 4E). Additionally, anxiety-like behavior was comparable between mice infused with miR-29b-3p inhibition and those in the control group (Fig. 4F–G).

miR-29b-3p may regulate DNA methylation targeting Dnmt3a

Given that miRNAs typically function by regulating target gene expression, we predicted the targets of human hsa-miR-29b-3p and mouse mmu-miR-29b-3p using the miRWalk 3.0 database [39], which integrates computational predictions from TarPmiR, TargetScan, and miRDB, as well as experimentally validated interactions from miRTarBase. A total of 56 common targets were identified in both species (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Tables S6–S7). Among these, Dnmt3a drew particular attention, as it was consistently predicted as a miR-29b-3p target by all three algorithms and was also supported by experimental validation. Notably, Dnmt3a is known to be regulated by miR-29b-3p in the central nervous system and encodes a DNA methyltransferase essential for neuronal maturation [52]. In our study, Dnmt3a expression in the mPFC exhibited a decreasing trend following EXT training, although the change did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5B). Importantly, infusion of miR-29b-3p inhibitor reversed this downward trend (Fig. 5B), suggesting that Dnmt3a is negatively regulated by miR-29b-3p in the context of extinction learning. Leveraging the whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data derived from Dnmt3a-knockout or point-mutant mice, we found that the expression and functional domains of Dnmt3a are essential for the methylation of CG and de novo non-CG (CH) sites across the cortex, hippocampus, striatum, and cerebellum, with a particularly critical role in CH methylation (Fig. 5C–D) [44, 45]. Additionally, Dnmt3a is involved in the formation of fear memory [53, 54]. Therefore, the role of miR-29b-3p in the formation of fear extinction memory may be mediated through its regulation of Dnmt3a, which requires further experimental validation.

Together, these findings demonstrate that miR-29b-3p is essential for the formation of fear extinction memory. The downregulation of miR-29b-3p in peripheral PBMCs of PTSD patients indicates it may serve as a promising biomarker for PTSD.

Fear extinction learning-induced tsRNA expression in mPFC

We next assessed whether other small noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) were differentially expressed in the mPFC following EXT. We examined the differences in the abundance of various RNA biotypes after extinction training, including transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs), tRNAs, and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs). As shown in Fig. 6A, tsRNAs exhibited relatively higher abundance in the mPFC, whereas precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) showed comparatively lower abundance. We then profiled the expression of 71 precursor tRNAs, including tRNA-Gln-CTG, Lys-CTT, Val-TAC, Gln-TTG, Ser-GCT, and Pro-TGG, but found no significant differential expression among them following EXT (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Table S8). Beyond their canonical role in translation, tRNAs are increasingly recognized for their noncanonical regulatory functions, particularly through their derived fragments, tsRNAs (Fig. 6C). We screened a total of 1,309 tsRNAs, with detected fragment lengths ranging from 20 to 50 nucleotides (Fig. 6D). Among these, 38 tsRNAs were significantly upregulated and 37 were significantly downregulated between mice that underwent EXT and retention control (RC) training (Fig. 6E, F, Supplementary Table S9). Notably, the DE tsRNAs showed a distinct length peak at approximately 36 nucleotides (Fig. 6D). We further analyzed the subtypes of the DE tsRNAs and found that among the upregulated group, 5′tiRNAs accounted for 63.16%, 5′tRFs for 34.21%, and 3′tRFs for 2.63%. In contrast, among the downregulated group, 3′tiRNAs comprised 37.84%, 3′tRFs 32.43%, 5′tiRNAs 16.22%, and 5′tRFs 13.51% (Fig. 7A). Interestingly, 5′tiRNA and 3′tiRNA account for the highest proportion of up-regulated and down-regulated tsRNAs, respectively. The underlying biological mechanisms for the observed population variance in tsRNAs remain unidentified. And it is essential to conduct further investigation into whether these observed differences are directly linked to the formation of fear extinction memory. Furthermore, DE tsRNA biogenesis appears to be derived from different population of full tRNA transcripts, with Gln-CTG, Val-TAC, Gln-TTG and Ser-GCT being the most abundant for up-regulated group, and with relatively higher levels of Lys-CTT, Lys-TTT and Pro-TGG in down-regulated tsRNAs (Fig. 7B).

To further investigate the potential functions of these DE tsRNA, tRFtarget 2.0 was utilized to predict potential tsRNA targets [36]. We got 349 and 159 potential targets for up-regulated and down-regulated tsRNAs, respectively. Further GO analysis indicated that the potential functions of up-regulated tsRNAs were focused primarily on cation channel and transporter complex, while down-regulated tsRNAs were most enriched for GTPase activator and regulator activity (Fig. 7C, D).

As a class of small ncRNA, snoRNA takes part in epitranscriptomic mechanism affecting processes associated with CNS diseases [55]. However, our analysis comparing the RC and EXT groups revealed that none of the detected 101 snoRNAs reached the threshold (FDR < 0.05) to be considered DE following EXT training (Supplementary Figure S4, Supplementary Table S10). The stringent statistical test utilized in our analysis in order to counteract the problem of multiple testing caused this and further qPCR validation needs to be considered.

Discussion

The identification of small regulatory non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) has significantly advanced our understanding of gene expression regulation in the brain, particularly in relation to synaptic plasticity and memory formation. In this study, we systematically profiled five small ncRNA classes in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) during fear extinction (EXT) training and identified extinction-induced differential expression of both transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that their predicted target genes were primarily involved in ion channel activity, GTPase regulation, and synaptic function, underscoring their potential roles in extinction-related molecular pathways.

In addition to miRNAs, our study identified a distinct profile of extinction-induced tsRNAs, characterized by the upregulation of 5ʹtiRNAs and downregulation of 3ʹtiRNAs. This pattern suggests a functional role for specific tRNA-derived fragments in extinction memory. Notably, these changes occurred without alterations in the levels of mature tRNAs, indicating that tsRNAs are generated through endonuclease-mediated cleavage rather than transcriptional regulation during extinction learning. Previous studies have reported the accumulation of 3ʹtiRNAs in the amygdala and hippocampus [56]. Furthermore, another study emphasized a developmental shift from predominantly 5ʹtiRNAs observed in vitro to mainly 3ʹtiRNAs in mature axons in vivo [57], indicating that tsRNA biogenesis may distinctly influence neural processes related to both development and behavior. A specific tsRNA, Glu-5ʹtsRNA-CTC, has been shown to impair mitochondrial function and glutamate biosynthesis in glutamatergic neurons, contributing to age-related memory decline [58]. In our study, we identified several extinction-induced 5ʹtiRNAs derived from tRNA-Glu(CTC), including 5ʹtiRNA-35-GluCTC-3 and 5ʹtiRNA-36-GluCTC-1, which were significantly upregulated following extinction training. These tsRNAs may contribute to extinction learning by modulating synaptic signaling or cellular metabolism. Collectively, these findings highlight tsRNAs as emerging regulators of experience-dependent plasticity and underscore the need for further investigation into their molecular targets and mechanisms in the context of extinction memory.

A key finding of this study is inhibition of miR-29b-3p impaired extinction memory, supporting its functional necessity in this process. Notably, miR-29b-3p exhibited a distinct expression pattern in the mPFC, with decreased levels following fear conditioning and increased levels after extinction training, highlighting its critical role in extinction memory formation. Fear-related behavior reflects a dynamic interplay between fear memory and extinction memory [19]. In this process, miR-29b-3p contribute to the formation of extinction memory. During FC, fear memory is established and is accompanied by a downregulation of miR-29b-3p in the mPFC. This strong learning experience may also lead to a systemic reduction in circulating miR-29b-3p, as evidenced by its decreased levels in plasma. In contrast, extinction training leads to an upregulation of miR-29b-3p within the mPFC and promotes the formation of fear extinction memory.

Previous studies have linked miR-29b-3p to various brain disorders. Previous studies have implicated miR-29b-3p in various brain disorders. In rodent models of depression, its expression is downregulated, while ketamine treatment or overexpression of miR-29b-3p alleviates depressive-like behaviors by restoring its levels in the prefrontal cortex [59]. In Huntington’s disease, miR-29b-3p promotes neurodegeneration by impairing autophagy through STAT3 targeting; reducing its expression or enhancing autophagy improves neuronal survival [60]. Additionally, in autism spectrum disorder (ASD) models, miR-29b-3p-enriched exosomes suppress IGF-1 signaling in the mPFC, contributing to ASD-like behaviors, while inhibition of miR-29b-3p reverses these phenotypes [61].

Additionally, Dnmt3a may be the key gene through which miR-29b-3p functions in extinction memory. Experimental evidence shows that miR-29b-3p binds to the 3′UTR region of Dnmt3a mRNA [52, 62, 63], thereby regulating its expression. Importantly, knockdown of Dnmt3a in the dorsal hippocampus impairs contextual fear memory formation [54]. Conversely, another study reported that overexpression of Dnmt3a in the dorsal dentate gyrus (DG) inhibits contextual fear memory expression [53]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) injection timing or Dnmt3a subtypes. Furthermore, overexpression of Dnmt3a in the dorsal DG also hinders fear renewal after extinction [53], a behavioral change unrelated to anxiety. Collectively, these findings suggest that Dnmt3a plays a crucial role in the expression of fear memory and fear renewal following extinction. Given strong evidence that miR-29b-3p regulates Dnmt3a, it is likely that miR-29b-3p functions in the formation of fear extinction memory by targeting Dnmt3a to control DNA methylation, which is crucial for gene expression related to synapse plasticity [32, 64–66].

Overall, we observed that miR-29b-3p expression is essential for the formation of fear extinction memory, whereas it is downregulated in the PBMCs of PTSD patients. These findings highlight the potential of miR-29b-3p as a promising biomarker for PTSD and a potential therapeutic target for fear memory-related disorders.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, although we identified extinction-related miRNAs through small RNA sequencing and validated their expression using RT-qPCR, the initial sequencing sample size was limited (n = 3per group). While all differentially expressed miRNAs passed FDR correction, the small number of animals may have increased the risk of false positives and limited the statistical power of our analyses. Future studies involving larger cohorts will be necessary to validate and expand upon these findings. Second, the conclusion that miR-29b-3p may regulate fear extinction memory via Dnmt3a is primarily based on bioinformatic target prediction and previously reported literature. Although this offers a plausible mechanistic hypothesis, direct experimental validation, such as detecting DNA methylation changes in the mPFC following miR-29b-3p inhibition, was not performed in this study and remains an important direction for future work. Third, the study is limited by the exclusive use of male animals. While this approach allowed us to establish a consistent molecular profile under controlled conditions, it restricts the generalizability of our findings across sexes. The dynamics of miR-29b-3p expression throughout the estrous cycle in females, and how these variations may influence fear extinction differently from males, remain unclear. Determining whether the regulation and functional role of miR-29b-3p during fear extinction is conserved between sexes will be a key focus of future investigations. One limitation of this study is that we did not directly examine the role of miR-29b-3p during fear memory acquisition. Although we observed its downregulation in the mPFC following fear conditioning, the miRNA inhibitor was administered after the conditioning phase, specifically to assess its function in extinction memory. As such, whether miR-29b-3p plays a causal role in the formation of fear memory remains unresolved and warrants further investigation.

Furthermore, while our findings highlight miR-29b-3p as a promising circulating biomarker for PTSD, based on its consistent downregulation in both PBMCs of PTSD patients and plasma from fear-conditioned mice, it is important to acknowledge its broader involvement in other disorders. Previous studies have reported altered miR-29b-3p down-regulation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [67], while elevated levels have been observed in exosomes from rodent models of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [61]. These findings suggest that miR-29b-3p may not be uniquely specific to PTSD but response to other diseases. Nonetheless, from a clinical perspective, PTSD, AD, and ASD differ significantly in symptom profiles, typical age of onset, and diagnostic criteria. [68] Differential diagnosis between these disorders is rarely a practical challenge. Given that PTSD diagnosis currently relies primarily on patient history and standardized questionnaires, the identification of peripheral biomarkers such as miR-29b-3p may provide a valuable complement to current diagnostic approaches. Still, the specificity of miR-29b-3p as a biomarker for PTSD remains to be firmly established. Future studies should evaluate its expression across a wider spectrum of psychiatric and neurological conditions to better assess its diagnostic relevance and specificity.

Conclusion

Our investigation highlights the dynamic changes in small ncRNAs in the context of fear extinction learning, revealing their significant implications for psychiatric conditions. Through rigorous quantification and comprehensive analysis, we identified distinct patterns of differential expression in tsRNAs and miRNAs. The most abundant differentially expressed tsRNAs were derived from various tRNA populations, indicating specific roles of tsRNAs in extinction memory. Notably, we discovered 63 miRNAs associated with synapse and dendrite function. Among these, miR-29b-3p emerged as a crucial regulator of fear extinction memory, likely through its modulation of Dnmt3a stability. Our study further emphasizes the potential of miR-29b-3p as a diagnostic and therapeutic target for PTSD. These findings enhance our understanding of the intricate mechanisms underlying fear extinction, highlighting small ncRNAs as promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets for psychiatric disorders.
