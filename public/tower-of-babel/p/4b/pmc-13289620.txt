Chronic kidney disease in India: Incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALYs, and State-level disparities, 1990–2023
Authors: Hari Shankar Meshram, Sanshriti Chauhan, Saurabh Puri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major non-communicable cause of premature mortality and disability in India, yet no study has systematically evaluated the State-level trajectories and inequalities underpinning its burden.MethodsWe extracted age-standardised incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALY) estimates for CKD (ICD-10: N18–N19) from the global burden of disease (GBD) 2023 database for the period 1990-2023. Estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was calculated using log-linear regression. States were classified into clusters based on burden relative to the national age-standardised rate and trend direction. Socioeconomic gradients were assessed using the socio-demographic index (SDI), concentration curves, and concentration indices. Uncertainty intervals (UIs) were incorporated.ResultsIn 2023, across Indian States the incidence ranged from 226.4 to 316.4 per 100,000, prevalence from 10,452 to 12,539 per 100,000, mortality from 6.71 to 19.84 per 100,000, and DALYs from 312.2 to 666.4 per 100,000. National EAPCs showed declining trends for incidence (-0.45%), prevalence (-0.22%), mortality (-0.90%), and DALYs (-1.10%). However, high-burden States such as Telangana and Punjab had sustained elevated mortality and DALYs despite declining trends. Concentration indices demonstrated significant pro-poor inequality for mortality [confidence interval (CI) -0.12] and DALYs (CI -0.24). Locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) models revealed non-linear SDI relationships, with high-SDI States achieving lower mortality but persistent high prevalence.Interpretations and conclusionsCKD poses a substantial and unequal burden across India, with widening disparities in mortality and disability. Targeted State-level interventions, early detection programmes, and equitable renal care infrastructure are urgently needed to reduce geographic inequity and achieve national NCD targets.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a rapidly escalating global health challenge and is now ranked as the 8th leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for over 3 million deaths annually in 2023 alone.1 India bears a disproportionate share of this burden, driven by the dual epidemics of diabetes and hypertension, rising life expectancy, and persistent environmental and occupational exposures.2-4 Despite this, CKD remains largely invisible in national health strategies and is not included as a priority condition within the National programme for prevention and control of non-communicable diseases (NPCDCS), resulting in major policy neglect.5

The global burden of disease (GBD) study has demonstrated that India exhibits one of the highest absolute CKD burden globally.6 However, national-level estimates mask the profound inter-State disparities driven by heterogeneity in socioeconomic development, healthcare access, environmental determinants, and demographic transition.7,8 In India’s decentralised health governance structure, States are the primary units of healthcare planning, financing, and service delivery. Therefore, State-specific burden estimates are essential for health system reforms, strategic purchasing, and equitable resource allocation under the Ayushman Bharat health assurance mission.9,10

Despite the recognised national burden of CKD in India, critical gaps remain. Prior studies have largely focused on national aggregates, dialysis cohorts, or select regions, obscuring inter-State heterogeneity in disease trajectories, mortality, and disability. Existing regional reports are limited in scope, based on small datasets, or restricted to dialysis populations, and are therefore not generalisable at the population level.11,12 Furthermore, socioeconomic gradients and inequality like socio-demographic index (SDI) in CKD outcomes have not been systematically quantified at the subnational level. This study addresses these gaps by providing a comprehensive State-level analysis of CKD incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs in India from 1990 to 2023, incorporating temporal trend analysis and formal inequality metrics to inform equitable, State-specific policy responses. These estimates are integral for operationalising CKD prevention, forecasting dialysis and transplant demand. This also helps in informing State-tailored strategies to meet India’s commitment to universal health coverage and sustainable development goal (SDG) Target 3.4.

Methods

The study was undertaken by the department of Nephrology, Institute of Liver and Biliary Sciences, New Delhi. The study utilised publicly available, de-identified GBD data.

Study design and data source

We synthesised this report abiding with the GATHER guidelines.13 This was a population-based observational study using the GBD database.14 We analysed the burden of CKD across all 28 Indian States and 3 Union Territories, with minor Union Territories aggregated into a single analytical unit (‘other Union Territories’), in accordance with GBD reporting conventions. Data were obtained from the Institute for health metrics and evaluation (IHME) Global health data exchange (GHDx) and GBD results tool.15 The data retrieval focused on four primary metrics: age-standardised incidence, prevalence, mortality rates, and DALY, reported per 100,000 populations from 1990 till 2023. SDI, and population at State level was also retrieved for 2023. The data downloaded from GBD is available publicly as open source: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17432524 .

Case definition, GBD data source, and GBD estimation framework

CKD was defined according to GBD Cause Level 3 classification and ICD-10 codes N18-N19. This definition was applied consistently across the entire study period (1990–2023) through retrospective harmonisation within the GBD analytical framework. GBD estimates are derived from a synthesis of multiple data sources. This included vital registration systems, sample registration system (SRS), verbal autopsy studies, population-based surveys, hospital discharge data, and disease registries. Data availability varies by State and time period, and the gaps are addressed using Bayesian meta-regression, DisMod-MR 2.1 to generate comparable estimates across geography and time. Cause-specific mortality is estimated using the cause of death ensemble model (CODEm),16 and DALYs are calculated as the sum of years of life lost due to premature mortality and years lived with disability, based on GBD disability weights.17

Outcome measures

(i)Current burden estimation: We reported age-standardised rates (ASRs) of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for each State from last available year in GBD data base, i.e. 2023.(ii)Temporal trend analysis: Long-term temporal changes were assessed using estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). A log-linear regression was fitted for each State: ln ASRt=α+ βt=EAPC= e{ β}–1×100 where t is calendar year. The 95% uncertainty interval (UI) was derived from the standard error of β. States were classified into four epidemiological clusters based on whether the ASR in 2023 was above or below the national mean (high-burden vs. low-burden) and whether the EAPC was positive or negative (increasing vs. decreasing trend).(iii)Trajectory modelling: For each State and metric, temporal trajectories from 1990 to 2023 were visualised using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) with 95% UIs. State-specific panels were arranged alphabetically to permit comparative interpretation. National trajectories were overlaid as a benchmark in each panel.(iv)Socio-demographic index (SDI) and cross-sectional analysis: The SDI, a composite measure of lag-distributed income, average educational attainment, and fertility rate in women under 25 years, scaled from 0 to 1, was used as a proxy for development. SDI values for 2023 were extracted for all Indian States. The relationship between SDI and each CKD metric was assessed using LOESS regression with 95% confidence shading.(v)Inequality assessment: Inter-State inequality was quantified using the population-weighted Gini coefficient and quintile ratio (mean ASR in the top 20% vs. bottom 20% of States ranked by SDI). Socioeconomic inequality was further evaluated using Lorenz concentration curves and concentration indices. States were ranked by SDI from lowest to highest, and cumulative proportions of population were plotted against cumulative proportions of outcome counts. The concentration index was calculated as: CI=2×AUC−1 where AUC is the area under the concentration curve. A negative CI indicates disproportionate burden among low-SDI States (pro-poor inequality). Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was used to generate 95% UIs for the concentration curve.(vi)Uncertainty reporting: All GBD estimates include 95% UIs generated from posterior simulations. Uncertainty was propagated through all analyses, including trajectory plots, EAPC estimates, and concentration indices. No separate sensitivity analyses were conducted because GBD metrics inherently incorporate uncertainty through probabilistic modelling, which has been extensively validated.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Python version 3.11 (pandas, numpy, statsmodels, and matplotlib libraries). LOESS smoothing and bootstrap procedures were conducted using locally weighted regression functions.

Results

Current burden of CKD

The national age-standardised incidence rate ranged from 226.4 per 100,000 (95% UI: 210.6-492.0) in Rajasthan to 316.4 (295.8-684.6) in Haryana, with most States clustering between 230 and 270 per 100,000 ( Table I). Prevalence exceeded 10,000 per 100,000 population in every State, ranging from 10,452 (UI: 9,802-22,467) in Uttar Pradesh to 12,539 (11,678-26,730) in Haryana, indicating a high chronic disease pool across the nation. Mortality rates ranged three-fold, from 6.71 deaths per 100,000 (3.80-21.72) in Rajasthan to 19.84 (13.92-54.84) in Telangana, demonstrating significant geographic disparities in fatal outcomes. DALY rates, reflecting combined morbidity and mortality, ranged from 312.2 (226.3-858.7) in Rajasthan to 666.4 (515.3-1704.4) in Telangana, with States such as Punjab, Other Union Territories, and Chhattisgarh also exhibiting consistently elevated DALY burdens.

DALY, disability-adjusted life-years

Temporal trends

From 1990 to 2023, age-standardised CKD rates declined nationally, although the magnitude and direction of change varied by State and metric ( Table II, Fig. 1). The incidence rate declined at a national EAPC of -0.45% (95% UI -0.50 to -0.40). Most States showed decreasing trends, while some demonstrated stagnation or slight increases. Prevalence declined more modestly (EAPC -0.22%), reflecting extended patient survival and rising absolute case counts driven by population ageing. Mortality exhibited a steeper decline, with a national EAPC of -0.90%, consistent with improvements in early detection and treatment access. DALYs declined at the fastest rate, (EAPC -1.10%), indicating gains in both longevity and quality of life. States were categorised into four epidemiological clusters based on burden (relative to national ASR) and trend (positive or negative EAPC): (i) High-burden, decreasing trend (HB-D) e.g., Telangana, Punjab, Goa; (ii) High-burden, increasing trend (HB-I) e.g., Uttar Pradesh, Odisha; (iii) Low-burden, decreasing trend (LB-D) e.g., Kerala, Himachal Pradesh; (iv) Low-burden, increasing trend (LB-I).

Socio demographic index association

In cross-sectional analysis of 2023 State-level data ( Fig. 2, Supplementary Table), a non-linear association was observed in all. ASR incidence increased with rising SDI, with a clear upward inflection beyond an SDI of 0.65, rising from approximately 240 per 100,000 in low-SDI States to >280 per 100,000 in the highest SDI States. In contrast, prevalence demonstrated an inverted U-shaped trajectory, increasing from low-SDI to mid-SDI States (peaking around 11,500-12,000 per 100,000) before stabilising or marginally declining in high-SDI States, indicating greater disease detection and improved survival rather than uncontrolled progression. ASR mortality showed a weak negative association with SDI, declining from ∼14-16 deaths per 100,000 in low-SDI States to <10 per 100,000 in higher-SDI States, though variability remained high across middle-SDI regions. This pattern was mirrored in DALY rates, which decreased from >600 DALYs per 100,000 in low-SDI States to <400 per 100,000 in the most developed States. Notably, LOESS smoothing curves demonstrated widening uncertainty bands at mid-range SDI values, indicating heterogeneous health system performance and unequal translation of socioeconomic progress into improved kidney health outcomes.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive State-level analysis of CKD burden in India using GBD 2023 estimates, spanning three decades and incorporating four key epidemiological metrics incidences, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs. The findings reveal a paradoxical epidemiological pattern: while age-standardised rates have declined nationally, absolute burden remains high and continues to increase due to demographic expansion, population ageing, and improved survival among CKD patients. This duality underscores that India is experiencing a shift from acute to chronic kidney disease epidemiology, mirroring trends seen in other middle-income countries undergoing rapid transitions.18

Despite national improvements, we identified striking heterogeneity across States. High-burden States such as Telangana, Punjab, and Haryana maintained CKD rates significantly above the national mean across all metrics, whereas Kerala and Tamil Nadu exhibited lower mortality and DALY rates despite high prevalence, reflecting improved survival and health system maturity. These findings suggest differential progression pathways: in high-SDI States, CKD is managed as a chronic long-term condition, whereas in low-SDI States, CKD disproportionately translates into premature mortality and disability due to late presentation and treatment inaccessibility.5

The observed pro-poor concentration of mortality (CI = -0.12) and DALYs (CI = -0.24) highlights structural inequities in access to dialysis, transplantation, and nephrology services. Similar gradients have been reported in South Asian and Latin American regions, where subnational health financing and infrastructure determine CKD outcomes more than national-level policies.19,20 The widening uncertainty bands around mid-level SDI States suggest a stalled epidemiological transition, where health gains have not matched socioeconomic improvements.

Our findings align with global GBD reports, which indicate a global decline in age-standardised mortality but increasing absolute burden due to population dynamics.21 However, the rate of decline in India (-0.9% per year) is slower than that reported in China and Brazil (-1.6% and -1.4% respectively), countries which have implemented national CKD registries and subsidised universal dialysis programmes.22,23 Conversely, India’s trajectory more closely resembles Southeast Asian nations where fragmented health financing and reliance on out-of-pocket expenditure impede equitable kidney care delivery.24

The non-linear association between SDI and CKD burden underscores the complex interplay of risk exposure, detection rates, and health system preparedness. Higher SDI States showed greater incidence likely reflecting enhanced surveillance and higher diabetes prevalence25 while lower SDI States had disproportionately high mortality and DALYs, reflecting delayed diagnosis, low awareness, and catastrophic expenditure.26 These findings reinforce the concept of CKD as a sentinel marker of health system inequity, rather than merely a biomedical condition.

Additionally, our report shows the etiological profiles varies across States. CKD due to hypertension showed high mortality-to-prevalence ratio, which suggests poor prognosis requiring aggressive early intervention. Similarly, other/unspecified causes’ dominance indicates diagnostic gaps across various States. These findings point towards region-specific strategies addressing local risk profiles.

The results have direct implications for national and State-level planning. First, CKD must be urgently integrated into India’s national NCD strategy (NPCDCS) with priority financing for dialysis and transplantation under Ayushman Bharat.27 Second, State-specific CKD surveillance and registries are essential for tailoring interventions to varying epidemiological profiles. Third, early detection programmes must be decentralised to primary care settings, particularly in low-SDI States, with emphasis on diabetes and hypertension control. Without these actions, India risks a widening CKD treatment gap that disproportionately affects disadvantaged populations.

Key strengths include the use of harmonised, comparable GBD framework data across time and geography, comprehensive inequality assessment, and alignment with the reporting standards. There are a few inherent limitations worth mentioning: (i) The KDOQI CKD definition28 was coined in 2002. However, the GBD framework applies a retrospective harmonisation approach using ICD-coded diagnoses (N18-N19) and Bayesian meta-regression to ensure internal consistency of CKD estimates across earlier time periods. Hence, CKD burden estimates prior to 2002 reflected modelled disease constructs rather than contemporaneous guideline-based diagnoses. (ii) Although individual-level unique identifiers are not uniformly available in Indian healthcare systems, the GBD framework mitigates potential duplication through probabilistic modelling and data triangulation. Nevertheless, residual over- or under-estimation of CKD burden cannot be entirely excluded, particularly in rural regions. (iii) There is under-representation of CKD patients seeking treatment from unorganised healthcare sector. This group may never get reported in the government data on GBD. Since such practice is expected to be there in all States, it is not likely to affect inter-State health indices. (iv) There would be a State-wise variation in data completeness and reporting quality. Some States with weaker civil registration and health information systems may have greater reliance on modelled estimates, contributing to wider uncertainty intervals.

Future studies should integrate State-level clinical registries and environmental exposure data to strengthen causal inference.
