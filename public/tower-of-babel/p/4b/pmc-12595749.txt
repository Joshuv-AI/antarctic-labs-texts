Acute effects of intrinsic foot muscle fatigue on single-leg static balance parameters in healthy young adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatmagül Varol, Ali Ilez, Ömer Faruk Çelik, Gülselin Kibaroğulları, Fatma İlknur Karaoğlu, Özmen Gürbüz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe intrinsic foot muscles (IFMs) are known to contribute significantly to postural control, particularly during tasks requiring single-limb stability. However, their acute role under fatigued conditions remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the acute effects of IFMs fatigue on static balance parameters during single-leg stance in healthy young adults.MethodsSixty-six participants performed a standardized IFMs fatigue protocol consisting of six exercises. Static balance was assessed using a stabilometric platform during single-leg stance with eyes open (EO) and closed (EC), both before and immediately after the fatigue protocol. Static balance parameters and trunk movements were analyzed. Paired t-tests were conducted, and effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d.ResultsFollowing the intrinsic foot muscle fatigue protocol, during EO single-leg stance, medio-lateral CoP displacement increased (p = 0.003, d = 0.37), while sway speed decreased in both medio-lateral (p = 0.01, d = 0.31) and antero-posterior directions (p = 0.03, d = 0.26), along with a reduction in sway perimeter (p = 0.004, d = 0.36). In EC stance, medio-lateral trunk sway (p = 0.009, d = 0.14) and total trunk sway (p = 0.011, d = 0.39) significantly increased, with no other parameters showing significant changes (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis study highlights IFMs fatigue was associated with decreased sway speed and ellipse area despite greater CoPx variability, suggesting a role in modulating fine adjustments. Under EC conditions, increased trunk sway appeared to reflect a compensatory postural adjustment. Overall, these findings indicate that IFMs act not only as structural supporters but also as active contributors to postural control.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Intrinsic foot muscle, Fatigue, Balance

Background

The foot is one of the most vital anatomical structures of the human body, playing a crucial role in both stability and mobility. The intrinsic foot muscles (IFMs), organized in four plantar layers, serve as primary stabilizers of the foot [1]. They support the medial longitudinal arch, enhance flexibility, contribute to postural stability, and assist in shock absorption while partially controlling pronation [1, 2]. These muscles play a critical role in maintaining posture and balance during both static and dynamic tasks, acting as the core stabilizers of the foot [3–5]. Maintaining postural stability is a complex process involving the integration of visual, vestibular, and somatosensory information. Among the somatosensory contributors, IFM have gained growing attention for their role in static balance [1]. Specifically, the abductor hallucis (AbH) and adductor digiti minimi (ADM) contribute to maintaining the longitudinal arch and likely assist in balance control during standing [1]. Increased activity in the AbH and flexor digitorum brevis (FDB) muscles has been observed as single-leg balance demands increase [2]. Furthermore, during quiet standing, the foot is positioned in a flexible state with arch deformation changing in respect to anterior–posterior shin sway and the intrinsic foot muscles can actively regulate arch deformation [6, 7]. Given their position as the final mediators in the kinetic chain, it is logical to assume that the nervous system recruits these muscles to maintain balance.

Changes in the activation patterns or functional capacity of the IFMs have been associated with several musculoskeletal and systemic conditions, such as diabetes mellitus and impaired postural control [8–10]. For instance, research has demonstrated that fatigue in the plantar intrinsic muscles significantly increases navicular drop, indicating a more pronated foot posture in static conditions [9]. Moreover, fatigue in the plantar intrinsic muscles has been shown to result in a pronated foot posture [11]. This postural alteration may affect the effectiveness of balance regulation. However, the specific effects of IFM fatigue on static balance remain unclear. Local muscle fatigue has emerged as a relevant factor influencing postural stability, particularly when affecting muscles involved in proprioceptive feedback and structural support [12]. The link between muscular fatigue and an increased risk of injury has been confirmed by numerous studies [13, 14]. While extrinsic foot muscle (EFM) fatigue has been linked to impaired postural control, especially with the eyes closed [15–17], the mechanical role of the IFMs in static balance remains under-explored.

This study aims to investigate the acute effects of IFM fatigue on static balance parameters. It was hypothesized that acute fatigue of the intrinsic foot muscles would impair static balance, as evidenced by increased postural sway and decreased stability during quiet standing. Understanding the contribution of the IFMs during a single-leg stance, where energy must be generated and absorbed, could have important clinical implications. Such findings could inform rehabilitation strategies and athletic training programmes. Although they are essential for foot core stability, IFMs are often overlooked in assessments and treatment plans. Therefore, this study seeks to clarify how IFM fatigue influences static balance and the foot’s ability to maintain an upright posture.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted between October 2023 and January 2024 at the Practice Laboratory of the Hamidiye Faculty of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation, University of Health Sciences. The minimum number of participants to be included in the study was determined through an a priori power analysis performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) [18]. Since the study employed a pretest–posttest design, the power analysis was based on a paired-samples t-test. As a reference for effect size, the study by Ferrari et al. (2020), which investigated postural control changes across different balance tasks and reported a large effect, was used. Based on the findings of that study, the effect size (Cohen’s d) was calculated as 0.95; however, to adopt a more conservative approach, a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8) was assumed in the analysis [3]. The alpha (α) level was set at 0.05, and to minimize the probability of Type II error, the target power (1-β) was set at 98%. According to these parameters, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 28 participants was required to achieve the desired statistical power. To maximize the statistical power of the study and eliminate the risk of potential data loss, 66 individuals were included in the study (Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria were: age between 18 and 25 years, body mass index between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m², foot posture index (FPI-6) score between 0 and 5, navicular drop test (NDT) < 10 mm, and a negative Jack test [19]. Exclusion criteria included: systemic, chronic, or musculoskeletal diseases; history of injury or surgery; vestibular or visual problems; any foot or ankle joint limitation or deformity. These additional tests were used in combination to confirm the FPI-6 findings and enhance the accuracy of foot posture classification. All foot assessments were performed by a licensed physiotherapist with at least five years of clinical experience to ensure reliable and consistent evaluations. Ethical approval was obtained from the Hamidiye Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences (Meeting No: 23/20; Decision No: 20/42), and all procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Preliminary assessment

To verify inclusion and exclusion criteria, all participants underwent a series of preliminary tests. The dominant foot was determined using the ball-kicking method [20]. Foot posture was assessed with FPI-6 [21], and the navicular drop distance was measured via the NDT [22]. Written informed consent was obtained. Demographic and foot-specific clinical measurements were recorded, including age, BMI, dominant foot, FPI-6, and navicular drop values. Participants were instructed to avoid anti-inflammatory or muscle-relaxing medications within 12 h, alcohol consumption within 24 h, and sports activities within 48 h before testing.

Intrinsic foot muscle fatigue protocol

The fatigue protocol consisted of six exercises targeting the intrinsic foot muscles, performed sequentially on both feet in sitting, double-leg standing, and single-leg standing positions. A detailed visual representation of the protocol is shown in Fig. 2. The protocol began with the Reverse Tandem exercise (30 s), followed by the Short Foot exercise (1 min), Toe Spread Out (10 repetitions), 2nd–5th Phalanx Extension, Hallux Extension, and finally Vele’s Forward Lean (30 s) [23–25]. During the single-leg phase of the protocol, all exercises were performed on the dominant limb only.

Intrinsic foot muscle fatigue protocol. The protocol included six consecutive exercises: (1) reverse tandem walk, (2) short foot exercise, (3) toe spreading, (4) 2nd–5th phalanx extension, (5) hallux extension, and (6) Vele’s forward lean

Our protocol was based on previous studies [26–28], which demonstrated that local muscle fatigue can be effectively induced through the repetition or sustained holding of simple segmental movements in various muscle groups. Inspired by these approaches, we applied dynamic muscular actions based on time and repetition to target intrinsic foot muscles.

Balance assessment

Static balance was assessed using a stabilometric platform (Prokin, TecnoBody S.R.L., Dalmine, Italy), which evaluates postural sway based on CoP displacement [29, 30]. All participants performed two single-leg stance tests on their dominant foot: one with their eyes open and one with their eyes closed, both before and immediately after the fatigue protocol. No rest period was provided after the fatigue protocol to preserve its acute effects before the balance assessment was repeated. The eyes-open condition was always administered first. Participants stood barefoot in a standardized position, with arms at their sides and gaze directed at a fixed target. Foot positioning was standardized using a V-shaped guide integrated into the device to ensure consistency during test initiation. However, this guide did not impose a strict foot progression angle, allowing participants to maintain their natural balance strategies throughout the assessment. Each test lasted 30 s, and no verbal feedback was provided during data collection. Center of pressure change (CoPx, CoPy), forward-backward sway (inches), medio-lateral sway (inches), average speed (Medio-Lateral, Front-Back) mm/s, oscillation area (ellipse area; mm²), used perimeter (mm), forward-backward and medio-lateral trunk sway, and total trunk balance data were recorded. Trunk sway measurements were obtained directly using a built-in apparatus integrated into the stabilometric platform.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of all continuous variables was assessed using both the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and the data were found to be normally distributed. Therefore, comparisons between pre- and post-fatigue conditions were performed using the paired samples t-test. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (n, %) for categorical variables. The effect size for the paired comparisons was calculated using Cohen’s d, where values of 0.2 were considered small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 or greater large. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the acute effects of IFM fatigue, induced through a repeated fatigue protocol, on postural sway and its transient characteristics during single-leg standing in healthy young adults. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between IFM fatigue and static balance capacity. This study showed that IFM fatigue increased in CoP medio-lateral direction variables and decreased in medial-lateral and front-back velocity, and ellipse area velocity during single-leg standing with EO compared with the pre-fatigue conditions. Under EC conditions, the only significant increase was observed in trunk sway during single-leg standing. These results highlight the crucial role of intrinsic foot muscles in maintaining static balance, particularly in visually guided postural tasks.

The present study focused on IFM fatigue, with balance assessed exclusively on the dominant leg. The present study focused on IFM fatigue, with balance assessed exclusively on the dominant leg. This emphasis on single-limb stance is supported by previous research showing that foot muscle fatigue in young adults did not significantly affect postural stability in double-leg stance [31]. Likewise, previous studies have demonstrated elevated IFM activity during single-limb stance, but not during standard bilateral stance in healthy individuals [3]. Higher CoP values in healthy subjects, rather than indicating instability, may actually represent a response for maintaining postural control, as subjects explore the location of their body in space in an attempt to find the most stable [32]. Our finding aligns with that IFMs are clearly important in postural control and are recruited in a highly coordinated manner to stabilise the foot and maintain balance in the medio-lateral direction, particularly during single-leg stance [2]. Therefore, a change in postural control could be related to decrease in force production by the IFMs after they are fatigued.

We interpreted the increase of COP only in the medio-lateral direction after IFMs fatigue as an indication that IFMs contribute predominantly to mediolateral foot stability during single-leg stance with eyes open, highlighting their dominant role in maintaining foot posture in this direction. AbH and FDB muscles contribute to postural control, and IFMs during balance tasks may involve sensorimotor interactions which are essential for integrating sensory input relevant to postural regulation [4]. The IFMs responsible for abduction of the toes are important for the maintenance of mediolateral balance and the reduction of medio-lateral sway. Further, the intrinsic foot abductors primarily act to produce force in the medio-lateral direction [33]; whereas the ankle plantar flexors primarily act in the antero-posterior direction. The concurrent increase in medio-lateral CoP displacement and reduction in sway velocity observed in this study may reflect a compensatory modulation of postural control, wherein the system shifts toward a strategy of larger but slower corrective actions [3]. Concluded that the increased movement of the CoP during single-leg balance is predominantly driven by excitation patterns of EFMs. The observed decrease in the ellipse area following IFM fatigue during the eyes-opened condition may provide insight into the contribution of the compensatory mechanism. Such a response likely represents an adaptive mechanism aimed at maintaining stability under conditions of compromised neuromuscular function, favoring movement efficiency and control over rapid responsiveness. One possible mechanism could be that the fatiguing foot exercises might stimulate the foot sensory input in the postural control system [34]. Studies indicated that fatigued the EFMs without additional focus on the IFMs [16, 31, 35]. Although the comparability of our results is limited due to the focus on IFMs within our fatigue protocol, the findings may nonetheless offer valuable insights into the differential contributions of intrinsic and extrinsic foot muscles to postural balance. Collectively, our findings suggest that under IFMs fatigue, EFMs may play a compensatory role in stabilizing the ankle joint, especially antero-posterior direction and maintaining balance, particularly during more demanding single-limb tasks.

In healthy adults, visual deprivation can increase the center of pressure by more than one-third [36]. However, the absence of significant changes, except for trunk sway, during the eyes-closed was unexpected, suggesting that visual input may not be the primary factor in postural control under IFMs fatigue. Indeed, postural stability is largely dependent on the sensory stimulus provided by vision [37]. In our study, a significant increase was observed only in trunk sway during single-leg stance with eyes closed. This study is the first to comprehensively investigate the effect of IFMs fatigue on the static balance with visual deprivation. Based on this finding, it appears that enhanced postural control in young adults under eyes-closed conditions may be linked to increased activation of trunk core muscles in order to support necessary postural adjustments. These findings emphasize the need for further research to clarify the thresholds and functional contributions of IFM’s role in maintaining postural stability, especially in single-leg stance with eyes-closed tasks.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, balance assessment was based on a single trial both before and after the fatigue protocol. Although this approach minimized participant burden and time-related fatigue effects, it may have reduced the reliability of the measurements due to the absence of repeated trials. Second, although a standardized fatigue protocol was applied consistently across all participants, individual fatigue levels were not objectively assessed, which may have introduced variability in the fatigue response. This limitation is partly attributable to the small and localized nature of the intrinsic foot muscles, which makes it difficult to apply conventional fatigue assessment methods with reliability.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant role of IFMs in static balance regulation during single-leg stance, particularly under eyes-open conditions. Furthermore, under increased postural demands such a single-leg stance, it has been observed that a decrease in ellipse area and sway speed despite greater CoPx variability, which may reflect IFM’s role in modulating fine adjustments. Although these findings do not fully establish a causal relationship, they suggest that intrinsic foot muscle fatigue may play a role in postural control, particularly during tasks requiring high stability. Under eyes-closed conditions, the greater trunk sway seemed to suggest a compensatory postural adjustment. These findings suggest that IFMs should not merely be considered structural supporters, but active contributors to postural control. Further research is warranted to explore these effects across diverse populations and under varying balance demands.
