Long term safety and efficacy of a functionalized nanogel with endothelin-1 and bradykinin receptor antagonist peptides for treatment of osteoarthritis of the metacarpophalangeal and distal interphalangeal joints in horses: a descriptive clinical pilot study
Authors: Antoinette Terlinden, Sandrine Jacquet, Seng Manivong, Virginie Coudry, Amelie Tallaj, Aurélie Cullier, Florence Legendre, Araceli Ac Garcia, Florina Moldovan, Pierre Sirois, Xavier Banquy, Philippe Galéra, Fabrice Audigié, Gaëlle Roullin, Magali Demoor, Lélia Bertoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntra-articular drug delivery systems (DDS) are emerging as promising therapies for osteoarthritis (OA), yet their efficacy in spontaneous clinical cases remains largely untested. This uncontrolled, descriptive pilot study was designed to provide a proof of concept for the feasibility, safety, and preliminary clinical effects of intra-articular administration in sport horses with naturally occurring OA. The study involved a peptide-functionalized nanogel composed of chitosan and hyaluronic acid, delivering endothelin type A (BQ-123) and bradykinin B1 (R-954) receptor antagonists, which have previously demonstrated anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective properties in preclinical models.ResultsEight client-owned sport horses with moderate OA of the metacarpophalangeal (MCPJ) or distal interphalangeal joint (DIPJ) received a single intra-articular injection of 2.4 mL nanogel and were followed for 12 months. No major adverse events were observed. Two horses developed mild, transient joint swelling that resolved within three days. Seven of eight horses showed improvement in lameness scores by week 12, although complete resolution on hard ground circles was observed in only two horses. All horses were sound on soft ground and returned to competition, with a median time of 128 days post-treatment. Six horses remained in active competition at one year without additional intervention. Four horses (50%) met the predefined primary outcome of return to the same level and frequency of competition as before lameness onset. Horses treated for DIPJ OA and those showing radiographic joint space narrowing were overrepresented among treatment failures.ConclusionsIntra-articular administration of a peptide-functionalized nanogel was feasible and well tolerated in sport horses with naturally occurring OA and was associated with partial but prolonged clinical improvement. Although only half of the horses achieved full return to pre-lameness performance, most showed sustained clinical benefit without additional treatment over one year. These findings support further investigation of this drug delivery system in larger, randomized controlled trials to better define its therapeutic efficacy and optimal indications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05368-0.Keywords: Horse, Osteoarthritis, Pain, Inflammation, Bioactive nanomaterials, Drug delivery system, Clinical trial

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a debilitating and irreversible joint disease that affects over 528 million people worldwide [1]. This major public health issue is mainly characterized by degradation of the articular cartilage and subchondral bone, with or without synovial effusion. OA similarly affects millions of horses and is central to welfare and economic concerns in the equine industry [2]. Given the anatomical and biomechanical similarities between human and equine joints, there is growing interest in developing therapeutic strategies that benefit both species, with the horse recognized as a relevant model for human translational OA studies [3]. In recent decades, intra-articular biological therapies—aimed at modifying the pathophysiological processes of OA and categorized as Disease Modifying OA Drugs (DMOADs)—have become increasingly used in equine practice to mitigate the side effects of anti-inflammatory drugs [4]. A survey published in 2022 reported that 87.5% of veterinarians currently use biologic therapies to treat OA [5]. However, there is still no treatment in either humans or horses that have been proven to arrest or reverse the progression of the structural damage in the joint [6, 7]. This underscores the urgent need to develop effective long-term strategies that can halt disease progression, promote the restoration of joint structure and function, and alleviate clinical symptoms such as pain, stiffness, and impaired mobility.

In recent years, multiple innovative strategies have been proposed to reduce inflammation—and consequently alleviate pain and lameness—including the development of more selective inflammatory inhibitors and locally delivered pharmacotherapies [8]. Among these approaches, two G protein–coupled receptors have been identified as promising therapeutic targets in osteoarthritis (OA), as they simultaneously modulate both inflammation and pain: the endothelin-1 ETA receptor and the kinin B1 receptor (BKB1). Both receptors are overexpressed in OA joints and contribute to exacerbated inflammation and heightened pain perception [9, 10]. Notably, two peptide antagonists—BQ123 (ETA receptor antagonist) and R954 (BKB1 receptor antagonist)—which act synergistically to reduce these pathological features [10] were previously identified. When delivered via nanogels, these antagonists exhibit prolonged biological activity, offering a promising therapeutic avenue for OA management [11].

Nanogels functionalized with bioactive molecules present a promising therapeutic approach, as they combine favorable mechanical properties with targeted biological activity. Biopolymer-based hydrogels with lubrication and viscoelastic features can serve as drug delivery systems (DDSs), enabling the sustained release of biomolecules with analgesic, anti-inflammatory, anti-catabolic, or pro-anabolic properties for long-lasting OA management [12–14]. Among these, functionalized nanogels composed of a combination of chitosan (CHI) and hyaluronic acid (HA) have been shown to improve the viscoelastic properties of injured joints [15–18], and stimulate the synthesis of cartilage matrix components [17, 19, 20] which constitutes a biomimetic platform of particular interest for osteoarticular applications. In these systems, the two polysaccharides are assembled by ionic gelation, and bioactive peptides are covalently grafted to the polymeric backbone prior to nanogel formation. Specifically, the endothelin-1 receptor antagonist BQ-123 is conjugated to chitosan, while the bradykinin B1 receptor antagonist R-954 is conjugated to hyaluronic acid, resulting in peptide-grafted nanogels rather than physically encapsulated formulations [16, 21].

In vitro, these nanogels have been shown to be biocompatible with equine chondrocytes and to exert chondroprotective and anti-catabolic effects, including modulation of inflammatory pathways and preservation of cartilage matrix marker expression, such as collagen type II and aggrecan [21]. In parallel, in vitro release studies in synovial fluid and cellular uptake experiments demonstrated that covalent peptide grafting enables sustained peptide delivery with prolonged residence time and effective interaction with chondrocytes [11]. In vivo, a randomized, double-blind local tolerance study conducted on eight horses assessed the response of healthy joints to a single injection of the selected effective dose and an overdose of the functionalized nanogels over a 3 months period [22]. All injections were well tolerated at the level of the joint, based on clinical examination, imaging and synovial fluid analyses. A mild, transient inflammatory response was observed in the injected joints, slightly more pronounced in the overdose group, but without any harmful effects, supporting the feasibility of this delivery strategy for local osteoarticular applications [22].

The therapeutic efficacy of these peptide-conjugated nanogels in horses with OA has not yet been clinically evaluated. The primary aim of this pilot study is to provide proof of concept for the feasibility of intra-articular administration of a peptide-functionalized nanogel in sport horses with naturally occurring OA, and to assess their ability to return to the same level of competition as prior to the onset of lameness over a one-year follow-up period. Secondary objectives are to determine the timeframe and duration of the clinical effect and to identify indications related to the case history, clinical presentation, and imaging features. Finally, this study aims to assess the incidence of major adverse reaction of osteoarthritic joints to the intra-articular administration of the nanogels in terms pain and inflammation.

Results

Study cases

Eight warmblood horses were enrolled over an 8-month period and monitored for 12-month local tolerance and effect evaluation. The cohort consisted of three mares and five geldings, with a median age of 12 years (range: 6–16 years; IQR: 11–15). All horses were used for competitive disciplines, including show jumping (n = 7) and three-day eventing (n = 1). All horses enrolled in the study were warmblood breeds. Seven horses presented with lameness of ≤ 4 months’ duration, while one (horse 8) had a chronic lameness lasting 13 months. Five horses exhibited lameness in the left forelimb, and three in the right forelimb. The median lameness score at inclusion was 2/5 (IQR: 2–2; range: 2–3/5). Joints involved included the distal interphalangeal joint (DIPJ) in four horses (50%) and the metacarpophalangeal joint (MCPJ) in the remaining four (50%) (Table 1).

Local tolerance results

Local tolerance was assessed in all enrolled horses. No major adverse events occurred following nanogel administration, and all cases were categorized as “tolerated”. In six out of eight horses, no local changes were detected by the investigator during remote evaluations conducted via video calls on D1, D3, and D7. The remaining two horses exhibited increased joint swelling on D1, which returned to baseline by D3 after local cryotherapy (ice-water immersion, 20 min twice daily). More precisely, horse 2 (treated in the DIPJ) and 3 (treated in the MCPJ) who had a grade 2 effusion score on baseline (Additional Dataset 1), showed a more convex appearance of the recess of the joints on D1 (grade 3). None of them showed obvious lameness while walking on a straight line.

No further local tolerance concerns were reported by the attending veterinarians during the 12-month follow-up period. Comparative radiographic and ultrasonographic assessments between D0 and W6 showed no progression of OA-associated changes (Additional Dataset 2). Additionally, the referring veterinarian of horse 4 provided follow-up radiographs obtained two years post-injection, which showed stable osteoarthritic findings based on comparable projections provided (Fig. 4).

At baseline, six horses were at rest (restricted turnout or stall confinement with hand-walking), while two were engaged in light flat work at all three gaits. Between D0 and W1, all horses underwent stall rest with hand-walking, as per the study protocol. Between W1 and W2, trotting in short straight-line intervals was initiated for all horses. Blinded video assessment of locomotion showed that 4/8 horses (50%) exhibited a one-grade improvement in lameness by W2 compared to baseline (D0), none showing complete resolution, and the other 4/8 horses had no change in the lameness grade (Additional Dataset 1). One horse (horse 2) presented with a > 2-grade lameness and remained on the same exercise level (trot intervals) for an additional two weeks (Fig. 5, Additional Dataset 1). By W4, this horse showed a 2/5 lameness on video analysis, characterized by persistent lameness on hard ground circles on the same side as the affected foot. The horse was therefore allowed to gradually increase exercise on soft ground, where no lameness was observed. In contrast, the workload of horse 4 was not increased after week 2 (W2) because of mild persistent lameness (grade 2/5) observed during circling on the side opposite to the affected fetlock, both on hard and soft ground. As the horse remained sound on the straight line and on the circle corresponding to the affected limb, trotting intervals were maintained under these specific conditions.

At W12, seven of the eight horses (87.5%) showed improved lameness scores compared to D0: four by one grade and three by two grades. Two horses (25%) exhibited complete resolution of lameness (grade 0). Five horses displayed satisfactory gait quality on the straight line and on soft ground but continued to show mild and inconsistent lameness on hard ground circles (grade 1/5). One horse (horse 5) returned to its baseline lameness score (2/5) (Fig. 5, Additional Dataset 1); this horse showed satisfactory gait quality on the straight line and on soft ground but a more persistent lameness on hard ground circles on the same rein as the injected MCPJ. All horses resumed jumping training except horse 4, which continued flat work. Despite initial improvement at W6, horse 4 developed intermittent lameness by W12 while trotting on the opposite rein to the injected MCPJ on soft ground (grade 1/5). The rider therefore elected not to increase the workload. Two weeks later, worsening lameness was reported and subsequently confirmed to originate from the previously injected MCPJ by intra-articular analgesia performed by the treating veterinarian. The horse was referred for re-evaluation at W15 and was assigned a 2/5 lameness score. A second nanogel injection was administered following the same protocol. No adverse events were observed after this second injection. Lameness resolved within six weeks, and the horse returned to jumping training (Additional Dataset 1).

Long-term effect and return to competition

Among the horses included in the study —three-quarters of which had competed at the international level—all returned to competition following treatment. The median interval from treatment to the first competition was 128 days (IQR: 103.5–182), approximately 4.5 months (Table 3). Six horses remained in active competition one year after injection, participating in a median of 17.8 events (range: 3–35). Four horses achieved a return to the same competitive level as prior to the onset of lameness and were therefore classified in the “success” group. Three of these horses were treated for MCPJ OA (horses 3, 4, and 5), and one was treated for DIPJ OA (horse 1). Horse 6, also treated for MCPJ OA, showed a favourable clinical course, returning to the same highest level of competition; however, it competed in slightly fewer events compared with the six months preceding the onset of lameness (Table 3) and was therefore not classified as a success according to the predefined criteria. The three remaining horses, all treated for DIPJ OA, were classified in the “failure” group. Horse 2 returned to competition at a lower level and participated in fewer events, horse 7 was retired at W23 due to lameness in the contralateral limb, and horse 8 was retired at W22 due to recurrence of lameness in the treated DIPJ, confirmed by intra-articular analgesia. Among the six horses still competing at one year, the follow-up performed by the treating veterinarians revealed that no recurrence of the lameness was reported by the riders, and no other medical treatment had been performed between W12 and the last competition recorded. No information was available regarding the use of adjunctive physiotherapy or manual therapy.

Discussion

This preliminary study was primarily aimed to evaluate whether lame horses with moderate radiographic and ultrasonographic signs of OA, and treated with intra-articular administration of 1.2 μM BQ-123 and 0.54 μM R-954 (corresponding to 2.4 mL of functionalized nanogel) were able to return to competition at the same level and frequency as before the onset of lameness, and to maintain this performance over a prolonged period. All eight show jumpers and eventers resumed competition within a median time of 128 days [103.5–182] following treatment. However, only half of them achieved the primary endpoint, defined as a return to the same competitive level and frequency as before lameness over the last six months of the one-year follow-up period.

The results suggest a relatively delayed clinical effect of the nanogel, as six horses had not returned to competition by week 12. This finding is consistent with common training practices in sport horses: these six horses were rested prior to treatment, and riders likely adopted a cautious and progressive return-to-work strategy to restore fitness before resuming competition. To reduce variability in the time to return to competition, a standardized rest period for all horses prior to inclusion would have strengthened the study design.

At one-year post-treatment, six of the eight horses remained in competition without requiring additional intervention, albeit sometimes with a reduced workload. Given the irreversible nature of OA [1], some degree of decline in performance and workload was expected. Overall, these findings suggest a prolonged clinical effect of the functionalized nanogel, with benefits persisting beyond one year in most cases. When compared with other intra-articular biological therapies evaluated in horses [23], the nanogel demonstrates promising potential for long-lasting return to competition.

Over the past two decades, another synthetic hydrogel composed of 97.5% sterile water and 2.5% cross-linked polyacrylamide has shown encouraging long-term effects in reducing lameness in horses with OA [24]. This hydrogel has also been reported to be as effective and safe as hyaluronic acid for the treatment of human knee OA symptoms for at least one year following a single injection [25]. It integrates into the synovial membrane, forming a thickened subsynovial cushion that may improve joint elasticity and reduce pain and stiffness [26]. In a prospective study, 81% and 82.5% of 43 horses treated in the metacarpo-/tarso-phalangeal or carpal joints were non-lame at 12 and 24 months, respectively [27]. Regarding DMOADs used in veterinary practice, 84% of 50 horses treated in the MCPJ with allogeneic chondrogenically induced mesenchymal stem cells returned to their previous level of work one year after treatment, although only 47% returned at the same competitive level [28]. Other studies have reported that 46% of 28 horses treated with allogeneic neonatal mesenchymal stem cells returned to work six months after treatment [29], while fewer than 70% of horses treated with blood-derived products returned to competition [30–33].

Secondary outcomes provided further insight into the therapeutic potential and onset of action of the BQ-123/R-954 functionalized nanogel, highlighting a partial and delayed effect. By week 2, only 50% of the horses showed an improvement of at least one lameness grade, and none exhibited complete resolution of lameness. Clinical improvement appeared progressively over approximately six weeks, with 75% of horses demonstrating improved lameness scores while undergoing increased workloads. At this time point, all horses showed satisfactory gait quality on soft ground and in straight-line evaluations, allowing continued work under these conditions. However, seven horses still displayed mild lameness on hard ground circles during veterinary examination, indicating an incomplete therapeutic response. Further improvement in clinical scores was observed at week 12, although only 25% of horses were completely free of lameness, with the remaining horses exhibiting mild irregularities on hard ground circles.

It should be noted that the return to training or competition observed in some horses occurred despite the persistence of low-grade lameness detected during standardized locomotor examination. However, these lameness grades were identified only under specific and demanding examination conditions, such as circles on hard ground, while horses showed normal and comfortable gaits in straight line and on soft ground, which more closely reflect real-life training and competition conditions. Gait asymmetries or mild subclinical lameness findings are commonly detected during thorough orthopedic examinations in sport horses that are otherwise considered sound and performing satisfactorily by their riders [34–36]. Previous prospective work has demonstrated that freedom from lameness in straight line is not a reliable indicator of overall soundness, and that subtle lameness may be revealed only under specific examination conditions without necessarily implying functional impairment [36]. All decisions regarding workload progression were made cautiously, on an individual basis, and under veterinary supervision, with particular attention paid to horse comfort and welfare. In cases of insufficient clinical response, repeat injection may be useful. This was illustrated by horse 4, which showed marked improvement after receiving a second injection 15 weeks after the initial treatment and successfully returned to competition for more than one year.

Horse 8, was retired 22 weeks after treatment due to recurrence of lameness and was classified as a treatment failure. A detailed comparison between this horse and the others was conducted to identify potential inappropriate indications for nanogel treatment. Notably, horse 8 had the longest duration of lameness prior to treatment (13 months), compared with less than 4 months in the other horses. This chronicity suggests advanced degenerative joint changes likely to be performance-limiting with established joint pain insufficiently addressed by the administered dose [37]. Radiographic and ultrasonographic severity scores, as well as the initial lameness grade, were otherwise similar across all horses. However, horse 8 exhibited radiographic joint space narrowing suggestive of articular cartilage thinning, a feature commonly associated with advanced OA and poorer prognosis [38]. Importantly, this finding was observed exclusively in the DIPJs, and the three horses presenting joint space narrowing on DIPJ radiographs (horses 2, 7, and 8) were those with the poorest outcomes, all classified as treatment failures. This observation is consistent with reports indicating a poorer prognosis when cartilage thinning is suspected [38]. These results underline potential differences in therapeutic response between joints such as the DIPJ and MCPJ. It emphasizes the need for further studies to better define target joints and lesion types most likely to benefit from this treatment. For horse 8, a second injection could have been informative, but further participation was declined by the owners, who elected to retire the mare for breeding.

This study also provided initial information on the local tolerance of the nanogel when injected into osteoarthritic joints. Prior in vivo tolerance data were limited to a three-month follow-up in healthy joints of resting horses [22]. No adverse events were reported following either the initial or second injection (in horse 4), based on clinical and imaging evaluations conducted up to six weeks and via veterinarian follow-up calls over one year. Two of the eight horses developed transient joint swelling resolving by day 3. The severity of this local reaction was similar to findings in the previous tolerance study on healthy joints, where all injected joints exhibited mild to moderate transient swelling [22]. However, subtle clinical signs may have been underestimated due to reliance on video consultations. Future safety assessments should therefore include more comprehensive evaluations, such as synovial fluid analysis, objective local parameters (joint circumference, flexion sensitivity), and long-term pharmacovigilance to detect potential systemic adverse effects.

Regarding OA-associated imaging findings, preliminary data did not indicate worsening of OA-associated features at week 6, nor on radiographs obtained two years post-injection in horse 4. These observations remain limited by the short follow-up period and the low sensitivity of radiography for early joint disease detection [39, 40]. Nonetheless, imaging follow-up was useful to exclude catastrophic OA progression. In experimental equine models of induced OA, changes in radiographic scores have been detected over 10 to 12 weeks, including osteophyte development identifiable by radiography and ultrasonography [41, 42].

To better characterize OA lesions at inclusion and monitor their progression, and to investigate potential beneficial or deleterious effects of the BQ-123/R-954 nanogel on cartilage integrity, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone density, advanced three-dimensional imaging techniques such as MRI or CT arthrography would be valuable [40]. CT arthrography has demonstrated superior sensitivity for detecting articular cartilage defects compared with MRI arthrography, MRI, or CT in experimental equine models [43]. Both MRI and CT can now be performed in standing horses without general anesthesia [44], although their use in clinical trials would require referral to specialized facilities. Ultrasonography, which is more sensitive than radiography and in some contexts even more sensitive than MRI for detecting osteophytes [38, 45], remains particularly attractive for field-based and multicentric studies. Furthermore, systematic use of advanced imaging would facilitate exclusion of horses with concomitant periarticular pathology, as diffusion of anesthetic solutions beyond the joint space may occur even after intra-articular analgesia [46].

Tolerance data from the present study are limited to the detection of major adverse effects such as marked lameness or reactive arthritis. These findings must be supported by larger-scale pharmacovigilance studies with follow-up of at least one year, especially in light of concerns regarding rapidly progressive OA reported in a small number of human patients treated with novel intra-articular therapies [47]. The nanogel tested in the present study may progressively release type A endothelin receptor antagonists (BQ-123) and type B1 bradykinin receptor antagonists (R-954) within the joint. Beyond their nociceptive effects, these peptide antagonists have demonstrated the ability to limit cartilage matrix degradation [10, 21]. Moreover, the BQ123/R954 functionalized nanogels made of chitosan (CHI) and hyaluronic acid (HA) components possess chondroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and pro-anabolic properties [17, 19, 20], making promotion of rapidly progressive OA unlikely. Joint homeostasis results from interactions between all components of the joint, including articular cartilage, subchondral bone, joint capsule, synovium, and synovial fluid [48]. Nanogel functionalized with BQ123/R954 may act on several of these components by combining biomaterial support, analgesic effects, and modulation of cartilage catabolism–anabolism balance [11].

To our knowledge, this is the first clinical evaluation of a drug delivery system (DDS) in spontaneous cases of equine OA. Intra-articular DDS platforms offer vast potential due to their versatility in carrier and biomolecule combinations tailored to specific therapeutic targets. A key future challenge will be the ability to measure synovial concentrations of endothelin-1 and bradykinin receptor antagonist peptides at multiple time points following injection to better characterize DDS pharmacokinetics. Given the partial efficacy observed, combination strategies with other therapeutic agents may also be explored. Several preclinical approaches, including exosome-encapsulated hydrogels, have shown promise in OA management [15, 49–52]. Significant advances are expected in this field in the coming years, with optimization of the choice of components to provide remanent DMOADs, able to reduce pain and improve physical function, by halting the biological, structural and mechanical deterioration of the joint [53].

Beyond limitations related to safety assessment, this proof-of-concept study also has important limitations regarding therapeutic efficacy evaluation. The absence of blinding and a control group precludes control of confounding factors such as gradual return to work or positive rider expectations. A prospective before–after design was implemented to mitigate some bias: horses had persistent OA symptoms for at least four weeks without improvement prior to inclusion. Primary outcomes were assessed objectively using official databases. Blinded evaluation of secondary outcomes, such as lameness grade, was enabled through video recordings, with assessments performed by the same experienced clinicians to minimize inter-observer variability.

Improvement in lameness was not selected as the primary outcome because the widely used AAEP 5-point grading system lacks sensitivity to detect subtle clinical changes, particularly in mildly lame horses [54, 55]. This system reflects the conditions under which lameness is observed rather than its true intensity, especially for grades 1 to 3. Given that most horses were at rest at baseline and that the nanogel was expected to have a medium-term effect, limited early changes in lameness grade were anticipated. Although alternative grading systems exist, none are universally accepted [54, 55]. Objective quantitative gait analysis systems offer a promising alternative by reducing subjectivity and improving detection of mild lameness [56, 57]. Among these, inertial measurement units appear to be the most practical for field use [57]. However, such systems also present challenges in multicentric studies, including technical constraints, risk of missing data, and the need for strict standardization of evaluation conditions, as gait asymmetry is influenced by speed and circle radius [58].

The use of return to competition as a primary outcome also has inherent limitations, as it can be influenced by numerous factors unrelated to joint health, including training strategies, environmental management, physiotherapy, rider decisions, and contralateral limb status. During follow-up, riders did not perceive lameness recurrence, but subtle lameness may have been overlooked, as riders are known to be poor at detecting lameness [59], and mild to moderate lameness is commonly identified during veterinary examinations of sport horses perceived as sound by their riders [35, 36]. In the present study, riders had detected lameness prior to treatment and discontinued competition, suggesting reasonable awareness of performance-limiting lameness. Moreover, six of the eight horses returned to international-level competition, where mandatory veterinary inspections and scrutiny by trainers and judges increase the likelihood of lameness detection [35].

Finally, the results of this case series should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and absence of a control group, which preclude statistical analysis. This first proof-of-concept study nevertheless demonstrates the feasibility of intra-articular nanogel injection in horses with spontaneous OA and provides encouraging preliminary safety data. These findings support further investigation in larger populations to better define the benefit–risk balance. While horses that resumed normal work or competition showed comfortable and symmetric gaits in straight line and on soft ground, mild lameness persisted for some horses under specific examination conditions (hard ground circles). Optimal therapeutic efficacy would require elimination of lameness under all examination conditions, suggesting that further optimization of the DDS composition may be needed. To establish clinical efficacy, prospective randomized controlled trials conducted under good laboratory practice conditions will be required. The present study provides preliminary data to inform such trials, including refinement of inclusion criteria, selection of primary outcomes and endpoints, and estimation of sample size required to achieve adequate statistical power.

Conclusion

This pilot study provides proof of feasibility that intra-articular injection of nanogels composed of chitosan functionalized with a type A endothelin receptor antagonist (BQ-123-CHI) and hyaluronic acid functionalized with a type B1 bradykinin receptor antagonist (R-954-HA) may be a therapeutic option for sport horses suffering from moderate OA, with a return to work observed for the 8 horses included, 4 of them reaching previous levels of performance, and without occurrence of any major adverse events. This is the first proof of concept study to evaluate an intra-articular DDS in clinical cases of equine OA and demonstrates the potential for future developments potentially applicable to other species, including humans.

Methods

Study population

This pilot study involved client-owned sport horses (show jumpers, eventers, or dressage horses) with a history of regular competition prior to the onset of lameness. This population was chosen to enable objective monitoring of performance levels using official equestrian federation websites. Eligible horses were in good general health, free from infectious disease, and referred by their attending veterinarian to the Center of Imaging and Research on Equine Locomotor Pathology (CIRALE), National Veterinary School of Alfort. Veterinarians were informed about the clinical trial and its protocol by email. The study protocol was approved by the ComERC/ENVA Ethical Committee (Permit No. 2022–01–11–1). Written informed consent was obtained from all owners prior to enrolment.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be referred for the study, horses had to present a unilateral forelimb lameness lasting at least 4 weeks, localized by intra-articular analgesia using 100 mg of mepivacaine hydrochloride (labelled as Mepidor) to either the DIPJ or MCPJ, and associated with radiographic evidence of OA in the DIPJ or MCPJ. On the day of examination at CIRALE (D0), horses were included if they exhibited persistent grade 2 to 3 lameness according to the American Association of Equine Practitioners (AAEP) 1–5 grading scale [60], and if radiographic and ultrasonographic evaluation confirmed OA-associated findings (e.g., periarticular remodelling, synovitis, cartilage thinning or defects, subchondral bone sclerosis or irregularities).

Horses were not included or excluded from the study if they had undergone surgery of the affected joint; had received bisphosphonate treatment within the 3 months preceding or the 12 months following day 0 (D0); or had been treated with intra-articular injections, or systemic anti-inflammatory treatment within the 3 weeks previous and the 12 months following D0. Horses were also excluded if their lameness was not fully abolished by diagnostic anesthesia; or if they had other concurrent conditions (e.g., fractures, bone fragments, subchondral cysts, tendinopathies, desmopathies, or a history of septic arthritis). To rule out other conditions that could have been desensitized by diffusion of the intra-articular anesthesia, a complete ultrasonographic examination of the fetlock or foot region was performed in accordance with standard techniques [61–65].

Imaging evaluation

For MCPJs, radiographs included latero-medial, dorso-palmar, dorsolateral 45° palmaromedial oblique, and dorsomedial 45° palmarolateral oblique views. For DIPJs, latero-medial, dorsoproximal 60° palmarodistal oblique, and dorsoproximal 5° palmarodistal oblique views were obtained. When cartilage thinning was suspected, comparative imaging of the contralateral joint was also performed.

Ultrasound examinations were performed by the same equine locomotion veterinary specialist (LB) using a 7.5 MHz linear transducer and a standardized protocol including transverse and longitudinal scans of the dorsal and collateral aspects of the joints [61, 66].

Radiographic and ultrasonographic images were retrospectively assessed by two equine locomotion specialists and board-certified specialists in equine sports medicine (SJ and VC), blinded to horse identity and examination dates. Scoring was based on adapted previously published systems [38, 40, 67], as detailed in Table 4. Discrepant scores were reviewed jointly, and consensus was reached in all cases.

Imaging scoring criteria

A total imaging score was calculated by summing radiographic and ultrasonographic scores. Horses were categorized into three OA severity groups: mild (score 1–5), moderate (score 6–10), or marked (score 11–15).

Study design

This study was designed as a prospective, open-label, uncontrolled before–after trial involving horses diagnosed with OA and exhibiting persistent lameness for more than four weeks. An overview of the study design is provided in Fig. 7.

Timeline of the study design

On the day of enrolment (D0), each horse underwent a comprehensive clinical assessment by the same experienced veterinarian (LB), including physical and lameness examinations to confirm the presence of lameness. Radiographic and ultrasonographic evaluations of the affected joint were performed to document OA-associated changes. Lameness evaluations were video-recorded, and imaging data were archived for subsequent timeline blinded scoring. Following confirmation of inclusion criteria, the target joint was injected with the nanogel on D0. Horses were stall-rested for one-week post-injection and walked in hand for 20 min daily. Video calls were scheduled on days 1, 3, and 7 post-injections between one investigator (ATe) and the horse caretaker to monitor for major adverse events, such as joint flare or marked lameness, that might require veterinary intervention (lameness obvious at the walk or minimal weight-bearing consistent with a grade 4 or 5 lameness). Lameness was evaluated on D1, D3 and D7 only by walking the horse on a straight line.

From Day 7 to Week 2 (W2), horses underwent a 20-min ridden walk followed by short trotting intervals on soft ground, in straight lines or on wide curves. At W2, lameness was video recorded by the treating veterinarian and reviewed by investigators (LB, ATe). If the lameness score was ≤ 2/5 and the horse had no lameness on soft ground, the horse resumed a gradual return to full workload by W6. If the score remained > 2/5, workload was not increased, and a follow-up evaluation was scheduled at W4. At W6, a full clinical examination was conducted on all horses at the investigational site, including physical, radiographic, ultrasonographic, and video-recorded lameness evaluations. An additional lameness video evaluation was conducted at W12 by the referring veterinarian for all horses. In cases of persistent lameness at W12, owners were offered a second nanogel injection, contingent on compliance with the same follow-up protocol.

Sport performance data before and after treatment were retrieved from national and international equestrian databases. At 12 months post-injection, referring veterinarians were contacted to report any recurrence of lameness and whether additional treatments were administered. If further treatment occurred, only competitions held prior to its administration were included in the analysis.

Nanogels preparation and injection

Functionalized nanogels were prepared according to previously published protocols [16, 21, 22]. The formulation consisted of two components: BQ-123-chitosan (BQ123-CS), composed of chitosan (CS) conjugated to BQ-123 (ChinaPeptides Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and R-954-hyaluronic acid (R954-HA), composed of hyaluronic acid conjugated to R-954 (gift from Prof. Sirois, Québec, Canada). Nanogels were prepared under aseptic conditions, purified, lyophilized with a cryoprotectant, and stored at –20°C. One hour before injection, the powder was brought to room temperature and reconstituted in sterile 5% glucose solution (Laboratories Chaix et du Marais, La Chaussée Saint-Victor, France), following the protocol used in a previous safety study [22] to obtain a 2.4 mL final volume corresponding to 1.2 μM BQ-123 and 0.54 μM R-954.

On D0, each horse received a single intra-articular injection of 2.4 mL functionalized nanogel into the affected joint under aseptic conditions, following sedation with intravenous detomidine (0.01 mg/kg) and butorphanol (0.01 mg/kg). A light bandage was applied over the injection site and removed by the owner approximately 12 h later.

Local tolerance and efficacy outcomes

Local tolerance outcomes

Based on findings from a prior tolerance study on healthy joints [22], joint reaction was monitored during the first post-injection week using two clinical markers: joint effusion (inflammation) and lameness (pain). On days 1, 3, and 7, a veterinarian (ATe) evaluated the injected joint for major adverse events via video call with the horse caretaker. Major adverse events were defined as marked joint swelling or lameness > 3/5 (i.e. lameness obvious on the walk, or minimal weight-bearing). Horses were then classified as either (1) “tolerated” (no major adverse event) or (2) “not tolerated” (presence of a major adverse event). If such an event occurred, local treatment (e.g., cryotherapy) and/or short-term non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were permitted. Horses were not excluded from the study unless treatment lasted more than two days.

Radiographic and ultrasonographic imaging was repeated at W6 to assess for progression of OA-related changes.

Efficacy outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the horse’s return to its previous level of sport activity. Performance data were extracted from national and international databases (“Fédération Française d’Équitation” and “Fédération Equestre Internationale”) over a 12-month follow-up. Horses were categorized as follows:

“Success”: return to the pre-lameness level of competition—corresponding to the same event category or a maximum bar height within 10 cm of the pre-lameness level—together with a competition frequency between 6 and 12 months after treatment that was greater than or equal to the number of events performed during the 6 months preceding the onset of lameness. This timeframe was selected to account for the duration of the post-treatment rehabilitation program.

“Success”: return to the pre-lameness level of competition—corresponding to the same event category or a maximum bar height within 10 cm of the pre-lameness level—together with a competition frequency between 6 and 12 months after treatment that was greater than or equal to the number of events performed during the 6 months preceding the onset of lameness. This timeframe was selected to account for the duration of the post-treatment rehabilitation program.

“Failure”: no return to competition, reduced level of performance with respect to the previous success criteria.

The secondary efficacy outcome was the evolution of lameness in the affected limb. Complete lameness examinations including walking and trotting on a straight line, and trotting on circles on hard and soft grounds, were performed and videorecorded on D0, W2, W6, and W12. A final review of the videorecords prepared by ATe in a random order, with a mixture of dates, was performed after completion of the study, by 3 experienced investigators (ATa, VC, SJ) who were blinded of the dates and horse numbers. A subjective lameness grade according to the 1–5 lameness grading system of the American Association of Equine Practitioners [60] was independently established by each observer. A final grade was then fixed by consensus agreement, during which horses with different grades assigned by the observers were reviewed and discussed.
