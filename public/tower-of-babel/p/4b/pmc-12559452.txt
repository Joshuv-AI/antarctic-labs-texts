Oxygen‐Tolerant, Red Light‐Driven Controlled Synthesis of Easily Degradable and High Molecular Weight α‐Lipoic Acid‐Vinyl Copolymers
Authors: Ivan O Levkovsky, Lucca Trachsel, Hironobu Murata, Krzysztof Matyjaszewski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstractα‐Lipoic acid (LA) has recently emerged as an attractive, inexpensive monomer for synthesizing degradable polymers via ring‐opening of its 1,2‐dithiolane, introducing easily cleavable disulfide linkages into polymer backbones. Reversible addition–fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) copolymerization with vinyl monomers enables access to degradable poly(disulfide)s with controlled molecular weights. However, conventional thermal RAFT methods suffer from oxygen sensitivity, limited LA incorporation (<40 mol%), and modest degrees of polymerization (DP < 300). Here, we report an oxygen‐tolerant, red‐light‐driven RAFT approach using methylene blue (MB⁺) as a photosensitizer, and triethanolamine (TEOA) as a sacrificial electron donor. This photoRAFT strategy affords well‐defined LA–vinyl copolymers with DPs exceeding 6000, relatively low dispersities (Đ = 1.1–1.6), and LA incorporations up to 68 mol%. The method is compatible with a broad range of functional comonomers, including hydrophilic, charged, and zwitterionic acrylates and acrylamides, yielding water‐soluble degradable polymers. The resulting copolymers are readily degradable by disulfide‐reducing agents, UV light, and ambient sunlight. Overall, this mild and efficient platform overcomes the limitations of thermal RAFT, providing improved access to functional, high‐molecular‐weight degradable LA copolymers, suggesting potential applications as biocompatible plastics and biomedical materials.Keywords: Degradable polymers, Lipoic acid, Methylene blue, RAFT polymerization, Photochemistry, Reversible deactivation radical polymerization

Introduction

Synthetic polymers with degradable repeating units in their backbones hold significant promise for reducing long‐term environmental impact and enabling biological clearance. Such materials are increasingly sought after for applications including biodegradable plastics,[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] nontoxic packaging,[ 4 , 5 ] and biomedical devices such as tissue scaffolds and targeted drug delivery systems.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] While many stimuli‐cleavable functional groups have been employed as degradable backbone units, a class of degradable polymers that is particularly attractive are poly(disulfide)s, which have repeating disulfide bonds along the polymer backbone.[ 10 ] Disulfide bonds are readily cleavable and can be rapidly converted to thiols through several mechanisms, including thermal or photolytic cleavage,[ 11 , 12 ] hydrolysis under basic conditions,[ 13 ] or reduction by thiol‐ or phosphine‐based agents.[ 14 ] Their facile degradability, low toxicity, and biocompatibility make poly(disulfide)s attractive not only for dynamic and responsive biomedical applications but also for developing next‐generation sustainable polymeric materials. Poly(disulfide)s can be synthesized via several routes, including the polycondensation of dithiols or the ring‐opening polymerization (ROP) of 5‐ or 6‐membered cyclic disulfides.[ 15 , 16 , 17 ] A commonly used monomer is α‐lipoic acid (LA), a naturally occurring and commercially available compound that contains a 5‐membered cyclic disulfide (1,2‐dithiolane) capable of undergoing ROP.[ 18 ] Various 1,2‐dithiolane ROP mechanisms are known, including thermally initiated,[ 19 ] cation‐mediated,[ 20 ] thiolate anion‐initiated,[ 21 ] and radical ROP,[ 22 ] and all have been utilized to obtain poly(disulfide)s from LA. However, controlling these polymerizations is challenging due to chain transfer reactions involving propagating species and disulfide bonds in the growing polymer chains.[ 23 ] Nevertheless, recent advancements have enabled the synthesis of LA‐based poly(disulfide)s with defined structures and predictable molecular weights. For example, Du et al. demonstrated that anion‐binding thiourea additives can be used in thiolate anion‐initiated ROP of lipoate esters to suppress the nucleophilicity of the propagating thiolate, thereby reducing chain transfer reactions and improving regioselectivity of the ROP. This enabled the synthesis of poly(disulfide)s with low dispersities (Đ < 1.2) and high molar masses (>100 000 g mol−1) (Figure 1a).[ 23 ] However, due to the use of strong base catalysts, prior synthetic protection of the carboxylic acid group of LA is required. The method is further limited by the requirement for specialty thiourea additives, which are either expensive, synthetically complex, or not commercially available.

Comparison of selected strategies toward polymerization of α‐lipoic acid (LA). a) Du et al. developed a thiolate anion‐initiated polymerization of lipoate esters, enabling controlled synthesis of highly degradable 1,2‐dithiolane homopolymers, but with monomer scope limited due to the strongly basic conditions required, and the need for a specialty catalyst.[ 23 ] b) Albanese et al. reported a conventional thermal RAFT approach toward obtaining copolymers of LA and acrylates/acrylamides, however, with limited molar mass (< 60 000 g mol−1, DP < 300), LA incorporation (< 40%), and monomer scope.[ 24 ] c) This work: red‐light driven methylene blue (MB+)/TEOA‐mediated RAFT polymerization platform that is fully oxygen‐tolerant, compatible with a broad range of acrylate and acrylamide monomers, and capable of producing polymers with high molecular mass (>600 000 g mol−1, DP > 6000) and high LA incorporation resulting in enhanced degradability.

Albanese et al. developed a synthetic route toward degradable LA‐vinyl copolymers with control over molecular weight and Đ via thermal reversible addition–fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization (Figure 2b).[ 24 ] Acrylates were chosen as comonomers due to their ability to cross‐propagate with the 1,2‐dithiolane of LA, in contrast to other vinyl monomers such as methacrylates or styrenics.[ 24 , 25 ] In these copolymerizations, unmodified LA was introduced at feed ratios of 5–40 mol% with respect to the acrylate. Because of the faster incorporation rate of LA compared to the acrylate, disulfide bond formation was promoted, resulting in disulfide‐containing dyads within the backbone. The resulting copolymers could be cleaved using the mild thiol‐reducing agent 2‐tris(2‐carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), leading to efficient degradation of the polymers. Although this approach is versatile and robust, it presents several limitations and opportunities for improvement. First, the conventional thermal RAFT polymerization requires rigorous deoxygenation, as oxygen rapidly quenches radical intermediates,[ 26 ] complicating the procedure. In addition, polymers with controlled Đ were only reported for limited targeted degrees of polymerization (DP T < 370). High LA feed ratios (>40 mol%) also led to limited monomer conversions restricting the maximum achievable disulfide content, and thus the degradability of the resulting copolymer.

Finally, while the successful copolymerization of LA with N,N‐dimethylacrylamide (DMA) was briefly mentioned by Albanese et al., and the thermal RAFT copolymerization of LA with several functional acrylamides was reported by Giri et al. for the construction of antimicrobial polymers,[ 27 ] the reactivity of LA with acrylamides and detailed characterization of the resulting copolymers remains somewhat underexplored.

Inspired by this work and seeking improved synthetic approaches toward degradable LA‐vinyl copolymers, we explored alternative polymerization techniques. Among reversible‐deactivation radical polymerization (RDRP) methodologies,[ 28 , 29 ] photoinduced techniques,[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] such as photoinduced atom transfer radical polymerization (photoATRP),[ 33 , 34 ] photocontrolled RAFT,[ 35 , 36 ] and photoiniferter polymerization,[ 37 ] are particularly attractive due to their ability to provide enhanced control over polymerization at ambient temperature across a broad range of vinyl monomers. Light‐driven techniques further offer spatiotemporal control and facilitate high‐throughput synthesis,[ 38 , 39 ] 3D printing,[ 40 ] and bioconjugation.[ 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 ]

We envisioned using a photoRAFT system driven by visible light, employing the cationic dye methylene blue (MB+), which has been reported as an effective photosensitizer in conventional free radical polymerization when paired with an electron donor such as triethanolamine (TEOA).[ 45 , 46 ] Recently, we reported the MB+‐driven photoRAFT homopolymerization of vinyl monomers,[ 47 ] which proceeds via a photoinitiated mechanism, wherein α‐aminoalkyl radicals formed from TEOA during reductive quenching of the MB+ triplet excited state (3MB+*), initiate polymerization. The polymerization mechanism is distinct from photoinduced electron/energy transfer (PET)‐RAFT, which does not occur in this instance, as the 3MB+* cannot reduce the trithiocarbonate (TTC) compounds used in the system as chain‐transfer agents (CTAs).[ 47 , 48 ] The MB+/TEOA system is fully oxygen‐tolerant, avoids expensive or metal‐based photocatalysts, and operates efficiently under a broad range of wavelengths, including near‐infrared (NIR) light.[ 47 ] These features enhance its compatibility with the radical ROP of LA, with thiyl radicals potentially leading to undesired side reactions under conditions involving metal catalysts,[ 49 ] and shorter wavelength irradiation leading to photolysis of the strained disulfide in 1,2‐dithiolanes.[ 50 , 51 ] Remarkably, this light‐mediated RAFT approach provided excellent control over molecular weight and Đ under ambient conditions without prior deoxygenation, and outperformed conventional thermal RAFT when targeting high degrees of polymerization (DP T = 10 000) or high LA feed ratios (50 mol%) (Figure 1c).

Here, we report a red‐light‐driven, MB+/TEOA‐mediated RAFT copolymerization of LA with acrylates and acrylamides, enabling the synthesis of degradable poly(disulfide)s under mild, fully oxygen‐tolerant conditions. Compared to previously reported thermal RAFT polymerizations, our approach provides access to LA–vinyl copolymers with high molar masses exceeding 600 000 g mol−1 and greater LA incorporations up to 68 mol%, while maintaining low to moderate dispersities. Additionally, our approach significantly expands the vinyl comonomer scope to include acrylates and acrylamides bearing polar, charged, and zwitterionic functionalities, yielding water‐soluble, degradable polymers. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the increased LA incorporation achieved with our light‐driven approach leads to copolymers with enhanced degradability upon treatment with disulfide reducing agents such as tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), dithiothreitol (DTT), and glutathione (GSH), as well as exposure to UV light from either an artificial source or ambient sunlight. This versatile and robust approach offers an efficient strategy toward degradable, disulfide‐containing polymers with potential applications as functional recyclable adhesives,[ 52 ] biomedical materials,[ 53 ] or biodegradable plastics.[ 54 ]

Results and Discussion

Optimization of Polymerization Conditions

We began our investigation into the MB+/TEOA photosensitized, light‐driven RAFT copolymerization of LA and methyl acrylate (MA) by screening a range of solvents commonly used for RAFT polymerization. Reactions were conducted in capped glass vials (headspace = 1.24 mL) without deoxygenation, using 2‐(dodecylthiocarbonothioylthio)‐2‐methylpropanoic acid (DDMAT) as the chain transfer agent (CTA), at a fixed ratio of [MA]/[LA]/[DDMAT] = 150/50/1 and total monomer concentration [M] = 3.0 M, under red‐light irradiation (λ max = 640 nm, 25 mW cm−2), using [MB⁺] = 450 µM (0.03 equiv. relative to CTA) and [TEOA] = 60 mM (4 equiv.).

Although MB+/TEOA systems are known to proceed favorably in aqueous conditions,[ 46 , 55 ] the hydrophobicity of both MA and LA necessitated the exploration of alternative solvents. Given its widespread success in photoRDRP methodologies,[ 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 ] including PET‐RAFT,[ 62 , 63 ] dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was our initial choice due to its high polarity, favorable solvent properties, and its ability to quench singlet oxygen.[ 64 ] However, under red‐light irradiation, no polymerization was observed in pure DMSO even after 10 h (entry 1, Table 1). Despite excellent MB+ solubility, polar protic solvents such as methanol and ethanol failed to produce any monomer conversion (entries 2–3, Table 1). Other commonly used polar aprotic solvents for RAFT, namely tetrahydrofuran (THF) and 1,4‐dioxane, also failed to support polymerization, despite sufficient MB+ solubility in the polymerization medium (entries 4–5, Table 1).

We next turned to ethylene glycol (EG), a polar, hydrogen‐bond‐donating solvent in which MB+ was previously reported to exist in an unassociated state required for its role as a photosensitizer in MB+/TEOA‐mediated conventional free radical polymerizations.[ 65 ] To explore whether ground‐state optical properties might help differentiate between associated and unassociated MB+, we measured UV–vis spectra of MB+ in aqueous buffer (pH = 8, 100 mM), EG, and DMSO (Figure S4). Although slight shifts in absorbance maxima (λ max) and molar absorptivities between water, EG, and DMSO were observed, no obvious change in optical properties was apparent that would indicate formation of associated MB+ in DMSO versus EG or water. Next, we examined MB+ absorbance recovery in aqueous buffer, EG, and DMSO in the presence of TEOA, after irradiation with 640 nm light (Figure S5). In all solvents, MB+ absorbance significantly decreased immediately after irradiation, corresponding to photoreduction to colorless and photoinactive leucomethylene blue (LMB).[ 47 , 66 ] In aqueous buffer and EG, partial recovery of MB+ absorbance was observed over time in the dark, however in DMSO this did not occur. These results suggest that while MB+ absorbance recovery may explain polymerization success, a robust mechanistic explanation is necessary to explain the differing polymerization behavior between solvents, which will be explored in future work.

To improve the solubility of hydrophobic MA and LA in EG, 10 vol% of DMSO was added. Under these conditions, polymerization reached 63% conversion for LA and 77% for MA after 5 h, as determined by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy (entry 6, Table 1). The higher conversion of LA relative to MA is consistent with previous reports on conventional thermal RAFT polymerizations,[ 24 , 27 , 67 ] supporting the preferential incorporation of LA and the formation of degradable disulfide bonds along the polymer backbone. As the reaction approached high conversion, precipitation of the growing polymer occurred, significantly slowing the polymerization rate, likely due to decreased solubility at higher polymer concentrations and molar masses. To mitigate this, the DMSO content was increased to 30 vol%, which prevented precipitation and enabled higher conversions: 90% for LA and 73% for MA after 6 h (entry 7, Table 1). The resulting P(LA‐co‐MA) copolymer exhibited low dispersity (Đ = 1.19), and the apparent number‐average molar mass determined by size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) relative to poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) standards (M n,app = 17 000 g mol−1), closely matching the theoretical number‐average molar mass calculated from 1H NMR spectroscopy (M n,theory = 19 200 g mol−1).

Next, we investigated whether other, less polar diol‐based solvents could support the copolymerization. Propylene glycol (PG) was first selected and exhibited the same MB+ absorbance recovery as EG (Figure S5). In PG with 10 vol% DMSO, the polymerization proceeded smoothly, with the reaction mixture remaining homogenous throughout and reaching high conversions of both LA (87%) and MA (70%) after only 3 h (entry 8, Table 1). Similarly, in 1,4‐butanediol (i.e., 1,4‐butylene glycol, BG) with 10 vol% DMSO, polymerization achieved 74% and 63% conversions for LA and MA, respectively (entry 9, Table 1). However, as observed with EG, polymer precipitation eventually occurred, which coincided with a slowing of the polymerization. Critically, the favorable outcomes observed in diol‐based solvents, particularly in EG, PG, and BG, suggest that efficient polymerization requires not only highly polar, hydrogen‐bond‐donating environments, but also the presence of diol functionalities. In contrast, no polymerization was observed in simple alcohols such as methanol and ethanol, despite their polarity and protic nature. PG with 10 vol% DMSO was chosen as the working solvent system for subsequent experiments due to favorable polymerization kinetics, good polymer solubility, low toxicity, low cost, and a good biocompatibility profile.[ 68 , 69 ]

Control experiments confirmed the essential roles of both TEOA and MB+ in the polymerization: omitting either component resulted in no monomer conversion after prolonged irradiation (entries 10 and 11, Table 1). In addition, decreasing [TEOA] to 20 mM resulted in no observable conversion (entry 12, Table 1), despite this concentration range being effective for the homopolymerization of vinyl monomers without LA.[ 45 ] This suggests sufficiently high TEOA concentrations are required in the presence of LA, as its efficacy as an electron donor is likely diminished upon protonation by the carboxylic acid groups. In the absence of DDMAT, conventional free radical polymerization was observed, resulting in a polymer with relatively high molar mass (M n, app = 98 200 g mol−1) and broad dispersity (Ð = 2.31) (entry 13, Table 1), confirming the necessity of the CTA for controlled polymerization.

The polymerization system also enabled efficient and controlled copolymerization of LA with N,N‐dimethylacrylamide (DMA). Reactions were conducted in capped glass vials (headspace = 1.53 mL) without deoxygenation. Targeting a DP of 200 with 25 mol% LA feed at [M] = 3.0 M, [MB+] = 450 µM (0.03 equiv. w/respect to CTA), and [TEOA] = 60 mM (4 equiv.), the polymerization proceeded smoothly in PG with 10 vol% DMSO, reaching 83% and 80% conversion for LA and DMA, respectively, within 3 h (entry 14, Table 1). To facilitate SEC analysis and avoid polymer‐column interactions, the pendent carboxylic acid groups of P(LA‐co‐DMA) were methylated with trimethylsilyldiazomethane (TMS‐diazomethane). The methylated copolymer, P(LAMe‐co‐DMA), exhibited low dispersity (Đ = 1.23), and the measured molar mass (M n,app = 20 400 g mol−1) was in close agreement with the theoretical value (M n,theory = 21 400 g mol−1). Interestingly, the difference in conversions between LA and DMA was smaller than in the copolymerization of LA and MA, indicating different relative reactivity ratios of LA with acrylamides compared to acrylates.

Additionally, we investigated the influence of oxygen on the MB+ RAFT system. A copolymerization of LA with DMA conducted in a vial open to air resulted in comparable conversions for both monomers (entry 15, Table 1) to polymerizations carried out in capped vials. Moreover, control over the polymerization was retained, with the methylated P(LAMe‐co‐DMA) exhibiting low dispersity (Đ = 1.24) and a molar mass (M n,app = 18 900 g mol−1) that agreed well with the theoretical value (M n,app = 20 600 g mol−1). Intriguingly, a deoxygenated copolymerization of LA with DMA proceeded much more slowly than those run in aerobic conditions, with monomer conversions only reaching 18% for LA and 14% for DMA after 2 h of irradiation (entry 16, Table 2). After 10 h, the polymerization reached comparable conversions and similar M n,app and Đ values to nondeoxygenated experiments (entry 17, Table 1). This result indicated that the presence of oxygen only influences polymerization kinetics rather than control or monomer conversions. The more rapid propagation in aerobic conditions can be attributed to faster regeneration of the photoactive MB+ catalyst from reduced, colorless LMB by oxygen,[ 66 ] which is consistent with our previously reported work on the homopolymerization of vinyl monomers through MB+‐mediated RAFT.[ 47 ]

Finally, we explored the effect of varying light intensity on the copolymerization of LA with DMA, seeking to use higher light intensities for improved monomer conversions. We conducted the copolymerization of LA with DMA at 53 and 95 mW/cm2. After 2 h, both polymerizations reached comparable conversions to the standard 25 mW/cm2 used in our experiments; however, they exhibited higher dispersities (entries 2–3, Table S1). Although higher light intensity can enhance the polymerization rate, it also entails greater radical generation, leading to a slight loss of control. Ultimately, conversion is likely limited by the reversible polymerization equilibrium of the LA rather than the light intensity used.

Kinetic Studies

Using optimized conditions, ([MA]/[LA]/[DDMAT]/[TEOA]/[MB+] = 150/50/1/4/0.03), polymerization kinetics were investigated (Figure 2). SEC analysis revealed clean shifts of traces to lower elution times, indicating uniform chain growth throughout the polymerization (Figure 2b). Linear pseudo‐first‐order kinetic plots for both monomers indicated constant radical generation during irradiation, with LA incorporating more rapidly than MA (Figure 2c). The measured M n,app values increased linearly with conversion and closely matched the theoretical M n,theory (Figure 2d).

Similarly, the kinetic behavior of LA with DMA was investigated under analogous conditions ([DMA]/[LA]/[DDMAT]/[TEOA]/[MB+] = 150/50/1/4/0.03). SEC analysis of methylated samples at each time point confirmed uniform growth, with traces progressively shifting to lower elution times (Figure 2e). Pseudo first‐order plots showed linearity for both LA and DMA (Figure 2f), indicating constant radical concentration throughout the polymerization. Notably, although the overall polymerization rate for LA and DMA was faster than for MA and LA, LA and DMA incorporated at similar rates, suggesting different copolymerization behavior of LA with acrylamides compared to acrylates. This indicates formation of a more statistical copolymer with DMA rather than a gradient copolymer with MA, with the former being more favorable for degradation into fragments of equal lengths. As observed for the LA‐MA system, M n,app increased linearly with conversion and aligned closely with theoretical values (Figure 2g). To confirm the slower polymerization rate observed in the absence of oxygen while screening conditions, we repeated the copolymerization of LA with DMA with deoxygenation (Figure S3). The apparent propagation rates of LA and DMA both decreased by approximately 10‐fold (k p,app = 6.82 × 10−5 s−1 for LA, 4.63 × 10−5 s−1 for DMA), highlighting the role of oxygen in continuous regeneration of MB+ from reduced LMB that is necessary to sustain the catalytic cycle.

Together, these results demonstrate that the MB⁺/TEOA‐mediated photoRAFT system enables well‐controlled copolymerization of LA with both acrylates and acrylamides under ambient, oxygen‐tolerant conditions.

Varying Molecular Weights

To demonstrate the ability to obtain degradable LA‐vinyl copolymers with predictable molecular weights, we copolymerized 25 mol% LA with MA at targeted degrees of polymerization (DP T) ranging from 100 to 2000 by varying the monomer‐to‐CTA ratio. For DP T = 100–1000, P(LA‐co‐MA) showed low to moderate dispersities (Ð = 1.13–1.43), with M n,app being in good agreement with M n,theory and monomodal SEC traces (entries 1–4, Table 2; Figure 3a). At DP T = 2000, dispersity increased (Ð = 1.57) and M n,app deviated more from theoretical values (entry 5, Table 2). Further increasing DP T to 5000 led to broad molecular weight distributions without a significant increase in M n,app (entry 3, Table S2), suggesting loss of polymerization control, possibly due to enhanced radical transfer to backbone disulfide bonds.[ 70 ]

Similarly, P(LA‐co‐DMA) copolymers with predictable molecular weights were obtained by adjusting the monomer‐to‐CTA ratio. These copolymers were methylated after polymerization to facilitate SEC analysis. Good control was maintained up to DP T = 1000 (entries 6–9, Table 2; Figure 3b), with loss of control observed at DP T > 2000 (entry 4, Table S2).

Given the limitations of PG/10 vol% DMSO at higher DP T, we next explored copolymerization in slightly basic aqueous phosphate buffer (pH = 8, 100 mM)/10 vol% DMSO at DP T = 5000 and 10 000 with 10 mol% LA. The aqueous conditions significantly reduced the polymerization rate compared to PG, and 10 mol% LA was used due to poor solubility of 25 mol% LA in aqueous buffers. Remarkably, the aqueous MB⁺ RAFT system enabled access to absolute number‐average molar masses (M n,abs) of greater than 600 000 g mol−1 with relatively low dispersities (Ð = 1.11), as determined by aqueous SEC equipped with a multi‐angle light scattering detector (SEC‐MALS) (entries 5–6, Table S2). This is a notable result, as these copolymers represent some of the highest molar mass degradable polymers reported to date.[ 71 ] Such materials open avenues for mimicking not only the size, architecture, and chemistry of naturally occurring ultrahigh molecular weight (UHMW; >1 000 000) biopolymers, such as mucins and lubricin, but doing so with synthetic analogs that retain facile degradability under biologically relevant conditions.[ 72 , 73 ]

Chain‐End Fidelity and Temporal Control

To confirm the ω‐trithiocarbonate (TTC) chain‐end fidelity of the polymers synthesized via MB⁺/TEOA photoRAFT polymerization, a copolymer of n‐butyl acrylate (BA) and LA was first synthesized (P(LA‐co‐BA)‐TTC, M n,app = 17 000 g mol−1, Ð = 1.18) and subsequently chain extended with MA, resulting in a P(LA‐co‐BA)‐b‐PMA block copolymer. SEC analysis showed a clean shift to lower elution volume after chain extension, with no evident tailing or a low‐molecular‐weight shoulder in the SEC trace, indicating complete chain extension from the macroinitiator (Figure 3c). Furthermore, the molar mass of the resulting block copolymer (M n,app = 21,700 g mol−1, Ð = 1.20) agreed well with the theoretical value (M n,theory = 24 100 g mol−1).

To assess the temporal control of the MB⁺/TEOA system, a copolymerization of LA and MA was carried out under optimized conditions, with the red‐light source switched on and off periodically (Figure 3d). In the dark, conversion of both monomers was negligible, and after reillumination, polymerization resumed without delay, with LA converting faster than MA as expected. Repeating the on/off cycle five times did not affect the polymerization control or kinetics, confirming excellent temporal regulation of the polymerization process.

PhotoRAFT at Low Temperatures

Previous studies on the conventional free radical polymerization of lipoic acid (LA) derivatives have highlighted a pronounced temperature dependence on the ring‐opening of the 1,2‐dithiolane moiety. Raeisi et al. reported that the homopolymerization of ethyl lipoate (EtLA) was disfavored at elevated temperatures due to the reversible nature of the polymerization and a temperature‐sensitive monomer–polymer equilibrium.[ 74 ] A later study by Albanese et al. demonstrated a temperature effect on the reactivity ratios of EtLA with BA, where higher temperatures yielded lower LA conversions relative to the acrylate comonomer, resulting in decreased LA incorporation and diminished copolymer degradability.[ 75 ] To evaluate whether this temperature effect on LA ring‐opening polymerization (ROP) could be leveraged to increase LA incorporation in copolymers, we conducted photoRAFT copolymerizations of LA with DMA (50 mol% LA feed, DP T = 200) at ambient (26 °C) and low (−10 °C) temperatures. At 26 °C, LA and DMA conversions were comparable (80% and 78%, respectively) (entry 1, Table 3), while, at −10 °C, LA conversion remained high (83%) while decreasing for DMA (54%) (entry 2, Table 3). The different reactivity at low temperature resulted in a higher final LA content in the purified copolymer: 68% at −10 °C compared to 54% at ambient temperature (Figure 4a). SEC analysis of the methylated copolymers revealed similar molar masses and dispersities regardless of temperature (Figure 4b,c). In contrast, a conventional thermally initiated RAFT polymerization conducted at 70 °C under comparable conditions resulted in lower overall monomer conversions and lower molar mass (13 200 g mol−1 , Ð = 1.37) (entry 3, Table 3; Figure S10), and notably, a reduced LA conversion (42%) relative to DMA (51%), resulting in diminished LA incorporation (49%, Figure S11).

To further elucidate the structures of P(LA‐co‐DMA) prepared at different temperatures, we performed filtered 1D 1H diffusion‐ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) to suppress residual solvent signals (Figure S14). This revealed signals at δ = 3.3–3.9 ppm, which correspond to DMA backbone methine protons present in LA–DMA dyads, thereby confirming the successful synthesis of copolymers. Furthermore, the integration of these protons relative to LA was significantly less in P(LA‐co‐DMA) synthesized at low temperature compared to ambient or high temperature, indicating a higher quantity of LA homopolymer sequences containing cleavable disulfide bonds.

P(LA‐co‐DMA) prepared at different temperatures was further characterized by 2D 1H‐13C heteronuclear single‐quantum correlation (HSQC) NMR spectroscopy (Figures S15–S17) to resolve overlapping signals for a more accurate calculation of LA incorporation. In addition, we prepared a homopolymer of DMA via MB+‐mediated RAFT that was also characterized through 1D DOSY and HSQC NMR spectroscopy (Figures S18,S19) for comparison with P(LA‐co‐DMA). A lack of characteristic signals that are otherwise present for LA–DMA copolymers was evident, further supporting successful copolymer synthesis.

Similar trends of enhanced LA incorporation at lower temperatures were observed for copolymerizations with a 25 mol% LA feed (entries 4 and 5, Table 3; Figures S12,S13). Together, these results show that it is possible to control LA incorporation, and enhance polymer degradability, by conducting the photoRAFT at low temperatures where the ROP of 1,2‐dithiolanes is more thermodynamically favored.[ 71 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 ] This low temperature‐tunable incorporation is a distinguishing feature of photoRAFT, and is not feasible with conventional RAFT methods that rely on thermally initiated radical generation and are typically limited to ambient or elevated temperatures.[ 78 ]

Monomer Scope

The MB+/TEOA mediated photoRAFT copolymerization was extended to a broad range of hydrophobic and hydrophilic vinyl monomers, including those bearing neutral, charged, or zwitterionic functionalities (Figure 5). Acrylates and acrylamides were chosen due to their efficient copolymerization with LA, over other vinyl monomers such as methacrylates, methacrylamides, and styrenics, which, in contrast, have low or incompatible reactivity with 1,2‐dithiolanes.[ 24 , 25 , 79 ] All copolymerizations were performed at 25 mol% LA feed at DP T = 200. In all cases, polymers were obtained with moderate‐to‐high monomer conversions, molar masses in good agreement with theoretical values, and low‐to‐moderate dispersities (Đ < 1.50), demonstrating the versatility of this fully oxygen‐tolerant system for synthesizing degradable polymers with diverse functional groups (Table 4).

We also evaluated lipoamide (LAm), a commercially available amide derivative of LA, as a comonomer with MA. Unlike LA, LAm has not been studied in RDRP systems and has only been polymerized using conventional free radical polymerization.[ 25 ] Due to its poor solubility in EG and PG/DMSO solvent mixtures, the reaction required vigorous vortexing and heating to achieve homogenization. Despite these challenges, the polymerization proceeded in a relatively controlled fashion, though with lower conversions and broader molecular weight distributions (Figure S34).

Polymer Degradation

To assess the degradability of LA‐vinyl copolymers prepared via MB+/TEOA mediated photoRAFT, we synthesized P(LA‐co‐MA) with varying LA feed ratios (10, 25, 50 mol%, Table 5). Critically, 1H NMR analysis of the purified copolymers revealed higher LA incorporation than the initial feed (14, 33, 62 mol%, Table 5), yielding degradable materials with up to 62 mol% LA content. DP T = 1000 was chosen to ensure sufficient molar mass for reliable degradation analysis by SEC. Following purification, the copolymers were treated with TMS‐diazomethane to methylate pendant carboxylic acid groups and minimize polymer–column interactions during SEC. The methylated polymers were then subjected to reductive degradation using 100 mM TCEP in THF/water (3/1 v/v) mixture at room temperature for 20 h.

We further examined degradation of P(LAMe‐co‐MA) (33 mol% LA incorporation, M n,app = 66 400 g mol−1) using two additional triggers: DTT, a commonly used thiol‐based disulfide reducing agent, and UV light. Treatment with 100 mM DTT in THF at ambient temperature for 18 h resulted in a nearly 87% loss in molar mass (Figure 6d). A similar reduction was achieved by UV irradiation (7.0 mW/cm2). Likely due to reversible disulfide exchange, DTT and UV light degraded the polymer less completely than TCEP, which, in contrast, irreversibly reduces disulfides to their corresponding thiols.[ 80 ]

Finally, we explored the degradation of water‐soluble, high molecular weight P(LA‐co‐DMA) (16 mol% LA incorporation, M n,abs = 675 000 g mol−1) using glutathione (GSH), a naturally occurring antioxidant found in bodily tissues,[ 81 ] along with UV irradiation from both an artificial light source and ambient sunlight. Action by 100 mM GSH resulted in 76% loss in molar mass (Figure 6e), and artificial UV light irradiation for 20 h (7.0 mW/cm2) resulted in reduction of molar mass to just 5% of its original value. Remarkably, after just 4 h under direct sunlight, the polymer lost 88% of its molar mass (Figure 6e), indicating a possibility for rapid environmental degradation of the high molecular weight polymer.

Together, these results demonstrate the ability to trigger degradation with mild reducing agents or UV light which offers a pathway to environmentally responsive disposal and end‐of‐life management strategies for advanced polymeric materials. Moreover, degradability is tunable simply by varying the LA feed ratio during polymerization, offering tailored degradation profiles for targeted applications.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a red‐light‐driven, methylene blue (MB⁺)/triethanolamine (TEOA)‐mediated RAFT polymerization platform for synthesizing degradable α‐lipoic acid (LA)‐vinyl copolymers. Compared to the previously established conventional thermal RAFT polymerization, this approach enables higher degrees of polymerization, greater LA incorporation, and improved control over molecular weight and dispersity. Notably, we achieved molar masses exceeding 600 000 g mol−1, placing these copolymers among the highest molar mass degradable polymers reported to date. The system is fully oxygen‐tolerant, operationally simple, and readily scalable, making it well‐suited for applications in high‐throughput synthesis, 3D printing, and bioconjugation. MB⁺‐mediated photoRAFT is also compatible with a broad range of acrylate and acrylamide monomers bearing neutral, charged, or zwitterionic functionalities, affording water‐soluble copolymers with diverse side chain chemistry. By varying the LA feed ratio, we achieved tunable polymer degradability, triggered by disulfide reducing agents or UV light or sunlight. Altogether, this strategy provides a versatile and robust route to functional, degradable polymers, expanding the toolkit for advanced materials that address the growing concern over the persistence of conventional, all‐carbon‐backbone polymers. The platform's simplicity, use of the renewable LA monomer, mild reagents, compatibility with aqueous conditions, and red‐light activation make it particularly promising for the scalable synthesis of degradable materials with controllable end‐of‐life profiles.
