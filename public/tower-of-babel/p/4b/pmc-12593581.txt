Ethical Issues in a Pediatric Post-acute Care Hospital: Implications for Patients, Providers, and Ethicists
Authors: Armand H Matheny Antommaria
Published: 2025

As pediatric health care evolves and the nature of the pediatric patient population changes, alternative sites of care become more important. There are increasing numbers of children with medical complexity or complex chronic conditions.1 They receive care not only in acute care hospitals and at home, but also in pediatric post-acute care (PPAC) facilities (also known as pediatric specialty or rehabilitation hospitals) and long-term care or skilled nursing facilities. PPAC facilities are heterogenous, including freestanding facilities and units within other facilities.2 Investigators are paying greater attention to the services that they provide.3

Study

The study by Salter et al4 makes a significant contribution to this literature and has important implications for patients and families, health care providers, and clinical ethicists. They conducted a survey of health care providers at a 60-bed, freestanding, PPAC hospital in the Saint Louis, Missouri metropolitan area. They did not provide incentives, which may have decreased their response rate. Their objectives were to determine the types of ethical issues providers encountered and the adequacy of resources to address or resolve them. Their survey included both quantitative and qualitative components; it asked participants the frequency with which they encountered 33 potentially ethically challenging issues as well as open-ended questions about additional issues they encountered and challenging ethical scenarios.

One hundred four of 351 eligible providers (response rate 30%) answered the frequency question. Approximately 25% of respondents were nurses and 25% therapists (excluding respiratory therapists, who were categorized separately). Physicians and nurse practitioners were 15% of respondents. The 5 issues that the largest number of respondents encountered frequently or occasionally were discharge disposition (87%), behavior problems (83%), team-parent communication issues (82%), intrafamily conflict (82%), and patient best interest (81%). Although few respondents offered additional issues in response to the open-ended question, the investigators identified 6 additional issues from their qualitative analysis of the ethically challenging scenarios: lack of caregiver support, state child welfare involvement, end-of-life decision-making, use of restrictions/restraints, billing, and bias/discrimination. Lack of caregiver support was the second most frequently coded theme (13%) in the scenarios after discharge disposition (15%).

Forty-six percent of those who responded agreed that they have appropriate institutional resources available to help address ethical dilemmas or conflicts, 33% neither agreed or disagreed, and 22% disagreed. The most frequently identified resources were supervisors, managers, and department leads (34%) and peer and team members (24%). These percentiles represent the percentage of those who listed resources (n = 59) as opposed to total respondents or those who agreed resources were adequate. Forty-seven percent of those who listed a resource that they wished was available (20 of 47, or 19% of the 104 total participants) listed an ethics committee.

Implications

The findings of Salter et al have important implications for patients and families. The ethical issues that children with medical complexity encounter should be addressed. The concern may not necessarily be whether patients in freestanding PPAC facilities experience unique ethical issues. Some PPAC facilities exist within acute care hospitals, and children with medical complexity may experience the same ethical issues within acute care hospitals or at home, especially in communities without PPAC facilities. The point may be that they experience the issues at a different frequency. The investigators asked participants whether they encountered issues as ethically challenging frequently, occasionally, never, or not applicable. Other ways of framing this question, like the number of times a respondent has encountered this issue in the last month, or whether in the last year they encountered this issue more than once a month, one a month, a few times, or never, may be beneficial and allow clearer comparisons between individuals and institutions.

Lack of caregiver support is an issue that deserves greater attention. The increasing demands on caregivers during a time of reduced social safety nets creates complex demands on caregivers and health care institutions. Issues include what constitutes a “safe” discharge and, if a “safe” discharge to home is not available, may an acute or post-acute care hospital discharge a patient to another setting without parental consent? Analyzing this issue potentially demands a wider ethical perspective than clinical ethics’ historical focus on the patient-provider relationship, and raises questions about what society owes its members and how social services and health care delivery systems should be organized and funded.

This study also has important implications for providers and ethicists. Moral distress was originally described as “when one knows the right thing to do, but institutional constraints make it nearly impossible to pursue the right course of action.”5 It contributes to employee burnout and intentions to leave.6 PPAC facilities report difficulties hiring and retaining clinical staff.3 To the extent that ethical issues and the lack of appropriate institutional resources to address them contributes to moral distress, addressing them may be important to their staff's wellbeing and retention.

One of the issues is what mechanisms are best to address moral distress and its consequences. Some respondents identified the need for an ethics committee. Establishing an ethics committee may not be sufficient without the involvement of an individual or individuals with training and experience in clinical ethics and ethics consultation. Such involvement might be accomplished by training staff members who are interested in ethics and/or contracting with a clinical ethicist.7 As the authors note in their discussion, collaboration with ethics committees and/or clinical ethics consultants at referring acute care hospitals may be beneficial. In addition to providing clinical ethics consultation, some institutions have established moral distress rounds.8 A challenge is the inability to bill for these services, although they may be cost effective for the institution if they improve retention.

With respect to ethicists, this study poses several methodological challenges. One of them is how to categorize ethical issues.9 For example, the authors identified end-of-life decision-making as a new theme that is not part of the original list of 33 issues and topics. It is, however, unclear how it differs from patient best interest, patient/family demands for treatment, nonbeneficial treatment, code status, advance directives, withholding/withdrawing care, and artificial nutrition. A typology with high inter-rater reliability and broad applicability is needed to categorize ethical issues and compare them over time and between institutions.

The larger methodologic issues include demonstrating the value of ethical expertise and evaluating the outcomes of ethics committees, ethics consultation services, and/or moral distress rounds. There is a paucity of literature evaluating the outcomes of ethics services and their association with levels of expertise of their providers, or different methods for providing them.8,10 It therefore is difficult to make evidence-based recommendations about what type of resources institutions should provide their staff.

Conclusions

Although its findings may have limited generalizability, the study by Salter et al contributes to the literature on PPAC facilities and children with medical complexity. It highlights the ethical issues that providers encounter and the potential need for additional resources to address them. It provides a basis for continuing to improve empirical research methodologies in clinical ethics and future work on this topic, including multisite studies, inclusion of patient and family voices (see, for example, Shapiro et al11), and evaluation of interventions.
