Knowledge, attitude, and practices of beta thalassemia: a cross-sectional study among young adults in Delhi-NCR, India
Authors: Oishi Choudhury, Akansha Dubey, Kallur Nava Saraswathy, Roumi Deb, Ravi Ranjan, J. S. Arora, Benrithung Murry, Ram Prasad Mitra, Naorem Kiranmala Devi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThalassemia is one of the most common monogenic blood disorders worldwide and has a considerable public health burden on India, with a 3.74% prevalence of beta-thalassemia trait. Most ideally, this genetic disease can be managed by prevention through awareness, screening, and counselling. However, prior to this, a background of knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of thalassemia among the population is necessary, which will determine the efficiency of the management programs. Given the paucity of KAP studies in India, the present study aims to report the KAP towards thalassemia among the young adults of Delhi, India.MethodsThis a cross-sectional study conducted among 1585 adults aged 18–35 years. The socio-demographic and KAP data were collected using a pretested and modified interview schedule.ResultsOnly 36.8% of individuals in the study had ever heard of the term “thalassemia”. Awareness (about the term) was higher among females, Muslims, individuals with higher education, those belonging to upper socio-economic stratum, and Western India, compared to their respective counterparts. In contrast, individuals from schedule caste and other backward classes exhibited lower awareness levels compared to those from unreserved category. A low percentage of 7.7% had adequate knowledge about thalassemia, and education, region, and socio-economic status were its predictors. Overall, 86.7% and 22.6% of sample exhibited positive attitude and practices, respectively. Knowledge was a significant predictor of positive practices; however, negative attitude prevailed despite the adequacy of knowledge. Overall, males exhibited higher odds of negative attitude, and lower education seemed to be an important predictor of both negative attitude and poor/ negative practices.ConclusionsThe study highlights the need for targeted awareness campaigns among young adults aimed at encouraging positive attitude, practices, and mitigation of associated stigma. College healthcare programs, compulsory inclusion of thalassemia in all high school or college curriculums, and community outreach programs can be effective measures in promoting awareness, thereby enabling the youth to make informed reproductive planning. This can further lay the groundwork for effective implementation of universal screening programs, subsequently contributing towards reduced burden of beta-thalassemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24065-5.

Background

The management of genetic diseases can be achieved either through screening and counselling or through treatment, as most genetic diseases are not completely curable. With advancements in medical research and improved treatment strategies, the lifespan of affected individuals could be prolonged, however, the health span is still compromised. This mostly accounts for the physical, psychological, financial, and social burden upon the affected and their family due to the expensive and formidable treatments. Thalassemia is one of the most common monogenic disorders prevalent worldwide, with a high frequency across the Mediterranean region, parts of Africa, Europe, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia [1]. India, being in this “Thalassemia belt,” accounts for 10% of the global incidence of thalassemia major (TM) [2] with a 3.74% prevalence of beta thalassemia trait (BTT) [3] thereby posing a considerable public health burden.

Individuals born with TM require lifelong treatments like blood transfusion, bone marrow transplantation, iron chelation management, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [4]. Due to the increased availability of blood transfusions in India, it is estimated that by 2026 approximately 275,000 will require treatment for thalassemia. Hence there will be an increased demand for blood up to 8.25 million units, thereby escalating the cost of treatment [5]. The cost-benefit analysis reveals that the cost of management of thalassemia in India can be reduced by 30% through wide-scale screening compared to treatment [6]. The increased number of TM patients attaining reproductive fitness and the practice of consanguineous marriages and inbreeding in different Indian communities are potential contributing factors to the increased genetic load of thalassemia [7].

In India, consanguineous marriages are common among the Hindus of South India and Muslims [8, 9] and inbreeding is practiced among the endogamous groups in the form of marriages within castes and communities [10]. Therefore, large-scale screening and counselling of thalassemia traits among different high-risk populations is the best management strategy, which can potentially prevent further incidence of the disease in these communities.

However, as we delve into the challenges of mass screening and counseling, certain factors need to be addressed in order to achieve a beneficial outcome from these programs. During screening, when an apparently normal healthy individual is suddenly exposed to their carrier status, it can instigate mental stress, fear, or trauma in them. Therefore, in order to mitigate any social stigma or misconceptions associated with the disease, the promotion of mass awareness or correct information in high-risk communities is a vital step that must precede screening. The participation of individuals in screening programs is also highly dependent on the awareness among the population. Although national guidelines for prevention and control of haemoglobinopathies have been published, a nationwide screening program for beta thalassemia carriers is yet to be implemented in India [11]. Few states and region-specific screening programs have been implemented earlier, like the Jai Vigyan Program in Maharashtra, Gujarat, West Bengal, Karnataka, Punjab, and Assam; Gujarat State Thalassemia Control Program, Project Rainbow in Punjab, and individual centers in a few other states [11]. Despite these initiatives, the management of beta thalassemia in India is not yet under control.

For implementing a successful awareness and screening program, the evaluation of the knowledge, attitude, and practices (KAPs) towards thalassemia among the general young adult population is a primary requisite. It is essential to have an idea of the baseline foundation of the general population in different regions regarding their KAPs towards thalassemia, to formulate and implement programs for awareness and screening accordingly. In addition, a KAP study also acts as a preliminary mode of promoting awareness. In India, few studies have assessed the KAPs of the general population; most studies were conducted in the eastern region, namely West Bengal and Odissa [12–15]. Provided the BTT prevalence in North India has been estimated to be 8.9% [16], and high-risk communities like Sindhis, Aroras, and Punjabi khatris reside in North India [11]there is a dearth of research on thalassemia KAP in this region. One study explored the KAPs among Aroras of Northern India through a comparative perspective between rural and urban region [17]. Previous studies have predominantly focused on thalassemia patients, their families, or carriers [18, 19]; however, there is a paucity of studies focusing on the KAP of the general young adult population. A study conducted among the adolescent population in India [20] revealed that awareness or screening during adolescence may be forgotten by the time individuals reach marriageable age. Hence particular focus on the young adult population is essential because awareness among this age group could enable them to be mindful towards the selection of partners and consider premarital screening prior to engaging in marital relationship thereby allowing them to take informed reproductive decisions before family planning.

Hence the present study aims to report the knowledge, attitude, and practices towards thalassemia among the young adults in Delhi and National Capital Region (Delhi-NCR). This study can potentially inform future prevention programs and policies by enabling efficient implementation of thalassemia screening and awareness programs within metropolitan cities like Delhi-NCR and possibly at a national level.

Methods

Study design

The present observational cross-sectional study was a part of a larger study on a cohort of 5000 individuals. Of these, the KAP data were collected from 1585 individuals (Females: 64.6%). The study focused on individuals aged 18–35 years. To ensure adequate sample representation, a post hoc power analysis was conducted yielding a statistical power of 84.4%, which is considered acceptable.

Participants were recruited from two Universities in Delhi-NCR, one government and one private. The colleges/departments/centres under the selected Universities were randomly approached for permission. Upon acquiring permission from the authorities, announcements were made on campus. The students who voluntarily consented to participate were recruited for data collection through face-to-face interaction. The participants included in the study were apparently healthy, i.e., didn’t have any self-reported major physical or mental disorders/disease, and currently resided in Delhi-NCR (at least for 6 months). The aim, objectives, risk and benefits of the study were explained in detail to participants and informed written consent was obtained in English prior to data collection. However, no specific information about the disease under investigation was disclosed at that stage to minimize potential bias. Following the completion of data collection, awareness about the disease was actively promoted among participants. The protocol of the present study was approved by the Departmental Ethics Committee, Department of Anthropology, University of Delhi (Ref. No./anth/2019/3888/12).

Data collection

In the present study socio-demographic and KAP data were collected using face to face interview with students in English language.

Socio-demographic data

The socio-demographic data (age, sex, social category, religion, education, region they are from or place of birth, and socio-economic status) were collected using a pretested and modified interview schedule. The SC (Scheduled Castes), ST (Scheduled Tribes), OBC (Other Backward Classes), and UR (Unreserved or General Category) are official social categories used in policy frameworks to address historical marginalization and ensure affirmative action in sectors such as education, employment, and political representation [21]. The Kuppuswamy scale has been used to assess the socio-economic status of the studied population [22]. The socio-economic classes were categorized into upper, middle, and lower classes, wherein, scores of 10 or below were categorized as lower class, 11–25 as middle class, and 26–29 as upper class [22]. The regions or place of birth of the participants were categorized in North East (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, and Tripura), North (Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, Chandigarh, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh), South (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu), East (Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, and West Bengal), West (Gujarat and Maharashtra), Central India (Madhya Pradhesh and Chattisgarh), and foreign countries which included Nepal, Saudi Arabia, Dubai, Kuwait and Thailand.

Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices

Schedule design

The interview schedule used to capture the KAP related to thalassemia was initially adapted from the tool used in a study conducted in West Bengal by Behera et al. [15] The tool was modified and validated by experts in Demographic Anthropology, Genetics, Medical Anthropology, and Hematology, and subsequently tested on a study population in Gujarat as part of another research project. Further, the tool was adapted in the present study with slight modifications to ensure its relevance and appropriateness for the current study population. The instrument in the present study was validated by experts in the field of Medical Anthropology, Hematology, and representatives of an NGO called National Thalassemia Welfare Society.

The initial phase involved a thorough review of the original items, after which modifications were made to tailor the schedule to the socio-cultural and literacy context of the target population. Specifically, additional items were incorporated to assess practices surrounding prenatal screening, therapeutic abortion, and consanguineous marriage. These additions aimed to generate a comprehensive understanding of behavioural responses and reproductive decision-making in the context of thalassemia risk. The wording and structure of several knowledge-related items were revised to ensure clarity, linguistic simplicity, and cultural alignment with the local vernacular and educational background of the participants. The tool was formulated and implemented in English language.

Following expert validation, the schedule was pilot-tested among 50 participants. Minor revisions were made based on participant feedback to improve clarity and reduce response burden.

The finalized interview schedule is included in Supplementary File 1.

Schedule structure and measurement

The KAP schedule consisted three parts and a total of 31 questions. The first part consists of 11 questions dedicated to assessing knowledge regarding thalassemia. Of these, one question enquired whether the participant has ever heard the word/term thalassemia before (awareness), and if the answer to the question was yes, the knowledge was further assessed using 10 items. The questions included both open-ended and close-ended questions like, “What is Thalassemia minor/carrier/trait? Please explain in your own words” or “Can a normal person be a Thalassemia Carrier?”. These items were further scored based on the correct or incorrect responses to evaluate the knowledge of the participants. The outcomes were categorized into three categories, viz. those who had just heard the name “thalassemia” and had no further knowledge regarding it, adequate knowledge, and inadequate knowledge. For the assessment, Bloom’s cut-off was adopted, which signified a score of below 60% to be poor KAP [23]; hence, participants scoring below 60% were categorized under “inadequate knowledge”, and participants scoring 60% and above were categorized under “adequate knowledge”.

The second part comprised of 10 questions to assess the attitude of the participants, among which 6 questions were dedicated to assessing the social belief. For instance, “Do you believe that thalassemia child is born due to sin committed by parents?” and the remaining 4 questions assessed the social distance of the participants pertaining to thalassemia, like “Would you feel discomfort accepting a life partner having Thalassemia minor/trait/carrier?”. The third part consisted of 10 questions regarding the practice towards thalassemia of the participants. Practice questions were mostly like, “Have you ever tested for thalassemia?” and “Will you like to go for a genetic test with your fiancé before marriage for Thalassemia?”. Further, the 10-item attitude and practices of individuals who have heard about “thalassemia” were scored, wherein 1 point was assigned for a positive attitude/practice and 0 for a negative attitude/practice. The overall attitude and practice score was categorized into positive and negative using Bloom’s cut-off (score below 60% was considered negative, and above and equal to 60% was assessed as positive) [21]. This threshold is widely accepted in KAP literature and is considered a standard criterion for determining minimum acceptable performance [14]. Moreover, uniform application of this cut-off across KAP domains also ensured consistency and interpretive clarity.

The McDonald’s Omega value of overall KAP was 0.63, for the Knowledge sub-scale was 0.55 (low value due to overall low variability in items related to the knowledge sub-scale), attitude sub-scale was 0.74, and practice was 0.79.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Version 22. Missing data was primarily handled by pairwise deletion wherever applicable, in other cases it was addressed by default methods of SPSS in terms of list-wise deletion. Descriptive statistics was used to evaluate the distribution of knowledge scores and attitude and practice responses. Chi-square tests have been used to determine the association of socio-demographic variables with KAP. Logistic regression analysis has been used to determine the socio-demographic predictors of KAP. The significance level used for this study was < 0.05.

Results

Awareness and knowledge of thalassemia

The present study reveals that 63.2% of the sample were unaware of the word “thalassemia” and had never heard of it before. Further, higher proportions of females (40.7%), muslims (47.4%), individuals from unreserved category (42.7%), with highest educational qualification (PhD) (78.9%), belonging from the Western India (70%), and from upper socio-economic status (58.3%) were more acquainted with the word “thalassemia” compared to males (29.9%); Hindu (36.3%) and other religions (45.9%), individuals from reserved category (OBC: 30.3%; SC:25.6%; ST: 33.3%), individuals pursuing or obtained graduation (36%) or post-graduation (47.6%) degrees, individuals from other regions of India or who were born abroad (North East: 48.4%, North: 36.5%, Central: 53.1%, East: 35.9%, South: 41.1%, Foreign: 60%), and individuals from lower or middle socio-economic status (Lower: 23.6%, Middle: 41.5%); respectively. (Supplementary Table 1)

Further, logistic regression analysis revealed that males, OBC, SC, middle and lower socioeconomic classes had significantly increased odds of being unaware of the word thalassemia than their respective counterparts. Meanwhile, individuals from West India, Muslims, and higher education (postgraduation and PhD) than their respective counterparts had lower odds of being unaware of thalassemia (Fig. 1).

To understand the distribution adequate/inadequate knowledge across the sociodemographic categories, the adequacy of knowledge was further assessed among the individuals who had heard about thalassemia. Analysis revealed that a higher proportion of individuals who were pursuing or had obtained the highest educational qualification (PhD) had adequate knowledge (26.7%) compared to the undergraduate (8.3%) or post-graduate individuals (2.9%). The significant difference in the distribution of knowledge adequacy was observed across different socio-economic statuses, where adequate knowledge seems to be more prevalent among the individuals belonging to the upper class (16.4%) compared to the lower (7.8%) and middle class (6.3%) (Supplementary Table 2).

Further, logistic regression analysis indicated that of all the studied socio-demographic variables, education, region, and socio-economic status were significantly associated with inadequate knowledge. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of education and those from the Eastern region of India had significantly lower odds of possessing inadequate knowledge about thalassemia, precisely 0.24 folds and 0.43 folds, respectively. Meanwhile, compared to upper socio-economic class, the middle and lower classes had 2.9-fold and 2.24-fold increased odds of having inadequate knowledge, respectively (Table 1).

Attitude towards thalassemia

The overall attitude of the participants towards thalassemia revealed that 86.7% has positive attitude. The distribution of positive/negative attitude across socio-demographic categories revealed that significantly higher proportion of females (91%), individuals from scheduled tribes (100%), highest education degrees like PhD (100%), and upper socio-economic status (92.8%) had a positive attitude towards thalassemia compared to their respective counterparts (Supplementary Table 3).

Further logistic regression analysis revealed that males had 2.6-fold increased odds of having a negative attitude towards thalassemia compared to females. Moreover, in socio-economic status, the lower class had 3-fold increased odds of having a negative attitude. Meanwhile, individuals who had higher education, viz., postgraduation had reduced odds of negative attitude than undergraduates. Further, individuals in the SC category had 2.6-fold increased odds of negative attitude than unreserved (Table 2)

On further analyzing the sex-specific differences in attitude, a higher proportion of males were observed to have negative attitude towards thalassemia where their social beliefs stated that thalassemia child is born due to parent’s sin (16.4%), or a family with thalassemic child is miserable (24.9%) and unlucky (17.5%) compared to females. In terms of social distance as well, a higher proportion of males stated that they would feel discomfort with a neighbor suffering from thalassemia (15%) or living with someone with thalassemia (18.5%) compared to females (7.9%, 12.5%, respectively) (Table 3)

Practice of thalassemia

The overall practices of the participants towards thalassemia revealed that 22.6% had positive attitude. In terms of socio-demographic variables, individuals from the highest educational degrees (52.6%) showed a significantly higher proportion of good practices compared to undergraduate (22.2%) or postgraduate students (33.1%) (Supplementary Table 3)

The logistic regression analysis revealed that in reference to undergraduates, postgraduates, and PhD students had reduced odds of negative practices towards thalassemia, specifically 0.5 folds and 0.2 folds, respectively. Further individuals from Southern India had reduced odds (OR = 0.601) of having negative practices in reference to those from North India (Table 4).

Discussion

Knowledge

Awareness regarding the term thalassemia was considerably low in the studied population. Only 36.8% had ever come across the term “thalassemia,” which contrasts sharply with findings from studies conducted in other countries such as Indonesia (74.5%), Saudi Arabia (48%), Turkey (58%), Bahrain (65%), Malaysia (76.4%), Italy (85%), and Greece (95%) [24–29]. This discrepancy may be attributed to distinct socio-cultural setups and the fact that most of these studies were conducted among adults aged 18 years and above, whereas the present study focused on a narrower age range of young adults aged 18–35 years [24–27]. Meanwhile, of the two studies conducted among young students, the study by Okyay et al. involved participants who were mostly already informed about haemoglobinopathies prior to data collection [28] while in the study by Olwi et al., the majority of senior students came from medical and science backgrounds [29]. Another institution-based study conducted in the South 24 Parganas district of West Bengal revealed a similar response, where 37.93% had heard about beta thalassemia [14] but the study sample was collected from a tertiary care teaching hospital.

Upon further assessment of the knowledge among individuals who had heard about thalassemia, it was observed that only 7.7% had adequate knowledge, 85.8% had inadequate knowledge, and the remaining 6.5% had just heard of the disease but are unaware of any further information regarding the same. This highlights a considerably low proportion of individuals with adequate knowledge, despite 36.8% of the sample having heard of the word/term “thalassemia”. This level of adequate knowledge was remarkably low compared to other studies in India. For instance, in a study conducted by Basu in West Bengal, 57.94% of the studied population had adequate knowledge [15]; however, this study was conducted on patients attending tertiary care hospital aged 18 years and above. The probable cause for the low knowledge adequacy among the studied population might be that the majority of the participants were students from arts and humanities backgrounds and might not have been introduced to the different haemoglobinopathies through their curriculum. This lack of knowledge regarding the disease among the college-going students of Delhi-NCR indicates the need for emphasis on beta-thalassemia and other haemoglobinopathies in the curriculum and focus on strategies to promote awareness among the general young population. For instance, utilizing social media platforms for awareness can be beneficial towards reaching the majority of the young adult population.

The distribution of awareness across socio-demographic variables highlights that in this study, females were more acquainted with the term “thalassemia” than males; similar findings have been observed in a few other studies [15, 30–32]. Socio-demographic variables play a significant role in determining the awareness of an individual. In the current study, individuals who belong to Islam religion had heard of the term “thalassemia” more than other religious groups. This can be attributed to the prevalence of consanguineous marriage in this community, which is a contributing factor to elevated burden [33, 34]. The present study population, being a heterozygous group, includes students from different regions of India, thereby highlighting a significant region-wise difference in knowledge regarding thalassemia, wherein more participants from Western India were acquainted with the term “thalassemia” compared to other regions of India or those born abroad. This evidence potentially highlights the success of government initiatives of thalassemia awareness and screening in Gujarat and Maharashtra, like the Gujarat State Thalassemia Control Program and Jai Vigyan Program, suggesting the potential of these campaigns towards the promotion of awareness. Additionally, enhanced health literacy [35] in the region might also be one of the contributing factors of the elevated awareness observed here. Furthermore, the study revealed low levels of awareness of thalassemia among the marginalized groups, particularly OBC and SC categories. Though not many studies have explored the difference in awareness level across the social categories, this outcome suggests the possibility that limited access to health literacy may be a significant barrier among these groups. The higher odds of unawareness among these groups can be attributed to restricted access to broader social advantages like higher socio-economic status, which has widely been observed as an important predictor of thalassemia knowledge and awareness [15] further evidenced by the outcomes of the present study.

In addition, education and income or socio-economic status were observed to be predictors for both knowledge and awareness of thalassemia; more individuals from upper socio-economic class and with higher educational qualification have heard about thalassemia and had adequate knowledge about the disease as well. Similar outcomes have been reported in other studies [15, 34]. High income enables improved accessibility to education, and improved socio-economic conditions accompanied by improved education widens the exposure to media and other sources of information that can preferably inform the masses about the diseases. Hence awareness program must be designed as such that it can reach individuals of different educational background, and those with lower and middle socio-economic status. Moreover, since the study was conducted in an institutional setting, it did not capture the knowledge and awareness levels of young adults without formal education. However, given the critical role of education in shaping knowledge and awareness, it is likely that this group may exhibit lower levels of understanding and hence requires targeted attention.

Furthermore, individuals from the eastern region of India demonstrated higher levels of thalassemia knowledge. This aligns with previous studies conducted in eastern India, which have also reported higher awareness levels among the population [14, 15, 36]. This may be attributed to the higher regional prevalence of thalassemia and the greater reach of awareness campaigns in these areas like that of Jai Vigyan Program and West Bengal Thalassemia Control Program. Notably, the majority of KAP studies on thalassemia in India have been concentrated in the eastern region [12–15, 36] indicating the need to explore awareness patterns more extensively across other parts of the country. This overrepresentation of eastern India in the literature also calls for caution in generalizing these findings at the national level, underscoring the need for regionally diverse studies.

Attitude

A majority of the studied population, viz., 86.7% demonstrated a positive attitude towards thalassemia, similar results have been obtained from other KAP studies as well [15, 34]. Further lower socio-economic status, males, lower education, and SC category was observed to be predictors of negative attitude towards thalassemia. Earlier studies have also reported education and socio-economic status to be significant predictors of attitude towards thalassemia [15]. This indicates that social disadvantages can play an important role in framing of health-related attitude, likely due to reduced access to health literacy. Meanwhile the negative attitude in these groups can also be rooted in structural and socio-cultural factors including perceived stigma and collective cultural narratives.

However, few individuals, around 23% believed that thalassemia is caused by sin committed by parents, or due to a defect in them, which reflects the lack of awareness and knowledge regarding the etiology of the disease which paves way for social stigmas like so. Interestingly, negative attitude was seen to prevail more among males compared to females both in terms of social belief and social distance, this outcome can be justified by the earlier finding which revealed females were more acquainted with thalassemia compared to males, hence improved awareness might be the reason for better attitude. Similar gender difference in overall attitude has also been obtained in few other studies [32, 37] which highlights how the mental framework is shaped under the influence of the expected social roles, wherein females are known to be more empathic than males [38]. Further, though only 14.6% stated that they would feel discomfort living with someone suffering from thalassemia, however around double of that, 30.9% and 35.7% stated that they would feel discomfort establishing marriage relation with someone who has a thalassemic individual in their family, or to accept a life partner with thalassemia carrier, respectively. Thus, social acceptance also has its boundaries especially when it involves forming marital relationships probably due to prevalence of associated misconceptions with the disease.

The study also tried to understand how knowledge of these individuals shapes their attitude towards thalassemia, and some unexpected outcomes have been observed. Surprisingly, though no significant correlation was found between knowledge and attitude, individuals with adequate knowledge about thalassemia demonstrated a negative attitude towards the disease. This highlights an aspect of cognitive dissonance [39] indicating the presence of conflicting beliefs and attitudes. Considering that negative attitudes primarily stem from stigmas and perceptions rooted in socio-cultural norms, this dissonance may arise when scientifically informed knowledge clashes with deep-seated cultural beliefs, fears, or societal pressures. Such a contradiction suggests that simply disseminating information may not be sufficient to change attitudes unless accompanied by efforts to address underlying social narratives and cultural constructs. Another study conducted among college students in India have also reported social discomfort and limited proactive practices underscoring the need for targeted education [36].

Practice

Beyond knowledge and attitude, incorporating good practice is essential to obtain expected outcomes from awareness programs towards the reduction of thalassemia burden. Promoting awareness among the masses will only be effective if it’s implemented through good practice. Only 22.6% of the study population exhibited positive practice towards beta thalassemia. Further education was observed to be a significant predictor of practices of thalassemia. Higher education exposes an individual to improved health literacy, critical thinking ability, and a sense of responsibility which might play a significant part in developing positive practices towards thalassemia. Furthermore, individuals from Southern part of India also had higher levels of positive practice. This association can be attributed to higher health literacy in Southern states [35] further evidenced by the positive correlation observed between practice and knowledge of thalassemia in the present study.

However, though majority of 97.2% of the population did not ever get tested for beta-thalassemia, 80.9% have stated that they would like to get tested; this implies that if the government takes up nationwide beta-thalassemia screening initiative people will be enthusiastic to get tested. A considerable majority, 71.1% confirmed they would like to get their partners tested before engaging in marital relationships, meanwhile only 14.7% showed interest to go for prenatal screening. Similar, high rates of aversion to go for prenatal screening have also been demonstrated in another study conducted among Aroras of North India [17]. Less than half of the participants (43%) stated that they would terminate their pregnancy in case the foetus is identified to have thalassemia major as it is better than letting the child suffer lifelong, however this percentage is quite lower than what has been observed in a study conducted in West Bengal study (81.31%) [14]. Given that the study population comprises relatively young individuals, they may not yet have developed the cognitive and emotional maturity to fully comprehend the implications of pregnancy termination in the context of genetic disorders, often associating it more with moral and ethical considerations. This can be further evidenced by another study conducted on university students where 75.9% disagreed that thalassemic fetus must be aborted [40]. Moreover socio-cultural components play a significant role in framing of this attitude as a study in Pakistan found that 86.1% of parents were aware of pregnancy termination for positive prenatal tests, but only 60% considered it religiously acceptable [31]. The practice of consanguineous marriages was prevalent among the communities of 8.5% participants, which is much higher than the study conducted in Kolkata (4.4%) [14] this can be attributed to the difference in socio-cultural context. Meanwhile, 59.5% are not aware that it increases the genetic load of the disease. Interestingly some individuals who considered consanguineous marriage to be inappropriate attributed unacceptability by social norms, moral restrictions, and religious customary laws to justify their opinion, and are not aware of the role of consanguinity in increase of genetic load of a disease, thereby highlighting the role of socio-cultural practices in shaping opinions of an individual. India being a country with diverse culture showcases a variety of practices in different regions, wherein practice of consanguineous marriage is a contributing factor towards incidence of thalassemia [41]. The Muslims and Hindus of South India are known to practice consanguineous marriage [42] which considerably increases the genetic load of the disease in these communities. However, the study revealed that individuals who had adequate knowledge about beta thalassemia were aware of the effect of consanguinity on genetic load of diseases like beta-thalassemia, and therefore stated they would preferably not engage in consanguineous marriage. The present study highlighted the influence of adequate knowledge on overall positive practice towards thalassemia. At the end of the day, positive practices yield effective outcome towards management of the disease, hence knowledge can shape the practices of an individual towards beta thalassemia contributing to the management of the disease.

In the study sample, 20.3% and 61.2% come from lower and middle-class households, respectively, highlighting the socio-economic diversity of the sample. In terms of religion, 86.8% are Hindus and 9.1% are Muslim, the remaining being from other religion, this aligns with the population composition of Delhi (81.7% Hindus, 12.9% Muslims, and 5.6% others) [43, 44]. The socially marginalized groups were represented as 9.4% from SC and 1.9% from ST. Although these proportions are slightly below the national averages (16.6% SC and 8.6% ST), they hold significance in the context of Delhi, where there are minimal recognized ST communities [45]. Further, 88.4% of the study sample were from undergraduate courses, 10.3% from post graduate courses, meanwhile 1.3% from Ph.D. or higher courses. This is in alignment with the composition of enrollment in overall India, wherein nearly 79 per cent of students are enrolled on undergraduate courses, 12 per cent at the postgraduate level and 0.5 per cent studying for PhD [46]. Hence the study sample provides a reasonable representation of the socio-economic and demographic composition of India, particularly Delhi, however, there are certain limitations that must be acknowledged. The study sample has a higher proportion of female participants, and the study is based on educational institute-based sample instead of a population level sample, which might influence the wide scale generalizability of the outcomes. Future studies can also take into account capturing the qualitative insights such as in-depth interviews or focus group discussions, to gain a richer understanding of the perceptions, attitudes, and social dynamics surrounding thalassemia screening and awareness.

Conclusions

This study underscores the lack of knowledge regarding thalassemia among the college going young adults in Delhi-NCR, with lower socio-economic status and education being important factors of inadequate knowledge. However, despite possession of adequate knowledge the framing of attitude seems to have the influence of social stigmas associated with the disease. The present study also elucidates how knowledge can positively shape the practices towards beta thalassemia thereby highlighting the importance of promoting awareness regarding beta thalassemia among the masses. Hence the ultimate need of the hour is to focus on targeted awareness campaigns among young adults aimed at encouragement of positive attitude, practices, and mitigation of deep-rooted stigma. College healthcare programs, compulsory inclusion of thalassemia in all high school or college student’s curriculum, and community outreach programs can be effective measures in promoting awareness thereby enabling the youth to take informed reproductive decisions. This in turn can set the foundation for efficient outcome in universal screening programs among young adults, which can thereby potentially contribute towards reducing the burden of beta-thalassemia in India and the world as a whole.
