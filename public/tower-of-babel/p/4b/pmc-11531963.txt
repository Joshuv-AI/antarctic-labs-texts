Chromatin lysine acylation: On the path to chromatin homeostasis and genome integrity
Authors: Feng Chen, Xingkai He, Wenchao Xu, Linmin Zhou, Qi Liu, Weicheng Chen, Wei‐Guo Zhu, Jun Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fundamental role of cells in safeguarding the genome's integrity against DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) is crucial for maintaining chromatin homeostasis and the overall genomic stability. Aberrant responses to DNA damage, known as DNA damage responses (DDRs), can result in genomic instability and contribute significantly to tumorigenesis. Unraveling the intricate mechanisms underlying DDRs following severe damage holds the key to identify therapeutic targets for cancer. Chromatin lysine acylation, encompassing diverse modifications such as acetylation, lactylation, crotonylation, succinylation, malonylation, glutarylation, propionylation, and butyrylation, has been extensively studied in the context of DDRs and chromatin homeostasis. Here, we delve into the modifying enzymes and the pivotal roles of lysine acylation and their crosstalk in maintaining chromatin homeostasis and genome integrity in response to DDRs. Moreover, we offer a comprehensive perspective and overview of the latest insights, driven primarily by chromatin acylation modification and associated regulators.Keywords: chromatin homeostasis, DNA damage response, genome integrity, lysine acylation, modification enzymes

INTRODUCTION

The integrity of genome is safeguarded by an intricate signaling network known as DDR. 1 This coordinated process encompasses tailored repair pathways for each DNA lesion type, alongside the activation of cell cycle checkpoints, DDR factor recruitment, and chromatin reorganization. 2 By orchestrating these multilayer signaling cascades, DDR ensures the preservation of genetic information over time.

The core histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 constitute the fundamental building blocks of chromatin. The histones pair up to form an octameric nucleosome core, wrapped by 147 bp of DNA, serving as the basic unit of chromatin organization. 3 Notably, the core histones adopt a spherical shape, with amino‐terminal tails protruding from the nucleosome surface. 4 These unstructured tails feature an array of modified residues, presenting an exposed interface for potential protein–protein interactions. 5 Intriguingly, histone modifications may induce chromatin conformational changes, suggesting tail modifications play a pivotal role in DNA processing. These tail regions encode a distinct histone language, operating sequentially or in combination, to form a “histone code” that is deciphered by other proteins to trigger specific downstream reactions. 6 Actually, extensive literature documents histone post‐translational modifications (PTMs) occurring on the tail regions among different residues, intricately fine‐tuning chromatin structure and function in response to DNA damage. 7 , 8 Given the pivotal role of histone PTMs in chromatin biology, understanding the scope and impact of these modifications in cellular processes, especially for disease management, is crucial.

Over the past decade, major strides have been made in elucidating how histone PTMs intricately influence cell behavior and fate. Recent research also highlights novel nonacetyl lysine acylations, 9 including lactylation, succinylation, crotonylation, propionylation, and butyrylation, which are gaining recognition for their crucial roles (Figure 1). These modifications are increasingly linked to physiological and pathological processes, yet their precise mechanisms remain elusive. 10 , 11 Exploring these emerging lysine acylations and their functional significance remains a vibrant and expanding research frontier, crucial for understanding fundamental cellular mechanisms underlying health and disease. This review offers a comprehensive overview of latest insights, primarily focusing on chromatin acylation modifications in chromatin hemostasis and genome stability maintenance.

An overview of lysine acylation modification connections to the metabolic process. The energy resources, such as glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids, are metabolized by cells to produce different types of acyl‐CoA, which function as donors for chromatin lysine acylation modification in response to DNA‐damaging stress.

PROTEIN LYSINE ACYLATION‐REGULATING ENZYMES

Protein lysine acylations are evolutionarily conserved and reversible PTMs. 5 , 12 Lysine acylation refers to the process of adding an acyl group to lysine residues, while deacylation is the removal of an acyl group. The donors of lysine acylation are usually acyl‐CoA molecules. Over the recent years, metabolic intermediate‐driven protein acylation has emerged as a pivotal field in cellular regulation. 13 , 14 This ascendance primarily stems from the crucial role metabolic intermediates play as indispensable cofactors and regulators in acylation modifications. 14

Most of histone acylations involve the so‐called effectors that can exert the role of depositing (writer) and removing (eraser) specific chromatin modifications. 15 These modifications are commonly regulated by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and deacetylases (HDACs), which catalyze acylation or deacylation. HATs are categorized as nuclear (type A) and cytoplasmic (type B) according to their cellular positioning, 16 with nuclear HATs further divided into three families: the MYST family, comprising TIP60 and human males absent on the first (hMOF); the P300/CBP family, encompassing p300 and CREB‐binding protein (CBP); and the general control nonderepressible 5 (GCN5)‐related N‐acetyltransferase family, represented by GCN5 and p300‐CBP‐associated factor (PCAF). 16 , 17 The HDAC family comprises four classes: I, II, III, and IV. Class I HDACs (HDAC1/2/3/8) are nuclear and ubiquitous, while class IIa HDACs (HDAC 4/5/7/9) shuttle between nucleus and cytoplasm for tissue‐specific functions. HDAC6 and HDAC10 belong to class IIb with two catalytic domains. HDAC11 is class IV due to its dual class I and II activity. 18 Class III HDACs are NAD+‐dependent sirtuins (SIRT1–7), where SIRT1/6/7 localizes in the nucleus, SIRT2 is primary cytoplasmic, and SIRT 3–5 resides in mitochondria. 18 , 19 All the HATs and HDACs play crucial roles in maintaining chromatin homeostasis by directly or indirectly modulating the chromatin acylation (Figure 2).

IMPACT OF LYSINE ACYLATIONS ON CHROMATIN HOMEOSTASIS AND GENOME STABILITY

Acetylation

The causal nexus between histone hyperacetylation and DDR was initially established in 1986. 20 The most evident manifestation of chromatin architecture regulation by histone PTMs is histone lysine acetylation. Histone acetylation neutralizes positive charges, pivotally altering chromatin conformation to a relaxed state. 21 , 22 , 23 This diminishes histone–DNA interactions, unveiling binding sites and augmenting nucleosomal DNA accessibility to enzymes and regulatory proteins. 5 , 12

Recent breakthroughs have deepened our understanding of how histone acetylation dynamically shapes chromatin structure near DSBs and the enzymes governing DDR. 21 , 24 Consequently, thousands of proteins have been identified as acetylation targets, alongside the discovery of proteins that bind to acetyl‐lysine and enzymes that either add or remove the acetyl groups, using acetyl‐CoA as a cofactor. 25 , 26 The relevance of histone (de)acetylation in DSBs highlights its crucial role in regulating acetylation dynamics, vital for understanding DSB repair mechanisms in nuclei and safeguarding the genome from detrimental rearrangements. 27

Later studies reveal that TIP60‐mediated ATM acetylation swiftly triggers DDR repair machineries. Notably, TIP60 suppression impedes ATM acetylation at K3016 residue, which is located in the highly conserved C‐terminal FATC domain adjacent to the kinase domain, thus blocking its kinase activation and preventing ATM‐dependent phosphorylation of crucial DDR substrates. 28 , 29 , 30 ATM‐mediated ATP‐citrate lyase (ACLY) phosphorylation at S455 generates acetyl‐CoA, augmenting H4K16 acetylation at DSBs. This enhancement facilitates HR‐mediated repair by enabling BRCA1 while suppressing P53‐binding protein 1 (53BP1) recruitment. 31 Aligning with this study, our recent study uncovered that pyruvate dehydrogenase E1α (PDHE1α) is rapidly recruited to chromatin in a polyADP‐ribosylation‐dependent manner. 21 , 32 This intricate process is crucial for acetyl‐CoA production, enhancing local chromatin acetylation on DSBs (Figure 3). Consequently, it fosters a relaxed chromatin structure, enabling repair factor loading, strengthening genome stability, and enhancing cancer cells' resilience against DNA‐damaging treatments in vitro and in vivo. By leveraging prompt and localized acetyl‐CoA supplementation, HATs effectively catalyze chromatin lysine acetylation, vital for chromatin reorganization and DDR. 21 , 33 , 34 For instance, upon ionizing radiation (IR) exposure, CBP/P300 constitutively acetylates H2AXK36, enhancing cancer cells' sensitivity to radiotherapy. 34 The comprehensive details of lysine acetylation sites in representative proteins are outlined in Table 1.

Collectively, recent advancements have offered profound insights into the ever‐expanding realm of acetylation‐mediated biological processes, yet many enigmatic aspects remain unexplored. The “access‐repair‐restore” model of chromatin dynamics during DSBs posits varying acetyl‐CoA demands, with the access process necessitating a robust acetyl‐CoA for local chromatin acetylation spanning thousands of kilobases around the DSBs, while the repair‐restore processes require relatively moderate modification of specific factors at DSB sites. It is plausible that different acetyl‐CoA enzymes likely play distinct roles at DSB repair stages, begging for further exploration of their functions, interplay, and compensatory mechanisms during DDR.

Lactylation

Initially dismissed as metabolic waste, lactate—a product of pyruvate breakdown—has emerged as a crucial cancer metabolite, bridging glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, with additional nonmetabolic functions. 35 , 36 In 2019, a study introduced a novel epigenetic modification known as lactylation—the attachment of a lactyl group to the lysine residues in histone tails. 37 Since then, significant efforts have been devoted to elucidate roles of lactylation across various organisms. 36 , 38 , 39 Like other epigenetic modifications, lactylation profoundly alters chromatin architecture, affecting DNA accessibility and regulating gene expression. 37 , 38 , 40

The proposal that P300 functions as a lactyltransferase, enabling histone lactylation via lactyl‐CoA, has been put forth. 37 Nevertheless, enzymes converting lactate to lactyl‐CoA remain undiscovered, and cellular lactyl‐CoA levels are extremely low, 41 hinting at unidentified mechanisms that facilitate lactyl group attachment to lysine residues. Recent studies uncovered a novel function of mitochondrial alanyl‐tRNA synthetases (AARSs), which sense lactate and catalyze protein lactylation directly from lactate, bypassing lactyl‐CoA, due to the structural resemblance between lactate and their cognate substrate, alanine. 42 , 43 , 44 AARS1 was found to have a nuclear localization signal (NLS) region but to normally localize throughout the cytoplasm, while AARS2 has been shown to specifically localize on mitochondria. This lactate‐binding ability of AARSs widens their functional range and regulatory potential for lysine modifiers. 42 , 43 , 44 Collectively, additional studies are needed to address these complicated issues and establish the relationships between different mechanisms of lactylation. Future work aiming at these issues will provide new insights into mechanisms of protein lactylation.

A recent study revealed that MRE11 undergoes lactylation at K673 by CBP in response to DNA damage (Figure 4). This lactylated MRE11 exerts a crucial role in modulating its DNA‐binding ability and subsequent DNA end resection. 45 In line with this observation, lactate‐driven lactylation of NBS1 was reported to promote HR‐mediated DNA repair. Tip60‐catalyzed lactylation of NBS1 at K388 is essential for MRN complex formation and the accumulation of HR repair proteins at the sites of DNA DSBs. 46 PARP1, an enzyme vital for ADP‐ribosylation in DNA repair, also undergoes lactylation, thus boosting its activity. 47 These studies collectively suggest lactylation's regulatory role in the complex machinery of DDR. Additionally, the engagement of histone lactylation in senescence and cell fates is believed to be intricately linked to the dysfunctional regulation of DNA repair and chromatin homeostasis. 38 , 39 , 40 These findings reveal lactylation as a potential regulator of DSB repair, offering new insights into the complex interplay between lactate metabolism and DDR. Succinylation

In 2011, succinylation emerged as a novel PTM altering histone chemistry. 48 Since then, lysine succinylation sites have been identified in linker and core histones, concentrated in globular and C‐terminal domains. 49 , 50 This modification shifts lysine's charge from +1 to −1, profoundly altering chromatin architecture during DDR. 11 , 48 Notably, lysine succinylation occurs in mitochondrial metabolic proteins and nuclear proteins related to DDR, 51 hinting at a crucial role in chromatin homeostasis.

Succinylation primarily occurs via either nonenzymatic or enzyme‐catalyzed reactions (Figure 5). 48 , 51 , 52 Succinyl‐CoA, a pivotal metabolic intermediate, enhances succinylation at specific sites in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms in a concentration‐ but not enzymatic‐dependent manner. 51 Additionally, GCN5 and P300/CBP have been shown to enzymatically catalyze H3K79 and H3K122 succinylation, respectively. 52 , 53 SIRTs play a crucial role in lysine desuccinylation mediated by DDR. Specifically, SIRT5 desuccinylates peroxisomal ACOX1, reducing H2O2 production and mitigating oxidative DNA damage. 54 Furthermore, SIRTs‐catalyzed H3K122 and H4K77 desuccinylation is essential for chromatin compaction during DDR, offering a mechanical insight into roles of desuccinylation in genomic stability. 55 , 56 SIRT5 interacts with and desuccinylates p53 at K120 residue, thereby impeding p53 activation and genomic stability maintenance. 57 This underscores succinylation's role in activating p53 for DNA repair, revealing SIRT5's significance in chromatin homeostasis. Crotonylation

Histone lysine crotonylation (Kcr), a novel acylation modification, was initially discovered in 2011. 58 Functional analyses have underscored the crucial roles of Kcr in diverse biological processes, including gene expression and genome stability. 58 Crotonyl‐CoA, the primary donor of crotonyl, is efficiently converted from crotonate by ACSS2. 59

Recent studies have shed light on the intricate regulatory mechanisms involving enzyme‐dependent Kcr dynamics. Notably, investigations revealed that P300 efficiently catalyzes H3K18 crotonylation, 59 MOF proficiently catalyzes multisite lysine crotonylation, including H3 K4/9/18/K23 and H4K8/12, 60 and GCN5 has been identified as a mediator of crotonylation‐dependent transcription of histones H3 and H4. 61 Intriguingly, PCAF catalyzes global crotonylation, adding yet another dimension to this complex regulatory network. 62 SIRT1–3 have been identified as enzymes hydrolyzing crotonylated histones, with SIRT3 specifically functioning as a decrotonylase to regulate histone Kcr dynamics. 63 Research has shown that depletion of HDAC1/2 results in increased global histone crotonylation at specific promoters but reduced total decrotonylase activity in a context‐dependent manner. 64 Moreover, HDAC1 and HDAC3, but not HDAC2, exhibit decrotonylase activity on nonhistone such as NPM1, indicating that HDAC1 and HDAC3 act as decrotonylases for nonhistone proteins. 62 Hence, the precise underlying mechanisms of HDACs in relation to crotonylation necessitate further elucidation, as they may manifest in a context‐specific manner, encompassing specific gene promoters, the proximity of transcription start sites (TSS), and other pivotal regulatory elements.

Chromodomain Y‐like protein negatively regulates histone Kcr by acting as a crotonyl‐CoA hydratase to convert crotonyl‐CoA to β‐hydroxybutyryl‐CoA. 65 , 66 Upon DDR, CDYL1 reduces H3K9cr levels at DSBs, impeding ENL function and causing transcriptional silencing. 65 Remarkably, blocking CDYL1's hydratase activity restores H3K9cr, alleviating DSB‐induced silencing. 65 Consistently, HDACs downregulate H3K9/27 and H2AK119 crotonylation post DNA damage. 67 , 68 , 69 Notably, CDYL‐modulated nonhistone crotonylation, such as RPA1, is crucial in DDR (Figure 6). RPA1Kcr mutations disrupt ssDNA binding and resection machinery, emphasizing its role in HR‐mediated repair. 70 However, the involvement of other nonhistone proteins in DDR, along with their underlying mechanisms, requires further exploration.

The metabolic regulation of crotonyl‐CoA generation plays a pivotal role in chromatin lysine crotonylation modification in response to DNA damage. In the cellular response to DNA insults, ACSS2 catalyzes the conversion of crotonate into crotonyl‐CoA, which serves as a crucial donor for both histone and nonhistone lysine crotonylation modifications that are intricately linked to the DNA damage response regulation.

Malonylation

Protein malonylation (Kmal), a conserved PTM, was discovered in 2012. 71 Malonyl‐CoA, serving as the primary donor of Kmal, is mainly derived from malonate by Acyl‐CoA synthetase. 72 Notably, malonate, as a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), shows therapeutic potential by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, 73 making it a promising target for future cellular physiology and pathology studies.

Recent advancements in proteomics offered a profound insight into lysine malonylation's intricate regulatory networks in biological processes. 71 , 74 SIRT5 specifically reduces transketolase (TKT) malonylation, boosts TKT activity, and thus suppresses the PPP and reduces R5P for nucleotide synthesis. This, in turn, halts DNA damage and tumor cell growth, 75 highlighting SIRT5‐mediated malonylation's role in DNA damage prevention, nucleotide maintenance, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis.

Given that acylation reverses the charge of the unmodified lysine from positive to negative at physiological pH, it is reasonable to speculate that malonylation might exert similar effects on chromatin structure and dynamics. Kawashima validated this theory, finding that H2AK119mal disrupted chromosome segregation during mitosis, primarily due to reduced proximal histone phosphorylation (Figure 7). 76 However, the precise link between protein malonylation and genomic stability remains unclear. Although protein malonylation has the potential to influence the functionality of specific proteins, notably histones, that are pivotal in maintaining genomic stability, the mechanisms and implications require further rigorous investigation. Future research should prioritize exploring this intricate relationship.

Distinct pathways for the metabolic regulation of malonyl‐CoA, glutaryl‐CoA, butyryl‐CoA, and propionyl‐CoA, culminating in protein lysine acylation modifications that are orchestrated by histone acetyltransferases (HATs). These modifications, each bearing unique functional characteristics, are integral to the cellular response to DNA damage. The subsequent steps leading to these acylation modifications are precisely coordinated, enabling the cell to appropriately adapt and respond to DNA damage.

Glutarylation

In 2014, lysine glutarylation (Kglu) emerged as a novel, reversible, dynamic, and conserved PTM. 77 Glutaryl‐CoA, the primary acyl donor in Kglu, plays a crucial role in lysine and tryptophan metabolism. 77 This modification, similar to Kac in amine linkage, 11 involves adding glutaryl groups to certain lysine residues. 78 The group transfer can alter protein structure, potentially impacting physiological functions and disrupting interactions between glutarylated proteins' lysine side chains and negatively charged molecules. 78 , 79

Recent advancements have uncovered novel computational tools that enable predicting glutarylation sites, 80 notably identifying three Kglu sites (H2BK5/116/120) in the core histone H2B. This revelation hints at the pivotal role of Kglu in chromatin‐mediated processes. 77 This significant research opens new avenues for further biological inquiries into Kglu. For instance, KAT2A interacts with the α‐ketoadipate dehydrogenase (α‐KADH) complex and uses glutaryl‐CoA for H4K91glu, which is erased by Sirt7, and mimicking Kglu by replacing H4K91 with glutamate disrupts nucleosome stability and promotes DNA accessibility (Figure 7). This, in turn, upregulates transcription and causes defects in cell cycle, DNA repair, and telomere silencing. 81 These findings underscore the critical role of H4K91glu in modulating gene expression and chromatin damage. Nonetheless, further research characterizing the involvement of Kglu in DDR and exploring its vital biological process is warranted.

Propionylation and butyrylation

In 2007, lysine propionylation (Kpr) and butyrylation (Kbu) were unequivocally identified as novel PTMs. 82 , 83 These modifications rely on propionyl‐CoA and butyryl‐CoA as donors, respectively, and typically activate transcription. 84 ChIP‐seq analysis showed that H3K14pr and H3K14bu are enriched at active gene promoters, enhancing transcriptional output compared with acetylation. 84

Beyond directly stimulating transcription, butyrylation can inhibit acetylation on H4K5 during spermatogenesis by restricting acetyl group binding to BRDT's bromodomains, potentially affecting male epigenome characteristics. 85 Additionally, in intestinal epithelial cells, propionylation and butyrylation on H3K9 and K27 overlap with active histone modifications, including H3K27ac and H3K4me3, thereby promoting transcription. 86 This study sheds light on the intricate interplay between host intestinal microbiota, the chromatin landscape of intestinal cells, histone acylation modifications, and gene expression regulation. Notably, it has been discovered that P300/CBP and HDAC1/3 regulates the dynamic of H2BK17pr and H2BK17bu (Figure 7). Intriguingly, propionylation has the capacity to reduce total protein levels in both Drosophila and mammalian cells via the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation pathway. This study unveils a novel mechanism where propionylation systematically modulates protein homeostasis, offering insights into Kpr‐ and Kbu‐mediated gut microbiota, epigenetic modifications, and protein regulation. 87

CROSSTALK OF LYSINE ACYLATIONS IN RESPONSE TO DNA DAMAGE

Notably, diverse PTMs, particularly acylation, can occur on identical or adjacent lysine residues in response to cellular stress, highlighting the intricate interplay and dynamic equilibrium among acylation modifications (Figure 8). The crosstalk between these acylation modifications exerts multifaceted impacts on DDR. 47 , 69 , 88 , 89 For instance, due to steric hindrance between acyl groups, acylation on a specific lysine residue may competitively block other acylation modifications. 47 , 69 Additionally, the functional interplay of different acylation modifications can synergistically or antagonistically modulate the activity, stability, and protein–protein interactions of DDR‐related proteins. 88 , 89

A depiction of the distribution of lysine acylation modifications across histones in response to DNA damage is presented. This schematic illustrates a nucleosome, comprising the four canonical histones (H2A, H2B, H3, and H4), along with the linker histone H1. Notably, the covalent acylations occurring at identical lysine sites are conspicuously marked atop the N‐ and C‐terminal tails of each histone residue.

As mentioned previously, PARP1 lysine modifications exert varied regulatory effects on PARP1's ADP‐ribosylation activity. Specifically, PARP1's hyperacetylated state disables its inherent ADP‐ribosylation function, whereas hyperlactylation conversely enhances its activity. Given that both modifications occur at identical sites, it is hypothesized that lactylation may compete with acetylation, thwarting acetylation's regulatory influence and reinstating PARP1's ribosylation activity. This points to a potential role for PARP1 lactylation in the DNA damage repair process. 47 A recent study reported a proteome‐wide analysis and revealed that the acetylation and succinylation are highly overlapped in proteins as well as in some lysine loci in Streptomyces coelicolor, suggesting a coordination crosstalk between acetylation and succinylation to regulate S. coelicolor physiology. 90 Nonetheless, the intricate interplay between acylations and their temporal dynamics in biological processes remains unclear, requiring further investigation.

CONCLUDING REMARKS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Lysine acylation modifications modulate the intricate interactions among histones, chromatin modifiers, and other chromatin‐associated proteins, ultimately influencing genome stability. Abnormalities in lysine acylation have been linked to various diseases. In this review, we emphasize the significance of deciphering the precise regulatory mechanisms underlying lysine acylation and its implications for human health. For future studies delving into lysine acylation, a comprehensive understanding of its intricate regulatory networks is imperative. The remaining scientific inquiries merit further exploration, encompassing but not limited to the following pivotal points:
