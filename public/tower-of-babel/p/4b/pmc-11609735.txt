Amyloban, extracted from Hericium erinaceus, ameliorates social deficits and suppresses the enhanced dopaminergic system in social defeat stress mice
Authors: Tianran Wang, Kazuya Toriumi, Kazuhiro Suzuki, Mitsuhiro Miyashita, Azuna Ozawa, Mayuko Masada, Masanari Itokawa, Makoto Arai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial dysfunctions are common in various psychiatric disorders, including depression, schizophrenia, and autism, and are long‐lasting and difficult to treat. The development of treatments for social impairment is critical for the treatment of several psychiatric disorders. “Amyloban 3399,” a product extracted from the mushroom Hericium erinaceus, markedly improves social dysfunctions in patients with treatment‐resistant schizophrenia and depression. However, the molecular mechanism(s) through which amyloban ameliorates social impairment remains unclear. To clarify this mechanism, in this study, we aimed to establish a mouse model of social defeat stress (SDS) and investigate the effects of amyloban on social deficits. Amyloban administration ameliorated social deficits and the dopamine system activity in SDS mice. These findings suggest that there is a possibility that amyloban may improve social deficits by suppressing the hyperactivation of the dopaminergic system. Amyloban may be an effective treatment for social dysfunctions associated with various psychiatric disorders.Keywords: dopamine metabolite, Hericium erinaceus, schizophrenia, social defeat stress, social dysfunction

INTRODUCTION

Social dysfunction is common in various psychiatric disorders such as social impairment (i.e., avoiding contact with people and participating in fewer social activities); it is one of the main symptoms of depression. Patients with schizophrenia show negative symptoms (mainly social impairment) that are long‐lasting and difficult to treat. 1 Impaired social communication is also the most common symptom of autism 2 and depression. 3 Thus, improving social deficits is crucial in the treatment of several psychiatric disorders. The onset of social deficits has been reported to be related to psychological stress. Several studies have shown that chronic stress, such as social defeat stress (SDS) and restraint stress, cause social deficits in mouse models. 4 , 5 , 6

The administration of Amyloban® 3399 (Sun Medica Co., Ltd.) markedly improves social symptoms in patients with treatment‐resistant schizophrenia. 7 , 8 A case report showed that the administration of amyloban for 2 weeks improved the symptoms associated with schizophrenia, including auditory hallucination, delusion, anxiety, and social dysfunction, without any adverse effects. 8 Overall, the severity score on the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) for schizophrenia improved from 62 to 30. These findings suggest that amyloban may be a novel treatment alternative for treatment‐resistant schizophrenia. The therapeutic effect of amyloban has also been demonstrated in nine patients with schizophrenia 7 ; amyloban substantially improved the average scores on the PANSS. Furthermore, amyloban ameliorates social deficits in patients with depression. 9 These findings suggest that amyloban may be useful for improving social deficits.

The main components of amyloban are hericenones extracted from the mushroom Hericium erinaceus. Hericenones, discovered in 1991, were the first natural compounds reported to promote nerve growth factor synthesis. 10 , 11 Additionally, amyloban inhibits inflammation, 12 reduces cholesterol levels, 13 and prevents platelet aggregation and endoplasmic reticulum stress that lead to cell death. 14 These pharmacological effects of amyloban are thought to exert anti‐aging effects on the brain. Amyloban improves senile dementia in patients with Alzheimer's disease. 15 , 16 However, information about the molecular mechanisms through which amyloban improves stress‐induced social deficits is scant. To this end, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the therapeutic effects of amyloban on stress‐induced social deficits and neurochemical dysfunction using a mouse model of SDS to delineate the molecular mechanism(s) underlying the effects of amyloban.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

C57BL/6J and Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) mice were obtained from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan). All experiments were performed in male mice, following the method of SDS as previously described. 17 The mice (5–6 per cage) were bred in a regulated environment (25°C, 60% humidity) under a 12:12‐h light/dark cycle. Food and tap water were provided ad libitum. SDS experiments were initiated when the test mice were 8–10 weeks old. This study was approved by the President of the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Sciences after a review by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Permission numbers: 21‐011, 22‐004, and 23‐045) and carried out according to the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Sciences Animal Experimentation Regulations. Procedures involving animals and their care were conducted according to the National Research Council's Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, revised in 2011.

Social defeat stress

The SDS experiment (Figure 1A ) was conducted as previously reported. 17 Retired male ICR mice were chosen as aggressors based on their fighting intensity against C57BL/6J mice. These mice were maintained alone for 1 week before the stress period to increase aggression. C57BL/6J mice were physically attacked by aggressive ICR mice once a day for 10 min and then exposed to sensory stimulations such as smell and sound, through a transparent divider, for the remainder of the day for 10 days. The C57BL/6J mice were attacked every day by a different ICR mouse.

Social interaction test

The social interaction test (SIT) consisted of three sessions. Each session lasted 10 min with a 3‐min interval between sessions. Session 1 focused on habituation to the experimental field; mice were placed in a square box of size 60 × 60 cm and depth of 40 cm and allowed to explore freely to acclimatize to the environment. Session 2 involved a pre‐test in which the mice were placed in the same field with an empty wire cage positioned at the center of one wall side. Their exploratory behavior was recorded. In Session 3, the mice were placed in the box again and allowed to freely explore the field with a wire cage containing an unfamiliar ICR mouse. The interaction zone, indicated by the yellow space around the wire cage, and the avoidance zone, represented by the blue space on the wall side, were recorded and analyzed using the EthoVision tracking system (Noldus, Netherlands), as shown in Figure 1C. As reported previously, the data were analyzed as follows: social interaction time = time spent in the interaction zone in session 3, social interaction ratio (SIR) = (time spent in the interaction zone in session 3)/(time spent in the interaction zone in session 2), social avoidance time (SAT) = time spent in the avoidance zone in session 3, difference in SIR = SIR on day 25−SIR on day 11, and difference in SAT = SAT on day 25−SAT on day 11. According to the results of the SIT, the mice were divided into two groups: (a) a susceptible group showing an SIR of less than 100% and (b) a resilient group showing an SIR of more than 100%.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze (EPM) test was conducted using a cross‐shaped platform placed approximately 50 cm above the floor. The two closed arms, each measuring 50 × 10 cm and facing each other, were enclosed within walls, whereas the two open arms of the same size had no walls. The mice were allowed to freely explore the apparatus for 10 min, and their behavior was recorded and analyzed using the EthoVision tracking system. The number of entries and time spent in the open arm were measured. The difference in open arm entry was analyzed as follows: difference in open arm entry = Open arm entries on day 25−open arm entries on day 11.

Amyloban administration

Amyloban tablets (lot no. 016006) were crushed and mixed with the mouse diet. The dose of amyloban administered was the same as that administered to a patient with schizophrenia (2220 mg/60 kg/day = 1.11 mg/30 g/day). After SDS, the mice were maintained individually during the amyloban administration period. As the mice ate approximately 3 g of the diet per day (Figure 1A), 0.37 mg of amyloban was administered per gram of the diet. The mice in the susceptible group were administered amyloban from days 11 to 25 (Figure 1B).

Measurement of monoamines and amino acids in the mouse brain

The brain tissue from mice was collected on day 26, the day after the social interaction test on day 25 (Figure 1B). The treatment of amyloban was continued until sample collection. In this study, to assess the effect of amyloban on the basal metabolism of monoamines, we chose to perform brain sampling the day after the social interaction test rather than immediately after the test on day 25. The levels of monoamines and amino acids were analyzed using a high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with an electrochemical detector (HITEC500; Eicom, Kyoto, Japan). Each frozen brain sample was weighed and homogenized with an ultrasonic processor in 0.2 M perchloric acid containing isoproterenol as an internal standard. The homogenates were then cooled on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 20 000× g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were combined with 1 M sodium acetate (pH 3.0), passed through a 0.22‐μm filter (Merck Millipore, MA, USA), and injected into an HPLC system equipped with a reversed‐phase octadecylsilyl column (Eicompak SC‐5ODS; 150 × 2.1 mm; Eicom, Kyoto, Japan) and an electrochemical detector.

To measure monoamines and their metabolites, the column temperature was maintained at 25°C and the detector potential was set to +750 mV. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 M citric acid and 0.1 M sodium acetate (pH 3.6), containing 14% methanol, 180 mg/L sodium‐1‐octanesulfonate, and 5 mg/L EDTA, with a flow rate of 0.23 mL/min.

To measure amino acid levels, the column temperature was set to 30°C and the detector potential was adjusted to +600 mV. The mobile phase consisted of 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), containing 30% methanol and 5 mg/L EDTA, at a flow rate 0.23 mL/min. Before quantification, each sample was mixed with the same amount of 4 mM OPA‐2ME (diluted with 0.1 M carbonate buffer) and allowed to stand for 10 min.

The content of each monoamine and amino acid is expressed as a ratio to the content in the no SDS group. The turnover of monoamine metabolism was calculated by first dividing the content of the metabolite by the content of the precursor for each individual and is expressed as a ratio of the average turnover in the no SDS group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences between groups were assessed using Student's t‐test. Statistical differences among more than three groups were assessed using a one‐way or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. A two‐sided F‐test was used to confirm that the data were normally distributed. Statistical tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. The sample size was determined based on the results of our previous study. Results are reported as mean ± SEM. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

SDS causes social deficits

To investigate the effects of amyloban on stress‐induced social deficits, we generated a mouse model using SDS. As shown in Figure 1A,B, mice were subjected to SDS for 10 days, and their stress vulnerabilities were assessed using the SIT on day 11 (Figure 1C). Some mice showed decreased SIR and enhanced SAT after SDS (SDS‐susceptible), whereas the others showed no change in social interaction or avoidance (SDS‐resilient) (Figure 1D,E). These results are consistent with those of a previous study, 17 suggesting that a mouse model of stress was successfully generated.

Amyloban treatment ameliorated social deficits and anxiety caused by SDS

After SIT on day 11, the SDS‐susceptible mice were divided into two groups with equal mean values based on SIR score (Figure 2C, Figure S1) and fed a normal diet (SDS group) or an amyloban‐containing diet (SDS/Amyloban) from day 12. No changes in food intake or body weight were observed during SDS or amyloban administration (Figure 2A,B ). The SDS group showed a significantly lower SIR than the no SDS group 2 weeks after SDS, whereas the SDS/Amyloban group showed an increase in the SIR, resulting in no significant difference between the no SDS and SDS/Amyloban groups (Figure 2D). Furthermore, the SDS/Amyloban group showed a significant increase in SIR on day 25 compared to day 11 (Figure 2E), with a significant interaction effect (treatment × day) in a two‐way ANOVA (p = 0.0392). The SDS/Amyloban group also exhibited a significant increase in the mean difference in SIR after 2 weeks of amyloban treatment (Figure 2F). In contrast, the SDS/Amyloban group did not show suppression of the enhanced SAT on day 25, and the interaction was not significant (p = 0.3879) (Figure 2G–I). However, the SDS/Amyloban group showed a trend toward a decrease in the mean difference in SAT after 2 weeks of amyloban treatment (p = 0.06) (Figure 2J). These findings suggest that amyloban at least partially effective in stress‐induced social deficits.

We further examined the effect of amyloban on SDS‐induced anxiety using the EPM test. On day 11, SDS exposure resulted in a reduction in the number of open arm entries (Figure 2K), indicating enhanced anxiety. By day 25, the SDS/Amyloban group showed no significant improvement compared to the SDS group (Figure 2L), with no significant interaction observed (p = 0.3714) (Figure 2M). Although the SDS/Amyloban group showed a trend toward an increase in the mean difference in open arm entries after 2 weeks of amyloban treatment (p = 0.09) (Figure 2N), these results suggest that amyloban may not significantly alleviate anxiety.

Amyloban treatment restored dopamine turnover in the hippocampus

First, to investigate the effect of amyloban on weight gain and behavior in naïve mice, C57BL/6J mice were fed amyloban for 14 days (Figure S1). There were no changes in food intake or body weight during amyloban administration (Figure S1B,C). Moreover, there was no change in activity, anxiety‐like behavior, and spatial recognition memory (Figure S1D–G) between the groups.

Next, to clarify the neurochemical changes through which amyloban ameliorated the behavioral impairments, the levels of monoamines and their metabolites in the prefrontal cortex (Pfc), hippocampus (Hip), nucleus accumbens (Nac), and striatum (Str), which are strongly related to sociality, emotional motivation, and anxiety, were measured using HPLC (Figure 3, Figure S3). No changes in dopamine (DA) levels were detected in any brain area (Figure 3A). The levels of the DA metabolites, 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC) and 3‐methoxytyramine (3MT), but not homovanillic acid (HVA), in the Hip were increased by SDS, and this increase was reversed by amyloban administration (Figure 3B,C). To determine functional alterations in the dopaminergic neuronal system, DA turnover was evaluated by calculating the ratio of total tissue DA levels to DA metabolite levels. DA turnover represented by DOPAC/DA and 3MT/DA in the Hip was also increased by SDS, and the effect was reversed by amyloban administration (Figure 3E,F). Moreover, DA turnover represented by HVA/DA in the Pfc, but not in the Hip, was increased by SDS, and the effect was reversed by amyloban (Figure 3G). Similarly, the levels of monoamines, their metabolites, and their turnover in the noradrenaline and serotonin (5‐HT) systems were examined; however, we found no changes in their levels under SDS. We also quantified amino acids in the brain and found almost no changes, except in the level of glutamine in the Pfc (Figure S4). These findings suggest that amyloban can suppress the upregulated dopaminergic system function in the Hip or Pfc.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we showed that amyloban could ameliorate social deficits (Figure 2) and decrease the increased DA metabolite levels and turnover (Figure 3) in SDS mice. The therapeutic effect of amyloban on social deficits was consistent with the clinical findings in cases of schizophrenia. 8 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the effect of amyloban on social deficits and the dopaminergic system. In schizophrenia, negative symptoms, including social dysfunctions, last longer than positive symptoms and are more difficult to treat. 18 Social difficulties that persist even after the occurrence of positive symptoms, including delusions, are resolved with antipsychotic drugs, 19 , 20 leading to chronic functional impairment. 21 , 22 Therefore, the effect of amyloban on the recovery of social functions is clinically beneficial for subsequent patient recovery.

Hip, which was mainly affected by SDS and recovered by amyloban in this study, is involved in social impairments observed in psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia, 23 depression, 24 and autism. 25 Grace et al. 26 reported hippocampal hyperactivity in a mouse model of schizophrenia, and several studies have suggested that the Hip, but not the Pfc, is a vulnerable region for social dysfunction in schizophrenia. 23 , 24 , 26 , 27 Although the Hip is thought to be a brain region for learning and memory, it has recently been reported to be strongly related to social and cognitive functions. 25 , 28 , 29 , 30 Our findings suggest that there is a possibility that amyloban may improve social deficits in SDS mice by suppressing the hyperactivation of the dopaminergic system in the Hip. However, the causal relationship cannot be confirmed from the present study results. Future studies should further clarify the relationship between the hippocampal dopaminergic system and social behavior.

Amyloban has the potential to ameliorate social deficits by exerting anti‐inflammatory effects. SDS induces chronic inflammation throughout the body, including the brain. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 SDS causes neuronal damage in the Hip via neuroinflammation. 36 Chronic inflammation leads to behavioral abnormalities, particularly social deficits, in mice. 37 , 38 As amyloban is known to exert anti‐inflammatory effects by suppressing inflammatory response and neuronal cell death, 12 , 39 it is possible that amyloban ameliorates SDS‐induced social deficits by suppressing inflammation in the brain. Moreover, inflammation caused by microinjection of the proinflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNFα) enhances DA metabolism. 40 The findings of these studies suggest that the anti‐inflammatory effect of amyloban may ameliorate social deficits and improve the functions of the hippocampal dopaminergic system in SDS mice.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that amyloban treatment improves social deficits and ameliorates the upregulated activity of the dopaminergic system in SDS mice. These results suggest that amyloban may be effective against stress‐induced social impairment. In the future, it will be necessary to verify the relationship between social deficits and hyperactivation of the dopaminergic system in the Hip. Furthermore, we believe that the effects of amyloban on the DA system can be clarified through clinical studies involving patients with schizophrenia.
