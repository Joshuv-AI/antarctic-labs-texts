Impact of intraoperative explanation combined with surgical video review on anatomy learning in laparoscopic distal gastrectomy
Authors: Yumei Ding, Xin Liu, Yuxuan Cai, Fangyuan Cheng, Liang Shi, Ke Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the educational value of combining intraoperative explanations with laparoscopic surgical video reviews for teaching the local anatomy of the stomach.MethodsThirty resident physicians undergoing standardised training in our hospital, including undergraduate resident physicians and clinical-type postgraduates in surgery who had not participated in radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer, were selected as study subjects. They were randomly divided into an experimental group and a control group, with 15 participants in each group. The experimental group received instruction through intraoperative explanations combined with laparoscopic surgical video reviews, while the control group participated in surgery without additional instructional methods. After the teaching sessions, both groups underwent clinical practice assessments, theoretical examinations and evaluations of teaching satisfaction. The distribution of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test along with Quantile-Quantile plots. Two-way analysis of variance was employed to evaluate the main effects and interaction effects of clinical practice scores across different groups and student types. Statistical power was examined through post hoc power analysis.ResultsThe clinical practice assessment results, theoretical examination scores and teaching satisfaction ratings of the experimental group were significantly higher than those of the control group, with all differences reaching statistical significance (p<0.05).ConclusionCombining intraoperative explanations with laparoscopic surgical video reviews for teaching local anatomy during distal gastrectomy is more effective than participation in surgery alone. This approach allows students to review surgical videos at their convenience, enhancing their understanding and mastery of local anatomical structures and significantly improving the quality of local anatomy education for resident physicians.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of minimally invasive techniques in surgical practice, laparoscopic surgery has become a pivotal approach in gastrointestinal surgery. These procedures offer significant advantages, including minimal trauma, faster recovery and fewer postoperative complications. Additionally, laparoscopic surgeries provide complete video recordings, which serve as valuable resources for medical education.1 For resident physicians, these videos can greatly enhance their understanding and mastery of complex surgical procedures and anatomical structures. Previous studies have demonstrated that using surgical videos or video clips to supplement traditional teaching methods can effectively enhance the surgical skills of resident physicians.2 However, due to the limited duration of exposure to specialised clinical knowledge, particularly concerning local anatomical structures in gastrointestinal surgeries, self-directed learning from surgical videos alone often yields suboptimal results.3 Therefore, despite significant advancements in surgical instruments and techniques, operating room training remains essential for developing surgical skills, especially for complex procedures.4

Numerous studies have examined how different teaching methods influence laparoscopic surgical training for doctors, though most have focused on single interventions or traditional anatomy-based approaches.5 For example, Mitesh Sharma et al conducted a randomised controlled trial showing that basic laparoscopic skills training using fresh-frozen cadavers significantly improved trainees’ tissue manipulation accuracy.6 Similarly, John et al found that a multimodule preoperative training programme accelerated skill acquisition among trainees in laparoscopic colorectal surgery, significantly increasing operative competence and reducing error rates.7 Traditional anatomy teaching methods mainly rely on textbook images, anatomical specimens and animal experiments, but these approaches have limitations in simulating surgical scenarios and providing authentic anatomical perception.8 Although anatomical specimen training can improve operational precision, its high cost and inability to simulate dynamic surgical processes make it difficult for young surgeons to develop profound surgical memory and clinical skills due to the lack of dynamic and real-time perceptual teaching. Therefore, compared with traditional anatomy teaching, dynamic visual teaching better meets the learning needs of laparoscopic surgery.

Intraoperative explanations, as a real-time teaching method, integrate local anatomical details with surgical operations, offering synchronous learning and practice opportunities. This approach allows resident physicians to enhance their understanding of local anatomy and surgical workflows by following the operating surgeon’s thought process.9 However, while intraoperative explanations offer immediacy and specificity, they depend heavily on the surgeon’s on-site teaching ability and are constrained by time. This limits their effectiveness in ensuring that resident physicians acquire a comprehensive understanding of all anatomical details. To address these limitations, incorporating laparoscopic surgery video reviews into anatomical education has proven to be a valuable supplement. Postoperative reviews of critical surgical steps enable physicians to repeatedly observe and reinforce their recognition of essential anatomical structures.10 Due to patient safety concerns and surgeons’ workload, a prolonged learning curve is inevitable. Therefore, it is essential to improve standardised training for residents’ proficiency in laparoscopic surgery and shorten the learning curve. However, the impact of intraoperative teaching combined with postoperative video review on trainees’ mastery of laparoscopic surgical skills remains to be validated. Building on the strengths of both teaching methods, this study explores the application and educational value of combining intraoperative explanations and laparoscopic surgery video reviews for teaching local anatomy and surgical processes, using distal gastrectomy for advanced gastric cancer as an example. The diversified visual dynamic integrated teaching approach is destined to gradually expand and flourish in future surgical education and even across the entire medical education landscape. This will require extensive experimentation and research exploration to drive its development, thereby enhancing the efficiency of clinical medical education and improving physicians' comprehensive competency.

Research subjects and methods

Research subjects

A total of 30 resident physicians undergoing standardised training in the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery at Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, between January and December 2022, were selected as study participants. All participants were either undergraduate resident physicians (who entered standardised training after completing a bachelor’s degree) or clinical-type postgraduates (professional degree Master’s students) who had not previously performed a distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer before their rotation in the department. The participants were randomly assigned to an experimental group and a control group using the random number table method, with 15 participants in each group. Comparisons of general characteristics such as age and gender between the two groups showed no statistically significant differences (p>0.05), indicating comparability. The surgeries were conducted by two senior attending physicians or associate chief physicians from our hospital’s Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, each with over 10 years of experience and proficient in independently performing laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer. All procedures in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Medical College, Affiliated with Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (2022IEC-094). Prior to this study, all participants provided written informed consent. The research methods used conform to the standards stipulated in the Helsinki Declaration.

Research methods

Control group

In the control group, resident physicians participated as assistants, holding the laparoscope during surgery. The primary surgeon provided intraoperative explanations about the surgical procedure, adjacent organs, anatomical layers and spaces, blood vessels and lymph nodes. Each resident physician assisted in one laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer.

Experimental group

In addition to their role in laparoscopic surgery, similar to the control group, resident physicians in the experimental group were required to study a video of a laparoscopic distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer. The surgery was recorded and compiled by the primary surgeon using laparoscopic equipment from our hospital. The 15-min video concisely and comprehensively presented the surgical process, emphasising key anatomical structures. It included detailed explanations and annotations of critical steps and important anatomical landmarks. Each resident physician in the experimental group was required to watch the video at least three times postoperatively, with the timing and location arranged at their discretion.

Observation indicators

Clinical practice assessment

The final laparoscopic distal gastrectomy video for gastric cancer surgery was selected for clinical practice assessment. During playback, the primary surgeon posed questions to resident physicians regarding each group of dissected lymph nodes and each major blood vessel identified during the procedure. The residents’ responses were recorded and scored. Each group of lymph nodes, major blood vessels and key adjacent organs was assigned 2 points. Specifically, the following items were each worth 2 points: left gastric omental artery, left gastric vein, right gastric omental artery, gastroduodenal artery, right gastric omental vein, posterior gastric artery, common hepatic artery, right gastric artery, hepatic artery proper, portal vein, spleen, pancreas and duodenum. This resulted in a total possible score of 30 points for the clinical practice assessment.

Theoretical examination

A theoretical examination was administered to evaluate the anatomical aspects of distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer. The exam was graded on a 100-point scale, with scores categorised into four levels: 0–59 points as fail, 60–79 points as pass, 80–89 points as good and 90–100 points as excellent.

Teaching satisfaction survey

A teaching satisfaction survey was conducted among resident physicians in both groups using a structured questionnaire. The survey assessed various aspects, including the enrichment of learning methods, increased interest in learning, deeper understanding and mastery of the subject matter, improved learning efficiency and quality, enhanced learning motivation and overall satisfaction with the teaching methods. The total satisfaction score ranged from 0 to 100 points, categorised as follows: 0–59 points as unsatisfied, 60–79 points as generally satisfied, 80–89 points as satisfied and 90–100 points as very satisfied.

Statistical methods

The data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 and R Studio 4.2.2 statistical software. Measurement data are expressed as mean±standard deviation (x¯±s). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (reporting the statistic W and P-value) and Quantile-Quantile (Q-Q) plots. Intergroup comparisons were performed using an independent samples t-test or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (reporting F values and P values for main effects and interaction effects), with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Post hoc power analysis was conducted to evaluate the ability to detect specific effects given the current sample size, calculating statistical power based on the observed effect size (Cohen’s d), significance level (α=0.05) and the actual sample size. The statistical power values (1-β) represent the probability values of post hoc test power and are usually reported in percentage form. The analysis was implemented using functions from the ‘pwr’ package in R.

The analysis of differences in clinical practice scores across groups is shown in figure 2. For most anatomical structures, the recognition rates were relatively high across all groups, particularly for the left gastric vein, left gastric artery, common hepatic artery and proper hepatic artery—all of which were correctly identified by physicians in every group. The overall scores in the control group were lower than those in the experimental group, especially in the identification of the left gastroepiploic vein and posterior gastric artery, where the experimental group had significantly more correct recognitions. However, for the gastroduodenal artery, portal vein and posterior gastric artery, both groups showed low recognition rates. Even in the experimental group with the combined teaching approach, only a few participants correctly identified these structures—specifically, the portal vein and gastroduodenal artery were correctly identified by only five and seven individuals, respectively.

Discussion

For surgical specialists in gastroenterology, possessing both solid theoretical knowledge and proficient clinical skills is essential. One of the most crucial aspects of learning surgical procedures is mastering local anatomy. Accurate understanding of anatomical landmarks ensures surgical safety and success. Traditional teaching methods for local anatomy typically rely on images, human specimens and animal experiments. However, these methods often lack three-dimensional and visual engagement. The combined approach of intraoperative instruction and laparoscopic video review serves as an effective intervention to enhance trainees’ proficiency in laparoscopic surgery. Compared with single-intervention methods11 and traditional teaching approaches, this dual-modality training enables surgical residents to acquire laparoscopic skills more rapidly while reducing both error rates and the required number of training cases. This integrated teaching strategy not only diversifies learning modalities and strengthens perceptual understanding but also reinforces trainees’ comprehension of regional anatomy and surgical procedures. By enhancing cognitive engagement with the material, the method stimulates learning motivation and significantly improves educational efficiency.

A review on the effectiveness of video teaching in surgical education shows that adding videos or 3D animations to simulation training has positive effects on training time, learning time, mastery of surgical skills and trainee satisfaction.12 The findings of this study indicate that combining intraoperative explanations with laparoscopic surgery video reviews significantly enhances residents' clinical practice abilities, theoretical exam scores and teaching satisfaction in the context of local anatomy instruction for distal gastrectomy. Especially in terms of clinical practice ability, the experimental group’s clinical practice scores significantly improved, verifying that the method of combining intraoperative explanation with video review can effectively promote trainees’ mastery of laparoscopic distal gastrectomy. Through real-time intraoperative explanation and repeated viewing of marked videos, trainees can strengthen their understanding of laparoscopic surgery and complex anatomical knowledge. These results align with similar studies, underscoring the importance of diversified teaching methods in medical education.13 14

The subgroup analysis (table 2) demonstrated that, regardless of whether the participants were clinical postgraduates or undergraduate resident physicians, the experimental group significantly outperformed the control group in clinical practice, theoretical assessments and teaching satisfaction (all p<0.05). This is similar to the findings of Yeung et al, who discovered that the video group outperformed the text group in terms of task completion time and task comprehension.15 However, in the study by Yoganathan et al, the 3D virtual reality video group scored significantly higher than the 2D video group in the evaluation of single-handed reef knot ligation,16 which suggests that the format and effectiveness of video teaching may vary in different surgical skills training. Moreover, the two-way ANOVA results indicated no significant difference in the intervention effect across different types of trainees (interaction p=1.000), suggesting that the combined teaching approach is universally applicable to standardised training physicians with diverse backgrounds. However, figure 2 revealed that although the experimental group showed significantly improved identification rates for conventional structures such as the left gastric vein and common hepatic artery (approaching 100%), both groups still struggled with mastering uncommon anatomical structures like the portal vein and gastroduodenal artery (identification rates ≤50%). These discrepancies may stem from the following reasons: (1) some anatomical structures are not commonly encountered in routine clinical diagnosis and treatment. These blood vessels are rarely seen in actual anatomical dissections, and young doctors in training have limited exposure to them, resulting in insufficient experience. (2) The superior performance of clinical-type postgraduates (Master’s level) in theoretical assessments may be attributed to their structured curriculum, which includes dedicated coursework in surgical anatomy and oncology. In contrast, undergraduate-based resident physicians typically enter standardised training immediately after completing their bachelor’s degrees, with limited prior exposure to advanced anatomical concepts specific to gastric cancer surgery. This foundational knowledge gap underscores the importance of targeted interventions, such as video review, to bridge differences in baseline competency. Moreover, the differences in cognitive load among students also lead to varying levels of mastery.17 Therefore, the teaching approach combining intraoperative instruction with postoperative video review holds substantial potential for application and development in surgical education.

Intraoperative teaching offers a dynamic educational approach, enabling residents to directly observe anatomical structures and surgical procedures in real time. This method enhances situational awareness and facilitates the integration of theoretical knowledge with practical application.18 It effectively addresses limitations inherent in traditional teaching methods, particularly in developing residents’ spatial understanding of complex anatomical structures.19 However, intraoperative teaching has limitations due to the rapid pace of surgery and the surgeon’s primary focus on the procedure, which may cause residents to miss critical information.

Postoperative laparoscopic video reviews address this gap by allowing repeated viewing, enabling residents to independently study essential structures and key surgical steps.20 Annotated videos highlight critical aspects, deepening residents’ understanding and retention. The visual impact of surgical videos far surpasses that of static images or textual explanations,21 particularly in illustrating the layered nature of anatomical structures.

Additionally, this study shows that compared with intraoperative explanations alone, combining surgical video reviews significantly increases residents’ interest and initiative in learning. Survey results indicate that this combination markedly improves residents’ learning experience and boosts their confidence in performing surgical operations. Research demonstrates that diversity and autonomy in learning enhance outcomes, and video reviews effectively provide this diverse learning approach.22 It should be noted that the effectiveness of video teaching may vary across different studies. For instance, some research has indicated that video teaching may not be as effective as traditional simulation training in enhancing surgical skills.23 24 This could be attributed to the fact that simulation training provides a more realistic surgical experience, while video teaching tends to focus more on theoretical knowledge and visual perception. Therefore, in practical applications, it is essential to select appropriate teaching methods based on specific needs and conditions.

This study has several limitations. First, the nature of the educational intervention meant both instructors and trainees were aware of group assignments, potentially introducing performance bias. Second, individual variations in surgeons’ skills and patients’ anatomical complexity may have influenced teaching outcomes. Third, the limited number of trainees from a single medical centre may affect statistical power and external validity. Furthermore, as an exploratory study, the sample size was determined primarily by clinical practicality and feasible recruitment over a fixed period, rather than by a formal a priori power calculation. This approach is common in initial educational intervention studies where preliminary effect size estimates for power calculation are often unavailable. Nevertheless, this study provides detailed effect size estimates and post hoc power analyses, which demonstrate high sensitivity in detecting the large effect sizes observed. Future definitive trials would benefit from a priori sample size estimations based on the effect sizes reported here to ensure adequate statistical power. Fourth, real-world clinical teaching typically involves multifaceted, personalised approaches, and our standardised intervention may not fully reflect this complexity. Finally, due to the cost and feasibility constraints, the follow-up period was relatively short, potentially missing the long-term effects and delayed outcomes of the intervention.

Nevertheless, this study represents the first validation of a combined intervention model for distal gastrectomy training, closely replicating actual surgical residency conditions. Provides a novel perspective for surgical teaching by demonstrating the broad applicability of a combined teaching method, integrating intraoperative explanation with postoperative video review, among learners from diverse backgrounds. The results indicated that the experimental group demonstrated superior performance in clinical practice assessments, theoretical examinations and overall satisfaction compared with the control group. This method enhanced the physicians’ recognition of common anatomical structures and deepened their understanding of complex anatomy. Furthermore, this validated approach can shorten the learning curve for trainees, improve training efficiency, address clinical education resource constraints and pave the way for intelligent, digital surgical teaching methods. Therefore, this instructional model could be extended to other complex procedures and anatomical training to further enhance trainees’ comprehensive competencies and clinical skills

Conclusion

In conclusion, compared with simply observing surgical procedures, the teaching model that integrates intraoperative explanations with surgical video review significantly improves standardised training physicians’ understanding and mastery of the anatomical structures involved in distal gastrectomy, enhances their clinical surgical skills and elevates both teaching effectiveness and learning outcomes. This approach not only diversifies learning methods but also allows for repeated review through video playback, reinforcing comprehension and retention of complex anatomical knowledge. Additionally, this teaching model is universally adaptable, making it suitable for trainees with varying backgrounds and skill levels. Thus, it is worth considering the broader implementation of this method in teaching other complex surgical procedures and anatomical concepts. Doing so would further strengthen trainees’ overall clinical competence while providing a scalable, digitally enhanced solution for surgical education.
