Patient Care Pathways and Outcomes Following Newly Diagnosed Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Emily P. Zeitler, Larry R. Jackson, Andrea Natale, Jonathan P. Piccini, Sean D. Pokorney, Andrea M. Russo, Roopinder K. Sandhu, Rahul Khanna, Maximiliano Iglesias, Yiran Rong, T. Jared Bunch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCare pathways for patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) are poorly understood and may influence the likelihood of guideline concordant oral anticoagulation or antiarrhythmic drug therapy and associated outcomes.MethodsAdult patients in the Optum Clinformatics Database (2015–2023) with incident AF were identified. Care pathways, by treating clinician specialty (primary care, cardiology, electrophysiology) during each AF‐related visit in the 1 year post diagnosis were examined. Cox regression models were used to assess associations between an electrophysiologist visit and AF‐related treatments including antiarrhythmic drug therapy and oral anticoagulation and outcomes including AF‐related hospitalizations, heart failure hospitalizations, and stroke.ResultsOf the 37 370 patients included, 7700 (20.6%) had a care pathway including an electrophysiologist visit. Older patients (70–79 and 80+ versus 18–49 years: hazard ratio [HR], 0.71 [95% CI, 0.64–0.78] and HR, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.51–0.63], respectively) and those with a higher CHA2DS2‐VASc score (2–3 and ≥4 versus 0–1: HR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.74–0.85] and HR, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.68–0.84], respectively) were less likely to have an electrophysiologist visit. Electrophysiologist visits were associated with a significant increase in rates of treatment with antiarrhythmic drug and oral anticoagulation. Patients aged ≥80 years with an electrophysiologist visit had significantly lower risks of stroke compared with those without an electrophysiologist visit (HR, 0.63 [95% CI, 0.42–0.95]).ConclusionsCertain demographic groups including older patients had lesser likelihood of electrophysiologist consultation, but guideline concordant care was more likely associated with electrophysiology care pathways. Electrophysiology care pathways were associated with improved outcomes especially among the groups with greatest stroke risk.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia in the United States and globally. 1 It has a sweeping impact on nearly all aspects of health care ranging from morbidity and mortality 2 to health care resource use 3 to quality of life. 4 Prevalence of AF continues to increase among Americans with a recent estimate among all American adults approaching 5%. 5 Age is a powerful risk factor for AF such that prevalence of AF increases steadily with age, and among the oldest Americans (90+ years), AF prevalence more than 20%. 6

At the patient level, the impact of AF can be catastrophic; population estimates suggest that stroke may be the initial manifestation of AF at a rate of 2 to 5 per 10 000 person‐years. 7 Once AF is identified, treatment is focused on managing risk factors through lifestyle modifications, reducing AF‐related symptoms with rate and rhythm control, limiting AF burden, and lessening AF‐related stroke risk. 8 This work is intensive and multidisciplinary often involving clinicians with a variety of backgrounds, training, and practice settings. As management strategies for AF have expanded, there is increasing opportunity for clinical and technical specialization. Thus, there is substantial variability in referral patterns and treatment that may impact patient outcomes that can create or enhance disparities in care. Indeed, variability in care pathways and guideline‐supported treatments of common cardiovascular conditions, like heart failure (HF), have previously been observed with associated impact on clinical outcomes. 9 , 10 Ideal care pathways have been proposed with recommendations for treatment goals, but these are aspirational for many clinical groups and out of reach for many Americans. 11 , 12

The objectives of this work are 3‐fold: first, define the current landscape of AF referral pathways in a US population with attention to the engagement of specialty care; second, explore treatment patterns based on these various referral patterns with a focus on guideline‐concordant antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) therapy and oral anticoagulation (OAC) overall and within important demographic subgroups with and without exposure to specialty care; and third, compare clinical outcomes based on these referral and treatment patterns.

METHODS

Data Sources

Data were obtained from the deidentified Optum Clinformatics Data Mart‐Socioeconomic Status Database. Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to Optum. Data were restricted to the period from 2015 to 2023. This period was selected because it narrowed the coding to International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) through the most recently available data. Optum comprises health insurance claims data from a combination of US private insurance and Medicare Advantage beneficiaries from geographically diverse regions across the United States. The database contains deidentified data derived from health plan members' enrollment data and facility, physician, and pharmacy claims. This analysis of the Optum Socioeconomic Status database was conducted under exemption from institutional review board oversight for US‐based studies using deidentified health care records, as dictated by Title 45 Code of Federal Regulations (45 CFR 46.101(b)(4)) (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR‐2011‐title45‐vol1/pdf/CFR‐2011‐title45‐vol1.pdf).

Populations of Interest

Patients were selected for inclusion from Optum based on several factors. The cohort was narrowed to adult patients (≥18 years) with a primary diagnosis of AF based on ICD‐10‐Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) I48.0 (paroxysmal AF), I48.1x (persistent AF), I48.2x (permanent AF), I48.91 (unspecified AF) between January 2016 and June 2022 (“index”). This period was selected to confine the analysis to the period after the transition to ICD‐10‐CM when AF subtype could be gleaned from codes as listed here and to reflect contemporary AF management practices. Patients were excluded if any of the following criteria were met (in this order) at any time before the index diagnosis: diagnosis of AF or atrial flutter before index, AF‐related catheter ablation before index, pharmacy claim for an AAD, pharmacy claim for OAC, no visit with AF as the primary diagnosis in the 12 months post incident AF, absence of continuous enrollment for 6 months before and 12 months after index, absence of clinician specialty information at index or any AF‐related visit within the year following incident diagnosis, and unknown baseline characteristics.

A subgroup analysis was planned in the population of included patients at least 80 years old due to the high prevalence of AF in this population 5 , 6 accounting for ∼50% of incident cases among Americans per year with an associated high risk of AF‐related stroke. These factors were expected to clarify any potential associations between exposures (ie, specialty care) and outcomes.

Outcomes of Interest

Primary Outcomes

In order to describe AF pathways, the specialty of each clinician involved in the AF care pathway in the 12 months following incident diagnosis was recorded. Clinician specialty is coded in the taxonomy codes in the Optum database. In addition, the type of clinician at each node in the pathway was recorded based on training: physician versus advanced practice practitioner (APP). If a clinician was found to have multiple taxonomy codes to suggest potential variation in clinical practice, their final specialty was determined by the priority order of electrophysiology > cardiology > primary care physician > APP. Multiple adjacent visits to the same clinician type were grouped together. The time between visits was also recorded.

Secondary Outcomes

Treatments and outcomes associated with variable care pathways were assessed. New prescription fills for AADs were tabulated as was new prescription fills for OAC among those patients with CHA2DS2‐VASc score 13 of ≥2. Second, outcomes were investigated inclusive of AF‐related hospitalization, AF‐related emergency department (ED) visit, HF related hospitalization, and stroke. For each of these outcomes, an E‐value was calculated to assess the robustness of the observed outcome.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of included patients are summarized including age, sex, race or ethnicity, annual household income, educational attainment, and geographic region. To estimate baseline clinical complexity, the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index was calculated for all included patients. The Elixhauser Comorbidity Index is a validated measure of clinical complexity that includes 31 distinct comorbid domains and is routinely used for risk adjustment in claims‐based research 14 to improve the ability to evaluate associations between exposures and outcomes. The CHA2DS2‐VASc score 13 is an imperfect but ubiquitous clinical tool for estimating annual stroke risk among patients with AF. This measure has been incorporated into countless scientific evaluations of AF and stroke, and it is included in professional society guideline recommendations for OAC in the setting of AF to reduce stroke risk. Given this extensive clinical and scientific use in the field of cardiology, it was calculated for patients in this cohort as a benchmark for stroke risk.

AF subtype was determined based on ICD‐10 diagnosis codes as listed previously. Other preindex clinical characteristics are reported based on diagnosis codes in the 6‐month preindex period. Relevant prescription drug fills in the 6 months before the index diagnosis were measured as well. Baseline characteristics between patients across different care pathways were compared using Welch's ANOVA for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for categorical variables.

Physician specialty associated with AF‐related visits were tabulated, and for each pathway, the frequency was recorded. Cox proportional hazard regression was conducted to assess the association between a visit with an electrophysiologist and AF‐related treatment and acute care outcomes, respectively, adjusted for study covariates (listed in Table 1). As is standard in claims‐based analyses, all available clinically relevant variables were included in the regression analysis because it was not known which (if any) would have an association with exposure (AF visit) and outcomes. Electrophysiologist visits were measured as a time‐varying exposure to account for the potential of immortal time bias. 15 Schoenfeld residual tests were performed and the scaled Schoenfeld residuals were plotted against time to verify the proportional hazards assumption. Analyses were repeated in the subgroup of the cohort ≥80 years at the time of index.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final cohort consisted of 37 370 patients (Table 1). The attrition diagram for the subject cohort is included in Figure 1. The overall cohort was a typical of an adult population with AF: about one quarter were ≥80 years old and ∼50% were aged 60 to 79 years. The population was slightly more male (57%) than female and was predominantly White race (84%). In ∼60% of patients, the index diagnosis was made in an outpatient setting and the remainder was in the inpatient (31%) or ED (8%) setting. Two thirds of patients in the cohort were estimated to have at least moderate risk of stroke based on the CHA2DS2‐VASc score. 13 for whom OAC is typically indicated. 8

Within the final cohort, 7700 (21%) patients had a care pathway that included a visit with an electrophysiologist (“electrophysiology pathway”). Of the remaining cohort, most (n=24 282, 65%) were seen by a cardiologist whereas 5388 patients (14%) were not seen by an electrophysiologist or a cardiologist (Table 1). Patients in an electrophysiology pathway tended to be younger, were more likely male, had higher average income and educational attainment, and had lower burden of coexistent medical conditions with the notable exception of greater likelihood of a diagnosis of cardiomyopathy.

Care Pathways

There were 64 unique care pathways observed. The 10 most common are included in Figure 2A. Nearly 40% were seen by a cardiologist only for a primary diagnosis of AF in the year following AF diagnosis, and 13% were seen only by a primary care physician. Factors predictive of an electrophysiology pathway included higher income, greater educational attainment, persistent AF (versus paroxysmal), and history of cardiomyopathy (Table 2).

One in 5 patients (n=7700, 20.6%) with AF were seen by an electrophysiologist in the year post index at some point in the care pathway. Among these patients in an electrophysiology pathway, approximately 60% were seen by a non‐electrophysiologist clinician first (Figure 2B). Approximately 7% of patients in an electrophysiology pathway had a care pathway from primary care to cardiology to electrophysiology, and 16% of patients went from a cardiologist to an electrophysiologist. Finally, among the 7700 in an electrophysiology pathway, 25% had electrophysiologist visits only when the primary diagnosis was AF.

Time to Specialty Care

Among the 7700 patients in an electrophysiology pathway, the mean interval between diagnosis and electrophysiologist consultation was 83 days (SD±94.32) (Table 3). The location of AF diagnosis significantly influenced the time to electrophysiologist referral, with the shortest referral times observed in cases diagnosed in the ED (mean: 78.97 days), compared with inpatient (mean: 78.97 days) and outpatient settings (mean: 87.19 days, P<0.001) (Table S1).

The associations between an electrophysiology pathway and health care resource use and clinical outcomes are reported in Table 5. Patients in an electrophysiology pathway were significantly less likely to have an AF‐related ED visit in the year after index diagnosis [hazard ratio (HR), 0.65 [95% CI, 0.53–0.81], P<0.001, E‐value 2.43]. They were more than twice as likely to fill a prescription for an AAD (HR, 2.57 [95% CI, 2.44–2.72], P<0.001, E‐value 3.23) and were more likely to fill a prescription for OAC (HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.15–1.28], P<0.001, E‐value 1.55). No significant association was observed between an electrophysiologyt pathway and HF hospitalization (HR, 1.05 [95% CI, 0.89–1.25], P=0.56, E‐value 1.29) or stroke (HR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.73–1.10], P=0.29, E‐value 1.48). This analysis was repeated in the prespecified subgroup of patients aged ≥80 years, and in this cohort a visit with electrophysiologist was associated with a significantly reduced hazard of stroke (HR, 0.63 [95% CI, 0.42–0.95], P=0.03, E‐value 2.55).

Each baseline and demographic factor listed in Table 1 was considered as a predictor of increased or decreased likelihood for OAC among the 7700 patients in an electrophysiology pathway during the 12 months following diagnosis (Figure S1). In this analysis, only Elixhauser Comorbidity Index 4+ (compared with 0–1) and South region (compared with Midwest) were associated with reduced likelihood of OAC prescription.

The likelihood of receiving a prescription for AAD or OAC (when CHA2DS2‐VASc ≥2) was assessed in subgroups based on sex (male and female), race and ethnicity (White, Black, and Hispanic), and age (18–59, 60–69, 70–79, and 80+). In all subgroups, AAD prescription was >2 times more likely following electrophysiologist consultation (Table 6). Except in the group 18 to 59 years old, OAC was more likely among all patients with CHA2DS2‐VASc ≥2 regardless of age, sex, race, or ethnicity subgroup following electrophysiologist consultation.

DISCUSSION

The prevalence of AF, driven by aging and the accumulation of cardiovascular risk factors, continues to grow globally such that most clinicians are likely to encounter AF among their patients irrespective of their background, training, and clinical area of focus. 16 This is in parallel to the increasing complexity of AF guideline‐concordant longitudinal care to reduce morbidity and mortality, optimize health care resource use, and maximize quality of life. 8 , 17 Work from a prior era in a population of patients receiving care at the Veterans Health Administration demonstrated an association between early clinical follow up for a new AF diagnosis and improved outcomes. 18 Another observational study from an overlapping time period (late 2000s–2010s) suggested that rhythm control of AF was applied in only a small minority of patients (<5%) seen in cardiology practices. 19 Little is known about how contemporary American patients travel through the health care system to receive care for AF.

This study was designed to explore this question and uncovered three major findings. First, there was variability in patient care pathways following an AF diagnosis. Second, patients most likely to receive electrophysiologist care for AF were those historically with greater access to medical care. Third, care pathways that included electrophysiologist consultation were more likely associated with guideline concordant care irrespective of patient characteristics.

In this analysis of a diverse US population, there was tremendous variability in the care pathways that followed the diagnosis of AF. Reasons for differences in care pathways could not be assessed with these data but are likely multifactorial. Although the morbidity associated with AF has long been acknowledged, appreciation for the benefits of rhythm control and clinical evidence supporting a rhythm control strategy are, in comparison, relatively new and rapidly evolving. Trials of rate versus rhythm control in the previous era failed to demonstrate benefit from rhythm control for mortality and other AF‐related adverse outcomes, 20 , 21 but these early trials typically enrolled patients with longstanding exposure to AF and did not include the full contemporary armamentarium of rhythm control strategies. In comparison, multiple contemporary randomized trials have demonstrated benefits of rhythm control including preventing or slowing AF disease progression, 22 reducing AF burden, 23 improving quality of life, 4 and reducing risks of HF, hospitalization, stroke, and mortality. 23 These benefits do not accrue equally to all patient groups. Patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction, for example, appear to have more substantial opportunity for clinical benefit from rhythm control. 24 , 25 , 26 Reassuringly, patients with a cardiomyopathy were more likely to have an electrophysiology care pathway perhaps highlighting the appreciation for the well‐documented benefits of rhythm control in this group. Questions remain about comparative risks and benefits of rhythm control strategies in an unselected population of patients with AF, but even when clinicians suspect there may be benefit from a rhythm control strategy for a specific patient, access to specialized cardiology and electrophysiology services for the pursuit of rhythm control is, at best, inconsistent across the country and, at worst, completely inaccessible in some areas due to workforce shortages, geographic barriers, and social determinants of health. 27 , 28 , 29

This work highlights the reality that barriers exist to accessing specialized electrophysiology care for patients with AF who may benefit from rhythm control. Barriers to specialized care include a combination of access, patient preferences, and cost among others. Indeed, only one in five patients with a new diagnosis of AF has an electrophysiologist consultation within the subsequent year despite the fact that early assessment for rhythm control (within a year) is recommended in the most recent guidelines. 8 , 22 , 23

Those patients without an electrophysiologist or cardiologist in their pathway were more often Black race, older, with lower incomes and less educational attainment. Despite a number of social determinants of health that may lead to decreased access to specialty care, a positive finding was that the care delivered after electrophysiologist consultation appears to be balanced. Prescriptions for OAC and AAD were significantly associated with electrophysiologist consultation irrespective of baseline patient characteristics and demographics. The E‐value for the association between electrophysiologist visit and AAD prescription (3.23) suggests a particularly strong relationship with low likelihood of an unmeasured confounder having a strong effect on this relationship. In the case of OAC prescription, the relationship with an electrophysiologist visit is likely more complex including unmeasured variables as evidenced by a more modest E‐value (1.55). These findings align with prior work demonstrating improved guideline‐concordant care for AF with increasing clinician specialization. 30 In addition to the role of specialty training in AAD use, this may reflect a referral bias in that patients with greater symptom burden may be more likely to be referred for rhythm control even though the benefits of early rhythm management have been shown in patients with and without traditional symptoms of AF. 31 The benefit of early rhythm control likely contributes to the reduced likelihood of an AF‐related ED visit in this group, and this is supported by a robust E‐value (2.43) indicating relatively low risk of unmeasured confounding affecting this finding. In addition, more directed anticipatory guidance regarding emergency AF scenarios associated with electrophysiologist visit may also contribute to a reduction in health care use.

A critical pillar of guideline‐based management of AF is to reduce the risk of stroke. The rate of stroke trended lower among patients in an electrophysiology pathway; however, this difference was not statistically significant after multivariate adjustment with an E‐value suggesting the observation was vulnerable to unmeasured confounding. Indeed, stroke risk is modified by a range of factors with age being the most powerful, so older patients (≥80 years) were evaluated as a prespecified subgroup with the understanding that greater baseline risk of stroke increases the potential benefit of guideline‐concordant care including OAC. 6 , 32 In this subgroup, an electrophysiology care pathway was associated with reduced risk of stroke despite the relatively short duration of AF exposure with limited follow‐up (1 year). From these data, it is not possible to fully understand the relationship between electrophysiology exposure and reduced stroke risk in this population. The association likely reflects a combination of measured and unmeasured variables. Sources of confounding include confounding by indication in which patients with greater stroke risk are referred for specialty care and selection bias in which generally healthier and more medically literate older patients are more likely to be referred to specialists and follow through with those referrals. This finding of reduced stroke associated with electrophysiology exposure may also reflect the increased use of OAC and possibly other nonmeasured AF‐related stroke risk reduction therapies (eg, left atrial appendage occlusion, risk factor modification) as well as more aggressive pursuit of rhythm control through use of AADs or catheter ablation. Early rhythm control within 1 year of diagnosis in the setting of comprehensive medical management significantly reduces stroke. 23 In the EAST AFNET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention Trial) trial that compared early rhythm control versus usual care, stroke was reduced by 35% in the rhythm control group. Future work with longer follow‐up and larger sample size could help clarify the broad relationship between specialty consultation and associated risks of stroke and other clinical outcomes.

Nearly 85% of patients diagnosed with AF were seen by a cardiologist, an electrophysiologist, or both in the 12 months following diagnosis, but large variability exists in referral pathways. However, real and predicted physician shortages make universal electrophysiologist consultation for AF impossible. In the current care paradigms, the deficit between the demand for and supply of AF specialists is likely to grow. Consequently, there is an urgent need to streamline and optimize existing AF care pathways to meet patient needs that include early diagnosis, risk factor modification, and referral to specialty care for early rhythm consideration. To address this need, efforts are required to maximize the role and influence of all potential care team members—nurse educators, APPs, physicians and others. AF centers of excellence that can provide multispecialty comprehensive care and AF cooperatives that group resources with electrophysiologist involvement could improve access to care for risk factor modification, stroke risk reduction, and AF rhythm control to improve clinical outcomes and overcome some of the barriers of access to subspecialty care. 11

LIMITATIONS

The primary limitation of this analysis is the observational nature of the data; causal relationships cannot be determined and the impact of unmeasured variables while estimated are not known. For example, events preceding the minimum 6‐month continuous plan enrollment for inclusion in this cohort may impact exposures and outcomes. The age cutoff for the high‐risk subgroup analysis was selected based on clinical relevance and epidemiological data 6 but was not empirically derived, so the findings should be considered hypothesis generating. Furthermore, although care pathways are generally interpreted to reflect the clinicians' recommended approach, the role of patient preferences and clinical appropriateness in care pathways or treatment strategies cannot be determined. An assessment of the impact of rhythm control is incomplete without the consideration of invasive strategies (eg, catheter ablation), but this therapy was excluded from the analysis in order to focus on those therapies available to all clinicians. Future work should evaluate the role of care pathways in access to catheter ablation given this therapy's association with improved clinical outcomes. Optum data allows for the assessment of prescription fills, but persistence of drug therapy cannot be determined from these data. APPs are listed according to degree rather than specialty of practice, so pathways including APPs may underestimate patient exposure to specialty consultation. Finally, although the Optum database includes a diversity of patients based on geography, demographics, insurance status, and other factors, it is not universally representative, so the findings in this analysis may not apply to other groups (eg, beneficiaries of veterans health care programs or Medicare fee‐for‐service program).

CONCLUSIONS

The pathways associated with AF care are highly variable with unexplained differences according to age and demographic factors that suggest less intensive and guideline‐concordant care for those historically disenfranchised from medical care. Only 1 in 5 patients receive subspecialty electrophysiology care in the year after diagnosis of AF in the United States. This finding is critically important in AF care among those at greatest risk for stroke because AF specialty care was associated with a reduction in stroke risk among older patients. Furthermore, the noninvasive medical care received by those in an electrophysiology pathway is remarkably consistent across age, race, ethnicity, and sex subgroups without significant variability by medical complexity. The combination of looming physician shortages and increasing demand for specialized AF care will require optimizing existing resources and developing technical and policy solutions to expand access to AF specialty care to maximize patient outcomes.
