Evaluating the prognostic association between race and perineal trauma: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Haifa Hussein Almalki, Fabrice I Mowbray, Susan Buchholz, Ibtihal Alnaimi, Emma C Schlegel, Janis Miller, Jessica Sender
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPerineal trauma is one of the most common complications of childbirth, impacting approximately 9 out of 10 women who undergo a vaginal delivery. Perineal trauma is a public health issue leading to increased maternal morbidity and decreased quality of life. Although race is being studied as a potential risk factor and predictor of perineal trauma, other contributing factors like racism and social determinants of health have not been adequately studied in the same context. We set out to synthesise the available peer-reviewed evidence evaluating the prognostic association between race and perineal trauma.Methods and analysisThis systematic review and meta-analysis adheres to the PRISMA-P (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols) and PROGRESS (Prognosis Research Strategy) guidelines and is registered with PROSPERO. The review explores the association between racial status (non-Hispanic white vs non-white) and perineal birth trauma using the PECOTS (Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparator, Outcome, Timing and Setting) framework. We will search PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science and Embase. Peer-reviewed observational studies will be included. Data extraction and screening will be done in duplicate. Analyses will use random-effects models in R, reporting both unadjusted and adjusted risk differences. Risk of bias will be assessed using ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions). Heterogeneity and certainty of evidence will be evaluated using I² and GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation), respectively.Ethics and disseminationThis is a systematic review based on previously published data, and therefore ethical approval is not required. The findings of this review will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at academic conferences.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42025590093.

Introduction

Perineal trauma is one of the most common complications of childbirth, impacting approximately 9 out of 10 women who undergo a vaginal delivery (VD).1 Perineal trauma occurs during VD or as a consequence of medical interventions, such as instrumental deliveries and episiotomy.2

Perineal trauma is categorised into four degrees: (1) first-degree limits to tear of perineal skin, (2) second-degree involves vaginal mucosa and perineal body, (3) third degree involves anal sphincter and (4) fourth-degree involves rectal mucosa.3 First-degree perineal trauma is prevalent in 6% of primiparous and 12% in multiparous women.4 However, second-degree perineal injury is the most commonly prevalent degree of injury among 40% of primiparous and 28% multiparous women.5 Third-degree and fourth-degree injuries are less commonly diagnosed, with 6% in primiparous women and 2% in multiparous women experiencing this severity of injury.1 6

Perineal trauma is a public health issue leading to increased maternal morbidity and decreased quality of life.7 In the immediate postpartum period, women may experience severe perineal pain, inhibiting their ability to complete activities of daily living and care for their newborns.813 Postpartum perineal pain can persist for months, often exacerbated by complications such as urine and faecal incontinence, pelvic floor dysfunction and sexual dysfunction.1418 These physical challenges are frequently accompanied by psychological distress, including post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety and depression.8 19 20 The continual nature of these complications can severely affect women’s ability to return to their prepregnancy health state, impacting overall quality of life.8 13

Despite an estimated high prevalence, perineal trauma is under-reported, and thus undertreated, leaving many women suffering from poor health and quality of life.4 5 The long-term complications often extend beyond physical and psychological distress. The functional impairment experienced as a result of perineal trauma can potentially affect the child’s care.2123 Additionally, the strain on maternal mental health can have downstream effects, negatively influencing partner relationships and sexual function.24 25 The economic burden of obstetrical anal sphincter injuries was estimated between £3.7 million and £9.8 million in the UK, while in the USA, perineal trauma-related complications cost about US$21 million annually.1

Understanding the patient and contextual factors that increase the risk of perineal trauma is essential to supporting the health of women and their families. Several risk factors for perineal trauma have been well-documented in the literature. These prognostic factors include maternal age (younger and older women deliveries), parity (first-time mothers) and maternal body mass index. Additionally, fetal factors such as birth weight, particularly macrosomia (a baby weighing more than 4000 g), have been consistently linked to an increased risk of perineal trauma.15 26 Other contributing factors include the use of obstetric interventions like forceps or vacuum delivery and prolonged second-stage labour.2729 A summary of the most common risk factors associated with an increase in the risk of perineal trauma is presented in table 1.

Although race is being studied as a potential risk factor and predictor of perineal trauma, other contributing factors like racism and social determinants of health have not been adequately studied in the same context.30 31 Women from specific racial groups, including black or African American, American Indian or Alaska Native and Asian, often face unequal access to high-quality prenatal care and experience implicit bias during labour and delivery, which can lead to delayed or inappropriate interventions.3236 Additionally, systemic racism in healthcare can result in fewer opportunities for shared decision-making, leading to higher rates of invasive procedures such as episiotomies or instrumental deliveries, which can increase the risk of perineal trauma.37

Addressing perineal trauma is not only a matter of improving women’s health but also a significant public health issue with broader socioeconomic implications.1 In this study, we will classify racial status as non-Hispanic white versus all other racial groups to examine disparities in perineal birth trauma as they relate to broader structural and systemic factors in maternal healthcare. Additionally, due to limited reporting in the current data (ie, heterogeneity of racial groups), aggregating non-white racial groups allows for sufficient statistical power and an ability to synthesise findings while still capturing disparities in perineal trauma risk. Yet, we acknowledge the diversity within non-white populations and recognise that distinct racial subgroups may experience varying levels of risk. Based on the data available in the literature, we will further categorise non-white racial groups into subgroups to better account for potential variations among different racial and ethnic populations.

The perineum is anatomically defined as the region between the vaginal opening and the anus, comprising skin, connective tissue and superficial muscles.38 While the term ‘trauma’ has been used in broader contexts—including sexual assault and pelvic floor injury—in this review, we will use perineal trauma in a clinical obstetric context, in line with established maternity care literature.39 40 We acknowledge that the terminology used to describe injury to the perineum during childbirth has been inconsistent. Tear, laceration, injury and trauma are sometimes used interchangeably, although they may carry different clinical definitions. In this review, perineal trauma is used as an umbrella term that encompasses first-degree through fourth-degree perineal lacerations during VD. This review will not include internal injuries to deeper pelvic floor structures, such as the levator ani muscles.

Previous reviews have explored risk factors for perineal trauma, including maternal and intrapartum characteristics, or focused on specific ethnic groups. For example, one review concluded that those of Asian descent may be associated with increased risk of severe perineal trauma in Western countries. However, findings were conflicting and limited by operational inconsistencies.41 Another review synthesised data on obstetric risk factors, such as instrumental delivery and fetal position, but did not examine race or ethnicity as a prognostic factor.42 To our knowledge, no prior systematic review has focused on race as a predictive determinant of perineal trauma across all degrees of severity.

We set out to synthesise the available peer-reviewed evidence evaluating the prognostic association between race and perineal trauma among women who have VD. We hypothesise that race is associated with the occurrence of perineal trauma during VD, considering the systemic inequities experienced by women from marginalised racial groups for maternal healthcare.

Methods

The reporting of our protocol is guided by The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P).43 We will then use the PRISMA reporting guidelines to guide the dissemination of the final review. We registered our protocol with PROSPERO (CRD42025590093).44 We will conduct our prognostic factor systematic review and meta-analysis per recommendations from Riley and colleagues and the PROGRESS (Prognosis Research Strategy Group).45

Research question

We will use the PECOTS framework (Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparator, Outcome, Timing and Setting) to guide our research design and ensure clarity in defining our study’s key components.46 Our primary research question is ‘What is the association between racial status non-Hispanic (white vs non-white) and perineal birth trauma?’. The breakdown of the research question according to the PCOTS framework is presented in table 2. Our secondary research question is ‘What is the association between racial status (white vs non-white) and perineal birth trauma, after statistically adjusting for parity and maternal age (minimum confounder set)?’ We defined perineal trauma as a laceration or tear to the perineal area, irrespective of degree.47 We specified a minimum confounder set of parity and maternal age because there is an abundance of evidence showing these factors increase the risk of perineal trauma.1

Data sources and search strategy

We will search a series of databases alongside a university-affiliated medical librarian (JS). We will search the following electronic databases: PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science and Embase. Our search will use text and MeSH terms related to perineal trauma. We developed a search strategy to include all studies that focused on perineal laceration/trauma in an inclusive manner (table 3). The initial literature search began in April 2025, and the review is planned to be completed by September 2025.

Search strategy

Study selection and screening

We will include studies in our review if they are peer-reviewed observational studies that evaluate the association between racial status and perineal trauma using a validated instrument, mindful that we are conducting a prognostic factor systematic review. Given our focus on marginalised racial and ethnic groups, we will consider studies that explicitly examine non-dominant racial identities within their respective contexts. For example, in the UK, studies may use terms such as ‘Black’ or ‘ethnic minority’, while in the USA, the term ‘African Americans’ or other racial classifications may be used.48 To ensure consistency, we will categorise racial groups according to the definitions used in each study while maintaining a broad inclusion of non-white populations.

We will exclude studies that are randomised in nature (exclusive of post hoc analyses), knowing that patients who enrol in clinical trials often have differing baseline characteristics and risk.49 We will also exclude studies that focus on a population of women who undergo C-section delivery, who do not give birth in a hospital setting, and studies that focus on twin deliveries. We will exclude women who give birth outside of the hospital because their models of care look different from in-hospital delivery (eg, lack of pain management and/or continuous fetal monitoring).50 We will exclude studies that focus on twin deliveries because women who give birth to multiple neonates have greater baseline risk of perineal trauma and delivery-related complications (eg, pre-eclampsia, episiotomy).5153 We will also exclude published abstracts, conference proceedings, literature reviews and case studies or series, given their lack of a comparison group. We will not exclude studies based on language or time to reduce the biases associated with these acts.54 55

Our data will be managed within Covidence (c), where we will conduct title, abstract and full text screening independently and duplicate by two graduate-level registered nurses (HA, IA). In cases of disagreement during titles and abstract screening, the full-text review will be considered to promote a liberal inclusion at this stage. For disagreement at the full-text stage, we will resolve all issues through discussion. A standardised and piloted decision-tree was created to support decision-making during article screening; this form can be found in the online supplemental file 1.

Data extraction

We will build an extraction template using Excel. Our template will include author(s), year of publication, study design (eg, cohort), setting, recruitment time frame, sample size, study population characteristics (eg, age, weight), single versus multiple study sites, data registries used, country of study, total sample size, predictors included in multivariable analytics, definition and timing of outcomes, proportion of those with perineal injury, inclusion/exclusion criteria, outcome(s), unadjusted and adjusted results (risk difference (RD), CIs and significance levels). Data will be extracted independently and in duplicate; conflicts will be resolved by a third author external to study abstraction.

Data synthesis and analysis

We will analyse eligible data R (V.4.0). We will use a random-effects model for statistical pooling, given that models of care, patient populations and outcomes are likely to vary between institutions and regions. We will pool study results according to the specific instrument used to assess perineal injury. We will present pooled absolute RDs, mindful that patients often care more about absolute risk and readers often overestimate effects when presented in relative terms.56 We will first provide a narrative synthesis of study characteristics, including population demographics, study design and key findings related to perineal birth trauma and racial status. For the quantitative analysis, we will examine the association between racial status (non-white) and perineal birth trauma. This analysis will be performed in two stages:

Unadjusted analysis

We will pool RD with 95% CIs from the studies, and we will conduct the analysis to assess the association between racial status (non-white) and perineal birth trauma without adjusting for any covariates to quantify this association, assuming the outcome measurement is congruent across studies.57

Adjusted analysis

We will pool RD with 95% CIs from the studies, then we will conduct the analysis to assess the association between racial status (non-white) and perineal birth trauma after statistically adjusting for our minimum confounder set (parity and maternal age).57 While social and healthcare factors are significantly associated with race, they are often under-reported in studies, making their inclusion as confounding factors challenging. Should the data allow, we will aim to include a measure of health access and/or socioeconomic status as a secondary minimum confounder set as sensitivity analysis. Additionally, our focus is on maternal factors rather than labour interventions or fetal factors, as we aim to examine the intrinsic maternal characteristics that influence perineal trauma risk.

Risk of bias within studies

Since we are conducting a prognostic factor systematic review, we will evaluate risk of bias (RoB) using the RoB in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I)58 ROBINS-I, a thorough tool for observational studies, covers confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended intervention, missing data, outcome measurement and reported outcomes. The tool provides a framework to rate the RoB in each domain as ‘low’, ‘moderate’, ‘serious’ or ‘critical’. Any study judged to have a high or critical RoB will be treated cautiously in the final analysis and may be excluded in sensitivity analyses to assess its impact on the overall findings.59 To identify overfit models, we will assess the event-per-variable for all multivariable models to ensure the ratio was >10. If not, this item was rated as considerable RoB under the domain Statistical Analysis and Reporting.

Sources of heterogeneity

We will evaluate statistical heterogeneity in two ways. First, we will evaluate forest plots to visually appraise the presence and degree of heterogeneity.60 61 Second, we will evaluate I², a statistic used to quantify the percentage of variability between studies due to heterogeneity rather than chance (≤40%). Yet this measure will be used with caution in a study with large sample size.62

Certainty of estimates

The certainty of estimates in this systematic review will be assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation).63 Each recommendation will be rated as high, moderate, low or very low certainty based on several criteria, including study design, RoB, inconsistency of results, indirectness of evidence, imprecision and publication bias, with observational studies considered high confidence until proven otherwise.64

Patient and public involvement

None

Ethics and dissemination

Mindful that this is a systematic review protocol, ethics approval is not required. The results of this review will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at academic conferences.

Discussion

Our protocol outlines a systematic review designed to evaluate the prognostic association between race and perineal trauma in women undergoing VD. Perineal trauma is a prevalent childbirth complication, affecting a significant proportion of women globally and contributing to both short- and long-term physical and psychological distress. While there have been many studies evaluating the prognosis of perineal trauma, most overlook race as a prognostic factor despite having collected data on racial status.1 27 31 37

Our review aims to address this gap by synthesising existing evidence on whether racial status (white vs non-white) is associated with the risk of perineal trauma, after statistically adjusting for potential confounders like parity and maternal age. The potential association between race and perineal trauma is particularly relevant in the context of maternal healthcare disparities.30 37 65 66 By identifying race as a potential risk factor for perineal trauma, our review could guide future research, encouraging more inclusive and race-conscious healthcare practices. We created a minimum confounder set to help account for exogenous variables influencing the association between race and perineal trauma outcomes. In conclusion, this systematic review will offer a more nuanced understanding of how racial status impacts maternal health outcomes. The intent of the study results is to significantly influence clinical practice and health policy aimed at promoting equitable maternal healthcare.

However, we note that there is a potential for bias in the diagnosis or reporting of perineal trauma, particularly across racial or ethnic groups.67 Differences in provider training, clinical judgement or documentation practices may influence how trauma is identified and recorded.68 This may result in either overestimation or underestimation of perineal trauma rates in specific populations. We will be mindful of this possibility when assessing the RoB in individual studies and interpreting the overall findings.
