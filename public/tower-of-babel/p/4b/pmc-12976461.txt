Machine Learning Based Diagnostic Models for Hip and Knee Prosthetic Joint Infection: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jaiden Nairne‐Nagy, Rudraksh Gupta, Boopalan Ramasamy, Lucian Bogdan Solomon, Stuart A. Callary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProsthetic joint infection (PJI) is a devastating complication that affects up to 1.7% of patients within 2 years following total hip arthroplasty (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA). PJI is associated with significant patient morbidity, reduction in quality of life, prolonged hospitalisation, and healthcare expenditure. With a 5‐year mortality reported as high as 21%, PJI is one of the most feared complications of joint arthroplasty. Identification of PJI is of critical importance to ensure successful and definitive treatment. However, diagnosis remains challenging due to the lack of a gold standard, culture‐negative infection, and varying sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic tests. The rapid expansion of machine learning (ML) in the literature has led to the emergence of models that utilise patient demographics, clinical features, serological studies, synovial fluid biomarkers, and imaging to improve PJI diagnostics. The purpose of this study was to describe the literature on using ML to diagnose PJI. A systematic review of the literature for original studies describing ML use in PJI diagnostics following THA or TKA was conducted. This review identified 12 studies applying ML to diagnose or predict PJI, through patient demographics, clinical features, and imaging. Most models demonstrated good predictive performance, with Area Under the Curve (AUC) from 0.68 to 0.993. However, few studies validated their models externally. In conclusion, ML presents a promising approach to enhance PJI diagnostic accuracy, which may reduce diagnostic delays and ensure appropriate treatment. Further studies are needed to assess the model's generalisability and validate these models in external cohorts. Statement of Clinical Significance: The diagnosis of PJI remains a challenge due to limitations in current diagnostic criteria. ML offers a data‐driven approach to improve diagnostic accuracy, potentially allowing earlier and more accurate identification to ensure appropriate treatment in a timely manner.

Introduction

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a devastating complication following primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1]. PJI is associated with significant patient morbidity, reduction in quality of life, prolonged hospitalisation, and healthcare expenditure [2]. In Australia, the estimated incidence of PJI is up to 3900 cases per year [3], with a cost burden of more than $250 million to the healthcare system [3]. With a 5‐year mortality after THA PJI of 21% [4], it remains one of the most feared complications of joint replacement surgery [5]. Furthermore, PJI is the most common cause for TKA revision surgery [6] and the second most common cause for THA revision surgery [7].

Obtaining an accurate and timely PJI diagnosis is critical for guiding successful definitive treatment [5]. Multiple definitions have been established to guide the diagnosis of PJI, which include the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS), the European Bone and Joint Infection Society (EBJIS), and the International Consensus on Musculoskeletal Infection (ICM) [8, 9, 10]. These criteria incorporate numerous features, including clinical features, serological tests, and microbiological cultures [10]. However, a significant implementation gap exists between criteria‐based PJI definitions for researchers and clinicians, with these criteria rarely being used correctly in everyday clinical diagnosis [11]. This gap exists as applying these definitions requires multiple steps such as ordering tests, unit conversion, using thresholds different to the health network, assigning scores, and calculating totals, a workflow too complicated for routine clinical practice – it has been shown that many initial PJI workups lack the required tests [11, 12, 13].

However, concern remains regarding the accuracy of these diagnostic criteria and the unclear significance of individual features [14]. Current PJI diagnostic methods face significant challenges, including non‐specific or low accuracy serum biomarker findings [15], high rates of culture‐negative infection [16], and variability across diagnostic criteria [17]. Furthermore, invasive procedures such as synovial aspiration, inconclusive results from scoring systems, and limited access to novel diagnostic tests such as alpha‐defensin or nuclear imaging may complicate timely diagnosis. This implementation gap and diagnostic limitations highlight the importance for innovative diagnostic workflows to PJI.

Machine learning (ML) is a subset of artificial intelligence, which enables the analysis of complex datasets, applicable to predicting PJI through multimodal data involving features such as clinical signs and symptoms, laboratory studies, microbiology, medical imaging [18]. Given the diagnostic challenges of PJI, ML offers a promising approach to enhance diagnostic accuracy and add benefit to clinical decision‐making [19]. Studies applying ML to PJI diagnosis have employed supervised learning models, such as random forests and neural networks. Additionally, some studies have utilised deep learning for imaging analysis of X‐rays or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [20].

This study aims to systematically review the literature for original studies that have developed ML models that aim to diagnose PJI of the hip or knee through various modalities, including patient demographics, clinical features, medical imaging, laboratory studies, and microbiology.

On the 17th of March 2025, the literature was reviewed through PubMed and Embase. The systematic search involved the search strategy outlined in Appendix 1. PubMed obtained 550 results in the last 10 years, and 33 were obtained from Embase (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow chart detailing inclusion and exclusion of studies throughout each stage of screening.

Two independent reviewers (J.N. and R.G.) screened studies by title, abstract, and then by full text using Covidence. The same reviewers extracted the data independently. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (S.C.). Data extracted for the systematic review were synthesised in narrative and tabular form, which included the following headings: title, author, year, prediction, study design, number of patients, number of infected/aseptic, treatment, average follow‐up, outcome variable, PJI diagnostic criteria, performance metric (AUC), data screening, fine‐tuning, number of input variables, input categories, input variables, data sources, and external validation. The third reviewer resolved any discrepancies.

AUC Interpretation

AUC was interpreted according to the following guidelines from the literature [21]: 0.5 = no discrimination; 0.5 to 0.7 = poor; 0.7 to 0.8 = acceptable; 0.8 to 0.9 = excellent; 0.9 = outstanding.

Results

Study Selection

The initial search of PubMed and Embase identified a combined 583 studies (PubMed = 550, Embase = 33). After removing 23 duplicates, 560 studies remained to be included for title and abstract screening. Of these, 13 remained for full‐text screening, 12 of which were included for data extraction. One paper was excluded on full‐text screening as it utilised machine learning to identity the differences in PJI and non‐PJI joint replacement cases to identify how characteristics change postoperatively, rather than a diagnostic outcome. One study described PJI as deep surgical site infection, which was included in this study as it remained within the scope of this study [22] (Table 1).

Study Characteristics

From the 12 included studies, two studies utilised primary THA, two primary TKA, one primary THA and TKA, two revision TKA, four revision TKA and THA, and one DAIR.

All studies used a retrospective design. Data sources included local academic institutes (11), a national database (2), multinational databases (2), and a central clinical laboratory (1). Only one study conducted external validation using a separate patient cohort.

Sample Size

The included studies reported the sample size as the total number of patients included in the dataset and detailed the number of patients with prosthetic joint infection. There was a wide range of sample sizes, with the smallest being 20 (10 PJI) [31] and largest 17,165 (3873 PJI) surgeries [23]. This training and testing cohort split ratio differed between studies (Table 2).

Training and Testing Cohort Split

In the included studies, patient cohorts were divided into training and testing sets to develop and evaluate the performance of ML models. However, the method of cohort segregation varied across studies. Many studies did not employ an independent external validation cohort, instead relying solely on internal validation or cross‐validation within a single institution's data. For instance, Kuo et al. [26] utilised a stratified fivefold cross‐validation approach with a cohort of 323 patients, dividing the datasets into five independent folds of approximately 64 patients. In each iteration, one‐fold serves as the test set, while the remaining four folds are used for training. In contrast, Salimy et al. [19] utilised an 80:20 stratified split on a larger cohort of 51,035 patients from a national database, with approximately 40,828 patients used for training and 10,207 for testing. The choice of a single split by Salimy et al. [19] reflects the advantage of having a large dataset, in comparison to the smaller cohort used by Kuo et al. [26] which necessitated cross‐validation (Table 3).

Input Features

The input categories used by the studies included in this review were patient demographics, clinical features, medical comorbidities, serological studies, radiological imaging, diagnostic criteria, microbiology, synovial fluid analysis, surgical factors, histopathology, and risk stratification scores. The number of input features (excluding studies that used only imaging or pathology) ranged from 22 to 6. Of the included studies, 11 utilised patient demographics, 10 studies included medical comorbidities 7 included serological studies, 3 included radiological imaging, 3 included microbiological studies (with pathogen type), 2 included PJI diagnostic criteria, 7 studies included ASA, and 4 studies included synovial fluid analysis (Table 4).

Machine Learning Models

Across the 12 studies, 23 different ML algorithms were described. These included linear models, tree‐based models, support vector machines, k‐nearest neighbours, naive Bayes, and deep learning models. Linear models included logistic regression, lasso, and elastic net. Tree‐based models included random forest, decision tree, extra trees, XGBoost, lightGBM, and stochastic gradient boosting. Support vector machines involved linear decision boundaries and kernel functions (Table 5).

Performance Metrics

All studies reported a value for AUC. Eight of the ML models had an AUC greater than 0.9, which is considered outstanding; 13 of the models had an AUC between 0.8 and 0.9, which is considered excellent; 7 of the models had an AUC between 0.7 and 0.8, which is considered acceptable.

Model Performance

The highest performing model for diagnosing PJI following revision arthroplasty was a meta‐learner with an AUC of 0.988 [26], pre‐operative and aiding diagnosis after primary or revision when PJI is suspected with an AUC of 0.993 using decision trees [23], “in‐time” prediction during second stage with an AUC of 0.968 using a meta‐learner [34], and PJI diagnosis following primary arthroplasty for knee PJI of 0.957 and hip PJI of 0.906 [25]. This demonstrates the superior ability of ML models to handle complex, nonlinear relationships between variables in predicting recurrent PJI following revision arthroplasty [27].

Diagnostic Timepoints

The ML models included in this review were applied across varying clinical timepoints in managing PJI. Diagnosis of PJI following primary arthroplasty (post‐primary, pre‐revision) was the most common timepoint in 4 of the 12 studies [19, 20, 22, 25, 30]. Diagnosing PJI pre‐operatively (before revision surgery to confirm PJI and guide surgical planning) was investigated by 4 of the 12 studies [20, 23, 24, 32]. Albano et al. addresses both timepoints, using MRI data for post‐primary and pre‐revision diagnosis [20]. Diagnosing PJI after revision arthroplasty (to detect recurrent or persistent PJI) was reported by 3 of the 12 studies [26, 27, 28]. One study reported intra‐operative real‐time diagnosis of PJI using preoperative and intraoperative features [34]. Another study reported on the persistence of PJI following irrigation and debridement (I&D) [35]. Typically, PJI diagnosis occurs post‐primary, pre‐revision when suspected and verified using intra‐operative samples.

Diagnostic Criteria

Across the studies included in this review, the diagnosis of PJI for labelling patients as infected or aseptic in the training data was typically established using consensus criteria, primarily the MSIS and 2018 ICM criteria. These frameworks integrate clinical findings, laboratory markers, microbiological cultures, and synovial fluid analysis. However, reliance on consensus criteria for both training data and model performance evaluation, which may inflate performance metrics due to circularity as the model is trained and tested against the same set of rules. Paranjape et al. [23] reviewed unsupervised clustering approaches, which defined training labels by identifying PJI biomarker patterns without depending solely on consensus criteria, offering a potential method to mitigate this bias.

Quality Appraisal

Quality appraisal of the included studies using the APPRAISE‐AI tool demonstrated a mean score of 49 out of 100, ranging from 39 to 57, with 7 studies scoring ≥ 50 [29]. A detailed breakdown of individual study scores by domain is presented in Appendix 2.

Discussion

The accurate and timely diagnosis of PJI remains a challenge [14]. Acute PJI cases are usually treated with debridement and implant retention, single‐ or two‐stage revision [31]. This differs from aseptic prosthetic loosening which is managed with prosthetic revision [36].

Misdiagnosis of PJI can lead to inappropriate treatment, for instance, mistaking a PJI for an aseptic failure may lead to performing a single‐stage revision without addressing the infection, potentially leading to persistent infection, implant failure, or systemic complications [33]. Conversely, treating an aseptic failure as a PJI may prompt extensive surgical interventions and unnecessary antibiotic therapy, which like two‐stage revision may increase patient morbidity, healthcare costs, and recovery time [37]. Therefore, accurate and timely diagnosis of PJI is of critical importance to ensure successful treatment. The most important findings from this study were that diagnosing PJI based on arthroplasty‐specific data has been successfully performed in the literature. ML models for diagnosing PJI following THA and TKA demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, with AUC values ranging from 0.680 to 0.993 across the included studies [19, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30, 32, 34, 35].

The earliest study included in this review was published in 2021, highlighting the recent emergence and growing interest in using ML to improve diagnostics in orthopaedics [27].

All studies except Paranjape et al. [23] used supervised machine learning algorithms, utilising labelled datasets containing a known target feature (PJI diagnosis) to train predictive models. The data undergoes pre‐processing to identify missing values and improve data quality; it is then split into a training and testing set, which has the target feature blinded. Various algorithms are then employed depending on their suitability for the dataset, and they are evaluated in parallel to determine the most suitable for the task. During training, the data may be divided further into a validation set to optimise model performance through hyperparameter training. After validation and testing, the final model is deployed to make predictions on the testing set. Model performance is then evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and Brier score.

The performance of ML models in diagnosing PJI varied considerably depending on the data used, specific algorithms implemented, and the target outcome. Studies utilising several features, such as patient demographics, clinical features, imaging, serum studies, and synovial studies, demonstrated high predictive performance [19, 22, 26, 34]. These findings highlight the importance of aligning datasets, model complexity, and target definitions to address the clinical question.

Only two studies utilised registry data for analysis [19, 30], despite ML models benefiting from large, high‐quality datasets to improve predictive performance, increase generalisability, and reduce bias. The high predictive performance of these studies (AUC 0.660‐0.880) suggests registries may be of value. The underutilisation of registry data represents an opportunity to leverage large‐scale, diverse patient cohorts for PJI diagnosis which could enhance ML model generalisability and scalability. However, concerns about varying completeness and validity of the individual variables have been raised in national databases which may limit their incorporation [38]. National joint‐replacement registries often use minimum datasets to increase data completeness, however, this falls short in providing in‐depth analysis of outcomes, confounders, and risk factors which are required in the analysis of PJI outcomes [39].

Feature Selection and Data Quality

The selection and quality of input features play a key role in the performance and reliability of ML models for diagnosing PJI. Each input feature contributes uniquely to predictive accuracy, but their impact is influenced by data completeness, consistency in reporting, and other features [34].

Clinical features such as patient demographics, clinical signs, comorbidities, and risk stratification scores (ASA, CCI) have significantly influenced model performance by providing relevant contextual information that aids in distinguishing between infected and aseptic cases. Incorporating patient demographics such as age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) may assist in predicting PJI [27, 30]. For instance, older patients (> 75 years) [40] and those with a higher BMI [41] are at an increased risk of developing PJI. Gender also plays a role, with male patients often showing a higher incidence of PJI [42]. Additionally, socioeconomic status has been identified as a significant predictor [43]. This association may be linked to differences in patient demographics, comorbidities, and access to care among individuals with different insurance types. Comorbidities are important predictors of PJI. Conditions and risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, smoking, alcohol use, and renal failure have been associated with an increased risk of PJI [44]. Risk stratification scores such as ASA and CCI are widely used to assess surgical risk and comorbidity burden [45].

Synovial fluid analysis, including white cell count, neutrophil percentage, and biomarkers such as alpha‐defensin, provides highly specific and sensitive indicators of PJI, making it a valuable feature in ML models to improve diagnostic accuracy [46].

Microbiological data, such as culture results and pathogen identification from joint aspirates or intraoperative samples, may provide evidence of infection and serve as high‐value features in ML models. They contribute to both diagnostic precision and the ability to distinguish between different types of infectious aetiologies [47].

Imaging techniques, including MRI and nuclear medicine scans, offer anatomical and functional insights that can enhance the diagnostic accuracy of ML models, particularly in complex cases where conventional clinical and laboratory findings may be inconclusive [48].

Diagnosing PJI

The diagnosis of PJI can be difficult and there are no gold‐standard diagnostic criteria. Currently, two widely used criteria are EBJIS 2021 and ICM 2018; however, they have challenges associated with the accuracy and interpretation of results [14]. Diagnostic categories include patient demographics, clinical features, serum studies, synovial fluid cytology and biomarkers, microbiology, histology, and imaging.

PJI can present in several ways, ranging from clear signs of infection to discrete symptoms such as pain or joint dysfunction depending on the pathogenesis and microbe. Clinical signs such as fever and erythema have high specificity. However, clinical features such as pain and joint dysfunction may overlap with aseptic prosthesis failure [49]. Communicating sinus tract, wound healing complications, or bacteraemia is diagnostic of PJI within the context of implantation timing [50].

Serum studies such as CRP, ESR, and WCC are critical inputs in many ML models due to their established association with infection, often as strong predictors in diagnosing PJI. However, they all reflect general inflammation and may be raised in non‐infective inflammatory conditions or be normal in low‐grade infections [15]. C‐RP is often used to evaluate for PJI. However, it cannot be used in isolation to determine the presence of PJI, and a normal value does not exclude PJI.

In the included studies, consensus criteria were frequently used to label cases as aseptic or PJI for model training and to assess diagnostic performance. This ensures consistency with current standards of PJI diagnosis, however, it raises the issue of the same consensus definition being used for both labelling and evaluation, where the model may simply replicate the criteria rather than determining an independent diagnosis. This lack of an external reference standard limits the ability to validate the model performance in isolation to diagnostic criteria. An alternative approach has been proposed by Paranjape et al. [23] which utilises unsupervised clustering to assign training labels for model development, which is then verified with consensus criteria representing an alternative approach.

Advantages of ML

The ML models included in this review demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional criteria. For example, Chen et al. [34] reported an AUC of 0.968 and sensitivity/specificity of 84.21%/100% for intra‐operative PJI diagnosis during second‐stage revision, highlighted for its meta‐learner approach combining multiple algorithms and novel integration of preoperative and intraoperative features to provide an in‐time diagnosis. This model matched the 100% specificity of traditional criteria (MSIS 2011, ICM 2018, EBJIS 2021) while exceeding their sensitivity (52.63%–78.58%) [34].

A major advantage is ML's ability to be trained on a dataset and prospectively predict outcomes, unlike traditional regression models that rely on static analysis [35]. This capability is particularly valuable in the diagnosis of PJI or its eradication, which guides clinical decision‐making to improve outcomes. Furthermore, it integrates complex multimodal data types, including patient demographics, clinical features, imaging, serum studies, and synovial studies, leading to more comprehensive diagnostic assessments. ML also shows flexibility in managing missing or incomplete data, often present in retrospective analysis. Some studies demonstrated how diagnostic accuracy could remain high when using methods such as multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to resolve missing data when certain data points were unavailable for participants [26].

Challenges of ML

ML models face challenges with accessing large amounts of high‐quality data for training [32]. In the case of missing data, such as incomplete serum study data, it may need to be excluded. Studies note that data collection can be difficult, particularly for tests that are not routinely collected, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate, which could have enhanced the model's predictive capabilities [32]. This has been noted across multiple studies in the review as a limitation of retrospective analysis [19, 35].

Many studies are limited by single‐institution data and relatively short follow‐up periods, which can lead to underestimating PJI occurrences [30]. The lack of external validation across different patient populations remains a significant concern. Models often require larger multicentre datasets with external validation to ensure they are representative and increase generalisability.

The “black box” nature of some ML algorithms can pose a challenge, as it can be difficult to fully comprehend how these models arrive at their decisions. This lack of interpretability may affect clinicians’ trust and willingness to implement ML into their workflow [35]. While ML models can provide helpful insight into PJI diagnostics, their adoption into clinical practice requires standardisation and consideration of regulatory requirements, as well as improving the interpretability of ML models. Feature importance analysis can be undertaken using methods such as Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), which assign a value to each feature and indicate its contribution to the model's performance [23]. For instance, when diagnosing PJI after primary arthroplasty, SHAP values may highlight synovial studies such as alpha‐defensin and CRP as key drivers of predictions.

Ethical Considerations

ML models hold significant potential to enhance treatment decision‐making for PJI by improving risk stratification and surgical management. However, ethical considerations must be addressed to ensure the responsible use of ML in surgical practice. Careful attention must be paid to potential biases in the datasets used to train ML algorithms, as these may disproportionately affect certain patient populations if the training data lacks diversity. This concern is especially relevant given that most existing studies have relied on single‐institution databases for PJI prediction, which may limit the generalisability of their findings. Incorporating national or international databases, insurance claims, or centralised laboratory data could help ensure that ML models are trained on more diverse and representative patient populations, improving generalisability and reducing the risk of bias in algorithm development and validation.

Quality Appraisal

Quality appraisal of the included studies was conducted using the APPRAISE‐AI tool, which is used to assess the quality of AI studies in medicine in key domains such as data quality, model evaluation, and transparency to provide a score out of 100 [29]. The higher scoring studies gained points through obtaining data from multiple institutions in different countries, utilised an external cohort for validation, provided a thorough description of the ML methodology and hyperparameter tuning used, evaluated models using calibration plots and decision curve analysis to demonstrate impact on clinical outcomes, and were transparent with the data, source code, and application used to run the model. Weaker studies obtained data from a single institution, lacked stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria with explanations, poor clarity regarding management of missing data, utilised random data splitting (e.g., 80:20 train‐test split), omitted description of hyperparameter tuning, and failed to include calibration plots or assess the clinical utility of using the ML model.

Conclusion

ML models demonstrate significant potential to transform PJI diagnosis following total hip and knee arthroplasty, achieving high diagnostic accuracy across the reviewed studies. By integrating multimodal features, including patient demographics, comorbidities, and laboratory studies, ML in some cases outperforms traditional criteria such as MSIS 2011 and ICM 2018. Future research should prioritise multicentre studies with standardised, diverse datasets, external validation, and interpretable methods like SHAP to enhance generalisability and clinical adoption. By addressing these challenges, ML can significantly improve PJI diagnosis and treatment decision‐making, ultimately reducing patient morbidity and optimising surgical outcomes.
