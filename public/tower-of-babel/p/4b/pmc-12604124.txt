Retrospectively reconstructed body mass index change patterns and their association with frailty among American adults: national health and Nutrition Examination survey 2007-2018
Authors: Wenrui Shi, Yangbin Shi, Jieun Park, Shaohui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBody mass index (BMI) has been identified as a critical risk factor for frailty. However, studies assessing the impact of dynamic changes in BMI across years on frailty are still limited. The current analysis aimed to explore the association between different BMI change patterns and frailty in a representative American population.MethodsWe analyzed 9,755 adults aged ≥36 years from the United States National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2007-2018. Frailty was defined as ≥3 of weakness, low physical activity, exhaustion, slow walking speed, and unintentional weight loss. Latent class analysis derived BMI change patterns.ResultsThe prevalence of frailty was 11.49%. Clustering analysis identified five distinct BMI change patterns: Maintain normal BMI (mean frailty score 0.82), Gradual increase to overweight (0.80), Rapid increase to overweight (1.14), Increase to obesity (1.31), and Increasing obesity (1.43). Compared to the "Maintain normal BMI" group, the "Rapid increase to overweight"(OR: 1.94, 95%CI: 1.24-3.03), "Increase to obesity"(OR: 2.46, 95%CI: 1.57-3.86), and "Increasing obesity"(OR: 2.96, 95%CI:1.61-4.81) groups had significantly higher frailty risk even with adjustment of sociodemographic, anthropometric, laboratory, and medical history factors, but the “gradual increase to overweight” group showed insignificant increase of frailty risk (OR: 1.03, 95%CI: 0.66-1.60). Furthermore, this association remained robust among several subpopulations.ConclusionRapid transitions to overweight or obesity and increasing obesity were linked to higher frailty risk compared to maintaining a normal weight. A gradual increase to overweight did not significantly affect frailty risk. Severity and speed of BMI changes are key factors of frailty.

Introduction

As individuals age, they typically experience a gradual accumulation of health-related deficits, which eventually culminate in a state of frailty. This clinical condition, marked by heightened vulnerability, reflects significant dysregulation within the biologically complex and dynamic systems that are intrinsic to the aging process [1,2]. Older adults with frailty are at increased risk for various health problems, such as falls, hospitalizations, disabilities, chronic illnesses, and mortality [3,4]. As the aging population grows and life expectancy increases, frailty has emerged as a critical public health issue. Therefore, identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors for frailty is essential for improving health outcomes.

Body mass index (BMI) has been identified as a modifiable risk factor of frailty [5]. A systemic review summarized that community-dwelling older adults with underweight, overweight, and obesity had an increased risk of frailty compared to those with a normal BMI value [6]. Furthermore, a recent epidemiological analysis showed that being overweight or obese is associated with higher frailty levels, and percent body fat significantly mediated the relationship between BMI levels and frailty [7]. However, the above studies focused on the impact of a single time measurement of BMI on frailty risk. Since body weight and BMI are dynamic parameters, and there has been an increasing trend of weight gain over the past several decades [8], the effect of different change patterns of BMI over time on the risk of frailty deserves more attention.

Results from a series of studies support the assumption that different patterns of BMI change have a varying impact on the risk of frailty. Early in 2013, Strandberg et al. analyzed data from 1114 participants (mean age of 47 years in 1974, older Finnish men) in the Helsinki Businessmen Study and revealed that participants with weight loss had an increased risk of frailty compared to those with a consistently normal weight or weight gain [9]. In 2016, Mezuk et al. classified 10827 participants (mean age: 65.14, retired American adults) from the Health and Retirement Study into four groups: weight gain, weight loss, consistent overweight, and consistent obesity. Contrary to the conclusion from Strandberg et al. results from Mezuk et al. showed that the weight gain class had the highest likelihood of incident frailty [10]. In 2020, Landre et al. also evaluated the association between BMI change and frailty in 11,784 participants aged 35–50 years at the time of 1989 (French occupational cohort followed into older age) [11]; The BMI change patterns in their population demonstrated that both increasing BMI and consistently high BMI were associated with frailty. A recent study based on 4509 subjects aged ≥45 years attending the Tromsø study in 1994–1995 (Tromsø4) and 2015–2016 (Tromsø7) (community-dwelling adults in Norway) showed that higher odds of pre-frailty/frailty were observed among those in ‘overweight to obesity’ or ‘increasing obesity’ patterns than those with stable normal BMI [12]. Most studies focus on the trend of BMI (gain or loss). Trend alone can miss two key features. Magnitude shows how much BMI has increased over time. Velocity demonstrates how fast it has increased. Magnitude reflects the cumulative exposure to adiposity, linking higher metabolic/inflammatory burden and mechanical load to frailty. Velocity captures recent acceleration of gain, which may precede functional decline and provides an actionable signal for early intervention. Therefore, considering both magnitude and velocity may capture the risk of prevalent frailty better than trend alone.

To address the limitations of previous studies, we used latent class analysis (LCA) to identify typical patterns of BMI change based on self-reported weight at 4 life stages (25 years old, 10 years prior to enrollment, 1 year before enrollment, during enrollment). Unlike earlier research that grouped people using simple categories or straight-line trends, our method detects hidden subgroups with similar weight-change histories. This approach enables us to capture not only the magnitude of BMI changes, but also the rate of change. By applying this method to nationally representative data from the American population, our study aims to better understand how different long-term BMI patterns relate to frailty.

Methods

Study population

This study utilizes data from the American National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), an ongoing cross-sectional survey conducted biennially by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). To ensure the sample represents the American population, a clustered random sampling method was employed, supplemented with calibration variables to enhance representativeness. Comprehensive details on the survey design and methodology are available on the NHANES official website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/analytic_guidelines.aspx), where the data is also freely accessible to researchers (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.html). This analysis incorporates data from six NHANES cycles (2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018) and focuses on individuals aged 36 and older. To ensure a valid and chronologically ordered reconstruction of BMI change patterns, our study restricted the analysis to participants aged 36 years and older. The threshold was necessary because BMI values were calculated from self-reported weights at four life stages: age 25, 10 years ago, 1 year ago, and current. Including only participants aged ≥36 ensured that these reference points were temporally distinct and logically sequential, allowing for consistent BMI change pattern modeling. Figure 1 outlines the enrollment process. A total of 9,755 participants were ultimately included in the analysis. Since the NHANES study design was approved by the NCHS Institutional Ethics Review Board, the ethics review board of Shanghai Chest Hospital has provided an ethics exemption (KS(Y)25471) for this analysis. This study was also conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measurement of BMI change patterns

The current weight was measured when the participants were enrolled in the NHANES survey, while weights at 25 years old, 10 years ago, and 1 year ago were documented according to participants’ answers to the following questions: ‘How much did you weigh at age 25?’ ‘How much did you weigh 10 years ago?’ and ‘How much did you weigh a year ago?’, respectively. Since this study only included participants with complete responses to these questions, it was assumed that their height remained consistent over time. Therefore, BMI values at age 25, 10 years ago, and 1 year ago were calculated by dividing the reported weight at each time point by the height measured during the survey.

After achieving the BMI history data, BMI change patterns were classified through LCA [13,14]. The computation of LCA is based on a probabilistic model, assuming that an unobserved latent categorical variable determines the joint distribution of observed variables. Under the assumption of conditional independence, the associations among observed variables are attributed to the latent classes. LCA estimates model parameters, including the probabilities of latent classes and the conditional probabilities of observed variables within each class, by maximizing the log-likelihood function. The Expectation-Maximization algorithm was used for computation, with the E-step calculating the posterior probabilities of each sample belonging to latent classes, and the M-step updating the class probabilities and conditional probabilities iteratively until convergence was achieved. Additionally, model selection is achieved using information criteria, such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), to help select the optimal number of latent classes. The number of BMI change patterns was determined based on AIC and interpretability. The 5-class model showed the best statistical fit and produced clinically meaningful patterns (Table S1). We quantitatively profiled each latent class using means (95% CIs) of BMI at age 25 and current BMI, and the annualized BMI change rate (kg/m2 per year), computed as (Current BMI-25 years old BMI)/(age-25).

Outcome

Frailty was assessed using a modified Fried frailty phenotype comprising five components [15], all derived from self-reported NHANES questionnaire items:Weakness, defined as self-reported lifting or carrying difficulty (PFQ061E = 2, 3, or 4);Low physical activity, defined as being in the highest quintile of sedentary time for age group, based on reported daily sedentary minutes (PAD680);Exhaustion, self-reported feelings of tiredness, little energy (DPQ040 = 2 or 3);Slow walking speed, defined as self-reported difficulty walking between rooms on the same floor (PFQ061H = 2, 3, or 4);Unintentional weight loss, defined as self-reported unintentional loss of ≥10 lbs in the past year (WHD020, WHD050, and WHQ060).

Frailty was defined as meeting three or more of the five criteria. All five components were consistently available across all NHANES cycles from 2007 to 2018. Frailty was ascertained at the NHANES examination; hence participants were assessed at different ages. NHANES assigned a unique participant ID and each person was surveyed once, with cycles constituting independent cross-sections (no longitudinal linkage). Age at survey was included in all multivariable models and age-stratified analyses.

Covariates

The covariates included demographic variables such as age, sex, race, education level, marital status, and the ratio of family income to poverty (PIR), as well as current smoking and drinking status, all of which were collected through standardized questionnaires. Race was categorized as Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races, including multiracial. Education level was categorized as less than high school, high school graduate/General Educational Development or equivalent, and more than high school. Marital status was grouped into never married, married/living with a partner, and divorced, separated, or widowed. PIR was classified into three categories: <1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, and >3.5.

Medical history data were also collected via questionnaires. Anti-diabetic therapy was determined as answering ‘yes’ to the question ‘Take diabetic pills to lower blood sugar’ or ‘Taking insulin now’. ‘Diagnosed diabetes’ was defined as an affirmative answer to the question ‘Doctor told you have diabetes’. Diabetes was diagnosed if fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7 mmol/L and/or self-reported use of anti-diabetic therapy and/or had diagnosed diabetes [16]. Anti-hypertensive treatment was documented when participants responded ‘yes’ to the question: ‘Now taking prescribed medicine for hypertension’. Hypertension was defined as an average systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mmHg or higher and/or an average diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 90 mmHg or higher. Additionally, self-reported use of antihypertensive medication was also considered as an indicator of hypertension [17].

Laboratory tests were performed in CDC-certified laboratories. FPG was measured using the oxygen rate method on the Beckman DxC800 modular chemistry analyzer. Serum creatinine (Scr) was assessed using the Jaffe rate method on the DxC800 modular chemistry platform. Blood lipid levels were quantified via enzymatic assays conducted on the Roche Modular P and Roche Cobas 6000 chemistry analyzers. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI formula [18].

Statistical analysis

Due to the NHANES survey design, statistical weights were applied to all data during analysis (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/weighting.aspx). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and 95% confidence intervals (CI), while continuous variables were presented as mean values with corresponding 95% CIs. To compare categorical variables, we used the Chi-square test, and for continuous variables, we employed the t-test. LCA was employed to divide participants into different BMI change patterns. A brief explanation of LCA is recorded in the above section. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to explore the association between different BMI change patterns and prevalent frailty, and the results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Covariates in the Logistic regression models were selected based on three criteria: (1) variables showed potential statistical association with frailty in univariate regression (p < 0.10, age, sex, race, education, marriage status, PIR, smoking, current BMI, WC, mSBP, FPG, TC, HDL-c, eGFR, anti-hypertensive therapy, anti-diabetic therapy); (2) variables that have been identified as frailty-related factors by prior literature (age, sex, race, drinking, smoking, current BMI, WC, mSBP, FPG, TC, LDL-c, HDL-c, eGFR); and (3) variables recognized as clinically relevant to frailty (education, marriage status, PIR, anti-hypertensive therapy, anti-diabetic therapy). The final set of covariates included sociodemographic, behavioral, anthropometric, laboratory, and medical history variables. This approach aimed to capture the most relevant sociodemographic and clinical confounders based on the available data. We defined the models as: Crude: no adjustment; Model 1 (adjusting sociodemographic/behavioral covariates): age, sex, race, education, marital status, PIR, current smoking, current drinking; Model 2 (plus clinical/anthropometric/laboratory parameters): Model 1 + current BMI, WC, mSBP, FPG, TC, LDL-c, HDL-c, eGFR, anti-hypertensive therapy, anti-diabetic therapy. Subgroup analyses were pre-specified to examine potential effect modification by factors commonly associated with frailty. These factors included age, sex, race, PIR, and the presence of diabetes or hypertension. Age was stratified at 60 years to reflect the transition to older adulthood and typical retirement status. Race was categorized as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and others. PIR was grouped into three categories: <1.3, 1.3–3.5, and >3.5, based on previous studies [19,20].

We reconducted the LCA analysis with the population, excluding participants with missing values of frailty and weight history data to test whether the missing values of covariates were randomly distributed across different BMI change patterns (Table S2). Then, multiple imputation by chained equations was conducted under the assumption that the missing data were missing at random. The imputation model incorporated all covariates included in the primary analysis, as well as frailty status and BMI pattern groups. 5 imputed datasets were generated, and pooled estimates were compared with our main results (Table S3).

No formal sample-size calculation was performed because this was a secondary analysis of NHANES. Precision was evaluated using 95% confidence intervals. Given 9,755 participants and ∼11% frailty (n = 1,121), the multivariable models achieved events-per-parameter well above conventional thresholds (>10), and each latent class had sufficient size to support stable estimation.

All the statistical analysis was conducted with Stata Statistical Software (version 18.0; StataCorp. LLC. 4905 Lakeway Drive, College Station, TX, 77845, USA) and Statistical software packages R (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation). LCA analysis was conducted via the ‘poLCA’ package. A two-tailed P value less than 0.05 was recognized as statistically significant.

The results showed that the major contributors to frailty were weakness, low physical activity, and exhaustion.

Across survey cycles from 2007 to 2018, frailty prevalence showed distinct patterns across age groups (Table S6). A notable upward trend was observed among younger individuals. Specifically, prevalence in the <50 years group increased from 12.76% to 21.52%, and in the 50–64 years group varied from 13.26% in 2007–2008 to 14.21% in 2017–2018, with a peak of 17.46% in 2015–2016. In contrast, prevalence among adults aged ≥65 remained relatively stable. These shifts suggest a rising burden of frailty in younger populations over time.

The clustering analysis divided the participants into five classes based on their patterns of BMI change over time (Figure 2 and Table 1). Quantitatively, Maintain normal BMI group remained near the normal range of BMI from age 25 (21.42 kg/m2, 95% CI: 21.28–21.55 kg/m2) to the time of enrollment (22.82 kg/m2, 95% CI: 22.66–22.98 kg/m2) with a very small annualized change (0.05 kg/m2/year, 95% CI: 0.04–0.05 kg/m2/year). The association between BMI change patterns and prevalent frailty was summarized in Table 2. Compared to participants maintained normal BMI, ‘Gradual increase to overweight’ group did not have an increased risk of frailty (OR = 0.92, p = 0.621), but ‘Rapid increase to overweight’ group (OR: 2.12, 95% CI 1.44–3.13), ‘Increase to obesity’ group (OR:2.93, 95% CI: 2.22–3.87), and ‘Increasing obesity’ group (OR: 3.18, 95% CI: 2.33–4.33) had significantly increased risk of frailty. After adjustment of age, sex, race, education, marriage status, PIR, current drinking and smoking status, the difference of risk increment between ‘Rapid increase to overweight’ group, ‘Increase to obesity’ group, and ‘Increasing obesity’ group enlarged (OR: 2.08 vs. 3.20 vs. 3.91). And the association between BMI change patterns and frailty was similar before and after multiple imputation (Table S3), suggesting that the primary findings were robust to missing covariate data.

Discussion

In this analysis, we identified a significant association between multiple BMI change patterns and the prevalence of frailty. Our data indicated that rapid transitions from normal weight to overweight, from normal weight to obesity, and increasing obesity were linked to a higher risk of prevalent frailty compared to maintaining a normal weight. In contrast, a gradual increase from normal weight to overweight did not significantly elevate the risk of frailty. Moreover, the risk increment was most pronounced in individuals with increasing obesity, followed by those whose BMI shifted from normal weight to obesity. The lowest risk increase was observed in those who underwent a rapid transition from normal weight to overweight. These findings suggest that both the severity of obesity and the speed of BMI changes play a significant role in influencing the risk of frailty.

One of the major strengths of our study lies in its refined methodological approach to characterizing long-term BMI change. Previous studies grouped individuals into categories such as ‘maintaining normal BMI,’ ‘gaining weight,’ ‘losing weight,’ or ‘persistently obese.’ These classifications often overlook the rate and magnitude of BMI change over time, which may conceal important intra-group heterogeneity and result in information loss [10–12]. In contrast, our study utilized LCA and multiple time-point BMI data across the life course to classify five distinct BMI change pattern classes, enabling us to differentiate between gradual and rapid increases, and between early-onset versus long-standing obesity. Therefore, our findings serve as a refined extension of previous research. By considering the rate and magnitude of BMI change across multiple time points, our study extends prior work by capturing more detailed BMI change patterns. This refinement helps to reveal heterogeneity in frailty risk that earlier categorizations may have missed. A notable point is that our analysis did not reveal such a BMI decreasing pattern. This divergence is likely attributable to demographic trends: the BMI level and obesity prevalence have steadily increased over the past decades [21]. Given our limited sample size (less than 10000), participants with sustained BMI decline throughout adulthood (695, 7.12% of the subjects) may have been too few to form a latent class. As a result, individuals with sustained BMI decline were likely underrepresented in our sample and may not have formed a distinct class in the data-driven clustering. Another strength of our study lies in the robustness of the findings supported by multiple imputation. To account for missing data, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation methods, and the results remained consistent with those from the complete-case analysis (Table S3). The direction and magnitude of the associations between BMI change patterns and prevalent frailty were comparable across both datasets, reinforcing the stability and reliability of our conclusions.

Although unintentional weight loss is a core component of the Fried frailty phenotype, emerging evidence suggests that both weight loss and weight gain can elevate the risk of frailty. Mezuk et al. used data from the Health and Retirement Study and identified four distinct BMI change patterns: weight gain, weight loss, consistent overweight, and consistent obesity [10]. Compared to the consistent overweight group, individuals in the weight gain group had the highest risk of developing frailty over a 10-year period (OR = 3.61; 95% CI: 2.39–5.46), followed by the weight loss group (OR = 2.81; 95% CI: 1.84–4.30) and the consistent obesity group (OR = 2.72; 95% CI: 2.06–3.58). These findings challenge the assumption that frailty is predominantly linked to weight loss, suggesting that excessive or increasing adiposity may also lead to physical decline and frailty. Several functional mechanisms may explain the association between increasing BMI and frailty. A key factor is sarcopenic obesity, a condition characterized by increased fat mass and declining muscle strength, which can lead to physical weakness and impaired mobility, which are two central components of frailty [22,23]. Excess body weight also imposes greater mechanical load on joints, contributing to chronic musculoskeletal discomfort and reduced physical activity. Moreover, obesity is often associated with lower cardiorespiratory fitness and impaired pulmonary function, which may cause early fatigue, poor endurance, and increased vulnerability to exhaustion [24,25]. These physiological limitations restrict mobility and energy output, reinforcing a cycle of physical decline. In this context, an increasing BMI may indicate declining functional capacity rather than improved health, suggesting a frailty subtype driven by physical inactivity and low physiological reserve. In the current analysis, we did not identify a separate weight-loss class. Only 695 of 9,755 participants (7.12%) showed a net decrease in BMI between anchors, and all were assigned to the ‘Maintain normal BMI’ class. This small number could not form a distinct class. Therefore, our findings mainly apply to stable or increasing BMI patterns. If weight loss increases frailty risk as illustrated by the previous studies, placing these participants in the ‘Maintain normal BMI’ group may weaken differences between groups. This point may partly explain why the difference between ‘Gradual increase to overweight’ and ‘Maintain normal BMI’ was not statistically significant, and it also suggests that the currently estimated higher risks for ‘Rapid increase to overweight’ and ‘Increasing obesity’ are likely conservative rather than exaggerated.

In the subgroup analysis, the results displayed that the main findings were robust among subpopulations of age, sex, race, income, diabetes, and hypertension, with all P for interaction >0.05. The association between BMI change patterns and prevalent frailty among most subgroups followed a similar pattern to that in the whole population. However, in a subgroup of age ≥ 60 years old, the risk of prevalent frailty only got a slight and insignificant increment event in the ‘Increase to obesity’ and ‘Increasing obesity’ groups; On the contrary, the risk of prevalent frailty showed a greater extent in the latter three classes among participants aged less than 60 years old. With a mean age of 63.80 years, the discrepancy between the two age subgroups could not be explained by a lack of sample size. Obesity paradox could be a possible mechanism underlying this phenomenon [26]. Increased body fat can serve as an energy reserve, which may help mitigate the adverse effects of frailty, such as unintentional weight loss and muscle weakness, that are common in older adults. Having extra fat stores can provide energy during illness or periods of malnutrition, which are key factors in the development of frailty. Moreover, a better nutritional status, often reflected by a higher BMI, can help maintain muscle mass and strength, thereby reducing the risk of frailty. However, the protective effects are more likely to occur when fat is accompanied by muscle mass, rather than in cases of sarcopenic obesity (fat accumulation with loss of muscle) [27,28]. BMI is a rough indicator of weight relative to height, but it doesn’t distinguish between muscle and fat. In older adults, especially those with sarcopenia, a normal or low BMI might reflect the loss of muscle mass and strength that contributes to frailty [29].

The risk increment was also minimal in the black subgroup, a finding that aligns with previous research. In 2006, Hirsch et al. reported that the heightened risk of frailty linked to African-American race was less pronounced among obese individuals compared to those of white race [30]. Several possible mechanism could explain this phenomenon: First, BMI can misestimate adiposity across races: for the same BMI, Blacks tend to have more lean mass and higher bone density [31,32], so BMI may overstate fatness and blunt its link to frailty. Second, fat distribution differs at a given BMI, Blacks often have less visceral and more subcutaneous, which may lead to a lower risk [33,34]. Third, body build and strength can shift frailty components (e.g. grip strength), further weakening the BMI–frailty association in this subgroup [35]. Finally, the Black sample was smaller (∼8.53%), leading to less precise estimates.

The rationale for including adults in their 30s–40s deserves discussion. While frailty research has traditionally focused on older adults, recent studies have demonstrated that signs of frailty can emerge in 30–40 years of age. Notably, population-based data suggest that frailty prevalence among individuals in their 30s and 40s already exceeds 3%, indicating early vulnerability in a subset of the population [36,37]. Including adults aged 36 and above in our analysis could enhance the generalizability of our findings across a broader age spectrum. In addition, individuals with less favorable BMI change patterns in our sample (Rapid increase to overweight, Increase to obesity, Increasing obesity) tended to be younger on average (Table 1). The associations between these patterns and frailty were also more pronounced in participants under 60 years of age (Figure 4). Although causal relationships cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional study, these patterns may suggest that changes in midlife BMI are already associated with a higher frailty burden and merit greater attention in early preventive health strategies. Furthermore, including a broader age range could reduce the risk of selection bias associated with excluding participants solely based on age.

Our findings carry meaningful implications for clinical practice and public health policy. The results suggest that different long-term BMI changing patterns are associated with varying risks of prevalent frailty. Specifically, individuals who experience rapid increases in BMI or increasing obesity are more likely to be frail. For community healthcare providers, this information can help to improve efficiency by identifying high-risk individuals based on easily accessible BMI history data. Instead of assessing frailty in all older adults, clinicians can prioritize those with unfavorable BMI patterns for further evaluation or early intervention. This targeted approach can reduce unnecessary workload while ensuring that those at greatest risk receive timely support. From a policy perspective, focusing prevention efforts on these high-risk groups may help to maximize the impact of limited healthcare resources and support more cost-effective public health strategies.

Our findings suggest that primary care clinicians and public-health practitioners could use BMI trajectories to flag adults at higher frailty risk, particularly midlife and older adults showing rapid gains or progressing obesity. Potential practical actions include: (i) incorporating BMI change rate into annual reviews to identify accelerating weight gain; (ii) offering targeted weight-gain prevention (nutrition counseling, physical-activity programs, sleep and alcohol counseling); (iii) earlier frailty/sarcopenia screening and fall-prevention referrals for those with rapid increases; and (iv) periodic self-monitoring of weight to maintain stable trajectories. Notably, a gradual shift to overweight did not materially elevate the risk of prevalent frailty in our data, so resources may be prioritized toward rapid transitions and increasing obesity. In general, integrating BMI trajectory and changing rate into routine care may help prioritize early prevention for adults with rapid gains or progressing obesity, who appear most vulnerable to frailty.

In our study, frail individuals were on average younger and had higher BMI levels (Table S5), indicating the presence of sarcopenic obesity. Sarcopenic obesity is a condition characterized by the coexistence of excess fat with the loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength [38]. It contributes to frailty primarily by masking muscle deterioration beneath a higher body mass [23,39]. Individuals with sarcopenic obesity may appear overweight or even well-nourished based on body weight, but their muscle function is significantly compromised. This change leads to reduced walking speed, impaired balance, and higher risk of falls, thereby fulfilling frailty criteria such as weakness and slowness, even in the absence of weight loss. This hypothesis was verified by the data in Table 1, unhealthy obesity groups had a higher prevalence of weakness, low physical activity, exhaustion, and slow walking speed. Furthermore, we observed a steady increase in frailty prevalence among adults aged <50 years across NHANES survey cycles, rising from 12.76% in 2007–2008 to 21.52% in 2017–2018, while prevalence among those aged ≥65 remained relatively stable (Table S6). These patterns suggest that frailty is increasingly affecting younger age groups, consistent with lifestyle and environmental shifts over the past decade [40,41]. Factors such as rising obesity, sedentary behavior, poor diet quality, and increasing psychological stress in younger populations may contribute to this shift. Our findings underscore the need to reframe frailty as not only a condition of aging but also a public health concern in midlife and even earlier, and the younger age and higher BMI level in the frail population suggest the necessity for intervention of obesity and prevention of sarcopenic obesity. However, the above explanation should also be validated by more longitudinal studies with repeated assessment of body composition.

Obesity significantly contributes to the development and progression of frailty through several harmful mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, muscle dysfunction, mechanical stress, nutritional deficiencies, and mental health deterioration. Chronic inflammation is a key driver of frailty in obese individuals. Adipose tissue, especially visceral fat, produces pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. These cytokines promote a catabolic environment that leads to muscle wasting (sarcopenia), insulin resistance, and impaired physical function, all of which are characteristic features of frailty [42–44]. Inflammation also disrupts muscle regeneration, further accelerating the loss of muscle mass and strength, leading to frailty syndrome [45]. Muscle dysfunction is another critical issue. Obesity, particularly when combined with sarcopenia, leads to an imbalance between fat and muscle mass. The increased fat mass, while providing energy reserves, contributes to muscle loss and weakness [38]. Fat infiltration, including intermuscular adipose tissue and intramyocellular lipids in skeletal muscle, is reported to be associated with muscle weakness [46]. This muscle dysfunction reduces strength, mobility, and endurance, making physical tasks more challenging. Reduced muscle mass and function are central to the frailty phenotype. Mechanical stress from excess weight exacerbates joint problems, particularly in weight-bearing joints like the knees and hips. This additional stress accelerates the onset of osteoarthritis, joint pain, and functional limitations, all of which lead to reduced physical activity [47]. Lack of movement further weakens muscles and accelerates frailty. Nutritional deficiencies are also common among obese individuals [48]. Despite consuming excess calories, many obese individuals have poor diet quality, lacking essential nutrients such as protein, vitamins, and minerals that are critical for maintaining muscle and bone health. This malnutrition contributes to muscle weakness, impaired immune function, and osteoporosis, all of which increase the risk of frailty. Finally, obesity has a negative impact on mental health. Depression, anxiety, and social isolation are more prevalent in obese individuals, which can reduce motivation for physical activity and further exacerbate frailty [49,50]. In conclusion, the harmful aspects of obesity—such as inflammation, muscle dysfunction, joint stress, nutritional deficiencies, and mental health challenges—collectively increase the risk of frailty.

Our study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of the NHANES dataset prevents us from establishing a causal relationship between BMI change patterns and incident frailty. Although our findings are suggestive, longitudinal cohort studies are needed to confirm these associations. Second, excluding participants with missing weight history or covariate data may introduce selection bias. However, the distribution of missingness appeared in an approximately random manner across BMI change patterns (Table S2), and the results based on multiple imputation (Table S3) were broadly consistent with the main findings. Furthermore, key demographic characteristics and frailty prevalence were similar before and after exclusion (Table S4), suggesting the impact of potential selection bias may be limited. Nevertheless, studies with fewer missing values should be used to verify our results in the future. Third, the use of self-reported weight history in NHANES raises concerns about recall bias and potential inaccuracies in the data, and such bias cannot be fully corrected by statistical means and could introduce measurement errors. Furthermore, using age 25 as a recall point introduced variable observation windows across participants of different ages. For older individuals, a longer time span may increase the chance of ‘Gradual increase to overweight’ being overrepresented, while the percentage of ‘Rapid increase to overweight’ may be underestimated. Since our enrolled participants had a mean age of 64.47 years. The potential misclassification would bias the estimates toward the null and reduce the observed difference between the ‘gradual’ and ‘rapid’ increase groups. Therefore, our conclusion—’Rapid increase to overweight’ was more strongly associated with frailty than ‘Gradual increase to overweight’—may be a conservative estimate. The actual risk difference could be greater, further supporting the robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, future studies should explore uniform, time-based recall points or use prospectively measured data to validate our findings. Fourth, our study assumed that participants’ height remained constant over time. Although age-related height reduction is generally minor (usually within a few centimeters), it may amplify the effect of weight gain on BMI, potentially influencing BMI change pattern classification. As NHANES lacks repeated height measurements, we were unable to evaluate this impact. Future studies with longitudinal monitoring of both height and weight are warranted to validate our findings. Fifth, although our models adjusted for multiple covariates, potential residual confounding by unmeasured factors, such as physical activity, cognitive function, and nutritional status, could still have their impact. These factors are linked to both BMI change and frailty. For instance, individuals with adequate physical activity may be more likely to maintain a stable BMI and simultaneously preserve muscle strength, reducing their frailty risk. Conversely, cognitive impairment could lead to unhealthy dietary habits or a sedentary lifestyle, contributing to both weight gain and functional decline. Similarly, malnutrition may lead to sarcopenia or frailty, regardless of BMI category. Therefore, future studies that consider lifestyle, functional, and dietary variables would help clarify the independent role of BMI change patterns in frailty development. Finally, since NHANES was conducted solely in the United States, the generalizability of our results to other populations is limited. Although our analysis showed consistent results among White, Black, and other races, further research in diverse populations is essential to assess the broader applicability of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings highlight the significant role of both the severity and the speed of BMI changes in frailty. Rapid transitions to overweight or obesity and increasing obesity are strongly associated with increased frailty risk, likely reflecting the harmful effects of long-term excess adiposity on muscle strength, metabolism, and physical function. In contrast, gradual weight gain from normal weight to overweight appears to have a minimal impact. Given the increasing prevalence of frailty among adults under 50 years, early monitoring of BMI changes and implementation of preventive strategies from midlife, around 40 to 50 years of age, may be crucial to mitigate the long-term burden of frailty.
