A national survey of gastrointestinal nematodes in Australian dairy goats using faecal egg counts and deep amplicon sequencing
Authors: Endris A Ali, Abdul Ghafar, Ghazanfar Abbas, Bahar E Mustafa, Sandra Baxendell, Elysia Ling, Charles Gauci, Ian Beveridge, Mark A Stevenson, Abdul Jabbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal nematodes (GINs) constrain goat health, welfare and productivity worldwide. In Australia, GINs are recognised as the highest-priority endemic pathogens of goats; however, comprehensive national data documenting the prevalence of GINs in goats remain limited. Here, we investigated the prevalence, infection intensity, species diversity and risk factors for GINs in dairy goats. In a cross-sectional study (November 2023 and July 2024), faecal samples (n = 1028) from 68 herds were analysed using the modified McMaster technique. Strongylid species were identified in 500 samples using deep amplicon sequencing of the nuclear ribosomal DNA second internal transcribed spacer. Animal-level prevalence estimates were adjusted for the effect of clustering of GIN positivity at the herd level, and risk factors associated with strongylid faecal egg counts (FEC) were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models. Overall, 92% (95% confidence interval, CI: 89–95%) of goats and 100% of herds were positive for at least one GIN taxon. Strongylids showed the highest adjusted animal-level prevalence (91%, 95% CI: 87–94%), followed by Trichuris spp. (13%, 95% CI: 10–17%) and Nematodirus spp. (5%, 95% CI: 3–8%). Strongylid FECs were markedly overdispersed, with 30% of goats contributing 80% of total egg output. Age, climatic zone, deworming history and co-infection with Eimeria were significantly associated with strongylid FECs. Metabarcoding identified 11 strongylid species, dominated by Haemonchus contortus, Teladorsagia circumcincta and Trichostrongylus colubriformis. Haemonchus contortus was most abundant and prevalent nationally, underscoring its central role in production loss and the dissemination of anthelmintic resistance. These findings provide nationally representative evidence to inform sustainable parasite control in goats.Keywords: Gastrointestinal nematodes, Next-generation sequencing, Nemabiome, Haemonchus contortus, Dairy goats, Faecal egg counts, Australia

Introduction

Gastrointestinal nematode (GIN) infections are among the major constraints to the health, welfare and productivity of grazing ruminants worldwide (Craig, 2018), causing substantial economic losses (Charlier et al., 2020). In sheep and goats, strongylids are of particular importance as the principal drivers of parasitic gastroenteritis (PGE) and associated production losses (Zajac, 2006; Fthenakis and Papadopoulos, 2018). Although sheep and goats share broadly similar nematode species, goats are generally more susceptible to strongylid infections and often harbour higher worm burdens (Le Jambre and Royal, 1976), which is partly due to host species-specific behavioural, physiological and immunological differences (Hoste et al., 2010). Consequently, strongylid infections are highly prevalent across goat production systems (Avery et al., 2025; Torres-Acosta et al., 2025) and are increasingly compounded by widespread anthelmintic resistance (AR) (Baudinette et al., 2022).

Among the strongylids commonly infecting goats, Haemonchus contortus, Teladorsagia circumcincta and Trichostrongylus spp. are the most prevalent and economically significant species worldwide (Albers and Le Jambre, 1983; Zajac, 2006). These species account for the majority of clinical and subclinical PGE, with manifestations including diarrhoea, anaemia, submandibular oedema and reduced weight gain (Albers and Le Jambre, 1983; Craig, 2018), leading to substantial production losses. Other GINs, such as Chabertia ovina, Nematodirus spp., Oesophagostomum spp., Trichuris spp., and Strongyloides papillosus, are generally less prevalent and of lower clinical significance (Craig, 2018). Nevertheless, mixed-species infections are ubiquitous in grazing goats (Avery et al., 2025), complicating diagnosis and parasite control due to marked interspecies differences in fecundity, pathogenicity and susceptibility to anthelmintic drugs (Rompo et al., 2026).

Conventionally, faecal egg counting (FEC) using the modified McMaster technique has been a common method (Gordon and Whitlock, 1939) for estimating GIN infection intensity and informing deworming decisions in goats. However, FEC results are influenced by parasite biology, host age, production type, physiological status, nutrition, concurrent infections (e.g. coccidia), region and anthelmintic use (Sylvester et al., 2018; Avery et al., 2025). While FEC provides a practical and cost-effective proxy for infection intensity at both animal and herd levels (Cringoli et al., 2008), it does not accurately reflect parasite burden nor differentiate among GINs. Faecal culture, based on the morphology of the third-stage larvae, has traditionally therefore been used to determine the species composition of GINs. However, this approach is labour-intensive, relatively insensitive and prone to misclassification bias due to overlapping larval morphologies of strongylid species (Avery et al., 2025). As such, the development and application of molecular diagnostics capable of accurately characterising GINs has been a major research focus for decades (Gasser, 2025).

Since 2015, deep amplicon sequencing targeting the nuclear ribosomal DNA second internal transcribed spacer (ITS2) has enabled high-resolution characterisation of most Clade V parasitic strongylid species (Avramenko et al., 2015; Miller et al., 2024). This “nemabiome” approach allows simultaneous identification of multiple species of strongylids and estimation of their relative abundance across diverse hosts (Avramenko et al., 2015; Antonopoulos et al., 2024). Following its initial application in cattle, ITS2 metabarcoding has been increasingly employed in a range of livestock species (Miller et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2024; Ehnert et al., 2025; Kelly et al., 2025; Ochigbo et al., 2025), with goats being relatively underrepresented (Costa-Junior et al., 2021; Francis and Šlapeta, 2023; Coomansingh-Springer et al., 2025; Rompo et al., 2026).

In Australia, GINs are recognised as the highest-priority endemic pathogens affecting goats (Lane et al., 2015). Producer surveys have consistently identified GINs as a major challenge to goat health and productivity, with H. contortus, Te. circumcincta and Trichostrongylus spp. ranked as the most important species (Brunt et al., 2019; Ali et al., 2025a). Despite the expansion of the Australian dairy goat industry (Zalcman and Cowled, 2018), contemporary data on GIN prevalence, infection intensity, species diversity and risk factors in dairy goats remain limited compared with sheep and meat goats. Existing evidence is largely extrapolated from sheep (Roeber et al., 2013) or derived from opportunistic surveys of feral goats in Queensland (McKenzie et al., 1979), South Australia (Beveridge et al., 1987), and New South Wales (Francis and Šlapeta, 2023). Although these studies consistently reported high prevalence of GINs (100%), they were constrained by small sample sizes, limited geographical scope and a lack of focus on domestic goats. A more recent study from Queensland and New South Wales reported a high prevalence of GINs (97%) and infection intensity (mean FEC: 1118 eggs per gram, EPG) in meat goats, identifying H. contortus and T. colubriformis as the dominant species (Dawson, 2025). Collectively, these findings underscore the high burden of GINs in Australian goats and highlight the need for large-scale (molecular) surveillance in domestic goats, particularly in dairy production systems where co-grazing goats with alpacas, sheep and/or cattle is prevalent (Ali et al., 2025a). Dairy goats may experience distinct epidemiological patterns compared with meat goats due to differences in production physiology, immune function, management practices associated with lactation and milk yield, and limited anthelmintic options available due to milk withholding period requirements.

This first nationwide study: (i) determined the prevalence of GINs; (ii) identified strongylid species using ITS2 metabarcoding; and (iii) quantified infection intensity of GINs and associated epidemiological risk factors for strongylids in Australian dairy goats.

Materials and methods

Study population and study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2023 and July 2024 to estimate the prevalence, infection intensity, species composition and risk factors for GINs in Australian dairy goats. The source population comprised dairy goat farms across New South Wales, Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania, Victoria and Western Australia that were registered with the Dairy Goat Society of Australia as of November 2023. The eligible population included herds within these states whose goats had not been treated with anthelmintics in the two months preceding sample collection to avoid temporary suppression of parasite egg shedding following anthelmintic treatment. Application of this criterion resulted in the exclusion of 160 samples, including all samples from three herds which had been included in our earlier study (Ali et al., 2026). The study population consisted of eligible herds whose managers had previously indicated willingness to participate (Ali et al., 2025a) or consented following an email invitation in November 2023.

For this study, a farm was defined as a physical location where one or more goats are kept under a common system of management. A herd was defined as a group of goats kept on a farm, usually under a single system of management. For the most part, farms were comprised of a single herd, although the study population was comprised of a small number of corporate farms with several herds managed at the same location. Herds were categorised by herd size into small (< 20 goats), medium (20–100 goats), or large (> 100 goats) herds. The climatic zones of Australia are divided into eight categories to guide GIN control strategies in sheep and goats: (1) Western Australian winter rainfall; (2) South Australian winter rainfall; (3) Victorian winter rainfall; (4) Tasmania; (5) New South Wales non-seasonal rainfall; (6) Queensland and New South Wales summer rainfall slopes and plains; (7) Queensland and New South Wales summer rainfall tablelands and slopes; and (8) pastoral climatic zones. For our data analysis, these climatic zones were collapsed into four broader categories to reflect epidemiologically relevant rainfall and moisture regimes that influence the development and survival of free-living nematode stages, while ensuring adequate sample size and statistical power across regions, following the methodology of Rashid et al. (2019): (i) Mediterranean (zones 1 and 2); (ii) winter rainfall (zones 3 and 4); (iii) summer rainfall (zones 6 and 7); and (iv) non-seasonal rainfall (zone 5).

Sample size calculations

Sample size calculations were undertaken to determine the number of dairy goats required to estimate the prevalence of GINs with acceptable precision. Ignoring the fact that GINs positivity clusters within herds and assuming an unknown individual goat population size and that the animal-level prevalence of GIN was between 80% and 100% (Beveridge et al., 1987) and a requirement for 5% absolute error, a total of 246 goats were required. To account for the clustering of GIN positivity within herds, we assumed that 20 goats (secondary sampling units, SSUs) would be sampled per herd (primary sampling units, PSUs), and there was a relatively high intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.10 (Otte and Gumm, 1997). These specifications returned a design effect of 2.9, which gave a revised sample size of 246 × 2.9 = 713. If 20 goats were sampled per herd, this meant the required number of herds to be sampled was 713 ÷ 20 = 36. Our sample size estimates were calculated using the “epi.ssclus2estb” function in the contributed epiR package (Stevenson et al., 2025) in R version 4.4 (R Core Team, 2025).

Herd recruitment followed methods described previously (Ali et al., 2026). In total, 68 herds were recruited from six states: New South Wales (n = 16), Queensland (n = 16), South Australia (n = 1), Tasmania (n = 8), Victoria (n = 18), and Western Australia (n = 9) (Fig. 1).

Faecal sampling

Faecal samples were collected as described previously (Ali et al., 2026). Briefly, herd managers were provided with sampling kits comprised of faecal sample collection bags, gloves, ice packs for sample shipment, sample submission forms, overnight prepaid courier satchels, study details and instructions providing details of procedures for collecting and shipping faecal samples. Herd managers were requested to complete a sample submission form that included the farm name and address, contact details, date of sample collection, herd size, number of sampled goats, the identity of goats sampled, their age and sex, the date of last deworming, the name of the last anthelmintic(s) used and whether Duddingtonia flagrans spores (available commercially in Australia, New Zealand and the USA as BioWorma®) were administered as a feed additive. Herd managers were requested to seal samples in zip-lock bags and dispatch them for overnight delivery to the laboratory. Each herd manager was requested to collect 21 samples: 7 samples each from kids, weaners and adults. If a herd manager had fewer than 30 goats, all goats in the herd were sampled. Ethics approval for this study was granted by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Melbourne (AEC no. 25068).

Faecal egg counts

The number of EPGs of faeces was determined using the modified McMaster technique (Gordon and Whitlock, 1939). Briefly, 2 g of faeces were mixed with 2.5 ml of water to make a faecal slurry. A sucrose solution (55.5 ml with a specific gravity of 1.27) was added and mixed. A pipette was used to collect the liquid from the centre of the slurry before loading it into two chambers of a Whitlock Universal slide (2 × 0.5 ml). The Whitlock Universal slide was left for 10 min to allow GIN eggs to float, followed by counting the eggs using a 4× objective of a compound light microscope (Olympus CH-2, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). A multiplication factor of 30 was used to determine the number of EPG. Individual FECs were determined and reported separately as strongyle-type, Nematodirus spp. and Trichuris spp. eggs.

Strongylid species identification

Genomic DNA isolation

Genomic DNA was extracted from all individual animal samples, regardless of the sample FEC result, following an established method (Rashid et al., 2019). Briefly, 5 ml of faecal slurry supernatant was transferred into a 50-ml Falcon tube and topped up with a sucrose flotation solution to a total volume of 50 ml. Samples were centrifuged at 760× g for 10 min, and approximately 1 ml of the upper layer was transferred into sterile 1.5-ml Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20 °C until processing. For DNA extraction, concentrated egg suspensions were thawed at room temperature, and 300 μl of concentrated egg material was extracted using the DNeasy Power Soil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA was eluted in 60 μl of elution buffer, labelled with a unique sample identification number, and stored at −20 °C for molecular testing.

DNA sample selection for next-generation sequencing

Within each herd, three DNA samples were randomly selected from each age group (kids, weaners and adults) and within each of three FEC intensity categories (low, < 500 EPG; medium, 500–1000 EPG; and high, > 1000 EPG), resulting in a maximum of 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 samples per herd where a sufficient number of animals were available. In total, 596 samples were included, comprising kids (n = 122), weaners (n = 110) and adults (n = 364). These samples were obtained from Mediterranean-type (n = 94), summer (n = 161), winter (n = 239) and non-seasonal (n = 102) rainfall zones. Approximately 41% of samples had low (n = 246), 27% medium (n = 160) and 32% high (n = 190) FEC intensity. Together, these samples covered a wide range of herd sizes, production systems, sampling seasons and geographical regions, ensuring broad representation of GIN exposure across Australian dairy goat herds.

Polymerase chain reactions, library preparations and next-generation sequencing

The extracted DNA samples were subjected to a two-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the ITS2 region. In the first PCR, an approximately 400-bp region was amplified using NC1 and NC2 primers (Gasser et al., 1993), modified with Illumina adapter sequences, following a previously published study (Ghafar et al., 2020). Each PCR reaction was performed in a final volume of 25 μl containing 12.125 μl nuclease-free water, 5 μl 25 mM MgCl2, 5 μl 5× GoTaq Flexi buffer, 0.5 μl dNTPs, 0.125 μl of each primer, 0.125 μl GoTaq Flexi DNA polymerase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and 2 μl of template DNA. Thermal cycling conditions comprised an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 24 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplification success was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis.

The first PCR products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and standard laboratory protocols (Ghafar et al., 2020). Each PCR run included positive (DNA extracts from adult Te. circumcincta) and negative controls (nuclease-free water) to monitor amplification performance and contamination.

A second PCR was performed to attach unique dual indices to each amplicon. Reactions were carried out in a final volume of 21 μl, comprising 10 μl of purified PCR-1 product, 10 μl of Taq® 2× Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and 0.5 μl each of forward and reverse index primers. A negative control was included per 96-well plate to monitor potential contamination. Each plate was indexed using a unique combination of eight forward and 12 reverse index primers. Thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 18 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min.

Following indexing, 5 μl of amplicons from each sample were pooled into a reservoir and mixed thoroughly. A 50 μl aliquot of the pooled amplicons was purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads, followed by elution in 90 μl of nuclease-free water. Fragment size distributions of the purified libraries were assessed using an Agilent TapeStation. From each indexed library, 20 μl were combined, multiplexing up to 384 samples (4 × 96). Pooled libraries were then quantified using Quantus fluorometry, and library molarity was calculated based on DNA concentration and mean fragment length. To increase sequence diversity and improve cluster generation for ITS2 amplicons, a 20% PhiX control spike-in was included. Libraries and PhiX control were denatured and diluted to 2 nM prior to loading. The final library pool was diluted to 1000 pM using RSB with Tween-20, and 20 μl was loaded onto a P1 XLEAP-SBS 600-cycle flow cell. Sequencing was performed in paired-end mode on an Illumina NextSeq 1000/2000 platform at the Werribee Campus, The University of Melbourne, Australia. Run configuration and demultiplexing followed the standard NextSeq 1000/2000 workflow, with sequencing output streamed to Illumina BaseSpace (basespace.illumina.com). Each sequencing run required approximately 18 h.

Bioinformatic analysis and amplicon sequence variant assignment

Amplicon sequence data were analysed using quantitative insights into microbial ecology (QIIME 2 version 2025.4) (Bolyen et al., 2019) on the University of Melbourne high-performance computing system (Spartan). Demultiplexed paired-end FASTQ files were obtained for 596 samples and 25 controls (10 positive and 15 negative controls), and a corresponding metadata file was prepared. Raw sequence data were imported into QIIME 2, and adapter, primer and index sequences were removed using the cutadapt plugin (Martin, 2011). Sequence quality was assessed by generating interactive quality plots from a random subset of 10,000 forward and reverse reads, visualised using the QIIME 2 interactive viewer (view.qiime2.org), to guide truncation and filtering parameters.

Denoising was performed using the DADA2 plugin (Callahan et al., 2016), with forward and reverse reads truncated at 200 bp and 190 bp, respectively. This step included quality filtering, dereplication, chimera removal and paired-end read merging, resulting in denoising statistics, a feature (amplicon sequence variant, ASV) table and representative sequences.

The nemabiome ITS2 reference database (Workentine et al., 2020) (version 1.6.0) was used for taxonomic classification. Reference FASTA and taxonomy files were obtained from the nemabiome repository (www.nemabiome.ca) and used to train a Naïve Bayes classifier within QIIME 2, and ASVs were assigned to taxa using the classify-sklearn method (Pedregosa et al., 2011).

Initial taxonomic summaries confirmed strongylid species detected or identified. Subsequent filtering retained ASVs belonging to six strongylid families: Chabertiidae, Cooperiidae, Haemonchidae, Molineidae, Protostrongylidae and Trichostrongylidae, removing non-target taxa and/or potential artefacts. Protostrongylidae was included due to the detection of Muellerius capillaris in the initial taxonomic summaries. A biological observation matrix (BIOM) table was exported as a comma-separated file (CSV) for downstream analyses.

Amplicon sequence variants assigned to the genus level only (mainly Haemonchus) were exported as FASTA files from QIIME 2 and queried against the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). Reference ITS2 sequences of Haemonchus spp. from Australia (GenBank: KC632567 and KF364631) were retrieved from NCBI and aligned together with study-derived ASVs using MUSCLE in the Geneious Prime program (version 2025.2.2; www.geneious.com). Based on a sequence identity threshold of > 97% (Avramenko et al., 2015) and multiple sequence alignment, the majority of previously unassigned Haemonchus ASVs were confirmed as H. contortus, with two ASVs matching H. placei (Stevenson et al., 1995). However, ASVs that remained unassigned to species level at this stage or exhibited clear insertions/deletions were considered spurious and excluded from further analyses. Additionally, samples with fewer than 10 post-filter reads were excluded (n = 96), a conservative threshold applied to minimise the influence of sequencing artefacts while retaining biologically plausible low-intensity infections and rare taxa (Ghafar et al., 2020).

The BIOM and feature tables, alongside the associated sample metadata, were imported into R (R Core Team, 2025) for further analyses using the phyloseq package (version 1.50.0) (McMurdie and Holmes, 2013). Relative read abundance of strongylid species was calculated as the proportion of reads for each species and divided by the total number of reads. Relative read abundance of strongylid species was also calculated at the herd level and stratified by selected variables (i.e. age group, climatic zone and BioWorma® use).

Questionnaire

Given the economic importance of strongylids and the high prevalence in goats, risk factor analyses were undertaken to identify variables associated with strongylid FECs only. Risk factors were collected in two ways. First, data, including age, sex, deworming history, BioWorma® use, herd size, farm location and season, were collected at the time of faecal sampling and hence were available from all herds. Additionally, the presence of co-infection with Eimeria spp. was available during faecal testing (Ali et al., 2026). To identify herd-level risk factors for strongylids, additional data were collected using an online questionnaire developed using the Research Electronic Data Capture facility, REDCap (Harris et al., 2009). The questionnaire was informed by published studies (Vieira et al., 2014; Zvinorova et al., 2016; Sylvester et al., 2018; Cai et al., 2023; Rerkyusuke et al., 2024; Tangus et al., 2024) and consultation with those familiar with the Australian dairy goat industry. A link to the survey was emailed to all producers who participated in faecal sampling in October 2024, with fortnightly reminders, and remained open until December 2024. Ethical approval for the questionnaire was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Melbourne (Ethics ID: 25037).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics

FEC data were compiled and coded alongside cleaned and validated questionnaire responses using Microsoft Excel® version 2016 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). The crude individual animal-level prevalence of GINs was calculated by dividing the number of goats that tested positive for GIN eggs by the total number of samples. A herd was declared as positive if one goat tested positive for any GIN eggs, and herd-level prevalence was calculated as the number of GIN-positive herds divided by the total number of herds enrolled. Animal- and herd-level prevalence estimates were also determined for strongylids, Nematodirus spp. and Trichuris spp.

To describe strongylid positivity for different levels of hypothesised risk factors such as goat age and the climatic zone in which the farm was located, accounting for clustering of strongylid positivity within herds, we developed a mixed-effects logistic regression model in which a given risk factor of interest (e.g. age) was included as a fixed effect and herd as a random intercept term. Predicted probabilities (i.e. adjusted strongylid prevalence estimates) and their 95% CIs were obtained using the emmeans package (Lenth and Piaskowski, 2025) in R. Compared with the crude animal-level prevalence estimates, the 95% CIs for the adjusted animal-level prevalence estimates from the mixed-effects model were wider, making us less likely to make Type 1 errors when testing for differences in strongylid prevalence across each level of a given risk factor. Throughout the remainder of this paper, adjusted animal-level prevalence estimates are reported, unless indicated otherwise.

To inform future studies where the objective is to estimate the individual animal-level prevalence of GINs, we calculated the ICC estimates for strongylids, Trichuris spp. and Nematodirus spp. positivity following the methods described previously (Ali et al., 2026).

Risk factors for strongylid faecal egg counts

Questionnaire data available for 60 herds (88% response rate) were exported from REDCap as a CSV file for analysis. Variables with minimal variability were excluded, and categorical variables with sparse levels in some were collapsed based on biological plausibility. In total, 15 variables were included for risk factor analysis. These 15 variables comprised: (i) individual animal-level (age, sex and Eimeria spp. co-infection); (ii) herd-level structure and management (herd size, herd type, production system, stocking rate, anthelmintic treatment, deworming of new goats, BioWorma® use, routine FEC use and drench checking); and (iii) environmental (farm location, grass height and seasonal) factors.

Outcome variables

In our study, three outcome variables were defined. The primary outcome variable of interest was the presence or absence of any GIN eggs in individual goat faecal samples. An individual goat faecal sample was classified as positive if at least one egg of strongylids, Nematodirus spp. and/or Trichuris spp. was detected and negative otherwise. The second outcome was strongylid infection intensity, quantified as EPG. For descriptive purposes, strongylid FECs were categorised as low (< 500 EPG), medium (500–1000 EPG) or high (> 1000 EPG) infection intensities. Because strongylid FECs were overdispersed (variance-to-mean ratio, VMR = 2664) and positively skewed (skewness = 3.35), the EPG data were transformed using the natural logarithm after adding a constant [ln(EPG + 15)] to achieve normality. The log-transformed strongylid FEC was fitted as a continuous outcome variable in each of the linear regression models. The constant (15 EPG) corresponds to half the analytical sensitivity (detection limit) of the modified McMaster method and was applied to accommodate zero counts and low-level infections. This approach assumes that true counts below the detection limit are uniformly distributed between zero and the detection threshold, with an expected value equal to half the detection limit (Dohoo et al., 2012). Normality of the transformed data was assessed graphically using histograms. The third outcome was strongylid species prevalence derived from deep amplicon sequencing in a subset of 500 goats. For each strongylid species, the relative read abundance was converted to binary outcomes (presence/absence) at the individual goat level. Strongylid species-specific prevalence estimates were then calculated, adjusting for the effect of clustering at the herd level.

Directed acyclic graph and linear regression models

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) using DAGitty (Textor et al., 2016) was developed to identify adjustment variables in our linear regression models. Each candidate risk factor for strongylids was selected in turn, and the DAG was used to derive minimal sufficient adjustment sets (Shrier and Platt, 2008); that is, variables to include in the model to return unbiased estimates of the association between the nominated explanatory variable and the outcome variable. When more than one sufficient adjustment set was available, the set with the smallest number of adjustment variables was selected.

Linear mixed-effects models were developed to estimate the association between each candidate risk factor and strongylid FEC, adjusting for the minimal sufficient adjustment set variables. In each of the linear mixed-effects models, herd was included as a random intercept term to account for clustering of goats within herds. Our models were developed using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) methods available in the “lmer” function in the contributed lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) in R (R Core Team, 2025). Predicted mean log-transformed EPG values and their 95% CIs were obtained from each of the linear regression models with random effects set to the population-average level and adjustment for DAG-identified confounders. Predicted values were then back-transformed to the original scale using EPG = exp (fitted value) – 15 to obtain adjusted mean FEC estimates for each explanatory variable category. Back-transformed CIs extending below zero were truncated at zero. Model assumptions were assessed using residual diagnostics, and multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using variance inflation factors. For our hypothesis tests, probability values that were less than 0.05 were declared to be statistically significant.

Alpha diversity metrics

Given the variation in sequencing read depth across samples, rarefaction was applied to normalise the data, after which the rarefied data were used to estimate three alpha diversity indices: observed richness, Simpson’s index and Shannon’s index using the phyloseq package. Observed richness measures the number of unique ASVs detected per sample and provides a direct estimate of species richness. Simpson’s index describes species evenness and dominance, with lower values reflecting communities dominated by fewer taxa. Shannon’s index encompasses both richness and evenness, with higher values reflecting more diversity and even distribution of parasite taxa.

Due to the high herd-level clustering of strongylids in Australian dairy goats (ICC = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.20–0.72), alpha diversity indices were analysed using a linear mixed-effects model fitted using the “lmer” function in the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). In each of our models, the alpha diversity metric was treated as a continuous outcome variable, and a given explanatory variable of interest (age group, climatic zone, BioWorma® use, dewormer type and FEC intensity) was included as a fixed effect. Herd was specified as a random intercept term. Adjusted (estimated marginal) means, standard errors and the corresponding 95% CIs were obtained using the emmeans package (Lenth and Piaskowski, 2025), with Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom applied (Kenward and Roger, 1997) for statistical inference.

Results

Prevalence of gastrointestinal nematodes by faecal egg counts

The adjusted animal-level prevalence of GINs was 92% (95% CI: 89–95%), with 100% herd-level prevalence (Fig. 2A). Strongylids, whose eggs are collectively termed ‘strongyles’ due to the inability to distinguish species, were the most prevalent group (91%, 95% CI: 87–94%), followed by Trichuris spp. (13%, 95% CI: 10–17%) and Nematodirus spp. (5%, 95% CI: 3–8%). Strongylids were detected in all herds (100%) (Fig. 2B), with the within-herd prevalence ranging from 14% to 100% (Fig. 2B). Conversely, Nematodirus spp. and Trichuris spp. occurred sporadically, with herd-level prevalence estimates of 38% and 63%, respectively (Fig. 2C and D).

Ranked error bar plots showing within herd-level prevalence of gastrointestinal nematodes (A), strongylid nematodes (B), Nematodirus spp. (C), and Trichuris spp. (D) in Australian dairy goat herds. Points show within herd prevalence. Error bars show the 95% confidence interval for each prevalence estimate.

Adult goats had the highest adjusted individual animal-level prevalence of strongylids (96%, 95% CI: 93–98%), while it was lowest in kids (73%, 95% CI: 59–84%) (Fig. 3A). By climatic zone, the highest adjusted individual animal-level prevalence of strongylids was in goats from the winter rainfall zone (95%, 95% CI: 89–98%). The lowest was in Mediterranean-type climate zones (83%, 95% CI: 63–93%) (Fig. 3B); however, differences between zones were not statistically significant. Goats with a history of recent deworming had a statistically significantly higher adjusted animal-level prevalence of strongylids (95%, 95% CI: 91–97%) compared with goats that had no deworming history (81%, 95% CI: 68–89%) (Supplementary Table S1).

Faecal egg count intensity of gastrointestinal nematodes

Strongylid FEC results varied markedly among individual goats, with FECs ranging from 0 to 16,710 EPG (median FEC: 480; Q1: 120; Q3: 1200). Overall, 51% (n = 526), 19% (n = 196) and 30% (n = 306) of goats were low (< 500 EPG), medium (500–1000 EPG) and high (> 1000 EPG) strongylid egg shedders, respectively (Fig. 4A and B). Strongylid FECs were highly overdispersed (VMR = 2664) and positively skewed (skewness = 3.35), with 30% (306 of 1028) of individual goats accounting for 80% of total egg output. Medium-to high-intensity strongylid egg shedding occurred predominantly in adult does (Fig. 4A) and was most pronounced in herds located in the winter and non-seasonal rainfall zones, particularly in the summer and autumn seasons (Fig. 4B). These FECs were retained in all statistical analyses.

The FEC results for Trichuris spp. and Nematodirus spp. were generally low. Trichuris spp. FECs ranged from 0 to 1440 EPG (median 0; Q1: 0; Q3: 0), with 86% (n = 886) of goats having no detectable eggs. Strongylid species relative abundance and composition

Deep amplicon sequencing of 500 DNA isolates yielded approximately 51 million high-quality reads after excluding reads from the positive controls, contaminants and 96 low-depth samples. In total, 2001 ASVs representing 11 strongylid species across seven genera were identified, with a median of 72,574 reads per sample (Q1: 539; Q3: 151,764). Haemonchus contortus was the most abundant species (50% of total reads; 44% of ASVs), followed by T. colubriformis (37%; 27%), Te. circumcincta (8%; 15%) and T. rugatus (2%; 6%). Haemonchus placei and Nematodirus spathiger were rare, each represented by two ASVs. Muellerius capillaris was detected in seven goats, representing the first report using ITS2 metabarcoding from flotation-purified faecal egg DNA (Table 2).

Effects of goat age, climatic zone and BioWorma® use on strongylid species relative abundance

The strongylid nematode relative species abundance differed by age groups (Fig. 7A). Ten species were detected in both kids and adults, whereas nine were detected in weaners. Although H. contortus, Te. circumcincta and T. colubriformis were most abundant across all age groups, some notable differences were observed. For example, H. contortus contributed a higher proportion of reads in kids than adults, while T. colubriformis showed the opposite pattern. Weaners exhibited the highest relative abundance of Te. circumcincta. Nematodirus spathiger was detected only in one kid and one weaner, M. capillaris exclusively in adults and H. placei in one kid and one adult goat (Fig. 7A).

The relative abundance of strongylid species also varied across climatic zones (Fig. 7B). Although the winter rainfall zone contributed only 26% of total reads, it supported the greatest species richness (n = 11 species). The summer rainfall zone (35%) and Mediterranean-type climate zone (25%) each comprised nine species, while the non-seasonal rainfall zone had both the lowest read contribution (14%) and species richness (n = 8 species). Haemonchus contortus was the most abundant species in all but Mediterranean-type climate zone, where T. colubriformis predominated. Teladorsagia circumcincta was most abundant in the winter rainfall zone and least abundant in Mediterranean-type climate zone. Overall, five strongylid species showed zone-specific absence, including Oesophagostomum venulosum from Mediterranean-type climate zone, H. placei and N. spathiger from both the summer and non-seasonal rainfall zones, and C. ovina and M. capillaris from the non-seasonal rainfall zone (Fig. 7B).

Relative abundance profiles were broadly similar between herds where BioWorma® was used and where BioWorma® was not used. In both groups, 10 strongylid species were detected, with H. contortus contributing half of the total reads. Teladorsagia circumcincta and T. rugatus showed a higher proportional representation in herds where BioWorma® was used (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Individual animal and herd-level prevalence of strongylid species

At the individual animal level, the most prevalent strongylid species were H. contortus (adjusted prevalence 78%, 95% CI: 69–85%), Te. circumcincta (64%, 95% CI: 54–74%) and T. colubriformis (60%, 95% CI: 49–70%) (Table 2). A comparable pattern was observed at the herd level, with a high prevalence of H. contortus (94%) followed by T. colubriformis (90%) and Te. circumcincta (88%). In contrast, the prevalence of H. placei, N. spathiger and M. capillaris was low (Table 2).

Age was statistically significantly associated with the adjusted prevalence of four strongylid species (Table 3). The adjusted prevalence of H. contortus was highest in kids (86%, 95% CI: 74–93%) and lowest in adults (78%, 95% CI: 67–85%). In contrast, T. colubriformis (68%, 95% CI: 56–78%) and T. rugatus (43%, 95% CI: 33–54%) were more prevalent in adults than kids, while weaners had the highest prevalence of C. ovina (8%, 95% CI: 3–19%) (Table 3).

Co-infection with strongylid species

Co-infection with two or more strongylid species was not uncommon. Most goats (85%; n = 424) harboured between two and eight species, with a mean (± standard deviation) of 3.0 ± 1.5 per animal. Infections with three (n = 129) or four strongylid species (n = 102) were most frequent, while 78 goats harboured five to eight species. In contrast, mono-infections were uncommon (n = 76) and often involved Te. circumcincta (n = 35) or H. contortus (n = 22). Interestingly, mono-infections with T. vitrinus (n = 9), T. rugatus (n = 5), T. colubriformis (n = 3), and C. ovina (n = 2) were rare, each with a prevalence below 2%.

At the individual goat level, observed richness was statistically significantly associated with age, climatic zone, strongylid FEC intensity and recent deworming history. BioWorma® use had no association with strongylid FEC intensity (Table 4). In contrast, Simpson’s and Shannon’s indices were less sensitive to host and environmental factors, with smaller numbers of statistically significant associations detected (Table 4).

Intraclass correlation coefficients

Based on the data collected in this study, the ICC for strongylids, Nematodirus spp. and Trichuris spp. infections in goats were estimated to be high at 0.46 (95% CI: 0.20–0.72), 0.47 (95% CI: 0.20–0.87) and 0.20 (95% CI: 0.00–0.40), respectively.

Discussion

This nationwide cross-sectional study provides a comprehensive assessment of the prevalence, infection intensity, species diversity and associated risk factors for GINs in Australian dairy goats. Infections with strongylids were highly prevalent (91%), and FECs were markedly overdispersed, with 30% of goats contributing 80% of total egg output. Adult goats exhibited the highest adjusted prevalence of strongylids (96%) and mean FEC (440 EPG), while goats in the winter rainfall zone had the highest adjusted prevalence (95%) and mean FEC (444 EPG). Age, climatic zone, recent deworming history and co-infection with Eimeria spp. were statistically significantly associated with strongylid FEC. Metabarcoding identified 11 strongylid species, with mixed-species infections predominating at both the individual animal (85%) and herd levels (98%). Notably, although the climatic zone did not have a statistically significant effect on the overall prevalence of strongylids, it showed a clear species-specific association, highlighting spatial structuring within strongylid communities.

In contrast to earlier Australian studies (McKenzie et al., 1979; Beveridge et al., 1987; Francis and Šlapeta, 2023; Dawson, 2025), which used conventional parasitological methods across a limited number of samples and study sites and did not account for data dependence, the present study integrated FECs with deep amplicon sequencing in a large, nationally representative sample from 68 herds and adjusted for herd-level clustering and estimated risk factors. The high ICC (0.46, 95% CI: 0.20–0.72) for strongylid infection demonstrates substantial within-herd clustering, underscoring the need to adjust for the herd effect to provide appropriate estimates of the precision of prevalence estimates. The ICC estimates from this study are reported to inform sample size calculations for future studies.

In this study, strongylids clearly dominated the GIN fauna of dairy goats, with high (adjusted) animal (91%) and herd-level prevalence estimates (100%), whereas Trichuris spp. and Nematodirus spp. occurred sporadically. This marked predominance provides a strong epidemiological rationale for focusing risk factor analyses and species identification on strongylids. A similar hierarchy has been reported in Australian alpacas, but with a lower prevalence of strongylids (59%, n = 1545) and Trichuris spp. (6%) and a higher prevalence of Nematodirus spp. (17%) (Rashid et al., 2019). Such differences reflect host-specific parasite assemblages shaped by grazing behaviour, immune competence and ecological suitability. For instance, Nematodirus spp., including the most pathogenic N. battus (yet to be reported in Australia), are more common in camelids and sheep than in goats, whereas goats are more susceptible to strongylid infections (Hoste et al., 2010; Torres-Acosta et al., 2025). The high prevalence of strongylids in our study is concordant with large-scale international studies published over the past decade (Torres-Acosta et al., 2014; Zvinorova et al., 2016; Sylvester et al., 2018; Mohammedsalih et al., 2019; Cai et al., 2023; Coomansingh-Springer et al., 2025; D’Amico et al., 2025; Kaur et al., 2025; Seevagan et al., 2026).

Strongylid egg shedding was highly overdispersed (VMR = 2664) and positively skewed (skewness = 3.35), consistent with a negative binomial distribution and the classical 20/80 rule (Hoste et al., 2001). In this study, 30% of goats accounted for 80% of total egg output, indicating that a relatively small subset of animals contributes disproportionately to pasture contamination. This marked overdispersion provides strong epidemiological justification for the transformation of strongylid FEC data prior to statistical analysis and the application of targeted selective treatment (TST), whereby only high egg shedders (e.g. > 1000 EPG in the present study) are treated, although the optimal treatment threshold in goats remains uncertain. The degree of aggregation observed here is higher than that reported in Australian meat goats, where 46% of goats contributed to 80% of total egg output in a cross-sectional study conducted in New South Wales and Queensland (Dawson, 2025), likely reflecting differences in study area (regional vs national), study populations (meat vs dairy goats), sampling season and production system. In contrast, a large national survey of GINs in the USA reported even greater aggregation, with only 24% of goats responsible for 80% of egg shedding (Avery et al., 2025). Nevertheless, consistent evidence across studies (Hoste et al., 2001, 2002; Torres-Acosta et al., 2014) shows that strongylid FEC overdispersion can be exploited to identify both naturally low egg-shedding (resistant) animals and to implement TST strategies that minimise (or otherwise avoid) unnecessary anthelmintic use while maintaining productivity and reducing the development of AR. Such approaches are particularly pertinent in dairy goats, where currently only a few registered anthelmintics are available in Australia (wormboss.com.au/drenches-for-goats-using-products-correctly-and-legally) and elsewhere due to the requirement of milk withholding periods, and where high milk producing goats tend to shed high numbers of eggs, meaning that TST can simultaneously improve goat health and milk yields (Hoste et al., 2002). Unexpectedly, medium-to high-intensity strongylid egg shedding occurred predominantly in adult female does. Further, adults exhibited both a significantly higher adjusted animal-level prevalence (96%) and mean FEC of strongylids (440 EPG) than kids (73%, 134 EPG). Although this pattern contrasts with the classical age-immunity paradigm, it is biologically plausible in the context of dairy animals. Because the population comprised dairy goats, most does were most likely pregnant and/or lactating. Under such physiological conditions, increased susceptibility to parasitic infections and increased strongylid egg shedding are expected due to the periparturient egg rise (PPR) (Jansen, 1982; Rahman and Collins, 1992; Dorny et al., 1995), which occurs during late gestation and early lactation as a consequence of transient relaxation of immunity associated with physiological and metabolic stress. Similar patterns have been reported from the USA, where periparturient milking does had a substantially higher adjusted mean FEC (867 EPG) than non-periparturient adult goats (501 EPG) (Avery et al., 2025). Our findings suggest that physiological status may override age-related immunity in dairy goats and should be considered when interpreting FEC results. Further, the periparturient period can be used as a critical window for targeted treatment (Hoste et al., 2002), given the well-documented association between PPR and reduced milk yield and kid weight gain (Mandonnet et al., 2005; Martínez et al., 2017). However, reproductive status was not recorded at the individual goat level in our study, the suggestion that elevated FECs in adult goats may reflect PPR should be interpreted cautiously and remains inferential.

Climate zone statistically significantly influenced strongylid FEC intensity but not overall strongylid prevalence. The highest adjusted mean FEC of strongylids was found in goats from the winter rainfall zone (444 EPG) followed by the summer rainfall zone (416 EPG), with lowest mean FECs in Mediterranean-type climate zones (136 EPG). Elevated FECs in the winter rainfall zone may reflect warming associated with climate change (The State of Victoria Department of Energy, 2024). The effect of sampling season also cannot be ignored, as most faecal samples were collected in the summer (n = 303) and autumn (n = 634). The winter rainfall zone also exhibited the greatest species richness (n = 11 species), suggesting that polyparasitism (i.e. mixed-species infections) may contribute to increased strongylid FECs through additive or synergistic egg output. In contrast, the high FECs in the summer rainfall zone are unsurprising and are likely driven by the dominance of H. contortus and T. colubriformis, the two most abundant species in this region (Fig. 7B). Haemonchus contortus, in particular, is highly fecund; a single female worm can produce up to 10,000 eggs per day. Consequently, even relatively modest worm burdens can generate high individual animal FECs in this region. Comparable patterns were also reported from the USA, where goats from the northeastern (877 EPG) and southeastern (733 EPG) regions had the highest mean FECs (Avery et al., 2025). In the USA study, increased rainfall during autumn and elevated precipitation and temperatures during the winter were proposed as key drivers of heightened infection intensity. Together, these findings reinforce the central role of temperature, rainfall and moisture as determinants of strongylid infection intensity in goats (O’Connor et al., 2006).

Goats co-infected with Eimeria spp. had a statistically significantly higher adjusted mean strongylid FEC (43 EPG) than goats without detectable oocysts (28 EPG), despite no association at the prevalence level. Consistent with our findings, strongylid egg excretion probability and intensity were significantly influenced by the presence of Eimeria co-infection in German dairy cattle (Hecker et al., 2025), with similar intensity-dependent associations reported in goats in the USA (Sylvester et al., 2018) and in African buffalo from South Africa (Gorsich et al., 2014). Our findings suggest that, although exposure pathways may differ, parasite infection intensity is shaped by shared host, management and environmental factors, as well as potential biological interactions (e.g. between extracellular nematodes and intracellular Eimeria spp.) (Lu et al., 2021; Grondin et al., 2024). Given the high prevalence of Eimeria spp. in goats (Ali et al., 2025b, 2026) and the accumulating evidence that coccidiosis is a risk factor for increased strongylid FECs (Gorsich et al., 2014; Sylvester et al., 2018; Hecker et al., 2025), it is prudent to include Eimeria in integrated parasite management programmes.

Goats with a prior deworming history had a significantly higher adjusted animal-level prevalence of strongylids (95%) and increased adjusted mean FEC (113 EPG) than untreated goats (81%, 62 EPG). This association is explained by reverse causality (Avery et al., 2025), whereby herd managers experiencing greater worm burdens are more likely to administer anthelmintic treatment. Reduced anthelmintic efficacy and treatment failure also cannot be excluded given widespread reports of AR (Dawson et al., 2026), the off-label drug use (Ali et al., 2025a) and the frequent underdosing of goats (Baudinette et al., 2022), which may allow resistant worms to persist and continue egg shedding after treatment (Kaplan, 2004). Pharmacokinetic differences between sheep and goats may further compromise efficacy when sheep dosing regimens are applied to goats (Ali et al., 2025a). Supporting our hypothesis, herds whose managers conducted drench efficacy checks had a significantly lower mean FEC (262 EPG) than those that did not (391 EPG) in the univariable analysis, suggesting that informed anthelmintic use may mitigate infection intensity. Similar associations between prior anthelmintic treatment of goats and elevated strongylid FEC were reported in the USA (Avery et al., 2025), whereas deworming was associated with lower FECs in Indian goats (Seevagan et al., 2026). These contrasting associations likely reflect differences in underlying infection pressure, anthelmintic efficacy, dose rates and/or study designs. Thus, the direction of association between anthelmintic treatment and FEC appears context-dependent and shaped by regional resistance patterns, management practices and strongylid species composition (Rompo et al., 2026).

Deep amplicon sequencing identified 11 strongylid species from individual goats (n = 500). This estimate excludes Trichuris spp. and Nematodirus spp. detected by egg morphology during FECs, meaning that the total species richness exceeds 12. The species richness in our study differs from previous Australian studies in feral goats, which reported either higher (18 GINs; n = 324 goats) (Beveridge et al., 1987) or lower diversity [8 species (n = 30), see Francis and Šlapeta, 2023; and 10 species (n = 40), see McKenzie et al., 1979]. Consistent with global patterns (O’Connor et al., 2006; Zajac, 2006), H. contortus, Te. circumcincta and T. colubriformis were the most abundant and prevalent species in our study. Given the high fecundity and pathogenicity of H. contortus, its high prevalence and abundance, including in historically lower-risk areas such as Tasmania and Victoria, pose major concerns for the sustainability of the small ruminant industry. Although multidrug AR H. contortus has been widely reported in Australian meat goats (Dawson et al., 2026) and internationally (Baudinette et al., 2022; Rompo et al., 2026), direct AR confirmation in our study population remains to be determined through further investigation.

In this study, the winter rainfall zone supported the highest species richness (n = 11 species). This finding is congruent with the cool and wet climatic conditions of the winter rainfall zone that favour larval survival for a broader range of strongylid species, including the less common O. venulosum (adjusted prevalence: 31%), T. axei (25%) and T. vitrinus (19%). In contrast, the summer rainfall zone contributed the greatest proportion of total reads (35%), driven by the dominance of H. contortus and T. colubriformis, species well adapted to warm and humid environments (O’Connor et al., 2006; Dawson, 2025). While a prior modelling study predicts that regions receiving 700–1200 mm annual rainfall in Australia are suitable for the occurrence of H. contortus (Emery et al., 2016), our adjusted prevalence estimates were not statistically significantly associated with climatic zone, indicating the widespread endemicity of this parasite in most dairy goat herds. Interestingly, a recent study predicted that future climate change will predominantly suppress the transmission potential of H. contortus in Africa (Tinsley and Morgan, 2025). Conversely, Te. circumcincta (79%), T. colubriformis (79%) and C. ovina (8%) exhibited significantly high adjusted prevalences in Mediterranean-type climate zones. These findings show that while strongylid infection is ubiquitous nationally, climate selectively influences species composition. Similar climate-driven structuring of strongylid species has been reported in Australian feral goats and sheep (Beveridge et al., 1987; De Chaneet and Dunsmore, 1988) and is likely mediated by climate constraints on the free-living larval development, overwinter survival and seasonal transmission dynamics (O’Connor et al., 2006).

The adjusted individual animal-level prevalence of some strongylid species significantly differed between age groups, despite the similar overall species abundance in kids and adults (n = 10 species). Haemonchus contortus was significantly more prevalent in kids (86%), consistent with the immunological naivety and limited ability of kids to mount effective protective responses against this highly fecund parasite (Zajac, 2006; Hoste et al., 2010). Young goats are most susceptible to H. contortus, which establishes rapidly and produces large numbers of eggs before effective immunity develops, a pattern widely reported in small ruminants (Le Jambre and Royal, 1976; Craig, 2018). In contrast, T. colubriformis (68%) and T. rugatus (43%) were significantly more prevalent in adult goats, likely reflecting cumulative pasture exposure and the tendency for Trichostrongylus spp. to persist as chronic infections once partial immunity against H. contortus has developed (O’Connor et al., 2006; Hoste et al., 2010). Weaners exhibited slightly lower species richness (n = 9 species) but showed a high adjusted prevalence for both Te. circumcincta (72%) and C. ovina (8%).

Notably, M. capillaris (a small lungworm) was detected in seven adult goats (∼1%) across five herds using ITS2 metabarcoding. To our knowledge, this is the first report of M. capillaris using a nemabiome approach applied to egg-enriched faecal DNA. Given that lungworms are conventionally diagnosed by Baermann larval recovery following faecal culture, the detection of M. capillaris here likely reflects the presence of first-stage larva-associated DNA within the flotation-purified fraction, highlighting the sensitivity of metabarcoding (Antonopoulos et al., 2024). The detection of M. capillaris across five herds in Victoria (n = 2), Western Australia (n = 2) and Queensland (n = 1) is consistent with the presence of suitable gastropod intermediate hosts and aligns with an earlier study in South Australian feral goats (1%; n = 324) (Beveridge et al., 1987). Interestingly, nearly half of survey respondents (31 of 66) ranked M. capillaris as important to their goat health in our prior study (Ali et al., 2025a). While the clinical significance of M. capillaris in Australian goats remains unclear, its detection here warrants further investigation.

Despite the novelty of our study, it is not without shortfalls. The cross-sectional study design allowed identification of host, management and environmental factors associated with elevated FEC and high prevalence of strongylids at a national scale at a single point in time. Future studies could further refine these insights by incorporating additional, time-dependent indicators of host resilience, environmental and management factors, including season, grazing practices, FAMACHA® score, breed effects, body condition, dag score, reproductive status (e.g. pregnancy and lactation stages), parity number, nutrition, concurrent infections, copper oxide wire particle use and longer-term evaluation of BioWorma® use. It would be interesting to quantify whether PPR in pregnant and/or lactating dairy goats has significant effects on strongylid FEC and associated production losses, as reproductive status was not recorded in our study. Furthermore, the nemabiome results require careful interpretation, as strongylid species read abundance reflects relative rather than absolute parasite burdens. Lastly, the near absence of Nematodirus spp. in the nemabiome results, despite the inclusion of Nematodirus spp. egg-positive samples, may reflect the low FEC results of Nematodirus spp. (Antonopoulos et al., 2024), hatching before DNA extraction or reduced recovery efficiency from flotation-purified egg DNA.

Conclusions

This study documents a high prevalence, diversity and burden of GINs in Australian dairy goats. Strongylid infections, dominated by H. contortus, were widespread across herds, including regions previously considered low risk, highlighting the need for proactive parasite management across all climatic zones. Marked strongylid FEC overdispersion indicates that pasture contamination is driven by a small subset of goats, predominantly adult females, underscoring the importance of routine FEC monitoring in all age groups to identify high egg shedders. Given the broad distribution of H. contortus, FAMACHA®-guided TST should be a cornerstone of control programmes. Associations between elevated FECs and key host, management and environmental factors further demonstrate the limitations of relying solely on anthelmintics. Instead, integrated parasite management strategies, including combining TST, rotational grazing, biological control, vaccination and nutritional interventions (Torres-Acosta et al., 2025) are likely to be essential to sustain productivity and slow the spread of AR. Overall, this study provides comprehensive and up-to-date national epidemiological evidence of GIN prevalence in Australian dairy goats. By integrating classical parasitology with next-generation sequencing of individual samples, it delivers high-resolution insights into strongylid species’ abundance, diversity and prevalence at both animal and herd levels, directly addressing critical knowledge gaps relevant to producers, veterinarians and policymakers.
