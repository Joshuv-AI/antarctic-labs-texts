Biomechanical analysis of countermovement jump performance before and after long-distance run with arch support footwear
Authors: Kui Wan, Junfeng Wang, Yifei Huang, Meng Sun, Yuming Wang, Yuan Wang, Junjie Fang, Qichang Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong-distance running induces acute lower-limb fatigue, and arch-support footwear is widely used to modulate foot mechanics; however, how arch-support height interacts with running-induced fatigue to affect countermovement jump (CMJ) take-off biomechanics remains unclear.PurposeTo investigate the effect of acute fatigue from half-marathon and arch support heights on countermovement jump (CMJ) biomechanics, and to elucidate the potential mechanism.MethodsTwenty-one male recreational runners completed countermovement jump (CMJ) tests before and after a half-marathon under two footwear conditions: low arch support (AS-5 mm) and high arch support (AS-20 mm), representing minimal versus pronounced midfoot support. Before testing, foot arch type was screened with the ink-footprint method and the Arch Index (AI; Cavanagh and Rodgers, 1987); all participants had normal arches (0.21 ≤ AI ≤ 0.26, 0.24 ± 0.02). Three-dimensional kinematics and ground reaction forces were captured using a Vicon motion-capture system and Kistler force plates, and lower-limb joint angles and net joint moments (hip, knee, ankle) were computed in OpenSim. Outcome measures were hip, knee and ankle joint angles and net moments, and jump height. Joint waveforms were time-normalized to 0-100% of the CMJ take-off cycle (from movement onset to toe-off). Jump height was analyzed with two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (factors: arch support and fatigue); joint waveforms were analyzed with two-way repeated-measures ANOVA in SPM1d to test the main effects of arch support and fatigue and their interaction.ResultsRegarding arch support, hip flexion angle in AS-5 mm was significantly greater than AS-20 mm during 0-100%, knee flexion angle during 0–99%, and ankle dorsiflexion angle during 0–23% (p < 0.01), with jump height in AS-5 mm approximately 20.5% greater than AS-20 mm (p < 0.001). Regarding fatigue, post-fatigue hip internal rotation angle increased significantly during 0–79% and 81–100% (p < 0.01), knee flexion angle increased during 32–58% (p < 0.01), and knee adduction angle increased (p < 0.01). Regarding interaction effect, under AS-5 mm condition, post-fatigue hip internal rotation angle increased significantly during 0-100%, and knee abduction angle decreased significantly during 0–32% and 87–100% (p < 0.01), whereas changes in AS-20 mm were relatively minor, indicating that high arch support effectively mitigates the negative effects of fatigue on lower limb joint stability.ConclusionsLow arch-support shoes were associated with greater lower-limb joint range of motion and an approximately 20.5% greater jump height than high arch-support shoes. Acute fatigue reduces neuromuscular control by increasing hip internal rotation and altering knee joint kinematics. High arch support shoes maintain foot stability and reduce compensatory movements of proximal joints through kinematic chain transmission, which may help maintain proximal-joint kinematic stability under fatigue.Keywords: Counter movement jump, Acute fatigue, Arch support, Lower limb biomechanics, Sports performance

Introduction

In recent years, long-distance running has gained increasing popularity, with athletes paying greater attention to footwear selection and its effects on performance and fatigue. Fatigue can be classified as acute or chronic, with acute fatigue being a reversible functional decline induced by single or short-duration high-intensity exercise, primarily affecting muscles involved in the activity [1, 2]. The effects of acute fatigue on lower limb biomechanics have been confirmed by multiple studies, primarily manifesting as decreased joint kinematics and kinetics, energy transfer efficiency, and neuromuscular control [3, 4]. Kinematically, acute fatigue significantly alters lower limb joint angles, with post-fatigue decreases in knee flexion and hip abduction angles during jumping [5, 6]. Kinetically, acute fatigue directly affects ground reaction forces and joint moments; during jumping, peak vertical ground reaction force decreases, and hip and knee extensor moments significantly decline [7]. Additionally, fatigue increases joint coordination variability and midfoot loading [4, 8], affecting movement stability and coordination [9]. Fatigue impacts athletic performance and has been reported to be related to arch type, muscle synergy, and movement adaptation [10]. For low-arch runners, acute fatigue from long-distance running exacerbates arch collapse, increasing navicular drop, impairing lower limb power output, reducing stability, and diminishing athletic performance [11]. High-arch runners maintain relative arch rigidity post-fatigue, but plantar pressure shifts toward the fourth and fifth metatarsal regions; this pressure redistribution alters the impact concentration during jumping, causing force imbalances and affecting performance [12]. Notably, the running shoes with midfoot support interacts with fatigue effects. Support shoes limit excessive post-fatigue pronation in low arches through midfoot support, reducing ankle eversion and knee internal rotation, thereby attenuating performance decline [13]. When high-arch runners wear support shoes, abnormal post-fatigue plantar pressure distribution improves, enhancing force symmetry during jumping [14]. Furthermore, fatigue impairs the efficiency of neuromuscular control, causing delayed muscle activation and force attenuation, particularly pronounced in plantar flexors, weakening muscle contraction velocity and power output during the push-off phase [15]. Running shoes with different support designs can mitigate fatigue effects on jump performance by influencing plantar pressure distribution and neuromuscular activation patterns [16].

Countermovement jump (CMJ) is a simple and repeatable neuromuscular function assessment method with minimal equipment requirements [17], which has been widely applied to monitor athlete fatigue status, directly reflecting fatigue-induced neuromuscular system changes through force-time and velocity-time curve characteristics, and kinematic and kinetic parameters. The testing procedure requires no complex preparation, only with hands-on-hips natural squatting, jumping, and landing movements, enabling the rapid acquisition of reliable data without additional physiological burden, perfectly meeting the practical requirements for assessing acute fatigue following half-marathon run in this study.

Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the effect of half-marathon fatigue and different arch support heights on CMJ take-off performance. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to quantify the main effects of “arch support height” and “running fatigue” and their interaction on jump performance, with the SPM1d (one-dimensional statistical parametric mapping) time-series analysis examining changes in lower limb biomechanical parameters during the take-off phase. Mechanistically, the arch support is expected to affect the proximal (knee and hip) joint mechanics through the lower-limb kinematic chain by constraining midfoot deformation and rearfoot pronation, arch support alters subtalar motion and the coupled tibial internal rotation, which is propagated proximally to the knee and hip [18]. Within the scope of the present measurements, the results may inform footwear selection for runners and provide preliminary evidence for arch-support design. Additionally, this study proposed the following three hypotheses: (1) hip, knee, and ankle joints would demonstrate biomechanical differences before and after fatigue during jumping; (2) jump height would differ significantly across different arch support heights and fatigue conditions; (3) high and low arch support shoes would demonstrate biomechanical differences at the hip, knee, and ankle joints during jumping.

Methods and materials

Participants

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 (repeated-measures ANOVA, within factors) with an assumed effect size f = 0.27, α = 0.05, and statistical power (1-β) = 0.8 [19], yielding a minimum required sample of 21 participants. Twenty-one male runners were recruited from the university track and field association, all with right-leg as dominant and demographic information of age 22.13 ± 2.42 years; height 173.00 ± 3.61 cm; body mass 64.00 ± 6.82 kg. Inclusion criteria were, (1) half-marathon completion experience with best time between 65 and 75 min; (2) running at least 2–3 times weekly, with each session lasting at least 45 min or covering at least 10 km; (3) no limb or systemic deformities; (4) no trunk, pelvis, lower limb, or ankle injuries within 6 months prior to testing; and (5) no history of limb surgery or orthopedic correction.

Foot arch type was assessed using the ink-footprint method [20], with each participant standing barefoot in a relaxed bilateral stance with body weight evenly distributed. The plantar surface was coated with a thin, uniform layer of non-toxic ink, and a static footprint of each foot was obtained on plain paper. Two trials per foot were collected and the clearest print was retained. On each footprint (excluding the toes), the longitudinal axis was defined and the print was divided into three equal regions (forefoot, midfoot, and hindfoot). Then, the Arch Index (AI) was calculated as the ratio of the midfoot area to the total footprint area (forefoot + midfoot + hindfoot), following the previous reported protocol [21]. Feet were classified as high-arched (AI < 0.21), normal/healthy (0.21 ≤ AI ≤ 0.26), or low-arched/flat (AI > 0.26). Footprint areas were quantified using ImageJ (NIH, USA), and the left and right AI values were averaged for each participant. Only participants with normal arches (0.21 ≤ AI ≤ 0.26) bilaterally were included to ensure a homogeneous, healthy-arch sample. Mean AI of the included participants was 0.24 ± 0.02. All footprints were measured by the same trained assessor to ensure consistency.

Prior to the experiment, all participants were informed of the study protocol, objectives, timeline, and potential risks. All participants provided written informed consent and voluntarily agreed to participate. The testing protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (TY2022001).

Data collection

This study employed a previously validated OpenSim musculoskeletal model (Stanford University, Stanford), specifically, including 48 reflective markers placed on the trunk, lateral thigh, lateral shank, hip, knee, and ankle joints, as shown in the Fig. 1 (front view). To minimize experimental error, all markers were placed by the same experimenter. Three-dimensional kinematic and kinetic data were synchronously collected using a Vicon infrared motion capture system (Vicon Metrics, Ltd., Oxford) and AMTI force platforms. Two running shoes with different arch support heights were used in this experiment, as shown in Fig. 1 (experimental footwear), with a uniform design for the sole and upper structure, only the systematic design of midfoot support height of 5 mm as AS-5 mm and 20 mm as AS-20 mm, selected to represent minimal versus pronounced midfoot support among commercially available designs; the two shoe models were identical except for arch-support height.

Experimental protocol

Participants avoided strenuous exercise within the past 24 h to ensure a non-fatigued state. Before testing, participants wore tight-fitting clothing and one of the two arch support shoes (AS-5 mm or AS-20 mm) provided by the laboratory. Under experimenter guidance, participants completed a standardized warm-up with a 10-minute slow jogging on a treadmill (Saturn h/p/cosmos, Nussdorf-Traunstein). For pre- and post-fatigue CMJ tests, participants performed three practice jumps to familiarize with the testing environment. During formal testing, six maximal-effort right lower limb jump trials were collected. The experiment consisted of three phases: (1) pre-fatigue right lower limb CMJ data collection; (2) half-marathon running at a submaximal pace on a standard 400 m track to induce fatigue; (3) post-fatigue right lower limb CMJ data collection within 5 min of completing the run. To avoid adaptation effects to different support heights, testing sessions were separated by at least 7 days, ensuring complete physical recovery and eliminating carryover effects from shoe adaptation.

Data processing

Based on the vertical ground reaction force curve, the CMJ was divided into three phases (as shown in the Fig. 1), P1 (eccentric phase), P2 (concentric phase), and P3 (flight phase). This segmentation reflects the distinct mechanical functions of energy absorption (P1), energy generation (P2), and take-off (P3), and was used to interpret the timing (in %) of significant SPM1d clusters. The waveforms of joint angle and moment were interpolated as 101 to represent the continuous 0-100% of the CMJ cycle until take-off phase. The complete CMJ take-off movement began with hip and knee flexion and ended when the toes left the force platform. Raw kinematic and kinetic data were preprocessed using Vicon Nexus 2.8.2 (Vicon Metrics, Ltd., Oxford). Marker trajectories and ground reaction forces were filtered using zero-lag fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filters at 6 Hz and30 Hz, respectively, with a 20 N vertical force threshold for take-off detection [40]. C3D file data were converted to ‘.trc’ and ‘.mot’ using a customized Matlab R2018a script and then imported into OpenSim for data processing. Firstly, the static marker positions of each participant were used for the baseline model scaling to obtain an individualized model precisely matched to the measured parameters. Secondly, the joint angles were calculated using the inverse kinematics (IK) tool in OpenSim. Finally, the net joint moments of the hip, knee, and ankle joints were calculated using the inverse dynamics (ID) algorithm.

Jump height was used to analyze the direct effects of fatigue and different arch support on explosive power [41]. As shown in the Eq. (1), the jump height was calculated from flight time, where H is jump height, g is gravitational acceleration (9.8 m/s²), and t is flight time.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Jump height was analyzed using SPSS software (Statistical Product and Service Solutions, USA) with two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Joint angle and moment data were time-normalized to 101 data points representing 0-100% of the CMJ take-off cycle (from movement onset to toe-off). Due to the one-dimensional time-series nature of angle and moment data, the topological analysis based on random field theory from statistical parametric mapping (SPM) was performed to determine the statistical significance of data clusters exceeding the threshold (t) [22]. The SPM1d method, commonly used for time-series and continuous data analysis, was applied to test statistical differences in hip, knee, and ankle joint angles and moments during CMJ [23]. All analyses were performed in MATLAB R2018a (The MathWorks, MA, USA), with significance level set at 0.05 for two-way repeated measures ANOVA.

Jump height

Significant main effects of arch support (P < 0.001, η²p = 0.481) and fatigue (P < 0.001, η²p = 0.368) were found for jump height, but no significant interaction effect (P = 0.965, η²p < 0.01). Overall, jump height in AS-5 mm (24.120 ± 6.487 cm) was significantly greater than AS-20 mm (20.011 ± 3.880 cm; ~20.5% difference; P < 0.001; 95%CI [2.937, 5.282]) (Table 1), and post-fatigue jump height (22.692 ± 5.343 cm) was significantly greater than pre-fatigue (21.439 ± 4.531 cm; P = 0.037; 95%CI [0.080, 2.426]) (Table 2). The slightly higher post-fatigue jump height (~ 5.8% increase) is a small magnitude and may reflect a post-activation potentiation-like effect of the submaximal half-marathon and/or increased arousal during the post-test rather than improved neuromuscular function; this interpretation is offered cautiously and warrants confirmation in future work.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effects of acute fatigue induced by half-marathon running and different arch support shoes on the jump performance and lower limb biomechanics. Results demonstrated that both acute fatigue and arch support height significantly affected hip, knee, and ankle kinematic and kinetic parameters during CMJ take-off, with significant interaction effects between them. These findings provide important evidence for understanding how foot support structures regulate proximal lower limb joint biomechanics through kinematic chain transmission mechanisms.

Effects of arch support height on jump performance

Jump height in low arch support shoes (AS-5 mm) was significantly greater than high arch support shoes (AS-20 mm), with an increase of approximately 20.5%. This performance difference can be explained by the transmission mechanism of arch support through the lower limb kinematic chain.

As the distal origin of the lower limb kinematic chain, arch deformation directly affects ankle range of motion and mechanical environment. In AS-5 mm, lower arch support may allow greater arch and plantar fascia deformation, which transmits to the ankle through the windlass mechanism [24]. This study found that ankle dorsiflexion angle in AS-5 mm significantly increased during early jumping (0–23%), which may be associated with greater arch and plantar-fascia deformation; however, because arch deformation was not directly measured, this association remains inferential. Increased dorsiflexion facilitates Achilles tendon and triceps surae pre-stretch, storing more elastic potential energy for rapid release during push-off [25, 26].

Ankle kinematic changes transmit proximally along the tibial longitudinal axis. Knee flexion angle in AS-5 mm significantly increased during 0–99%, synergistically with increased ankle dorsiflexion. Biomechanically, increased ankle dorsiflexion increases tibial anterior inclination, guiding greater knee flexion to maintain center of mass stability. This ankle-knee coupling pattern may favor the utilization of elastic energy [27].

Increased knee flexion further transmits proximally, triggering compensatory hip adjustments. This study found that hip flexion angle in AS-5 mm was significantly greater than AS-20 mm throughout the entire take-off phase (0-100%). Notably, despite increased range of motion, hip flexion moment in AS-5 mm was significantly smaller (48–87%). This “high mobility-low moment” pattern indicates that low arch support optimizes muscle working length by increasing joint range of motion, enabling muscles to contract within optimal length-tension relationships, reducing the moment required for equivalent movement outcomes, reflecting a more efficient neuromuscular control strategy [28].

These three-segment transmissions form a complete lower limb kinematic chain. Low arch support design allows greater deformation of the arch, plantar fascia, Achilles tendon, and lower limb extensors, forming a “distal-to-proximal elastic potential energy storage gradient.“[42] During push-off, this stored elastic energy rapidly releases in the opposite direction, enhancing vertical power output and ultimately achieving approximately 20.5% improvement in jump height.

Effects of acute fatigue on lower limb biomechanics

Acute fatigue induced by half-marathon running significantly altered lower limb joint kinematic and kinetic characteristics during jumping, with underlying mechanisms involving peripheral muscle fatigue, decreased neuromuscular control efficiency, and joint stability deterioration at three levels.

Post-fatigue hip internal rotation angle significantly increased throughout nearly the entire jump (0–79% and 81–100%). This change may be related to fatigue-induced functional decline of the hip external rotators [29], which contribute to hip rotational stability during running and may become fatigued after a half-marathon, potentially reducing their ability to resist internal rotation [30]. Increased hip internal rotation alters femoral spatial position relative to the pelvis, subsequently affecting knee and ankle mechanical alignment and reducing effective vertical transmission efficiency of push-off force [31].

Post-fatigue knee biomechanical changes manifested in both sagittal and frontal planes. In the sagittal plane, knee flexion angle significantly increased during 32–58% (corresponding to late eccentric to early concentric phase), reflecting a possible compensatory increase in squat depth to compensate for reduced force output while maintaining jump height [32]. In the frontal plane, knee adduction angle significantly increased during multiple phases (0–32%, 34–40%, 88–98%), suggesting that fatigue may have reduced frontal-plane dynamic stability of the knee.

The concurrent changes in hip internal rotation and knee adduction form the classic “dynamic valgus” pattern, a significant risk factor for lower limb injuries [33]. From a mechanical transmission perspective, the deviation of kinetic line shifts the vertical component of ground reaction force medially, reducing force transmission efficiency along the kinematic chain [34]. Although runners partially maintained jump height through compensatory strategies such as increased knee flexion, neuromuscular control precision and inter-joint coordination clearly declined.

Interaction between arch support height and fatigue: proximal regulation mechanism of foot stability

The most important finding of this study is the significant interaction between arch support height and running fatigue. The core mechanism of this interaction lies in arch support modulating foot stability, transmitting differential biomechanical effects proximally along the kinematic chain.

Transmission mechanism of interaction effects

The essence of the arch support-fatigue interaction is differential decline in foot control capacity and its cascading amplification along the kinematic chain.

Long-distance running fatigue may impair the function of plantar-flexor and intrinsic foot muscles that contribute to dynamic stabilization of arch height [35]. When these muscles fatigue, arch stability relies primarily on passive structures (plantar fascia, tarsal ligaments) and external support (footwear arch support) [36]. The high support design of AS-20 mm provides effective passive substitute support during plantar flexor fatigue, limiting excessive arch collapse and foot pronation. Conversely, the low support design of AS-5 mm cannot provide sufficient passive support, exacerbating post-fatigue arch collapse and foot pronation.

Foot pronation directly affects subtalar joint pronation, which has an anatomical coupling relationship with tibial internal rotation [37]. Post-fatigue foot pronation increases in AS-5 mm, driving increased tibial internal rotation through the subtalar-tibial coupling mechanism. This change is reflected in ankle dorsiflexion angle interaction effects, with significant kinematic differences between AS-5 mm and AS-20 mm post-fatigue.

Increased tibial internal rotation alters the spatial relationship between tibial plateau and femoral condyles, affecting the knee’s “screw-home” mechanism and reducing knee stability in frontal and transverse planes [38]. The observed post-fatigue decreases in knee abduction angle (increased adduction) and reduced internal-external rotation stability in AS-5 mm directly reflects this mechanism. In contrast, AS-20 mm maintains foot stability, reduces tibial internal rotation input, and thereby improves knee kinematic stability under fatigued conditions.

Knee kinematic changes further transmit proximally, affecting hip rotational control. When knee adduction and internal rotation increase, the hip is forced to produce compensatory internal rotation to maintain overall lower limb alignment and movement completion [39]. The significant increase in post-fatigue hip internal rotation throughout the entire phase in AS-5 mm exemplifies this “distal instability-proximal compensation” pattern. AS-20 mm effectively mitigates this adverse transmission chain by providing stable arch support, reducing compensatory hip internal rotation.

Clinical and practical implications of interaction effects

The transmission mechanism of these interaction effects reveals the “lever effect” of arch support design, as the distal origin of the kinematic chain, changes in foot stability progressively amplify proximally along the limb. This finding has important practical implications. Within the limits of the measured variables, athletes prioritizing jump performance may benefit from lower arch-support shoes via increased joint range of motion, whereas higher arch-support shoes were associated with more stable proximal-joint kinematics under fatigue. Furthermore, footwear selection for different arch support heights should consider usage context, athlete fatigue status, and individual injury risk factors.

Limitations and future directions

This study has the following limitations deserving considerations. (1) Only male participants were included. Due to sex differences in pelvic width, Q-angle, and ligament laxity, results cannot be directly generalized to female runners. Future studies should include female participants to explore sex differences. (2) Only two arch support heights (5 mm and 20 mm) were examined. Future research could incorporate finer arch support gradients or stratify analysis by participant arch type to establish dose-response relationships between arch support height and biomechanical effects. (3) Surface electromyography (sEMG) was not incorporated. Future studies could use EMG data to quantify fatigue effects on key muscle activation timing and amplitude, elucidating neuromuscular control mechanisms underlying interaction effects. (4) Plantar pressure distribution was not measured. Future research could incorporate pressure insole data to directly quantify arch support effects on plantar load distribution, strengthening the empirical basis for transmission mechanisms. (5) Only male runners with normal feet were included; the results cannot be generalized to those with flat feet or high-arched feet.

Conclusions

By analysing the take-off biomechanics during CMJ before and after half-marathon running with different arch support shoes (AS-5 mm and AS-20 mm), this study systematically reveals the mechanisms by which arch support height, acute fatigue, and their interaction affect the lower limb kinematic chain. Main conclusions were that (1) Arch support height significantly affects jump performance, and the low arch-support shoes (AS-5 mm) were associated with an approximately 20.5% greater jump height than high arch-support shoes, accompanied by increased hip, knee, and ankle joint range of motion and a pattern consistent with enhanced elastic energy utilization. This “distal-to-proximal” kinematic chain transmission indicates that arch deformation sequentially affects ankle dorsiflexion, knee flexion, and hip flexion through the windlass mechanism, forming a synergistic energy transfer pattern. (2) Acute fatigue alters lower limb neuromuscular control patterns, that the half-marathon-induced fatigue causes significant increases in hip internal rotation throughout jumping, increased knee flexion, and reduced frontal plane stability. Concurrent hip internal rotation and knee adduction form a “dynamic valgus” pattern, reflecting fatigue-induced functional decline of hip external rotators and reduced neuromuscular control precision, increasing injury risk. (3) Significant interaction effects exist between arch support and fatigue that the post-fatigue hip internal rotation and knee adduction significantly increase under low arch support, while changes under high arch support are minimal. The mechanism: high arch support provides effective passive support during plantar flexor fatigue, limiting arch collapse and foot pronation, reducing tibial internal rotation through subtalar-tibial coupling, thereby protecting knee stability and reducing compensatory hip internal rotation.
