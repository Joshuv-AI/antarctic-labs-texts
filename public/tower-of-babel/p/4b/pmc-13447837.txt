Psychological associations of sports technology use with technostress, self-efficacy, cognitive weariness, and athlete burnout
Authors: Pan Xiugang, Erum Rehman, Khalid Abdullah Alotaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing integration of digital technologies in sport training environments has raised concerns regarding their potential psychological implications. This study examined the associations among technostress, athlete burnout, self-efficacy, and cognitive weariness among university students enrolled in sports-related academic disciplines and organized university athletic training programs. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,612 university students majoring in sports-related disciplines and/or formally engaged in organized university-level athletic training programs across universities in Xi’an, China, between November and December 2025. Data were collected using validated self-report measures of technostress, self-efficacy, cognitive weariness, and athlete burnout. Structural equation modeling and regression analyses were performed using IBM SPSS and AMOS. Indirect associations were examined using bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess robustness to potential unmeasured confounding. Technostress was directly associated with athlete burnout (β = 0.22, p < 0.001) and indirectly associated with burnout through cognitive weariness and self-efficacy. The path from technostress to cognitive weariness (β = 0.42, p < 0.001) and, subsequently, from cognitive weariness to athlete burnout (β = 0.43, p < 0.001) represented the stronger indirect pathway. In contrast, the pathways from technostress to self-efficacy (β = − 0.34, p < 0.001) and from self-efficacy to athlete burnout (β = − 0.24, p < 0.001) indicated concurrent but comparatively weaker indirect associations. The model accounted for 56% of the variance in athlete burnout (R2 = 0.56). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the indirect associations remained stable unless an unmeasured confounder explained approximately 30% of the residual variance (|ρ| ≈ 0.55). These findings underscore the importance of adopting a balanced perspective on technology integration in sport training environments, where performance enhancement and athlete well-being are considered simultaneously. In student-athlete populations, attention to cognitive demands and individual coping resources may be particularly relevant for understanding technology-related strain. Future research using longitudinal and multi-source designs may further clarify these relationships in evolving sport contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55132-5.

Introduction

The integration of digital technologies into sport training environments has accelerated markedly in recent years, transforming how individuals engaged in sport training, performance development, and sport-related education monitor performance and engage with coaching processes1–3. Tools such as wearable sensors4, performance analytics platforms5, and AI-assisted coaching systems6 are now widely embedded within sport education and training programs, enabling continuous monitoring of physiological, biomechanical, and tactical indicators7,8. In university-based sport and sport-education contexts, these technologies are increasingly incorporated into training and instructional routines, providing real-time feedback and data-driven insights intended to optimize performance and skill development1–5. While such advancements offer clear benefits in terms of precision, personalization, and efficiency, they also introduce new forms of demand, including constant performance evaluation, information overload, and heightened expectations for responsiveness9,10. As a result, technology use in sport-related contexts can be characterized as a double-edged phenomenon, simultaneously functioning as a performance-enhancing resource and a potential source of psychological strain. Despite the growing reliance on digital tools in sport settings, the psychological implications of sustained technology exposure remain insufficiently understood. In this context, the concept of technostress provides a relevant framework for examining how technology-related demands may be associated with strain and well-being outcomes in contemporary sport environments.

Technostress refers to the psychological strain experienced as a result of difficulties in adapting to, managing, or coping with the demands associated with technology use9,11. Within sport training environments, technostress may arise from factors such as information overload, constant performance monitoring, rapid technological changes, and heightened expectations for responsiveness to digital feedback12,13. From a theoretical perspective, this association can be understood through the Transactional Model of Stress (TMS), which posits that stress emerges when perceived demands exceed an individual’s coping resources14, as well as the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, which suggests that sustained demands may be associated with strain and exhaustion when not adequately balanced by resources15. In sport-specific contexts, athlete burnout—characterized by emotional and physical exhaustion, reduced sense of accomplishment, and sport devaluation—has been linked to prolonged exposure to demanding training conditions and psychological stressors16–18. Emerging empirical evidence further indicates that technostress is associated with increased fatigue, psychological strain, and reduced well-being in student and performance-oriented populations19,20. Within technology-enhanced sport training contexts, technostress may therefore represent a relevant correlate of athlete burnout. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that technostress is associated with athlete burnout (H1).

Self-efficacy, defined as individuals’ beliefs in their capability to organize and execute actions required to manage prospective demands, represents a central psychological resource in stress and coping processes21. According to Social Cognitive Theory (SCT)22, self-efficacy influences how individuals appraise challenges, regulate effort, and persist in the face of difficulty. In technology-enhanced environments, individuals who perceive technological demands as complex or overwhelming may report lower confidence in their ability to manage them effectively, thereby shaping their stress responses. Empirical evidence suggests that exposure to digital technostress is associated with diminished self-efficacy and increased perceptions of technological helplessness, particularly in student populations where rapid technological change and information overload are prevalent23,24. For instance, individuals experiencing higher levels of technostress are more likely to question their ability to cope with technological challenges, leading to reduced perceived control and heightened strain24. Furthermore, perceptions of technological complexity and overload have been linked to frustration, anxiety, and reduced confidence in one’s ability to manage digital demands, reinforcing the role of self-efficacy in shaping stress responses9,25. Empirical research in educational and digital learning contexts has shown that higher levels of technostress are associated with reduced self-efficacy, reflecting difficulties in coping with rapidly evolving technological requirements20,23. In turn, lower self-efficacy has been linked to higher levels of burnout-related outcomes, as individuals with weaker efficacy beliefs may perceive demands as less controllable and more taxing26–28. In technology-enhanced sport environments, self-efficacy may play an important role in shaping how technology-related demands are experienced. As such, technostress may be associated with athlete burnout through its relationship with self-efficacy. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that technostress is associated with athlete burnout through self-efficacy (H2).

Cognitive weariness, reflecting mental fatigue, reduced cognitive energy, and diminished attentional capacity, represents a key component of strain in demanding environments29,30. Within the JD-R model, such symptoms are conceptualized as outcomes of sustained demands that deplete individuals’ cognitive and energetic resources15. In technology-enhanced sport settings, student-athletes are frequently required to process continuous streams of performance data, interpret real-time feedback, and adapt to digitally mediated training systems, all of which may increase cognitive load. Prior research has shown that technostress is associated with cognitive overload and mental fatigue in technology-intensive contexts, reflecting the taxing nature of persistent digital demands9,31. In turn, cognitive weariness has been linked to burnout-related outcomes, particularly exhaustion and disengagement, as depleted cognitive resources may limit individuals’ capacity to sustain effort and maintain performance17,30. Compared to broader psychological resources such as self-efficacy, cognitive weariness may represent a more proximal correlate of burnout, as it directly reflects the depletion of energy required for ongoing task engagement. In technology-enhanced sport settings, individuals are frequently required to process performance-related information and adapt to digitally mediated training systems, which may increase cognitive load. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that technostress is associated with athlete burnout through cognitive weariness (H3).

While prior research has examined the associations between technostress and adverse psychological outcomes, much of this work has focused on single explanatory mechanisms in isolation, limiting understanding of how multiple processes may operate concurrently. The present study adopts a parallel mediation perspective to capture two theoretically distinct yet complementary pathways through which technostress may be associated with athlete burnout. Specifically, the proposed model integrates a cognitive pathway, reflected in cognitive weariness as an indicator of energy depletion, and a motivational pathway, reflected in self-efficacy as a key personal resource influencing coping and appraisal processes. This dual-path approach is consistent with broader theoretical perspectives suggesting that strain-related outcomes emerge from both resource depletion and variations in perceived capability15,21. Importantly, these mechanisms are conceptualized as operating simultaneously rather than sequentially, as cognitive fatigue and efficacy beliefs may co-occur in response to technology-related demands rather than follow a fixed temporal order. Within technology-enhanced university sport contexts, individuals must continuously process performance-related information while maintaining confidence in their abilities, underscoring the importance of examining these pathways concurrently. By simultaneously testing these mechanisms and comparing their relative strength, the present study extends prior research that has typically emphasized single mediators and contributes to a more nuanced understanding of psychological responses to technology in sport settings.

Despite the growing body of research on technostress and psychological outcomes, several important gaps remain. First, existing studies have predominantly focused on workplace or general student populations, with comparatively limited attention to sport-specific contexts where performance demands, structured training routines, and technology integration are particularly pronounced9,20. Second, although technostress has been linked to indicators of strain and well-being, prior research has rarely examined its association with athlete burnout, a construct that is highly relevant in sport settings characterized by sustained physical and psychological demands17. Third, the mechanisms underlying the association between technostress and burnout remain insufficiently integrated, as most studies have examined cognitive or motivational processes in isolation rather than their concurrent operation. In particular, limited attention has been given to the joint roles of cognitive weariness and self-efficacy within a unified framework. Moreover, few studies have compared the relative strength of these mechanisms, leaving it unclear whether energy depletion or personal resources represent more salient correlates of burnout in technology-enhanced environments. Finally, methodological approaches in this area have often relied on conventional mediation analyses without assessing the robustness of findings to potential unmeasured confounding. Addressing these gaps is essential to developing a more comprehensive understanding of how technology-related demands are associated with athlete burnout in technology-enhanced sport settings.

In response to these gaps, the present study aims to examine the associations between technostress and athlete burnout, with particular attention to the roles of self-efficacy and cognitive weariness as parallel correlates. By situating the analysis within sport education contexts, this study extends existing technostress research beyond general student and workplace populations to a setting characterized by structured training demands and intensive technology use. Theoretically, the study integrates perspectives from the TMS, the JD-R model, and SCT, thereby capturing technology-related stress appraisal processes, resource depletion (cognitive weariness), and personal resource (self-efficacy) mechanisms within a unified framework. Analytically, the study adopts a parallel mediation approach to simultaneously examine these mechanisms and assess their relative strength, providing a more nuanced understanding of how different psychological processes are associated with burnout. Methodologically, the inclusion of sensitivity analysis further strengthens the robustness of the findings by evaluating their susceptibility to potential unmeasured confounding. Collectively, these contributions offer insights into the psychological implications of technology use in contemporary sport environments and may inform more balanced approaches to integrating digital tools within sport training and education contexts.

Although the present study draws primarily on broader psychological frameworks, including the TMS, the JD-R model, and SCT, these perspectives remain conceptually relevant to sport environments because athletes and university-based sport participants are similarly exposed to sustained performance demands, stress appraisal processes, resource depletion, and coping-related challenges. At the same time, the present study acknowledges that sport settings also involve unique contextual factors—such as competitive evaluation, coach–athlete dynamics, training intensity, and performance expectations—that may shape experiences of technostress and burnout in distinct ways.

Methods

Study Design and Setting This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to examine the associations of technostress, self-efficacy, and cognitive weariness with athlete burnout among university students enrolled in sports-related academic disciplines and/or engaged in organized university-level athletic training programs. Data were collected between November and December 2025 from universities located in Xi’an, Shaanxi Province, China, a major educational center with a substantial concentration of higher education institutions offering programs in physical education, sports training, exercise science, and related disciplines. The cross-sectional design was considered appropriate because the study aimed to assess the contemporaneous relationships among technology-related psychological strain, personal coping resources, cognitive fatigue, and burnout symptoms within a defined athletic student population.

Participants and Target Population The target population comprised Chinese university students in Xi’an enrolled in sports-related academic disciplines involving structured sport training, physical performance, coaching, or movement science and/or formally engaged in organized university-level athletic training contexts. To ensure conceptual alignment with the study topic, participants were recruited specifically from students whose educational or training routines regularly involved sport participation, structured physical training, performance monitoring, or technology-supported training environments. This population was selected because athlete burnout is most meaningfully examined among individuals who are exposed to sustained athletic demands rather than among general university students with only casual involvement in physical activity. For this reason, the eligible population was defined as students meeting at least one of the following criteria: (1) enrollment in a sports-related academic discipline, such as Physical Education, Sports Training, Exercise Science, Sports Coaching, Sports Rehabilitation, Kinesiology, or other closely related majors; and/or, (2) participation in formal university-level athletic activities, including varsity teams, competitive university sports teams, specialized sport training groups, or institutionally organized high-performance sport programs.

To preserve conceptual consistency, the study focused on students from disciplines in which sport participation, training, coaching, physical performance, or sport science constituted a central component of study or practice. Where institutional structures differed across universities, closely related departments or programs were also considered eligible if their curricula were clearly centered on sport training, sport performance, coaching, or movement science. Students from non-sport disciplines were excluded unless they were formally affiliated with university athletic teams or structured competitive training programs. This discipline-based approach is methodologically defensible because students in these fields are more likely to encounter the forms of sports technology most relevant to the present study, including wearable monitoring systems, video-based performance analysis, online training management platforms, fitness tracking applications, AI-assisted coaching tools, and digital feedback systems. As a result, they constitute an appropriate population for studying the potential dark side of sports technology in relation to technostress and burnout.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria Participants were eligible to participate if they met all of the following criteria: (1) were enrolled as full-time students in a university located in Xi’an, Shaanxi Province; (2) were studying in a sport-related academic discipline or were formally engaged in organized university-level athletic training; (3) had regular exposure to sports-related digital technologies, such as wearable devices, training applications, performance monitoring systems, online coaching platforms, or video-analysis tools; (4) were able to read and understand Chinese; and (5) provided informed consent prior to participation.

Participants were excluded if they: (1) were not enrolled in sports-related academic programs and were not formally engaged in organized university-level athletic training; (2) reported only occasional recreational exercise without structured athletic engagement; (3) submitted incomplete questionnaires with substantial missing data; or (4) showed evidence of careless responding, including uniform responses across all items or implausibly short completion times.

Following data screening and quality checks, the final analytic sample comprised 1,612 participants. This set of criteria ensured that the final sample represented students for whom sports technology use formed a routine component of their academic or athletic activities.

Sampling Strategy A multistage stratified convenience sampling approach was adopted. This approach is more realistic and defensible than claiming pure probability sampling in university-based survey research, particularly when participant access depends on departmental cooperation and administrative permissions. In the first stage, universities in Xi’an with identifiable sport-related departments, schools, or training units were approached for participation. These included institutions with programs in physical education, sport science, sports training, or related fields. In the second stage, eligible academic units within participating universities were identified, including departments of physical education, schools of sport science, and university sport training centers. In the third stage, students were recruited through intact classes, departmental communication channels, training groups, and university athletic team networks.

To improve representativeness within the accessible population, stratification was applied where feasible according to key academic and athletic characteristics, such as discipline/major, year of study, and type of structured athletic involvement (e.g., students primarily engaged through sports-related academic training programs, varsity athletic participation, or both). This helped reduce the risk that the sample would be dominated by a single subgroup, such as first-year students or students from only one sport-related major. Importantly, all eligible participants were required to have regular exposure to structured sport training, performance-related demands, and sports technology use, thereby ensuring conceptual alignment with the study’s focus on technostress and athlete burnout in technology-enhanced sport environments. This sampling strategy is appropriate for several reasons. First, it aligns with the institutional reality of participant access in higher education settings. Second, it allows recruitment from multiple universities and subdisciplines, thereby improving sample heterogeneity. Third, it ensures that the study remains focused on a theoretically relevant population while acknowledging that a full sampling frame of all sports-related students in Xi’an may not be practically obtainable.

Recruitment Procedure After obtaining institutional approval from relevant departments or university units, the research team contacted program coordinators, course instructors, coaches, and departmental administrators to facilitate recruitment. Eligible students were invited to participate either during scheduled class sessions, through official student communication platforms, or via organized sport training groups. The survey was administered in Chinese via an online questionnaire platform, enabling efficient distribution across institutions while preserving anonymity. Before beginning the survey, participants were presented with an introductory page that described the study’s purpose, emphasized voluntary participation, explained confidentiality, and provided contact information for the research team. Respondents were informed that there were no right or wrong answers and were encouraged to respond honestly based on their own experiences with training, academic demands, and sports technology use. The average completion time was expected to be approximately 10–15 min. To enhance data quality, the questionnaire included clear instructions and simple response formatting. Prior to statistical analysis, the dataset was screened for missing values, duplicate responses, outliers, and response patterns indicative of inattentive participation. Only those who indicated consent were permitted to proceed to the questionnaire. To reduce duplicate entries, the system was configured to limit repeated submissions from the same device or account where technically feasible. Responses were screened prior to analysis to identify incomplete cases and low-quality data.

Because the study involved multiple latent constructs and hypothesized associations among technostress, self-efficacy, cognitive weariness, and athlete burnout, a relatively large sample was required to ensure stable parameter estimation and adequate statistical power for Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). In general, SEM studies benefit from samples that substantially exceed minimum item-to-parameter recommendations, particularly when multiple latent variables are modeled simultaneously. Accordingly, the study aimed to recruit a large multi-university sample of sports-related students to improve the robustness and precision of the structural estimates. After screening for eligibility and response quality, the final sample of 1,612 participants was considered adequate for the planned analyses.

Instrumentation

Demographics Demographic variables included age, gender, year of study, type of athletic involvement, training frequency, and extent of sports technology use. The extent of sports technology use was assessed using a single self-report item asking participants how frequently they used sports-related digital technologies (e.g., wearable devices, performance analytics platforms, training applications, or digital feedback systems) during their sport training or academic activities. Responses were recorded on a 5-point ordinal scale ranging from 1 = rarely to 5 = very frequently. Based on theoretical relevance and prior research, age, gender, year of study, training frequency, and extent of sports technology use were included as control variables in the structural analyses to account for their potential influence on technostress and athlete burnout. Because the extent of sports technology use was included as a descriptive covariate rather than a latent psychological construct, internal consistency reliability was not applicable. The demographic analysis is presented in Supplementary Material (Table S1).

Technostress Technostress was assessed using the Technostress Creators Scale11. The original scale consists of 23 items across five dimensions: techno-overload, techno-invasion, techno-complexity, techno-insecurity, and techno-uncertainty. Consistent with prior research conducted in Chinese populations, an adapted Chinese version of the scale was used23,32,33. Minor contextual wording modifications were introduced to reflect sports technology environments (e.g., wearable monitoring systems, performance analytics platforms, training applications, and digital feedback tools), while preserving the conceptual meaning and dimensional structure of the original items. The adapted wording was reviewed by bilingual researchers with expertise in sport psychology and technology-related research to ensure contextual appropriateness and conceptual consistency. In addition, the adapted items were pilot-tested with a small group of sports-major university students (n = 30) to assess clarity and comprehension prior to full-scale data collection. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the hypothesized multidimensional structure, with acceptable factor loadings and satisfactory overall fit indices. A sample item is: “I am forced by technology to work much faster than I can handle.” Participants rated their agreement on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, with higher scores indicating greater perceived technostress.

Self-Efficacy Self-efficacy was measured using the General Self-Efficacy Scale34, which comprises 10 items assessing individuals’ general belief in their ability to cope with various challenging demands. A validated Chinese version of this scale has been widely used in prior research and was therefore adopted in the present study35. A sample item is: “I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough.” Responses were recorded on a Likert scale (e.g., 1 = not at all true to 4 = exactly true), with higher scores indicating stronger perceived self-efficacy.

Cognitive Weariness: Cognitive weariness was assessed using the cognitive weariness subscale of the Shirom Melamed Burnout Measure (SMBM)30. This subscale consists of 8 items that capture mental fatigue, reduced cognitive energy, and difficulties in concentration resulting from prolonged demands. Chinese adaptations of the SMBM have been used in prior research8,9, and the scale was translated and applied in accordance with standard forward–backward translation procedures, reviewed by bilingual experts familiar with sport and psychology research, and pilot-tested with a small group of eligible students to ensure clarity and contextual relevance. A sample item is: “I feel mentally exhausted.” Participants rated their responses on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = almost never to 7 = almost always), with higher scores indicating greater cognitive weariness.

Athlete Burnout Athlete burnout was measured using the Athlete Burnout Questionnaire36, consisting of 15 items across three dimensions: emotional/physical exhaustion (5 items), reduced sense of accomplishment (5 items), and sport devaluation (5 items), for which a validated Chinese version37 was directly employed in the present study. A sample item is: “I feel overly tired from my sport participation.” Participants responded using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = almost never to 5 = almost always), with higher scores indicating greater athlete burnout.

Ethical Considerations The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) of Xi’an Jiaotong University prior to data collection (ref#R/40091/2025). All participants provided informed consent electronically before completing the survey. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents were informed of their right to discontinue the survey at any time without penalty. Data were collected anonymously and analyzed in aggregate form to protect participant confidentiality.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for preliminary analyses and AMOS or an equivalent SEM package for confirmatory factor analysis and SEM. First, descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables, including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis, to assess data distribution. Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. The measurement model was then assessed using confirmatory factor analysis to examine factor loadings, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Following evaluation of the measurement model, the hypothesized structural model was tested to examine the direct effects of technostress, self-efficacy, and cognitive weariness on athlete burnout. Based on the current conceptualization of the study, these variables were modeled within a parallel structural framework, in which technostress, self-efficacy, and cognitive weariness were simultaneously specified as predictors of athlete burnout rather than as sequentially ordered mediators. This approach was considered more appropriate because the present study is centered on the unique and concurrent contributions of technology-related strain, personal coping beliefs, and cognitive fatigue to burnout among student-athletes and sports-major students. Standard model fit indices were reported, including the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ2/df), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Conventional cutoffs were used to judge acceptable model fit. Demographic covariates such as age, gender, year of study, training frequency, and extent of sports technology use were controlled for in the structural model to reduce potential confounding.

Results

Preliminary Diagnostics Given that the data were collected using a self-reported, cross-sectional design, common method bias (CMB) was assessed using both Harman’s single-factor test and a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)-based common latent factor (CLF) approach. For Harman’s test, all measurement items were entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis, and the first factor accounted for 31.27% of the total variance, which was below the recommended threshold of 40%, suggesting that CMB was not a serious concern. In addition, a CLF was incorporated into the measurement model by allowing all items to load onto both their respective constructs and a common factor. The comparison between the baseline and CLF models revealed no substantial improvement in model fit, and differences in standardized factor loadings were minimal (i.e., below 0.20), further indicating that CMB did not significantly influence the study results.

Reliability and Validity Diagnostics Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics, reliability indices, and measurement model fit for all study variables. Overall, the variables demonstrated acceptable distributional properties, with skewness values ranging from − 0.49 to 0.31 and kurtosis values from − 0.44 to 0.27, indicating no substantial deviation from normality. Internal consistency was high across all constructs (α = 0.88–0.89; CR = 0.89–0.90). Convergent validity was supported, as average variance extracted (AVE) values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.50 (AVE = 0.621–0.667). The measurement models also showed good fit, with RMSEA (0.046–0.049), SRMR (0.031–0.039), CFI (0.94–0.95), and TLI (0.94–0.95) all within acceptable ranges. Taken together, these results indicate that the measurement properties of the constructs were adequate for subsequent analyses.

Correlational Analysis Table 2 presents the bivariate correlations and discriminant validity, as assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion. Technostress was positively correlated with cognitive weariness (r = 0.575, p < 0.001) and athlete burnout (r = 0.523, p < 0.001), and negatively correlated with self-efficacy (r = − 0.414, p < 0.001). Cognitive weariness showed a positive association with athlete burnout (r = 0.625, p < 0.001), representing the strongest correlation among the study variables. Self-efficacy was negatively correlated with both cognitive weariness (r = − 0.297, p < 0.01) and athlete burnout (r = − 0.478, p < 0.001). Additionally, the square roots of the AVEs (0.788–0.817) exceeded the inter-construct correlations, supporting discriminant validity. Overall, the pattern of correlations was consistent with the hypothesized relationships and did not indicate multicollinearity concerns.

In addition, discriminant validity was supported using the Fornell–Larcker criterion, as the square roots of the AVE for each construct (ranging from 0.788 to 0.817) exceeded the corresponding inter-construct correlations. This indicates that each construct accounts for more variance among its indicators than among indicators of other constructs.

Among the control variables, extent of sports technology use was positively associated with cognitive weariness (β = 0.18, p < 0.001) and athlete burnout (β = 0.16, p < 0.001), and negatively associated with self-efficacy (β = −0.14, p < 0.001). Training frequency was negatively associated with cognitive weariness (β = −0.12, p = 0.001) and athlete burnout (β = −0.11, p = 0.002). Other demographic variables showed smaller but statistically significant associations. The models explained moderate proportions of variance in self-efficacy (R2 = 0.31), cognitive weariness (R2 = 0.47), and athlete burnout (R2 = 0.56), indicating that the included predictors accounted for a substantial share of the variability in the outcome variables.

Parallel Mediation Analysis Figure 1 presents the parallel mediation model with standardized path coefficients and bootstrapped confidence intervals. The results indicated that technostress was directly associated with athlete burnout (β = 0.215, 95% CI [0.112, 0.355]) and indirectly associated with athlete burnout through two independent pathways involving self-efficacy and cognitive weariness. The bootstrapped indirect effect through self-efficacy was statistically significant (β = 0.080, 95% CI [0.041, 0.145]), indicating that lower self-efficacy was associated with higher athlete burnout. Similarly, the indirect effect through cognitive weariness was significant (β = 0.178, 95% CI [0.098, 0.255]), suggesting that higher technostress was associated with greater cognitive weariness, which in turn was associated with higher athlete burnout. The total indirect effect across both mediating pathways was β = 0.258. The indirect pathway involving cognitive weariness appeared comparatively stronger, consistent with the larger association between cognitive weariness and athlete burnout observed in the regression analysis. Overall, the findings were consistent with a parallel mediation framework, in which self-efficacy and cognitive weariness independently mediated the association between technostress and athlete burnout, with the cognitive weariness pathway appearing comparatively stronger.

Sensitivity Analysis Sensitivity Analysis: To evaluate the robustness of the indirect effects against potential unmeasured confounding, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted following the framework of Kosuke Imai, Luke Keele, and Teppei Yamamoto (2010)38. This approach estimates the extent to which an unobserved confounder would need to simultaneously influence both the mediator and the outcome to invalidate the Average Causal Mediation Effect (ACME). The analysis was implemented using the mediation package in R with 5,000 nonparametric bootstrap replications. Results indicated that the indirect effect of technostress on athlete burnout via cognitive weariness remained statistically significant unless a hypothetical confounder accounted for approximately 30% of the residual variance in both the mediator and the outcome (R2 = 0.30). A comparable pattern was observed for the indirect pathway involving self-efficacy. The sensitivity parameter (ρ) at which the ACME approached zero was approximately |ρ| ≈ 0.55, suggesting that relatively substantial unmeasured confounding would be required to fully attenuate the observed indirect associations within the simplified single-mediator sensitivity models. However, because the present study involved multiple concurrent mediators examined within a broader structural framework, these sensitivity estimates should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive evidence of robustness for the complete mediation model.

Discussion

The present study examined the associations between technostress, self-efficacy, cognitive weariness, and athlete burnout among university students engaged in sports-related academic and athletic contexts. Drawing on a parallel mediation framework, this study contributes to the growing literature on the psychological implications of technology use in sport settings by simultaneously considering both strain-related and protective psychological correlates. The findings indicated that technostress was positively associated with cognitive weariness and athlete burnout, whereas self-efficacy was negatively associated with both. Notably, cognitive weariness demonstrated the strongest association with athlete burnout, suggesting a comparatively prominent role within the observed pattern of relationships. In addition, the results were consistent with the proposed model in which technostress was associated with athlete burnout both directly and indirectly through cognitive weariness and self-efficacy, with the pathway involving cognitive weariness appearing comparatively stronger. These findings highlight the relevance of considering both technology-related strain and individual psychological resources when examining athlete burnout among university-based sport populations in technology-enhanced sport environments.

Consistent with the study’s first hypothesis, technostress was positively associated with athlete burnout. This finding aligns with a growing body of literature suggesting that technology-related demands are associated with heightened psychological strain and exhaustion, particularly in performance-oriented environments. Within sport settings, the increasing integration of digital tools—such as wearable monitoring systems, performance analytics platforms, and AI-assisted training applications—has introduced additional cognitive and performance-related demands within university-based sport environments. Although these technologies are intended to support training efficiency, they may also be associated with perceptions of overload, constant monitoring, and reduced autonomy, all of which have been theoretically linked to burnout-related outcomes9,33. From a theoretical perspective, the observed associations can be interpreted through the TMS, which posits that strain may emerge when perceived demands exceed available coping resources14. Among university students engaged in structured sport training and technology-enhanced athletic activities, continuous exposure to digital performance demands may be associated with heightened perceived pressure and exhaustion-related experiences. Empirical research has similarly shown that higher levels of technostress are associated with indicators of psychological fatigue and reduced well-being in student and performance-oriented populations. In higher education contexts, technostress has been associated with impaired cognitive functioning, increased anxiety, and reduced learning quality among university students, reflecting broader associations with mental well-being39. More recent evidence also indicates that prolonged exposure to digital demands is associated with cognitive strain, emotional exhaustion, and reduced well-being, particularly under conditions of information overload and constant connectivity40. Additionally, studies in technology-intensive environments have reported associations between continuous interaction with digital systems and heightened fatigue and reduced psychological functioning, reinforcing the broader relationship between technostress and well-being outcomes in performance-oriented populations20,40,41. The present findings extend this evidence to university-based sport contexts, suggesting that technology-related strain may represent a relevant correlate of athlete burnout among students engaged in structured sport training environments.

The findings were also consistent with the second hypothesis, indicating that self-efficacy was associated with the relationship between technostress and athlete burnout. This pattern aligns with SCT22, which emphasizes that individuals’ beliefs in their capability to manage demands play an important role in how stressors are appraised and responded to. In technology-intensive sport environments, individuals engaged in structured sport training are frequently required to interpret performance feedback, adapt to digital training systems, and respond to continuously updated performance expectations. When these demands are perceived as overwhelming, they may be associated with reduced confidence in one’s ability to manage training- and academic-related responsibilities effectively. Empirical research has shown that higher levels of technostress are associated with reduced self-efficacy in educational and digital learning contexts, reflecting the challenges individuals face in coping with complex and evolving technological demands19,20,23. For instance, studies among university students have reported that technostress is associated with diminished confidence in managing academic and technology-related tasks, underscoring the role of self-efficacy as an important psychological resource23. In turn, lower self-efficacy has been consistently associated with higher levels of burnout-related outcomes, as individuals with weaker efficacy beliefs may perceive demands as less controllable and more taxing, thereby increasing vulnerability to exhaustion and disengagement42,43. Within university-based sport settings, self-efficacy may therefore represent an important psychological correlate in understanding how technology-related demands are associated with burnout. The present findings suggest that technostress was associated with athlete burnout, both directly and indirectly through its relationship with self-efficacy, highlighting the relevance of individual coping beliefs when examining technology-related strain in technology-enhanced sport environments.

The findings further supported the third hypothesis, indicating that cognitive weariness was associated with the technostress-athlete burnout relationship. This pattern is consistent with theoretical perspectives such as the JD-R model, which proposes that sustained demands requiring continuous cognitive effort may be associated with energy depletion and strain-related outcomes. In technology-enhanced sport environments, individuals engaged in structured sport training are frequently exposed to continuous streams of performance-related information, real-time feedback, and digitally mediated training demands, particularly through wearable devices and analytics platforms that are increasingly integrated into training monitoring and performance evaluation processes44,45. Such environments often require sustained attention, rapid information processing, and ongoing responsiveness, conditions that have been associated with increased cognitive load and mental strain in technology-intensive settings46. Over time, prolonged exposure to these demands may be associated with mental fatigue, reduced attentional capacity, and difficulties sustaining concentration, consistent with core features of cognitive weariness46,47. Empirical evidence has shown that technostress is positively associated with cognitive fatigue and mental exhaustion in academic and performance-oriented settings, reflecting the taxing nature of continuous technology use9,31. In turn, cognitive weariness has been consistently associated with burnout-related outcomes, particularly in populations exposed to sustained performance demands, as diminished cognitive resources may coincide with reduced engagement and greater exhaustion16,17,48,49. Within university-based sport settings, cognitive weariness may therefore represent an important psychological correlate linking technology-related demands with burnout-related experiences. The present findings suggest that technostress was associated with athlete burnout not only directly but also indirectly through its relationship with cognitive weariness, highlighting the relevance of cognitive fatigue when examining technology-related strain in technology-enhanced sport environments.

An additional observation from the present findings is that the pathway involving cognitive weariness appeared comparatively stronger than that involving self-efficacy. This pattern was reflected in the magnitude of the observed associations, in which cognitive weariness showed stronger associations with both technostress and athlete burnout than self-efficacy. The findings are broadly consistent with theoretical perspectives suggesting that resource-depletion processes may represent more proximal psychological correlates of burnout-related experiences than individual coping beliefs15,29. Within university-based sport environments characterized by sustained cognitive and physical demands, mental fatigue may therefore be more closely associated with exhaustion-related outcomes than comparatively broader psychological resources. At the same time, the pathway involving self-efficacy remained statistically significant, indicating that both mechanisms may operate concurrently, with differing relative strengths. These findings underscore the relevance of considering both energy depletion (cognitive weariness) and personal psychological resources (self-efficacy) when examining athlete burnout within technology-enhanced university sport settings.

Finally, the robustness of the observed indirect associations was further examined through sensitivity analysis, which suggested that the indirect associations remained relatively stable across a range of plausible sensitivity parameter values within the simplified single-mediator models. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the sensitivity analyses were conducted separately for each indirect pathway rather than within the complete multiple-mediator framework. In addition, given the cross-sectional and self-reported nature of the data, the findings remain subject to potential unmeasured confounding and should not be interpreted causally. Future research using longitudinal, time-lagged, or multi-source designs would be valuable in further examining these relationships.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal ordering among variables, and the observed associations should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future research employing longitudinal or time-lagged designs would be valuable in examining the stability and directionality of these relationships over time. Second, all variables were assessed using self-report measures, which may introduce CMB despite the procedural and statistical steps taken to mitigate its influence. Incorporating multiple data sources, such as coach evaluations or objective performance indicators, could strengthen the validity of future findings. Third, the study focused on university students engaged in sports-related academic and athletic contexts within a specific regional setting in China. Although all participants were exposed to structured sport training and technology-enhanced athletic environments, the sample primarily consisted of student-athletes and university students involved in sports who experience both academic and athletic demands. Consequently, the findings may not fully generalize to full-time professional athletes or other sport populations operating outside university-based training and educational contexts. In addition, some variability may still exist in the nature of training demands and burnout experiences across different sport-related academic and athletic subgroups. Fourth, although key demographic variables were controlled for, other potentially relevant factors—such as personality traits, coping styles, or sleep quality—were not included and may also be associated with technostress and burnout. Future research may benefit from integrating these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying athlete burnout. Fifth, while the present study examined parallel associations between self-efficacy and cognitive weariness, future studies could explore alternative model specifications, such as sequential pathways or moderated relationships, to further clarify the interplay between technology-related demands and psychological outcomes in sport settings. Additionally, future research may benefit from integrating sport-specific theoretical perspectives alongside broader psychological frameworks to further contextualize technology-related strain and burnout within athletic environments.

Study implications

The present findings have several potential implications for university-based sport education and training contexts, where digital technologies are increasingly integrated into routine practice. First, the observed associations between technostress and athlete burnout suggest that integrating performance-related technologies warrants careful consideration in technology-enhanced sport environments. Future research may benefit from examining whether structured guidance regarding the use of sports technologies—including the management of monitoring frequency and data-feedback expectations—is associated with lower perceptions of overload and evaluation-related strain among student-athletes and sports-related university students. Second, given the stronger association with cognitive weariness, future studies may benefit from exploring whether strategies aimed at managing cognitive load in training environments are associated with reduced cognitive fatigue and burnout-related experiences. For example, future intervention-based research could examine whether balancing technology-assisted feedback with periods of reduced informational input or recovery-oriented training sessions is associated with improved cognitive recovery in technology-intensive sport settings. Third, the observed role of self-efficacy highlights the potential relevance of individual confidence in managing both technological and performance-related demands. Future research could investigate whether educational or training approaches focused on improving students’ understanding and management of sports technologies are associated with more adaptive responses to technology-related demands. Such approaches may include developing students’ confidence in interpreting performance data and navigating digital training tools within structured sport environments. Finally, the findings suggest that sports technology use may represent an important contextual characteristic within university-based sport settings rather than merely a background factor. Future studies may benefit from examining how the frequency, intensity, and nature of engagement with digital training technologies are associated with psychological strain, cognitive fatigue, and burnout-related experiences across different sport-training contexts. Overall, these implications highlight the importance of adopting a balanced, context-sensitive perspective on technology integration in university-based sport environments, while recognizing that further longitudinal and intervention-based research is necessary to clarify the practical implications of these associations.

Conclusion

This study examined the associations between technostress, self-efficacy, cognitive weariness, and athlete burnout among university students engaged in sports-related academic and athletic contexts. The findings indicated that technostress was positively associated with athlete burnout and was also indirectly associated with burnout through its relationships with cognitive weariness and self-efficacy. Among these pathways, cognitive weariness demonstrated a stronger association with athlete burnout, while self-efficacy showed an inverse concurrent association. These findings highlight the potential relevance of both technology-related demands and individual psychological resources when examining athlete burnout within technology-enhanced university sport environments. Overall, the results suggest that the increasing integration of digital tools into sport training contexts may warrant consideration of their broader psychological associations, particularly among university-based sport populations exposed to both academic and athletic demands.
