How does interactional justice differentiation influence tourists’ revisit intention: the roles of tourism experience quality, tourist loyalty, and tourist tolerance
Authors: Hua Yang, Yiyun Ying, Rujie Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study, grounded in Service-Dominant Logic, aims to investigate the underlying mechanisms through which interactional justice differentiation influences tourists’ revisit intention. A cross-sectional research design was implemented using a questionnaire survey administered to domestic and international tourists at 10 major scenic spots. Through a structured convenience sampling approach, 384 valid responses were collected across multiple time slots to capture variations in visitor demographics and behaviors. Data analysis was conducted using Mplus 8.0. The results indicate that interactional justice differentiation exerts a significant negative effect on revisit intention. Furthermore, the quality of tourism experience and tourist loyalty not only function as independent mediators but also form a sequential mediation pathway between interactional justice differentiation and revisit intention. In addition, tourist tolerance significantly moderates the direct relationship between interactional justice differentiation and revisit intention. These findings extend the theoretical framework of interactional justice into experiential and service-oriented contexts, elucidate the behavioral mechanisms in guide-tourist value co-creation, and provide practical insights for designing equitable service strategies and enhancing destination management performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03528-9.Keywords: Interactional justice differentiation, Quality of tourism experience, Tourist loyalty, Tourist tolerance, Revisit intention

Introduction

In the tourism domain, tourists’ revisit intention has always been a critical topic [1]. Revisit intention refers to the probability that tourists are willing to return to the same destination [2], which can be used to measure the likelihood of them revisiting a specific destination for repeated or similar experiences [3]. Research shows that the advertising investment required to attract and retain repeat customers is significantly less than that for new customers [4, 5]. Repeat customers usually stay longer, spend more, are more satisfied, and spread positive word - of - mouth [6]. Tour guides play a key role in tourists’ overall experience, and their interaction with tourists can greatly affect tourists’ revisit intention. In the tourist - guide interaction, interactional justice, defined as the perceived justice level during social interactions [7, 8], is a significant situational factor [9–11]. Interactional justice includes informational justice (guides should provide accurate, comprehensive, timely, and understandable information) and interpersonal justice (guides must respect tourists’ individuality, needs, and opinions and treat them equally). Based on the Social Exchange Theory, interactional justice affects individuals’ attitudes and behaviors through the reciprocity norm. Those treated justly are more likely to be enthusiastic and loyal [5, 12–14]. Moreover, interactional justice is more perceptible and intervenable than other forms of justice, and its level directly affects individuals’ emotional and psychological states [15, 16]. Due to time and energy constraints, tour guides often struggle to ensure justice in interactions with tourists, leading to inevitable interactional justice differentiation. This differentiation can negatively impact tourists’ psychological processes and behavioral decisions, potentially influencing their revisit intention [17]. Existing studies on tourists’ revisit intention, an important indicator of tourism experience effectiveness, mainly focus on antecedents like perceived value [18–20], satisfaction [21, 22], destination image [23, 24], and tourism experience [25]. Research on interpersonal interaction is limited to interactional justice [26–28], and there is a lack of studies on interactional justice differentiation [29]. In the field of tourism management, there is still a lack of research on the relationship between tour-guide-tourist interactional justice differentiation and tourists’ revisit intention. This leads to several key research questions: Does a significant correlation exist between interactional justice differentiation in tour-guide-tourist interactions and tourists’ overall revisit intention? If such a correlation is present, is it positive or negative? Furthermore, what underlying mechanism explains this relationship? These questions warrant further scholarly investigation.

Literature review and research hypothesis

Service-dominant logic

Service-Dominant Logic (SDL), introduced by Stephen and Robert [30], provides a theoretical framework emphasizing value co-creation through exchange, thereby challenging conventional goods centered views. While traditional logic prioritizes tangible products, SDL identifies service, defined as the application of specialized knowledge and skills for the benefit of others, as the fundamental basis of economic exchange [31, 32]. Value is uniquely determined by the customer based on their experience and perception, meaning the same offering may be valued differently among individuals [32]. Although firms can propose value through product design or communication, the actual realization of value depends on the customer’s usage and evaluation [30, 33].

Guided by SDL’s core tenet that value is co-created through interaction, this study develops a conceptual model in which service providers such as tour guides and tourists jointly create value [34]. Central to this framework is the construct of interactional justice differentiation, which reflects perceived disparities in interpersonal treatment and directly influences tourists’ value perceptions and subsequent behaviors. Consistent with SDL, the model incorporates quality of tourism experience, tourist loyalty, and tourist tolerance as essential variables. The quality of tourism experience represents a multidimensional evaluation profoundly influenced by social interaction [35], encompassing experiences that tourists actively recall and attribute significance to [36–38]. It has become crucial for enhancing destination performance [39, 40]. Tourist loyalty, defined as a commitment to revisit or recommend, leads to repeated engagement and contributes to marketing efficiency and stable revenue [33, 41–43]. Tourist tolerance, the willingness to endure inconveniences during travel [44], serves a moderating role in value co creation [45] by mitigating relational asymmetries [46] and realigning fairness perceptions after poor interactions [47]. However, existing studies remain limited by a narrow focus on hotel contexts [48–50], neglecting the multi actor nature of tourism systems that involve guides, residents, and service staff [44]. Therefore, this study establishes a moderated mediation model incorporating these constructs to elucidate how interactional justice differentiation influences revisit intention.

Interactional justice differentiation and revisit intention

Drawing on the Social Exchange Theory, the interactional justice differentiation between tour guides and tourists is highly likely to have a substantial negative impact on tourists’ revisit intention. Firstly, drawing on the Social Exchange Theory [12, 51], the differentiation in interactional justice can shatter tourists’ expectations about the principle of reciprocity, thus reducing their likelihood of revisiting. The difference in interactional justice between tour guides and tourists can result in a split between “insiders” and “outsiders”. Such a split not only erodes the sense of justice but may also arouse discontent and a sense of estrangement among group members [52]. Similarly, in the interaction between tour guides and tourists, if a tour guide shows obvious preferences or differential treatment towards certain tourists, this interactional justice differentiation will disrupt tourists’ expectations of the reciprocity principle [14], and subsequently reduce their revisit intention. When tourists perceive unjust treatment from the tour guide, they may feel marginalized or unvalued, which can give rise to negative emotions such as disappointment, dissatisfaction, and even anger. These negative emotions will erode tourists’ overall satisfaction with the tourism experience, further reducing their revisit intention [53, 54]. Secondly, from the perspective of the Trust-Based Decision-Making Theory [55], interactional justice differentiation may also affect tourists’ trust in the tour guide and the tourist destination. Trust serves as a crucial predictor of tourists’ revisit intention. If tourists believe that the tour guide’s behavior lacks justice, their trust in the tour guide and the tourist destination will decline significantly [56, 57], thus decreasing the likelihood of a return visit. Moreover, based on the Social Identity Theory [58, 59], interactional justice differentiation may further weaken tourists’ revisit intention by influencing their sense of belonging and identity. Research indicates that when tourists perceive unjust treatment from the tour guide, they may feel excluded or unaccepted, which can lower their sense of belonging and identity towards the tourist destination [60]. This decrease in the sense of belonging and identity will further dampen tourists’ revisit intention, as they may no longer consider the tourist destination a place worthy of another visit. Finally, interactional justice differentiation will reduce tourists’ perceived value of the tourism experience, thereby lowering their satisfaction [61]. When tourists’ satisfaction with the tourism experience decreases, their revisit intention will also decline accordingly. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Interactional justice differentiation between tour guides and tourists negatively affects tourists’ revisit intention.

The mediating role of the quality of tourism experience

Social interaction plays a crucial role in enhancing individuals’ positive emotions. As one of humans’ basic psychological needs, a sense of belonging is closely related to positive emotional experiences. When individuals lack effective interaction, their positive emotional experiences will be significantly reduced [62]. Conversely, high-quality social communication can notably strengthen positive emotional experiences [63, 64]. In the context of tourism, tourism practitioners can make tourists feel respected and recognized by demonstrating good professional qualities, thereby stimulating their sense of self-worth and realizing the value of social experiences [65]. As the foundation of the tourism experience, the quality of interaction is of great significance. Good interaction can significantly improve tourists’ tourism experience [65]. Firstly, according to the Equity Theory [66], interactional justice can significantly enhance tourists’ satisfaction, thus directly improving the quality of their tourism experience. On the contrary, interactional justice differentiation, such as neglect or favoritism, is likely to trigger tourists’ dissatisfaction and directly reduce their overall experience [67]. Secondly, interactional justice differentiation can weaken tourists’ emotional trust in tour guides. The lack of such trust directly leads to negative evaluations of the quality of tourism experience from tourists [67]. In addition, tour guides can promote the establishment of relationships among tourists through organizing activities and coordinating interactions, which can deepen the collective sense of belonging and pleasure [68]. Conversely, interactional justice differentiation (such as excessive attention to or neglect of specific tourists) is likely to cause conflicts and a sense of alienation, directly weakening group cohesion and subsequently reducing the quality of tourists’ tourism experience.

The quality of tourism experience serves as the core determinant driving tourists’ intention to revisit, and its influence mechanism is actualized via multi-dimensional psychological pathways. According to the Expectancy-Value Theory [69], enhancing the quality of tourism experience can remarkably boost tourists’ satisfaction and perceived value, thereby directly facilitating the formation of their revisit intention [18, 70]. Moreover, a high-quality tourism experience can also directly strengthen tourists’ willingness to revisit by shaping a positive destination image [70]. Furthermore, a high-quality tourism experience can deepen the emotional connection between tourists and the destination. On the one hand, the pleasure, relaxation, and immersion in the experience (i.e., the key dimensions of experience quality) significantly increase tourists’ loyalty to the destination [71]. On the other hand, unique and pleasant experience content effectively enhances tourists’ emotional attachment to the destination [72]. As important psychological commitments, loyalty and emotional attachment form a solid antecedent for revisit intention and behavior [71, 72]. Meanwhile, the memory effect of the tourism experience should not be ignored. Profound experience memories not only increase tourists’ appreciation of the experience [73] but also serve as an important reference for their future decisions [37]. In conclusion, the quality of tourism experience is not only a key way to spread positive word-of-mouth and stabilize the source of repeat tourists [37, 74] but also a direct strategy to improve the possibility of tourists’ revisit [75]. Therefore, we propose the hypothesis:

H2: The quality of tourism experience plays a mediating role between tour-guide-tourist interactional justice differentiation and tourists’ revisit intention.

The mediating role of tourist loyalty

The relationship between interactional justice differentiation and tourist loyalty is mediated through three sequential mechanisms: perceived value, emotional experience, and overall evaluation. Here, overall evaluation refers to a comprehensive assessment formed by tourists that integrates not only the perceived value of the destination—including functional benefits and relational quality—but also affective responses and holistic judgments derived from the entire travel experience. Firstly, based on the Equity Theory [66],, interactional justice directly affects tourists’ perceived value and satisfaction. Research shows that differential treatment can significantly reduce tourists’ satisfaction [76]. The root cause is that the service experience violates tourists’ core expectations and weakens their value recognition of the destination. Since tourists’ positive evaluation of the service is based on the perception of justice, the decline in satisfaction directly undermines the psychological foundation of loyalty. Secondly, interactional justice differentiation triggers negative emotional experiences. Differential interaction is likely to arouse tourists’ dissatisfaction and disappointment [77]. Such negative emotions indirectly weaken the psychological bond that maintains loyalty by reducing the positivity of tourism memories. Emotional experience has a significant mediating effect in the formation of loyalty. Finally, unjust interaction undermines the overall experience evaluation. High-quality interaction should enhance tourists’ sense of participation and identity [78], but differential service undermines this effect, leading to a lower assessment of the comprehensive value of the destination by tourists. The decline in this overall evaluation further suppresses the willingness to revisit and recommend [79], systematically weakening behavioral loyalty.

The mechanism of action between tourist loyalty and revisit intention encompasses multi-dimensional psychological and social behavioral pathways. Firstly, tourist loyalty is manifested as emotional attachment to the destination and positive behavioral intentions such as revisiting and recommending it [80]. The core drivers of these intentions stem from tourists’ emotional attachment to the destination and their positive experiences there. Secondly, high-quality tourism experiences can generate positive emotional memories, strengthen destination identity, and thus directly increase the probability of revisiting [81]. Meanwhile, the destination image and perceived value serve as crucial cognitive factors. When tourists have a high evaluation of the destination’s image and value, it significantly enhances the conversion from tourist loyalty to revisit intention [82]. In addition, loyal behaviors spill over into word-of-mouth communication, which, through the social reinforcement mechanism, feeds back into tourists’ own decisions to revisit [83]. Empirical studies further confirm that an increase in tourist loyalty can significantly strengthen revisit intention [84–86]. Moreover, profound tourism experience memories not only enhance tourists’ appreciation of their experiences but also become important reference points in subsequent decision-making [37, 39, 87], thereby continuously reinforcing revisit intention. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3: Tourist loyalty plays a mediating role between tour-guide-tourist interactional justice differentiation and tourists’ revisit intention.

Quality of tourism experience and tourist loyalty

The quality of tourism experience is the core factor driving tourist loyalty, and its influence mechanism exhibits multi-dimensional and multi-level characteristics. Research indicates that the quality of tourism experience has a direct positive impact on tourist loyalty [88, 89]. Specifically, the quality of tourists’ experiences in terms of services, environmental atmosphere, and interaction processes profoundly affects their overall evaluations, which in turn directly influence their level of loyalty [71, 90, 91]. Moreover, when tourists perceive that the experience is worth more than the cost, it stimulates positive emotional attachment, thereby directly enhancing their loyalty [92, 93]. This aligns with the emotional attachment theory [94], which suggests that positive emotional experiences lead to stronger attachments and loyalty. More importantly, tourism experiences can directly promote tourists’ loyal behaviors by shaping a positive destination image and value perception [83]. Meanwhile, tourism experiences also further consolidate tourists’ loyalty by influencing their emotional memories and attachments [82]. Therefore, the quality of tourism experience not only acts directly on tourist loyalty but also comprehensively strengthens loyal behaviors through various paths. Combining Hypotheses 2 and 3, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4. The quality of tourism experience and tourist loyalty play a chain-mediating role between tour-guide-tourist interactional justice differentiation and tourists’ revisit intention.

The moderating effect of tourist tolerance

The Social Exchange Theory posits that individuals engage in social behavioral exchanges driven by self-interest and expectations. When the perceived benefits surpass the costs, they are inclined to continue the interaction; otherwise, they may disengage [45]. This theoretical framework provides an important basis for understanding the moderating effect of tourist tolerance on the relationship between interactional justice differentiation and revisit intention.

The first is the attribution-buffering path. As per the Attribution Theory [95], when tourists sense injustice in the interaction, their tolerance level dictates how they attribute the event, which then affects their revisit intention. High-tolerance tourists are likely to attribute negative events, like interactional justice differentiation, to external situational factors such as work pressure or resource scarcity. This attribution fosters empathetic understanding of the service [96], lessens negative emotions, and sustains a relatively high revisit intention [97]. In contrast, low-tolerance tourists tend to blame the tour-guide’s personal flaws, such as a poor attitude or lack of competence. This attribution intensifies negative emotions and curbs the revisit intention [47]. This attribution difference underlies the moderating effect of tolerance [98, 99].

The second is the behavior-regulating path. It acts as a psychological buffer for tourists after a service failure [47]. High tolerance can reduce tourists’ retaliatory tendencies, such as giving low ratings and weakening the revisit intention [100], and mitigate the negative impact of justice differentiation by maintaining the customer-guide relationship [101]. Conversely, low tolerance strengthens the likelihood of negative word-of-mouth, enhancing the inhibitory effect of justice differentiation on the revisit intention [100]. The tolerance level directly determines the strength of the relationship between justice differentiation and revisit intention. Hence, we propose the hypothesis:

H5: Tourist tolerance moderates the relationship between tour-guide-tourist interactional justice differentiation and tourists’ revisit intention.

Methods

Data collection

This study employed a questionnaire survey to collect data. The questionnaire design was divided into two parts. The first part clarifies the research purpose and ensures data confidentiality, emphasizing that the collected information is solely for scientific research and not for commercial use. The second part includes items for each variable. To reduce respondent bias, the order of the items was randomly arranged.

Before the formal survey, a pre-test was conducted. Through a trial test on 50 tourists, the reliability and validity of the questionnaire were examined. The pre-test results showed that the Cronbach’ α coefficients of all variables were higher than 0.75, indicating a high level of internal consistency of the questionnaire. The factor loadings of all items were greater than 0.60, and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) of each variable was greater than 0.50, indicating good construct validity of the questionnaire. Additionally, the average time taken to complete the questionnaire in the pre-test was 6 min (SD = 1.6 min), which provided a basis for the time-length principle of excluding invalid samples in the formal survey.

This study employed offline data collection using a convenience sampling approach. Although this method may limit the generalizability of the findings due to its non-random nature and possible lack of full representativeness, it remained the most feasible option under the practical constraints of this research. Specifically, probability sampling would have required a well-defined sampling frame, randomized selection procedures, and substantial resource investment—conditions difficult to meet within the study’s timeframe. In contrast, convenience sampling allowed for efficient and timely data gathering. Moreover, the tourist population is highly dynamic and diverse, with its size and characteristics varying significantly across time, seasons, and locations, making it particularly challenging to construct a representative sampling frame. Within this context, convenience sampling offered a pragmatic solution that facilitated access to a varied and readily available sample of tourists, thereby striking a balance between methodological soundness and practical implementation.

The steps of the convenience sampling method are as follows: We selected 10 nationally recognized high-traffic attractions—such as the Forbidden City (Beijing), Mount Huangshan (Anhui), the Terracotta Army (Shaanxi), and Longmen Grottoes (Henan)—based on official tourism statistics indicating each receives over 5 million annual visits and holds national or UNESCO World Heritage status, ensuring both visitor volume and socio-cultural representativeness. Big data reports (e.g., from Ctrip and China Tourism Academy) confirm these sites consistently attract tourists from all 31 mainland provinces, with age distributions spanning adolescents (12–18), adults (19–45), and seniors (46+), covering diverse educational and occupational backgrounds. To capture temporal variation, each attraction was sampled during three daily periods: morning (9:00–12:00), afternoon (13:00–16:00), and evening (17:00–20:00), thereby including visitors with different schedules and further enhancing sample diversity.

The distribution and collection of questionnaires were conducted from October 17, 2024, to February 2, 2025. Throughout each designated time period, members of our research team were stationed at key locations within tourist attractions, including rest areas, entry and exit gates, and well-known viewing spots. Whenever an approachable tourist passed by, a trained team member would approach them, politely explain the purpose of the survey, and invite them to participate. After obtaining verbal agreement, we provided each participant with a hard copy of the informed consent form, a printed questionnaire, and a pen to facilitate completion. All team members had undergone rigorous training to ensure they communicated in an affable, professional, and respectful manner. They clearly explained that participation was entirely voluntary and that all responses would be kept strictly confidential. To enhance the response rate, we offered a small gift worth 10 yuan to every tourist who completed the questionnaire. This token of appreciation acknowledged their time and effort and encouraged more visitors to share their insights. Additionally, participants were asked to record the start time and end time directly on the paper questionnaire to allow for subsequent screening of response quality and duration.

The participants in this study consisted of adult tourists across various age groups; minors were excluded from the sample. A total of 600 questionnaires were administered, and 427 were returned, yielding a recovery rate of 71.2%. From the returned questionnaires, invalid responses were excluded based on the following criteria established in line with methodological literature [102–105]: (1) Incomplete filling: Questionnaires with one or more missing responses were discarded to prevent biased or incomplete statistical analysis. (2) Obvious logical contradictions: Responses containing irreconcilable inconsistencies—such as strongly agreeing with mutually exclusive statements—were removed, as these suggest inattentiveness or careless responding and threaten data validity. (3) Patterned responding: Answers with five or more identical consecutive choices were excluded to avoid “straight-lining,” a behavior indicative of low engagement or satisficing. (4) Extreme completion time: Questionnaires completed in less than 2 min or more than 10 min were omitted. This range was empirically derived from pre-test results, which indicated that meaningful participant engagement typically occurred within this interval. Excessively short times may reflect random or rushed responses, while overly long times could suggest distraction or loss of focus. After applying these criteria rigorously, 384 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis.

Among the 384 valid samples, the average age of the respondents was 35 years old (SD = 10.5 years). The demographic characteristics are as follows: 180 males (46.9%) and 204 females (53.1%); 120 people with a high-school education or below (31.3%), 210 with a university degree (54.7%), and 54 with a master’s degree or above (14.0%); 60 self-employed individuals (15.6%), 40 civil servants (10.4%), 70 technicians (18.2%), 140 corporate employees (36.5%), and 74 in other occupations (19.3%); 250 tourists from other provinces (65.1%) and 134 tourists from the same province (34.9%).

Measurement items

In this study, we initially employed the interactional justice scale developed by Colquitt [106] for scoring. This scale comprises four items, with a representative item being “The tour guide treated me in a polite manner”. Subsequently, referring to the research method of He et al. [107], after calculating the interactional justice level of each team member, we computed the standard deviation (SD) of interactional justice within the team to represent the differentiation of interactional justice. A larger standard deviation implies a higher degree of interactional justice differentiation.

The measurement of the quality of tourism experience referred to the four-item questionnaire scale developed by Xu & Zheng [108]. A representative item is “I was fully immersed in the experience.”

The measurement of tourist loyalty drew on the three-item scale developed by Rasoolimanesh et al. [109]. A representative item is “I would recommend this tourism site to someone who seeks my advice.”

Tourist tolerance was measured using four items adapted from Hartmann et al. [110] and Hamdy et al. [111]. A representative item is “I can quickly overcome negative emotions when encountering service inadequacies or unexpected inconveniences during travel.”

Revisit intention was measured with three items from Zhang et al. [112]. A representative item is “I intend to revisit this destination in the future.”

All the above variables were measured using the Likert 7-point scoring method (1 = Strongly disagree, 7 = Strongly agree).

Tools

In this study, data analysis was performed using SPSS 25 and Mplus 8.0. SPSS 25 was utilized for conducting descriptive analyses of the sample. Mplus 8.0 was employed to examine the complex relationships outlined in the research model, which include interactional justice differentiation, quality of tourism experience, tourist loyalty, tourist tolerance, and revisit intention. The structural equation modeling (SEM) approach, supported by Mplus 8.0, enables simultaneous testing of multiple relationships while accounting for measurement error. Furthermore, the bootstrapping method available in Mplus 8.0 was applied to evaluate the mediating effects of tourism experience quality and tourist loyalty, as well as the moderating effect of tourist tolerance—a method recognized for its robustness in handling non-normal data and limited sample sizes.

Sample common method bias test

During the questionnaire design phase, procedural and statistical controls were applied to minimize common method bias. Items were systematically ordered to avoid response patterns, and anonymity was ensured. Within the structural equation modeling framework, a latent common method factor was incorporated, leading to improved model fit: RMSEA decreased by 0.01, SRMR by 0.02, while CFI and NFI increased by 0.04 and 0.03, respectively. Additionally, Harman’s single-factor test [113] showed the first factor explained 36.15% of the variance, below the 50% threshold, confirming that common method bias was not a substantial issue in this study.

Discussion

This study, grounded in SDL, reveals a significant negative correlation between interactional justice differentiation and tourists’ revisit intention. While conventional tourism research has focused on isolated perceptual or evaluative factors such as perceived value, satisfaction, and destination image [18–25], and while prior justice studies emphasize the benefits of interactional justice itself [26–28], this research adopts an SDL lens to reframe justice differentiation as a critical disruptor of value co-creation.

​​From an equity theory perspective integrated within the SDL framework, justice differentiation violates the principle of equitable resource integration essential to co-creation [34]. At the individual level, “neglected tourists” sense injustice in the distribution of key co-creation resources, leading to relative deprivation that violates SDL’s principle of mutual value generation [34], thereby arousing anger or resentment that dampens individual revisit intention [114]. At the systemic level, such disparities suggest inconsistent adherence to SDL’s fundamental premises, undermining institutional trust and causing even “favored tourists” to reduce their revisit intentions due to anticipated future co-creation risks [115]. Additionally, through a two-factor theory lens within SDL’s value co-creation context, guide-tourist interaction acts as a hygiene factor. While just interactions merely meet basic co-creation requirements for “favored tourists” (resulting in “no dissatisfaction”), they fail to activate motivators that enhance value-in-use [116, 117]. In contrast, “neglected tourists” experience active value co-destruction: the lack of hygiene factors triggers rights deprivation and intense dissatisfaction [118, 119], directly suppressing their revisit intention. This asymmetry confirms that in co-creation contexts, the negative impact of hygiene factors outweighs the positive effects of motivators [120].

The validated model shows that tourism experience quality and tourist loyalty serve as sequential mediators, reflecting core SDL constructs. These mediating pathways embody SDL’s operant resources: differential treatment degrades tourists’ relational operant resources (FP4), reducing social-emotional value co-creation and the quality of the tourism experience [62–64]. Subsequently, the deteriorated experience quality weakens relationship-based loyalty, which is a key indicator of sustained resource integration capacity. Loyalty promotes revisit decisions through word-of-mouth reinforcement [83], consistent with SDL’s view that loyalty stems from successful iterative co-creation [71, 90, 91].

Furthermore, tourist tolerance functions as a key moderating resource consistent with SDL’s emphasis on operant resources and contextual variability. This moderation reveals SDL’s contextual adaptability: tourist tolerance serves as a boundary-spanning operant resource that regulates justice evaluations in co-creation. While existing studies have largely examined tolerance in confined settings such as hotels [48–50], this research expands its role through an SDL-guided framework to open, multi-actor service ecosystems. Highly tolerant tourists prioritize holistic value integration (reflecting SDL’s FP1) over transactional asymmetries [45], thereby attenuating the effect of justice differentiation on experience quality [45, 100]. Conversely, low-tolerance tourists exacerbate value co-destruction when justice thresholds are exceeded [121]. This confirms tolerance’s role in sustaining co-creation viability by mediating actors’ behavioral exchanges (as per Social Exchange Theory [12, 122]) within SDL’s service ecosystems [96, 100].

Theoretical contributions

On one hand, this study advances the theoretical understanding of justice dynamics by expanding the antecedents of tourists’ revisit intention and contextualizing interactional justice differentiation within the Service-Dominant Logic (SDL) framework. Existing literature predominantly examines individual-level justice perceptions [17] or isolated factors such as perceived value and satisfaction that affect revisit intention [18, 22], overlooking the systemic implications of inevitable differential treatment in tour-guide services. By empirically revealing a significant negative correlation between interactional justice differentiation and the collective revisit intention of tourist groups, this research challenges the conventional assumption that justice differentiation effects offset at the group level. It thereby demonstrates how such differentiation disrupts value co-creation under SDL, systematically reducing group loyalty through diminished collective value perceptions [43]. Furthermore, the study extends interactional justice theory from organizational contexts to open tourism service systems through an SDL lens, uncovering the mechanism through which justice differentiation affects consumption behavior when guides act as value co-creation nodes, thereby refining the boundary conditions of justice theory in dynamic service ecosystems.

On the other hand, this study establishes an SDL-informed moderated mediation framework to deconstruct the mechanism linking justice differentiation to revisit intention. The dual-mediation path through tourism experience quality and loyalty reflects SDL’s emphasis on value realization and relational outcomes. Specifically, differentiation drives double-value attenuation by devaluing individual experience quality and eroding emotional commitment to loyalty [123]. Importantly, introducing tourist tolerance as a moderator, consistent with SDL’s focus on operant resources, transcends hotel-centric models [48–50] and reveals its contextual buffering role in open tourism environments. High-tolerance tourists mitigate the negative impact of justice differentiation on experience quality through enhanced focus on holistic value co-creation [45], thereby reducing the decrease in revisit intention [44]. This integrative model advances dynamic service justice theory while offering a mechanistic explanation for SDL’s application in differentiated service contexts.

Practical implications

This study offers several practical implications for tourism management. First, tourism enterprises should prioritize interactional justice between guides and tourists by establishing a fair service distribution mechanism and assigning experienced guides to specific groups. They should strengthen guide training on service equality and implement monitoring systems, including tourist feedback and on-site inspections, to correct unjust behaviors promptly. Second, tour guides should cultivate impartial service awareness, treat all tourists equally, and encourage participation from less-active visitors. They need to enhance communication and conflict-resolution skills to mediate disputes fairly and build trust through sincere engagement, thereby improving tourist satisfaction and experience quality. Third, tourism managers should optimize product design and route planning by adding personalized options to cater to diverse tourist preferences. Maintaining high-quality infrastructure and organizing culturally rich activities are also essential to enrich the tourist experience and ensure equal participation for all. Fourth, marketing strategies should highlight the enterprise’s commitment to service justice, showcase guide training systems, and develop tailored promotion plans for experience-sensitive tourist groups. Implementing word-of-mouth marketing with incentives for sharing positive experiences can enhance brand image and tourist loyalty. Fifth, industry regulators should develop clear service justice standards and behavioral guidelines for guides, establish supervision and penalty mechanisms, and organize industry-wide exchanges to collectively improve service quality and justice across the sector.

Limitations and future research directions

This study is based on a cross-sectional design, which inherently has limitations in inferring causal relationships between variables. To address the deficiencies of cross-sectional research, future studies can adopt a longitudinal research design. This approach will enable a more accurate determination of the causal relationships and dynamic changes among variables.

Conclusions

This study was designed to address three core research questions regarding the correlation, direction, and underlying mechanism between interactional justice differentiation in tour-guide–tourist interactions and tourists’ revisit intention. The findings demonstrate a significant negative correlation between these variables, revealing that disparities in interpersonal treatment substantially reduce tourists’ intention to revisit. Furthermore, the research identifies a chain-mediating pathway in which the quality of the tourism experience and tourist loyalty sequentially transmit the influence of justice differentiation, illustrating both experiential and relational mechanisms that drive the decline in revisit intention. Additionally, tourist tolerance serves as a critical moderator that attenuates the negative effect of justice differentiation on experience quality. Collectively, these results not only confirm the detrimental impact of interactional injustice on revisit behavior but also deepen the theoretical application of justice frameworks in tourism through a SDL perspective, which elucidates the mediating and moderating mechanisms underlying this relationship.
