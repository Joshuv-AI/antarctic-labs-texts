Comorbid Abetalipoproteinemia and Neurofibromatosis Type 1: Potential Clinical Implications of an Ultrarare Co-occurrence
Authors: Yaqqira H M Womack, Misha B Asif, Fadi E Shamoun, Linnea M Baudhuin, Mayowa A Osundiji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbetalipoproteinemia (ABL) affects fewer than 1 per 1 million people, whereas neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) occurs in approximately 1 in 3000 to 4000 individuals. We describe the clinical findings in a 38-year-old woman with both disorders. ABL was diagnosed in the neonatal period, followed by NF1 at age 7 years based on multiple café-au-lait spots, intertriginous freckling, and Lisch nodules. Genetic testing revealed a homozygous pathogenic MTTP frameshift variant [c.215del (p.Pro72Leufs*8)] and a heterozygous pathogenic NF1 splice variant [c.205–2A>C]. To our knowledge, this co-occurrence has not been previously reported. The estimated likelihood of the co-occurrence is approximately 1 in 3 billion individuals.Keywords: Diagnostic medicine, Neurofibromatosis type 1, Lipids, Genetic testing, Pathogenesis, Fatty acids, Very low–density lipoprotein, Phenotypes, Clinical genetics, Proteins, Abetalipoproteinemia

Background

Abetalipoproteinemia (ABL) is an ultrarare (<1 per 1 million) autosomal-recessive disorder (1). It is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the MTP gene on chromosome 4, which encodes microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP), required for apolipoprotein B (ApoB) lipoprotein assembly and neutral lipid transport. Microsomal triglyceride transfer protein deficiency prevents chylomicron and very low–density lipoprotein (VLDL) formation (1, 2).

Abetalipoproteinemia causes growth failure, steatorrhea, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, hepatic abnormalities, acanthocytosis, anemia, neuropathy, and progressive retinal degeneration (1). Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1; OMIM 162 200; von Recklinghausen disease) is an autosomal-dominant condition caused by pathogenic variants in the tumor-suppressor gene NF1 on chromosome 17 (3). The protein product of the NF1 gene, called neurofibromin, regulates Ras signaling and cell growth (3, 4). NF1 features include pigmentary findings (café-au-lait macules, axillary/inguinal freckling), tumors (neurofibromas, plexiform neurofibromas, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors [MPNSTs]), ophthalmologic findings (Lisch nodules, optic pathway gliomas), skeletal abnormalities, developmental issues, and vascular/neurologic complications. NF1 affects approximately 1 in 3000 individuals worldwide (3).

We report a patient with both ABL and NF1 (estimated co-occurrence ~1 in 3 billion). To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of coexistence of ABL and NF1, making this case a valuable contribution to the literature on rare genetic comorbid conditions involving inherited disorders of lipid metabolism.

Case Report

We evaluated a 38-year-old nondysmorphic woman of Pacific Islander descent diagnosed with ABL in infancy after absent ApoB and acanthocytosis were found during work-up for failure to thrive and diarrhea. She received lipid infusions for several years but continued to have vitamin deficiencies and microcytic anemia. Neurofibromatosis type 1 was diagnosed when she was around 7 years old based on multiple café-au-lait macules, Lisch nodules, and axillary freckling. She previously had neurofibromas excised from the right breast and abdomen, a neurofibroma removed near the nipple during pregnancy, and a history of glomus tumor. She also had a Chiari I malformation. Family history included breast, nasopharyngeal, and colon cancer. There was no known family history of ABL or NF1 or consanguinity (Figure 1). On examination, her height was 149 cm, her BMI was 24.6 kg/m2, and her occipitofrontal circumference was 55.3 cm.

Her total cholesterol level was 58 mg/dL (reference interval [RI] is <200 mg/dL), her HDL cholesterol level was 58 mg/dL (RI is ≥50 mg/dL), and her triglyceride level was <10 mg/dL (RI is <150 mg/dL). Consistently, fat-soluble vitamin analysis demonstrated significant deficiencies, including α-tocopherol (vitamin E) level <0.5 mg/L (RI is 5.5–17.0 mg/L), vitamin A level of 6.7 μg/dL (RI is 32.5–78.0 μg/dL), vitamin K1 level of <0.03 ng/mL (RI is 0.10–2.20 ng/mL), and total 25-hydroxy vitamin D2 and D3 level of 13 ng/mL (RI >50 ng/mL). Furthermore, essential fatty acid profiles indicated that most of her fatty acid levels were either on the low end of normal or deficient, with the exceptions being mead acid, nervonic acid, and the triene-to-tetraene ratio, which were elevated (Table 1). A validated custom capture–based gene panel test for hereditary tumor predisposition syndromes and dyslipidemia was subsequently pursued (Supplement) using genomic DNA via next-generation sequencing in a laboratory certified under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments. All MTTP coding exons and flanking intronic regions were sequenced to a minimum depth of 50×. The assay was validated to detect single nucleotide variants, small indels (<15 bp), and exon-level deletions/duplications (copy number variants [CNVs]) with >99% analytic sensitivity and specificity.

The gene panel test revealed a homozygous pathogenic MTTP frameshift variant [transcript NM_000253.3 (GRCh37): c.215del (p.Pro72Leufs*8)], and a heterozygous pathogenic NF1 splice variant [transcript NM_000267.3 (GRCh37): c.205–2A>C] consistent with a concurrent diagnosis of ABL and NF1. CNV analysis of the MTTP gene did not identify any exon-level deletions or duplications, providing no evidence for a large deletion or duplication affecting the second allele. Parental samples were unavailable for segregation analysis.

Abdominal ultrasound for ABL-associated abnormalities showed normal liver, spleen, gallbladder, pancreas, aorta, inferior vena cava, extrahepatic duct, and kidneys.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported coexistence of ABL and NF1. This ultrarare co-occurrence underscores the value of comprehensive genetic evaluation in complex phenotypes. Dual molecular diagnoses have been reported in NF1 (4), but this appears to be the first such report in ABL. While ABL is pan-ethnic, this appears to be the first report in an individual of Pacific Islander ancestry. The MTTP c.215del (p.Pro72Leufs*8) variant was previously reported in ABL in a child of consanguineous parents (2). However, ethnicity was not specified; thus, shared ancestry is unclear. Although both parents in this case are Pacific Islander and the variant is extremely rare in population databases, current data cannot confirm a founder effect. In gnomAD, this variant is reported in 1 individual whose genetic ancestry is categorized as “remaining” (i.e., not assigned to the major continental ancestry groups), which does not establish population-specific enrichment. Parental testing was unavailable, limiting segregation/homozygosity confirmation. Further population-based studies or identification of additional affected individuals will be required to clarify this possibility.

Even though capture-based next-generation sequencing may miss some structural variants, the laboratory’s validated CNV analysis found no exon-level deletions/duplications in MTTP, making a deletion in trans unlikely (though not fully excluded). Clinically, the patient’s presentation fits classic ABL (5): markedly low plasma lipids, fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, acanthocytosis, and microcytic anemia (Table 2). Persistent low levels of vitamins A, D, E, and K despite supplementation may reflect impaired chylomicron/ very low–density lipoprotein assembly and possible MTTP-related dysbiosis (6). Elevated mead acid and nervonic acid levels, along with elevated triene-to-tetraene ratio (Table 1), support compensatory lipid changes from essential fatty acid deficiency. The mead acid level rises with depletion (7), whereas the nervonic acid level is a key structural component of myelin (8). Elevated nervonic acid levels in ABL have been rarely reported (9). The patient’s NF1 phenotype included multiple café-au-lait macules, axillary freckling, and cutaneous neurofibromas but no plexiform or spinal neurofibromas. The finding of Chiari malformation type I is consistent with previously recognized NF1-associated congenital anomalies (10). Although the patient had no known history of neoplasms, lifelong surveillance for both NF1 (3, 4) and ABL (1, 5) was recommended.

Although the coexistence of ABL and NF1 appears coincidental, both conditions influence lipid signaling and neuronal function. Abetalipoproteinemia disrupts myelination (7, 8) and axonal integrity, whereas NF1 alters Ras-mediated pathways (3) that depend partly on lipid signaling (4). Some neurologic or ophthalmologic findings may therefore overlap or be compounded (Table 2). This case expands the clinical literature on dual genetic diagnoses and emphasizes the need for integrated, multidisciplinary management in adults with complex genetic phenotypes. Even though both clinical diagnoses were established more than 3 decades before genetic testing, molecular confirmation provides meaningful added value. The genetic diagnosis refines recurrence risk assessment and enables accurate genetic counseling for family members, including cascade testing. It also confirms the molecular cause underlying the patient’s phenotype, supports alignment with evolving genotype-informed management considerations, and facilitates eligibility for clinical trials and longitudinal care planning. Accordingly, the genetic findings remain clinically impactful despite the longstanding nature of the comorbid diagnoses.

Digenic and multilocus disease presentations are increasingly recognized with the expanded use of comprehensive genomic testing (4, 11–13). Recognition of such co-occurrences is important, as multiple molecular diagnoses may contribute additively or independently to the clinical presentation and have implications for interpretation, genetic counseling, and long-term management. Future studies will be needed to better define the frequency and clinical impact of genetic comorbid conditions in the general population.
