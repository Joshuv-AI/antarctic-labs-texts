The mechanical loading environment associated with eccentric exercise is one of the key stimuli to trigger sarcomerogenesis: It’s a stretch to say eccentric exercise does not promote serial sarcomerogenesis
Authors: Geoffrey A Power, Martino V Franchi, Avery Hinks
Published: 2025

The addition of sarcomeres in series (sarcomerogenesis) in skeletal muscle has increasingly fascinated exercise scientists in recent years due to its potential to positively impact performance.1,2 In their new review article, Triggering sarcomerogenesis: Examining key stimuli and the role attributed to eccentric training—Historical, systematic, and meta-analytic review, Blazevich et al.3 provide a commendable overview of the history behind this area of research from the 1600s to present. They also provided a short meta-analysis of eccentric-biased exercise in animal models (which, due to methodological limitations, comprise most of this field to date) to bolster their overarching theme that eccentric exercise does not trigger sarcomerogenesis. They conclude explicitly that “Current evidence does not support eccentric training as an effective stimulus for sarcomerogenesis”, with “a lack of effect of eccentric resistance training in animal models”. Over the past 6 years, our research group has been investigating eccentric training-induced sarcomerogenesis in animal models, and based on experimental data from studies designed to optimize the serial sarcomerogenic response,4, 5, 6, 7, 8 we arrive at the opposite conclusion. Here we offer important context related to various interventions. We hope to demonstrate that the above-stated conclusion, while well-intentioned, forgoes several important considerations and can be misleading.

In the review by Blazevich et al.,3 Fig. 7A includes data from all eccentric training studies in animals (downhill running and dynamometry with electrical muscle stimulation for all muscles investigated) and shows a significant effect (effect size (ES) = 0.51, p < 0.001), going against their stated conclusion regarding eccentric loading and sarcomerogenesis. It is only when the table/analysis is separated into different interventions and/or muscles that they observed no effects (ES = 0.19, p = 0.449 for electrical stimulation; ES = 0.27, p = 0.240 for downhill running in the vastus lateralis + electrical stimulation in all muscles; Fig. 7D and 7E). However, we are of the opinion that there are misguided inclusions in these latter analyses, and even in the initial combined analysis, that we list below, which influenced the outcome and interpretation of the available literature.

Context is key

When investigating a training intervention’s effect on a given outcome measure, one should ideally focus on interventions that were optimized to alter that outcome measure. One would not, for example, include data from an acute bout of exercise in a meta-analysis on adaptations to chronic training interventions, as the acute bout would not sufficiently induce the adaptations being investigated. The 2005 study by Butterfield et al.9 concluded that 10 days of downhill exercise was necessary to induce an increase in serial sarcomere number (SSN), while 5 days was insufficient – we also showed recently that 1 bout of eccentric exercise was insufficient to increase SSN.10 However, both the 10-day and 5-day data were included in Blazevich et al.’s meta-analysis.3 Similarly, the 2006 study by Butterfield and Herzog11 concluded that electrically stimulated eccentric training with pre-activation before lengthening was optimal to induce sarcomerogenesis because it provides sufficient time to develop force, while activation at the onset of lengthening did not. They also found that performing an eccentric contraction to a long muscle length was more optimal to induce sarcomerogenesis than to a short muscle length.11 Yet, all 3 of these intervention types (pre-activation to a long muscle length, pre-activation to a short muscle length, and onset of activation to a short muscle length)11 were included in the meta-analysis—thereby skewing the results. In another example, our own study of maximal electrically stimulated eccentric training in the plantar flexors6 found that young rats added SSN as expected, whereas old rats underwent dysfunctional remodeling, likely due to accumulated damage – not adapting SSN and becoming much weaker, stiffer, and less powerful. Surprisingly, data from those dysfunctional old rats were included in the meta-analysis as well. In our follow-up study,7 we found that sarcomerogenesis in old rat plantar flexors could instead be optimized with a submaximal eccentric training stimulus, inducing 11% and 8% increases in soleus and medial gastrocnemius SSN, respectively—these data were not included in the meta-analysis.

What are individual muscles acting about a common joint doing? Only downhill running data from the rat vastus lateralis was included in the final meta-analysis (Fig. 7E).3 The authors’ support this decision by pointing out that sonomicrometry data has only demonstrated eccentric fascicle behavior for the vastus lateralis during downhill running.12 The vastus intermedius (VI), conversely, has been shown to more consistently increase SSN with downhill running,9,13 however, they did not include the VI simply off the basis that there is no published sonomicrometry data for it. Furthermore, while no sonomicrometry data during downhill exercise has been collected for the rat soleus, others have shown it undergoes eccentric contractions during its regular gait cycle.14,15 We showed that adding weighted vests to rats during downhill running, presumably increasing eccentric loading (or greater forces during active muscle lengthening) produced a greater increase in soleus SSN (+8%)5 than unweighted downhill running (non-significant +3%).4 In effect, greater forces applied to muscles during downhill running better optimized sarcomerogenesis in the soleus. Despite this clear data on the rat soleus in downhill running, and the large magnitudes of sarcomerogenesis observed for the VI, only the vastus lateralis was included in the final meta-analysis.

Even if the soleus and VI downhill running data had been included in the final meta-analysis, downhill running and electrically stimulated eccentric training are 2 completely different training types, aligning with the definitions of aerobic and resistance exercise, respectively.16 The diverging stimuli and signaling pathways between these training types are well-studied.16 We would thereby warrant caution when interpreting a conclusion that “current evidence does not support eccentric training as an effective stimulus for sarcomerogenesis in animal models” based primarily on downhill running studies in one muscle.

Using the same statistical approach as the Blazevich et al.,3 but modifying the meta-analysis to include eccentric exercise interventions designed to optimize sarcomerogenesis (e.g., early time points removed, activation protocols which drive SSN addition), and along with our newly added study,7 a clearer and more compelling pattern emerges (Fig. 1). For downhill exercise studies (blue lines) there is a moderate-to-large effect (ES = 0.76; p < 0.001) of eccentric-biased exercise on SSN adaptation. For studies using electrically evoked contractions (orange lines) there is a large effect (ES = 1.05; p = 0.03) of eccentric exercise on SSN adaptation. Taken together, eccentric exercise is one (not the only) way of getting the necessary mechanotransduction stimulus to promote SSN addition.

Blazevich et al.3 state that a stimulus of active force production in a lengthened muscle is what drives the addition of sarcomeres in series, and this may not be unique to only eccentric exercise—and we fully agree! Eccentric exercise is just one type of intervention that can induce this stimulus, in addition to isometric training in a lengthened position (as potently suggested by Uçar et al.17), and perhaps even concentric training in a lengthened position. To make the biggest impact going forward, we should focus on the breadth of training interventions that can leverage this stimulus to induce sarcomerogenesis and improve performance. Our main point to the readership, coaches, practitioners, and authors’ is: It is a bit of a stretch to state that eccentric exercise does not promote serial sarcomerogenesis.
