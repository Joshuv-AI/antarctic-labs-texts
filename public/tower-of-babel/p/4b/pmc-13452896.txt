Biochemical pregnancy outcomes following dual versus human chorionic gonadotropin-only trigger in gonadotropin-stimulated intrauterine insemination cycles for unexplained infertility: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mehtap Akin Efe, Ozlem Dural, Hevra Ekin Ulusoy, Atacem Mert Aytekin, Cemil Akgül
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND: Unexplained infertility remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. Dual trigger, combining gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), has been proposed to more closely mimic the physiological mid-cycle gonadotropin surge than hCG alone in gonadotropin-stimulated intrauterine insemination (IUI) cycles.OBJECTIVES: To evaluate whether dual trigger improves biochemical pregnancy outcomes compared with hCG-only trigger in gonadotropin-stimulated IUI cyclesDESIGN: Retrospective cohort studySETTINGS: Single-center, tertiary referral centerPATIENTS AND METHODS: Between April 2021 and September 2022, 96 patients underwent IUI after ovarian stimulation with gonadotropins (53 hCG-only, 43 dual trigger). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics included female age (29.5±2.5 vs. 27.2±3.5 years), male age (33.3±4.0 vs. 31.6±3.5 years), body mass index (BMI) (24.0 vs. 25.0 kg/m2), gonadotropin dose (median 675 IU), treatment duration (median 9 days), dominant follicle size (18 mm vs. 17 mm), and endometrial thickness (9 mm vs. 8 mm).MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Biochemical pregnancy, defined as serum β-hCG ≥5 mIU/mL.SAMPLE SIZE: 96 patients (53 hCG-only, 43 dual trigger)RESULTS: Biochemical pregnancy occurred in 18.6% of the dual trigger group and 11.3% of the hCG-only group (P=.315). The odds of biochemical pregnancy were higher with dual trigger but not statistically significant (OR=1.8, 95% CI: 0.6–5.6). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant association between infertility duration and biochemical pregnancy (P=.04), with 76.9% of conceptions occurring in women with 2–3 years of infertility.CONCLUSIONS: Dual trigger did not significantly improve biochemical pregnancy rates compared with hCG-only trigger (OR=1.8, 95% CI: 0.6–5.6). Infertility duration was the only significant predictor of treatment success. Larger multi-center trials are required due to the current study's limited power.LIMITATIONS: Retrospective design, single-center setting, and limited sample size may restrict generalizability.

INTRODUCTION

Unexplained infertility is defined as the absence of an identifiable cause during the initial evaluation of couples who fail to conceive after at least one year of unprotected intercourse.1 It accounts for approximately 20–30% of infertility cases.2 This condition represents a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, underscoring the importance of optimizing treatment strategies. Ovulation induction followed by intrauterine insemination (IUI) is widely used as a first-line approach in this population.

In controlled ovulation induction cycles, final oocyte maturation is typically achieved using recombinant hCG (r-hCG), which mimics the luteinizing hormone (LH) surge by binding to LH receptors. However, unlike the natural mid-cycle surge, hCG does not induce a follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) surge, which plays an essential role in upregulating LH receptors on granulosa cells, supporting luteinization, and promoting oocyte meiotic progression and cytoplasmic maturation.3

Alternatively, gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists can be used alone to induce endogenous LH and FSH surges, thereby more closely mimicking the natural mid-cycle hormonal environment. Previous studies in assisted reproductive technology cycles have compared GnRH agonist and hCG triggers for oocyte maturation, reporting comparable.4 However, the LH surge induced by GnRH agonists is short-lived, which may compromise luteal phase support.

To further optimize outcomes, the “dual trigger” approach—combining a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist with hCG—has been developed. In in vitro fertilization (IVF) settings, the dual trigger has been associated with improvements in oocyte maturity, fertilization rates, and pregnancy outcomes.5 The mid-cycle FSH surge induced by GnRH agonists has been proposed to enhance oocyte competence and endometrial receptivity.6,7

Several meta-analyses and randomized trials in IVF populations have demonstrated improved reproductive outcomes with dual trigger protocols.8–10 This has prompted a growing interest in evaluating whether similar benefits might be seen in non-IVF settings, such as IUI cycles. Preliminary studies investigating dual trigger in IUI cycles have yielded mixed results: Halim et al reported a significantly higher clinical pregnancy rate in the dual trigger group,11 while other studies in specific populations, such as patients with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), have shown non-significant trends favoring dual trigger.12,13

Despite the increasing use of dual trigger protocols in clinical practice, robust evidence regarding their effectiveness in IUI cycles, particularly in unexplained infertility, remains limited. This study aims to evaluate whether dual trigger improves biochemical pregnancy outcomes compared with hCG-only trigger in gonadotropin-stimulated IUI cycles for unexplained infertility. The findings may provide clinically relevant insights for reproductive medicine practice and individualized treatment strategies in this diagnostically challenging group.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study population and design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary center, Istanbul University, Istanbul Faculty of Medicine, Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Division of Reproductive Endocrinology and Infertility. The study retrospectively reviewed patients who underwent intrauterine insemination (IUI) following ovulation induction with gonadotropins due to unexplained infertility between April 2021 and September 2022. During this period, hCG alone and dual trigger protocols were applied at different time frames as part of routine clinical practice, without randomization. Patients were categorized into two groups: the hCG-only trigger group and the dual trigger group (hCG plus gonadotropin-releasing hormone [GnRH] agonist). To minimize bias, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Outcomes were based on objective laboratory data collected retrospectively.

The study protocol was approved by the Istanbul University, Istanbul Faculty of Medicine, Clinical Research Ethics Committee, Ref. No: 2022/206370. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Unexplained infertility was defined according to the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) evidence-based guideline, 2023.14 Couples were recruited; both female and male partners were evaluated. Eligible participants were women aged 18–35 years with a body mass index (BMI) <35 kg/m2, diagnosed with primary infertility, without uterine anomalies, and with at least one patent fallopian tube confirmed by hysterosalpingography (HSG). Exclusion criteria included endocrine disorders such as hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, diabetes mellitus, or prolactinoma; known medical or immunological disorders that could affect reproductive outcomes, including autoimmune diseases; and incomplete medical records. Patients with male infertility were excluded from the study. Each patient was included only once, and the number of cycles corresponded to the number of patients in each group. In total, 53 gonadotropin-stimulated IUI cycles were analyzed in the hCG-only trigger group and 43 cycles in the dual trigger group.

Evaluation of infertility

All patients underwent standard infertility evaluations, including semen analysis after 3–7 days of sexual abstinence, endocrine assessment between menstrual cycle days 2 and 4, pelvic ultrasonography to exclude endometriomas, and hysterosalpingography (HSG) during the follicular phase. Male factor infertility was defined as abnormal sperm parameters according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, including reduced semen volume, sperm concentration, or motility. Uterine factor infertility was defined as structural abnormalities detected by imaging or hysteroscopy, including fibroids distorting the uterine cavity, intrauterine adhesions, or congenital anomalies, and such patients were excluded. Tubal factor infertility was defined as bilateral tubal occlusion on HSG and led to exclusion from the study. Only patients meeting the criteria for unexplained infertility were included, ensuring that no identifiable causes such as diminished ovarian reserve, male factor, uterine factor, or tubal pathology contributed to infertility (Figure 1).

Ovulation induction protocol

Ovulation induction with gonadotropins (GONAL-f®, Merck Serono Biopharma, Darmstadt, Germany; MENOGON®, Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Saint-Prex, Switzerland) was initiated on day 2 or 3 of the menstrual cycle. The starting dose (37.5-150 IU) was determined according to the antral follicle count on baseline ultrasonography and the number of follicles developed in any previous ovulation induction cycles. Sonographic monitoring commenced on day 6 of treatment, with follow-up frequency determined by follicle size. The trigger criterion was the presence of one or two follicles ≥16 mm in diameter. If three or more follicles exceeded this size, the cycle was canceled to reduce the risk of multiple pregnancy, and couples were advised to abstain from intercourse.

For final oocyte maturation, 250 μg (6500 IU) recombinant hCG (OVITRELLE®, Merck Serono Biopharma, Darmstadt, Germany) was administered in the hCG-only trigger group. In contrast, the dual trigger group received both r-hCG and triptorelin acetate 0.1 mg (Decapeptyl® and Gonapeptyl®, both by Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Saint-Prex, Switzerland). Insemination was performed 34-36 hours after triggering in all cycles.

Sperm preparation

A 1 mL aliquot of 90% gradient medium (SpermGrad®, Vitrolife Sweden AB, Gothenburg, Sweden) was pipetted into a conical tube, followed by 1 mL of 45% gradient medium, which was layered gently on top. The entire liquefied semen sample was measured, mixed, and carefully layered over the gradient medium. The sample was centrifuged at 300 g for 15 minutes. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and 1 mL of washing medium (SpermRinse®, Vitrolife Sweden AB) was added to the pellet and resuspended. The suspension was centrifuged again at 300 g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in washing medium to a final volume of 0.6 mL. Sperm concentration and motility were assessed, and the prepared sample was maintained under a laminar flow hood until use.

Intrauterine insemination

Patients were instructed to arrive with a full bladder. After positioning in the lithotomy position on a gynecological examination table, the cervix was visualized with a speculum and washed with saline. The prepared sperm sample was slowly injected into the uterine cavity using an insemination catheter. Patients remained in the supine position for 10-15 minutes before resuming normal activities. They were advised to have intercourse every other day for one week following insemination. All patients received 200 mg of micronized vaginal progesterone (Progestan®, Koçak Farma, Istanbul, Türkiye) for luteal phase support.

Biochemical pregnancy diagnosis

The primary outcome was biochemical pregnancy, defined as serum β-hCG ≥5 mIU/mL, consistent with established definitions in reproductive medicine (e.g., Zegers-Hochschild et al, 2017; International Committee for Monitoring Assisted Reproductive Technology [ICMART] glossary).1

Statistics

Sample size estimation was based on the study by Lin et al (Fertil Steril, 2013),15 which reported a clinically meaningful difference of 20% in pregnancy rates between groups. Using a one-sided test with α=0.05 and β=0.20 (power=80%), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 90 patients in total (45 per group). This power analysis was conducted prior to data collection. A post hoc power analysis indicated that the statistical power of the study was 17% at a 5% type I error rate.

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for each continuous parameter. Variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (25th–75th percentiles). Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage). For within-group comparisons over time, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi-square test when the expected frequency assumption was met (i.e., all expected cell counts ≥5). In cases where this assumption was violated, Fisher's exact test was used. A significance level of α=0.05 was applied for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics were comparable between the hCG-only and dual trigger groups, with no statistically significant differences in infertility duration, BMI, basal FSH, basal LH, basal E2, gonadotropin dose and duration, follicle sizes, hormone levels on the day of trigger, endometrial thickness, smoking status, or semen parameters (Tables 1–3).

The mean age of females was 29.5 (2.5) years in the hCG-only group and 27.3 (3.5) years in the dual trigger group (Table 1). The corresponding mean male ages were 33.29 (4.0) and 31.6 (3.5) years, respectively (Table 3). Biochemical pregnancy rates were 18.6% (8/43) in the dual trigger group and 11.3% (6/53) in the hCG-only group. This corresponds to an OR of 1.8 and an absolute increase of 7.3%, highlighting the magnitude of the observed difference even though it did not reach statistical significance.

Subgroup analysis revealed a significantly higher biochemical pregnancy rate among women with an infertility duration of 2–3 years compared with those with >3 years (P=.04), with 76.9% of conceptions occurring in the 2–3 year subgroup (Table 4). However, infertility duration distribution between the two trigger groups was similar (P=.25) (Table 5). The average infertility duration was 3.00 years (range 1.00–13.00) in the hCG-only group and 4.00 years (range 2.00–8.00) in the dual trigger group, with no significant difference (P=.08) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective study of gonadotropin-stimulated IUI cycles for unexplained infertility, dual trigger yielded a higher biochemical pregnancy rate than hCG alone (18.6% vs. 11.3%), but the difference was not statistically significant. Infertility duration was the only significant predictor, with the highest success observed in women with 2–3 years of infertility.

In unexplained infertility, treatment typically follows a stepwise approach, beginning with oral ovulation induction and progressing to gonadotropin-stimulated IUI before considering IVF.16 However, evidence suggests that infertility duration should be factored into treatment planning, particularly in women with prolonged infertility.17 The Fast Track and Standard Treatment Trial (FASTT) trial demonstrated that bypassing extended IUI in favor of earlier IVF improved time- and cost-efficiency,18 and more recent cost-effectiveness analyses have supported early transition to IVF in selected patients.19 Our subgroup analysis reinforces these findings, underscoring that shorter infertility duration is associated with higher pregnancy rates and may represent the optimal window for IUI success.

Physiologically, an endogenous FSH surge during the natural cycle plays a key role in the resumption of oocyte meiosis and promotes oocyte maturation, as demonstrated in IVF studies.6,20,21 In IVF settings, dual trigger has been associated with improved live birth rates,22,23 implantation rates,24 ongoing pregnancy rates,25 and lower miscarriage and cycle cancellation rates.9,10,24 However, its role in IUI cycles remains less clear. Thaker et al reported no difference in pregnancy rates between the dual trigger protocol and hCG-only trigger protocol.26 In addition, Li et al compared hCG alone, GnRH-a alone, and dual trigger protocol and concluded that there were no significant differences in pregnancy outcomes among the three methods.12 In contrast, Wang et al found that in the dual trigger group, live birth, clinical pregnancy, and β-hCG positive rates were all higher than in the hCG-only group (20.18% vs. 18.98%, 25.69% vs. 23.39%, and 28.44% vs. 25.08%, respectively).13

Additionally, Halim et al demonstrated that dual trigger significantly improved pregnancy outcomes in intrauterine insemination (P<.001) and increased the clinical pregnancy rate 2.52-fold.11 Conversely, another study reported that the miscarriage rate was significantly higher in the dual trigger group compared with the hCG group (P=.045).27 These conflicting findings underscore the need for further research in the setting of IUI.

Our study contributes to the limited body of evidence by evaluating the dual trigger in a well-defined cohort of patients with unexplained infertility who underwent gonadotropin-stimulated IUI. The relatively young mean female age (<30 years) in both groups may have contributed to the relatively high pregnancy rates, as fertility decline is minimal before age 32.28,29 Although male age differed slightly between groups, current evidence indicates that male age has only a limited effect on fertility outcomes in men under the age of 40.30–32 To further address potential confounding by age, we performed a binary logistic regression analysis with female and male age included as predictors of pregnancy outcome. Neither age variable was significantly associated with β-hCG positivity, suggesting that the observed age differences between groups did not affect pregnancy outcomes in this cohort.

A brief explanation for why dual trigger may not demonstrate a clear advantage in IUI cycles compared with IVF cycles relates to differences in cycle characteristics. In IVF, dual trigger is often used to maximize oocyte yield and maturity, where the mid-cycle FSH surge induced by GnRH agonists may enhance competence across a larger cohort of oocytes.3 In contrast, IUI cycles typically involve fewer follicles and lower gonadotropin stimulation, which may limit the incremental benefit of dual trigger. Moreover, the clinical endpoints differ: IVF studies often assess oocyte maturity and fertilization rates, whereas IUI outcomes are restricted to pregnancy rates, potentially obscuring subtle biological advantages of dual trigger. These differences in follicular dynamics and outcome measures may explain why dual trigger did not confer a significant advantage in our IUI population.

Practical implications of our findings suggest that dual trigger protocols may be considered in selected patients to optimize biochemical pregnancy outcomes, although the limited statistical power of the present study warrants cautious interpretation. The significant association between shorter infertility duration and biochemical pregnancy success underscores the importance of individualized treatment planning in unexplained infertility.

Strengths of the present study include the homogeneous patient population restricted to unexplained infertility, a subgroup that remains relatively underrepresented in dual trigger research, the use of a standardized stimulation protocol, and the clinical relevance of evaluating dual-trigger protocols in routine practice. In addition, the integration of infertility duration into the analysis may provide valuable insights for patient selection and inform the design of future trials.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design, a modest sample size—partly due to reduced patient volume during the COVID-19 pandemic—and the lack of long-term outcomes, such as live birth rates, due to incomplete follow-up. Although the age difference between groups reached statistical significance, logistic regression analysis indicated that it did not introduce meaningful bias in pregnancy outcomes; nevertheless, we acknowledge this as a limitation, and future prospective studies with strict age-matching are warranted to validate these findings. In addition, a post-hoc power analysis indicated that the statistical power of the study was 17% at a 5% type I error rate, confirming that the study was underpowered to detect small differences between groups. Furthermore, protocol allocation was non-randomized and based on institutional practice during different time periods, which may have introduce time-related confounding inherent to retrospective studies.

Future research should focus on larger, prospective, age-matched cohorts with long-term follow-up, including live birth rates, to validate and extend these results. Well-designed studies are also needed to identify patient subgroups who may benefit most from dual trigger protocols in non-IVF settings.

CONCLUSION

In gonadotropin-stimulated IUI cycles for unexplained infertility, dual trigger was associated with a higher but not statistically significant biochemical pregnancy rate compared with hCG alone. The study may be underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences. Infertility duration appears to be an important predictor of success. Larger, well-designed prospective studies using live birth as the primary outcome are warranted.
