Integrating left atrial appendage morphology and lipoprotein(a) improves thrombus risk stratification in atrial fibrillation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xueli Song, Jingyan Yang, Ying Li, Zixiong Zhao, Dongling Xu, Juan Wang, Xin Wang, Juan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate assessment of thrombus risk in atrial fibrillation (AF) requires the integration of structural and metabolic factors, which are insufficiently addressed by current clinical risk scores. We developed a model that added LAA morphology and metabolic markers, and compared it with the CHA2DS2‑VA score.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 418 participants (262 AF patients without thrombus and 156 AF patients with thrombus). Data on medical history, contrast‑enhanced computed tomography with three‑dimensional reconstruction, and metabolic profiling were retrospectively collected.ResultsThe final model comprised six independent predictors: non‑paroxysmal AF, CHA2DS2‑VA score ≥ 2, anticoagulation use, AF duration, LAA orifice ellipticity index (LAA OEI), and lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)). The model demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.861, 95% CI 0.829–0.892) and good calibration (Hosmer‑Lemeshow test p = 0.462). It significantly outperformed the CHA2DS2‑VA score (AUC = 0.643; ΔAUC = 0.2186; p < 0.0001), with a net reclassification improvement of 0.844 (p < 0.001) and an integrated discrimination improvement of 0.311 (p < 0.001).ConclusionsIntegrating LAA OEI and Lp(a) with clinical factors improves thrombus risk stratification in patients with AF compared with the CHA2DS2‑VA score alone.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is not just a rhythm problem. Its most feared consequence is thromboembolism, particularly stroke [1]. Despite the growing use of oral anticoagulants, a considerable number of AF patients still develop thrombotic events or are found to have an existing left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus on imaging. Current clinical practice relies heavily on the CHA2DS2‑VASc score or its version without sex, CHA2DS2‑VA [2], [3], [4]. These scores are easy to use, based on routine clinical variables such as age, hypertension, diabetes, and prior stroke. But they do not predict thrombus well at the individual level. For instance, the GLORIA‑AF Phase III registry reported an AUC of about 0.64 for CHA2DS2‑VA in predicting thromboembolic events [5]. This suggests its performance for thrombus is similarly limited. Existing risk scores rely on clinical history and demographics. They do not capture the individual mechanisms that lead to thrombus formation [6].

In patients with non-valvular AF, the incidence of left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus is approximately 2.8–8% despite anticoagulation [7], [8], [9]. Patients with the same risk scores may have substantially different risks due to individual variations in cardiac anatomy and hematological parameters [10], [11]. The LAA is the primary site of thrombus formation in AF patients, mainly because of its unique anatomical and hemodynamic characteristics [12]. Key morphological abnormalities of the LAA, particularly its orifice shape, have been identified as independent predictors of thrombus formation [13]. The CHA2DS2‑VASc score was originally designed to predict stroke and thromboembolism, not specifically LAA thrombus. However, because the LAA is the main source of cardioembolism, the score has been used in clinical practice to estimate LAA thrombus risk as well [14]. Its predictive value for LAA thrombus is modest, which justifies its use as a comparator while highlighting the need for an improved model.

Systemic metabolic problems like dyslipidemia may also raise thrombotic risk [15]. Several metabolic‑inflammatory markers, including lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), the triglyceride‑glucose index (TyG index, a surrogate for insulin resistance), and high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein (hs‑CRP), have been linked to thrombotic risk [16], [17], [18].

Several studies have attempted to improve thrombus prediction beyond CHA2DS2‑VA. The association of LAA morphological parameters with stroke risk has been reviewed, but none has been standardized for clinical use [19]. A quantitative measure of LAA shape has never been combined with a prothrombotic metabolic marker in a single model. We propose an anatomical‑metabolic dual‑axis strategy for LAA thrombus risk stratification. We focus on LAA‑specific morphology and systemic metabolic‑inflammatory abnormalities. These areas are largely overlooked by current risk scores. We aim to develop a parsimonious and clinically actionable prediction model that integrates these novel structural and metabolic markers. The model is intended to identify high‑risk patients who may be missed by conventional scoring, thereby enabling more personalized thromboprophylaxis.

Materials & methods

Study design and participants

This single‑center retrospective cohort study included patients who presented to the Second Hospital of Shandong University between 1 July 2021 and 31 December 2024. De‑identified data were accessed in June 2025, ensuring participant privacy. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 418 AF patients were included, comprising 262 without thrombus and 156 with thrombus (Fig. 1) . The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Shandong University (KYLL‑2021(KJ)P‑0348), which waived the requirement for informed consent. All data were fully anonymized to ensure participant confidentiality. The protocol was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100054680).Fig. 1Patient flowchart. Data collection and assessment of metabolic parameters

Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, AF features (type, duration), and laboratory parameters were collected from electronic medical records. Lipid profile parameters included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and Lp(a). The TyG index, a validated marker of insulin resistance, was calculated as ln[fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL) / 2] [20]. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was defined as the ratio of neutrophil count to lymphocyte count, both obtained from complete blood count.

CTCE

All patients had either coronary CTA or dedicated LAA‑enhanced CT as part of routine clinical care. Neither protocol included a dedicated delayed‑phase thrombus sequence. The CTCE images were retrieved and analyzed retrospectively. When a possible LAA filling defect was identified on CTCE, we retrospectively reviewed the patient medical records to determine whether TEE had been performed. All thrombus cases included in this study were confirmed by TEE, meaning only patients with TEE-confirmed thrombus were assigned to the thrombus group. In this retrospective cohort, CTCE was performed for routine clinical reasons, including pre-ablation/cardioversion assessment, cryptogenic stroke workup, anticoagulation contraindication, and high clinical suspicion of LAA thrombus. Due to the retrospective design, the exact indication for each patient could not be fully captured from medical records.

Three-dimensional (3D) imaging modalities

Raw data images were transferred to AW Volume Share 7 software, and surface images were extracted and processed for three‑dimensional reconstruction using EP Xpress software. First, the entire LAA structure was manually identified and a cut point on the cardiac surface was selected using the pulmonary vein and circumflex artery as a reference. The LAA was then segmented from the cardiac structure and unnecessary parts of the cardiac surface were removed (Fig. 2A). After rotating the LAA, the maximum and minimum diameters of the LAA ostium and the LAA depth were measured (Fig. 2B and 2C). The LAA ostium ellipticity index (LAA OEI) was defined as the ratio of the maximum diameter to the minimum diameter. LAA volume was quantified using a 3D imaging system, and LAA volume index (LAAVI) was calculated as the ratio of LAA volume to body surface area (BSA) using the Du Bois formula. Statistical analysis

Variable selection and model building.

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro‑Wilk test. Non‑normally distributed variables were presented as median (IQR) and compared with the Mann‑Whitney U test; categorical variables as frequency (%) and compared with the chi‑square test. Candidate variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into LASSO regression with 10‑fold cross‑validation (λ.min = 0.00344). Variables with non‑zero coefficients were then entered into a prespecified multivariable logistic regression model. A sensitivity analysis using λ.1se was performed to assess robustness (Supplementary Table S2).

Model performance.

Discrimination was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve with 95% confidence interval; the optimal cutoff was determined by the Youden index. Calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, the Brier score, and a calibration plot. Clinical utility was assessed by decision curve analysis comparing the six‑factor model with the CHA2DS2‑VA score. For comparison with the CHA2DS2‑VA score, AUCs were compared using DeLong's test, and net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement were calculated. The CHA2DS2‑VA score was first fitted as a logistic regression model to obtain predicted probabilities, which were then compared with those from the six‑factor model. For internal validation, bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was used to provide bias‑corrected and accelerated 95% confidence intervals for the AUC.

All tests were two‑sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0, MedCalc 20.0, and R 4.2 (glmnet package).

Patients in the thrombus group were significantly older (67.5 vs. 65.0 years, p = 0.002) and had a higher prevalence of non-paroxysmal AF (48.7% vs. 29.4%, p < 0.001), longer AF duration (28.0 vs. 14.0 months, p < 0.001), and a higher proportion with CHA2DS2‑VA score ≥ 2 (93.6% vs. 76.3%, p < 0.001). Anticoagulation use was markedly lower in the thrombus group (24.4% vs. 55.3%, p < 0.001). LAA morphological indices, including LAA OEI (1.69 vs. 1.45, p < 0.001) and LAAVI (2.55 vs. 2.28 mL/m2, p = 0.029), were significantly elevated in the thrombus group. Markers of metabolic dysregulation, such as Lp(a) (136.4 vs. 83.9 nmol/L, p < 0.001) and TyG index (8.96 ± 0.54 vs. 8.77 ± 0.48, p < 0.001), were significantly higher in patients with thrombus. Inflammatory markers, including NLR (2.31 vs. 1.95, p < 0.001) and hs‑CRP (6.85 vs. 2.65 mg/L, p < 0.001), as well as coagulation markers such as D‑dimer (0.49 vs. 0.35 µg/mL, p < 0.001) and fibrinogen (3.33 vs. 2.96 g/L, p < 0.001), were also significantly higher in the thrombus group. Heart failure was more prevalent in the thrombus group (46.8% vs. 25.2%, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between groups for sex, body mass index, or other conventional cardiovascular risk factors (all p > 0.05).

The intraobserver intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for LAA OEI was 0.973 (95% CI 0.945–0.987), and the interobserver ICC was 0.964 (95% CI 0.925–0.983), indicating excellent reproducibility.

LAA OEI and Lp(a) as independent predictors of thrombus

Univariate logistic regression identified 11 candidate variables (Supplementary Table S1). LASSO regression (λ.min = 0.00344) selected 9 variables (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Fig. S1); LAAVI was among the variables excluded due to a zero coefficient. In the final multivariable model, 6 variables remained independent predictors: non‑paroxysmal AF, CHA2DS2‑VA score ≥ 2, anticoagulation, AF duration, LAA OEI, and Lp(a) (Table 2). The integrated model combining LAA OEI and Lp(a) showed good discrimination, clinical utility, and stability

The integrated model achieved an AUC of 0.861 (95% CI 0.829–0.892). The optimal cutoff was 0.321, yielding a sensitivity of 84.0% and a specificity of 75.0% (Fig. 3A). Calibration was good (Hosmer‑Lemeshow χ2 = 7.72, df = 8, p = 0.462; Brier score = 0.14) (Fig. 3B). The integrated model significantly outperformed the CHA2DS2‑VA score

The CHA2DS2‑VA score had a significantly lower AUC (0.643, 95% CI 0.602–0.683; ΔAUC = 0.2186, 95% CI 0.1602–0.2761; DeLong test p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3A). Reclassification analysis further demonstrated superiority of the integrated model, with an NRI of 0.8441 (95% CI 0.7134–0.9749, p < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.3114 (95% CI 0.2578–0.3649, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis by anticoagulation status

Subgroup analyses stratified by anticoagulation status (Supplementary Table S4) revealed effect modification. In non‑anticoagulated patients (n = 235), non‑paroxysmal AF, CHA2DS2‑VA score ≥ 2, AF duration, and Lp(a) were independently associated with LAA thrombus (all p < 0.05), while LAA OEI was not (OR = 1.350, p = 0.369). In contrast, in anticoagulated patients (n = 183), LAA OEI became a significant predictor (OR = 3.669, p = 0.003), along with AF duration and Lp(a). Non‑paroxysmal AF was no longer significant. These results suggest that anticoagulation modifies the relative importance of risk factors, with LAA OEI being particularly valuable in anticoagulated patients.

Clinical tools for individualized risk assessment

To facilitate clinical implementation, we developed a nomogram based on the six independent predictors (Supplementary Fig. S2) and a simplified risk scoring system (Supplementary Table S3). These tools provide exploratory options for individualized assessment of prevalent LAA thrombus probability pending external validation in independent cohorts.

Discussion

For years, thrombus risk assessment in AF has relied heavily on the CHA2DS2‑VA score and its predecessor, CHA2DS2‑VASc. These tools are easy to use because they pull information from medical records. That makes them well suited for population screening. But they have clear limitations. They cannot capture local LAA hemodynamics. They also ignore metabolic changes that favor a prothrombotic state [21], [22], [23], [24]. This explains a common clinical puzzle: some patients with low scores develop thrombus, while others with high scores remain event‑free for years. Our study addressed this gap by building a model that combines clinical factors, LAA morphology, and Lp(a).

In our retrospective cohort of 418 AF patients, we developed a six‑factor model. It includes LAA OEI, Lp(a), and four clinical variables: non‑paroxysmal AF, AF duration, CHA2DS2‑VA score ≥ 2, and anticoagulation. The model showed good discrimination and calibration. It performed better than the CHA2DS2‑VA score.

The LAA is the primary site of thrombus formation in AF, accounting for more than 90% of cardioembolic strokes in nonvalvular AF [25]. The role of LAA anatomy as a predictor of stroke risk is widely recognized [26], [27], [28]. CTCE helps assess LAA anatomy and detect thrombus [29]. In this study, we introduced the LAA OEI, defined as the ratio of the maximum to minimum diameters of the LAA orifice. Unlike conventional qualitative LAA classification systems (e.g., chicken‑wing vs. non‑chicken‑wing), LAA OEI enables a quantitative, operator‑independent assessment of orifice morphology. This dimensional parameter offers a more straightforward quantification of LAA orifice geometry than traditional assessment methods. A higher LAA OEI reflects a more eccentric orifice, which may promote blood stasis and contribute to thrombus formation [30]. LAA OEI is an anatomical parameter. It does not depend on the presence of thrombus. It can be measured from CT images taken for other reasons (e.g., coronary CT angiography) before a thrombus forms. In this cross‑sectional study, LAA OEI was strongly associated with prevalent LAA thrombus. Because LAA OEI is a stable trait, this association raises the hypothesis that it may predict future thrombotic risk. This hypothesis requires prospective validation. This is the intended clinical scenario.

In patients with nonvalvular AF, LAA orifice area, morphology, and blood flow dynamics have been shown to be significantly correlated [31]. In our study, elevated LAA OEI was associated with irregular geometric configurations of the LAA, which may contribute to local flow stasis through altered hemodynamic patterns. The literature reports mixed findings regarding the relationship between LAA size and thrombotic risk. Conventional LAA shape classifications and simple volume measurements do not directly predict thrombosis risk [32]. Reduced LAA orifice flow velocity appears to be a more reliable indicator. Nevertheless, both retrospective and prospective studies have repeatedly identified LAAVI as an independent predictor of thrombus formation in patients with nonvalvular AF, including those receiving appropriate oral anticoagulation [33], [34]. In our study, LAAVI did not survive LASSO selection despite a univariate p < 0.05, whereas LAA OEI remained an independent predictor. This suggests that orifice ellipticity, which reflects the eccentricity of the LAA opening, captures more relevant geometric information for thrombogenesis than does simple volume measurement.

Lp(a) was also incorporated into the model. It directly promotes thrombus formation by inhibiting plasminogen activation and enhancing oxidative stress. It also works through other interconnected mechanisms, including enhanced platelet aggregation, inhibition of tissue factor pathway inhibitor, reduced plasmin generation, and increased expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor‑1 [35]. Elevated Lp(a) has been identified as an independent risk factor for left atrial thrombus in non-valvular AF patients with low CHA2DS2‑VASc scores [16]. These molecular and clinical findings generate the hypothesis that Lp(a) could serve as a metabolic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in AF‑related thrombosis, but this requires prospective validation. In our study, Lp(a) remained an independent predictor of LAA thrombus after adjustment for clinical risk factors and anticoagulation, with an odds ratio of 1.02 per 10 nmol/L increase (p < 0.001). This finding aligns with a growing body of evidence that Lp(a) contributes to a prothrombotic state beyond what is captured by conventional clinical scores. Lp(a) is cheap and easy to test. In patients with elevated Lp(a), clinicians may have a lower threshold for further imaging (CTCE or TEE) to rule out LAA thrombus, or for more intensive anticoagulation. Low Lp(a) offers reassurance. These suggestions are exploratory and need prospective confirmation.

The modest performance of the CHA2DS2‑VA score in our cohort (AUC = 0.643) is consistent with previous studies. The score was made to predict ischemic stroke, not LAA thrombus. It does not identify patients with high‑risk anatomy or metabolic profiles. So patients with the same score can have very different risks. LAA thrombus is a local event driven by structural and hemodynamic abnormalities not captured by clinical scores, which explains why the CHA2DS2‑VA score performed poorly. By incorporating LAA OEI and Lp(a), our model addresses this limitation and provides a more mechanistic approach to risk stratification.

The model offers several potential clinical advantages for assessing prevalent thrombus risk. For example, it may help identify patients with a high probability of existing LAA thrombus despite low CHA2DS2‑VA scores. It might also assist in phenotype‑specific considerations, such as LAA closure for structural abnormalities or lipid‑lowering for metabolic dysregulation. Finally, it may help detect residual risk in anticoagulated patients. A nomogram (Supplementary Fig. S2) allows precise individualized probability estimation, while a simplified risk scoring system (Supplementary Table S3) enables rapid bedside risk categorization. Based on the predicted probability of prevalent LAA thrombus, a total score ≤ 20 suggests low probability (<10%), while a score ≥ 71 suggests high probability (>70%). Scores 21–70 represent moderate probability. These tools were derived from a single‑center cohort and have not been externally validated. Therefore, they should be considered exploratory. Prospective validation in independent populations is needed before clinical application. If confirmed, they could eventually help translate the complex six‑factor model into user‑friendly formats to support personalized thromboprophylaxis decisions.

Subgroup analysis showed that LAA OEI was associated with thrombus only in patients on anticoagulation. Non‑paroxysmal AF predicted thrombus only in those not on anticoagulation. This suggests that anticoagulation lowers risk from clinical and metabolic factors, but does not compensate for a bad LAA shape. So in anticoagulated patients, LAA OEI may help identify those with residual risk. It could guide decisions like LAA closure or closer follow‑up. More studies are needed to confirm this.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, 37.3% of our cohort had LAA thrombus. This reflects the retrospective design and the clinical indications for CTCE (e.g., pre‑ablation, stroke workup, high suspicion). So our findings apply only to a high‑risk selected population and may not generalize to all AF patients. Prospective validation in unselected cohorts is needed. Second, we performed internal validation with bootstrap resampling. However, external validation in independent cohorts is still needed to confirm generalizability. Third, D‑dimer and fibrinogen were excluded because they are acute‑phase reactants. However, we cannot rule out residual confounding from subclinical inflammation. Fourth, LAA OEI measurement depends on the imaging method and operator skill. Standardized protocols are needed. Fifth, we did not evaluate LAA flow velocity, which could provide additional hemodynamic information. Sixth, most anticoagulated patients in this cohort were on NOACs, but detailed information on treatment duration, adherence, and therapeutic levels was not available. Therefore, we treated anticoagulation as a binary variable, which may have introduced residual confounding. Finally, the nomogram and risk score have not been tested in real‑world practice. Their feasibility and reproducibility need future studies.

Conclusions

In this cross‑sectional study, a six‑factor model including LAA OEI and Lp(a) improved the identification of prevalent LAA thrombus compared with the CHA2DS2‑VA score alone. The model was robust and well calibrated. Whether it can predict future thrombotic risk remains unknown and needs prospective testing. Our findings support further research into structural and metabolic biomarkers for thrombus risk assessment.
