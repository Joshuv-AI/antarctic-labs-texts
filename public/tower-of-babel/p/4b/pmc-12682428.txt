Occipitocervical Fusion Using Cervical Pedicle Screws for Metastatic Tumors to the Craniocervical Junction: Surgical Outcomes and the Role of Postoperative Chemotherapy
Authors: Hiroyuki KATO, Yusuke NISHIMURA, Shoichi HAIMOTO, Yoshitaka NAGASHIMA, Eisuke TSUKAMOTO, Kazuichi TERAO, Yuki SUNOHARA, Naoto KAWAGUCHI, Masahito HARA, Masakazu TAKAYASU, Ryuta SAITO
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Advancements in chemotherapy have extended the survival of patients with metastatic cancer, increasing the need for effective treatment strategies for spinal metastases. Metastasis to the craniocervical junction significantly affects activities of daily living. This study evaluates the safety and effectiveness of cervical pedicle screws in occipitocervical fusion for metastasis to the craniocervical junction and reassesses the role of surgery within a multidisciplinary approach incorporating novel chemotherapy. A retrospective review was conducted on 21 patients with metastasis to the craniocervical junction who underwent occipitocervical fusion with cervical pedicle screws at Nagoya University Hospital and Aichi Cancer Center between November 2017 and August 2024. Clinical data, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status, imaging findings, surgical outcomes, perioperative complications, and adjuvant therapies were analyzed. The revised Tokuhashi score and the Modified Bauer score were used for prognostic assessment. All patients experienced significant postoperative pain relief, and 17 cases showed improvement in Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status. Cervical pedicle screws provided rigid stabilization, allowing for limited fusion levels (fixation down to C3 in 11 cases). Seven of the 11 patients who received postoperative chemotherapy survived beyond their predicted prognosis. No intraoperative complications related to pedicle screws were reported. These findings suggest that occipitocervical fusion using cervical pedicle screws effectively stabilizes metastasis to the craniocervical junction while preserving limited fusion levels. A multidisciplinary approach integrating surgery and chemotherapy may improve survival in this challenging patient population.

Introduction

The spine is the most commonly affected site of bone metastasis in terminal cancer patients, with an incidence rate of up to 70%.1) Spinal metastases occur in the cervical spine in 8%-25% of cases,2) but only 0.5% involve the craniocervical junction (CCJ).3) The CCJ, a complex transition area between the skull and cervical spine, consists of synovial facet joints and ligaments that are vital for stability and mobility. These delicate structures can be disrupted by metastasis to the CCJ (CCJ mets), resulting in spinal instability, intense pain, paralysis, and even death.4-7)

Surgical intervention is often essential for managing CCJ instability, as conservative treatments frequently fail to provide adequate stability. Devices such as the halo vest and cervical orthosis may offer only limited support and are often associated with adverse effects, including dysphagia, pressure sores, and poor patient compliance, especially in long-term treatment cases.4,7-9) Surgical intervention, including posterior-only decompression and stabilization (usually occipitocervical fusion [OCF]) with or without radiotherapy, is often warranted due to tumor-induced instability of the CCJ.

In cases of CCJ mets, tumor invasion often restricts available screw trajectories, typically requiring additional fixation levels; however, rigid cervical pedicle screws may allow adequate fixation with fewer segments. Surgical techniques in this region remain challenging and controversial due to its anatomical complexity, bone fragility in immunocompromised cancer patients, and limited screw trajectories resulting from osteolytic bone destruction.4,6,8,10-15) In this context, achieving rigid spinal stabilization with minimal fusion levels is ideal. Therefore, we exclusively use cervical pedicle screws in OCF, as they provide the necessary stability even with limited screw options.16)

With recent advances in chemotherapy for primary lesions, surgery has become a key component of the aggressive multidisciplinary approach aimed at extending overall prognosis, rather than solely alleviating neurological symptoms caused by spinal instability.4,9) The remarkable progress in cancer chemotherapy, including molecular-targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors, has raised expectations that surgical intervention can improve patient Performance Status (PS), facilitate postoperative chemotherapy, and enhance overall prognosis. With the development of novel chemotherapies, such as molecular-targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors, it is essential to re-evaluate surgical strategies and outcomes within a multidisciplinary approach.

This study aims to reassess the safety and effectiveness of cervical pedicle screws for OCF, and the role of surgery within a multidisciplinary approach including novel chemotherapy. We retrospectively analyze the clinical features, overall prognosis, and surgical outcomes of OCF using cervical pedicle screws in patients with CCJ mets. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate OCF using cervical pedicle screws for CCJ mets within an aggressive multidisciplinary treatment approach.

Materials and Methods

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 21 consecutive cases with metastatic tumors to the CCJ, which consists of the occipital condyle (O), atlas (C1), and axis (C2). All cases presented with spinal instability of the CCJ and underwent OCF at Nagoya University Hospital or Aichi Cancer Center in Japan between November 2017 and August 2024. All cases were discussed at the tumor board to reach a consensus on management. Surgery was indicated for cases with an expected survival period of 3 months or more, as determined by the attending physician for the primary tumor. If chemotherapy remained a viable option, the surgical objective was to enhance the patients' PS, thereby making them eligible for postoperative chemotherapy. All relevant clinical data of demographics, neurological symptoms, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group PS (ECOG PS), type and prognosis of the primary tumor, other systemic metastatic lesions, perioperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy, imaging findings, and surgical outcomes were analyzed by reviewing the patients' medical records. The Modified Frankel Classification was adopted for the evaluation of neurological symptoms. The revised Tokuhashi score (RTS)17) and the Modified Bauer score (MBS) were utilized to predict the prognosis of the patients prior to treatment. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nagoya University Hospital (Number 2021-0292), and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived due to its retrospective design and the use of anonymized data.

Radiological diagnosis

Computed tomography (CT) scan images were assessed to identify location of metastatic tumors with osteolytic or osteoblastic changes, and pathological vertebral fractures. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) T2-weighted scans were mainly utilized to assess the presence of extraosseous soft tissue invasion and spinal cord compression. The degree of cervical instability was assessed using the Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score (SINS),18) while the extent of spinal cord compression was determined using the Epidural Spinal Cord Compression (ESCC) scale.19)

Surgical procedure and postoperative treatment

All cases in the present study underwent OCF using occipital plates, rods, and cervical pedicle screws under general anesthesia, performed by 2 senior certified neurosurgeons at Nagoya University Hospital or Aichi Cancer Center. The most caudal level of fixation was generally set 2 levels below the affected vertebrae; however, the fixation length was adjusted depending on the availability of reliable pedicle screw insertion without tumor involvement. The patient's general condition and bone quality were also considered when determining fixation levels. Preoperative CT-based navigation (Medtronic StealthStation) was prepared following adequate exposure of bony structures. First, the screw trajectory was precisely created using a navigated drill (Medtronic Stealth-Midas) without placing additional stress on the spine. The screw hole was tapped and expanded, with trajectory length measured on the navigation screen prior to screw placement. Pedicle screws were then inserted with full navigation guidance as well. Subsequently, an occipital plate and rods were placed. We proceeded with the procedure of laminectomy and decompression depending on the presence of spinal cord compression. Bone grafting was not performed due to insufficient local bone availability. Radiotherapy was given to all cases preoperatively or postoperatively, except for radioresistant tumors. Chemotherapy was administered in selective cases, either preoperatively or postoperatively, after thorough discussion at the tumor board.

Results

Preoperative clinical variables

The cases in the present study consisted of 15 males and 6 females, with an average age of 67.9 ± 10.9 (range 42-82). Clinically, all cases preoperatively presented with severe neck pain and occipital neuralgia triggered by spinal instability. Neurological deficits were uncommon, with 20 cases classified as Frankel grade E and 1 case as grade D3 due to mild weakness. Distinctively, 4 cases presented with dysphasia due to inferior cranial nerve or hypoglossal nerve palsy caused by direct tumor invasion. The mean preoperative ECOG PS was 2.95, with 15 cases classified as ECOG PS 3 or 4, indicating an inability to walk. Regarding the primary tumors, head and neck cancers, including oral cavity, larynx, hypopharynx, and salivary gland cancers, were listed as predominant types, presenting in 8 cases (38.1%), followed by lung cancers in 5 cases (23.8%). Breast cancers were observed in 3 cases (14.3%), while liver cancers were present in 2 cases (9.5%). The prognosis was predicted preoperatively based on the RTS, which revealed that 14 cases (66.7%) had a predicted survival of less than 6 months (RTS ≤ 8), while the remaining 7 cases (33.3%) had an estimated prognosis of 6-12 months (RTS 9-11). On evaluation using the MBS, 10 cases (47.6%) were assigned a score of 1, indicating a recommendation for supportive care without surgical intervention, while 9 cases (42.9%) had a score of 2, suggesting short-term palliation via dorsal surgery. Two cases (9.5%) received a score of 3, indicating an approach targeting mid-term local control (Table 1).

Radiological findings

Eleven cases (52.4%) presented with single-level tumors: 6 cases specifically at the C1 level, 3 at C2, and 2 at C0. In contrast, 6 cases (28.6%) displayed tumors extending over 2 levels: 4 involved both C1 and C2, while the other 2 spanned from C0 to C1. Four cases (19.0%) demonstrated expansive tumors spreading across 3 levels. Twenty cases (95.2%) exhibited pathological fractures of vertebral bodies, and 19 cases showed extension of extraosseous soft tissue invasion. Notably, 18 cases (85.7%) scored more than 13 on SINS, indicating significant spinal instability requiring surgical stabilization. Most cases demonstrated no spinal cord compression, while only 5 cases had spinal cord compression (ESCC ≥2) (Table 1).

Surgical details

All cases underwent posterior OCF using cervical pedicle screws for spinal instability. The most caudal level of fusion was C3 in 11 cases, C4 in 3 cases, C5 in 6 cases, and C6 in 1 case. The procedure of spinal cord decompression was performed in 5 cases presenting with spinal cord compression (ESCC ≥2). The average blood loss was 103.7 ± 103.5 mL. There were no intraoperative surgical complications, such as nerve tissue injury, vascular injury, or unintentional dural tear (Table 2).

Surgical outcomes and adjuvant therapy

Postoperatively, neck pain and occipital neuralgia completely and immediately resolved in all cases. One case was lost to follow-up after being transferred to another hospital. There was an improvement in ECOG PS: 11 cases (52.4%) improved by 1 point, and 6 cases (28.6%) improved by 2 points within 1 month after surgery. Among the 20 cases that could be followed up, 17 cases were able to ambulate again, scoring 1 or 2 in ECOG PS. One case with a preoperative ECOG PS of 2 was lost to follow-up, and his postoperative PS could not be assessed (Table 2). Two cases with preoperative dysphagia experienced postoperative aspiration pneumonia (Case 3, 8). Chemotherapy was successfully administered postoperatively in 11 cases, with 7 cases achieving a longer actual survival than the preoperatively predicted prognosis based on RTS. Seven cases were treated with novel chemotherapy, including molecular-targeted agents (Case 1, 10, 15, 17, 21) and 2 with immune checkpoint inhibitors (Case 2, 4), resulting in a longer prognosis compared to the 4 cases treated with traditional chemotherapy (Case 9, 16, 18, 20). Notably, the case with laryngeal cancer achieved significant recovery with a postoperative survival of 34 months, as a result of postoperative radiation and chemotherapy with an immune checkpoint inhibitor, despite the newly onset postoperative dysphagia linked to the primary tumor (Case 2). In contrast, 9 cases without postoperative chemotherapy had a less favorable prognosis, with a median of 3 months (≤ 6 months in all cases) (Table 3). Postoperative chemotherapy was omitted for the following reasons: tumor progression despite preoperative chemotherapy in 4 cases, poor postoperative ECOG PS (PS 3 at 1 month) in 2 cases, deteriorated respiratory function in 2 cases, and patient refusal in 1 case. No local recurrence was noted during the follow-up period of 11.0 months on average (range 1-65 months). The median postoperative survival period was 5 months (range 1-65 months). Illustrative case (case 1)

The case is a 68-year-old female with a history of metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma involving the thoracic spine (T9-T11) and left clavicle, previously managed with radiotherapy. She presented with a swollen neck and severe pain. She showed no neurological deficits. Cervical X-rays, CT, and MRI scans identified a C2 pathological fracture without spinal cord compression (Fig. 1A-D). Initially, she was placed in a halo vest, and subsequently, she underwent a posterior OCF down to C4 (Fig. 1E and F). Cervical pedicle screws at C3 and C4 on both sides were successfully placed with navigation guidance and a navigated drill (Fig. 1G and H). All screws were 3.5 mm in diameter and 22-26 mm in length. Excellent restoration of spinal alignment was achieved, with no screw loosening or displacement observed throughout the postoperative course. Postoperatively, she exhibited significant improvement in ECOG PS from 3 to 1, which facilitated the administration of postoperative chemotherapy as well as radiotherapy. She received sorafenib, a molecular-targeted agent for hepatocellular carcinoma, and survived for 9 months postoperatively, in contrast to the predicted survival period of less than 6 months calculated by the RTS and the MBS.

Discussion

CCJ mets represent a rare clinical event, comprising approximately 0.5% of all spinal metastases.3) Management remains a controversial topic, particularly concerning the indication for surgery, the establishment of surgical strategy, and the outcomes of multidisciplinary treatment. Recently developed novel chemotherapies, such as molecular-targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors, have been introduced into clinical practice. Patient outcomes should be reevaluated in accordance with the advent of these novel chemotherapies. This study retrospectively analyzes the clinical features of CCJ mets, the overall prognosis as a result of multidisciplinary approach, and the surgical outcomes of OCF using cervical pedicle screws. By sharing our experiences, we aim to provide further insights into the management of this unique and challenging metastasis.

In alignment with existing literature, most of our cases presented with neck or occipital pain without neurological deficits.4-6,13,14,20) Due to the relatively spacious canal in the upper cervical spine, neurological deficits caused by tumors in this area are quite uncommon.6,13) The mean age at presentation was comparable to previous reports, which ranged between 50 and 70 years.20) The predominant primary tumors in our case series were lung and breast cancers, mirroring prior studies that identified breast, lung, renal, and prostate cancers as the most frequently involved.4-6,13,14) Head and neck cancers encompass a broad spectrum of malignancies and are generally not classified among the most common primary sources of CCJ metastases.

The pain of CCJ mets usually stems from spinal instability, which is typically not fully alleviated by radiotherapy alone.7) Therefore, assessment of spinal instability is crucial when considering surgical indications. The importance of the SINS has been widely acknowledged since its introduction in 2010. In the present study, 85.7% of the cases scored 13 or higher, indicating instability and recommending surgical stabilization. The remaining cases scored 7-12, which was technically categorized as potentially unstable; however, surgical intervention has been reported to be more effective, particularly in lesions scoring between 10 and 12, compared to those scoring 7-9.21) In addition, bone grafting is typically considered to promote solid arthrodesis and prevent pseudoarthrosis or hardware failure. However, adequate autologous bone cannot be harvested during standard OCF procedures, even with C1 laminectomy. Considering the advanced cancer status of these patients, harvesting bone from other parts of the body, such as the iliac crest, would be excessively invasive. Therefore, omission of bone grafting may be warranted in this context.

CCJ mets are frequently associated with osteolytic changes of vertebral bodies, and thereby the available screw trajectories are limited.15) Consequently, longer fixation is possibly required. However, cervical pedicle screws, which provide more rigid stabilization than any other screws,16) may reduce the number of required levels. In more than half of all our cases, fixation was limited to only the C3 level without any reported complications, a relatively shorter fixation compared to previous reports.14,15) Although cervical pedicle screws carry a higher risk of vascular and nerve tissue injuries,22) the use of a navigation system and a navigated drill can facilitate safer screw insertion. CT-based navigation is valuable for identifying the unique anatomical complexities of the pedicles and determining the optimal entry points for screw placement, which are essential for preventing misplacement.23) Compared to navigated probes, navigated drills can significantly reduce the risk of lateral perforations, which could lead to vertebral artery injury. When using a probe, the trajectory often shifts outward due to the thick medial wall of the pedicles, rotational forces on the vertebrae during probe insertion, and the contraction of the paraspinal muscles. Navigated drills help maintain a precise trajectory with minimal rotation and controlled shaving of a part of the medial wall, avoiding the need to counter muscle resistance during insertion.24) In the present study, all cervical pedicle screws were safely inserted using a preoperative CT-based navigation system and a navigated high-speed drill, resulting in successful screw placement without any intraoperative complications.

The availability of effective chemotherapy for the primary tumor greatly influences the surgical outcomes of CCJ mets. Prognostic scoring systems, such as the RTS and MBS, play crucial roles in predicting survival and guiding surgical indications for metastatic spinal tumors. In the present study, the RTS predicted a prognosis of 6 months or less in two-thirds of cases and 12 months or less in the other one-third. However, substantial postoperative improvement in ECOG PS, primarily due to CCJ stabilization, enabled chemotherapy administration and prolonged patient survival. Our findings showed that patients who recovered sufficiently to receive appropriate chemotherapy for the primary tumors had a longer actual survival compared to those who did not undergo postoperative chemotherapy. Furthermore, cases treated with molecular-targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors demonstrated longer survival than cases that received traditional chemotherapy. RTS and MBS, which were developed before the advent of novel chemotherapies, have limitations as prognostic predictors in modern oncology. More recent scoring systems such as the Katagiri-New score25) and Skeletal Oncology Research Group nomogram26) may offer more accurate guidance as they reflect the effects of recent chemotherapy, although they are not specifically designed for spinal metastases requiring surgical intervention. While these prognostic scoring systems have inherent limitations, it can still be suggested that the surgical interventions of OCF not only provide immediate symptom relief but may also extend patient survival by enabling subsequent novel chemotherapy, as previous reports have suggested.4,9) In this context, the surgical objective should focus more on extending survival in patients who become eligible for chemotherapy after surgery, rather than focusing solely on palliative pain relief. The treatment for CCJ mets should be centered on a multidisciplinary approach, where surgical intervention needs to play a crucial role.

Conversely, it is essential to consider the potential adverse effects of surgical intervention when determining indications, as OCF can impose a significant burden on patients with advanced cancer. In the present study, 5 cases showed actual survival of less than 3 months, despite an estimated prognosis of 3 months or more. This suggests the challenge of accurately predicting prognosis preoperatively and raises the possibility that OCF may have indirectly contributed to shortened survival in these cases. While acknowledging this risk, for patients with preoperative ECOG PS 3 or 4 who desire active treatment, surgical intervention remains the only viable option to improve PS and facilitate subsequent adjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Furthermore, although chemoradiotherapy alone may be considered for patients with PS 2, surgical stabilization should take priority when spinal instability is indicated by SINS, as was the case with 6 patients in this study, to prevent functional decline during treatment.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, a small patient sample from only 2 centers, and the exclusive use of a single type of screw and rod system without comparing the effectiveness and complications of different fixation methods. Additionally, patient-reported outcomes, such as quality of life and mental health assessments, were not evaluated. Despite these limitations, we believe our study contributes valuable insights to the existing literature, particularly in discussing clinical features and outcomes of a specific surgical technique applied to a rare group of patients with CCJ mets. Future research is expected to investigate how surgical intervention in patients with advanced-stage cancer may influence quality of life and mental well-being, especially considering their eventual transition to a terminal phase.

Conclusion

CCJ mets clinically result in severe neck pain without neurological deficit, significantly impacting ECOG PS. Our findings suggest that OCF using cervical pedicle screws for these lesions effectively alleviates pain and improves PS with shorter fixation, thus facilitating the administration of chemotherapy, including molecular-targeted agent and immune checkpoint inhibitors. This combined multidisciplinary approach may contribute to prolonged patient survival.
