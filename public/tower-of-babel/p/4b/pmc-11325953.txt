Surgical modifications to the conventional kidney transplant technique: the Miami Transplant Institute approach in a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Gaetano Ciancio, Marina M Tabbara, Javier Gonzalez, Angel Alvarez, Jeffrey J Gaynor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:At our center, surgical modifications to the conventional kidney transplant technique were developed with two goals in mind: to minimize the risk of developing post-transplant urologic/vascular/other surgical complications, and to simultaneously eliminate the need for initial ureteral stent placement and surgical drainage.Methods:Here, the authors describe these modifications along with (what we believe are) their advantages over the conventional technique: creating an abdominal flap for easier abdominal closure (reflecting the parietal peritoneum from the abdominal wall), mobilizing the bladder before transplant (creating more space for bladder dissection, allowing it to move upward during abdominal wall closure), minimizing the dissection of iliac vessels to only anterior lymphatic tissue (attempting to minimize the incidence of fluid collections), using plastic arterial vascular bulldog clamps (causing less trauma to the iliac artery), performing vascular anastomosis of the renal artery first (making it easier for the surgeon to perform the anastomoses), creating longer ureteral spatulation, and inclusion of bladder mucosa along with some detrusor muscle layer in performing the ureteral anastomosis (attempting to minimize the incidence of urologic complications). Of note, no initial ureteral stent placement or surgical drainage was used. The authors report our experience during the first 12 months post-transplant of a single transplant surgeon who used each of these modifications among 707 consecutive recipients of kidney-alone transplants at our center since 2014.Results:During the first 12 months post-transplant, 2.3% (16/707) of patients developed a urologic complication; only 1.0% (7/707) required surgical repair of their original ureteroneocystostomy. Additionally, 2.7% (19/707) developed a vascular complication; 8.8% (62/707) developed some other type of surgical complication (wound complication, lymphocele development, or development of a peri-renal hematoma or peri-renal collection). These overall results were advantageous when compared with other studies.Conclusion:The authors believe that this modified kidney transplant technique clearly helped in reducing post-transplant risks of developing urologic/vascular/other surgical complications. Importantly, these results were achieved without initial ureteral stent placement or surgical drainage.Keywords: conventional kidney transplant technique, surgical modification

Despite some improvements/refinements of the conventional surgical technique in kidney transplantation, urologic/vascular/other surgical complications may still occur post-transplant and are associated with unfavorable outcomes, including recurring morbidity, repeat hospitalizations, and potentially even graft failure or death1–4. Some complications may require multiple radiological and/or surgical procedures to fully correct the problem(s)5. The reported incidence of urologic complications following renal transplantation in defined cohorts of at least 200 patients ranges between 1.7 and 15.0%1. The reported incidence of vascular complications ranges from 1.0 to 23.0%3,4; the reported incidence of other surgical complications ranges from 0.6 to 18.0% for lymphocele development and is about 8.0–10.0% for wound complications3. Potential risk factors for the development of urologic complications post-transplant include older donor age, pre-existing recipient comorbidities, iatrogenic ureteral injury, extensive dissection of the iliac vessels, and routine ureteral stent and/or surgical drainage use2.

The conventionally accepted surgical technique for performing kidney transplantation has almost not changed since its initial description in 1951, which included extensive dissection of the iliac vessels and performing the renal vein anastomosis first (before the renal artery)6. Few modifications to this original technique have been introduced over the years7–10. The renal artery anastomosis first approach7,8 was previously introduced as an alternative to the renal vein anastomosis first approach9, and was also recently reported to yield low post-transplant vascular and urological complication rates11.

Our transplant program since its incecption in 1978 also used the renal vein anastomosis first technique with extensive dissection of lymphatic tissues9. However, some surgical modifications of the original kidney transplant technique were developed over the years, including a new extravesical ureteroneocystostomy, with the goal to minimize urologic complications, and without the need of ureteral stent placement5. We also recently reported our experience in using a drain-free strategy12, with the goal to avoid/minimize surgical complication risks post-transplant5,12.

Here, we describe our modified kidney transplant technique and report its experience among 707 consecutively transplanted kidney-alone transplant recipients. The technique includes: creating an abdominal flap, reflecting the parietal peritoneum from the abdominal wall, for easier abdominal closure, mobilization of the bladder before bringing the kidney to the surgical field, limited dissection of lymphatic tissue of the iliac vessels, use of Fibraspring clips G-1250 12 mm soft vascular clamps for clamping the iliac artery, performing the renal artery anastomosis first (avoiding the flip-flop maneuver), our ureteral anastomosis without ureteral stent placement, and abdominal wall closure without surgical drainage.

Methods

Data description

Between 17 March 2014 and 18 February 2023, 50 pediatric and 657 adult recipients of a kidney-alone transplant were performed consecutively by a single surgeon (G.C.) at our center, using this modified kidney transplant surgical technique. The Institutional Review Board at our institution (IRB 20140129) approved the study, which was in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (as revised in 2013). All patients (or guardians) signed an informed consent prior to transplant. This research is being reported in line with strengthening the reporting of cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies in surgery (STROCSS) 2021 guidelines13.

Patients who underwent a deceased donor (DD) kidney transplant along with another organ were excluded. All patients had a routine (baseline) ultrasound of the kidney allograft performed during the first week post-transplant.

All urologic, vascular, and other surgical complications were recorded. Urologic complications included ureteral leak, ureteral stricture, ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), bladder rupture/rupture of the ureteral anastomosis, and urinoma. Vascular complications included renal artery thrombosis, renal vein thrombosis, and renal artery stenosis (RAS). Other surgical complications included lymphocele development, wound complication (infection, dehiscence, or seroma), and development of a peri-renal hematoma or peri-renal collection.

Suspected urologic complications were evaluated with renal ultrasonography, mercaptoacetyltriglycine scan, and/or antegrade nephrostogram. Urologic complications were treated by either percutaneous radiological procedures, surgery, or a combination of both. Suspected ureteral stenosis/stricture was evaluated along with serial monitoring for BK virus replication (in urine and blood).

Voiding cystourethrogram was not routinely performed, as routine evaluation for VUR was not considered to be necessary in our transplant recipients; therefore, only symptomatic reflux was evaluated and considered as a urologic complication.

Suspected vascular and/or other surgical complications were first evaluated by renal ultrasonography and, if necessary, by computerized tomography scan or magnetic resonance angiography. Surgical vs. conservative treatment was determined according to test results and complication severity.

Surgical technique

Our surgical approach, which applies to both DD and living donor (LD) kidney transplantation, includes certain technical aspects of others6–11 along with certain aspects that are uniquely different. Specifically, following DD recovery of the right or left kidney, the aortic patch is preserved to create a carrel patch for arterial anastomosis. All DD kidneys were connected to the LifePort Renal Preservation Machine upon arrival at our center (Fig. 1). These DD kidneys were arterially cannulated and perfused with Kidney Perfusion Solution (Fig. 1). Perfusion temperature was set at 4°C, and systolic perfusion pressure was initially fixed at 30 mmHg, with adjustment made after 30 min to obtain a flow goal of >100 ml/min. Pumping flow (ml/min) and resistive indices (mmHg/ml/min) were serially monitored. DD kidneys were maintained under these conditions until transplant14,15. All right DD kidneys had the renal vein extended by using a portion of the donor’s inferior vena cava (IVC) as extension (Fig. 1). The most common procedure is the suturing of both ends of the IVC with 6-0 Prolene. The second reconstruction of the right renal vein is to close the proximal end of the IVC and left renal vein ostium with 5-0Prolene, and in performing the renal vein anastomosis, the IVC is trimmed to avoid kinking or having a redundant renal vein (Fig. 1).

Drawing of the preparation of the right renal vein of deceased and living donor kidneys. Placement of the deceased donor kidney allograft inside the LifePort.

LD kidneys, once procured, are prepared on the back table prior to transplant. All LD right kidney allografts have the renal vein extended using a previously procured DD iliac vein. A DD iliac vein segment is anastomosed end-to-end with 6-0 Prolene (Fig. 1). Previously procured DD iliac veins were preserved in histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate solution, stored at 4oC for 2 weeks, and then discarded afterwards. The utilized DD iliac veins were ABO-compatible and had a final negative crossmatch with the recipient.

Once kidney allograft preparation on the back table is completed, a right or left lower quadrant hockey-stick incision is made in the recipient to gain access into the retroperitoneal space and expose the iliac vessels. The inferior epigastric vessels are ligated, stick tied with 2-0 silk and divided; the round ligament is also ligated with 2-0 silk and divided (in the female recipient). The spermatic cord is retracted medially, once the Bookwalter retractor system is placed into the abdominal wall opening. Since 2018, dissection of the abdominal wall and hemostasis of vessels are performed using the Thunderbeat device. The parietal peritoneum is peeled off from the abdominal wall to create a 1 cm flap, making abdominal wall closure easier to perform when completing the kidney transplant procedure (Fig. 2).

Drawing of the kidney transplant incision, use of the Thunderbeat, and the peeling off of the parietal peritoneum from the abdominal wall to create a 1 cm flap.

The next step is bladder mobilization to facilitate upward mobility (with the kidney) following abdominal wall closure (with the goal of avoiding potential urological complications). The bladder is gravity filled with normal saline-containing antibiotic solution with Foley catheter clamping. The umbilico-vesical fascia is opened, allowing better expansion and easy bladder mobilization5 (wide space is available while the kidney allograft is not yet in place). Of note, we used the recipient’s bladder for all ureteral anastomoses – longer time on dialysis or prolonged pretransplant anuria made no difference5.

A major modification is the limited dissection that we perform of the lymphatic and areolar tissue of the external iliac artery and vein (to minimize occurrence of fluid collections). Only the anterior lymphatic tissue is dissected, creating enough space to place the vascular clamp (Fig. 3). Dissection of lymphatic tissue is performed with the Thunderbeat device. Since 2014, Fibra spring clips G-1250 12 mm soft vascular clamps are used to clamp the external (or common) iliac artery (with the goal of minimizing trauma to the iliac artery), and the external iliac vein is clamped with a LambertKay atrauma anastomosis vascular clamp (Fig. 3).

Visual of minimal dissection of the lymphatic tissue of the external iliac artery and vein, and placement of the vascular clamps.

Another modification is that the renal artery is anastomosed first (Fig. 4). For a DD kidney, the proximal and distal corners of the carrel patch (6-0 Prolene) are placed while the kidney is still on ice. The external iliac artery is opened with an 11-blade scalpel, and the incision is extended with a Potts-Smith scissors, with medial and lateral sutures (6-0 Prolene) being placed to keep the two arterial walls apart. The lateral side of the renal artery anastomosis is performed first by continuous suture using 6-0 Prolene of the distal corner and finishing in the middle of the anastomosis (lateral stay suture 6-0 Prolene then being removed). Suturing is then continued from the proximal corner in a continuous manner, finishing in the middle of the anastomosis and tied with the distal 6-0 Prolene. The kidney allograft then gets flipped to the lateral side and is placed in a comfortable position on the psoas muscle (Fig. 4). The medial side of the renal arterial anastomosis is performed in a continuous manner, and both 6-0 Prolene corners are tied in the middle, removing the stay suture. Next, the external iliac vein is opened with an 11-blade scalpel, and the incision is extended with a Potts-Smith scissors. The renal vein is anastomosed by placing two corner proximal and distal sutures (5-0 Prolene). A stay suture (5-0 Prolene) is placed through the middle of the medial wall to keep the external iliac vein open. The lateral side of the renal vein anastomosis is performed first from inside of the lumen and finishing at the distal corner, being tied with the distal corner suture (5-0 Prolene) (Fig. 4). The medial side is performed next, outside the renal vein, with corner sutures (5-0 Prolene) being used in a continuous manner, finishing at the middle of the renal vein anastomosis and being tied. The medial stay suture (5-0 Prolene) is then removed, once one of the corners gets to the middle of the medial wall. By performing the arterial anastomosis first, the flip-flop maneuver is avoided; it also avoids the need of retracting the renal vein (which can be very cumbersome) when performing the arterial anastomosis.

Visual of the creation of common Carrel patch with two renal arteries, and steps for the arterial and venous anastomosis.

In performing a LD kidney transplant, the renal artery anastomosis is also performed first using the same approach as described above, with the main difference being that the corners and stay sutures are 7-0 Prolene. Each of the four sutures includes the wall of the renal artery, keeping the renal and external iliac arteries wide open. The LD renal artery anastomosis is also performed in a continuous fashion.

The kidney allograft is then reperfused by releasing the venous vascular clamp first followed by the distal external iliac arterial vascular clamp, and finally by the proximal external iliac artery vascular clamp. For a right kidney allograft with renal vein reconstructed, a Cooley pediatric vascular clamp is placed above the reconstructed IVC. The external iliac vein clamp is subsequently released, and assuming no bleeding, the Cooley clamp is removed.

After kidney reperfusion, attention is paid to any ureteral blood flow demarcation. The transplanted ureter is also trimmed to avoid any rotation or kinking, allowing for a tension-free anastomosis with the mobilized bladder.

The extravesical ureteral anastomosis is another modification, whereby we use our own (previously described) ureteral reimplantation technique5. Briefly, the bladder is gravity filled again with normal saline-containing antibiotic solution and Foley catheter clamping. A 3 cm cystostomy slightly oblique on the anterolateral wall of the bladder dome is performed, exposing the bladder mucosa with detrusor layer (BMDL). The transplanted ureter is posteriorly spatulated by about 2 cm. The ureter to BMDL anastomosis is then performed using a 6-0 polydioxanone suture (PDS) starting from the heel of the ureter. Its heel (proximal) and apex (distal) are anchored to the BMDL at almost full thickness. Two stay sutures with 6-0 PDS are placed laterally and medially anchored to the BMDL to keep the ureter open. The ureteral anastomosis is then performed in a running fashion manner with 6-0 PDS, first for the lateral wall and then for the medial wall including bladder mucosa and some detrusor layer (Fig. 5). Once the ureteral anastomosis is completed, an antireflux tunnel is performed using the remaining detrusor muscle and closed with interrupted sutures of 4-0 PDS. The last step before closing is to perform hemostasis and check for any bleeding, proceeding with the abdominal wall closure if no bleeding is observed.

Drawing of the ureteral anastomosis to the bladder mucosa with some detrusor layers, and creation of an antireflux tunnel using the remaining detrusor muscle. Bladder incision is 3 cm, and the ureteral spatulation is 2 cm. The right lower circle (Tunneling) shows the right angle introduced into the tunnel once finished, making sure that is not too tight.

The final modification of our technique is that there is no initial placement of a ureteral stent or surgical drainage, as they are no longer considered to be required following implementation of the other modifications. Additionally, the Foley catheter is removed on postoperative day 4–5 as long as there are no bladder issues5.

Statistical analysis

All patients were followed for at least 12 months post-transplant. The primary outcome was the occurrence of a urologic, vascular, and/or other surgical complication (with each type also considered separately) during the first 12 months post-transplant. Secondary outcomes included the development of a urinary tract infection (UTI) (or recurrent UTI) requiring hospitalization during the first 12 months post-transplant, and graft loss (death-uncensored) during the first 12 months post-transplant. Graft loss was defined as the date of graft failure (return to permanent dialysis, graft nephrectomy, or retransplant) or death, whichever occurred first.

Frequency distributions were determined for baseline categorical variables, and mean along with standard error (SE) were calculated (along with median and range) for baseline continuous variables. Tests of association between baseline variables and the primary and secondary outcomes were performed using Pearson (uncorrected) χ 2 tests and log-rank tests.

Urologic complications

The observed percentage of patients that developed a urologic complication was 2.3% (16/707); 14/16 were adults, and 12/16 received a DD kidney. The percentage developing either an ischemic/necrotic ureter or a ureteral leak was 0.8% (6/707); their times-to occurrence were 0.1 months, 0.6 months, 1.1 months, 1.3 months, 1.7 months, and 2.9 months post-transplant, respectively. The first patient had an ischemic ureter (with ureteral necrosis) at 0.1 months post-transplant and was successfully treated with ureteral reimplantation and stent placement. The next two patients developed ureteral leaks (with ureteral necrosis) at 0.6 months and 1.1 months post-transplant and were successfully treated with ureteral reimplantation and stent placement. The 4th patient developed a ureteral leak mid-ureter at 1.3 months post-transplant which was treated with stent placement. This patient subsequently developed a ureteral obstruction/stricture at 5.7 months post-transplant which extended from the UPJ to UVJ due to fibrous tissue surrounding the ureter; this patient was successfully treated with surgical dissection of the fibrous tissue, ureteral reimplantation, and nephroureteral stent placement. The fifth patient developed a ureteral leak at the UVJ at 1.7 months post-transplant and subsequently developed a ureteral stricture at the UVJ one month later (at 2.7 months post-transplant); both events were successfully treated by interventional radiology (IR) (the ureteral leak with a temporary percutaneous nephroureteral stent placement, and the ureteral stricture with ballon dilatation and nephroureteral catheter placement). The sixth patient developed a ureteral leak at 2.9 months post-transplant and was successfully treated by nephroureteral catheter placement.

Four additional patients developed a ureteral stricture. The first patient developed ureteral strictures at the UVJ at 0.6 months and then again at 7.6 months post-transplant; both strictures were treated with balloon-plasty and nephroureteral catheter placement. This patient was finally diagnosed as having multiple kidney stones that were occluding the distal ureter at 9.4 months post-transplant and was successfully treated by surgical removal of the kidney stones and ureteral reimplantation. The second patient developed a ureteral obstruction/stricture at 1.2 months post-transplant which extended from the UPJ to UVJ due to fibrous tissue surrounding the ureter and was successfully treated with surgical dissection of the fibrous tissue, ureteral reimplantation, and nephroureteral stent placement. The third patient had malrotation of the transplanted kidney at 2.8 months post-transplant, causing strictures to occur at the UPJ and UVJ, which was successfully treated by surgical repositioning of the allograft and nephroureteral stent placement. The fourth patient developed a mid-ureteral stricture at 4.8 months post-transplant, which was successfully treated by IR with dilatation and nephroureteral stent placement.

Two additional patients experienced grade 4 VUR at 5.1 months and 12.0 months post-transplant, with the second patient also having poor bladder compliance. The first patient was successfully treated with deflux injection into the ureter along with ureteral stent placement. The second patient, who was 3 years of age at transplant, was surgically treated with bladder augmentation, including ureter, ileum, and ileal Monti Mitrofanoff. Two other patients experienced UPJ obstruction at 4.4 months and 10.9 months post-transplant; both patients were successfully treated with dilatation and nephroureteral stent placement. One additional patient experienced rupture of the ureteral anastomosis and bladder at 0.2 months post-transplant which was successfully treated by ureteral reimplantation and bladder closure. Finally, one patient who developed a urinoma and suspected ureteral leak (but not found) at 2.6 months post-transplant was successfully treated with nephroureteral stent placement.

In total, surgical repair with ureteral reimplantation was required in 1.0% (7/707) of patients. Surgical repair was required in two additional cases (allograft repositioning in one case; bladder augmentation in one case). The other seven patients who developed a urologic complication during the first 12 months post-transplant were successfully treated by IR. Of note, none of the 16 patients who developed a urologic complication experienced graft failure or died during the first 12 months post-transplant as a direct consequence of their urologic complication(s).

Vascular complications

The observed percentage of patients who developed a vascular complication during the first 12 months post-transplant was 2.7% (19/707). Two patients experienced transplant RAS at the anastomosis level, in one LD recipient and one DD recipient. The allograft in the DD recipient had two renal arteries without a carrel patch which were anastomosed together side-to-side with 7-0 Prolene, with the superior renal artery developing stenosis. Both patients were successfully treated, with balloon angioplasty for the LD recipient and stent placement for the DD recipient.

Ten patients developed proximal RAS with patent renal artery anastomosis. Two additional patients had kinking of one of the branches of the main renal artery, and three additional patients had stenosis of one of the branches of the main renal artery. Balloon angioplasty and/or stent placement was successfully performed in all 15/19 cases.

One patient experienced renal artery and renal vein thrombosis with simultaneous graft failure (transplant nephrectomy) at 0.3 months post-transplant. None of the other 18 patients developed graft failure or died as a consequence of their vascular complication. In 2/19 cases, a thrombectomy was performed (including the graft failure case). One additional patient who developed renal vein thrombosis and abdominal compartment syndrome at day 1 post-transplant had successful surgical repair (reanastomosis of the left renal vein and abdominal wall closure with a mesh).

Other surgical complications

Overall, 8.8% (62/707) developed one (or more) of the other surgical complications during the first 12 months post-transplant. The observed percentage of patients who developed a wound complication, lymphocele, peri-renal hematoma, and peri-renal collection during the first 12 months post-transplant was 1.3% (9/707), 0.3% (2/707), 2.3% (16/707), and 5.9% (42/707), respectively. Two patients who developed a wound complication (at 3.6 months and 4.4 months post-transplant) had an abdominal wall infection caused by mycobacterium abscessus at the peritoneal dialysis catheter removal site; both patients ultimately had successful surgical repair requiring multiple abdominal wall debridements. Two additional patients required a transplant nephrectomy as a direct consequence of their surgical complications: in one patient at 2 days post-transplant due a large peri-renal hematoma occurring along with lower pole rupture (events possibly arising as a complication of the pretransplant donor biopsy), and in one patient at 3.3 months post-transplant due to development of a Page kidney caused by a large peri-renal hematoma arising immediately following the performance of a clinically indicated kidney allograft biopsy at 2.7 months post-transplant. None of the other 58 patients who developed one (or more) other surgical complications during the first 12 months post-transplant required surgical repair.

Any (urologic, vascular, or other) surgical complication

The observed percentage of patients who developed any (urologic, vascular, or other) surgical complication was 12.3% (87/707). As stated above, 3/87 developed graft failure (requiring graft nephrectomy) as a direct consequence of their surgical complications (vascular in one case; peri-renal hematoma in two cases). Additionally, 11/87 had successful surgical repair of their surgical complication (urologic in nine cases; wound infection in two cases). In total, 2.0% (14/707) of patients required surgical repair.

UTIs

The percentage of patients who developed a UTI requiring hospitalization during the first 12 months post-transplant was 11.2% (79/707). In addition, the observed percentage who developed a recurrent UTI requiring hospitalization during the first 12 months post-transplant was 3.5% (25/707). Of note, 18.8% (3/16) of patients who developed a urologic complication subsequently developed a UTI requiring hospitalization during the first 12 months post-transplant; this percentage was only marginally higher than the overall incidence (11.2%).

Graft loss

The percentage of patients who developed graft loss during the first 12 months post-transplant was 5.8% (41/707), with 1.7% (12/707) experiencing death-censored graft failure, and 4.1% (29/707) experiencing death with a functioning graft. Among the 87 patients who developed any surgical complication, 8.0% (7/87) subsequently (or simultaneously) developed death-censored graft failure during the first 12 months post-transplant (somewhat higher compared with the overall incidence of 1.7%). Additionally, 11.5% (10/87) died with a functioning graft during the first 12 months post-transplant (again, somewhat higher compared with the overall incidence of 4.1%).

Discussion

The risk of developing a surgical complication following kidney transplantation has continued to be a major concern. Over the years, one transplant surgeon at our center started modifying the conventional kidney transplant technique with two main goals: to make the transplant surgery easier to perform, and to avoid or minimize the incidence of post-transplant urologic, vascular, and other surgical complications. Since 2014, his modified version of the conventional kidney transplant surgical technique was utilized in 707 consecutive kidney-alone transplant recipients. The observed percentage of patients in this rather large observational study who developed any surgical complication during the first 12 months post-transplant was exceptionally low, 12.3% (87/707), with 2.3% (16/707), 2.7% (19/707), and 8.8% (62/707) of patients developing a urologic, vascular, and other surgical complication (lymphocele, wound complication, peri-renal hematoma, and peri-renal collection), respectively. Surgical repair was required in only 2.0% (14/707) of patients.

The standard technique involves extensive dissection of lymphatic tissue along the entire length of the external iliac artery, from the inguinal ligament to the internal iliac artery - sometimes this dissection also includes the common and/or internal iliac artery7–10. The goal in performing such extensive dissection is to allow rotation of the kidney allograft along its longitudinal axis, but such dissection could be associated with increased risks of lymphocele development and fluid collection16. Metal vascular clamps or Fogarty hydragrip clamps are still conventionally used in performing the renal artery/vein anastomoses – these types of vascular clamps could be associated with vascular injuries21.

Conventionally, the bladder is mobilized after reperfusion, but there are space constraints because of the kidney allograft’s presence. In this scenario, only a small portion of the bladder dome is exposed, and the ureteral anastomosis is performed mostly by a modification of the Politano-Leadbetter17 or Lich-Gregoir technique18,19.

Releasing the proximal external iliac vascular clamp first could cause arterial dissection or injury to the proximal external iliac artery; therefore, we release the distal external iliac artery vascular clamp first. High blood pressure needs to be achieved before kidney reperfusion20, and by releasing the proximal vascular clamp first, an increased risk of dissection of the iliac artery may exist while the distal vascular clamp is still in place. There are also predisposing factors, including arteriosclerosis, hypertension, and trauma caused by vascular clamping and suturing21. None of our patients had an injury or dissection of the external iliac artery.

Transplant RAS is a vascular complication that can cause allograft dysfunction. Our incidence of its occurrence was very low, as only two patients developed RAS at the anastomosis site. An additional 10 patients developed proximal RAS, and 3 patients developed stenosis in one of the branches of the main transplant renal artery. Each RAS case was successfully treated with percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and/or stenting. Many risks factors are associated with this pathology22 – traction trauma during kidney retrieval can cause intimal trauma, also fibrosis, hyperplasia, and immunological risk of the recipient22,23.

Mangus and Haag1 in their meta-analysis demonstrated a significant difference in the post-transplant incidence of urologic complications based on randomized, controlled studies of 796 patients who received ‘routine’ vs. ‘no routine’ ureteral stent placement at kidney transplant and extravesical ureteroneocystostomy: 1.5% (6/407) vs. 9.0% (35/389). Mangus and Haag1 also reported a difference in urologic complication incidence among 13 322 patients from 44 cohort (nonrandomized) studies who received ‘routine’ vs. ‘no routine’ ureteral stent placement at kidney transplant and extravesical ureteroneocystostomy: 3.2% (137/4,245) vs. 4.8% (433/9,077). The observed low incidence of 1.5% among randomized patients receiving ‘routine’ ureteral stent placement may therefore serve as a high standard for assessing results from other studies. Our observed incidence of urologic complications, 2.3% (16/707) during the first 12 months post-transplant (with no initial stent placement), therefore compares favorably with their reported 9.0% among randomized patients not receiving routine stent placement (as well as when compared with both of their nonrandomized subgroups), and was also close to the 1.5% incidence observed among randomized patients who received routine stent placement.

UTIs account for ~42–75% of all infections occurring postkidney transplant and 30% of recipients admitted with sepsis24. Abbott et al.25 reported a cumulative UTI incidence of 17% during the first 6 months post-transplant. Therefore, our UTI incidence of 11.2% (79/707) requiring hospitalization during the first 12 months post-transplant was acceptably low. With only 3.5% (25/707) of our cohort developing a recurrent UTI during the first 12 months post-transplant, these low UTI incidences could be related (at least in part) to the absence of routine ureteral stent placement5 and early Foley catheter removal26.

Again, we want to emphasize the importance of mobilizing the bladder which allows greater mobility in case the kidney is pushed upward during abdominal wall closure. We also want to emphasize the technical aspect of including a layer of detrusor along with the bladder mucosa which adds strength to the ureteral anastomosis, along with anchoring the ureter distally and proximally to almost full thickness of the bladder.

An additional advantage in performing an initial stent-less ureteral anastomosis is that no patients required an extra surgical procedure simply for such stent removal. With the technical advantages offered by our surgical modifications of the conventional kidney transplant technique, and our results, we believe that further investigation of these modified techniques is now warranted.

One study limitation was the fact that our results were not based on a randomized controlled study, nor was there an available control group of patients at our center who received the conventional kidney transplant technique. The favorable results reported here could also be confounded with the fact that a single transplant surgeon (with many years of experience) performed each of the 707 consecutive transplant surgeries in this report. Nonetheless, we believe our simple-to-perform modified techniques could easily be adopted by other transplant surgeons, with similarly favorable results also likely.

In summary, we believe that the advantages in using this modified kidney transplant technique, that is, the parietal peritoneum is peeled off from the abdominal wall to create a 1 cm flap, making the abdominal wall closure easier to perform at the end of the procedure, mobilized bladder first, limited dissection of the lymphatic tissue, using plastic bulldog arterial vascular clamps, renal artery anastomosis first to avoid the flip-flop maneuver, longer spatulation of the ureter, and inclusion of bladder mucosa along with detrusor muscle layer in the ureteral anastomosis, have clearly helped in lowering the post-transplant risk of developing of any surgical (including urologic and vascular) complications. Importantly, these favorable results were achieved without the need for initial ureteral stent placement or surgical drainage.
