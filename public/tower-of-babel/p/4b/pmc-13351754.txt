A multi-level charged micelle-based microneedle transdermal system for immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment regulation and synergistic therapy
Authors: Lijing Qin, Yongyi Bi, Xiu Wang, Tongjuan Liang, Wenzhong Li, Wanjun Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The presence of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in breast cancer impedes the effective delivery of inducible drugs to the tumor core and hinders immune cells from penetrating the vascular wall to enter the tumor, severely limiting the therapeutic efficacy of drugs that induce immunogenic cell death in cancer cells. In this study, we creatively designed a composite micelle (P2SM@Sun) featuring a terminal-charged “hydrophobic-hydrophilic-hydrophobic” tri-segmented structure. Combined with microneedle transdermal delivery technology, we developed a novel drug delivery system (PSD MN) capable of achieving efficient combined delivery of Sun and Dox at breast tumor sites for precise synergistic therapy. This system can achieve the combined delivery and precise controlled release of Dox and Sun at tumor sites. Among them, sunitinib enhances the ability of doxorubicin to induce immunogenic cell death, maximizes the release of DAMP, activates the systemic immune response, promotes the infiltration of CD8+ T cells, and stimulates the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IFN-γ), demonstrating significant synergistic anti-tumor activity. Overall, this work establishes a highly efficient and safe transdermal co-delivery platform for the treatment of breast cancer. By leveraging synergistic immune activation mechanisms, it offers broad application prospects for breast cancer therapy.

Introduction

Breast cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignant tumors in women [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Its aggressive nature, high metastatic risk, reliance on chemotherapy, and poor prognosis continue to present formidable challenges in treatment [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The persistent struggle to overcome major obstacles posed by solid tumors drives researchers to explore increasingly innovative therapeutic approaches. Among these, inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) in cancer cells emerges as a promising novel strategy for tumor treatment [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. ICD is not simple apoptosis; it is characterized by the release of a series of “danger signaling molecules”—damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMP)—from dying tumor cells. These include surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT), high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), and the release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). These signals promote the uptake of tumor antigens by dendritic cells (DCs) and trigger the activation of antigen-specific T cells [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. The systemic antitumor immune response triggered by ICD further enhances the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), promotes CD8+ T cell infiltration, and inhibits tumor cell growth [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Doxorubicin (Dox) has been reported as a potent inducer of such ICD [[26], [27], [28]], demonstrating significant tumor suppression in multiple cancer treatments [29,30]. Notably, anthracycline drugs like Dox face strict dosage limitations in clinical practice. Excessive doses cause severe systemic side effects such as cardiotoxicity and bone marrow suppression and readily induce drug resistance. However, studies indicate that conventional Dox dosages yield suboptimal therapeutic outcomes in breast cancer treatment [31]. This underperformance stems from the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) in breast cancer—characterized by hypoxia and high interstitial pressure—which impedes drug delivery to the tumor core and hinders immune cell infiltration through the vascular barrier [[32], [33], [34]]. This underscores the imperative to modify the immunosuppressive TME and overcome constraints on ICD induction.

In response to these challenges, combination therapy strategies may demonstrate significant potential [35,36]. In oncology, inhibiting angiogenesis initially appeared to be an effective “starvation” strategy against tumors. However, reality proved more complex. Recent clinical studies revealed that angiogenesis inhibition alone fails to sustain long-term tumor suppression and may even induce drug resistance or immune escape [[37], [38], [39]]. Initial anti-angiogenic drugs were designed to sever the tumor blood supply and halt growth. Yet research increasingly reveals that appropriately dosed anti-angiogenic agents can remodel vascular structures, restoring their initial function and alleviating immunosuppressive TME factors like hypoxia and high stromal pressure—thereby reactivating the immune system's anti-tumor capabilities [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Sunitinib (Sun), a prototypical anti-angiogenic inhibitor, is widely used in treating multiple cancers [[44], [45], [46]]. Furthermore, studies indicate that Sun can reduce infiltration of immunosuppressive cells like regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and enhancing tumor immunity [47,48]. Therefore, combining DOX (which induces ICD) with Sun (which modulates the TME) may create synergistic effects in signal transduction and TME barrier penetration, potentially unleashing potent antitumor immune responses.

However, implementing this combination therapy strategy in clinical practice still faces significant challenges. First, Sun exhibits extremely poor water solubility and low oral bioavailability. Systemic administration often fails to achieve effective therapeutic concentrations at tumor sites, while excessive dosing may induce adverse effects such as fatigue, diarrhea, and hand-foot syndrome [49,50]. Similarly, Dox has relatively highwater solubility but is constrained by dose limitations and restricted tumor site accumulation. Excessive use may also induce toxic effects such as cardiotoxicity and bone marrow suppression [[51], [52], [53]]. Therefore, developing a combined drug delivery system that precisely and efficiently delivers both drugs to breast tumor sites—maximizing synergistic effects while minimizing systemic exposure—is key to fully leveraging drug efficacy for effective breast cancer treatment.

In this study, we creatively designed a composite micelle material featuring a terminally charged “hydrophobic-hydrophilic-hydrophobic” tripartite structure. By integrating microneedle transdermal delivery technology, we developed an efficient and precise drug delivery system capable of achieving combined Sun and Dox administration at breast tumor sites for synergistic therapy (Fig. 1). First, addressing Sun's hydrophobicity and positive charge, we prepared sulfonated 11-mercaptoundecanoic acid (MUA) by mimicking heparin sodium. This sulfonated MUA was then linked via a disulfide bond to a negatively charged hydrophobic moiety and a hydrophilic segment (PEG), yielding a triblock amphiphilic polymer chain (P2SM) with end-charged segments. The micelles self-assembled from this chain significantly enhance the loading capacity for poorly soluble Sun through the mechanism of opposite charges attracting each other, owing to the negative charge carried by its hydrophobic end. They also perfectly improve its solubility and stability. Building upon this, we further encapsulated Sun-loaded micelles (P2SM@Sun) alongside water-soluble Dox within hyaluronic acid (HA)—a material characterized by rapid dissolution, excellent biocompatibility, and high strength. Using polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as the substrate, we developed a combined delivery microneedle transdermal delivery system: P2SM@Sun/Dox (PSD) MN. This microneedle delivery system features a high aspect ratio, strong penetration capability, and minimal invasiveness. Upon insertion into the breast tumor site, HA dissolves, releasing Dox and P2SM@Sun at the tumor location. Within the tumor microenvironment's high GSH conditions, the disulfide bonds in the P2SM@Sun scaffold break, releasing the loaded Sun. Sun exerts antitumor effects while simultaneously improving the immunosuppressive TME. Within this enhanced TME, once immunosuppression is lifted, Dox fully activates as an ICD inducer. It triggers ICD in breast cancer cells, thereby releasing DAMP that promotes DC maturation. This process increases CD8+ T cell activity, ultimately enhancing synergistic therapeutic efficacy against breast cancer. Methods

Characterization of P2SM polymers

The ability of polymers to self-assemble into micelles is crucial for achieving drug delivery. First, the P2SM polymer and intermediate products were analyzed using 1H NMR and IR spectroscopy. Additionally, the critical micelle concentration (CMC) of P2SM was determined using a fluorescent probe method. Specifically, a 1.0 × 10−3 mol L−1 standard solution of pyrene in methanol was prepared. Fifty microliters of this standard pyrene solution were dispensed into eight 15 mL centrifuge tubes. Under nitrogen purging, methanol was evaporated. Subsequently, 10 mL of P2SM test solutions at varying concentrations were added to the eight centrifuge tubes, resulting in the following concentrations: 4 × 10−1, 3 × 10−1, 2 × 10−1, 1 × 10−1, 5 × 10−2, 1 × 10−2, 5 × 10−3, 1 × 10−3, 5 × 10−4, and 1 × 10−4 mg/mL. Sonicate all 8 tubes for 30 min, then let them stand for 24 h. Measure the excitation wavelengths at 333 nm and 339 nm for each sample using a fluorescence spectrophotometer with excitation at 373 nm. Finally, calculate the I339/I333 ratio, plot the curve using LogC, and fit to obtain the CMC.

Preparation and characterization of P2SM@Sun micelles

Successful loading of hydrophobic drugs into micelle cores is a prerequisite for drug delivery, necessitating the preparation of drug-loaded micelles. GSH-sensitive P2SM@Sun micelles were prepared via the dialysis method. Briefly, 15 mg of P2SM polymer and 1 mg of Sun were dissolved in 5 mL DMSO. The mixture was placed in a dialysis bag and dialyzed for 48 h to remove unencapsulated Sun and solvent, yielding P2SM@Sun micelles. P1SM@Sun, P3SM@Sun, P6SM@Sun, and P2SMUn@Sun micelles were prepared using the same procedure. P2SM and P2SMUn micelles were prepared similarly but without the Sun addition. To quantify Sun loading within micelles, the micelles were diluted in methanol, and Sun concentration was measured at 431 nm using UV-visible spectrophotometry. The EE was calculated as m1/m2 × 100%, and the DL was calculated as m1/(m2 + m3) × 100%, where m1 represents the drug-loaded mass of the carrier, m2 represents the total drug mass added, and m3 represents the total mass of the carrier. Furthermore, to verify Sun loading in the prepared micelles, UV-Vis absorption spectra of P2SM, P2SM@Sun, and Sun were measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Additionally, micelle particle size and morphology were characterized. Particle size and zeta potential of blank and drug-loaded micelles were determined via dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Zeta Nanosizer. Morphology was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

The dissolution of micelles in response to high-concentration GSH environments, thereby releasing the drug, is a prerequisite for therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, dialysis was employed to investigate Sun release from P2SM@Sun micelles under varying GSH concentrations. Specifically, P2SM@Sun micelles were placed in dialysis bags (MWCO 3.5 kDa) and immersed in 0.1 M PBS buffer containing 1% (w/v) Tween 80 and various GSH concentrations (0 mM, 5 mM, and 10 mM). At predetermined time points, equal volumes of medium were removed from the samples and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. Sun release from the micelles was measured using UV spectrophotometry. The cumulative release formula (Er) was Ve∑1n−1Ci+VoCnm. Here, Ve represents the displaced PBS volume, Vo denotes the total released medium volume, Ci is the concentration of the released solution at the i-th displacement sampling, m is the total drug mass loaded into the micelle, and n is the number of PBS displacements. To further validate that micelle disintegration results from disulfide bond cleavage in the presence of GSH, infrared spectroscopy was employed to detect the IR of disulfide bonds in P2SM@Sun after 24 h exposure to 0 mM and 10 mM GSH, respectively. Additionally, to examine micelle size changes under varying GSH concentrations, DLS was employed to measure the size distribution of P2SM@Sun after 24-h exposure to 0, 5, and 10 mM GSH environments.

Micelle stability impacts drug delivery efficacy; thus, stability was assessed by measuring the particle size of P2SM@Sun micelles. To simulate biological conditions, P2SM@Sun micelles were placed in PBS (0.01M, pH = 7.4) containing fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10%, v/v) and stirred at 37°C for 10 days. At days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 10, 100 μL aliquots were removed for DLS measurement, with triplicate samples tested.

Anti-breast cancer activity assessment in vitro

To determine the optimal combination ratio for the two drugs, their combination index (CI) values were measured using the CCK-8 assay. The 4T cancer cell line was further employed for in vitro cytotoxicity studies. 4T1 cells were seeded at 5000 cells/well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight to allow cell attachment. Cells were then treated with different concentrations (0.1–12 μg/mL) of Dox:Sun (ratios of 5:1, 3:1, 1:1, 1:3, and 1:5), with five parallel samples per group. After 24 h incubation, 10 μL CCK-8 was added to each well. Incubation continued in a CO2 incubator for 2 h, followed by measurement of absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1) to calculate cell viability. Compusyn was used to calculate the CI from the obtained results. To investigate the effects of different formulations on cell viability, 4T1 and MCF-7 cancer cell lines, along with HUVEC cells, were further used in in vitro cytotoxicity studies with various formulations. The procedure followed the steps described above, except cells were treated with different concentrations (0.1–12 μg/mL) of free Sun, P2SM, P2SM@Sun, free DOX, and PSD micelles, with five parallel samples per group. Untreated cells served as the blank group, while cells treated only with CCK-8 constituted the control group. Cell viability was calculated as: Cell viability = (As - Ab)/(Ac - Ab) × 100%, whereas represents the absorbance of the experimental group, Ab is the absorbance of the blank group, and Ac is the absorbance of the control group.

The ability of micelles to enter cells and release drugs intracellularly impacts therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, to evaluate micelle internalization and intracellular drug release behavior, 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with C6- or NR-labeled P2SM@Sun micelles in serum-free medium, followed by observation at designated time points. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for observation under an inverted fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS IX83). For MCF-7 cells, a scratch assay was additionally performed to evaluate PSD's inhibitory effect on cell migration. In brief, a linear scratch was created on confluent cell monolayers in 6-well plates. After washing, cells were incubated with various formulations, including blank medium, free Sun, P2SM, P2SM@Sun, free DOX, or PSD micelles. Cell migration toward the wound area was monitored and measured at designated time points using an inverted microscope.

Investigation of the mechanism of in vitro anti-breast cancer activity

Investigate the ability of different formulations to induce ICD in 4T1 cells by measuring CRT, HMGB1 exposure, and ATP expression. For CRT and HMGB1 exposure assays, 4T1 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Serum-free medium containing either blank medium, free Sun, P2SM, P2SM@Sun, free DOX, or PSD micelles was added, followed by 24 h of incubation. Cells were then treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Next, add goat serum diluted 1:20 and incubate for 20 min. Incubate with anti-rat calreticulin antibody diluted 1:200 or HMGB1 antibody at 37°C for 1.5 h. Then incubate with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody diluted 1:300 at 37°C for 1 h. Incubate with DAPI for 10 min. Place the 96-well plate under an inverted fluorescence microscope for observation and imaging. Determine ATP expression levels in 4T1 cells treated with different formulations via ELISA.

Design of a microneedle drug delivery system

To achieve spatiotemporal synergy between two drugs and target the lesion site, microneedle-based drug delivery represents a promising approach. For preparing soluble microneedles, a female mold is first fabricated from a male mold. Subsequently, the drug solution is added to the female mold and fixed to form microneedles. The microneedles measure 1050 μm in length, with a base diameter of 480 μm and a tip space of 800 μm. A PSD solution was prepared, and 100 μL was pipetted into the mold. Centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 3 min was performed, repeated three times. A PVP solution (300 mg/mL) was then filled into the mold as the base. After drying, the PSD MN was peeled off. DOX MN and P2SM@Sun MN were prepared using the same procedure. To validate successful microneedle fabrication, morphology was first observed by photographing macroscopic images of microneedle patches. PSD MN morphology was examined via SEM, and the array structure was visualized using an inverted microscope. Additionally, drug loading was assessed by measuring the DL of the microneedle patch. Microneedle tips were collected from the patch using a surgical blade, dissolved in 100 μL ultrapure water, diluted with methanol, and analyzed by UV spectrophotometry to determine absorbance for drug loading calculation. The drug distribution within the microneedles was observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope. P2SM@Sun/C6/DOX MN prepared using C6 micelles was placed under an inverted fluorescence microscope to observe its fluorescence distribution. The morphology and particle size of P2SM@Sun after the micro-needle dissolution release process were evaluated. The MN needle tips of P2SM@Sun were scraped off with a scalpel and fully dissolved in ultrapure water. The morphology of the micelles was observed using TEM, and the particle size was detected by DLS.

Successful skin penetration and dissolution-release of microneedles are prerequisites for drug efficacy. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the dissolution behavior and skin interaction of microneedles post-insertion. To study the dissolution of the microneedle body, PSD MNs were inserted into mouse skin, and images were captured under an optical microscope at different dissolution time points. Additionally, to assess skin recovery, the abdominal skin of mice was pre-cleaned prior to the experiment. PSD MNs were inserted into the skin for 10 min, followed by image acquisition at different time points. To investigate MN insertion into mouse skin, PSD MNs were pressed into freshly excised mouse skin using thumb pressure. After 10 min of insertion, 0.4% trypan blue solution was applied to the skin for staining, followed by imaging of the stained skin. To further observe the effect of microneedles on skin at the microscopic level, H&E-stained skin sections were examined to visualize the delivery channels formed after microneedle insertion. Microneedles were removed 10 min after insertion into mouse skin. The skin was fixed, and stained tissue sections were examined under an optical microscope.

Anti-breast cancer treatment in vivo

BALB/c mice weighing 16–18 g were maintained according to the standards of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the School of Pharmacy (Institute of Pharmaceutical Sciences), Shandong First Medical University. The mouse cancer model was established based on previous work [4]. The BALB/c mouse model bearing 4T1 tumors was established by subcutaneous implantation of 1 × 106 cells (in 100 μL PBS) into the right fourth mammary fat pad. When tumors reached an appropriate size, mice were randomly assigned to five treatment groups (n = 5) receiving injections every other day for seven treatments: (i) PBS MN (control), (ii) Dox MN, (iii) P2SM@Sun MN, (iv) PSD MN, and (v) Injection PSD. Equivalent doses for Dox and Sun were 2 mg/kg and 0.67 mg/kg, respectively. Tumor volume and body weight were recorded every other day. Tumor volume (V) was calculated as V = a2b/2, where a and b represent the shortest and longest diameters, respectively. Tumor suppression rate (T0) was calculated as T0 = (1 - Vtreatment/Vcontrol) × 100%. To further investigate the retention and distribution of the drug in the body after micro-needle and tail vein injection administration, live imaging of mice was conducted regularly during the experiment to monitor the situation. Firstly, micro-needles labeled with rhodamine B (PSD/Rh MN) and the tail vein injection formulation (IV PSD/Rh) were prepared. After administration, small animal live optical imaging was performed on the mice at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h after administration.

To further investigate the therapeutic effects of different groups on breast cancer, mice were euthanized on day 14. Tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were collected for subsequent analysis. Sections of major organs underwent H&E staining and were examined microscopically to assess the safety profile of the formulations. Additionally, tumors were stained using H&E, TUNEL, Ki67, CRT, HMGB1, and CD8+, CD4+, FOXP3+, LY6G+, CD11B, CD3+ to further evaluate treatment efficacy. ATP, TNF-α, and IFN-γ levels were measured using kits. Mice were euthanized on day 14, and serum samples were collected to determine ATP, TNF-α, and IFN-γ concentrations.

Safety evaluation in vivo

To evaluate the safety of different groups in relation to breast cancer. Blood samples were collected from mice via orbital venipuncture and subjected to blood chemistry analysis. Renal safety was assessed through ALT, AST, and ALP tests, while hepatic safety was evaluated using UREA, CREA, and UA tests.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5. All results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). All statistical analyses were assessed using a one-way ANOVA test. Statistical significance is set to *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, with ns(p ≥ 0.05) indicating no significant difference.

Results

Synthesis and preparation of P2SM polymers

To achieve effective delivery of Sun to breast cancer tumor sites and enable precise release, we custom-designed the P2SM polymer specifically for this purpose, with its synthetic route illustrated in Fig. 2a. In this design, PEG serves as the hydrophilic moiety, while MUA functions as the hydrophobic terminal group. This configuration constructs the Tet PEG-SS-MUA-Sul (P2SM) polymer chain by linking MUA to both ends of PEG. First, PEG was linked to MUA via cystamine dihydrochloride in the chain design. This pre-embedded disulfide bridge structure within the backbone can cleave in tumor microenvironments with high GSH concentrations, conferring GSH-responsive properties based on disulfide bonds. Subsequently, we introduced sulfonated butenedioic acid at the MUA terminus via a Michael addition reaction. This endows the hydrophobic segment with a negative charge, enabling efficient loading of positively charged Sun molecules through the principle of heterocharging attraction. Ultimately, this enhances the drug's encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading capacity (DL). The chemical structures of both the intermediate and final products synthesized during the polymerization process of this delivery system were thoroughly validated via 1H NMR and infrared absorption spectroscopy (IR) (Fig. S1-6, 1b, c). The IR spectrum of the intermediate 3-butene-1,2-diol-SUL is shown in Fig. S6. This spectrum indicates the presence of absorption peaks at 1265–1244 cm−1 and 1400–1375 cm−1 attributed to -SO3- and S=O, respectively. Concurrently, the carbon-carbon double bond absorption peak at 1700–1583 cm−1 remains intact. This clearly indicates that the carbon-carbon double bond was retained during the reaction, confirming the successful synthesis of 3-butene-1,2-diol-Sul. The 1H NMR results for P2SM are shown in Fig. 2b. In this spectrum, the peak at 3.5 ppm indicates the characteristic peak of PEG, the peaks at 1.48 and 1.24 ppm indicate the characteristic peaks of MUA, the peaks at 2.76 and 2.99 ppm indicate the characteristic peaks of cystamine dihydrochloride, and the peaks at 5.06, 5.26, and 5.85 ppm indicate the characteristic peaks of sulfonated butenedioic acid. All these findings confirm the successful synthesis of P2SM. Additionally, the IR spectrum was employed to further validate the successful synthesis of P2SM, with the results shown in Fig. 2c. First, the weak absorption peak observed at 520–500 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of the disulfide bond (S–S), indicating the presence of a disulfide structure in P2SM. Simultaneously, characteristic peaks in the spectrum confirm the incorporation of key functional groups: the absorption peak at 1965–1720 cm−1 corresponds to the carbonyl group (-C=O), while the broad absorption peak at 1600–1540 cm−1 corresponds to the vibrations of the amino group (-NH) and cyano group (-CN). Furthermore, the enhanced absorption peak at 1265–1244 cm−1 confirms the presence of a sulfonic acid group (-SO3-). To optimize micelle formation, we systematically investigated the effects of solvent type, P2SM-to-Sun loading ratios (1 mg per batch), and P2SM molecular weight on drug EE and DL. First, to calculate Sun content, EE, and DL in the prepared micelles, we established a Sun standard curve based on the measurement of Sun's absorption peak at 431 nm in micelles of varying concentrations (Fig. S7). Previous studies have demonstrated that drug solubility influences carrier loading capacity [55]. Therefore, selecting an appropriate solvent is crucial. Here, we prepared two types of P2SM@Sun micelles using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and formamide as solvents, respectively, and determined their respective EE and DL, as shown in Figs. S8 and S9. Analysis of the results revealed that the highest EE achieved with DMSO as the solvent reached 50.11%, whereas the maximum EE obtained with formamide as the solvent was only 36.88%. Evidently, the DL and EE of P2SM@Sun micelles prepared with DMSO as the solvent were significantly higher than those prepared with the formamide system. This difference likely stems from Sun's superior solubility in DMSO. Consequently, DMSO was selected as the loading solvent for subsequent experiments.

Additionally, the loading ratio of Sun to the P2SM carrier is a key factor influencing drug EE and DL. Using DMSO as the loading solvent, we further investigated how different Sun-to-P2SM loading ratios affect drug EE and DL. Under a series of varying mass ratios of Sun to P2SM, we measured the EE and DL of the loaded Sun, with results shown in Figs. S8 and S9. These results indicate that the highest DL and EE (50.11% EE and 3.13% DL) were achieved at a P2SM:Sun ratio of 15:1, outperforming other ratios. Therefore, this ratio was determined to be optimal and was used for drug loading in subsequent experiments of this study. Additionally, the composition ratio of hydrophilic and hydrophobic segments in PSM polymers affects Sun's EE and DL. Thus, validation and optimization of these ratios are essential. Here, we synthesized a series of polymeric carriers—P1SM, P3SM, and P6SM—composed of different PEG hydrophilic segments (molecular weights: 1000, 2000, 3500, and 6000) with identical hydrophobic segments. Using these carriers to load Sun, we synthesized drug-loaded micelles P1SM@Sun, P2SM@Sun, P3SM@Sun, and P6SM@Sun. P2SM, P3SM, and P6SM and used them to synthesize drug-loaded micelles P1SM@Sun, P2SM@Sun, P3SM@Sun, and P6SM@Sun by loading Sun onto them. Subsequently, we determined the EE and DL of these drug-loaded micelles, with results shown in Fig. 3b, S10. These results indicate that the P2SM@Sun micelle (EE: 50.11%, DL: 3.13%) exhibits significantly higher EE and DL compared to the other micelles: P1SM@Sun (EE: 8.10%, DL = 0.51%), P3SM@Sun (EE = 13.48%, DL = 0.84%), and P6SM@Sun (EE = 21.22%, DL = 1.33%). This may be attributed to the superior hydrophilic-lipophilic balance at both ends of the polymer synthesized from this hydrophilic segment, resulting in enhanced drug binding capacity.

Subsequently, we measured the UV-visible absorption spectrum of P2SM@Sun micelles (Fig. 3c). The results revealed absorption peaks characteristic of both P2SM and Sun, conclusively demonstrating successful drug encapsulation within the delivery carrier. Therefore, micelles prepared using a polymer carrier with PEG2000 as the hydrophilic segment were selected for subsequent experiments. Additionally, the stability of drug loading onto polymer carriers is further influenced by the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged polymer carriers and drugs. Considering Sun's positively charged core, we sulfonated the polymer to impart a negative charge, thereby enhancing the binding strength between Sun and the P2SM carrier through electrostatic interactions between positive and negative charges. As a control, we prepared the unsulfonated polymer P2SMUn and loaded Sun onto it to form P2SMUn@Sun micelles. Measurements revealed that P2SMUn@Sun exhibited significantly lower EE (18.29%) and DL (1.14%) compared to the control. This result further confirms that the negative charge introduced by sulfonate groups enhances binding to positively charged drug molecules, thereby improving loading efficiency. In summary, the optimal parameters and preparation conditions for this delivery system involve using DMSO as the solvent, a P2SM: Sun feed ratio of 15:1, and PEG2000 to prepare the sulfonated P2SM.

Colloidal stability is a key quality attribute determining its clinical efficacy and safety. To further screen for optimal stability conditions of drug-loaded micelles, we examined the particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of P1SM@Sun, P3SM@Sun, P6SM@Sun, P2SM, P2SM@Sun, P2SMUn, and P2SMUn@Sun micelles (results were shown in Fig. 3d, e, S11, and 12). First, hydrodynamic particle size measurements revealed that all groups except P2SM@Sun exhibited larger hydrodynamic particle sizes (Fig. 3d, S11). This discrepancy likely stems from poorer polymer hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance in the non-Sun groups, leading to looser assembly structures and consequently larger particle sizes. Loading hydrophobic drugs onto the hydrophobic core enhances its compactness, thereby reducing the overall size—a finding consistent with previous reports [56,57]. Comparing results reveals that P2SM@Sun exhibits the smallest particle size compared to the hollow P2SM micelles, confirming that Sun loading increases micellar internal density. Based on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, micelles with sizes ranging from 10 to 200 nm can accumulate more effectively in solid tumor tissues [58]. Our measurements indicate that the hydrodynamic particle size of the prepared P2SM@Sun micelles is 105 ± 0.52 nm, demonstrating significant potential for tumor accumulation via the EPR effect. To further evaluate the uniformity of hydrodynamic particle size in these micelles, we measured the PDI across different groups, with results shown in Fig. S12. Analysis revealed that P2SM@Sun exhibited the lowest PDI (0.104) among all micelles, indicating relatively uniform particle size distribution. Furthermore, previous studies [59] have demonstrated that a higher negative surface charge reduces non-specific binding to negatively charged proteins in blood, thereby enhancing colloidal stability and circulation time in vivo. Indeed, gaining a deep understanding and effectively regulating the surface electrochemical properties of dispersed systems is central to addressing stability challenges in drug delivery carriers. Consequently, we measured the zeta potential across different groups (Fig. 3e) and compared it with other control groups. Through comparison, we found that the zeta potentials of P2SM@Sun were the lowest at −20.7 ± 0.36 mV, compared to −14.5 ± 0.26 mV for P2SMUn and −14.5 ± 0.26 mV for P2SM@Sun. The increased negative charge on P2SM@Sun further prevents binding to negatively charged proteins in blood for clearance, enhancing its stability in vivo. In summary, among all types of drug-loaded micelles, P2SM@Sun exhibits the highest EE and DL, the smallest hydrodynamic particle size and PDI, and the lowest zeta potential, making it the optimal drug-loaded micelle. Consequently, P2SM@Sun was selected as the reference micelle for subsequent experiments.

The morphology, responsive release characteristics, and storage stability of micelles are crucial parameters for drug-loaded micelles as drug delivery carriers and also serve as key indicators for evaluating micelle delivery capabilities. First, we characterized the morphology of P2SM@Sun using TEM (Fig. 3f). The TEM images revealed a distinct spherical morphology with a particle size of 105 ± 0.52 nm, exhibiting good uniformity. This indicates that the prepared product exhibits favorable quality control. Subsequently, we further evaluated the responsive release characteristics of P2SM@Sun. To assess drug release from P2SM@Sun in the presence of GSH, we prepared PBS solutions with a series of GSH concentration gradients (0, 5 mM, 10 mM). The drug-loaded nanomicelles were incubated in these PBS solutions, and the concentration of released Sun was measured at specific time points, as shown in Fig. 3g. The results indicate that Sun release varies significantly across different GSH concentrations, with cumulative release percentages after 24 h being 18.05% (0 mM GSH), 26.77% (10 μM GSH), 53.32% (5 mM GSH), and 87.67% (10 mM GSH). The sustained drug release under high GSH conditions also indicates that Sun is rapidly released upon entering the tumor, thereby achieving prompt therapeutic effects. Furthermore, to verify that P2SM@Sun releases the drug through GSH-responsive mechanisms, we exposed P2SM@Sun to 0 mM GSH and 10 mM GSH solutions, respectively, and measured the IR of disulfide bonds in these solutions after 24 h. As shown in Fig. 3h, the disulfide bond absorption peak at 510 cm−1 was observable in P2SM@Sun without GSH. However, this peak disappeared in high-GSH conditions, indicating disulfide bond cleavage. This precisely demonstrates the GSH-responsive release characteristics of P2SM@Sun at elevated concentrations. Additionally, we measured the micelle size changes of P2SM@Sun after 24 h exposure to 0, 5, and 10 mM GSH solutions (Fig. 3i). The results revealed that P2SM@Sun exhibited a sharp single peak in the absence of GSH. However, under high GSH concentrations, the particle size increased, and a double peak appeared, indicating that P2SM@Sun underwent decomposition in the high GSH environment. Furthermore, to validate the stability of P2SM@Sun, the micelles were stored in a simulated biological system environment. Their particle size distribution and stability were measured over specific time periods (Fig. 3j). Statistical analysis revealed minimal variation in micelle size within 6 days of storage. This clearly demonstrates the excellent storage stability of this nanomaterial system, providing crucial assurance for its integrity and functionality in in vivo applications. In summary, this study successfully constructed P2SM@Sun nanomicelles with regular morphology, pronounced GSH responsiveness, and excellent stability, laying a solid foundation for their efficacy and safety in subsequent in vivo and in vitro antitumor studies.

Investigation of the mechanism of in vitro anti-breast cancer activity

It has been reported that during the process of ICD, CRT generally translocates from the endoplasmic reticulum lumen to the cell membrane, HMGB1 is released from the nucleus into the cytoplasm, and ATP is released from the intracellular space to the extracellular space [16,62,63]. To investigate whether PSD combination therapy could induce immunogenic cell death in 4T1 breast cancer cells, we conducted a systematic evaluation through experiments designed to assess CRT, HMGB1, and ATP release (Fig. 5b, c, e-g). Results showed that compared to the free Sun group, the P2SM@Sun group exhibited significantly increased fluorescence intensity of CRT translocation from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cell membrane surface. Semi-quantitative analysis revealed an increase from 12.64 a.u. to 15.87 a.u. (Fig. 5b, e). This effect was attributed to P2SM coating facilitating drug release within tumor cells after P2SM@Sun uptake, thereby promoting CRT expression. Further, compared to the P2SM@Sun and free Dox groups, the PSD combination treatment group exhibited significantly enhanced CRT expression fluorescence intensity. Semi-quantitative analysis of the PSD group showed an increase to 47.57 a.u., with CRT expression levels markedly higher than any single-drug group. This indicates that combination therapy more effectively triggered endoplasmic reticulum stress and associated death signals. Concurrently, measurements of HMGB1 release from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (Fig. 5c, f) revealed markedly reduced HMGB1 fluorescence intensity at the nuclear location in the P2SM@Sun group compared to the free Sun group. Semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity showed a decrease from 47.70 a.u. to 39.30 a.u. This strongly indicated that P2SM coating facilitates tumor cell uptake of P2SM@Sun, and intracellular drug release promotes HMGB1 exposure from the nucleus. Furthermore, compared to the P2SM@Sun and free Dox groups, the PSD co-treatment group showed progressively reduced nuclear fluorescence intensity and semi-quantitative analysis, decreasing to 14.46 a.u. These findings indicate that P2SM coating enhances HMGB1 exposure in tumor cells, while the synergistic effect of both drugs further amplifies this exposure, ultimately yielding superior efficacy compared to monotherapy. Subsequently, ATP secretion levels in 4T1 cells were measured (Fig. 5g). Results revealed that there was significantly increased ATP secretion in the P2SM@Sun group compared to the free Sun group. The PSD group exhibited the highest extracellular ATP secretion levels, significantly outperforming both the free Dox and P2SM@Sun monotherapy groups. These findings demonstrate that P2SM encapsulation enhances P2SM@Sun's capacity to promote ATP release from tumor cells, with combined drug administration further elevating ATP secretion levels. The above findings demonstrate that P2SM@Sun, due to P2SM encapsulation, significantly increased CRT expression, HMGB1 exposure, and ATP release capacity in 4T1 cells. Concurrently, PSD co-administration synergistically enhanced ICD and markedly increased DAMP release, collectively providing experimental evidence for the drug's in vivo efficacy. In summary, this study demonstrates that P2SM coating facilitates P2SM@Sun cellular uptake and releases Sun for intracellular action. When Sun and Dox are co-administered, they effectively induce ICD by promoting CRT exposure, HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion, thereby enhancing immune responses. This provides experimental evidence and a research foundation for subsequent in vivo antitumor studies.

Design of microneedle drug delivery systems

As a local drug delivery system, MNs typically feature the ability to precisely deliver drugs to specific pathological sites for therapeutic effect [64,65]. A major advantage of MNs is their capacity to penetrate the skin's stratum corneum while avoiding stimulation of nerve endings, enabling pain-free drug delivery. Furthermore, the soluble matrix of MNs rapidly dissolves in the skin's interstitial fluid to release drugs, significantly enhancing local drug concentration while substantially reducing systemic toxicity risks. HA is an endogenous component of the human body and has been demonstrated to dissolve rapidly in interstitial fluid upon skin penetration. Therefore, in this study, we utilized HA as the needle matrix and PVP as the base material. Through multiple centrifugal molding techniques, we encapsulated Sun-loaded micelles (P2SM@Sun) and Dox together within the matrix to prepare a microneedle delivery system, as shown in Fig. 6a. Using this approach, we simultaneously prepared a series of microneedle delivery systems with different compositions, including PBS MN, Dox MN, P2SM@Sun MN, and PSD MN. To validate the structural integrity and drug loading capacity of this microneedle delivery system, we systematically characterized its morphological features and drug loading status using macro-imaging, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and optical microscopy, as shown in Fig. 6b, c, and S26. The results indicate that each microneedle patch contains a 15×15 array of needles, with an individual needle height of 1050 μm, a base diameter of 480 μm, and an inter-needle spacing of 800 μm. The needles exhibit a conical structure, providing sufficient mechanical strength to penetrate the skin barrier. Subsequently, for drug loading, we established a standard curve for Dox (Fig. S27) and quantified its content within the drug-loaded microneedle delivery system. After three cycles of centrifugal processing to promote drug distribution toward the needle tips, the loading capacities of Dox and Sun within the needles were determined to be 40 μg and 13 μg, respectively. Furthermore, fluorescence imaging was employed to visualize drug distribution based on Dox's intrinsic fluorescence and C6-labeled P2SM@Sun (Fig. 6d and e). Results revealed that both Dox and P2SM@Sun predominantly accumulated at the microneedle tips—a distribution pattern conducive to efficient drug delivery to tumor tissues. Meanwhile, we conducted TEM observations and particle size tests on the micelles after the complete dissolution and release of the microneedles. The results are shown in Fig. S28. The results indicate that after the drug-loaded microneedle delivery system was released, the size of the micelles slightly increased from 105 nm to 120 nm, but there was no obvious change in morphology, remaining in a regular spherical shape. This proves that the nano-delivery system has excellent structural stability in the microneedle matrix and physiological environment. These results demonstrate the successful development of a soluble microneedle-based combined drug delivery system with excellent mechanical properties and high drug loading capacity. Notably, drugs co-encapsulated within this delivery system are predominantly loaded at the needle tip, enabling combined, rapid, and precise drug administration. In vivo anti-breast cancer therapy

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of PSD MN in vivo, we established a BALB/c mouse model bearing 4T1 tumors and used this model to validate the antitumor effects of combined administration. First, we established a 4T1 breast cancer tumor mouse model and randomly divided it into the following five groups: (i) Control group (PBS MN), (ii) Dox MN group, (iii) P2SM@Sun MN group, (iv) PSD MN group, and (v) Intravenous injections PSD (I.V. PSD). As illustrated in Fig. 7a, each group received treatment every two days for a total of two weeks. Simultaneously, tumor size and mouse body weight were measured every two days. Results showed no significant weight differences among the MN treatment groups, whereas the I.V. PSD group exhibited weight loss (Fig. 7b), indicating good biocompatibility and safety of the MN treatment regimen. The tumor growth inhibition data revealed that tumors in the control group continued to grow rapidly, reaching 443.54 mm3 by day 14, while all treatment groups demonstrated varying degrees of antitumor effects. The PSD MN group exhibited the most pronounced tumor suppression, with tumors reaching only 26.93 mm3 by day 14, and its tumor growth curve was markedly lower than those of other groups (Fig. 7c–h). These findings also indicate that while Dox MN or P2SM@Sun MN alone can inhibit tumor growth, their therapeutic effects are inferior to the combined PSD MN treatment. Further, the tumor growth curve of the I.V. PSD group was higher than that of the PSD MN group, indicating that MN administration significantly outperformed the tail vein injection method. This discrepancy may arise because MN local administration delivers drugs directly to the tumor site for therapeutic effect, whereas tail vein injection distributes drugs systemically, resulting in lower tumor-specific drug concentrations and consequently weaker tumor growth inhibition. Besides, we statistically analyzed tumor dimensions in mice after two weeks of treatment (Fig. 7i). Results showed that tumors in the PSD MN group exhibited the smallest size compared to other treatment groups, indicating that the PSD MN group demonstrated the optimal tumor suppression effect. We also statistically analyzed the weight of excised tumors, and the results aligned with the aforementioned trend (Fig. 7j), further confirming that the PSD MN group possessed the most effective therapeutic outcome. Through further quantitative analysis and comparative evaluation, we found that compared to the control group, the PSD MN group achieved a final tumor growth inhibition rate of 93.93% (Fig. 7k). The Dox MN and P2SM@Sun MN groups exhibited inhibition rates of 82.45% and 72.74%, respectively. In contrast, the I.V. PSD group showed an inhibition rate of 72.15%, significantly lower than that of the PSD MN group. These results indicate that PSD MN achieves more efficient drug release at the tumor site through MN-mediated local drug delivery. Notably, upon rapid dissolution of the HA needle body, both Dox and P2SM@Sun are simultaneously released. Under high GSH concentrations in the TME, P2SM@Sun disintegrates to release Sun. The released Sun not only exerts antitumor effects but also improves the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Within this improved TME, once immunosuppression is lifted, Dox fully exerts its role as an ICD inducer, triggering ICD in breast cancer cells. This synergistic antitumor effect of both drugs ultimately yields significantly superior outcomes compared to monotherapy. The lower efficacy of the I.V. PSD group likely stems from its lack of tumor targeting, resulting in insufficient drug accumulation at the lesion site. Investigation of the mechanism of action against breast cancer in the body

To further evaluate the synergistic antitumor effects of PSD MN and the mechanisms underlying this enhancement, we performed necrosis and apoptosis analyses on tumor tissues. Post-treatment tumors underwent H&E staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assays. Observation of H&E sections revealed extensive necrosis in PSD MN tumors compared to Dox MN, P2SM@Sun MN, and I.V. PSD groups. TUNEL immunofluorescence analysis of tumor tissues revealed the strongest fluorescence signal in the PSD MN group (175.27 a.u.). Semi-quantitative analysis further confirmed significantly higher apoptosis levels in this group compared to Dox MN (67.65 a.u.), P2SM@Sun MN (51.79 a.u.), and I.V. PSD group (112.51 a.u.) (Fig. 8a, c, h). These findings indicate that PSD MN treatment indeed induces more extensive ICD in breast cancer cells, thereby effectively promoting tumor cell death. Additionally, immunohistochemical assessment of Ki67 serves as a crucial method for evaluating cell proliferation capacity [66]. When the Ki67 was applied to evaluate cell proliferation, a higher number of positive cells indicated more pronounced proliferative activity. Our Ki67 staining results are shown in Fig. 8b, g. Positive cells were widely distributed in the Control group (53.38%), while Ki67-positive areas decreased across all treatment groups. The PSD MN group exhibited the smallest positive area (5.41%). These findings clearly demonstrate that tumor suppression is closely linked to the ability to significantly inhibit tumor cell proliferation. To evaluate the in vivo biodistribution of the drug at the tumor site, we recorded the changes in fluorescence signals at different time points after administration in mice receiving different administration methods. The results are shown in Fig. S29. In the microneedle group, a strong fluorescence signal was detectable at the tumor site at 0 h after administration, and this signal gradually weakened over time. By 48 h after administration, the fluorescence intensity at the tumor site in the microneedle group had almost disappeared. In contrast, in the tail vein injection group, no obvious fluorescence signal was observed at the tumor site throughout the entire observation period, while obvious fluorescence signals were detected in normal organs. In summary, PSD MN delivered via microneedles can achieve efficient enrichment and long-term retention at the tumor site, confirming that microneedles are an excellent delivery strategy for local treatment of superficial tumors. The above results also indicate that compared to the single-drug treatment groups (Dox MN and P2SM@Sun MN), the PSD MN group demonstrated stronger effects in inducing tumor cell necrosis, promoting apoptosis, and inhibiting cell proliferation. This further confirms that the combination of Dox and Sun indeed produces a synergistic anti-tumor effect. Further, the tumor suppression efficacy of the PSD MN group was significantly superior to that of the I.V. PSD group. This phenomenon can be attributed to the microneedle-based delivery system's ability to deliver drugs directly to specific local pathological sites—namely, tumor locations—thereby achieving higher drug concentration enrichment and better therapeutic outcomes. In contrast, intravenous administration distributes drugs throughout the body, resulting in limited drug accumulation at tumor sites and consequently restricted therapeutic efficacy. In exploring the mechanisms of tumor suppression, examining the expression of ICD-related markers [[67], [68], [69]]—particularly the detection of three key molecules: CRT, ATP, and HMGB1—is crucial. In this study, we performed immunofluorescence assays on tumor models to detect CRT translocation and immunohistochemical assays to assess HMGB1 expression in vivo, with results shown in Fig. 8d, e, i, j. Results indicate that PSD MN group tumor cells exhibited the highest levels of CRT translocation and HMGB1 exposure among all groups. Additionally, ATP expression analysis (Fig. 8l) revealed significantly higher ATP release in the PSD MN group compared to other groups. Notably, Dox monotherapy alone induced ICD, and its combination with Sun further amplified this effect, indicating synergistic promotion of the ICD process. The ICD-mediated anti-tumor effect depends on the dynamic regulation of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) are key immunosuppressive cells, and their infiltration levels directly affect the formation of the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are the core effector immune cells that play a crucial role in the ICD-induced systemic anti-tumor immunity. Therefore, we detected the expression of relevant characteristic factors by immunofluorescence to clarify the expression status of these three immune cells. The result analysis (Fig. 7f, k, S30) shows that compared with the control group and the single drug treatment group, the PSD MN group presents significant immune cell infiltration characteristics: the positive signals of LY6G+ and CD11b+ are the weakest, suggesting that the number of MDSCs in this group is the least; the expression levels of CD4+ and FOXP3+ are the lowest, indicating that the infiltration degree of Tregs is significantly reduced; while the positive expressions of CD8+ and CD3+ are significantly upregulated, reflecting that the infiltration of CTLs is the most abundant. These findings demonstrate that, compared to the Dox MN and P2SM@Sun MN groups administered alone, the PSD MN group synergistically enhances the antitumor effects of Dox and Sun at the tumor site. Among them, Sun improved the immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby promoting the release of DAMPs by tumor cells induced by Dox. This, in turn, effectively reshaped the tumor immune microenvironment by reducing the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells and increasing the infiltration of effector T cells, amplifying the systemic anti-tumor immune response mediated by ICD, further verifying the immune remodeling effect of the tumor microenvironment and the immune activation effect of the synergistic treatment system. Besides, due to the direct delivery of drugs to the tumor region via local administration, PSD MN demonstrated significantly higher drug accumulation at the lesion site compared to the intravenously administered I.V. PSD group, further enhancing the therapeutic effect. In summary, PSD MNs achieve precise co-delivery through a combined delivery model. Leveraging the synergistic antitumor mechanism of Dox and Sun, they improve the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. This approach maximizes synergistic effects while minimizing systemic exposure, addressing both dose limitations and restricted tumor site enrichment. It also enhances the bioavailability of both drugs, demonstrating outstanding anti-tumor efficacy.

Cytokines secreted by immune cells also play a crucial role in regulating immune responses and enhancing T cell activation [[70], [71], [72], [73]]. In this study, during the PSD MN-induced ICD process, we further validated the induction of ICD and subsequent immune activation levels by measuring various cytokine levels in mice. When inducing antigen-specific immunity, immune cells release IFN-γ, a key activator of macrophages, leading to increased expression levels of this pro-inflammatory factor. Therefore, the induction of ICD and subsequent immune activation can be assessed by detecting serum levels of the cytokines TNF-α and IFN-γ using ELISA kits. As shown in Fig. 8m and n, PSD MN exhibited the highest levels of TNF-α (1343.75 pg/mL) and IFN-γ secretion (760.14 pg/mL) compared to other groups. Given the pivotal roles of these cytokines in T cell activation and anti-tumor immunity, their upregulation further substantiates the conclusion that PSD MN activates systemic immune responses by inducing ICD. These findings demonstrate that Sun not only exerts anti-tumor effects but also effectively modifies the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Within this modified microenvironment, its immunosuppressive properties are neutralized, allowing Dox to fully exert its role as an ICD inducer. This induces ICD in breast cancer cells, triggering the release of DAMP, promoting DC maturation, enhancing immune cell activity, and ultimately amplifying the synergistic therapeutic efficacy against breast cancer.

In vivo toxicity evaluation

Assessing the in vivo safety of PSD MNs [74,75] is also a crucial step. Following treatment completion, we performed H&E staining on heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney sections from mice treated with different materials. Additionally, serum biochemical analyses were conducted on breast cancer model mice across various groups to determine whether PSD MNs pose harm to major organs during their metabolic processes in vivo. First, in the serum biochemical analysis, we evaluated changes in levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) reflecting liver function, as well as urea (UREA), creatinine (CREA), and uric acid (UA) reflecting kidney function (Fig. 9a–f). Results showed significantly reduced ALP levels and markedly elevated UA levels in the I.V. PSD group, indicating potential hepatotoxicity and renal injury risks. Conversely, all MN treatment groups exhibited normal ranges for these markers with no evident toxic reactions. This discrepancy primarily stems from systemic toxicity caused by the non-specific distribution of drugs throughout the body following tail vein injection. In contrast, MNs enable targeted drug delivery to tumor regions through local administration, significantly enhancing drug concentration at the lesion site while limiting systemic exposure. These findings demonstrate that the microneedle drug delivery strategy not only exhibits favorable drug bioavailability but also offers distinct advantages in terms of biological safety. Additionally, H&E staining (Fig. 9g) revealed consistent histopathological findings in heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues across all treatment groups as expected. No morphological alterations were observed in the MN treatment group, indicating that local MN administration did not cause significant tissue damage. In contrast, the I.V. PSD group exhibited myocardial tissue disruption and disorganized arrangement, alongside cytoplasmic vacuolation in renal cells, suggesting cardiac and renal toxicity in this group. We found that, in contrast, the MN delivery method reduced cardiac and renal toxicity in the Dox MN and PSD MN groups. These results indicate that systemic drug distribution in the I.V. PSD group led to exposure of non-target organs, triggering toxic side effects. In contrast, the MN system maintains high drug concentrations in the tumor region through localized targeted delivery while minimizing systemic exposure, significantly enhancing treatment safety. Conclusion

In summary, we effectively developed a terminally charged “hydrophobic-hydrophilic-hydrophobic” (P2SM@Sun) ternary composite micelle material, with Sun encapsulation rate reaching 50.11%. Based on this material, we coupled microneedle technology to create a transdermal delivery system (PSD MN) for combination drug delivery and synergistic treatment. Leveraging the rapid dissolution of HA in microneedles and the GSH responsiveness of P2SM@Sun within the tumor microenvironment, this system achieves combined delivery and precise controlled release of Dox and Sun at the breast tumor site. When Dox and Sun were delivered together, Sun not only suppressed tumors but also significantly enhanced the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. This approach dramatically improved Dox's ICD impact, increased DAMP release, and consequently triggered systemic immune responses. This promoted CD8+ T cell infiltration and the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IFN-γ, demonstrating clear synergistic anti-tumor activity. Compared to conventional intravenous injection, PSD MN demonstrated superior safety while significantly inhibiting tumor growth. Overall, PSD MN represents an efficient and safe transdermal delivery platform. Through its synergistic immune activation mechanism, it offers broad application prospects for breast cancer therapy.
