Assessment of Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) of living surroundings and school environment among adolescent high school students in Northwest Ethiopia: mixed method study
Authors: Matyas Atnafu Alehegn, Mogessie Ashenafi, Messay Mulugeta, Nigatu Regassa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchool-based WASH programs in low- and middle-income countries showed significant impacts on health, nutrition and education. Poor WASH practices contributed to malnutrition, anemia, infections, and gender inequities, particularly among adolescents. Inadequate facilities, sociocultural and economic disparities are key challenges. This study aimed to assess the availability, quality, accessibility and practice of WASH in the living surroundings and school environments among adolescent high school students in the study area.MethodologyThis study employed a convergent explanatory mixed-method design across four high schools in Machakel district among high school students during the 2022/2023 academic year. Purposive sampling technique was applied for qualitative data collection including thirty-three key informants and six focus group discussions. Using proportional random sampling technique, 410 students were sampled and surveyed. Data collection utilized surveys, interviews, focus groups, and school WASH observations. WASH was categorized as "good" or "poor" based on mean scores of adolescent WASH. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and logistic regression. Qualitative findings were triangulated for a comprehensive understanding of adolescent WASH. Determinant factors were identified through multi-variable binary logistic regression (p < 0.05).ResultAdolescent high school students in the district faced poor water access (70.2%) and inadequate sanitation (74.9%). Nearly 90% rely on untreated water sources, whereas 21.2% of students rely on passive water treatment techniques. Most handwashing stations were dysfunctional and lacked soap, undermining hygiene practices. Poor WASH, experienced by over 70% of students, was compounded by water shortages, inadequate infrastructure, and cultural norms limiting hygiene practices. School location and living arrangements are determinants of WASH of adolescent students. Students from Shelel high school had higher odds of having a favorable WASH compared with those from Amanuel high school. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) was 2.15 (95% CI: 1.03–4.48, p = 0.041). Living arrangement was also independently associated with adolescent WASH. Students living with their family had significantly higher odds of favorable WASH compared with those living alone, with an AOR of 2.58 (95% CI: 1.30–5.11, p = 0.006).ConclusionThe study in the district revealed critical gaps in WASH among high school students, posing serious health and educational challenges. Water accessibility is inadequate with poor treatment practice. Female students faced gender-specific issues, including poor menstrual hygiene facilities. Socioeconomic disparities amplify these problems, particularly for those students living alone away from family. Integrated and contextualized WASH improvements are needed to address these issues and enhance students’ health and overall well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28024-6.

Introduction

Access to safe, reliable WASH services in schools remains uneven worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where many institutions still lack basic sanitation, handwashing facilities, and private, gender-sensitive spaces for menstrual hygiene. While expanding infrastructure is essential, a growing body of evidence points to a critical “hardware vs. software” divide: the mere presence of facilities does not reliably translate into improved health or educational outcomes. Instead, infrastructure (such as latrines and water points) must be complemented by “software” components, including hygiene education, behavior change strategies, and sustainable maintenance systems. Although hygiene education has been shown to improve student practices, its broader effects on health and attendance remain inconsistent when implemented in isolation. Taken together, the literature underscores that hardware and software are deeply interdependent-neither can compensate for deficiencies in the other, and meaningful impact depends on their integration [1–4].

Though global water coverage is slowly expanding, hygiene progress is lagging significantly, with one in three schools still lacking basic handwashing facilities. A key pattern emerging in recent literature is the "climate-resilience gap," where existing facilities are failing due to increasing water scarcity and extreme weather events, disproportionately affecting adolescent learners who require consistent access for health and dignity [5, 6].

Across multiple studies in India, adolescents showed high rates of poor WASH practice; most notably, open defecation and limited menstrual hygiene. Poverty, inadequate water access, and weak engagement of adolescents in health programs contributed to these issues. School-based assessments also liked unhealthy eating habit and poor hygiene to under-nutrition [7–9]. A recurring pattern across LMICs is the disconnect between available facilities and actual hygiene practices. In diverse settings including India, Korea, Indonesia, and Nepal; studies consistently show that handwashing is frequently neglected despite school-based WASH interventions. This neglect is often driven by a "triad of barriers": inconvenience, lack of consistent supplies, and low perceived risk [10–13].

In Sub-Saharan Africa, the challenge is characterized by a "broken link" between the existence of facilities and their actual functionality. Recent studies across the region indicate that even when schools possess toilets, the student-to-latrine ratios often exceed 90:1, rendering them unhygienic and inaccessible for adolescent girls. There is a documented pattern of "hardware-heavy" interventions that fail because they lack the "software" of institutional management and sustainable financing. This results in a recurring cycle where new infrastructure becomes dilapidated within years, a trend that continues to undermine educational outcomes and increase absenteeism among high school student [14, 15].

A significant knowledge gap exists regarding the "dignity" aspect of WASH. Evidence from Ghana, Kenya, and Ethiopia highlights that even when water is available, gender-sensitive standards such as private, lockable latrines and disposal systems are rarely met [16, 17]. In Ghana, sociocultural factors were found to "buffer" some negative impacts of poor infrastructure, suggesting that community support systems might play a role that infrastructure alone cannot [16]. On the other hand, in Ethiopia’s Amhara region, the lack of private spaces directly correlates with menstrual absenteeism, identifying a clear link between poor WASH and educational inequity [18, 19].

Literature establishes a strong, though complex, link between WASH and nutritional status. Across Asia and Africa, inadequate sanitation is a primary driver of undernutrition, anemia, and diarrheal infections. However, some studies present conflicting data. For instance, large-scale research in Southern Ethiopia found no significant connection between household sanitation and hookworm infection, or between infection and stunting [20]. These "negative" results are critical; they suggest that the relationship between WASH and health is not always linear and may be confounded by environmental factors or the quality of integrated health programs.

Ethiopia has made strides for WASH, yet a significant knowledge gap remains regarding the specific needs of secondary school adolescents compared to primary students. Recent research highlights a critical contradiction: while urban centers show higher infrastructure coverage, the quality and privacy of these facilities often do not meet the standards required for Menstrual Hygiene Management (MHM), leading to high dropout rates and reduced classroom participation among adolescent girls in governmental high schools [21, 22].

In some Ethiopian school settings, over half of girls reported that water was never available at school, and unsanitary toilets were common, directly undermining hygiene behaviors and health. UNICEF Ethiopia also reports that improving school sanitation blocks, handwashing stations, and MHM rooms significantly reduced absenteeism and dropout among adolescent girls by improving self-esteem, comfort, and regular attendance. Overall, adolescent student WASH conditions in Ethiopia remain inadequate, particularly for girls, and strengthening school-based water supply, sanitation infrastructure, handwashing services, and menstrual health support is essential for both health and educational equity [19].

Current evidence suggests that adolescent students in Northwest Ethiopia still face unhygienic environments that compromise their health and academic potential, necessitating a deeper investigation into their specific conditions [23]. Despite growing attention to WASH in schools, a significant literature gap persists regarding the specific conditions of adolescent students in Northwest Ethiopia, specifically to those students who move from rural areas to urban centers for search of high school education. These students often live alone in low cost rented, substandard rooms detached from parental supervision and traditional family support systems, making them uniquely vulnerable to poor hygiene environments both at school and in their "independent" homes.

While general studies focus on primary schools, they frequently overlook how these migrant adolescents navigate the physical and psychological demands of puberty, such as menstruation, while managing their own domestic WASH needs in resource-constrained town settings. Consequently, there is a critical need to investigate how this dual burden of inadequate school facilities and unmonitored home environments impacts their health and academic persistence. Assessing the WASH of these self-reliant students is essential to generate evidence for targeted interventions that support their transition into urban education and safeguard their well-being during this formative stage. This study addresses these gaps by quantifying the operational and behavioral barriers to sustained WASH in secondary schools, specifically focusing on the unique vulnerabilities of self-reliant living alone students.

Methods and materials

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in Machakel district, East Gojam Zone of the Amhara region. Covering 746.43 square kilometers, the district has a population density of 158.22 persons per square kilometer. With an average of 4.22 people per household, the majority (98.87%) of residents are Ethiopian Orthodox Christians, while 1.1% are Muslim. Amanuel, the district capital, is located 328 km from Addis Ababa and 228 km from Bahir Dar. The area's agro-ecological zones include 54% Woyina Dega (mid-altitude), 37.7% Dega (highlands), 4% Wurich (alpine), and 2.3% Kola (lowland). It is one of the productive districts in the region, with farmers primarily practicing mixed agriculture [24, 25]. The district currently has four high schools: Amanuel, Dega Segn, Gira Kidamin, and Shelel. Amanuel High School is located in the capital where others located in rural and semi-urban areas of the district. Institutional based mixed method study design was applied for adolescent WASH survey. Study design

A convergent explanatory mixed-method study was conducted across all four high schools in the district, combining both qualitative and quantitative research approaches simultaneously, with a greater emphasis on quantitative data. The study aimed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the WASH phenomenon by integrating multiple perspectives.

Sampling procedure and sample size determination

The total study target population consisted of 7,561 adolescent students from grade 9–12, with 3,811 females and 3,750 males during the 2022/2023 academic year. Sample size determination for the quantitative survey was conducted using a proportional simple random sampling technique according to the sampling formula for known population [28] with the final sample size of 410. The sampling formula is Nf = ni/(1 + ni⁄N) where ni is the initial sample size and N is the total population size. Nf = 362 Sample. Based on the formula, the sample size for this study was 362.

By calculating the non-response rate of 12% to the sample size taken from the calculation above the final sample size is (362/(1–0.12)) = 362/0.88 = 411 which is rounded to 410. Therefore, 410 samples have been selected from all four high schools found in the district. A 12% non-response rate was considered in the sample size determination because the study involved two rounds of data collection (pre-harvest and post-harvest), which increased the likelihood of participant attrition over time. In addition, since the study participants were high school students, relatively higher non-response was anticipated due to possible absenteeism, withdrawal from the study, academic schedules, lack of continued interest, and difficulties in tracing the same respondents during the second round of data collection. Therefore, adding a 12% non-response adjustment helped ensure that the final sample size remained adequate to maintain the statistical power and representativeness of the study.

The formula adjusts sample size using the finite population correction (FPC), ensuring accurate representation when the population size is limited. The formula relies on key statistical assumptions, including a confidence level at 95%, with a significant level of 0.05, a margin of error 5%, and a standard normal Z-score 1.96 based on the chosen confidence level 95%. Since the population proportion is unknown, it is assumed to be 50% (0.5) to account for maximum variability. We have tried our best to allocate samples nearly proportional for gender and residential respective. Two hundred five male adolescent high school students have been selected using simple random selection proportionally from each school. Similar procedure was applied to female adolescent high school students.

For the qualitative part of data collection, a purposive sampling technique was utilized. In-depth interviews were carried out with a total of thirty-three key informants who have better knowledge and experience about the WASH of adolescents in the district. This includes eight students, four school directors, eleven teachers from various disciplines, four health care workers, two woreda officers (one from woreda education office and the other from woreda health office), and four house rentees (community members). Moreover, WHO/UNICEF school WASH observation checklist was used.

Data collection procedures

Data for this study was collected through multiple methods: a quantitative survey using self-administered paper-based questionnaires was applied. As part of qualitative data collection, key informant interviews (KII), focus group discussions (FGD), and school WASH observations were conducted. Quantitative WASH assessment questionnaires were asked to students concerning water supply, sanitation of school and living environments, latrine utilization practice, and personal hygiene. A total of fifteen questions were asked about WASH of adolescent high school students of both their living and school environment with different response options based on the intention of the questions. These questionnaires were adapted for this study [29].

Using a single question, “How do you see water availability, sanitation, and hygiene condition of students”, perception about WASH of adolescent high school student in the district was explored from KIIs and FG discussants qualitatively. In addition, school WASH observation was conducted in all four high schools using WHO/UNICEF standard school WASH observation checklist [30]. There are checklist points concerning the main source of drinking water provided by the school. In general, all the checklist points are concerned about availability of water in school, accessibility of school water, quality of water in school, availability of information related to water in school, availability, functionality, and privacy of latrines in school, availability, and functionality of personal hygiene and menstrual hygiene management.

Data analysis

For the analysis of WASH of high school adolescent students, fifteen basic WASH assessment questions were used. The source of water was for instance converted to improved/unimproved sources for which one was assigned for improved whereas zero was assigned for unimproved water sources. For all fifteen WASH assessment questions, one was coded for correct responses/positive responses and zero for wrong/negative responses. The responses of each participant have been summed up and divided by number of participants to get mean score of the WASH of study participants. Based on the average value, those respondents below the mean value were considered as having poor WASH whereas those study participants having score of mean or above the mean were considered as having good WASH which leads to categorical outcome variable. Again, one was coded for those study participants who have good WASH, whereas zero was coded for those respondents having poor WASH.

The use mean cutoff for the WASH score is justified by its ability to provide a statistically grounded threshold that reflects the average WASH across the four high schools in the district, effectively distinguishing between relatively better and worse WASH. Assigning equal weight to all 15 WASH questions ensures that every component from water availability to personal hygiene management is treated as a critical, non-negotiable pillar of a comprehensive WASH, preventing the overshadowing of essential localized needs by a single dominant factor. This approach simplifies the scoring system for practical implementation while providing a balanced overview of the holistic WASH related health risks present in the woreda’s (district’s) school settings.

Descriptive statistics, bi-variable and multi-variable binary logistic regression were applied. For logistic regression, those variables which had 0.25 p-value during bi-variable logistic regression analysis were included in the multi-variable analysis. For the bivariable logit analysis, all demographic and socioeconomic variables are examined; however, only those independent variables that showed a statistically significant association (p < 0.05) are presented. After multi-variable logistic regression analysis, those variables with p-value less than 0.05 were considered as significant factors of WASH of adolescent high school students in the district. Data from KIIs, FGDs, and WASH observations have been triangulated and summarized through joint display to get a clear picture of adolescent students WASH in the district.

Data quality assurance

Data quality was ensured through rigorous pre-data-collection, instrument, and field-process controls. The quantitative questionnaire and qualitative interview guides were developed and validated. Tools were translated into Amharic and back translated into English to preserve semantic consistency. Feedback from the pilot was used to refine wording, skip patterns, culturally sensitive expressions, and estimated interview duration. Data collectors and supervisors received intensive training on study objectives, ethical procedures, standardized interviewing techniques, observation checklists, probing methods for qualitative interviews, and confidentiality of adolescent participants. A detailed field manual, role-play exercises and competency assessments before deployment have been done. During data collection, supervisors conducted daily onsite monitoring, random spot checks, repeat interviews on a small subsample, direct observation verification, and immediate correction of incomplete forms.

For post-collection and analytical data quality assurance, completed forms were undergone same-day review for completeness, consistency, legibility, and logical flow before leaving each school.

Quantitative data was double-entered independently, reconciled through validation checks, and screened for missingness, outliers, impossible values, straight-lining, and school-level clustering anomalies. Reliability tests, coding verification, and periodic database backups were implemented. The 15-item survey instrument was pre-tested to evaluate its internal consistency and reliability prior to the main data collection process. Reliability analysis was conducted using Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.725 (72.5%). This result indicates an acceptable level of internal consistency among the survey items, suggesting that the instrument was sufficiently reliable for measuring the intended constructs.

For the qualitative component, audio recording (with consent), verbatim transcription, translation accuracy, and transcript–audio comparison was checked. Triangulation was applied across student surveys, school environmental observations, key informant interviews, and focus group discussions to confirm consistency of WASH findings. In addition, all model tests are conducted including Goodness of fit, VIF and R-Squared test.

Operational definitions

The mean score was defined as the average WASH score of 410 students, calculated by dividing the total WASH score by the total number of participants (410), resulting in a mean value of 10 for this study. Students who scored below the mean WASH score (< 10) were categorized as having poor WASH conditions, whereas those who scored equal to or above the mean (≥ 10) were classified as having good WASH conditions.

WASH of adolescent high school students

The findings revealed that Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) conditions among high school adolescents remain a significant concern affecting both health and educational outcomes. Although access to safe drinking water is essential for maintaining hydration and supporting cognitive performance, many students experienced intermittent water supply and dependence on untreated water sources, exposing them to waterborne diseases. The study also showed that inadequate waste disposal practices and poor environmental sanitation, including improperly managed solid waste and ineffective drainage systems, contributed to unhygienic school environments. In addition, the results indicated that the effectiveness of WASH facilities largely depended on students’ personal hygiene practices. Adolescents required not only access to functional handwashing facilities but also adequate hygiene education to encourage regular handwashing and proper menstrual hygiene management.

In the qualitative data, it is indicated that male students exercise open defecation than females. “There is separated toilet for males and females in the school. Another issue of latrine is their functionality and separation for males and females. Toilets are not constructed to the standard and not separated for males and females in living environments. “There are separate toilets in the school. The toilet in our living environment is not separated for males and females. The toilet in the school is not to the expected standard. School location was a significant predictor: students from Shelel High School had higher odds of having a favorable WASH compared with those from Amanuel high school. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) was 2.15 (95% CI: 1.03–4.48, p = 0.041), indicating that students in Shelel were more than twice as likely to experience better WASH than their counterparts in the reference school. Living arrangement was also independently associated with adolescent WASH. Students living with their family had significantly higher odds of favorable WASH compared with those living alone, with an AOR of 2.58 (95% CI: 1.30–5.11, p = 0.006).

In addition, from the school WASH observation checklist, the investigation across the four high schools revealed notable areas of concern. In terms of water availability and quality, only one school had access to drinking water sources; however, in the rest three schools, a non-functional water point was observed per school, limiting accessibility. Drinking water lacked essential quality indicators, such as clarity and colorlessness, though it was generally odorless and tasteless. Maintenance practices were inconsistent, with no records of satisfactory regular upkeep and inadequate protective measures around water sources. Informative materials on hydration were present in two schools, but signs of proactive measures like water treatment were absent.

The sanitation facilities presented significant challenges. All schools had pit latrines without slabs, but privacy and cleanliness were deficient. None of the toilets were deemed private or sufficiently clean. Ventilation was non-functional, and there were no provisions for anal cleansing or waste bins within the toilet facilities. Maintenance records were either incomplete or outdated, and there was an absence of posters promoting hygienic use of facilities. Although facilities for staff were separate, accessibility for students with limited mobility was not adequately addressed, demonstrating non-inclusiveness in design.

Hygiene practices and menstrual hygiene management (MHM) were particularly concerning. While handwashing facilities were available and most were not functional, they lacked soap, which undermines effective hygiene. Handwashing stations were not accessible to all students, and maintenance records and cleaning materials did not align with the best practices. Menstrual hygiene support was inadequate, with no private spaces for MHM in most schools, no sanitary bins in girls' toilet cubicles, and no menstrual hygiene products available. This gap in MHM infrastructure could significantly affect female students' school attendance and comfort.

Discussions

The result from this study which indicates the reliance on single water sources persists even in settings where infrastructure investments exist, highlighting not just scarcity but fragility and lack of resilience in water supply systems. The lack of diversified water sources and the prevalence of unsafe water use correlated with the region’s high rates of communicable diseases, undernutrition, and child mortality from like diarrhea [30, 31]. In practical terms, it indicates that WASH services for adolescents remain vulnerable to interruptions, placing students at continued risk of water insecurity and exposing gaps in the resilience, maintenance, and diversification of water supply options within school and community settings. Similarly, other studies highlighted that sub-Saharan Africa faces a substantial gap in achieving safe and sustainable WASH services, with rural areas bearing the brunt of these deficiencies which corroborates our findings [32, 33].

Investments in infrastructure, such as deep boreholes and urban sanitation, have increased access to safe water, particularly for students, mirroring successful efforts in Tanzania. However, progress is uneven, with rural schools relying on single-source systems, contrasting with more integrated urban approaches, underscoring ongoing disparities which again supports our findings [34–36].

The findings on water treatment knowledge and practice reveal that limited adoption of modern purification methods is not solely an issue of availability, but of awareness and institutional support. The high proportion of adolescents reporting no knowledge of water treatment mechanisms underscores a critical gap in school-based health education. This indicates that students may not fully understand the importance of proper water treatment or lack the guidance, training, and school-level support needed to adopt safer practices. As a result, even when purification technologies or materials are present, they remain underutilized highlighting a critical need for strengthened WASH education, behavior‑change communication, and supportive school policies to improve water safety practices.

A study in Sub-Saharan Africa indicated that water treatment awareness among adolescents in Zimbabwe was similarly low, resulting in widespread reliance on unsafe water sources [37]. The reliance on traditional methods like boiling mirrors findings in Ethiopia, where [38] reported that boiling is often trusted due to its simplicity and effectiveness, despite the challenges of fuel scarcity. Passive methods like letting water stand and settle indicated limited access to modern technologies, a trend also noted in rural Botswana, where cultural practices often shape water management [39]. Meanwhile, the use of advanced solutions like ceramic filters and Aquatabs remain low, reflecting economic constraints and limited awareness, as highlighted in other studies in Ethiopia [38, 40], who emphasized the need for targeted educational interventions to improve modern water treatment adoption. The qualitative data, pointing to inadequacies in water treatment infrastructure, underscored the necessity of integrating health education and resource support to address this recurring issue for adolescents in the district.

In relation to sanitation, this study contributes a more differentiated understanding of latrine access versus utilization. Although household latrine availability was relatively high, continued open defecation particularly among male students highlights the role of behavioral and cultural factors rather than mere facility presence. The persistence of open defecation despite adequate access points to deeper behavioral, social, and cultural influences shape sanitation practices. This suggests that improving WASH outcomes requires more than constructing facilities; it demands targeted behavior‑change interventions, gender‑sensitive programming, and community engagement to address norms and attitudes that discourage consistent latrine use.

This reflected the relatively better availability of sanitation infrastructure in households, though significant gaps remain in behavioral practices and facility standards. A study in Amhara region noted that urban adolescent students in urban areas have improved access to sanitation facilities, yet issues such as functionality and privacy remain critical concerns which may be the cause for open defecation practiced by some adolescents [41]. Conversely, a systematic review in 2020 indicated persistent challenges in rural Ethiopia, with higher rates of open defecation and communal latrine reliance due to limited infrastructure and weaker health education efforts which has similarities with this finding [42]. The observed gender disparities, where male students are more likely to practice open defecation despite the availability of facilities, aligned with cultural and behavioral factors highlighted in both studies. These findings emphasized the need for tailored interventions, including improving facility standards, addressing gender-specific barriers, and reinforcing behavior change campaigns.

Similarly, the analysis of waste disposal practices extends previous research by situating adolescent behaviors within constrained community-level systems. The coexistence of communal pits, burning, and undesignated dumping reflects adaptive but environmentally suboptimal practices in the absence of structured waste management. This study uniquely frames adolescents not just as beneficiaries of WASH services, but as active agents whose practices can either mitigate or exacerbate environmental health risks when supported by appropriate systems.

The findings on waste disposal methods highlighted key challenges and opportunities in promoting sustainable waste management practices. The predominance of communal pit usage reflected a collective effort among students but also indicated limited infrastructure for individual waste disposal. Similarly, the significant reliance on burning and household pits mirrored findings in other Ethiopian studies where household-level solutions often emerged due to gaps in organized waste management systems [43, 44]. The continued dependence on burning and household pits mirrors broader national trends, where households resort to improvised disposal methods due to limited organized waste‑management systems. Overall, the results show that while students demonstrate willingness to participate in collective waste‑handling practices, the absence of structured, school‑ or community‑level waste‑management services constrains the adoption of more sustainable and hygienic WASH behaviors.

In contrast, other studies emphasized the potential of structured systems such as street bins to significantly reduce environmental hazards when adequately promoted. However, the persistence of undesignated waste dumping and minimal use of designated areas revealed a persistent lack of awareness and engagement in sustainable practices. These behaviors aligned with observations in other Ethiopian contexts where community-level environmental sanitation remains inadequate due to limited resources and public education [45]. Thus, addressing these gaps requires both improved infrastructure and targeted awareness campaigns tailored to adolescents to foster collective environmental responsibility.

The findings on WASH among adolescent high school students in the study district aligned with broader literature on the topic. Poor WASH of adolescents echoed findings from studies indicating that adolescents in low-resource settings often face challenges in accessing clean water, adequate sanitation, and hygiene facilities [46, 47]. Studies in Ethiopian and systematic reviews highlighted similar disparities, emphasizing economic inequalities that restrict access to piped water and other resources [3, 48, 49]. The association between infrastructure deficits and poor WASH outcomes, as evidenced in this study, resonated with findings on the role of school environments in shaping sanitation outcomes for adolescents [22]. These parallels underscore the systemic nature of WASH challenges, necessitating multi-sectoral interventions.

Importantly, the study highlights intra-school and demographic inequities, particularly the heightened vulnerability of students living away from family and those attending resource-poor schools. By identifying school-specific disparities and linking them to living arrangements and age groups, the findings move beyond aggregate prevalence estimates to pinpoint where interventions are most urgently needed.

The significant variations found in this study in WASH across schools and demographic groups reflected inequities documented in Ethiopian studies. The disproportionate prevalence of poor WASH among schools indicated inequalities in school infrastructure and resource allocation are apparent. Similar findings linked disparities in WASH access to the socioeconomic context of schools [11, 50, 51]. The higher vulnerability of students living alone away from family, reported in this study aligned with other studies which emphasized the role of familial support in mitigating hygiene risks [52]. Other studies corroborated that rural and resource-poor settings exacerbate WASH inequities, particularly for adolescents reliant on non-functional or unhygienic water sources [53].

Social Support Theory explains how social relationships and support systems influence individuals’ behaviors, well-being, and ability to cope with daily challenges. In the context of student WASH, we link the theory suggesting that students living with their families benefit from emotional, financial, and practical support provided by household members. Parents or guardians often act as gatekeepers by ensuring that water is treated, soap is available, and hygiene routines are maintained, thereby reducing the burden on students. In contrast, students living alone away from family frequently experience time poverty because they must balance academic responsibilities, employment, household chores, and water collection on their own. Without strong social support, these competing demands can lead them to neglect essential hygiene practices such as regular handwashing or water treatment, increasing their vulnerability to health risks [54].

While the study found no significant gender-based differences in WASH, qualitative data suggested nuanced experiences, particularly in menstrual hygiene management (MHM). Other studies in Ethiopian revealed that female students often face barriers to MHM, including limited access to private toilets and sanitary materials, which can lead to school absenteeism which has similarities with our findings [55]. Similarly, older adolescents, like the 19-year-olds disproportionately affected in this study, are highlighted by a systematic review as a group at risk of reduced WASH support [56]. These patterns reflect broader socio-cultural dynamics and developmental shifts that shape hygiene practices. Addressing these issues requires gender-sensitive and age-appropriate interventions, as underscored by multiple Ethiopian researchers.

Although the study found an equal gender distribution among respondents, the high proportion of students experiencing poor access to water (70.2%) has particularly serious implications for female students in relation to Menstrual Hygiene Management (MHM). Inadequate water supply limits girls’ ability to maintain personal hygiene during menstruation, including changing sanitary materials, washing reusable pads, and cleaning themselves privately and safely. This situation can compromise their dignity, comfort, and confidence while at school, often leading to embarrassment, anxiety, and fear of stigma from peers. Poor water access may also contribute to increased absenteeism among female students during menstrual periods, negatively affecting their academic participation and overall well-being. Therefore, improving reliable water access in schools is not only a matter of general sanitation but also an essential step toward ensuring menstrual dignity, gender equity, and a supportive learning environment for female students.

WASH challenges among adolescents are not merely a function of limited infrastructure, but of interacting educational, behavioral, and contextual factors operating at household, school, and community levels. These insights underscore the need for localized, integrated, and adolescent-centered WASH interventions that move beyond uniform policy solutions toward equity-driven implementation.

The findings stress the urgency of policy and infrastructural interventions to address WASH disparities among adolescents in Ethiopian schools. Improving water supply reliability, enhancing toilet facilities, and providing hygiene materials, including soap and menstrual products, are immediate priorities. The role of proximity to resources, highlighted by this study further underscored the need for localized and equitable resource distribution [57]. A comprehensive approach, integrating community participation and stakeholder collaboration, can help sustain improvements in WASH. The systemic barriers revealed in this, and other studies underlined the need for targeted investments to bridge infrastructure and service gaps in Ethiopian schools.

Conclusions and recommendations

Adolescent high school students in the district face a deeply entrenched WASH crisis driven by infrastructural deficits, behavioral shortcomings, and socioeconomic inequalities. Most rely on a single, often unsafe water source primarily rivers highlighting vulnerability to contamination and seasonal variability, while water treatment awareness remains minimal. The widespread use of untreated water poses significant public health risks. Sanitation challenges are equally severe, with dependence on communal latrines and persistent open defecation, particularly among males. Inadequate, non–gender-sensitive school facilities further disadvantage female students, especially in managing menstrual hygiene. Poor waste disposal practices include open dumping and burning and underscore systemic environmental management failures. Additionally, school location and living arrangements significantly influence WASH outcomes, with students living independently facing heightened vulnerability.

To improve WASH outcomes, interventions should move beyond school compounds and adopt an integrated “School–Home WASH” policy framework that addresses both educational environments and the low-cost rental housing where a majority of students reside in the district. Investments should prioritize reliable and equitable water supply infrastructure in schools and surrounding residential communities to ensure continuous access to safe drinking water. Schools should establish regular maintenance systems for sanitation facilities and ensure that latrines are clean, functional, gender-segregated, private, and accessible for students with disabilities.

Given that majority of students live in low-cost rental rooms, local governments should introduce minimum WASH standards and inspection mechanisms for rental housing, including requirements for safe water access, hygienic shared toilets, handwashing facilities, drainage systems, and waste disposal services. Municipal authorities should collaborate with landlords, community leaders, and public health offices to improve sanitation conditions in densely populated rental compounds where students are particularly vulnerable to WASH-related health risks.

Comprehensive hygiene education programs should also be expanded to connect school and household practices by promoting water treatment, personal hygiene, environmental sanitation, and safe waste management behaviors. Schools should further strengthen menstrual hygiene management through the provision of sanitary materials, private changing spaces, water access, and appropriate disposal mechanisms to improve girls’ dignity, attendance, and educational participation.

A multi-sectoral and equity-driven approach involving schools, municipal authorities, housing regulators, health sectors, landlords, and community organizations is essential for sustainable WASH improvements. Targeted support should prioritize underserved schools and vulnerable students living in inadequate rental conditions. Without integrated interventions addressing both school and home environments, the cycle of poor WASH, absenteeism, and educational disadvantage is likely to persist, undermining broader public health and development goals.
