Factors associated with sleep quality in community-dwelling older adults: a latent class analysis
Authors: Yunan Tao, Liya Wang, Yinyi Zhao, Huiqin Xi, Xiaohong Zhang, Yuru Yan, Yuanjie Chen, Zhihan Yu, Aiyong Zhu, Qianqian Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina is home to 264 million adults aged ≥ 60 years, with 46% of community-dwelling older adults reporting poor sleep quality. Sleep quality is closely associated with biological, psychological, and social factors; however, few studies have explored its heterogeneity, leading to poorly targeted interventions. The aim of this study is to explore latent classes of sleep quality among community-dwelling older adults and to analyze their biopsychosocial correlates, providing evidence for tailored intervention approaches.MethodsFrom April to November 2025, convenience sampling was used to recruit community-dwelling individuals aged ≥ 60 years at the Pujin Community Health Service Center of Minhang District, Shanghai. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire and the Social Support Rating Scale, Geriatric Depression Scale-15, and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Latent class analysis was conducted to identify distinct sleep quality groups. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to explore relationships between latent classes of sleep quality and sociodemographic characteristics, physical health, lifestyle behaviors, depression, and social support.ResultsSleep quality was categorized into Class 1 (high sleep quality, non-pharmacological dependence), Class 2 (high sleep efficiency, high functional impairment), and Class 3 (long sleep latency, low sleep efficiency), observed in 19.37%, 42.03%, and 38.60% of the sample, respectively. Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that gender, body mass index, educational attainment, living arrangements, Number of Chronic Diseases, number of medications, number of missing teeth, physical activity, visual impairment, depression, and social support levels significantly influenced sleep quality classes.ConclusionsResults demonstrated that 62.6% of participants experienced impaired sleep quality. Significant associations were identified between latent classes of sleep quality, and biological, psychological, and social factors. Healthcare professionals should conduct early screenings and implement biopsychosocial model–based interventions to improve sleep quality, quality of life, and life expectancy among community-dwelling older adults. Future research should focus on conducting multicenter longitudinal studies, optimizing assessment tools, and exploring other factors influencing sleep. These efforts will provide scientific evidence to support the development and implementation of personalized sleep intervention strategies for community-dwelling older adults.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Community, Older adults, Sleep quality, Depression, Social support, Latent class analysis

Background

According to the seventh national population census [1], the number of individuals aged ≥ 60 years in China has reached 264 million as of 2020, accounting for 18.7% of the total population. With the continued intensification of population aging and changes in population health, an increasing number of older adults are experiencing functional decline and disability, and sleep has become a significant risk factor for disability in this age group [2]. In recent years, numerous studies published in high impact factor journals have extensively focused on sleep as a global health issue, revealing a series of complex relationships between sleep and physical and mental health, along with underlying neurobiological mechanisms [3, 4]. Sleep quality refers to an individual’s overall satisfaction with various aspects of their sleep experience, including sleep efficiency, latency, and duration [5]. Epidemiological data have demonstrated [6] that 46.0% of individuals aged ≥ 60 years in China report sleep problems. Poor sleep quality affects the physical health of older adults and also increases the risk of adverse health outcomes. Therefore, elucidating the heterogeneity of sleep quality among community-dwelling older adults and its influencing factors poses significant clinical importance and value [7].

Previous studies have reported that physiological factors, such as gender, body mass index (BMI), and visual impairment, are closely associated with sleep quality deterioration among community-dwelling older adults [8–10]. Psychological factors such as depression can interfere with the synthesis of melatonin and neurotransmitters through physiological mechanisms like neuroinflammation and dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, leading to hypercortisolemia. This, in turn, disrupts the circadian rhythms, increases nighttime awakenings, and contributes to daytime sleepiness and other sleep quality issues [11]. Additionally, social environmental factors, such as social support, are correlated with sleep quality. Adequate social support can provide older adults with a safe sleep environment and decrease vigilance and arousal, thus promoting optimal sleep [12]. The biopsychosocial model emphasizes that a single biological mechanism cannot explain an individual’s health; instead, health is a result of the interplay between biological (e.g., physiological conditions, disease characteristics, and treatment methods), psychological (e.g., emotional states), and social factors (e.g., economic status and social support), among others [13]. Sleep quality is closely associated with biological, psychological, and social factors. However, existing studies on older adults’ sleep quality have largely examined isolated factors, overlooking the heterogeneity and combined effects of multiple determinants, and this lack of focus has resulted in limited applicability of existing findings in clinical practice, leading to suboptimal intervention outcomes.

Therefore, guided by the biopsychosocial model, this study uses latent class analysis (LCA), a statistical method that classifies homogeneous individuals with similar surface characteristics into categories with distinct traits [14], to identify latent categories of sleep quality among community-dwelling older adults. From a comprehensive biopsychosocial perspective, it analyzes the distinctive characteristics of each latent category, providing a theoretical basis for the development of targeted interventions aimed at preventing or delaying declines in sleep quality among community-dwelling older adults.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used convenience sampling to recruit older adults for a face-to-face, paper-based survey conducted at the Pujin Community Health Service Center in Minhang District, Shanghai, from April to November 2025. To reduce potential interviewer bias and measurement error, all nursing staff involved in data collection received standardized training prior to the survey. The questionnaires were primarily self-administered by the participants. For participants with limited literacy, trained nursing staff provided assistance using standardized instructions to ensure consistency. Completed questionnaires were checked onsite for missing responses. All data were independently double-entered by two researchers to ensure accuracy. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 60 years, with good understanding, communication, and cooperation abilities; (2) clear consciousness, without intellectual disabilities; and (3) informed consent and voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) in an acute phase of acute or chronic diseases, (2) with severe cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders, and (3) with severe physical disabilities or sensory impairments.

The calculation of the sample size was based on two objectives. The first objective was to ensure sufficient statistical power for the analysis of factors influencing sleep quality. Based on the calculation method for factors that influence sleep quality, which is 10–20 more than the number of independent variables [15], the study included 18 demographic variables, 7 dimensions of sleep quality, 3 dimensions of social support, and 1 dimension of depression, for a total of 29 potential influencing factors. Given a 20% dropout rate, the required sample size ranged from 348 to 696 cases. According to a meta-analysis by Wang [6] on the prevalence of sleep disorders among community-dwelling older adults in China, 91 cross-sectional studies with a total of 81,354 participants were included. The pooled prevalence of sleep disorders among adults aged ≥ 60 years across different regions of China was 46%. Therefore, the current study adopted a prevalence rate of 0.46. With a significance level of α = 0.05 and a margin of error of 2% (δ = 0.02), the sample size was calculated as 1,066. Considering a 20% invalid questionnaire rate, the final sample size was estimated at 1,279. Based on these two calculations, the final sample size was set to 1,279 to meet the accuracy requirements for prevalence estimation and analysis of influencing factors. Given available manpower and resources, a total of 1,430 questionnaires were collected, and 1,399 community-dwelling older adults were ultimately included. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Medicine & Health Sciences (ethics approval number: 2025-yjskt-02-320921198207136725) and was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment tools

General information questionnaire

Based on the biopsychosocial model framework and an extensive review of the literature on sleep quality and its associated factors among older adults, a general information questionnaire was developed for this study. The questionnaire included 18 items across three domains: sociodemographic characteristics, physical health status, and healthy lifestyle behaviors.

Sociodemographic characteristics include age (in years), gender (male, female), educational attainment (illiterate, primary school, junior high or higher), marital status (married, other), living arrangements (living alone, other), and average monthly income (≤ 3000, > 3000). Of note, the local minimum monthly wage in Shanghai was adjusted to 2,750 yuan in 2025, and a study of older adults in Shanghai showed that 48.7% of the elderly had a monthly income above 3,000 yuan [16, 17].

Physical health status included BMI (< 18.5, 18.5–23.9, > 23.9), number of chronic diseases (0–1, 2–3, ≥ 4), history of falls in the past year (yes or no), history of surgery in the past year (yes or no), number of missing teeth (≤ 10, > 10), hearing impairment (yes or no), visual impairment (yes or no), and number of medications (≤ 3, > 3), excluding traditional Chinese medicines and dietary supplements. Moderate polypharmacy, defined as the use of more than three medications, has been associated with increased medication burden, functional decline, and poorer health outcomes [18].

Healthy lifestyle behavior characteristics include physical activity (yes or no), defined as at least 3 times per week, with each session lasting for more than 30 min, intellectual activity (yes or no), defined as daily engagement in activities such as reading, mobile internet use, and puzzle games, alcohol consumption status (yes or no), and smoking status (yes or no).

Social support rating scale

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), developed by Chinese scholar Xiao Shuiyuan in 1986 [19], is used to assess social support in Chinese populations, with items consistency coefficients of 0.89–0.94 reported in the original validation studies, indicating good reliability and validity. The scale comprises three dimensions (objective support, subjective support, and utilization of social support) with a total of 10 items. The total score denotes the sum of individual item scores. Scores of ≤ 22, between 23 and 44, and between 45 and 66 indicate low, moderate, and high levels of social support, respectively.

Geriatric depression scale-15

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) was designed in 1986 by Sheikh and Yesavage [20] based on the characteristics of older adults. This scale comprises 15 items, answered with “Yes” or “No.” Responses indicating depressive symptoms take a value of 1. Total scores range from 0 to 15 (0–4 = no depressive symptoms and a good psychological state; 5–9 = mild depressive symptoms, 10–15 = potential for moderate to severe depressive symptoms). Tang [21] adapted and revised the scale for the Chinese context, reporting a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.793, indicating good reliability and validity.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

In 1989, Dr. Buysse, a psychiatrist at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center [22], developed the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) to assess sleep quality. The scale consists of 19 items and includes seven dimensions: sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medications, and daytime dysfunction. Each dimension was rated on a scale from 0 to 3 points, and the total PSQI score denotes the sum of the scores for the seven dimensions, which ranges from 0 to 21 points (< 7 = good sleep quality, ≥ 7 = presence of sleep disorders) [23]. High PSQI scores indicate poor sleep quality among older adults. Liu [24] conducted the translation and reliability and validity testing of the Chinese version of the scale. The authors found that the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the seven dimensions was 0.8420, while the split-half reliability coefficient was 0.8661, which indicate good internal consistency and reliability.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 and Mplus 8.3. First, data preprocessing was performed, with missing data imputed using the mode imputation method. Outliers for each scale were detected using Z-scores, and cases with an absolute Z-score greater than 3 (|Z| > 3) were removed [25]. Data normality was assessed using the criteria proposed by Kim [26]. When the absolute value of skewness was < 2 and the absolute value of kurtosis was < 10, data were considered approximately normally distributed, which was the case in the present study. Thus, categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Ẋ ± S). In addition, we conducted multicollinearity diagnostics for all independent variables and found that all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were < 5, indicating that the model did not have multicollinearity issues [27].

LCA was performed using Mplus 8.3. To achieve better model fit, ensure accurate identification of sleep disturbances, and facilitate interpretability of the results, the four-category variables (i.e., very good, good, poor, and very poor) for the seven dimensions of PSQI were first converted into binary variables (i.e., good and poor). Corresponding scores remained unchanged for 0 points, while scores of 1–3 points were converted to 1 point; thus, the scoring system was changed from (0–3) to (0,1). The initial model with one class was progressively expanded by increasing the number of classes until optimal model fit was reached. First, for the model evaluation criteria, the study included Akaike’s information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), in which smaller values for AIC and aBIC indicate better model fit. Second, the Lo–Mendell–Rubin (LMR) likelihood ratio test and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used to compare fit differences between latent class models, with p < 0.05 indicating that the model with k classes fits significantly better than the model with k − 1 classes. Finally, entropy was used to assess the accuracy of model classification, with values ranging from 0 to 1; a value of ≥ 0.8 indicates ≥ 90% accuracy, with values closer to 1 representing higher classification accuracy. The final model selection also considered model interpretability and practical significance.

After determining the optimal sleep quality latent classes, the chi-square or Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare differences in baseline characteristics between the latent classes of sleep quality. One-way analysis of variance was used to examine differences in levels of depression and social support across the latent classes. A single unordered multinomial logistic regression model was conducted, in which all variables that were significant in univariate analyses were concurrently included as independent variables, and latent classes of sleep quality were modeled as the dependent variable. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1,430 questionnaires were collected. After excluding 31 responses with absolute Z-scores greater than 3, 1,399 valid questionnaires were retained, which resulted in an effective response rate of 97.83%.

Common method bias

Data were primarily collected via a self-administered questionnaire completed by the participants within the same context, which could potentially lead to common method bias. To address this concern, the study conducted Harman’s single-factor test prior to data analysis. Exploratory factor analysis was performed on all items of the scales used. The results demonstrated that 13 factors obtained eigenvalues > 1, with the first factor accounting for 17.662% of variance (< 40%). This finding indicated that the results for the three variables are unlikely to be substantially influenced by common method bias, thereby enhancing their credibility of the findings.

Sample characteristics

Out of the 1,399 participants, the mean age was 72.88 ± 7.19 years. Among them, 813 (58.1%) were women, 912 (65.2%) completed at least junior high school, 808 (57.8%) were within the BMI range of 18.5–23.9 kg/m2, 441 (31.5%) were taking three or more types of medications, 586 (41.9%) reported a lack of physical activity, and 713 (51.0%) were diagnosed with a visual impairment. Basic characteristics of the scales

Out of the 1,399 participants, the mean GDS-15 score was 2.78 ± 2.00, with 256 participants (18.3%) potentially at risk for depression. The mean total SSRS score was 39.30 ± 4.49, with mean scores of 7.75 ± 1.44, 23.05 ± 2.82, and 8.50 ± 1.84 for the objective support, subjective support, and utilization of social support dimensions, respectively. A total of 1,217 (87%) and 182 (13.0%) participants obtained moderate and high levels of social support. The mean total score for the PSQI was 7.96 ± 4.02, with 876 participants (62.6%) exhibiting poor sleep quality.

Latent classes of sleep quality

Using the seven dimensions of the PSQI as manifest indicators, latent class models with 1 to 5 classes were sequentially estimated; Table 2 presents the results. AIC, BIC, and aBIC values initially decreased with the increase in the number of classes. From the four-class model onward, BIC began to increase, whereas the reduction in aBIC became less pronounced. The three-class model exhibited an entropy value of > 0.8, and LMR and BLRT were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Given the overall model fit indices and interpretability, the three-class model was selected as the optimal model. At this point, the average posterior probabilities of class membership among the participants were all more than 96%, which indicated good classification accuracy and reliable differentiation between latent classes.

A latent class profile was plotted based on the classification results (Fig. 1). The three latent classes were named according to the conditional probability distributions obtained across the PSQI dimensions. The first class was composed of 271 participants (19.37%), with low conditional probabilities for the majority of the sleep dimensions but the best overall sleep quality and no dependence on sleep medications; therefore, it was named Class 1: high sleep quality, non-pharmacological dependence. The second class included 588 participants (42.03%) and was characterized by the highest sleep efficiency and highest level of daytime dysfunction; therefore, it was named Class 2: high sleep efficiency, high functional impairment. The third class comprised 540 participants (38.60%), with high probabilities for the majority of the sleep dimensions, the longest sleep latency, and relatively poor sleep efficiency; therefore, it was named Class 3: long sleep latency, low sleep efficiency.

Univariate analysis of latent classes of sleep quality in community-dwelling older adults

Multifactorial analysis of latent classes of sleep quality in community-dwelling older adults

Sleep quality was classified into three latent classes. Using Class 1 as the reference group, all variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were simultaneously entered as independent variables into an unordered multinomial logistic regression model to identify factors associated with each latent class. Unordered multinomial logistic regression automatically accommodates categorical variables without the need to manually create dummy variables. The variables were recoded as follows: gender (male = 1, female = 2), BMI (< 18.5 = 1, > 23.9 = 2, 18.5–23.9 = 3), educational attainment (illiterate = 1, primary school = 2, junior high school or higher = 3), marital status (other = 1, married = 2), living arrangements (living alone = 1, other = 2), average monthly income (≤ 3000 = 1, > 3000 = 2), number of chronic diseases (≥ 4 = 1, 2–3 = 2, 0–1 = 3), number of medications (> 3 = 1,≤3 = 2), number of missing teeth (> 10 = 1, ≤ 10 = 2), physical activity (no = 1, yes = 2), intellectual activity (no = 1, yes = 2), hearing impairment (yes = 1, no = 2), visual impairment (no = 1, yes = 2), alcohol consumption status (yes = 1, no = 2), and smoking status (yes = 1, no = 2). The original scores of the GDS-15 and SSRS were treated as continuous variables, whereas categorical variables were modeled as nominal to avoid assuming a linear trend across categories. For each independent variable included in the regression model, the category with the largest sample size was selected as the reference group.

Table 3 demonstrates that, compared with Class 1, men (OR = 1.62) were more likely to belong to Class 2, whereas older adults with primary school education (OR = 1.61) and with > 10 missing teeth (OR = 1.77) were more likely to belong to Class 3. Community-dwelling older adults with a BMI > 23.9, taking > 3 types of medications, physical inactivity, and higher levels of depression were more likely to belong to Classes 2 and 3. Among these factors, physical inactivity (OR = 2.04) and a BMI > 23.9 (OR = 2.75) were most strongly associated with belonging to Class 2, whereas taking > 3 types of medications (OR = 3.83) and higher levels of depression (OR = 1.82) were most strongly associated with belonging to Class 3. In addition, older adults with 2–3 chronic diseases (OR = 0.49) were less likely to belong to Class 2. Participants without visual impairment, who lived alone and who had higher levels of social support, had the highest probability of belonging to Class 1 and lower probabilities of belonging to Classes 2 and 3. Among these factors, older adults without visual impairment (OR = 0.60) had the lowest probability of belonging to Class 2, whereas those who lived alone (OR = 0.26) and had higher levels of social support (OR = 0.94) had the lowest probability of belonging to Class 3.

Discussion

Prevalence of impaired sleep quality and classification of sleep quality

The results demonstrated that among 1,399 community-dwelling older adults, the mean total PSQI score was 7.96 ± 4.02, and 876 (62.6%) participants experienced impaired sleep quality, which is a markedly higher value compared with the global prevalence of sleep disorders (45%) [28] in this population; this indicates overall moderate to high levels of sleep impairment. Given the characteristics of the city in which the survey was conducted [29, 30], community-dwelling older adults are chronically exposed to multiple stressors such as polypharmacy, depressive symptoms, and social pressures, which may contribute to the high prevalence of sleep problems. Therefore, healthcare professionals should pay early attention to sleep status in this population, identify factors associated with sleep problems, and implement targeted nursing interventions to prevent or delay the onset and progression of sleep disturbances.

The results of the LCA indicated that sleep quality among community-dwelling older adults is highly heterogeneous and can be grouped under three distinct latent classes, which is partially consistent with the findings of a study on sleep patterns in nursing home residents [31]. In the current population, those belonging to Class 1 accounted for 19.37% of the sample, which may be related to better psychological status and stronger social support [32]. Meanwhile, those belonging to Classes 2 and 3 accounted for 42.03% and 38.60%, respectively. Older adults belonging to Class 2 exhibited overall poorer sleep quality; their short sleep latency and long sleep duration, characteristic of high sleep efficiency, may be associated with increased sensitivity to hypnotic medications. However, the additive side effects of medications likely contributed to daytime dysfunction due to poorer physical health and polypharmacy [33]. Furthermore, although older adults belonging to Class 3 relied on hypnotic medications, improvements in their sleep-related dimensions were not observed. This result may be attributable to improper long-term use of hypnotics, which decreases treatment effectiveness and fails to enhance overall sleep quality [31].

Analysis of factors influencing latent classes of sleep quality among community-dwelling older adults

Impact of sociodemographic characteristics on latent classes of sleep quality

Results of the multinomial logistic regression analysis indicated that living arrangements, gender, and educational attainment significantly influenced the latent classes of sleep quality among older adults. Participants living alone were less likely to belong to Classes 2 and 3, while male participants were more likely to belong to Class 2. This finding may be explained by the fact that older adults living alone enjoy full autonomy over their daily life, and their proficient use of smartphones for social engagement provides an effective social support system; additionally, some older adults experience functional separation due to a spouse’s admission to long-term care facilities as a result of functional impairment, thereby alleviating the caregiving burden previously borne by the partner [34, 35]. Although men exhibit a higher prevalence of sleep problems (e.g., fatigue and daytime sleepiness associated with obstructive sleep apnea), they generally bear less caregiving responsibility than women, which may contribute to the higher sleep efficiency [8, 36]. In addition, older adults with a primary school education, compared with those with junior high education or higher, were more likely to belong to Class 3. They may strongly desire to acquire knowledge about sleep health, but limited access to information and lower levels of literacy may reduce their ability to manage medications effectively, which renders difficulty in regulating sleep through scientific approaches [37]. However, it should be noted that the number of older adults living alone and the number of illiterate older adults classified into Class 1 in our sample were relatively small; therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Impact of physical health status on latent classes of sleep quality

The results demonstrated that biological factors, including BMI, number of chronic diseases, number of medications, visual impairment, and number of missing teeth, were significant determinants of latent sleep quality classes among older adults. Older adults with a BMI > 23.9 and those taking > 3 medications were more likely to belong to Classes 2 and 3. In addition, older adults with a greater number of missing teeth were more likely to belong to Class 3. A higher BMI and a greater number of medications may increase the risk of systemic inflammation and exacerbate drug-related adverse effects, and these overlapping biological mechanisms may ultimately contribute to poorer sleep quality and a higher likelihood of sleep disturbances among community-dwelling older adults [9, 33, 38]. Moreover, a greater number of missing teeth may alter oral and airway morphology, increasing the risk of sleep-related disorders, which in turn may impair sleep quality and duration, ultimately contributing to overall deterioration in sleep status [39, 40].

Older adults with 2–3 chronic diseases were less likely to belong to Class 2, which may be related to their higher number of potentially inappropriate medications, leading to increased nighttime awakenings or reduced deep sleep, thereby decreasing sleep efficiency [41]. Furthermore, older adults with visual impairment were less likely to belong to Classes 2 and 3, while exhibiting the highest probability of belonging to Class 1. Visual impairment can attenuate circadian rhythm entrainment and shorten photoperiod exposure, thus leading to disruption in circadian rhythms, sleep fragmentation, and early morning awakening, whereas normal vision contributes to the maintenance of good sleep quality [10]. Of note, among older adults classified into Class 1, those with a BMI < 18.5 and taking ≥ 4 types of medication were few; therefore, the lack of statistical significance in the lower likelihood of individuals with BMI < 18.5 or taking ≥ 4 medications belonging to Classes 2 and 3, relative to Class 1, should be interpreted with caution.

Impact of healthy lifestyle behavior on latent classes of sleep quality

The regression results indicated that physical activity significantly influenced the latent classes of sleep quality among older adults. Participants with physical inactivity were more likely to belong to Classes 2 and 3. A potential reason is that physical activity improves vagal nerve activity by decreasing levels of appetite-regulating peptides and inflammatory factors, which decreases the occurrence of insomnia and ultimately improves sleep quality [42]. Furthermore, some older adults with physical inactivity may still maintain a regular daily routine, which shortens sleep latency and ensures high sleep efficiency [43].

Impact of depression on latent classes of sleep quality

The results illustrated that older adults with depression were more likely to belong to Classes 2 and 3. Relevant studies have reported [44, 45] that older adults without depression exhibit better psychological resilience, social adaptability, and coping mechanisms when facing challenges related to aging and illness, which contribute to maintaining good sleep quality. Depression can disrupt the circadian rhythm of older adults and increase the number of nighttime awakenings, leading to a series of sleep problems such as daytime sleepiness and nighttime insomnia [11, 46]. Depression also hinders social participation and engagement in social activities; therefore, mandatory social interactions cannot truly meet the need for autonomous socialization, thereby preventing the alleviation of negative emotions and further exacerbating sleep-related problems among older adults with depression [47, 48]. Additionally, the higher likelihood of belonging to Class 3 may be related to the fact that older adults with mental health conditions are 20 times more likely to be dependent on sedative-hypnotic medications compared with the general population [49]. The long-term use of hypnotic medications can lead to inappropriate medication use; it can trap individuals in a vicious cycle of sleep disorders and medication dependence, preventing improvement in sleep efficiency and resulting in overall poor sleep quality [31].

Impact of social support levels on latent classes of sleep quality

The results of the multinomial logistic regression indicated that compared with older adults belonging to Classes 2 and 3, those who belong to Class 1 displayed higher levels of social support. Perceived care and concern from neighbors and friends help meet the personal needs of older adults, while adequate material resources and good family functioning may buffer the decline in sleep quality caused by economic challenges [44, 50]. In addition, such support can provide practical assistance such as help with medication management, facilitate the establishment of regular daily routines, and ensure sufficient sleep duration [51]. Moreover, older adults with higher levels of utilization of social support are more likely to actively express their concerns and seek help. Through receiving comfort and support, they experience trust and emotional warmth, thereby maintaining a positive cycle between emotional well-being and sleep quality [52].

Implications and recommendations for sleep quality interventions among community-dwelling older adults

The prevalence of sleep disturbance among community-dwelling older adults is high, while a significant heterogeneity exists across the latent classes of sleep quality. Therefore, healthcare professionals should prioritize the early screening and comprehensive assessment of sleep problems.

Integrated interventions for sleep quality should address biological, psychological, and social dimensions to comprehensively improve sleep among older adults. At the biological level, the study recommends the incorporation of sensory function and oral health into comprehensive geriatric assessments, collaboration with ophthalmologists to alleviate visual discomfort, and implement synchronous screening for sleep-related breathing disorders in dental care, with sex-specific interventions. For unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, individualized nutritional management should be implemented to help older adults maintain a normal BMI. In developing personalized chronic disease management plans, medication management should be standardized and individualized precision pharmacotherapy implemented. Simultaneously, personalized exercise programs should be designed for community-dwelling older adults, including group activities such as Tai Chi and brisk walking, thereby establishing diversified non-pharmacological strategies for chronic disease management.

At the psychological and social levels, a multidimensional, collaborative support network that involves families and communities can be established to provide older adults with clear decision-making information and emotional support. Strengthening psychological empowerment for older adults is essential (e.g., guiding them to actively participate in social activities), because it can aid in alleviating psychological stress during decision-making processes. Measures such as cognitive–behavioral therapy can be implemented to enhance their self-management abilities. At the same time, efforts should be exerted to increase the dissemination of sleep health knowledge, particularly for older adults with low educational attainment, while respecting the autonomy of those living alone and their preferred lifestyles.

Limitations and outlook

This study has certain limitations, which also indicate clear directions for future research. First, the recruitment of participants from a single community hospital using convenience sampling limited the generalizability of the findings to older adults in other regions of China or with different economic conditions, and the cross-sectional design prevented the capture of dynamic changes in sleep quality and inter-variable relationships. Future studies could employ multicenter, stratified sampling approaches that consider population distribution by sex to enhance representativeness, adopt objective inclusion and exclusion criteria to minimize selection bias, and utilize longitudinal designs and complex network analysis models to monitor sleep quality over time and identify relationships and pathways among observed variables. Second, this study relied solely on the PSQI, a subjective measure of sleep quality, and dichotomized its subscales to enhance model stability and clinical interpretability, which limited the capture of objective and fine-grained changes in sleep indicators and constrained the identification of distinct latent sleep quality classes. Future studies could combine objective sleep monitoring technologies with subjective assessments and use larger samples to examine the robustness of class structures under different measurement approaches. Third, given the exploratory nature of this study, multiple statistical tests were conducted without adjustment for multiple comparisons, and some exposure variables included relatively few participants within specific latent sleep quality classes, which may have limited statistical power and increased the risk of Type I errors. In addition, potential non-linear relationships, such as between alcohol consumption and health, were not accounted for. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future large-scale studies with more balanced samples and refined analytical strategies are warranted to validate and further elucidate the heterogeneity of sleep quality among older adults. Finally, this study excluded several important contextual variables. Specifically, we did not examine sleep-related behavioral habits (e.g., napping patterns, late-evening dietary patterns, pre-sleep behaviors, and pre-sleep smartphone use). Future research should comprehensively capture the full spectrum of sleep behaviors, identify modifiable lifestyle factors related to sleep, and further explore their interactions with autonomy. Such efforts would broaden the biopsychosocial perspective and provide valuable evidence for developing targeted and age-inclusive sleep intervention strategies. Moreover, future studies should integrate key environmental factors (e.g., noise and light interference in living environments, and levels of light exposure) and a broader definition of medication use (e.g., ≥ 5 medications and the inclusion of traditional Chinese medicines and dietary supplements) into analytical models to construct a more comprehensive and scientifically rigorous framework for assessing sleep quality, thereby enhancing the practical applicability and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study found that 62.6% of the participants experienced impaired sleep quality. LCA revealed significant heterogeneity in sleep quality, which was categorized into Class 1 (high sleep quality, non-pharmacological dependence), Class 2 (high sleep efficiency, high functional impairment), and Class 3 (long sleep latency, low sleep efficiency). Healthcare professionals should prioritize early screening of sleep status among older adults and implement comprehensive interventions based on the biopsychosocial model. Furthermore, they should incorporate sensory function and oral health into comprehensive geriatric assessment, manage chronic diseases through individualized interventions, improve unhealthy lifestyle habits, standardize medication management, establish collaborative family and social support networks, and strengthen psychological empowerment. These strategies aim to improve sleep quality among older adults, thus enhancing quality of life and life expectancy. Future research should conduct large-sample, multicenter longitudinal studies, optimize assessment tools, and explore other factors that could influence sleep quality to provide scientific evidence for the development and implementation of personalized sleep intervention strategies for community-dwelling older adults.
