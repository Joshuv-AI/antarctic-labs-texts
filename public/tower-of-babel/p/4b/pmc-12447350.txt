Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colon Cancer (Lynch Syndrome): An Emerging Public Health Concern
Authors: Md Mohiuddin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLynch syndrome, also known as hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline mutations in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes that confer increased lifetime risks for colorectal, endometrial, and other cancers. Lynch syndrome accounts for only 3%–5% of colorectal cancer cases; however, most patients with Lynch syndrome are not diagnosed, leading to missed opportunities for enhanced surveillance and preventive interventions.DiscussionThis study examined the molecular genetics, epidemiology, and shortcomings of conventional, selective screening strategies for Lynch syndrome. Universal tumor testing using MMR immunohistochemistry (IHC) or microsatellite instability (MSI) analyses is recommended for all newly diagnosed colorectal and endometrial cancers, with reflex BRAF/methylation testing applied to distinguish likely sporadic cases. We also need to consider essential components of coordinated Lynch syndrome management, including cascade screening of family members, electronic health record (EHR) integration, multidisciplinary care teams, and public education. Selective screening for Lynch syndrome, based on the age or family history, misses many cases, whereas universal tumor testing is more cost‐effective for identifying more patients. In addition to genetic counseling, surveillance colonoscopy, prophylactic surgical measures, and psychosocial support, we must provide equitable access to these services for all potentially affected patients. Advances in technology, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) assays and polygenic risk scores, represent new methods for cancer screening and risk stratification.ConclusionIn summary, a coordinated, equity‐centric public health model for Lynch syndrome should incorporate universal tumor testing, cascade screening, integration of clinical workflows, and community outreach. This framework could potentially turn the “underrecognized” aspect of Lynch syndrome into a “largely preventable” cancer syndrome. The proposed model could also represent a way to develop a broader strategy for other hereditary cancer syndromes, as future research focuses on scalable implementation, real‐world cost‐effectiveness, and genomic population screening.

Introduction

Lynch syndrome, also known as hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), is the most common hereditary colorectal cancer syndrome; however, it continues to be severely underdiagnosed [1, 2, 3]. Carriers inherit germline mutations in one of the four main DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2), or large deletions of EPCAM, which transcribes MSH2 mRNA that is transcriptionally silenced by deletion [3, 4]. Loss of MMR activity leads to microsatellite instability (MSI), a hypermutator phenotype that accelerates the oncogenic process across multiple tissue types [5, 6]. For Lynch syndrome carriers, the lifetime risk of colorectal carcinoma may reach 80%, and the lifetime risk of endometrial cancer may reach 60% [3]. Unfortunately, awareness of this disease remains low at the population level, with only a few percent of estimated carriers identified before the diagnosis of cancer in some countries. This disconnection perpetuates and leads to preventable morbidity and mortality. Therefore, a change in the approach to Lynch syndrome should be made by shifting the approach from selective criteria to universal tumor testing with an emphasis on detection at earlier stages and the cascade screening of relatives to increase care.

Molecular Basis of Lynch Syndrome

MMR proteins identify and repair unpaired base–base mismatches and insertion–deletion loops that develop after DNA replication [7, 8]. In Lynch syndrome, heterozygous germline mutations disrupt one allele followed by somatic “second hits,” which disables the remaining allele and eliminates base–base repair activity [9, 10, 11]. Tumors growing under these circumstances accumulate frameshift mutations within microsatellite sequences, which can be detected by proving heterozygous MMR genes by PCR‐based MSI assays or by loss of the MMR protein by tissue immunohistochemistry (IHC). While MLH1 and MSH2 mutations provide the highest penetrance, mutations in MSH6 and PMS2 provide a later relative onset and lower absolute risks [12, 13, 14]. Deletions in the EPCAM gene can lead to Lynch syndrome by causing transcriptional read‐through into the neighboring MSH2 gene, resulting in the epigenetic silencing of MSH2 [15, 16]. The genotype–phenotype correlation further supports the differential surveillance intensity. Colorectal cancer (CRC) screening is recommended in the United States beginning at 20–25 years of age for MLH1 and MSH2 carriers, with interval colonoscopy every 1–2 years [17]. Screening for PMS2 and MSH6 is recommended starting at 25–30 years of age, with interval colonoscopy every 1–3 years [17]. Stratified surveillance colonoscopy age should be determined by the Lynch syndrome‐associated gene, according to the British guidelines [18]. For MLH1 and MSH2 mutation carriers, colonoscopy should be recommended at 25 years of age, whereas for MSH6 and PMS2 mutation carriers, colonoscopy should be recommended at 35 years of age [18].

Global Burden and Epidemiology

Lynch syndrome is responsible for 3%–5% of CRC cases worldwide, which translates to tens of thousands of people being diagnosed worldwide every year [3, 19]. In addition, Lynch syndrome carriers are also at increased risk for extracolonic malignancies, including endometrial, ovarian, gastric, urinary tract, small bowel and others. According to a recent study, Lynch syndrome affects approximately 1 in 354 individuals in a US cohort, with most cases caused by pathogenic variants in the MSH6 and PMS2 genes [20]. Approximately 1 million individuals have Lynch syndrome in the United States but are unaware of their diagnosis, suggesting that there is a significant undertreatment of this condition. In the UK, it is estimated that approximately 175,000 individuals have Lynch syndrome; however, only 5% of these individuals are aware of their condition [21]. Under‐recognition of Lynch syndrome can occur for multiple reasons. First, the disease can have variable presentation and patterns of inheritance. Second, many patients do not have thorough documentation of the medical records of family members with cancer. Third, organizations do not have a consensus on screening for Lynch syndrome. Thus, patients may present with cancer at a later stage and different preclinical stages that would have benefited from endoscopic or surgical means of prevention.

Weaknesses of Selective Screening

Many carriers of Lynch syndrome are not identified using selective screening strategies because they rely on incomplete clinical criteria and family history [22, 23]. The classical approaches for screening for Lynch syndrome are to use clinical criteria such as Amsterdam II or revised Bethesda guidelines or to wait until the patient is diagnosed with cancer before 50 years old. The Amsterdam I and II criteria and revised Bethesda criteria are not sensitive enough to identify Lynch syndrome with pathogenic variants in MSH6 and PMS2 [24]. Information about family history is often not fully captured and many patients do not know what their relatives are diagnosed with. Therefore, the approach to selective screening loses a significant number of carriers and indeterminate carriers, especially for patients without early onset CRC or those without a strong family pedigree. Age‐restricted screening strategies also perpetuate inequities in the care continuum; older patients, racial and ethnic minorities, and those without stable family information are also under‐screened.

Universal Tumor Testing and Reflex Testing

Universal tumor testing combined with reflex testing is becoming a key strategy for identifying Lynch syndrome and improving cost‐effective cancer care [25, 26, 27]. To maximize detection, professional societies now recommend Universal MSI testing for all CRC and endometrial cancers, regardless of the patient age or family history [28, 29, 30]. The use of IHC in tumor samples is an advantageous method for identifying missing proteins, specifically the lack of MSH6, which can then be used to identify the underlying cause of the tumor. MSI testing yields a binary result relative to the genomic stability. If a specimen shows MLH1 loss, reflex testing for BRAF V600E mutations and MLH1‐promoter hypermethylation can help to separate sporadic (epigenetic) silencing from germline (hereditary) MLH1 mutations [31, 32]. Compared with selective testing for newly diagnosed CRC patients, universal Lynch syndrome screening has been found to be cost‐effective by most measures [33, 34]. Integrating mandates for universal tumor testing into national cancer guidelines and insurer reimbursement policies would ensure thorough coverage and normalization.

Genetic Counseling and Cascade Testing

Cascade testing and genetic counseling are critical aspects of Lynch syndrome management, enabling the earlier detection and prevention of the disease [35, 36]. Once the index case is identified, it facilitates cascade testing and allows first‐ and second‐degree relatives to undergo targeted germline testing. Widespread accessibility of centralized stakeholders (public health registries) provides and protects index cases for efficient and effective future outreach to families regarding testing options, potentially in the payment plan of the index case. The use of technology, including automated systems and telehealth, minimizes geographic and other logistical hurdles to obtaining genetic counseling for first‐ and second‐degree relatives of probands. Supportive policies should guarantee genetic counseling before and after testing as an essential benefit, with no cost in public and private health insurance policies. By following recommended surveillance, undergoing genetic testing, and considering risk‐reducing procedures, relatives with Lynch syndrome can significantly reduce their cancer risk, potentially to near the general population level [37, 38].

Integration Into Clinical Workflows and EHR Systems

In clinical workflow, electronic health record (EHR) systems can enhance efficiency and streamline processes [39]. Any barrier introduced as part of the clinical pathway may result in missed referrals, delayed communication of results, and non‐integration of specialties, diminishing the benefits of universal tumor testing. Clinical decision support tools within EHRs can flag pathology reports for MMR deficiency, order genetic consultation automatically, and trigger alerts when patients fulfill the Amsterdam or revised Bethesda criteria. Automated referral pathways and reminders for follow‐up help reduce loss to follow‐up. EHR integration is expected to facilitate efficient genetic evaluation following abnormal tumor screening, allowing carriers to enter high‐intensity surveillance programs in a timely manner.

Multidisciplinary Care Teams

The complex nature of colon cancer with Lynch syndrome and the need for comprehensive, individual care make multidisciplinary team meetings essential for managing the disease [40]. Management of Lynch syndrome relies on the synergistic efforts of gastroenterology, oncology, pathology, genetics, surgery, and behavioral health specialists. Formal “Lynch care teams” meet to discuss patient cases, develop individual surveillance schedules, and discuss prophylactic surgery options. Incorporating a team approach into institutional accreditation standards and quality metrics can help guide resources and accountability in the clinical setting. Coordinated scheduling for colonoscopies at 1–2‐year intervals, endometrial sampling where indicated, and gynecological risk‐reducing surgeries will benefit patients with Lynch syndrome [41]. Multidisciplinary collaborative efforts also streamlined enrollment in clinical trials for new chemoprevention or immunotherapy strategies before illness progression.

Provider Education and Public Awareness

Lynch syndrome patients benefit from genetic education and family guidance regarding diagnostic testing as well as cancer surveillance and prevention [42]. Any policy or clinical practice changes will necessitate successful educational efforts. Lynch syndrome genetics, screening algorithms, and management guidelines should be incorporated into the core learning in medical schools and residency programs. Continuing educational medical modules for primary care, gastroenterology, gynecology, and pathology specialties can help contribute to the establishment of best practices. Furthermore, physicians of all specialties should have basic knowledge of Lynch syndrome, as a thorough family history and genetic testing will improve the chances of diagnosing Lynch syndrome [43]. Simultaneously, public awareness initiatives may utilize social media, patient advocacy organizations, and community health promotion events to demystify familial cancer risk and promote engagement with family health history discussions. Outreach efforts should include culturally relevant health education materials in consumer‐friendly formats—decision aids, fact sheets, and web‐based resources—to help empower patients to request testing and engage with screening recommendations.

Psychosocial Support and Ethical Issues

Psychological support can help Lynch syndrome patients cope with emotional challenges [40]. Genetic testing and the discovery that one carries an increased risk for cancer can lead to anxiety, distress, and tension between family members. There are benefits to providing psychosocial support through genetics services, including licensed genetic counselors and peer support groups, as well as decision aids when considering options for increased surveillance or preventative surgery. Policies should be shaped by ethical issues, including confidentiality of genetic information, fear of discrimination, and family member autonomy. Current legal policies in the United States, such as the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA), have provided a starting point, but advocacy is needed for life insurance and long‐term care insurance [44].

Economic Issues and Resource Allocation

A multifaceted approach, including significant public health investment and policy oversight, is required to successfully implement universal tumor testing and cascade screening for hereditary cancer syndromes, such as Lynch syndrome [35]. Although startup costs are associated with testing all tumors and cascade screening, economic studies continue to demonstrate net savings from cancer prevention, faster diagnosis at earlier stages, and less money spent on treatment. Reflex testing for BRAF mutations and MLH1 promoter hypermethylation can significantly improve the efficiency of testing, especially when evaluating patients for Lynch syndrome [32, 45]. There are considerations regarding resource allocation from public health authorities, including supporting pathology laboratories with operational costs for MMR assays for testing, subsidizing genetic testing for underserved populations, and grants for implementing EHRs to manage testing, care, and data. Public health authorities should monitor the key performance indicators of testing uptake, time to diagnosis, adherence to screening recommendations, and the incidence of cancer in carriers to adjust the policy.

Future Opportunities and Obstacles

Although there is strong evidence for the clinical and economic effectiveness of universal tumor testing and cascade screening for Lynch syndrome, particularly in high‐income regions, important implementation challenges still exist in real‐world healthcare settings. For those that are low‐resource or decentralized models of care, barriers, including infrastructure for molecular diagnostics, a limited number of trained pathologists and genetic counselors, and inconsistent payments and reimbursements for genetic services impede widespread adoption. These issues are compounded by fragmented pathways of care, lack of centralized cancer registries, and competing health priorities for the implementation of universal tumor testing and cascade screening. Targeted policy approaches, combined with coordinated efforts at the international level and investment in healthcare capacity building, must focus on leaky gaps in care and ensure equitable access to screening.

While inequities are pronounced in access to genetic counseling, particularly among underserved groups including racial and ethnic minorities, rural residents, and individuals with lower socioeconomic status, access to screening is generally inequitable. System‐level barriers that reduce timely referrals to genetic evaluations for testing include a lack of insurance coverage, health literacy, and transportation. The healthcare system should prioritize using educational and outreach strategies aimed at improving the cultural competence of targeted people groups as well as increasing the use of tele‐genetics services and insurance mandates.

New technologies provide great opportunities for enhanced access and improved outcomes in Lynch syndrome screening. For example, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and liquid biopsy technologies provide noninvasive methods for measuring microsatellite instability and identifying tumor‐specific mutations, making screening more appropriate for patients unable to undergo tissue‐based testing [46, 47]. In the future, polygenic risk scores will likely increase on a population basis the risk stratification of cancer risk of patients along with more traditional models, as well as multi‐cancer early detection (MCED) using blood‐based DNA methylation and genomic fragmentation signatures [48].

Future research should explore real‐world implementation of universal tumor screening protocols through the lens of implementation science to identify context‐specific barriers and facilitators for the implementation of universal tumor screening as a scalable model of screening. Further research is needed in cost‐effectiveness studies across public and private healthcare models, as well as population‐level genomic screening to identify at‐risk carriers before the onset of cancer.

A universal tumor testing and screening protocol that includes, but is not limited to, universal and reflex testing of tumors, cascade screening, EHR integration of diagnostic testing, and multidisciplinary care may be useful not only for Lynch syndrome screening but also for BRCA1/2‐associated hereditary breast and ovarian cancer screening and prevention [49, 50]. Collectively, these points could ultimately translate into a way to transform personalized prevention, reduce cancer‐related mortality, and provide equitable care for precision oncology.

Conclusion

Lynch syndrome represents some of the opportunities, challenges, and potential for precision in public health. By applying universal tumor testing and a seamless genetic service for health professionals, integrated clinical implementation, multidisciplinary cooperative care, community education, and psychosocial support for patients and their families, we have the potential to transition Lynch syndrome from being an underappreciated health threat to a success story in the field of cancer prevention, with broader implications for hereditary cancer syndromes. Future research can look at new circulating biomarkers for noninvasive detection of carriers, study the cost‐effectiveness of genomic screening for entire populations, and explore patient futures based on risk stratified by genotype. When policies, practices, and engagement efforts are linked by the system, the burden of Lynch‐associated cancer can be seen and can serve as a model for hereditary diseases worldwide.
