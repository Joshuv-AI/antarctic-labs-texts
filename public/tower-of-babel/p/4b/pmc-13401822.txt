Semaphorin 3E regulates the response of regulatory T cells to lipopolysaccharide-induced systemic inflammation
Authors: Sina Taefehshokr, Ifeoma Okwor, Lianyu Shan, Abdelilah S Gounni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a life-threatening systemic inflammation marked by an initial hyperinflammatory phase. Tregs are pivotal in tempering this early immune response. Semaphorins, originally neuronal guidance cues, have emerged as immune modulators. Notably, Sema3E influences dendritic cell and T cell responses, but its role in Treg-mediated regulation during endotoxemia is unclear. WT and Sema3e−/− mice were subjected to LPS-induced endotoxemia. Treg frequencies, proliferation and migration were assessed by flow cytometry and Transwell assays, respectively. Serum cytokines (eg IL-10) were quantified using ELISA. A TNFRSF25 agonist was administered to expand Tregs in vivo, and adoptive transfers of WT or Sema3e−/− Tregs were performed. Clinical scores and survival were recorded. Sema3e−/− mice exhibited significantly impaired Treg expansion and proliferation, altered migratory patterns with reduced Treg accumulation in spleen and lymph nodes, and consequently lower serum cytokine levels (eg IFN-γ) compared to WT, correlating with exacerbated disease severity. DR3-mediated Treg expansion in Sema3e−/− mice did not improve their clinical outcomes. Furthermore, adoptively transferred Sema3e−/− Tregs failed to confer protection in endotoxemic recipients, unlike WT Tregs. Sema3E is essential for optimal Treg responses and immune regulation during severe LPS-induced inflammation. Its absence compromises Treg expansion, localization, and function, worsening sepsis outcomes. These findings highlight Sema3E as a critical component of Treg-mediated immunosuppression and a potential therapeutic target for improving immune homeostasis in sepsis.Keywords: regulatory T cell, Sema3E, sepsis, systemic inflammation

Introduction

Sepsis represents a critical global health challenge, affecting approximately 49 million people annually and resulting in around 11 million deaths worldwide.1 Globally, an estimated 48.9 million individuals developed sepsis in 2017, resulting in approximately 11.0 million deaths. This accounts for 19.7% of all global deaths that year, highlighting the significant burden of sepsis on public health worldwide. Despite a decline in age-standardized incidence and mortality rates over recent decades, the age-standardized mortality rate in 2017 remained high at 148.1 deaths per 100,000 population, underscoring the continued need for improved prevention, early recognition, and treatment strategies, particularly in low and middle-income countries where the burden is disproportionately higher.1

The pathogenesis of sepsis involves a complex interplay between pathogen-associated molecular patterns, such as LPS, and the host immune response.2 LPS, a major component of gram-negative bacterial outer membranes, triggers systemic inflammation through its interaction with TLR4, initiating a cascade of immune responses that can lead to organ dysfunction and death if not properly regulated.3

Semaphorins, initially identified as axon guidance molecules, have emerged as crucial regulators of various physiological and pathological processes.4,5 The Semaphorin family comprises multiple classes, with Classes 1 and 2 found in invertebrates, Classes 3–7 in vertebrates, and Class 8 in viruses.6 Among these, Sema3E, a secreted protein, has garnered particular attention for its diverse roles beyond neuronal development.7,8 Recent research has revealed the significant functions of Sema3E in immune system regulation, including cell migration,9 proliferation,10 and cytokine release,11 primarily through its interaction with the PlexinD1 receptor.

Tregs play a pivotal role in sepsis by modulating the immune response, balancing inflammation control and pathogen clearance, and influencing the progression of septic complications.12 Sepsis typically begins with a hyper-inflammatory response, followed by an anti-inflammatory phase that can suppress effective immune defenses, leaving patients vulnerable to secondary infections.13 Tregs help control this inflammatory process, potentially limiting tissue damage, but their immunosuppressive effects can also hinder pathogen clearance.12,14 Recent research has demonstrated that Tregs play a crucial role in the development of septic complications, significantly influencing the suppression of the adaptive immune response.15 There are 2 main types of Tregs that influence peripheral immunity: natural Tregs and adaptive Tregs.16 Natural Tregs, identified as CD4+ CD25+ Foxp3+ cells, are derived from the thymus and are well established for their roles in maintaining tolerance and preventing autoimmunity.17 These cells represent about 5%–10% of CD4+ T cells in peripheral blood and lymphoid tissues.18,19 On the other hand, adaptive Tregs, such as Treg1 and other helper T cells, develop in the periphery from the activation of mature CD4+ CD25- T cells under the influence of cytokines like IL-1 and TGF-β.20

The relationship between Sema3E and Treg function has been demonstrated in the context of Leishmania major infection, where Sema3e deficiency results in reduced Treg numbers and enhanced resistance to infection.21 However, the specific role of Sema3E in modulating Treg responses during sepsis remains poorly understood.

This study aims to investigate the impact of Sema3E on Treg function, proliferation, and migration in the context of LPS-induced inflammation, using both in vitro and in vivo approaches. Our findings may contribute to the development of more effective treatments for sepsis and other inflammatory conditions where Treg dysfunction plays a crucial role.

Materials and methods

Animal housing and breeding

The Sema3e−/− mice (129 P2) utilized in this study were generously supplied by Dr. F. Mann from the Developmental Biology Institute of Marseille, Luminy, Université de la Méditerranée, Marseille, France.22 All experimental animals used in the study were male and were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at the Central Animal Care Services, University of Manitoba. All procedures adhered to the Canadian Council for Animal Care guidelines and were approved by the University of Manitoba Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol no. B2013-016/1).

Clinical score and body temperature assessment

Groups of 8- to 10-wk-old mice were injected intraperitoneally with 30 mg/kg of LPS from Escherichia coli 0111: B4 (Sigma-Aldrich, Mississauga, ON, Canada) or with saline. Body temperature and clinical scores were assessed every 3 h during the first 24 h post-LPS injection, including continuous overnight monitoring at 3-h intervals, and mice were assessed for movement, body condition, and alertness. A clinical score of 4 was used as the humane endpoint, as outlined previously.23 Briefly, disease severity was rated as follows: 0 = no abnormal clinical signs; 1 = ruffled fur but active; 2 = ruffled fur, reduced movement, hunched posture, and sick; 3 = ruffled fur, closed eyes, minimal movement, and severely ill; 4 = moribund; and 5 = dead. Institutional ethical regulations did not permit a clinical score of 5.

Sample collection and preparation

At the specified time points, animals were euthanized using 3% isoflurane gas. PL and blood samples were collected. Blood samples were left to clot at room temperature and then centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the serum, which was stored at −80 °C. PL was obtained by injecting 3 mL of RPMI 1640 medium into the abdominal cavity. The supernatant was stored at −80 °C for cytokine analysis, while the cell pellet was prepared for flow cytometry as we described previously.11

Flow cytometry

Cells were collected from LPS or saline-treated mice, as previously described.21 The PL cells were stained with fluorochrome-labeled antibodies against CD3 (clone 145-2C11, Pacific Blue), CD4 (clone RM4-5, PE-Cy7), CD25 (clone PC61, APC), and Foxp3 (clone FJK-16s, PE). Ki-67 (clone 16A8, PerCP/Cyanine5.5) was purchased from BioLegend. All other antibodies were obtained from eBioscience. Samples were acquired with FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences). Data were subsequently analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, Ashland, Oregon, USA).

ELISA

The levels of IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-17A, IL-1β, TNF, IL-10, IL-33, and TGF-β in PL and serum were quantified using ELISA MAX™ Deluxe Set Mouse kits (BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s suggested protocols. The detection limits for each cytokine were IL-6 (4 pg/mL), IFN-γ (4 pg/mL), IL-17A (0.8 pg/mL), IL-1β (2 pg/mL), TNF(4 pg/mL), IL-10 (4 pg/mL), IL-33 (4 pg/mL), and TGF-β (60 pg/mL).

Transwell migration assay

Spleens and peripheral lymph nodes were harvested from WT and Sema3e−/− mice. CD4+ T cells were enriched using the EasySep Mouse CD4+ T Cell Enrichment Kit (Stemcell Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified CD4+ T cells were then assessed for migration in response to chemokine gradients. Migration assays were performed in 24-well Transwell plates with 5 µm pore polycarbonate membranes (Corning). A suspension of 2 × 105 CD4+ T cells in 100 µL RPMI 1640 supplemented with 0.5% FBS was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 600 µL of medium alone or medium supplemented with various concentrations of chemokines (CCL19 and CXCL1, from R&D Systems). Plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 3 h. After incubation, cells that had migrated into the lower chambers were collected, stained for CD4, CD25, and FoxP3, and analyzed by flow cytometry. The percentage of CD4+ CD25+ FoxP3+ Tregs among migrated cells was compared between WT and Sema3e−/− groups.

Treg expansion using DR3 antibody

Briefly, 8- to 10-wk-old male and female WT and Sema3e−/− mice on a C57BL/6 background were used. Mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of 10 µg of agonistic anti-DR3 antibody (clone 4C12, BioLegend) or 10 µg of isotype control IgG. WT mice were assigned to either DR3-treated or control IgG groups. Sema3e−/− mice received either anti-DR3 or control IgG. Peripheral blood was collected on days 0, 3, 5, and 8 post-injections to monitor Treg expansion by flow cytometry. On day 5 post-treatment, mice were challenged with 30 mg/kg LPS or PBS by intraperitoneal injection. Clinical symptoms and rectal temperature were recorded at predetermined time points up to 48 h post-challenge. Survival was monitored, and endpoint samples were collected for serum cytokine analysis.

Treg adoptive transfer

Splenocytes were collected from WT and Sema3e−/− donor mice. Regulatory T cells were purified by negative selection using the EasySep Mouse CD4+ CD25+ Regulatory T Cell Isolation Kit II (Stemcell Technologies), achieving greater than 95% purity following the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 5 × 106 Tregs were resuspended in sterile PBS and injected intravenously into recipient mice via the tail vein. For transfer experiments, WT Tregs were transferred into Sema3e−/− recipients, and Sema3e−/− Tregs were transferred into WT recipients. Approximately 16–24 h after transfer, systemic inflammation was induced with an intraperitoneal injection of LPS (30 mg/kg). Recipient mice were monitored for clinical scores, body temperature, and survival.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as mean ± SEM. Significant differences in clinical signs, rectal temperature were assessed using 2-way ANOVA, with multiple group comparisons conducted via Tukey’s posttest. The significance of other data sets was determined using one-way ANOVA and unpaired t test. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA), with a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Expression of Sema3e and PlexinD1 in different organs

To examine the effect of systemic inflammation on the expression of Sema3e and its receptor PlexinD1, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 30 mg/kg of LPS, and mRNA levels were quantified in selected tissues at various time points post-injection using real-time PCR. As shown in Fig. 1, PlexinD1 was constitutively expressed in the liver, spleen, lymph nodes, and lungs, and its expression levels significantly increased over time following LPS administration. In contrast, Sema3e was constitutively expressed at higher levels in the liver and lung. Upon LPS challenge, its expression increased in the lung, spleen, and lymph node, while it was downregulated in the liver, with changes peaking between 6 and 12 h post-injection. These findings suggest that Sema3e and PlexinD1 are dynamically regulated in response to endotoxemia, with tissue-specific expression kinetics that may contribute to their differential roles in the immune response to LPS-induced systemic inflammation.

Data are from two independent experiments using age- and sex-matched mice (n = 3–4 per group). Statistical significance was assessed using two-way ANOVA with *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Sema3e deficiency impacts body temperature, clinical score, survival, and serum cytokine expression following LPS challenge

To investigate the role of Sema3E in the host response to acute systemic inflammation, WT and Sema3e−/− mice were injected intraperitoneally with 30 mg/kg LPS and monitored for body temperature, clinical score, and survival. Sema3e−/− mice exhibited a significantly greater drop in body temperature compared to WT mice (Fig. 2A). Clinical scores were initially higher in WT mice within the first 24 h post-injection; however, this trend reversed by 24 h (Fig. 2B). Moreover, Sema3e−/− mice showed a significantly lower survival rate compared to WT controls (Fig. 2C).

Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA for time-course analyses and one-way ANOVA for cytokine comparisons. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.

To assess the systemic inflammatory response, serum cytokine levels were measured 8 h post-injection. Sema3e−/− mice had significant reduced levels of IFN-γ (Fig. 2E) and TNF-α (Fig. 2I); however, IL-6 (Fig. 2D), IL-17A (Fig. 2F), IL-10 (Fig. 2G), and IL-1β (Fig. 2H) exhibited a lower trend that did not reach the significance in Sema3e−/− mice compared to WT mice, indicating an attenuated systemic cytokine response in the absence of Sema3e.

Sema3e KO mice had significantly lower Treg number in response to LPS in MLN, spleen and PL

Tregs are central to limiting tissue damage and restoring immune homeostasis during sepsis and systemic inflammation.24 Alterations in Treg dynamics can critically influence disease outcome by affecting the balance between inflammation and immune suppression.25 To assess the effect of Sema3E on Treg accumulation in response to systemic inflammation, WT and Sema3e−/− mice were injected intraperitoneally with LPS (30 mg/kg), and regulatory T cell (CD4+ CD25+ FoxP3+) populations were analyzed by flow cytometry at the indicated time points. Gating strategy for Treg identification is shown in Fig. S1.

In the MLNs, WT mice showed a progressive increase in Treg percentages following LPS injection, peaking at 24 h. In contrast, Sema3e−/− mice exhibited significantly lower Treg percentages throughout the time course (Fig. 3A). Similar results were observed in the spleen, where WT mice demonstrated a marked elevation in Treg percentages at 12 and 24 h post-injection, whereas this response was blunted in Sema3e−/− mice (Fig. 3B). Analysis of PL samples revealed a comparable trend, with WT mice showing a steady accumulation of Tregs over 24 h, and Sema3e−/− mice displaying significantly reduced Treg presence (Fig. 3C). These data suggest that Sema3E is required for optimal Treg expansion or recruitment in both lymphoid and peripheral compartments during acute inflammatory responses.

Data represent two independent experiments using age- and sex-matched mice (n = 4–5 per group). Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.

Sema3e deficiency impairs proliferation and alters migration of regulatory T cells in response to LPS stimulation

We analyzed Treg proliferation in vivo following LPS administration. WT and Sema3e−/− mice were injected intraperitoneally with LPS (30 mg/kg), and spleens and lymph nodes were harvested 2 h later. The percentage (Fig. 4A) and number (Fig. 4B) of proliferating Tregs (CD4+FoxP3+Ki67+) were significantly reduced in the spleens of Sema3e−/− mice compared to WT. A similar reduction was observed in the lymph nodes, with both percentage (Fig. 4C) and absolute cell counts (Fig. 4D) of Ki67+ Tregs being significantly lower in Sema3e−/− mice. These results indicate that Sema3E is required for optimal Treg proliferation in lymphoid organs following acute inflammatory stimulation.

Flow cytometry was employed to analyze Treg proliferation by assessing Ki67+ FoxP3+ CD4+ T cells in the spleen (A, B) and lymph nodes (C, D) 2 h in response to LPS in vivo (30 mg/kg, i.p.). Transwell assays were performed on CD4+ T cells isolated from spleen of WT and Sema3E−/− mice under control (E), also on CD25+ FoxP3+ Treg CXCL1 (F, G), and CCL19 (H, I) in similar conditions. Data represent two independent experiments using age and sex-matched mice (n = 3–4 per group). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA and unpaired t test. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.

Next, to assess the impact of Sema3E on Treg migratory capacity, CD4+ T cells isolated from spleen of WT and Sema3e−/− mice were subjected to transwell migration assays under basal conditions or toward CXCL1 or CCL19. Under control conditions (media only), Sema3e−/− CD4+ T cells exhibited enhanced migration compared to WT cells (Fig. 4E). Moreover, CXCL1 (Fig. 4G) and CCL19 (Fig. 4H) increased number of CD25+ FoxP3+ Treg migratory cell in both genotypes, but Sema3e−/− cells consistently showed a higher trend migratory responses. In parallel, the percentage of CD25+ FoxP3+ Tregs among migrated cells showed a higher trend in Sema3e−/− under both CXCL1 and CCL19 conditions (Fig. 4F, I).

TNFRSF25 (DR3) activation promotes Treg expansion but fails to improve clinical outcomes in Sema3e deficient mice

Given our earlier findings that Sema3e deficiency is associated with impaired Treg proliferation, we sought to determine whether enforced expansion of Tregs could restore immune regulation and improve outcomes in the LPS-induced systemic inflammation model.

TNFRSF25; also known as DR3, is a well-established method for expanding Tregs, as demonstrated in both mouse and human studies26,27DR3 is constitutively and highly expressed by CD4+ FoxP3+ Tregs, and its activation via agonistic anti-DR3 antibody leads to their rapid and preferential expansion in vivo.26

We first validated the effectiveness of DR3 stimulation in expanding Tregs. As shown CD25+ FoxP3+ Tregs were identified from CD45+ CD3+ CD4+ lymphocytes in whole blood. Following DR3 stimulation, Treg frequencies in the peripheral blood of both WT and Sema3e−/− mice increased significantly, peaking between 3 and 5 h post-injection before gradually declining by 8 h compared to control Ig treatment in both WT and Sema3e−/− mice (Fig. 5A). This dynamic response confirmed the transient but robust proliferative effect of TNFRSF25 engagement.

Body temperature (B), survival percentage (C), and clinical scores (D) were monitored over 48 hours. Serum cytokine levels were measured 8 hours post-LPS injection for IFN-γ (E), IL-1β (F), IL-6 (G), IL-10 (H), IL-33 (I), TGF-β (J), and TNF (K). Data represent 2 independent experiments with age and sex-matched mice (n = 3–5 per group). Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA for time-course analyses and one-way ANOVA and unpaired t test for cytokine comparisons. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.

Despite this successful expansion of Tregs, DR3 treatment did not result in improved clinical outcomes. Core temperature analysis revealed that both WT and Sema3e−/− mice treated with DR3+LPS experienced profound hypothermia, with body temperatures dropping to approximately 22 °C by 15 h post-injection, whereas WT mice receiving LPS alone maintained relatively higher temperatures (Fig. 5B). The survival analysis shows that despite DR3 treatment, both WT and Sema3e−/− mice exhibited significantly reduced survival rates, dropping to 40% by 40 h post-LPS injection (Fig. 5C).

Interestingly, clinical scores were significantly higher in the WT DR3 + LPS group compared to WT LPS alone, reaching nearly 2 by 9 h, suggesting that expanded Tregs may have worsened systemic inflammation in this context. In contrast, clinical scores in the Sema3e−/− DR3+LPS and Sema3e−/− LPS groups remained similar, indicating that DR3-induced Treg expansion failed to improve clinical signs in the absence of Sema3e (Fig. 5D).

Analysis of cytokine profiles (Figs. 5E–K) shows DR3 treatment had minimal impact on the inflammatory response in Sema3e-deficient mice. IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-33, TGF-β, and TNF levels remained largely unchanged between Sema3e−/− LPS and Sema3e−/− DR3 + LPS groups, with no statistically significant differences. These results indicate that DR3 treatment did not substantially alter the cytokine profile in Sema3e deficient mice.

Together, these data indicate that while DR3 stimulation effectively expands Tregs in vivo, this expansion fails to confer clinical benefit in Sema3e-deficient mice.

Regulatory T cell adoptive transfer reveals Sema3E-Dependent Treg-Intrinsic regulation

To assess whether Sema3e expression in Tregs is intrinsically required for their immunosuppressive function, we conducted adoptive transfer experiments using purified Tregs from WT or Sema3e−/− mice. WT Tregs were transferred into Sema3e−/− recipients (WT-Sema3E), and Sema3e−/− Tregs were transferred into WT recipients (Sema3E-WT), followed by LPS challenge.

Mice that received Sema3e-deficient Tregs (Sema3E-WT) exhibited significantly worse clinical outcomes. They developed higher clinical scores (Fig. 6A), experienced a more rapid and pronounced drop in rectal temperature (Fig. 6B), and showed significant reduced survival, with all animals succumbing by 14 hours post-injection (Fig. 6C). In contrast, mice receiving WT Tregs (WT-Sema3E) were partially protected, displaying lower clinical scores, more stable temperatures, and a 60% survival rate beyond 24 h.

WT and Sema3e−/− Tregs were adoptively transferred into Sema3e−/− or WT mice, respectively, followed by LPS injection (30 mg/kg, i.p.). Clinical scores (A), rectal temperature (B), and survival percentage (C) were monitored post-injection. Data represent 2 independent experiments using age- and sex-matched mice (n = 5–6 per group). Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA and Log-rank test where appropriate. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 and ****P < 0.0001.

These results demonstrate that the protective function of Tregs during systemic inflammation depends on cell-intrinsic expression of Sema3e. Even in a Sema3e-deficient host environment, WT Tregs retained regulatory capacity, whereas Sema3e-deficient Tregs failed to confer protection in otherwise WT recipients.

Discussion

Our findings reveal a multifaceted role for Sema3E in shaping the immune response during endotoxemic shock. We observed that Sema3e expression is dynamically upregulated in key organs (eg liver and lungs) following LPS challenge, paralleling reports that inflammatory stimuli induce Sema3e in various tissues.28 Functionally, Sema3e deficiency profoundly altered sepsis outcomes. Sema3e−/− mice experienced exacerbated hypothermia, worse clinical scores, and higher mortality compared to WT controls, despite mounting a significantly attenuated cytokine response (lower serum IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-10, and IL-17A at 8 h post-LPS). This seemingly paradoxical combination of reduced early inflammation yet worse survival suggests that Sema3E is not simply a pro or anti-inflammatory molecule but rather a context-dependent immunoregulatory factor.

The lower cytokine levels in Sema3e−/− mice indicate that Sema3E is required for a full-blown initial inflammatory response. This aligns with our previous data, Sema3E through PlexinD1, enhances macrophage signaling (ERK, AKT, STAT3, NF-κB) in endotoxemia.11 Consistently, Sema3e−/− mice released less TNF-α and IL-6 after LPS. Paradoxically, this weaker early response did not improve survival. Instead, Sema3e−/− mice fared worse, implying that some level of early cytokine surge (eg TNF-α) is necessary for host defense.29,30 Indeed, TNF-α activates protective immunity and helps clear pathogens, so insufficient TNF may leave the host vulnerable despite less tissue damage.29,30 These observations suggest that Sema3E helps calibrate the balance enabling a robust initial response that is later kept in check by regulatory mechanisms, avoiding both uncontrolled inflammation and inadequate immunity.

The pleiotropic nature of the immune effects of Sema3E is further illustrated by its cell-type-specific actions. Sema3E can directly bind PlexinD1 on DCs, which influences their cytokine production and migration.31 In one context (Leishmania infection), DCs from Sema3e−/− mice produced more IL-12p40 and costimulatory molecules than WT DCs, indicating Sema3E normally restrains DC activation.21 Similarly, Sema3e−/− bone marrow DCs exhibit higher CCR7, Rac1 activity, and F-actin, leading to enhanced migration in allergic inflammation.32 By contrast, in a sterile LPS challenge, Sema3E may act in a pro-inflammatory capacity, as evidenced by reduced NF-κB signaling in Sema3e−/− macrophages.11 Thus, Sema3E can dampen DC-driven responses in some settings while promoting macrophage activation in others. This pleiotropy is echoed by other Class III Semaphorins, for instance, Sema3A can both induce cytokines in sepsis and ameliorate sepsis and allergy.33 Similarly, Sema3E was recently found to mitigate intestinal inflammation in colitis by restraining NF-κB activity in CD11c+ cells,34,35 yet absence of Sema3E exacerbated pathology in an acute bacterial infection model due to failure to mount effective Th1/Th17 immunity.36

The most striking effect of Sema3e deficiency we observed was on regulatory T cells. Normally, the percentage of CD4+ CD25+ FoxP3+ Tregs steadily rises after LPS challenge (peaking 12–24 h), reflecting their expansion after the initial cytokine storm.37 Sema3e−/− mice, however, failed to show this expansion in spleen, MLNs, or peritoneal cavity. Consequently, Sema3e−/− mice had significantly fewer Tregs in lymphoid and peripheral tissues during inflammation. Tregs are known to surge during sepsis and contribute to the immunosuppressive phase;29,38,39 too few Tregs can allow unchecked inflammation, while too many cause “immune paralysis.”29,30,38 This role is consistent with infection models. In Leishmania major, Sema3e−/− mice showed reduced Tregs and IL-10 at lesions, correlating with stronger Th1 responses and faster parasite control.21 Conversely, in Chlamydia lung infection, Sema3e−/− mice accumulated more Tregs with a Th2 bias, impeding bacterial clearance.36 These contrasting outcomes likely reflect different pathogen contexts, but both highlight that Sema3E influences Treg populations. Mechanistically, T cells themselves express PlexinD1,40 and Semaphorin signals can directly modulate T cells; for instance, Sema4A binds neuropilin-1 on Tregs to maintain their stability.41,42 Sema3E does not use neuropilin-1 as coreceptor43 but may still act as a co-stimulatory cue for Tregs. Additionally, Sema3E can shape the DC environment, DCs from Sema3e−/− mice have higher PD-L1 and lower IL-12,36 conditions that favor Treg development. Conversely, adding Sema3E lowers PD-L1 on DCs and shifts T cell responses toward Th1/Th17.21,44 Together, these findings suggest Sema3E coordinates DC-T cell crosstalk to promote Treg expansion when needed. Importantly, these examples imply that targeting Sema3E could have complex effects depending on the infectious context. Recent evidence suggests that semaphorins can regulate T cell function through metabolic reprogramming.45 A recent study demonstrated that Semaphorin3A suppresses CD4+ T-cell activation by inhibiting the AKT/mTORC1 signaling pathway, leading to reduced glycolysis, mitochondrial respiration, and ATP production, while promoting fatty acid oxidation.45 This metabolic shift effectively drives activated T cells toward a more quiescent, regulatory-like state. Although our study focuses on Sema3E, these findings suggest that semaphorin family members may share conserved mechanisms in modulating T cell responses through immunometabolic pathways. In this context, it is plausible that the impaired Treg expansion observed in Sema3e-deficient mice may reflect, at least in part, defective metabolic adaptation during systemic inflammation. Future studies will be required to determine whether Sema3E directly regulates metabolic signaling pathways such as AKT/mTOR in Tregs.

Our functional assays revealed two counterbalancing defects in Sema3e−/− Tregs. In vitro, Sema3e−/− Tregs showed enhanced migration toward media or chemokine gradients (CCL19, CXCL1). This fits with a known chemorepellent role of Sema3E46 in neutrophils and DCs, Sema3E/PlexinD1 signaling inhibits Rac1/F-actin polymerization and chemotaxis32,47 Without Sema3E, Tregs may exit lymphoid organs more readily, possibly redistributing to other compartments. This altered trafficking could leave some niches under-protected, even if more Tregs are in circulation, or lead to excessive Tregs in certain sites, further dysregulating immune balance. Despite their increased motility, Sema3e−/− Tregs were intrinsically impaired in proliferation. Just 2 h post-LPS, WT mice had many Ki-67+ Tregs in spleen and lymph nodes, whereas Sema3e−/− mice had a much smaller proliferative surge. This suggests a cell-intrinsic defect or missing growth signals. Treg expansion in sepsis depends on IL-2 (from effector T cells) and TNF signaling via TNFR2.29,30

Recent studies further support the concept that semaphorins regulate immune responses through metabolic reprogramming of T cells. For example, Semaphorin3A has been shown to inhibit the AKT/mTORC1 signaling pathway in CD4+ T cells, leading to reduced glycolysis, mitochondrial respiration, and ATP production, while promoting fatty acid oxidation. This metabolic shift drives activated T cells toward a more quiescent or regulatory-like state. Although our study focuses on Sema3E, these findings suggest that semaphorin family members may share conserved mechanisms in modulating T cell function through immunometabolic pathways. In this context, it is plausible that Sema3E contributes to Treg expansion and function not only through effects on migration and proliferation but also by influencing metabolic programs that support regulatory activity. Future studies will be required to determine whether Sema3E directly modulates metabolic signaling pathways such as AKT/mTOR in Tregs.

Indeed, TNF-TNFR2 interactions are critical for post-sepsis Treg proliferation and blocking them can improve early outcomes,29,30 In our Sema3e−/− mice, systemic TNF was low (Fig. 2I), which likely limited Treg expansion. Sema3E may also directly promote Treg cycling for example, its signaling could influence Akt/mTOR pathways,11,31 as it does in other cells. Clarifying this will require molecular studies of Sema3E-PlexinD1 signaling in Tregs.

We next asked whether the Treg defect in Sema3e−/− mice was quantitative or qualitative. Using an agonistic anti-DR3 antibody (TNFRSF25), we potently expanded Tregs in vivo.26 Anti-DR3 drives rapid, preferential Treg proliferation and has been used therapeutically in models of autoimmunity and transplantation.48,49 Indeed, DR3 treatment greatly increased Tregs in both WT and Sema3e−/− mice. However, expanded Sema3e−/− mice remained as susceptible to LPS as before; their clinical scores, temperature drops, survival rates, and cytokine profiles were unchanged. Thus, simply increasing the number of Sema3e-deficient Tregs was insufficient to rescue their function. Even in WT mice, DR3-expanded Tregs did not improve survival (and appeared to slightly worsen early clinical scores), consistent with reports that excessive Tregs can impede necessary immune responses.37 These outcomes echo prior findings, while adoptive Tregs can be protective in some sepsis models,50 indiscriminate early expansion can also be harmful.37 Altogether, our DR3 data indicate that Treg quantity alone cannot compensate for Sema3E loss and Tregs themselves must be functionally competent, which in turn requires Sema3E.

Finally, adoptive transfer experiments provided strong evidence that Sema3E is needed intrinsically in Tregs. Transferring WT Tregs into Sema3e−/− mice significantly protected them. These mice had lower clinical scores, more stable body temperatures, and much higher survival. By contrast, transferring Sema3e−/− Tregs into WT hosts failed to confer protection, these mice declined rapidly and died. Thus, even a normal (Sema3e-sufficient) environment could not compensate for Sema3e-deficient Tregs. This mirrors the concept that intrinsic signals in Tregs (such as IL-2 responsiveness, co-stimulation, or Semaphorin engagement) are critical for their suppressive function.42,51 We speculate that Sema3E may influence how Tregs form immunological synapses. For example, PlexinD1 signaling can inhibit Rap1,52–54 modulating integrin-mediated adhesion. If Sema3E similarly controls Rap1 in Tregs, its absence could weaken Treg adhesion or synapse stability with target cells, further undermining their function.

In conclusion, this work sheds light on a previously underappreciated factor, Sema3E, in the immunopathology of sepsis. By integrating evidence from acute endotoxemia model, we propose that Sema3E is a critical facilitator of a balanced immune response, and it helps drive the initial defense and simultaneously poises the regulatory arm (Tregs) to prevent excessive damage. The requirement for Sema3E within Tregs themselves to mediate protection is particularly novel, revealing an intrinsic role for Semaphorin signals in Treg function. These insights open new avenues for research, dissecting the molecular signaling in Sema3e deficient Tregs, exploring role of Sema3E in septic patients, and potentially targeting the Sema3E-PlexinD1 axis to restore immune homeostasis in sepsis. Ultimately, therapeutic fine-tuning of the immune system in sepsis may benefit from leveraging such pathways to achieve the right equilibrium between attack and regulation. Our study provides a strong rationale for further exploration of Sema3E as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in systemic inflammatory syndromes.
