Simultaneously improved dipolar interaction at inorganic-inorganic and organic-inorganic interfaces for multimode energy harvesting from heterostructured CoFe2O4@BCZT loaded P(VDF-TrFE)
Authors: Abhishek Sasmal, Payel Maiti, J. Arout Chelvane, A. Arockiarajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hybrid CoFe2O4@0.5(Ba0.7Ca0.3)TiO3-0.5Ba(Ti0.8Zr0.2)O3 (CF@BCZT) heterostructured nanomaterials have been incorporated into poly(vinylidene fluoride–trifluoroethylene) (P(VDF-TrFE)) to achieve enhanced magnetoelectric (ME) performance in flexible polymeric composites. The magnetostriction of CF, in this case, stimulates both the P(VDF-TrFE) and highly piezoelectric BCZT dipoles at inorganic-organic and inorganic-inorganic interfaces, respectively, thereby achieving ME coupling coefficient as high as 96 mV·cm−1·Oe−1. The optimized device shows excellent DC and AC magnetic field sensitivity and associated magnetic field energy harvesting ability. On the other hand, due to high polarity of the developed composites and high tribo-negativity of PVDF, the developed films show excellent performance as piezoelectric and piezo-tribo hybrid nanogenerators (PENGs and HNGs, respectively) too. The optimized PENG delivers ~ 75 µW/cm2 of power density with ~ 46% of efficiency and is able for efficient static and dynamic pressure sensing, bending strain sensing, and associated piezoelectric energy harvesting. On the other hand, the optimized HNG, with ~ 255 µW/cm2 of output power density, shows its excellent performance for various regular and irregular biomechanical and ambient mechanical energy harvesting, associated self-powered sensing, and powering up small electronics. Thus, the developed composites can be regarded as an excellent multifunctional material for highly efficient multi-mode (magnetoelectric, piezoelectric, and piezo-tribo hybrid) energy harvesting and sensing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-13253-3.

Introduction

To address the issues of energy crisis, renewable energy harvesting from various natural and ambient sources has gained immense attention in recent times. These devices are targeted to develop in such a way that they can operate in self-powered mode for a long period of time with zero carbon emission so that they can become a suitable alternative of conventional batteries. Piezoelectric, triboelectric, thermoelectric, magnetoelectric (ME), photovoltaics, etc. devices are the forerunners in this regard. ME devices, in particular, are of great interest since the discovery of ME effect due to its various advantageous applications such as magnetic field sensing, developing non-volatile memory, touchless sensing, spintronics, energy harvesting and many others1. However, most of the ME devices developed or commercially available so far are rigid (or less flexible) in nature which limits their applications in flexible form. But, in modern day lifestyle, progress and development of flexible electronic devices are rapidly growing up in different forms such as wearable sensors, flexible energy conversion, storage, and sensing devices, rollable displays, flexible healthcare monitoring devices, and many others due to their various advantages1. Therefore, considerable attention has been paid during the past few years to develop highly flexible ME devices too. If the ME devices are developed in flexible form, they can be very advantageous for various important real-life applications including soft robotics and artificial intelligence, flexible health condition monitoring, human-machine interaction in touchless form, and many others1,2. One another importance of any kinds of flexible energy harvesting devices is their ability to generate electricity from very low amplitude of input stimuli3. Many bioactive soft systems are also able to mimic the mechanical properties of human tissues making them very useful for self-powered bio-electronic applications. Unlike piezoelectric and triboelectric devices, magnetoelectric devices exhibit low internal impedance and can render high output current density making them very advantageous1,4. Therefore, the primary focus of this work is to develop flexible ME devices and their various energy harvesting and sensing performance.

One of the very common and easy ways to develop highly flexible ME devices is to incorporate magnetostrictive nanostructures into piezoelectric polymer matrix. Magnetostriction of these nanostructures renders the desired mechanical stimuli to the piezoelectric polymeric dipoles thereby producing the output electricity5. As the main piezoelectric matrix is polymeric, very small mechanical stimulation to the said dipoles are sufficient for the desired electricity generation making them suitable for low magnetic field harvesting and sensing3. The main limitation in this case is their low output ME coupling and tendency of breakdown for higher amount of filler loading3. Previous reports suggest that after ~ 20 wt% of CoFe2O4 loading in poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) based polymers, breakdown strength and output performance of the composites deteriorate due to filler agglomeration3,6. So, here we focus on using some amounts of piezoelectric nanomaterials (commonly insulating) along with the magnetic nanostructures so that the breakdown strength can be increased. The magnetostriction in this case can render the mechanical stimulations both on the dipoles of piezoelectric nanomaterials and piezoelectric polymers. As inorganic piezoelectric materials exhibit very high piezoelectric coefficient compared to that of polymers, the output ME coefficient in this case is expected to be very high. Although, the co-loading of individual piezoelectric and magnetostrictive nanomaterials in piezoelectric polymer can be a suitable choice in this regard7 here we focus on using heterostructured filler in piezoelectric polymeric matrix. The main idea of using the said heterostructures is to improve the interfacial connectivity between the inorganic magnetostrictive and inorganic piezoelectric component so that high output performance can be achieved.

For this purpose, highly piezoelectric and well-known poly(vinylidene fluoride – trifluoroethylene) (P(VDF-TrFE))8 has been chosen here as the host polymeric matrix. Similarly, morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) based 0.5(Ba0.7Ca0.3)TiO3−0.5Ba(Ti0.8Zr0.2)O3 (BCZT), which has been widely explored as filler in PVDF matrix9–11 has been chosen here as the inorganic piezoelectric component. On the other hand, CoFe2O4 (CF) is the most widely used filler in PVDF based matrix for the development of highly flexible ME devices6,12–16. Therefore, here we focus on growing nano CF on the surface of BCZT particles to develop the desired heterostructures via chemical synthesis technique. Then the developed CF@BCZT heterostructures is focused to be incorporated into the P(VDF-TrFE) matrix. Although CF has been previously used in PVDF or P(VDF-TrFE) matrix for ME application6,12–16 CF@BaTiO3 has also been used in P(VDF-HFP) for ME devices17co-loading of BaTiO3 and CF in PVDF has been used for dielectric application7 CF@BCZT heterostructures have been incorporated into PVDF for dielectric energy storage18and BaTiO3 coated CF core-shell heterostructures has also been used earlier for ME applications3 the use of CF@BCZT heterostructure in P(VDF-TrFE) for ME application has not been studied earlier. Therefore, this work is expected to enrich the knowledge base in this field tor the future research and development. On the other hand, PVDF based composites are previously reported to be very excellent for piezoelectric and triboelectric energy harvesting applications. The use of polymer composites for multifunctional energy harvesting and sensing has gained immense attention in present days19–23. Therefore, the developed ME composites in this work are also focused on to be used for the development of piezoelectric and piezo-tribo hybrid nanogenerators (PENGs and HNGs, respectively) for the investigation of multi-mode energy harvesting and self-powered sensing.

Experimental section

Synthesis of BCZT nanoparticles

The BCZT nanoparticles were synthesized via a conventional modified sol-gel route as reported in our earlier works9,24. Briefly, 18.828 g of C6H8O7·H2O (Merck, ≥ 99.0%) was first dissolved in ~ 80 mL of deionized water (DI water) (Evergreen) via magnetic stirring for ~ 20 min and then 20.04 mL of ethylene glycol (Merck, ≥ 99.0%, density = 1.11 kg/L) was added to it and mixed well. After preparing this solution, 4.869 g of Ba(CH3COO)2 (Sigma Aldrich, 99%) and 0.532 g of Ca(CH3COO)2 (Fisher Scientific, > 98%) were dissolved in it one by one via room temperature magnetic stirring. 5.737 g of C12H28O4Ti (Sigma Aldrich, 97%) dissolved in ~ 40 mL of absolute ethanol (Merck) and 1.049 g of C12H28O4Zr solution (70 wt% in 1-propanol) (Sigma Aldrich) dissolved in ~ 20 mL of absolute ethanol were then slowly added to the previous mixed solution under continuous magnetic stirring. This mixed solution was then slowly provided with heat (up to hot plate temperature: 240 °C, solution temperature: ~ 90–95 °C) and stirred continuously for ~ 4–5 h. The solution became completely transparent, and its volume significantly reduced to ~ 50 mL. After 1 more hour of stirring in this condition, the solution turned into a fluffy viscous gel (almost white in colour). It was then dried at 250 °C for ~ 16 h and the resulting black dried gel was grounded well with a mortar-pestle. This black powder was then calcined at 950 °C for 4 h and finally grounded again to obtain the final BCZT nanoparticles (milky white in colour).

Synthesis of CF@BCZT heterostructures

CF@BCZT heterostructures were synthesized via a hydrothermal technique. For this purpose, 9.6 g of NaOH (Merck) was dissolved in 30 mL of DI water and 0.2 g of the synthesized BCZT nanoparticles were dispersed in this solution via magnetic stirring and ultrasonication (for ~ 15–20 min in total) (solution-A). Meanwhile, required amounts of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (LOBA Chemie, 98%) and Co(NO3)2·6H2O (Merck, ≥ 98.0%) were dissolved in 30 mL of DI water to prepare another solution (solution-B). The solution-B was then slowly added to the solution-A (during 7–10 min window) and mixed well via stirring and sonication (10–15 min in total). It was then transferred to a 100 mL Teflon lined stainless steel autoclave and heated at 130 °C for 15 h. Then, after the autoclave naturally came to room temperature, the precipitate was collected via centrifugation. During the centrifugation, the precipitate was washed several times with DI water until the pH reached to 7 and finally twice with absolute ethanol. The precipitate was then dried at 80 °C for ~ 24 h to get the final CF@BCZT heterostructures. The amounts of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O and Co(NO3)2·6H2O were varied to 0.48 and 0.173 g, 0.96 and 0.345 g, and 1.92 and 0.69 g to obtain a series of CF@BCZT heterostructures which were named 1CFBCZT, 2CFBCZT, and 3CFBCZT, respectively. The schematic illustration of the synthesis of BCZT and CF@BCZT is shown in Fig. 1.

Fabrication of P(VDF-TrFE)/CF@BCZT composite films

P(VDF-TrFE) based composite films were prepared via simple solution casting technique. For this purpose, 2.195 g of P(VDF-TrFE) powder (70:30, Solvene 300/P300, Sigma Aldrich) was dissolved in 20 mL of methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) (Merck) via room temperature magnetic stirring to prepare a P(VDF-TrFE) stock solution. Meanwhile, 0.082 g of filler particles were dispersed in 1.5 mL of MEK inside a capped glass bottle via ~ 1 h of ultrasonication. Then 1.5 mL of P(VDF-TrFE) stock solution was added to it and sonicated again for ~ 2 h. The temperature of the ultrasonicator water bath was maintained at ~ 30 °C during this process by using ice cubes. The obtained homogeneous solution (total volume: 3 mL) was then poured into a clean petri-dish (diameter ~ 5.8 cm) and annealed at 138 °C for ~ 1 h inside a levelled clean oven to evaporate MEK completely and to facilitate good nucleation of β polymorph. Then the annealed composite was allowed to cool down to room temperature naturally and the flexible film of ~ 0.07 mm thickness was peeled-off from the petri-dish and preserved for further use. Here, it is to be mentioned that the amount of P(VDF-TrFE) in the stock solution and the amount of filler particles in the mentioned 1.5 mL of dispersion was so chosen that the proportion of P(VDF-TrFE) become 12 wt% with respect to MEK and the proportion of filler become 20 wt% with respect to P(VDF-TrFE) in the final 3 mL of casting solution. For all the fillers, this 20 wt% loading was kept same. A pristine P(VDF-TrFE) film was also prepared by the same method and was named as PTR. BCZT, 1CFBCZT, 2CFBCZT, and 3CFBCZT loaded P(VDF-TrFE) films were named PTR-BCZT, PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT, respectively.

Fabrication of ME, PENG, and HNG devices

To prepare the ME device, both sides of the composite films cut into specific sizes were electroded with high quality silver paint (electrode size ~ 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm). They were then poled for ~ 3 h at room temperature by the application of a DC discharge of ~ 10 kV using a Corona poling unit. Both the electrodes were then connected with silver (Ag) wires by using silver epoxy which was dried at room temperature. The PENGs were prepared by simply sandwiching the composite films of specific size in between two adhesive aluminium (Al) electrodes of 2 cm × 3 cm dimensions. Two copper wires were then connected to these electrodes for the measurement of electrical signal. The whole device was encapsulated by using silicone elastomer (SYLGARD™ 184 silicone elastomer base and curing agent (The DOW Chemical Company)) in order to protect them from mechanical damage and any possible unwanted triboelectricity. In order to fabricate flexible HNGs, flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) films were used as the support structure and the devices were fabricated by using contact-separation mode of triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG). Here, it is to be mentioned that as the triboelectric layers to be used here (developed composite films) are also piezoelectric in nature, the output should contain both the effect of piezoelectricity and triboelectricity and hence, the devices are termed piezo-tribo hybrid nanogenerators (HNGs). To prepare the HNGs, two PET sheets were first cut into desired sizes (~ 5 cm × 7 cm) from a big commercial PET film. On the centre of one PET sheet, an adhesive Al electrode of 2 cm × 3 cm size was affixed and connected with a conducting copper wire to prepare sheet-A. On the other hand, one side of a PTR based composite film (cut into specific size) was affixed with Al electrode of same size and connected with a copper wire. This electroded side was then affixed on the centre of another PET sheet to prepare sheet-B. These two PET sheets (sheet-A and sheet-B) were then assembled into a device form (the HNG device) by using two sponge spacer such that the Al electrode of sheet-A and the bare film surface (PTR based films) of sheet-B stays face-to-face in the centre with an air gap of ~ 3–4 mm. The schematic illustration of all of the mentioned three types of devices and the digital photographs of some typical devices are shown in Fig. S1 (Supporting Information).

Characterizations

Structural investigation of the CF@BCZT heterostructures was performed using the powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique (Rigaku, MiniFlex) with the help of Cu Kα radiation and their morphological investigations were performed using transmission electron microscope (TEM (Tecnai)) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM (JEOL)). The associated elemental confirmation was achieved by using the energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscope equipped with the TEM instrument. A Lake Shore Cryotronics-7404 vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) was utilized to determine the applied magnetic field (H) dependent magnetization (M) hysteresis loops (M-H loops) of the synthesized heterostructures and the developed composite films. The morphology of the composite films wad investigated using a scanning electron microscope (SEM (Zeiss)) and the associated elemental studies were carried out using a EDX spectroscope (HORRIBA) equipped with it. The structural property of the composites was investigated by a thin film XRD instrument (Rigaku). Phase analysis of the composites was performed by studying their vibration characteristics using Bruker-α-Platinum attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscope. Their dielectric properties were investigated using a Wayne Kerr 6500B impedance analyzer. The polarization (P) – electric field (E) hysteresis loops (P-E loops) of the composite films were recorded by using a custom designed modified Sawyer-Tower circuit. For this purpose, the necessary input signal (1 Hz triangular waveform) to the sample (kept inside an insulated high voltage sample holder) was provided with the help of a Tektronix AFG3022B function generator which was amplified by a Trek PD05034 high voltage amplifier before being applied to the sample. For measurement and control purposes, a KEITHLEY 6517B electrometer and a conditioner circuit powered by a scientiFic multiple power supply (PSD3304) were connected with the said Sawyer-Tower circuit. Outputs of all the necessary components were interfaced with LabView software (version: 17) via a NI (national instruments) 9223 DAC card (Data Acquisition Card) in order to record the desired data. A LASER stand (placed perpendicular to the sample holder where a silver reflector was affixed on the movable connector to the input of sample) connected with a Polytec NLV-2500 LASER vibrometer was interfaced with the same setup in order to record the electrostrain (S) – electric field (E) butterfly loops (S-E loops) of the composite films. The direct piezoelectric coefficients (d33) of the poled samples were measured by using a piezometer unit. A SIGNAL RECOVERY 7265 DSP lock-in amplifier and a Stanford Research System SR865A DSO lock-in amplifier were utilized to record the output voltage response from the fabricated ME devices. For this purpose, the required DC magnetic field was applied by an electromagnet and the AC magnetic field was applied by custom designed Helmholtz coils. The signal to the electromagnet was supplied by a KEPKO DC master-slave amplifier which was supplied with the input DC signal from one channel of a Tektronix AFG3022C function generator. At the same time, the signal to the Helmholtz coils were supplied by a KEPKO AC amplifier (or a custom designed circuit during the measurement of frequency response) which was supplied with the input AC signal from another channel of the same function generator. The electromagnets and the Helmholtz coils were such placed that the superimposed DC and AC magnetic fields were applied in the in-plane direction (parallel to the plane surfaces) of the ME devices. The applied magnetic fields in the vicinity of the ME devices were measured using a Cryotronics F41 Teslameter. The magnetostriction of the samples was recorded via using strain gauges and strain indicator (SYSCON) with this setup. The output voltage signals from the PENGs and HNGs were recorder by using a Tektronix oscilloscope (DPO 2012). For some cases of demonstration, a bridge rectifier IC (DB 107) was utilized to convert the generated AC signals from PENGs and HNGs (generated due to the application of periodic mechanical force on the devices) into DC. An Anton Paar NHT3 instrument was used in load-controlled mode in order to determine the mechanical properties of the composite films at room temperature utilizing a diamond Berkovich nanoindenter (tip radius ~ 200 nm) with maximum of 1 mN of load, 6mN/min. of loading rate, and 20 s of holding time. The load-depth plots obtained from these experiments have been used to evaluate the values of Young’s modulus and hardness of the samples. For this purpose, the well-known Oliver–Pharr model, which utilizes the slope of the initial unloading part, has been employed here. Three different samples of the same composition and for each sample a 3 × 3 array was applied at different locations. The values of Young’s modulus and hardness were finally determined by taking the average of the results obtained from all these measurements.

Results and discussion

Characterization of the synthesized fillers

Before incorporating the synthesized fillers into P(VDF-TrFE), their structural, microstructural, and magnetic properties have been elaborately studied here. Figure 2(a) presents the XRD patterns of the synthesized fillers. As shown in our earlier works9,24 the phase pure XRD pattern of BCZT reveals the co-presence of rhombohedral (R3m), orthorhombic (Amm2), and tetragonal (P4mm) phases which is advantageous for its high piezoelectric performance. XRD patterns of CF@BCZT reveals that the intensity of XRD peaks associated with CoFe2O4 increases gradually with the increase in CFO concentration during the synthesis of the said heterostructures. This confirms that the desired synthesis of CF@BCZT heterostructures with varied CF concentration on BCZT surface has been achieved. Presence of no other impurity or additional phases in any of the synthesized fillers confirms their phase purity.

Figures 2(b-e) present the bright field TEM images of BCZT, 1CFBCZT, 2CFBCZT, and 3CFBCZT, respectively. Bright field TEM images of these samples at some other magnifications are shown in Fig. S2-S5 (Supporting Information), respectively. These TEM images suggest that tiny CoFe2O4 nanoparticles with ~ 5–10 nm sizes are well decorated on BCZT nanoparticle (average size ~ 78–85 nm) surfaces. As expected, the gradually increased concentration of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles on BCZT surface for BCZT, 1CFBCZT, 2CFBCZT, and 3CFBCZT, respectively, are also well observed from the TEM images which is associated with the gradually increased Co and Fe based solution concentration for the corresponding samples during the hydrothermal synthesis of the heterostructures. This result supports the result of XRD study as described earlier. However, detailed study (Fig. S5) revealed that for 3CFBCZT heterostructures, many CF nanoparticles, which are not decorated on BCZT, were distributed all over the TEM grid. This suggested that after this solution concentration, the interconnection between BCZT and CF becomes loose and in polymer composites also there may be many regions where the CF will interact with organic P(VDF-TrFE) dipoles only. The crystalline nature of both BCZT and the decorated CoFe2O4 nanoparticles has also been confirmed here from the HRTEM images of 2CFBCZT at BCZT surface and at CF@BCZT interface (Fig. S6, Supporting Information), respectively.

To evaluate the elemental purity of the synthesized heterostructures, EDX spectroscopic analysis has been performed. As shown in Fig. S7-S10 (Supporting Information), the EDX spectrum of BCZT, 1CFBCZT, 2CFBCZT, and 3CFBCZT confirms the presence of the desired elements for the corresponding sample. The gradual increase in the atomic percentage of Co and Fe with respect to Ba, Ca, Zr, and Ti for the BCZT, 1CFBCZT, 2CFBCZT, and 3CFBCZT samples, respectively, confirms the increase in CoFe2O4 concentration on BCZT surface for the respective samples as expected earlier. This also supports the XRD and TEM investigations. The EDX analysis of 3CFBCZT at the region where only tiny particles were distributed (Fig. S11, Supporting Information) shows almost negligible presence of the elements associated with BCZT. Mostly, CoFe2O4 related elements are present there. Thus, the EDX spectroscopic study confirms that the larger particles are BCZT and the tiny particles are CoFe2O4 indeed.

The M-H hysteresis loops of the CF@BCZT heterostructures (Fig. 2(f)) suggest that the magnetization of the samples increases with the increase in CoFe2O4 concentration on BCZT surface. This might be advantageous for the improved ME performance of the corresponding polymeric composites. The coercive field also reduces upon increasing CF concentration confirming the enhancement of their soft nature. The reduced coercivity might be associated with the enhanced interconnection between CF nanoparticles and their agglomeration25.

Microstructural and structural investigations of the composite films

To evaluate the purity and quality of the composite films and the mixing of the constituents within them, their detailed microstructural and structural properties have been investigated here. As shown in Figs. 3(a-e), the SEM images of PTR, PTR-BCZT, PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT, respectively, suggests that there is almost no void space, micro-cracks, microstructural defects, or significant filler agglomeration (which may affect their electrical performance) within any of the composite films. The morphologies of all the films are very compact. This observation confirms excellent quality and compactness of the fabricated films. All the composite films exhibit needle like crystalline lamellae5,26,27 distributed all over their surfaces along with the presence of randomly distributed fillers for the filler loaded composites. However, as evident from the SEM images, the size (length) of these needle-like crystalline lamellae has been found to be significantly reduced for the filler loaded composites compared to that of neat P(VDF-TrFE). This is due to the interfacial interaction of fillers with P(VDF-TrFE) dipoles28,29. The inorganic fillers act as the heterogeneous nucleation centres during the growth of the crystalline lamellae. Presence of high number of fillers facilitate this nucleation all over the composite films and as a result, growth of long crystalline lamellae is hindered. Thus, the reduction in the size of the said crystalline lamellae indirectly suggests the presence of strong interfacial interaction between filler surface and polymer dipoles28,29. Although, the same wt% of filler was incorporated into all the filler loaded films, for PTR-3CFBCZT film, the amount of filler seems to be lower compared to the other filler loaded films. This might be associated with the existence of high amount of undecorated CF for 3CFBCZT filler (as described in TEM study). Actually, in SEM study, the observed fillers are BCZT only. CF particles could not be observed distinctly in this magnification due to their extreme low size (~ 5–10 nm). As the 3CFBCZT filler exhibits many undecorated CF, these fillers could not be seen in the SEM image of PTR-3CFBCZT. As a result of this fact, the appearance of filler particles in the SEM image of PTR-3CFBCZT seems to be lower despite of the same amount of filler loading for all.

To study the elemental purity of the composites, the EDX spectra of the composites (Fig. S12, Supporting Information) has been recorded here. The EDX spectroscopic mapping of a region of the PTR-2CFBCZT sample (Fig. S13, Supporting Information) also shows the distribution of elements. The desired elements almost in desired proportion (if the proportion between only the inorganic components is carefully observed) were observed in the EDX spectroscopic study without the significance presence of any other impurity elements. This confirms the elemental purity of the composite films. However, Ca and Zr could not be observed in the EDX spectrum as well as in the mapping. This is due to the very low presence of these elements in BCZT itself which after being incorporated into P(VDF-TrFE) become almost negligible.

The XRD patterns of the composite films (Fig. 3(f)) exhibit all the desired diffraction peaks associated with the constituents of the corresponding sample. No other additional third phase was also observed for any of the composite films. All the constituents retain their crystallinity even after mixing. These confirm the structural purity of the composites and the desired mixing of the constituents for all of them. The occurrence of the diffraction peaks at 2θ ~ 20°, 35.3°, and 40.9° (characteristic diffraction peaks of P(VDF-TrFE) associated with (1 1 0) and (2 0 0), (0 0 1), and (1 1 1) and (2 0 1) diffraction planes) confirms the predominance of polar β chain conformation for all the composites including the neat P(VDF-TrFE)28,29. Phase analysis

Electrical, piezoelectric, and magnetoelectric properties of PVDF based composites are greatly influenced by their polar phase. Therefore, it is essential to explore the phase analysis of the fabricated P(VDF-TrFE)/CF@BCZT composites. P(VDF-TrFE) itself is reported to exhibit very high amount of polar β phase due to its all-trans chain conformation even in pristine condition28,29. This mainly depends on the ratio of VDF and TrFE contents within P(VDF-TrFE). For 50–80 mol% of VDF content in P(VDF-TrFE), it is known to exhibit sufficiently stable all-trans chain conformation8,30. In the present case, the molar ratio of VDF and TrFE within the used P(VDF-TrFE) is 70:30. Therefore, the pristine P(VDF-TrFE) in the present study is also expected to exhibit very high amount of polar phase. However, the interfacial interaction of P(VDF-TrFE) dipoles with the filler surfaces may tune the said amount of polar phase. To investigate all of these facts, FTIR study has been employed here. Figure 4(a) presents the ATR-FTIR absorption spectra of all the composite films. The predominant presence of 842, 1285, and 1400 cm−1 band confirm the predominance of polar β phase within all of the composite films31–33. Dielectric properties

Along with polarity, the dielectric properties of PVDF based composite films also render a strong effect on their piezoelectric and triboelectric energy harvesting performance. Therefore, detailed dielectric investigations have been carried out here. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the frequency response of dielectric permittivity for all the composite films follows the well-known Maxwell-Wagner-Sillars (MWS) polarization mechanism (decrease in permittivity with increase in frequency due to the gradual disappearance of space charge polarization at higher frequency)9. In a similar fashion, the frequency response of dielectric loss tangent (tan δ) (Fig. 5(b)) follows its typical behaviour for PVDF based composites28. At ~ 10 kHz of frequency, tan δ exhibits its lowest value for all the composites and below and above this frequency, it increases. The increase in tan δ at lower frequency is attributed to the interfacial polarization and its increase at higher frequency might be attributed to the presence of glass transition relaxation of PVDF at ~ 10 MHz34,35. To determine the materials effect of dielectric properties, the values of dielectric permittivity and tan δ at 1 kHz frequency for all the composite films have been plotted in Fig. 5(c). From this figure, it is observed that the dielectric permittivity of PTR first increases abruptly for PTR-BCZT and then gradually decreases for PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT, respectively. It is reported earlier that the high value of polar β phase in PVDF based composites is advantageous for improving their dielectric permittivity36,37. But, in the present case, the values of dielectric permittivity for different films do not follow the same trend of variation as their \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F\left(\beta\:\right)$$\end{document}. Rather the obtained results of dielectric permittivity can be well explained based on the law of mixing only. It is well known that the dielectric permittivity of BCZT is much higher than both P(VDF-TrFE) and CoFe2O438,39. On the other hand, though CoFe2O4 exhibits higher value of dielectric permittivity than P(VDF-TrFE), its permittivity is much lower than BCZT28,38,39. Thus, the abrupt increase in dielectric permittivity for PTR-BCZT is due to the higher amount of BCZT content for this film compared to others. For PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT films, the BCZT content gradually decreases resulting in the decrease in permittivity. Thus, though the PTR-2CFBCZT film exhibits the highest value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F\left(\beta\:\right)$$\end{document}, its dielectric permittivity is not the highest here due to the higher effect of individual permittivity of BCZT compared to the effect of polymer dipole orientation related polarization. On the other hand, tan δ gradually increases for PTR, PTR-BCZT, PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT films, respectively. This is due to the higher conducting nature of inorganic magnetic ferrites (CoFe2O4 here) compared to the piezoelectric titanates (BCZT here)40. The initial increase in tan δ for PTR-BCZT is associated with the formation of conducting pathway caused by filler addition. For the CF@BCZT loaded films also this effect is there (may be considered same due to same wt% of filler loading for all). In addition to this, increased CoFe2O4 concentration (more conducting in nature than BCZT) and decreased BCZT concentration result in the gradual increase in tan δ for PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT, respectively. The calculation of AC conductivity for all the films (Fig. 5(d)) also supports this result.

Ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties

Along with good piezoelectric characteristics, P(VDF-TrFE) is also known to exhibit excellent ferroelectric properties41. Therefore, these properties have been investigated here in order to study their effect on the final multimode energy harvesting performances. The P-E hysteresis loops and S-E butterfly loops of the composite films at their respective breakdown strengths are shown in Fig. 6(a-e). The results confirm good ferroelectric and piezoelectric nature of the composites. However, all the films could not sustain same applied electric field strength. The breakdown strengths of PTR, PTR-BCZT, PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT samples have been found to be ~ 835, 742, 666, 654, and 622 kV/cm, respectively. Possibly due to this fact, all the composites could not attain properly saturated P-E and S-E loops. The maximum polarization (Pmax) and the calculated converse piezoelectric coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{33}^{*}$$\end{document}) for all the composites are summarized in Fig. 6(f). PTR-BCZT sample contains the maximum Pmax and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{33}^{*}$$\end{document} which might be due to the effect of high individual polarization and piezoelectricity of BCZT. But, according to FTIR result, the PTR-2CFBCZT was also expected to exhibit good polarization and piezoelectricity which is not inferred from the ferroelectric studies. This might be due to the non-existence of proper saturation of P-E and S-E loops for the composites with higher amount of CoFe2O4 caused by higher conducting nature of this component as explained earlier40.

gnetoelectric energy harvesting

After exploring the polar phase and electrical properties of the composite films, their magnetoelectric energy harvesting performances (which is one of the key focuses of the present work) have been explored here. As only three composites, namely PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT, contain magnetic components, the magnetic and ME characterization have been performed here only for these three samples. The M-H hysteresis loops of the composite films (Fig. 7(a)) follow the similar trend as that for the respective nanoparticles. The ME performance of any particulate composite depends on the magnetostriction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document}) of the sample and its piezoelectricity. Higher magnetostriction signifies the ability of the sample to generate higher mechanical vibration (caused by applied magnetic field) required to stimulate the piezoelectric dipoles. On the other hand, piezoelectric coefficient determines the availability of these piezoelectric dipoles. Thus, for good ME performance, higher magnetostriction and higher piezoelectricity are simultaneously necessary. The piezoelectricity of the PTR/CF@BCZT films, which is the result of the predominant effect of interfacial interaction between fillers and polymer dipoles at inorganic-organic interface and slight effect of individual piezoelectricity of BCZT, has already been studied earlier. Figure 7(b) presents the measured magnetostriction of the composites. It increases gradually for PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT films which is attributed to the availability of gradually increasing amount of magnetic CoFe2O4 component for the respective films. Enhanced magnetostriction suggests that the corresponding devices are suitable for strain sensing applications. The strain sensitivity of a ME device is measured by differentiation of the magnetostriction curve with respect to the applied magnetic field (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d\lambda\:/dH$$\end{document})42,43. As shown in Fig. S16 (Supporting Information), the strain sensitivity of the PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT devices increases gradually with PTR-3CFBCZT having the maximum value of ~ 6.75 × 10−9 Oe1 at ~ 1450 Oe of applied DC magnetic field. This suggests that the developed ME devices are very suitable for low-field magnetic strain sensing applications.

To measure the ME performance of the developed devices, the setup as shown in the schematic representation in Fig. 7(c) has been utilized here (also see experimental section). Interestingly, the observation of significant amount of self-biased ME effect (non-zero \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{ME}$$\end{document} at zero DC bias field) for all the studied devices suggested their possible ability for excellent technological applications. Not only the energy harvesting from and sensing of DC magnetic field, the developed ME devices were also able for the sensing of AC magnetic fields too up to a certain limit. The AC magnetic field energy harvesting and sensing performance of PTR-2CFBCZT is shown in Fig. S19 (Supporting Information). Thus, the ME characterizations suggest that the developed devices are very suitable for different types of magnetic field energy harvesting and sensing applications5,46.

The obtained high ME performance of the developed device is due to the proposed mechanism of the ability of magnetostriction to simultaneously excite BCZT and P(VDF-TrFE) piezoelectric dipoles effectively as shown schematically in Fig. 7(f). The interfacial interactions of the filler surfaces with polymer dipoles at the inorganic-organic interfaces help in improving the amount of polar polymer dipoles within the composite films. On the other hand, the magnetostriction of CoFe2O4, which are nicely interconnected on BCZT particle surfaces via heterostructure formation, can effectively excite the piezoelectric BCZT dipoles at the inorganic-inorganic (BCZT-CoFe2O4) interfaces. The same magnetostriction also excites the polymer dipoles at organic-inorganic (polymer-filler) interface. As a combined effect of these two facts, higher ME performance is observed here. For comparison purpose, a P(VDF-TrFE) based composite film with incorporation of same amount of (20 wt%) CoFe2O4 only was also prepared, and the corresponding ME device was fabricated via the same technique. The obtained maximum value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{ME}$$\end{document} was ~ 45 mV·cm−1·Oe−1 in this case which was significantly low compared to that of PTR-2CFBCZT device. This justifies our result in terms of excellent interaction both at the inorganic-inorganic and inorganic-organic interfaces. For only CoFe2O4 filler, its magnetostriction excites only the P(VDF-TrFE) polar dipoles at the inorganic-organic (filler polymer) interfaces for the desired charge generation. On the other hand, as mentioned earlier, for CF@BCZT fillers, the magnetostriction of CoFe2O4 can excite both BCZT diploes (at CF-BCZT inorganic-inorganic interface due to very strong interconnection between these two via heterostructure formation) and P(VDF-TrFE) dipoles (at CF@BCZT-P(VDF-TrFE) inorganic-organic interface due strong interfacial interaction between them). It is well-known that the morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) based inorganic materials (like BCZT) pose very high piezoelectricity than piezoelectric polymers (like P(VDF-TrFE))47,48. Thus, the combined effect of the above mentioned two interactions at inorganic-inorganic and inorganic-organic interfaces results in the observed high ME performance. Furthermore, here it is also to be mentioned that the said interfacial interaction at inorganic-organic interfaces for heterostructured fillers (CF@BCZT here) is more effective in nucleating the polar phases of PVDF based polymers (also observed here from FTIR study) which is also an effective cause for the high ME performance of the fabricated device. This interfacial interaction is also advantageous for other energy harvesting performances, especially, the piezoelectric energy harvesting performance, of these composites as discussed in the following sections.

In the present case, the same wt% of filler loading for all the composite films suggests almost same elastic moduli for all. Therefore, if similar input applied force is provided to the PENGs corresponding to all the films, Eq. 3 suggests that the output voltage is directly proportional to the piezoelectric coefficient and inversely proportional to the relative dielectric permittivity8,52. The measurement of the output peak-to-peak open circuit voltage (VOC) from all the PENGs (upon the application of similar periodic mechanical force (~ 8 kPa at 5 Hz) on them) as presented in Fig. 8(a) also supports the hypothesis as concluded from Eq. 3. The PTR-2CFBCZT based PENG with the highest value of piezoelectric coefficient and low dielectric permittivity of the active layer delivers the highest VOC of ~ 60 V of average value. Furthermore, comparatively higher lossy nature of this sample (see dielectric characterization) also helps the corresponding PENG to deliver higher VOC through the effect of space charge polarization. Here, it is to be mentioned that though the PTR-3CFBCZT film exhibits the lowest dielectric permittivity and highest tan δ, its very low piezoelectric coefficient enables the corresponding PENG to deliver lower VOC compared to that of the PTR-2CFBCZT.

Not only high output performance, the investigation of VOCs of PTR and PTR-2CFBCZT based PENGs (upon similar applied periodic stress) at different seasons after their fabrication (during the span of almost one year) suggests hardly any changes in their output performances (Fig. S20, Supporting Information). This confirms sufficient durability and stability of the performance of developed devices in different conditions (humidity, temperature, etc.) within the limit of their ambient energy harvesting applications. As the PTR-2CFBCZT based PENG has shown the highest output energy harvesting performance, this device has been further used for various real life mechanical energy harvesting and sensing applications. As shown in Fig. 8(b), this device shows its ability to deliver different output VOC upon the varied amplitude of input mechanical stress at fixed frequency of ~ 5 Hz. This proves good dynamic pressure sensitivity (~ 7.5 V/kPa) of the device. However, after ~ 8 kPa of applied stress, the output VOC almost saturates. This could be due to the saturation of dipole orientation. Actually, these kinds of unpoled PENGs works on the basis of the stress induced poling affect which is basically related to the alignment of piezoelectric dipoles caused by the applied stress53,54. The saturation of this dipole alignment after a certain limiting applied pressure thus can saturate the output VOC. Beside good dynamic pressure sensitivity, the device shows excellent static pressure sensitivity too. For the determination of static pressure sensitivity of the device, an indirect method via its capacitance measurement has been employed here. As mentioned earlier, when pressure is applied on the device, the stress-induced poling effect comes into the play. As a result of the generated piezoelectric charges due to this dipole alignment adjacent to the electrodes, opposite charges are also induced on these electrodes which could be studied via the capacitance measurements. As shown in Fig. 8(c), the capacitance of the device has been found to be increased with the increase in the amount of the applied static pressure and for sufficiently higher value of the applied static pressure (~ 1.5 kPa), it saturates. The saturation could again be attributed to the saturation of dipole alignment. Along with the mentioned static and dynamic pressure sensitivity, the developed devices were able for good strain sensing application too. To test this performance, the PTR-2CFBCZT based PENG was provided with different amounts of periodic compressive strain along its length so that the device bends in its out-of-plane direction and the corresponding average output VOC was determined29. As shown in Fig. 8(e), the average output VOC from the device increases with the increase in the amount of bending strain. Thus, the device can be considered very suitable for bending strain sensing too. The typical output VOC signal from the device upon the application of a fixed periodic bending strain (~ 13.4%) is shown in Fig. 8(f).

Apart from these versatile sensing applications, the device can also be used for powering up external low-power-consuming electronic devices. To demonstrate this fact, varied external load resistance (RL) was connected across the device and the output voltage (VL) was recorded across it upon similar amount of input mechanical stimuli (~ 8 kPa, 5 Hz). The VL increases with the increase in RL (Fig. 8(g)) and for sufficiently high value of RL it becomes equal to VOC. The maximum output power density from this measurement has been calculated to be ~ 75 µW/cm2 at 6 MΩ of load resistance (Fig. 8(h)) which is in accordance with (even on the higher side) previously reported similar kinds of PENGs8,29,58–63. The load current (IL: current in the load resistance) as a function of RL is shown in Fig. S21 (Supporting Information). As can be seen from Fig. 9(a), unlike the VOCs of PENGs (where PTR-2CFBCZT based PENG has shown the highest performance mainly due to the high piezoelectricity of this film), PTR-BCZT based HNG delivers the highest value of VOC due to the highest value of relative dielectric permittivity of this film. However, the gradual decrease in VOC for PTR-BCZT, PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT based HNGs are not as abrupt as their dielectric permittivity. Thus, though the output energy harvesting performance of the HNGs is greatly affected by the dielectric permittivity of the corresponding films, some other effects also seem to be present there. This effect is mainly the piezoelectricity and enhanced space charge polarization. As the developed devices are not only triboelectric device, but piezo-tribo hybrid devices too, the output of the HNGs is also dependent on the piezoelectricity of the used active layers28,54,75. Here, the PTR-2CFBCZT film exhibits the highest piezoelectricity. On the other hand, the space charge polarization of PTR, PTR-BCZT, PTR-1CFBCZT, PTR-2CFBCZT, and PTR-3CFBCZT increases gradually which can improve the charge transportation (similar to PENGs). Thus, the combined effect of all these facts do not allow to VOC of the HNGs to decrease as abruptly as the decrease in dielectric permittivity for the corresponding films. As a result of this fact, while PTR-BCZT based HNG delivers the highest value of VOC of ~ 255 V, the PTR-1CFBCZT and PTR-2CFBCZT based HNG also deliver almost similar amount of VOC of ~ 250 V. Therefore, to make analogy with other studies, PTR-2CFBCZT based HNG has been used here for further demonstration of hybrid energy harvesting and sensing activities of the developed HNGs. To check the ability of the HNGs to drive external devices, similar external load resistance dependent studies and capacitor charging experiments (as that for PENG) have been performed for the PTR-2CFBCZT based HNG. The external load resistance dependent output voltage measurements (Fig. 9(d)) suggest the output power density as high as ~ 255 µW/cm2 at 20 MΩ of load resistance (Fig. 9(e)) which is in the comparable range (even bit higher) with that of earlier literature24,28,54,76. The corresponding RL dependent variation of IL is shown in Fig. S23 (Supporting Information). The capacitor charging experiment suggests that the device is able to charge the commercial 1 and 2.2 µF capacitors up to ~ 4.2 and 3 V, respectively (Fig. 9(f)), resulting in 4.2 and 6.6 µC of stored charges in them (Fig. S24, Supporting Information) upon only 1 min of periodic mechanical stimulation (~ 8 kPa, 5 Hz) on it. This is very high compared to the corresponding PENG.

Owing to this highly efficient performance, the device has been further used for various ambient energy harvesting and sensing applications. As shown in Fig. S25 (Supporting Information), the device is able to harvest energy from different biomechanical input stimuli such as finger tapping and foot tapping. To further explore this activity, the device was affixed on the thigh of a male human participant and warm-up exercise was performed which led to significant output VOC from the device. Even, when the device was affixed on human throat, it was able to harvest energy (and sense) slight throat movement during normal random talking and chanting musical notes. This phonation monitoring activity suggests the applicability of the developed device in biomedical field77. As shown in Fig. 9(g), when the device was affixed on the back side of a backpack and a male human participant just walk with the backpack (with some object inside), it was able to deliver significant amount of VOC. All these experiments confirm the excellent energy harvesting and sensing ability of the developed HNGs during random biomechanical activity which might be very advantageous in various self-powered wearable energy harvesting, sensing, and healthcare monitoring purposes77,78.

As shown in Fig. 9(h), when a rubber ball (~ 0.26 g) was dropped on the PTR-2CFBCZT based HNG from a certain height (~ 10 cm), it was able to deliver significant amount of VOC. It was also able to track the dropping and re-bouncing activity of the rubber ball. Even, the device was able to sense very light weight objects like small piece of paper (~ 0.15 g) and water droplets falling on it from certain height (~ 3 cm) (Fig. S26, Supporting Information). These experiments confirm that the device is very suitable to harvest electricity from very small input mechanical stimuli and to sense ultra-small mechanical deformation which might be very helpful for its applications in developing self-powered security monitoring sensors. It was even able to sense gentle finger touching on it (Fig. 9(i) and Fig. S27 (Supporting Information)) with perfectly tracking the intensity and periodicity of the finger-touching action. This confirms the excellent touch sensing activity of the developed HNGs. As a whole, due to highly efficient mechanical to electrical energy conversion performance of the HNGs, they can be considered very suitable for energy harvesting from ambient and non-conventional irregular mechanical stimulations and self-powered vibration sensing73–78.

Conclusion

Ultrafine magnetic CoFe2O4 (CF) nanoparticles decorated piezoelectric BCZT heterostructures have been synthesized here with tuneable CF concentration on BCZT surface. These heterostructured materials have been then used as fillers to P(VDF-TrFE) to develop the corresponding flexible composite films. The interfacial interaction of the dipoles of P(VDF-TrFE) and the surfaces of heterostructures at organic-inorganic interface has helped to improve the polarity (up to ~ 94% of β phase) and hence the piezoelectricity of the composite films. On the other hand, upon applying external magnetic field, the magnetostriction of CF was able to stimulate both the organic piezoelectric polymer dipoles and inorganic piezoelectric BCZT dipoles. Due to the heterostructure formation, the transfer of the said magnetostriction to BCZT dipoles (strain transfer) was very efficient at the inorganic-inorganic interface in this case. As the piezoelectricity of inorganic materials (here BCZT) is much higher than polymers (here P(VDF-TrFE)), the said interaction has led to higher magnetoelectric performance of the corresponding ME devices. Furthermore, due to high tribo-negativity of PVDF based composites, the developed films have also shown excellent piezo-tribo hybrid energy harvesting performance. As a whole, the dipolar interaction at the inorganic-inorganic interface (CF-BCZT) and improved dipolar interaction at organic-inorganic interface (P(VDF-TrFE)-CF@BCZT) have helped the composite films to become a versatile material for efficient multi-mode (magnetoelectric, piezoelectric, and piezo-tribo hybrid) applications. For the optimized composite film, the corresponding ME device has delivered a very high ME coupling coefficient of 96 mV·cm−1·Oe−1 which suppresses the result of only CF loaded PVDF based composites. This high performance of the ME device has made it suitable for strain sensing, low-magnetic field (AC and DC) sensing, and magnetic field energy harvesting. On the other hand, the optimized PENG has delivered ~ 75 µW/cm2 of power density with ~ 46% of efficiency and was able for efficient static and dynamic pressure sensing, bending strain sensing, and associated piezoelectric energy harvesting. Beside this, the optimized HNG, with ~ 255 µW/cm2 of output power density, has shown its excellent performance for various regular and irregular biomechanical and ambient mechanical energy harvesting, associated self-powered sensing, and powering up small electronics. Thus, the developed composites can be regarded as an excellent multifunctional material for highly efficient multi-mode (magnetoelectric, piezoelectric, and piezo-tribo hybrid) energy harvesting and sensing applications.
