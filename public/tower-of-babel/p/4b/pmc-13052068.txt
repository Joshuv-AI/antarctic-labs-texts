Strengthening Pharmacogenomics Implementation in Africa: Challenges and Opportunities ‐ A Narrative Review
Authors: Esther Mukabyiringiro, Andrew Wainaina Kinyanjui, Gachie Ruth Nthenya, Okoth Zifion Ndiege, Daniel Alaak Majok, Samuel Inshutiyimana, Ermias Mergia Terefe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimThe discipline of pharmacogenomics (PGx) studies the influence of genetic diversity on drug response, bringing the potential to personalized treatment and reduction of adverse effects. Although PGx integration into healthcare is progressing at a global scale, its implementation in Africa faces unique challenges and opportunities. The purpose of this review was to evaluate the status of PGx in Africa, outlining the challenges to its integration into healthcare systems and highlighting available opportunities for improving personalized medicine across the continent.MethodsA narrative review was conducted by retrieving relevant literature from PubMed, Google Scholar, and websites for African health agencies. The search identified 91 records, of which duplicates were removed. Thereafter, 58 papers underwent full‐text evaluation resulting in 39 relevant articles and policy reports. Articles were considered based on their relevance to the objectives of this review and publication year range (2020–2025). Studies without full text, focusing on continents other than Africa, or published outside the defined period were excluded.ResultsSignificant challenges hinder PGx integration in Africa. Technical barriers include inadequate laboratory infrastructure, insufficient sequencing capacity, and underrepresentation in genomic studies globally. Policy challenges include the absence of context‐specific regulatory frameworks, and data security measures, while socioeconomic barriers entail limited research funding and low public awareness. Besides, emerging digital health infrastructure, especially mobile health tools, brings innovative pathways for PGx adoption. Additionally, local disease priorities such as malaria, HIV/AIDS and tuberculosis provide clear targets for impactful PGx research. Regional and diaspora partnerships are building capacity, biobanks, and knowledge‐sharing platforms.ConclusionAfrica's path to PGx integration requires coordinated strategies that combine policy adoption, education, research equity, and sustainable investment. With continental collaboration, PGx promises to improve health systems and enhance treatment outcomes in Africa.Clinical Trial NumberNot Applicable.

Introduction

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) investigates how an individual's genes respond to medications of various types. This field of study has been transforming medicine worldwide by enabling personalized treatment of various diseases [1]. It allows medical doctors and pharmacists to tailor drug therapy based on genetic differences that affect outcome of medications, how the body processes them, and their potential side effects [2, 3]. Treatment is more efficient, less likely to cause harm, and more resource‐efficient. PGx‐guided prescribing is increasingly being accepted in developed countries as a suitable therapy for conditions such as cancer, psychiatric illnesses, and cardiovascular disease [4].

Despite this progress, Africa cannot afford to lag in PGx implementation. The continent faces a disproportionate burden of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), treatment failures, and escalating costs associated with HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria therapies. For example, ADRs contribute significantly to hospital admissions and poor adherence to chronic treatments, worsening the existing heavy disease burden. In contrast, high‐income countries have advanced integration of PGx through initiatives such as the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guidelines and multiple United States FDA‐approved PGx‐based drug labels [5]. This contrast underscores the widening global equity gap in access to precision medicine.

The potential of PGx is even greater in Africa due to the genetic specificity of the population and the incidence of infectious and chronic diseases. African countries face high health and economic consequences from diseases such as HIV/AIDS, malaria, tuberculosis, and cancer [4]. Additionally, lack of representativeness of Africa's genetic differences in the global PGx information increase the risk of enormous variations between the safety and efficacy of medications [6]. For instance, genes that determine the metabolism of antiretroviral or antimalarial drugs in the body might have a significant impact on treatment outcomes in patients from Africa. Thus, by applying PGx techniques, drug safety would be enhanced, treatment failure rates decreased, and guidelines based on population parameters made possible [7].

Although its potential is gigantic, African PGx development remains limited. The obstacles are multiple and range from inadequate infrastructure to weak policy backing for PGx, minimal incorporation into medical practice, and a general deficiency of local research capacity [8]. Opportunities, however, exist including regional genomics initiatives, increased funding into biobanks, and enhanced visibility of precision medicine among researchers and policymakers. Several recent reviews have comprehensively addressed pharmacogenomics in Africa, focusing on its applicability in diseases such as HIV, TB, and malaria, and outlining broad barriers and roadmaps for implementation. The purpose of the current review is to provide a focused lens on sociocultural barriers, donor dependency, and regulatory readiness, integrating evidence from African‐specific contexts and policy frameworks. This approach highlights underexplored dimensions of PGx implementation that are critical for practical translation. We also propose a practical step‐by‐step model for successful and context‐specific implementation, based on African and global best practices.

Materials and Methods

A narrative review was conducted by retrieving relevant literature from PubMed, Google Scholar, and databases of African health agencies. Key search terms included pharmacogenomics, personalized medicine, Africa, genetic diversity, and healthcare integration. The search identified 91 records. After removal of duplicates and initial relevance screening of titles and abstracts, 58 papers underwent full‐text evaluation. Thereafter, 38 publications met the inclusion requirements and were added to the final synthesis. English‐language research, reviews, reports, and policy documents that addresses pharmacogenomics, genomic medicine, genetic diversity, or implementation challenges and opportunities in Africa between 2020 and 2025 were considered eligible sources. Studies carried out solely outside of Africa that had no bearing on African people, conference abstracts that lacked full text, and opinion pieces that had no bearing on policy or empirical findings were excluded.

The African Pharmacogenomics Landscape

The African populace has the highest number of genetic variations among current‐day global populations. This genetic diversity has been made possible by several factors including migrations, admixture, gene flow, dietary differences, response to human disease and environmental factors. The vast African tropical climate has been tied to increasing incidences of contagious diseases such as HIV/AIDS and Tuberculosis [9]. For this reason, increased use of medicines to treat, prevent, and/or cure these maladies is warranted which raises concern to whether the drugs are equally safe and efficacious. At this point, clinical pharmacogenomics serves a critical role in facilitating effective treatment. Despite the persuasive evidence for the potential gains of pharmacogenomics implementation in Africa, a number of setbacks currently exist, such as the underrepresentation of African populations in pharmacogenetics, technological challenges, and the intricacy of health systems [1]. There is still a need to identify more PGx markers through large‐scale PGx research and testing. The Genome‐Wide Association Studies through a meta‐analysis on African cohorts revealed novel CYP2C9 and VKORCI as some of the clinically significant gene variants which were widely associated with warfarin drug response and through this proper adjustment were made for subsequent doses. This is strong evidence on the importance of PGx implementation for African genetics.

Several initiatives have been launched to address the barriers, yet their success has been uneven. While programs such as Human Hereditary and Health Africa (H3Africa) have made notable contributions in capacity building and data generation, others struggle with sustainability, limited funding, or inadequate translation of research findings into clinical practice. This mixed progress highlights the need for critical evaluation of existing projects, identifying which models are scalable and which remain underperforming due to structural, financial, or political limitations [10]. In addition, Africa's investment in genomics still lags behind other regions of the Global South. For example, Latin America and parts of Asia have developed regional pharmacogenomics networks and national precision medicine programs with more consistent government backing [11]. In contrast, African initiatives remain fragmented and heavily donor‐dependent, limiting long‐term sustainability.

Current Pan‐African Research Initiatives

Human Heredity and Health Africa (H3 Africa)

H3Africa is an initiative that emboldens African researchers to be cutthroat in genomic sciences. It does so by establishing and nurturing effective collaborations among African researchers and generating unparalleled data that could be used to improve both African and global health [12]. The H3 Africa consortium expedites fundamental research into diseases in Africa simultaneously with developing infrastructure, resources, training and ethical guidelines to support a sustainable African research enterprise [12]. The initiative comprises 51 African projects that include population‐based genomic studies of common non‐communicable disorders including heart and renal diseases as well as communicable diseases such as tuberculosis [9]. H3Africa has made notable contributions in capacity building, infrastructure development, and the establishment of biobanks. However, its long‐term sustainability remains uncertain due to heavy reliance on international donor funding and slow integration of research findings into clinical practice.

The African Genomic Medicine Training (AGMT) Initiative

This initiative was formulated during an amalgamated conference of the African Society of Human Genetic (AFSHG) and the H3 Africa Consortium in 2016 in Senegal. This was a reaction for the needs of developing knowledge and skills in genomic medicine [13]. The AGMT was found to put into effect a sustainable genomic medicine training initiative preeminently for healthcare professionals who are not geneticists [14]. This initiative also capitalizes on pre‐existing research consortia and networks to train and create a pool of trainers and has institutionalized evidence‐based approaches to guide curriculum and contact development/adaptation [13]. While AGMT has strengthened capacity‐building, its reach remains limited, with few national‐level training programs incorporated into formal medical curricula.

The Southern African Human Genomic Program (SAHGP)

The SAHGP is a ground‐breaking federal and regional initiative that aims to unfold the distinctive genetic character of southern African communities [15]. The program was officially launched on the 27th and 28th of January 2011 with the objective of upgrading the standards of life through thorough comprehension of human genetic variations. This program collectively brings on board scientists, medical practitioners, industry representatives, ethicists, lawyers and government representatives and is managed and led by southern Africa [15]. Although it has produced valuable genomic data and engaged multiple stakeholders, challenges such as limited funding, political buy‐in, and translation into national health strategies have constrained its broader impact.

Nigerian 100 K Genome Project

To capitalize on the genetic diversity in Nigeria, the Non‐Communicable Diseases Genetic Heritage Study (NCD‐GHS) Consortium was found to aid in producing a comprehensive catalog of human genetic variation and appraise the burden and root‐cause‐related characteristics of non‐communicable diseases among 100,000 Nigerian adults spanning 300 ethnic groups [16]. The project has the potential to be the largest genomic dataset in Africa, offering insights into disease burden and non‐communicable disease risk factors. Yet, implementation has faced difficulties, including high costs of sequencing, infrastructural limitations, and ensuring robust ethical frameworks for data sharing.

Existing Infrastructure and Pharmacogenomics Integration in African Health Systems

There are existing biobanks in Africa such as the H3 Africa Biobank which was developed by the H3 Africa with a view of building the capacity and resources to advance genomic research on the African continent [12]. It is a continental effort involving over 500 researchers across 30 African countries which has resulted in the publication of over 700 research articles and hundreds of trained postgraduates [12]. Another important initiative is the African Collaborative Initiative to Advance Diagnostics (AFCAD) which was formed with one of its sole purposes to speed up registration of current diagnostic technologies in Africa. This aims to address the problem of sub‐optimal access to diagnostics due to limited human, financial and technical resources [17]. There are no comprehensive national‐level pharmacogenomics policies in Africa. However, the African Pharmacogenomics Consortium (APC) was formally launched in 2018 to address the issue (See Table 1).

Deep analysis has shown that PGx implementation in Africa is hastily shifting from fragmented research projects to a centralized and structured regional agenda. This has been made possible courtesy of the emergence of the African Medicines Agency (AMA) ratified in 2021 and the reinforcement of Regional Economic Communities (RECs) which present a fresh platform for harmonized regulation [19]. The AMA, also being the chief regulator in Africa, should lay out African guidelines for PGx labeling as most of the drugs in Africa carry labels based on European or North American data. It should also establish a special committee within it which would be tasked to evaluate the PGx biomarkers that are specific to the African populace [19]. However, from a research and policy perspective, it is equally important to adopt a broader view by scrutinizing all available CPIC guidelines with important regional adaptations to establish a strong foundation for future clinical translation [20]. A critical next step in making this happen involves integrating existing pharmacogenomic research findings into national healthcare frameworks while developing Africa‐specific guidelines. These efforts will help create pharmacogenomics frameworks that reflect the genetic diversity and clinical realities of African populations [21].

Grand Challenges to Implementation of Pharmacogenomics

The implementation of PGx in Africa faces significant challenges including technical, policy‐related, sociocultural, abd economic barriers. Additionally, many healthcare professionals lack the necessary knowledge and resources to interpret and effectively apply pharmacogenomics data in clinical practice [20].

Technical Barriers

Clinical laboratory diagnostics are traditionally priced based on the cost of performing the test, whereas drug pricing is determined by perceived therapeutic benefit and market demand [20]. This discrepancy complicates the integration of PGx into healthcare systems. The existing business model for molecular testing requires profound changes to account for the full value of targeted medicines. Many African countries lack the necessary facilities, such as advanced laboratories and sequencing technologies, to conduct large‐scale genomic research. The shallow pool of sequenced genomic data in Africa further hinders the implementation of PGx in Africa. These hamper the development of pharmacogenomics and limit its application in addressing the continent's unique health needs [22].

Brain drain is another challenge affecting PGx in Africa. Many skilled African scientists and researchers migrate to countries with better research opportunities, leaving a gap in local expertise [22]. This exodus of talent undermines efforts to build research capacity and develop pharmacogenomics within the continent. Limited integration of PGx training among healthcare providers hinders proper public education while reluctance of healthcare workers to report adverse drug reactions limits PGx database growth. This further contributes to slow implementation of PGx in Africa. Additionally, existing healthcare workers are unsure of the importance of PGx in disease management [8]. Lethargy in keeping up with the latest guidelines on genomics greatly reduces its utilization in patient management. Early adoption of genomic curricula into health training will promote the efficient use of precision medicine in Africa and hasten its uptake [23].

Policy Framework

Regulatory and policy challenges also impede adoption of PGx. The absence of clear regulatory frameworks, policies for genetic testing and data privacy creates uncertainty [23]. International frameworks such as those developed by H3Africa provide comprehensive recommendations. However, they are often generic and not readily adaptable to the unique socio‐cultural and regulatory contexts of individual African countries [24]. Current PGx guidelines are derived from non‐African populations, raising concerns about their generalizability. African populations carry a high frequency of clinically significant alleles, yet these remain underrepresented in global datasets [1]. As of 2021, African genomic data accounted for only 1.1% of genome‐wide association studies, highlighting the need for greater inclusivity in global genomic research [22]. This underrepresentation limits the relevance of precision medicine in Africa. Few African countries have developed national PGx strategies and clinical implementation is almost exclusively confined to research settings. However, absence of enforceable, context‐specific guidelines limits the ability of African nations to regulate and promote PGx effectively. The launch of the African Pharmacogenomics Consortium (APC) in 2018 [14] signaled an important step toward harmonizing efforts, but its impact has been constrained by limited funding, donor dependency, and weak governmental adoption.

Privacy concerns and the commercialization of health data further complicate efforts to create successful collaborations. Issues such as unclear data rights, inadequate material transfer agreements, and limited protection against the misuse of genetic information frequently arise. Historical concerns about exploitation and the export of samples without local benefit further exacerbate mistrust and hinder the development of PGx [24].

Sociocultural Barriers

Challenges around informed consent highlight deeper sociocultural barriers. Misunderstandings of genetic concepts, fears of sample misuse, and reliance on community or family decision‐making complicate individual participation in PGx initiatives [25]. Limited appropriate terminologies in local languages to refer to genomes further impede community understanding. This underscores the struggle communities face in grasping core concepts related to genomics. Myths that samples collected may be used for witchcraft, reinforce psychological barriers to communal embrace of PGx [8]. Furthermore, members of some African communities associate sample collection with diagnosis, which undermines collection to improve biobanking [25]. Skepticism and mistrust of healthcare providers prevent the free sharing of community hereditary information [8].

Economic Barriers

Economic challenges complicate clinical pharmacogenomics implementation. African governments and institutions often allocate insufficient resources to scientific research [22]. To further research, local scientists are left to rely on donor funding [4]. This greatly limits research scope as donor funding comes with limitations of use and objectives. Donor dependency in African health systems has historically deprioritized preventative and precision health initiatives [2]. Pharmacogenomics, which largely falls under preventative care, risks being sidelined in favor of donor‐driven agendas focused on acute infectious disease control. Although pharmacogenomics has shown immense potential to improve drug safety and efficacy, evidence on cost‐effectiveness in African health systems remains sparse. In absence of robust health technology assessments, PGx risks being perceived as resource‐intensive and unsustainable [14]. Ethics review processes are also often under‐resourced, and the role of government in oversight is poorly defined. High out‐of‐pocket health expenditures further exacerbate economic hardship for patients, making adoption of PGx even more challenging (See Figures 1, 2, and Table 2).

Opportunities for Genomic Leapfrogging

Genomic Diversity as a Scientific Asset

Africa has the highest level of genetic diversity globally, characterized by higher frequencies of population‐specific alleles and smaller linkage disequilibrium blocks compared to non‐African populations, thus making it a rich source of data for PGx. However, this genetic diversity is not fully utilized. For instance, a study done on 400 African individuals revealed over three million new genetic variants [2]. Shockingly, approximately 5% of global PGx data comes from the African continent although certain gene variants such as the CYP2D6*17 originate from over a third of the African genetic matrix which can appreciably influence how populations and individuals respond to medication regimens [18]. Due to the disproportionately high frequencies of certain clinically actionable alleles in African populations, this variation has direct pharmacogenomic significance. For instance, sub‐Saharan African people are far more likely than many non‐African cohorts to have decreased function and inactive variants of CYP2D6 (e.g., CYP2D6*17, CYP2D6*29), which affect the metabolism of opioids, beta‐blockers, and antidepressants. Navigating through this gene pool offers an outstanding opportunity to improve drug safety, development and treatment outcomes globally, not only in Africa [26]. Concrete case studies underscore this potential. Research on efavirenz metabolism in South African cohorts demonstrated that CYP2B6 polymorphisms directly impact antiretroviral therapy. This is linked to altered drug metabolism, occurring in fewer than 5%–10% of people in many non‐African groups, but in up to 20%–35% of people in some parts of southern and eastern Africa. This genetic profile correlates with greater rates of neuropsychological toxicity at normal dose, highlighting the clinical significance of PGx‐based dosing for the treatment of HIV in African settings. The CYP2B6 star alleles have been found at significant frequencies in recent high‐coverage genome studies conducted throughout sub‐Saharan populations [27]. These alleles are associated with variations in drug disposition. African cohorts have also shown high rates of slow‐acetylator NAT2 gene variants, which may contribute to inter‐individual variation in tuberculosis patients' isoniazid response [28]. Failure to factor in this variation risks perpetuating unsatisfactory outcomes, while tailored PGx deployment offers a high‐yield potential to enhance both safety and efficacy for varied patient populations.

mHealth and Digital Infrastructure

Technology has evolved over time in Africa which is an opportunity to integrate PGx into the healthcare sector via digital tenets. Although electronic health records are gradually growing, mobile health (mHealth) equipment can manage the storage of genetic data and provide warnings and alerts when prescribing medications and developing patient care plans [24]. Smartphones can be influential health tools even in resource‐restrained areas since there are already existing mobile platforms used for diagnosing diseases such as hypertension. For instance, Rwanda's Babyl platform combines telemedicine with patient records to expand access to care, Kenya's mPedigree and SMS‐based adherence systems [29] support monitoring in HIV and TB treatment, and South Africa's MomConnect program [30] has demonstrated the scalability of national health initiatives by reaching millions of mothers with health information. These successes highlight the feasibility of leveraging mobile platforms for clinical innovation. Thus, personalized medicine can be successfully achieved by linking pharmacogenomic test outcomes to digital tools, making it more accessible in both rural and urban healthcare institutional settings.

Local Disease Priorities

High rates of infectious diseases such as malaria, tuberculosis and HIV/AIDS pose a challenge in Africa [31]. This is an avenue through which PGx research can be targeted and have a great impact. For example, CYP2B6 polymorphisms common in southern Africa significantly affect efavirenz metabolism, leading to dosing adjustments that reduce neurotoxicity in HIV treatment [32]. Similarly, NAT2 genotypes in Ethiopian TB patients have been linked to isoniazid‐induced hepatotoxicity, suggesting a clear pathway for PGx‐guided TB management [33]. Antimalarial response variability, including altered lumefantrine clearance linked to CYP3A5 variants in Kenyan populations, further illustrates the clinical relevance of PGx in Africa's top health priorities [34]. Embedding such evidence into clinical guidelines could both improve patient outcomes and reduce the economic burden of treatment failure. Initiatives such as are using genetic data to enhance medication prescriptions for local populations, making treatment regimens more effective and reducing medication‐related problems [2]. Relevant genes such as SLCO1B1 and CYP2D6 have a great impact on the biological system involved in processing and biotransformation of common antimalarial and HIV medications [8]. Tailoring these treatments using pharmacogenomic information can reduce mortality, improve quality of life and the healthcare system as well as reduce healthcare costs incurred.

South‐South Collaboration and Diaspora Science

A key factor that would drive development, implementation and successful progress in PGx is collaboration across Africa and beyond. H3Africa and the African Pharmacogenomics Consortium (APC) are some of the Pan‐African initiatives aiming to build research networks and facilitate sharing of data [14]. An example of pharmacogenomic initiative in Africa is the iPROTECTA project by the African Institute of Biomedical Science and Technology(AiBST). Future Directions

The future of PGx in Africa will depend on bold, coordinated steps that connect science with policy, education, and society. For PGx to truly take root, Africa needs more than scientific breakthroughs; it requires supportive policies, skilled professionals, and long‐term research built on fairness and sustainability.

Policy Integration and National Roadmaps

When governments and regulatory agencies recognize PGx in clinical guidelines and treatment policies, it signals the necessity of genomic data. While PGx is not a medicine to be included on the essential medicines list, its inclusion in the regulatory framework and guidelines ensures that it drives funding, shapes insurance coverage, and guides a smoother rollout of PGx across health systems [1]. By setting both short‐term and long‐term PGx integration goals aligned with national health priorities such as managing antimicrobial resistance or improving cancer care, countries can ensure PGx grows in a responsible way. Regional cooperation, through platforms such as the African Union or Africa CDC, can further streamline progress and avoid duplication of effort across the continent [36].

Education and Capacity Building

A future in PGx is possible with people trained to use it. This implies weaving PGx into the education of medical doctors, pharmacists, and nurses so that the next generation of healthcare professionals can confidently apply genomic insights in patient care [37]. Regional centers for excellence could play a game‐changing role in this program. They would not only drive research and innovation but also serve as training hubs offering mentorship, fellowships, and professional development engagements. Online tools like e‐learning and tele‐mentoring can extend these opportunities to rural and resource‐limited areas, ensuring no community is left behind [14].

Research Equity and Biobank Sustainability

For PGx to truly serve African populations, research must be ethical, inclusive, and sustainable. Biobanks, the repositories of genetic data and biological samples are central to this effort. However, they must operate under strong ethical frameworks that emphasize community involvement, informed consent, and fair sharing of benefits [38]. Adopting FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) while protecting participants' rights is essential. Local ownership of data must be prioritized and African scientists, communities, and health systems must see tangible benefits from international collaborations [23]. To keep biobanks sustainable, governments, private sector players, and new investment models will need to work together going beyond short‐term grants to ensure long‐lasting impact.

Health Economics and Implementation Considerations

To ensure translation of PGx research into practice in Africa, scientific breakthroughs and discoveries are not enough. Perspectives from health economics and implementation science are also required to ensure that the science translates into practical application [1]. For instance, it is crucial to consider evidence‐based cost‐effectiveness and budget implications of PGx, especially for developing and resource‐limited regions of Africa [2]. Other tools such as health technology assessment and pragmatic clinical trials, can also assist in determining whether the application of PGx is beneficial, cost‐effective, and practical for the healthcare system of Africa [4] (See Figure 3).

Conclusion

Pharmacogenomics has great potential to improve health care in Africa by enabling precision medicine through focusing on the continent's genetic diversity and disease burden. However, implementation is hindered by barriers including but not limited to inadequate sequencing capacity, underrepresentation in global genomic studies, inadequate laboratory infrastructure and ongoing challenge of brain drain. In addition, policy frameworks are either absent or too generic, leaving gaps in regulations, governance and data protection. Furthermore, inadequate funding to support research and high out‐of‐pocket expenditure, hinders integration of PGx into the health care system. Nevertheless, advances in digital infrastructure and mobile health provide innovative avenues for connecting genetic testing to clinical practices, even in limited‐resource settings. Local diseases priorities such as tuberculosis, malaria, and HIV/AIDS create a clear path for PGx research to have immediate impact. Moreover, regional and diaspora partnership are strengthening networks, knowledge exchange, and infrastructure development. To harness these opportunities, Africa should have a coordinated continental strategy. Investments in different areas such as education, infrastructure and ethical framework is very essential. PGx integration into healthcare policies by national governments, regional bodies, and global partners should be prioritized. With right and supportive policies, adequate research, and sustainable use of resources, Africa will have a future where personalized medicine is accessible and equitable. PGx integration is not merely a scientific necessity but also a public health concern for the continent's future.
