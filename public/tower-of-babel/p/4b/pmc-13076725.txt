Quantitative EEG signatures of power and functional connectivity alterations in Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia
Authors: Shahid Iqbal, Humaira Nisar, Kim Ho Yeap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dementia is a common neurodegenerative disease in the elderly, which affects the structural and functional connectivity of the brain. Recent studies indicate that electrophysiological measures, such as power spectral features and functional connectivity (FC), show promise for the diagnosis and classification of dementia. However, findings across studies remain inconsistent, and distinct electrophysiological patterns separating dementia subtypes, as well as Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD) and cognitively normal (CN) individuals, are not yet well established. This study focuses on spectral power and functional connectivity (FC) analyses of the Electroencephalography (EEG) frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma) in Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and FTD. A publicly available eyes-closed (EC), resting-state (RS) EEG dataset comprising 88 age-matched participants, 36 with AD, 29 CN, and 23 with FTD, was used in this study. Absolute power was computed using Welch’s method, while FC within each frequency band was assessed using Inter-Site Phase Clustering (ISPC) and network-based statistics, edge and node strength. The global power analysis revealed a significantly higher alpha power in CN compared to both AD and FTD. Regional analysis revealed a significantly lower temporal and parietal alpha in AD relative to CN and a significantly lower occipital alpha and beta in both AD and FTD compared to CN. Topographical power analysis showed unique significant differences within lobes in delta, theta, alpha, and gamma bands in AD and FTD, with AD illustrating a relatively more heterogeneous power distribution than FTD. Furthermore, FC analysis indicated that compared with CN, AD exhibited significantly lower edge strength in delta, theta, beta, and gamma bands, while significantly lower node strength in delta, theta, and gamma bands. Likewise, compared with CN, FTD showed significantly lower edge and node strength in the delta and theta bands, while significantly higher in the beta band. Furthermore, when compared to FTD, AD revealed a significantly lower edge and node strength in the delta, beta, and gamma bands. In conclusion, AD was associated with widespread FC disruptions, while FTD retained partially preserved connectivity, with the temporal lobe more affected than the frontal lobe. These findings suggest that band power and FC alterations may serve as potential biomarkers for diagnosing and classifying dementia into AD and FTD.

Introduction

Dementia is a neurodegenerative disorder. It is characterized by the accumulation of abnormal proteins in the brain, which distorts the transmission of brain signals1. The primary risk factors of dementia are age-related, and specifically cardiovascular diseases2. Dementia progresses gradually, and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is regarded as the intermediate stage between normal aging and dementia3. There are several types of dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Vascular Dementia (VD), Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD). Among these, AD is the most common in older adults, accounting for the majority of diagnoses4. In contrast, FTD more often affects relatively younger adults (45–65 years of age). The pathological differences between AD and FTD are quite significant, resulting in distinct patterns and effects on cognitive and behavioral impairments. A common pattern in both conditions is progressive cognitive decline; however, specific symptoms and affected brain regions differ. Unfortunately, the complexities of detecting and treating the neurodegenerative process have made dementia a largely untreatable condition. As a result, interventions primarily focus on symptom management and improving quality of life5.

A key pathological hallmark of both AD and FTD is the accumulation of abnormal proteins. For instance, AD is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta plaques and tau tangles in memory-related regions6, while FTD typically involves tau or TDP-43 aggregates in the frontal and anterior temporal lobes, the areas of the brain associated with personality and behavior7,8. Another difference is that AD often begins with memory decline, whereas FTD patients first experience behavioral changes, followed by dementia symptoms9. The overlapping effects and symptoms complicate the diagnosis and classification of dementia. However, timely and accurate diagnosis is crucial in every neurodegenerative disease because early detection may delay neurodegeneration and positively impact the quality of life of patients and their caregivers10.

Brain signal-based biomarkers are considered valuable tools for distinguishing between AD and FTD. Neural signals have been investigated as biomarkers in a range of neurological disorders, including epilepsy11, amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI), and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)12,13. In practice, the neural signals are examined using various diagnostic techniques like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scans, while electrophysiological imaging methods are also employed to assess cognitive decline and assist in the diagnosis of AD14,15. Similarly, Electroencephalography (EEG), which records the electrical activity of the brain, is widely used to detect abnormalities in the brain waves and provides a detailed insight into the cortical activity associated with various disorders16. Compared to other brain imaging methods, EEG is often preferred because it can map the electrophysiological alterations in brain networks at early stages, offering high temporal resolution combined with affordability17.

The literature suggests that quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) analysis can provide neuropsychological biomarkers useful for the early diagnosis of dementia18. Recent studies have demonstrated that spectral power and functional connectivity (FC) analyses of the EEG can be used as potential biomarkers19,20. Specific patterns in qEEG are expected to play a key role in detecting cognitive impairment at the preclinical stage of AD and predicting its future progression21. To explore qEEG patterns, resting-state EEG (RS-EEG) is often quantitatively analyzed through methods like power curve analysis, wavelet analysis, and power spectrograms, which provide insight into brain FC22. In short, relative variations in EEG characteristics identified through quantitative analysis of neural oscillations are recognized as important biomarkers of AD20,23,24.

Although EEG biomarkers are well recognized in AD, their role in differentiating AD from FTD has not been fully explored. Only a few studies have explored the use of qEEG to distinguish between AD and FTD. For instance25, found that an increase in theta power was associated with DLB, while alpha power was reduced in AD compared to FTD. Another study designed a multimodal EEG approach to differentiate between AD and FTD26. Their results showed that AD had greater spectral power in the delta band than FTD. In addition, compared to controls, AD patients showed reduced global field power and mean occurrence but increased mean microstate duration. However, despite the depth of this study, no information was provided on FC outcomes for distinguishing AD from FTD. Moreover, most existing studies are limited by small sample sizes and a narrow range of analytical approaches. Many studies have only concluded with a relative increase or decrease in band power27,28.

Similarly, time-frequency domain analysis of qEEG has shown substantial promise in uncovering neuronal alterations associated with dementia29. A study on FC of AD reported that AD is characterized by slowing of oscillations, while the FC in theta band is decreased while an increase was noted in gamma band in AD30. Another study using time-frequency and band-pass filtering FC matrices has reported decreased FC in the theta within central regions in AD when compared to FTD31. However, despite limited findings for classification of AD and FTD, the study has not established a distinct differentiating pattern between FTD and CN. Moreover, FC was estimated using diverse approaches for lower and higher frequencies to study the alterations in brain connectivity associated with AD and FTD32. Nevertheless, the findings highlight the potential of qEEG measures as biomarkers for differentiating AD and FTD, there remains a need to establish distinct power and connectivity patterns for classifying dementia into AD and FTD.

In summary, EEG and qEEG analyses have shown significant potential in identifying biomarkers of dementia. However, comparative studies between AD and FTD remain limited, particularly regarding changes in spectral power and FC, or they do not report distinct power and connectivity patterns for classification of dementia into AD and FTD. Addressing this gap may contribute to early and reliable identification of AD and FTD. Therefore, this study contributes to the growing body of research on dementia by examining power spectrum and FC alterations in AD and FTD, thereby providing deeper insight and identifying distinct patterns that may aid in their classification. We hypothesize that AD and FTD exhibit distinct spectral and FC patterns, which may serve as potential neural biomarkers for differential diagnosis.

Materials and methods

The study begins with dataset acquisition, followed by data preprocessing. The power spectral density (PSD) is first estimated with Welch’s method, followed by the calculation of absolute power across the five frequency bands and the five lobes, frontal (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, Fz), temporal (F7, F8, T3, T4, T5, T6), central (C3, C4, Cz), parietal (P3, P4, Pz), and occipital (O1, O2) in AD, Cognitively Normal (CN) individuals, and FTD. After computing spectral power, the second phase of the study involves examining the FC within each frequency band across the three groups. Network-based statistics are used to determine the edge and node strength across each band across the three groups. Finally, statistical analysis of power and network measures were computed to enable comparative analysis among AD, CN, and FTD. The overall workflow of the study is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Participants

The study utilized a publicly available EC, RS EEG dataset33, which includes recordings from 88 age-matched subjects divided into three groups: 36 with AD, 29 with CN, and 23 with FTD. EEG recordings were obtained from the 2nd Department of Neurology, AHEPA General Hospital, Thessaloniki, Greece. Two neuropsychological assessments, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), were used for the preliminary evaluation of all participants. Both assessments asses cognitive and functional status of the brain on different scoring scales. The AD group had a mean MMSE score of 17.15 (sd = 4.5) and a CDR score of 1 (sd = 0.54), FTD group had a mean MMSE score of 22.17 (SD = 8.22) and a CDR score of 0.26, while the Control (CN) group had an average MMSE score of 30. Resting-state, eyes-closed EEG recordings and preprocessing

A clinical EEG headset, the Nihon Kohden EEG 2100, was used for data collection. It has 19 scalp electrodes (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, and O2) positioned according to the international 10–20 system across the five brain lobes: frontal (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, Fz), temporal (F7, F8, T3, T4, T5, T6), central (C3, C4, Cz), parietal (P3, P4, Pz), and occipital (O1, O2). In this system, 1 represents the left hemisphere and 2 represents the right hemisphere. Fp refers to the frontopolar (very front) region of the left hemisphere. “z” represents the midline; hence, Fz is the frontal midline electrode, similarly, Cz is the central midline electrode, and Pz is the parietal midline electrode. Additionally, two reference electrodes, A1 and A2, were positioned on the mastoids of the temporal lobe as specified in the device manual. The recordings were collected in a clinical setting. Before each recording, a few variables were kept constant. The skin impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ, the sampling rate was set at 500 Hz, and the resolution was kept at 10 µV/mm. Although higher density headsets can provide more detailed spatial resolution, previous studies have shown that the connectivity pattern, and the complex features of EEG signals can be reliably captured with 19 and 20 channels headsets34,35. Additionally, analyses using low-density EEG headsets are needed, particularly in low-resource settings, to make the technology accessible to underrepresented populations who cannot afford high-density headsets.

The dataset is available in a preprocessed form with the following steps applied: first, a Butterworth bandpass filter with a frequency range of 0.5 to 45 Hz was applied. Second, the data were re-referenced to the average value of the reference electrodes (A1 and A2). Third, Automatic Subspace Reconstruction (ASR), an automated method for artifact removal, was performed. Fourth, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was used to decompose the 19 EEG channels into 19 independent components, which were carefully inspected to remove ocular and muscular artifacts. As a fifth step, we rejected the remaining artifacts through visual inspection, since some artifacts remained even after preprocessing. Finally, as the dataset included recordings of varying lengths for different subjects after artefact removal, only the 5 min (300 s) of each subject’s recording were selected to ensure an unbiased analysis. Different studies available in the literature have employed different time duration for brain network analysis. For example, Caravaglios et al. assessed Functional Networks in neurodegenerative conditions using 2 min (120 s) of data36. Another study has used 3 min (180 s) of EEG data to examine brain FC in the investigation of pain37.

Methods

Spectral power analysis

Spectrum analysis is a common technique in signal processing. The primary goal of spectral analysis is to decompose a signal into its constituent frequency components to study even the minute details. This approach is particularly important for studying physiological signals such as the Electrocardiogram (ECG) and non-stationary EEG recordings. In the present work, RS, EC-EEG was analyzed across frequency bands associated with distinct brain functions: delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–45 Hz), which are widely employed in neurophysiological research38. The absolute power spectral density (PSD) was estimated in EEGLAB using Welch’s method, a widely used approach in clinical EEG research39, because of its potential to capture total power within a frequency band without normalization. The literature has demonstrated its value in examining neurodegenerative disorders and neurofeedback training to understand brain electrical activity40–42. Unlike relative PSD, this method avoids distortions from normalization and provides a more accurate measure of neural oscillatory activity43.

For spectral analysis, the EEG signals were segmented into 2-second epochs with 50% overlap to balance frequency resolution and variance in the spectral estimate44. Each epoch was multiplied by a Hann window to taper the data at the edges and minimize spectral leakage45. Functional connectivity

FC is used to establish formal relationships between signals recorded from pairs of electrodes. Depending on the application and experimental requirements, connectivity analyses can broadly be categorized into power-based and phase-based approaches. Power-based connectivity quantifies correlations in time-frequency power fluctuations between electrode pairs, either within the same or across different frequency bands and trials. In contrast, phase-based connectivity determines the distribution of phase angle differences between channels to assess the degree of neuronal synchronization; however, the latter is frequently preferred in EEG research because of its neurophysiological interpretability and computational efficiency47. Accordingly, the present study employed a phase-based approach to estimate FC.

A method for calculating EEG phase-based FC is Inter-site phase clustering (ISPC)48. This method evaluates connectivity by quantifying the phase angle differences between pairs of electrodes across time, as shown in Eq. 4. Due to high temporal resolution and flexible band-specific filtering, the Hilbert transform (HT) was used to extract phase-related information, enabling the interpretation of frequency-specific features. ISPC has been widely applied in both EEG and fMRI studies, including those focused on neurodegenerative disorders and neurofeedback applications, to characterize inter-regional synchronization49–51. The data were first z-score normalized (standardized to zero mean and unit variance) and subsequently filtered into the five frequency bands. Connectivity matrices and graphs represent the same underlying network information in different forms: the connectivity matrix provides a matrix-based representation of pairwise relationships, while the graph represents the same information in a network framework composed of nodes and edges. In graphs, nodes represent electrodes and edges represent the connections between them. Only non-zero edges were displayed52.

Statistical analysis

A hierarchical framework was used to systematically assess group differences in band-limited power. First, global band power was compared across the three groups to identify overall differences within each frequency band. Second, for bands showing significant group effects, regional analyses were conducted to localize these differences by comparing power across individual lobes. Finally, within each frequency band, lobe-wise power distributions were examined separately within each group to characterize regional patterns of band-specific activity.

Global band power was compared across the three groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Significant bands identified at the global level were localized in the regional analysis using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) in JASP (version 0.95.4.0), with group and cortical lobe as fixed factors and subject as a random intercept, followed by comparing power across lobes in each band within each group to characterize regional patterns of band-specific activity53. EEG power values that deviated from normality were log-transformed before analysis. Likewise, to examine differences in individual band node and edge strength across the three groups, we have used the Kruskal-Wallis test. Bonferroni correction was applied across all independent tests to control for multiple testing.

Results

The results of this study are presented in two major sections: (1) Power Spectral Analysis and (2) Functional Connectivity Analysis. The first section focuses on the distribution of absolute power across different frequency bands and brain lobes in each diagnostic group (AD, CN, and FTD), highlighting group-wise differences. The second section examines phase-based functional connectivity patterns, with an emphasis on alterations in global network structure. Together, these analyses provide a comprehensive view of the neurophysiological distinctions among the three groups.

Discussion

This study investigated power and functional connectivity patterns across AD, FTD, and CN individuals. Synaptic loss and region-specific alterations in spectral activity have been suggested as potential biomarkers for dementia diagnosis41. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that dementia subtypes are characterized by distinct alterations in neural connectivity. These alterations, as discussed in the following section, may serve as potential biomarkers to aid in the early diagnosis and classification of dementia.

Power spectral analysis

Global alterations in band power across groups

From Fig. 2; Table 3, the AD group demonstrated a spectral profile consistent with impaired cognitive function, characterized by elevated low-frequency and reduced high-frequency power, significantly decreased alpha, compared to healthy individuals. These results are mostly in agreement with previous research, which indicates that AD is associated with increased theta, a later increase in delta activity, and decreased alpha and beta activity54. Spectral slowing, attributed to cholinergic deficits, is regarded as a marker of AD progression and has also been linked to neurodegeneration55. Similarly, FTD showed lower power across all bands, significantly lower alpha, compared to CN. Reduced alpha power in FTD relative to CN has also been documented in earlier studies5. Furthermore, in comparisons between AD and FTD, the FTD group showed lower power in the slow frequency bands (delta and theta) relative to AD, and higher power in the alpha and beta bands. A study has reported a significantly higher delta power in early-onset AD than in FTD26. Moreover, FTD exhibited lower gamma power compared to AD. The global spectral profile of FTD observed in our study is in agreement with the reports indicating that FTD shares similarities to the spectral characteristics of AD in certain frequency bands, while resembling CN in others56. Furthermore, the lower delta power in FTD compared to AD, and reduced gamma power in FTD relative to CN, may possibly reflect early functional impairment.

Regional localization of band power differences across groups

The observed pattern in our study suggests that higher alpha power in CN relative to AD and FTD may serve as a diagnostic marker of dementia. These findings are in line with earlier reports of theta, alpha, and beta band differences between healthy individuals and those with dementia56. Regional localization of band power differences as provided in Table 4 highlighted the diagnostic relevance of temporal and parietal alpha activity in AD, as well as occipital alpha and beta activity in improving diagnostic accuracy for both AD and FTD. Furthermore, compared to CN, AD showed significantly lower temporal and parietal alpha, while both AD and FTD demonstrated significantly lower occipital power in alpha and beta bands. While reduced parietal and occipital power in the alpha band of AD is documented in previous studies57, other findings may provide additional diagnostic support for dementia. To the best of our knowledge, this consolidated set of findings is the first to identify region-specific EEG band power differences for differentiation of healthy aging from demented aging.

Topographical distribution of band power across lobes within groups

The differences in spectral profile of AD and CN is documented in previous studies26,27. Several studies have also elaborated spectral patterns for differentiating between AD and FTD25,28, . Although the studies have demonstrated changes in power between different channels and bands, there is a scarcity of studies systematically differentiating AD and FTD based on significant power changes.

This study examined lobe-wise power distributions within each frequency band to characterize regional patterns of band-specific activity across diagnostic groups. As shown in Fig. 3, a notable pattern for the classification of dementia into AD and FTD is the distribution of power across lobes in the delta, theta, alpha, and gamma bands. Additionally, as shown in Table 5, the heterogeneous distribution of power in the delta, alpha, and gamma bands in AD, as compared to FTD, could serve as a potential biomarker for the classification of dementia into AD and FTD. As delta activity occurs during deep sleep, elevated delta activity during wakefulness is generally considered indicative of brain pathology58. Therefore, the heterogeneous distribution of power within the delta band may be attributed to an overall elevation in delta power. Similarly, the heterogeneous distribution of power in the alpha band in CN, followed by AD and FTD, suggests its utility as a biomarker for diagnosis and classification of dementia into AD and FTD. Given that elevated alpha and beta power are associated with attentional engagement59, the homogeneous distribution of alpha-band power in AD and FTD may reflect the overall reduction in alpha power.

Furthermore, the more homogeneous power distribution across frequency bands observed in FTD, when compared to AD, may reflect the early onset and uniform progression of neurodegeneration. Such a loss of regional differentiation aligns with neuroimaging findings suggesting that regional neuronal activity could serve as a potential biomarker for identifying subjective cognitive decline and disease severity60. In contrast, the more heterogeneous power distribution across bands observed in AD may reflect a more chaotic and widespread disruption of neural activity, potentially associated with advanced stages of neurodegeneration.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic investigation of within-group power distribution, revealing novel spectral patterns for AD and FTD classification. Although we have thoroughly examined the power pattern by quantitatively analyzing within-group power distributions across lobes, these findings should be validated in future longitudinal studies.

Functional connectivity analysis

The FC among the three groups is analyzed from edge and node strength computed from the weighted connectivity matrix, as shown in Figs. 6 and 7, and summarized in Tables 6 and 7, respectively.

In comparison with CN, it is observed that edge strength in AD is significantly lower in all bands except alpha. This reduction in edge strength reflects impaired inter-regional communication and network disconnection. In addition, the node strength in AD is consistently lower across all bands when compared to CN, which reflects the weak participation of nodes in network communication. Previous studies have linked reduced complexity and synchrony to AD, reflecting impaired functional interactions in the brain. In particular, a study suggests that Alzheimer’s pathology spreads throughout the default mode network, which results in alterations of FC61. Furthermore, as synaptic loss has been considered a key pathological indicator of memory impairment in AD62, the reduced node strength may reflect the underlying depletion of synaptic density. Similar reports of reduced connectivity strength across brain regions in AD patients further support this interpretation63. Nevertheless, in AD, the relative (not statistically significant) increase in alpha band edge strength compared to CN may reflect an abnormal synchronization, given that node strength in the alpha band is reduced relative to CN.

Likewise, when compared to CN, the edge and node strength in FTD is significantly lower in delta and theta, while significantly higher in the beta band. The increased strength in the beta band may mirror fMRI findings where the Default Mode Network is disrupted in AD but enhanced in behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD)64. Furthermore, in regional-based analysis, a study found hyper connectivity in anterior regions in FTD and in posterior regions in AD patients65; nevertheless, the additional engagement of neural resources, whether functional or dysfunctional, is still debated.

When compared with FTD, AD demonstrated a significantly lower node and edge strength in delta, beta, and gamma bands, and a similar strength in the theta band. This overall increase in FTD, relative to AD, suggests better preserved cognitive function and stronger network participation in FTD. Given the findings of a study on epilepsy, which related lower theta in the prefrontal cortex with impaired memory and executive functions66, the similar significant reduction in theta edge and node strength in FTD and AD relative to CN suggests shared compromised memory and executive functions.

The decrease in edge and node strength in AD relative to FTD, and that of both AD and FTD relative to CN suggest that while both AD and FTD conditions involve disrupted connectivity, AD is marked by widespread disruptions, whereas FTD shows selective disruptions. The following discussion examines lobe-wise patterns, specifically frontal and temporal, of preservation and degeneration in AD and FTD by comparing node strength among AD, CN, and FTD.

The lower frontal lobe node strength in AD compared to FTD and CN, and a relatively preserved strength in FTD compared to AD and CN, suggests compromised lobe functions in AD, with relatively milder compromise in FTD. MRI studies of early behavioral-variant FTD (bvFTD) report no significant frontal volume loss at onset7, supporting the idea that connectivity alterations may vary according to subtype and disease stage. In the same manner, relative to FTD, AD exhibited significantly lower frontal lobe node strength in delta and beta bands, while comparatively lower in theta, alpha, and gamma bands. Given that higher-level processes are associated with the frontal lobe67, the present findings suggest that frontal lobe functions were more severely impaired in AD, with relatively preserved functions in FTD.

Similarly, the lower temporal lobe node strength in AD and FTD compared with CN suggests a predominant temporal lobe dysfunction than frontal lobe in FTD. As earlier studies have linked language and sensory processing with the temporal and parietal lobes68, our findings indicate that these functions are likely compromised in FTD compared to CN, though the disruption is less severe than in AD. Furthermore, it can be concluded from these findings that the FTD pathology was more prominently associated with the temporal lobe, with comparatively lower involvement of the frontal lobe.

FC analysis suggested that CN exhibited stronger and more balanced connectivity across lobes and frequency bands, reflecting preserved network integrity. In contrast, AD demonstrated widespread reductions in node degree, especially in the alpha, beta, and gamma bands, indicating severe disruption of long-range synchronization. FTD was characterized by milder frontal deficits and more severe temporal impairments but relatively preserved or even increased node degree in some bands. Direct comparisons confirmed that AD is characterized by a more diffuse and severe network breakdown than FTD.

Overall, the lobe and band-specific alterations in spectral power in AD and FTD extend the previous literature by demonstrating that lobe-specific patterns may provide additional diagnostic accuracy for dementia. Furthermore, the topographical analyses further identify unique differences in delta and alpha band power across lobes for the classification of dementia into AD and FTD. Moreover, connectivity analysis demonstrated widespread disruptions in AD and relatively preserved networks in FTD, with temporal lobe changes being most prominent. Together, these distinct power and connectivity signatures provide complementary markers that can support the differentiation and classification of AD and FTD.

Limitations of the study

This study provides an analysis of EEG power and functional connectivity to identify biomarkers for early diagnosis and classification of dementia; however, there are several limitations. First, the work relies on a publicly available dataset; the follow-up EEG recordings are not available, making it difficult to track how FC patterns change over time. Second, though medication alters the FC of the brain in neurodegenerative diseases69, there is no information on the medications that the participants are using. Future studies should account for the effects of medications when assessing the FC in dementia. Third, given that socioeconomic factors can affect brain health70, our findings should be validated in diverse populations to ensure generalizability. Lastly, our results provide preliminary evidence of power and FC findings; these associations require further validation through longitudinal and multimodal studies, including combining EEG with MRI or PET imaging.

Future work

Prior studies suggest that neurodegenerative conditions affect the structural and functional connectivity of the brain differently across genders71. In the future, we will study the FC patterns between men and women for AD and FTD.

Conclusion

Our findings identified distinct spectral and connectivity patterns in AD and FTD, which may aid in a better diagnosis of these conditions. Specifically, AD exhibited significantly lower temporal and parietal power compared to CN in the alpha band, while both AD and FTD showed significantly reduced occipital power relative to CN in the alpha and beta bands. These findings may enhance diagnostic accuracy for AD and FTD. Topographical analyses further revealed unique, significant differences within lobes in delta, theta, alpha, and gamma band power for the classification of AD and FTD. Similarly, quantitative analyses demonstrated relatively more heterogeneous power distribution in AD compared to FTD. Likewise, the connectivity analysis revealed significant band and lobe-specific changes that showed widespread FC disruptions in AD, while retained connectivity in FTD at levels between AD and CN. Lastly, FTD pathology was more prominently associated with the temporal lobe, with comparatively lower involvement of the frontal lobe. Such a multimodal, quantitative approach may improve diagnostic accuracy, support early detection, and provide more targeted clinical interventions.
