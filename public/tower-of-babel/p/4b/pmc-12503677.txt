A 39-year-old otherwise healthy man presented to the emergency department with severe rectal pain and a 3-day history of constipation. He had presented to another community emergency department twice in the previous 2 days for the same concerns. He reported consuming raw pumpkin seeds with intact shells from 3 pumpkins during Halloween pumpkin carving 4 days prior.

During his first 2 emergency department visits, manual disimpaction was attempted, releasing only a few pumpkin seeds from a hard mass in his rectal vault. Investigations were unremarkable. He was discharged with instructions for twice-daily use of polyethylene glycol (PEG) and fleet enemas as required for constipation, as well as topical lidocaine anesthetic and nifedipine ointments for anal fissures, 4 take-home doses of hydromorphone, and advice to take sitz baths. He was instructed to return to the emergency department for increasing pain, fevers, chills, or signs of perianal abscess.

On the patient’s third emergency department presentation, he reported worsening rectal pain and tenesmus. He had been taking PEG as prescribed and had passed some gas and a small amount of liquid stool. He denied any hematochezia, melena, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, chills, urinary symptoms, chest pain, or shortness of breath. He did not have any underlying psychiatric diagnoses and he had not ingested any additional seeds.

On examination, the patient had a temperature of 36.5°C, heart rate of 70 beats/min, respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, blood pressure of 117/58 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Although he was in obvious discomfort, he did not appear unstable. His abdomen was nondistended, soft, and non-tender to palpation, with no palpable masses. Digital rectal examination (DRE) revealed a large, firm mass with discernible pumpkin seeds in his rectal vault.

Laboratory investigations were unremarkable. An abdominal plain-film radiograph was interpreted as showing mild rectal fecal loading (Figure 1). Serial transabdominal point-of-care ultrasonography (POCUS) showed a dense hyperechoic mass in the rectum (Figure 2).

The working diagnosis was a large rectal pumpkin seed bezoar. We attempted manual disimpaction under procedural sedation. Few pumpkin seeds were dislodged, and the large bezoar remained in the rectal vault. A gastroenterologist was consulted and the patient underwent flexible sigmoidoscopy, which identified innumerable pumpkin seeds. The endoscope was able to manoeuvre around and break down the bezoar. Multiple fleet enemas and application of a Roth net removed only half the seeds. He was given PEG, and the gastroenterologist planned for a repeat flexible sigmoidoscopy the next morning to reassess seed burden.

Overnight in the emergency department monitoring unit, another unsuccessful manual disimpaction under procedural sedation was attempted for increasing pain and tenesmus. The repeat endoscopy revealed a newly accumulated seed bezoar in the rectal vault, with minimal seed extraction. The procedure was aborted because of sedation-related hemodynamic instability. We consulted a general surgeon for exploration under general anesthesia to remove the remaining bezoar. Overnight, the patient passed many seeds in 1 bowel movement. The subsequent exploration under anesthesia, which included a DRE and colonoscopy, found no seeds in the rectum, and we assumed that the remaining seeds were passed preoperatively. The patient was discharged home the same day after tolerating oral liquids.

Discussion

A bezoar is a mass of indigestible material in the gastrointestinal tract. Seed bezoars are a subtype of phytobezoars composed of indigestible plant seed fibres.1,2 Seed bezoars of various compositions have been described in the literature, including seeds from watermelons, prickly pears, dates, sunflowers, and pumpkins, as well as popcorn kernels.2–4 Regional dietary habits and cultural practices often influence which seeds are the culprit.1 Rectal seed bezoars are rare in North America and are an uncommon cause for presentation to the emergency department. A systematic review of cases of rectal seed bezoars found that only 19% of cases were from Western Europe and North America.2 Sunflower and pumpkin seeds are available in North America year round, with pumpkin seeds holding cultural importance during the fall months.

Seed bezoars typically occur in healthy patients without known risk factors for bezoar formation (i.e., reduced gastric motility, prior gastric surgery, neuropsychiatric conditions, endocrinopathies affecting gastrointestinal motility, and poor mastication). 1,2,5 Unlike other bezoars, seed bezoars most commonly occur in the rectum because the ingested seeds are small enough to pass through the pylorus and ileocecal valve but then accumulate in the distal colon and rectum.2 Once in the rectum, seeds become dehydrated, compact, sharp, and difficult to evacuate, causing rectal pain and constipation.4 A thorough history, including a dietary and oral intake review, and a DRE are crucial to diagnose a seed bezoar in patients presenting with relevant symptoms. If a full oral intake history cannot be obtained, the presence of a rectal seed bezoar may still be suggested by a positive colonic crunch sign on DRE: the palpation of a prickly mass of seeds or seed shards with sharp or irregular edges in the rectal vault.6

Although a rectal seed bezoar is primarily diagnosed clinically, imaging modalities such as abdominal radiography, POCUS, or computed tomography (CT) can enhance diagnostic reliability.7 Plain-film radiographic features are often nonspecific and may show a density in the rectum (Figure 1) or signs of bowel obstruction; these features are limited in distinguishing a bezoar from fecal impaction or an abscess.7 In contrast, POCUS may demonstrate more characteristic features of a bezoar, which typically appears as an intraluminal mass with a hyperechoic arc-like surface and acoustic shadowing (Figure 2).7 Point-of-care ultrasonography can also be used to identify features of bezoar-related gastrointestinal obstruction, although its utility may be limited by operator proficiency and the depth or location of the bezoar within the abdomen. Imaging by CT plays an important role in diagnosing bezoar-related complications such as bowel obstructions or perforations and is indicated when clinical signs and symptoms of an acute abdomen are present or to exclude alternative diagnoses. However, CT is not routinely indicated for the initial diagnosis and management of an uncomplicated rectal seed bezoar.7

We used serial POCUS assessments to monitor our patient’s treatment response, which limited the need for repeating radiographic imaging in the emergency department. In this case, CT imaging was not indicated, as the clinical diagnosis of rectal bezoar had been established on examination; the patient was vitally stable and systemically well, with no clinical concern for complications or other intra-abdominal pathology requiring further investigation. Point-of-care ultrasonography of the rectum is traditionally done with an anterior approach while the patient is supine. The curvilinear probe is placed in a transverse position cephalad to the pubic symphysis while fanning the probe caudad to obtain an axial image, and then rotating the probe longitudinally and rocking caudad to obtain an orthogonal sagittal view. Image acquisition is improved with a distended urinary bladder to serve as an acoustic window.8

No guidelines on the specific management of rectal bezoars are available, and symptom resolution ultimately relies on disimpaction. 2 In the emergency department, management of rectal bezoars is similar to that of fecal impaction. Conservative measures such as enemas and laxatives may be used initially, although a review of available case reports shows that very few cases of rectal seed bezoars resolve with conservative measures alone.9 Manual disimpaction under procedural sedation is often required and should not be unduly delayed if conservative approaches are unlikely to be effective. In refractory cases or if complications like perforations arise, therapeutic options range from endoscopy to surgical interventions.8

In our patient’s case and as noted in other published case reports, seed bezoars often require several attempts at disimpaction as the bezoar can reaccumulate in the rectum as additional seeds descend through the gastrointestinal tract.5,6,10–12 Manual disimpaction should be done under procedural sedation to relax the anal sphincter and minimize discomfort. One or 2 lubricated, gloved fingers are inserted into the rectum and a scissoring motion is used to gently break down and extract the fragmented bezoar. This process is repeated until the rectum is cleared. The benefits of disimpaction generally outweigh the risks, as untreated rectal seed bezoars can result in serious complications, including rectal pressure necrosis, bleeding, intestinal obstruction, and, rarely, bowel perforation. Although manual disimpaction carries potential risk of anal fissures, rectal bleeding, perforation, or infection, these are uncommon. Rare cases of bradycardic arrest have been reported in older patients, which is likely due to disimpaction-induced autonomic changes.13

In summary, we present a case of a large rectal pumpkin seed bezoar that caused the patient severe rectal pain and fecal impaction requiring repeat manual disimpaction, endoscopies, and, ultimately, surgical exploration for symptom resolution. Different imaging modalities are available to support this clinical diagnosis. Prompt recognition and early disimpaction are necessary to prevent complications and, ideally, reduce the need for invasive interventions.
