Fever of unknown origin: adult-onset Still’s disease as the hidden culprit—a case report
Authors: Shailesh Kurmi, Jyoti Kalwar, Neetika Paudel, Bishal Budha, Bikki Gupta Rauniyar, Shivam Jha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdult-onset Still's disease (AOSD) is a rare systemic autoinflammatory disorder characterized by prolonged fever, arthritis, and a maculopapular rash. Due to its nonspecific presentation, it often mimics infections, malignancies, and autoimmune diseases, making diagnosis challenging.Case presentationWe report the case of a 19-year-old Caucasian, Nepalese female presenting with persistent fever, joint pain, rash, and systemic symptoms. Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis, anemia, and elevated liver enzymes, while imaging showed lymphadenopathy and mild hepatomegaly. Extensive evaluation ruled out infectious, autoimmune, and malignant causes. Significantly elevated serum ferritin and C-reactive protein supported the diagnosis of AOSD based on Yamaguchi criteria. The patient responded dramatically to corticosteroid therapy, with full resolution of symptoms and normalization of laboratory findings within 2 weeks.ConclusionsThis case highlights the importance of considering AOSD in patients with prolonged fever and systemic features after common causes are excluded. Timely diagnosis using clinical criteria and inflammatory markers can facilitate prompt treatment and favorable outcomes.

Background

Adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) is a rare, systemic autoinflammatory disorder with an incidence between 0.16 and 0.4 per 100,000 people and prevalence between 1 and 34 cases per 1 million people [1]. AOSD has a wide-ranging clinical presentation [2]. It is mainly characterized by fever, evanescent maculopapular rash, and arthritis or arthralgia, each are present in approximately 75–95% of patients while a majority also presents with myalgia, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, and splenomegaly. Hepatomegaly, pleurisy, pericarditis, and abdominal pain are seen in a few cases [3]. Currently, there are no specific biological markers. The laboratory findings show elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, serum ferritin, neutrophilia. The diagnosis is based on Yamaguchi criteria after ruling out infections, malignancy, other inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [4].

To contribute to the findings associated with the disease, we present the case of a 19-year-old female who presented with fibrotic changes in right upper and left lower lung lobes with mild hepatomegaly, which is rarely reported in ASOD. Such pulmonary involvement is uncommon and misleading toward infectious and malignant pathology. The case report is written as per the CARE guidelines [5].

Case presentation

A 19-year-old Caucasian, Brahmin Nepalese lady from western part of Nepal, presented to the rheumatology outpatient department of medicine of a tertiary care center with chief complaints of fever for 1 month with a maximum documented temperature of 103°F which was continuous and associated with chills and rigor. Other symptoms included dry cough, sore throat, nausea, joint pain involving bilateral knees, wrists, proximal interphalangeal and metacarpophalangeal joints, and pink colored rashes on her chest. Her physical examination revealed pallor, pink colored maculopapular rash on the chest along with cervical and axillary lymphadenopathy. There was no history of similar complaints, and no other family members had them. The patient was psychologically sound and was well before the symptoms appeared. No comorbidities known and there was no history of similar symptoms in the past.

Her laboratory findings were significant with markedly raised total leucocyte count of 18,800/microliter of blood with neutrophilic predominance (90%), platelet count of 2,12,000/microliter of blood and anemia with hemoglobin of 7.4 g/dL. Her renal function test was normal, however liver function test showed slightly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 80 U/l (normal range: 5–40 U/l). Keeping infectious etiologies like UTI, infective endocarditis, TB and dengue as important differential diagnoses, we sent routine urine, urine culture, blood culture, sputum profile, chest X-ray, dengue panel, tropical panel and serology, all of which came normal. In order to rule out malignant conditions further investigations were done. USG (abdomen + pelvis) showed normally visualized organs. CT scan of neck, chest and abdomen was done which showed homogeneously enhancing cervical, axillary and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. It also showed fibrotic changes in the right upper lobe and left lower lobe with mild hepatomegaly. Then in order to rule out autoimmune causes, rheumatoid factor (RA), antinuclear antibodies (ANA) levels, ASO titer and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were sent, all of which came normal. Although ESR is typically elevated in AOSD, our patient had a normal ESR despite markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum ferritin. Similar discrepancies have been noted in AOSD, where CRP and ferritin reflect rapid cytokine-driven inflammation mediated predominantly by IL-1 and IL-6, whereas ESR may lag or be influenced by hematological factors such as anemia and altered rouleaux formation [6–11]. CRP and ferritin, in contrast, are direct acute-phase reactants and may rise disproportionately in AOSD [9–11].

Eventually when all the common causes were ruled out, we started to think of adult-onset Still's disease (AOSD). We sent serum ferritin and C-reactive protein (CRP) as supportive evidence. Surprisingly both came high; ferritin- > 2000 mg/ml (normal range: 4.5–204.0 mg/ml) and CRP- 251.24 mg/ml (normal range: 0–6 mg/ml). Thus, our history, physical examination, and laboratory investigations fulfilled Yamaguchi Criteria for AOSD. She was then started on intravenous (IV) methylprednisolone as systemic corticosteroids are considered the first-line treatment for AOSD, particularly in patients with high-grade fever, polyarthralgia, and systemic involvement, as they rapidly suppress the underlying cytokine-driven inflammatory response. This approach is well-supported in the literature, where steroids have been shown to produce quick clinical and laboratory improvement, while additional agents such as methotrexate or biologic therapies (for example, IL-1 or IL-6 inhibitors) are reserved for refractory or steroid-dependent cases [7, 8, 11]. Following the treatment, her symptoms improved in 3 days. She became afebrile, joint pain and laboratory parameters improved. She was subsequently discharged on oral prednisolone.

Patient was discharged after being made aware of complications such as chronic destructive arthritis, interstitial lung disease, macrophage activation syndrome, hepatic involvement, and cardiopulmonary manifestations. After 2 weeks, she came for a follow-up visit and there were no symptoms and patient reported no other difficulties. Repeat laboratory investigations were done and showed normal results. Treatment with corticosteroids is continued with a gradual taper while assessing response and preventing relapse. If symptoms recur or tapering becomes difficult, steroid-sparing agents such as methotrexate or biologics (for example, IL-1 or IL-6 inhibitors) may be considered, as recommended for refractory disease. Counselling focused on educating her about recognizing early signs of flare such as fever spikes, joint swelling, or worsening fatigue. Routine monitoring parameters include complete blood counts, ESR/CRP, serum ferritin, liver enzymes, and clinical evaluation of joint function at each visit. The patient is also advised regarding infection precautions while on immunosuppressive therapy, maintenance of adequate nutrition, and gradual physical activity to support joint mobility.

Discussion

AOSD was first described by Eric Bywaters in 1971 [12]. The etiology and pathogenesis of the disease is unclear. However, genetic, infectious, environmental factors and immune dysfunction contribute to the occurrence of the disease. Several cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-18, macrophage colony stimulating factor, interferon gamma (INF-γ) are correlated in the pathogenesis of AOSD [6, 7].

The patients present with spiking fever, rash, arthralgia, sore throat, and hepatosplenomegaly. Fever usually exceeds 39.0 °C which may be the only presentation in some patients and may undergo evaluations for fever of unknown origin [6]. Rashes are salmon-pink, evanescent maculopapular that affects trunk and extremities [13]. The rashes can be pruritic papules and plaques [14]. Arthralgia and arthritis of knees, wrists, ankles and elbows have also been reported which flares up with spikes of fever [6]. Our patient presented with continuous fever associated with chills and rigor, dry cough, sore throat, nausea, pink colored maculopapular rashes on her chest. Joint pain was marked involving bilateral knees, wrists, proximal interphalangeal and metacarpophalangeal joints. Cervical and axillary lymphadenopathy was also present.

Laboratory investigations in AOSD shows leukocytosis (neutrophilia), anemia, elevated ferritin, C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and abnormal liver function tests (AST and ALT) [7, 8]. Serum ferritin considered to be diagnostic tool for ASOD while some consider it insignificant [7]. Ferritin levels increases mostly without liver damage [6, 9]. Imaging studies shows hepatosplenomegaly [7, 10], progressive erosion and narrowing of joint space [7, 11]. Our patient presented with markedly raised total leucocyte count with neutrophilic predominance, anemia, slightly elevated AST, elevated serum ferritin and CRP on laboratory investigations. Computed tomography (CT) scan of neck, chest and abdomen showed homogeneously enhancing cervical, axillary and mediastinal lymphadenopathy and fibrotic changes in right upper lobe and left lower lobe with mild hepatomegaly.

Since AOSD is the disease of exclusion, other common diseases like infections, malignancies, rheumatic diseases should be ruled out [4, 6]. To rule out urinary tract infection (UTI), infective endocarditis, TB and dengue, we sent the following investigation: routine urine, urine culture, blood culture, sputum profile, chest X-ray, dengue panel, tropical panel and serology; all of which were normal. To rule out malignant conditions, ultrasonography (USG) (abdomen + pelvis) and CT neck, chest and abdomen were done which revealed cervical, axillary and mediastinal lymphadenopathy and mild hepatomegaly with fibrosis of right upper lobe and left lower lobe. Further, to rule out autoimmune causes like rheumatoid factor (RA), antinuclear antibodies (ANA) levels, ASO titer and ESR were sent, all of which came normal. After that, serum ferritin and CRP were sent which helped to diagnose the disease based on Yamaguchi criteria.

The disease is diagnosed based on Yamaguchi criteria (1992) which is widely used with a sensitivity of 93.5% [15]. There are 4 major criteria, 4 minor criteria with 3 exclusion criteria:

Our patient met 4 major criteria and 4 minor criteria after ruling out exclusion criteria.

Treatment of AOSD is based on anti-inflammatory medications and anti-rheumatic agents. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, methotrexate, disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), cyclosporine are used. Anti-interleukin 1, anti-interleukin 6, anti-IL1β, TNF-α inhibitors are the newer drugs for treatment of refractory AOSD [7]. Our patient was started on IV methylprednisolone following which her symptoms improved rapidly. She became afebrile, joint pain and laboratory parameters improved. She was discharged on oral prednisolone. She is under regular follow-up with no abnormal findings.

Conclusion

AOSD remains a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific clinical presentation and the need to exclude infectious, malignant, and autoimmune causes. This case emphasizes the importance of considering AOSD in prolonged fever of unknown origin, especially when supported by elevated inflammatory markers such as ferritin and CRP. Early recognition and prompt corticosteroid therapy can lead to rapid clinical improvement and favorable outcomes.
