The effects of a supervised exercise training program during pregnancy on placental cytokines, and the potential role of fetal sex and maternal weight status
Authors: Pedro Acosta-Manzano, Marta Flor-Alemany, Luis J Martínez-González, María Jesús Alvarez-Cubero, Laura Baena-García, Teresa Nestares, Mireille NM Van Poppel, Virginia A Aparicio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Evidence of how exercise affects placental phenotype remains almost non-existent.•Maternal concurrent exercise during pregnancy is related to increased concentrations of granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) and lower levels of interleukin (IL)-1 receptor antagonist, epidermal growth factor (EGF), and IL-6 in term placenta samples.•Some effects of exercise are sex-dependent, with female placentas showing higher levels of G-CSF and lower concentrations of tumor necrosis factor alpha and IL-6.•An optimal gestational weight gain appears to be necessary to increase G-CSF levels in response to exercise.•Exercise-induced changes in these cytokines may explain some of the previously reported beneficial adaptations in the placenta.Keywords: Lifestyle, Gestation, Placental adaptations, Sexual dimorphism, Protein expression

Introduction

The placenta is a versatile organ that plays a crucial role in sustaining pregnancy and supporting fetal growth.1 Its remarkable plasticity facilitates adaptations to maintain homeostasis at the fetoplacental interface and to protect the fetus from adverse intrauterine perturbations.1 However, an excessive metabolic load (e.g., immunometabolic derangements related to obesity or a sedentary lifestyle) can compromise placental homeostatic efficiency, leading to adverse fetal outcomes.1

Exercise during pregnancy is a powerful intervention that can promote beneficial placental adaptations and protect against adverse birth-related outcomes. For instance, exercise has been shown to enhance placental efficiency,2,3 uteroplacental perfusion,2,4 immunometabolic responses,5 and nutrient transport,6 all of which are involved in fetal growth.4 Unfortunately, the biological mechanisms underlying these beneficial effects of exercise on the human placenta are poorly understood.

The few studies available in pregnant women are limited to investigating the effects of maternal exercise on placental angiogenic and oxidative stress markers7 and the relationship of physical activity with the placental expression of genes related to nutrient transport and angiogenesis.8,9 However, nothing or very little is known6 about the influence of exercise on placental cytokines that play a pivotal role in placental development and function, such as colony stimulating factors, growth factors, chemokines, and interleukins. For instance, as shown in human and rodent studies, the granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF)10 and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)11 play an essential role in trophoblast invasion and differentiation during implantation and immune tolerance, and thus in successful placental development. Human platelet-derived growth factor-AA (PDGF-AA) and epidermal growth factor (EGF) also play central roles in embryogenesis, modulating the proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis of trophoblasts,12,13 as well as the amino acid transport system.14 Moreover, in the human placenta, monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1),15 fractalkine16 and interleukin (IL)-8 (CXCL8 (refers to C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8))17 regulate the recruitment of immune cells, invasion and proliferation of trophoblasts and decidual stromal cells, as well as angiogenesis. As for other interleukins, IL-6 and IL-1β contribute to blastocyst attachment and trophoblast invasion, immunometabolic interactions at the maternal-fetal interface, and to myometrial contractions during labor, as shown in human and rodent studies.6,17,18 Moreover, the IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra) and IL-10 modulate the immune environment and often attenuate placental inflammation in both humans and rodents.19,20 Furthermore, human placental tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)21 and interferon gamma (IFN-γ)22 participate in trophoblast invasiveness, turnover, and renewal as well as immune responses, endometrial vascular remodeling, and uterine activity for labor induction.

Hence, investigating the impact of maternal exercise on these placental cytokines is crucial for gaining a better understanding of exercise-induced placental molecular adaptations and how exercise may optimize pregnancy outcomes. Importantly, sexual dimorphism23 and maternal weight status1,24 should be considered in these investigations due to their potential influence on placental phenotype. Moreover, exercise-modifiable factors such as gestational weight-gain,24 lifestyle,8,9 and physical fitness,7,25 which could influence molecular adaptations, should be considered to ascertain whether the effects of exercise on placental cytokines are independent or partially driven by these factors.

Thus, the primary objective of this study is to determine the impact of a supervised concurrent exercise intervention, starting from gestational Week 17 until birth, on placental cytokines strongly involved in placental development and function. Secondary aims are to analyze the moderator role of fetal sex and maternal weight status, and to determine whether gestational weight gain, lifestyle behaviors (i.e., diet and sleep patterns, and physical activity), and physical fitness components (i.e., muscle strength and cardiorespiratory fitness) mediate the effects of this exercise program on these cytokines.

Methods

Study design and population

The GESTAtion and FITness (GESTAFIT) project commenced as a randomized controlled trial conducted at San Cecilio and Virgen de las Nieves University Hospitals as well as the Sport and Health University Research Institute in Granada, Spain. Detailed descriptions of the study procedures and inclusion-exclusion criteria (Supplementary Table 1) can be found elsewhere.26 In summary, pregnant women aged 20–40 who were free from any severe maternofetal disorder and who were capable of walking, reading, and writing unassisted in Spanish were eligible for recruitment. Of the 384 pregnant women informed about the project in their gynecological appointment at gestational Weeks 12, 159 were recruited over the course of three recruitment waves. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving individual explanations of the study methodology. The GESTAFIT project was granted approval by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Granada, Government of Andalusia, Spain (code: GESFIT-0448-N-15). Clinical trial registration: NCT02582567.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated specifically for gestational weight gain (n = 52),26 but not for the placental outcomes analyzed in this study, as analyses on these outcomes were exploratory in nature.

Randomization

Pregnant women recruited during the 1st and 2nd waves of recruitment were randomized into the exercise or control group using computer-generated simple randomization. However, some challenges were encountered with adherence to the control regimen (standard care). Several control participants resisted their assigned group after randomization, despite being offered a free postpartum exercise training program as compensation. As a result, approximately half of the participants were assigned to either the exercise or control group based on their preferences, especially during the 2nd wave of recruitment. This deviation from the randomization process meant that the random component was not fully maintained from the outset of the study. A 3rd wave of recruitment was conducted to increase the size of the control group. The only personnel not blinded to the allocation of participants were the experts responsible for conducting the training sessions.

General procedure

During the study, participants were assessed by experienced researchers at 3 time points: gestational Weeks 16–17, 33–34, and at birth, with each assessment being conducted in a single day. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, weight and height, sleep and dietary habits, and physical fitness were assessed at Weeks 16–17. Before leaving, women were given accelerometers to wear for 9 days. At Weeks 33–34, the same assessments were conducted. Immediately after birth, obstetrics and gynecological histories were collected. Women’s body composition was assessed at 6 weeks postpartum. The general assessment procedures are outlined in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Exercise intervention

The exercise intervention was based on a supervised concurrent training program from Week 17 until birth, with sessions held 3 days per week, each lasting 60 min. The program, which combined aerobic and resistance exercises of moderate-to-vigorous intensity (primarily moderate with peaks of vigorous intensity), has been previously detailed.26,27 It was designed by an expert multidisciplinary team, following the reference guidelines.28,29 To control and adapt exercise progression, key variables such as the rating of perceived exertion (RPE), the number of repetitions, the weight of dumbbells, and heart rate were considered. Heart rate during training sessions was monitored only during the first month and a half in one-third of the participants using Polar monitors (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland).

Exercise progression was managed by increasing both intensity (weight) and volume, taking into account the participant’s gestational week29 and training week.26 Regarding aerobic sessions, adjustments in intensity were made based on the predetermined RPE and target heart rate range (for participants with heart rate monitors).

During the intervention, 7 talks were provided to the participants in the exercise group with basic advice for a healthier pregnancy (Supplementary Material 1). The participants from the control group were instructed to continue with their usual activities and were also invited to these talks, both for ethical reasons and to ensure their adherence to the study.

Exposures and confounding factors

Sociodemographic-clinical data, obstetric history, and perinatal outcomes

Sociodemographic and clinical data, obstetric-reproductive information, maternal-offspring adverse events, and smoking and alcohol habits were obtained from medical files and questionnaires. Information regarding the type and duration of birth, epidural anesthesia, birthweight, and fetal sex was registered from perinatal obstetric records.

Body mass, height, gestational weight gain, and body composition

Pre-pregnancy weight was self-reported. Height and weight were measured (with no shoes and wearing light clothes) using a calibrated stadiometer (Seca 22; SECA GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany) and an electronic scale (InBody R20; Biospace, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Gestational weight gain was calculated as the weight difference from pre-pregnancy to Weeks 33–34. Postpartum fat percentage and lean mass were measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; Hologic Series Discovery QDR, Bedford, MA, USA).3

Dietary habits

The amount and frequency of consumption of various foods were assessed using a food frequency questionnaire. The estimated grams consumed for each food item were calculated by multiplying the typical portion size for Spaniards (as defined by guidelines, without weighing the food) by the frequency of consumption. These data were used to estimate nutritional values and total energy intake (kcal/day) using the Evalfinut 1.0 software (FINUT, Granada, Spain). The Mediterranean Diet Score index30 was developed to evaluate participants’ adherence to Mediterranean dietary patterns.

Sedentary time and physical activity

Triaxial accelerometers (ActiGraph GT3X+; Ametris, Pensacola, FL, USA) worn on the waist for 9 consecutive days (24 h/day; frequency 30 Hz, epoch length 60 s) were used to objectively measure sedentary time and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) levels. Only data with a minimum record of 7 days with >10 h/day of wear time were analyzed. Additional details are available elsewhere.31

Sleep duration and quality

Sleep duration was evaluated using triaxial accelerometers (ActiSleep, ActiGraphGT3X+) placed on the non-dominant wrist,32 along with the Cole–Kripke algorithm. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index Questionnaire (University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) was used to assess sleep quality.

Physical fitness

Detailed information regarding the assessment of physical fitness has been published before.25 Upper body muscle strength was measured using the handgrip strength test.26 This test involved squeezing the grip of a digital dynamometer (TKK5101 Grip-D; Takei, Tokyo, Japan) as strongly as possible, with the device adjusted to each woman’s hand size. The highest score from 2 trials for each hand was selected, and the mean score relative to body weight was used in the analyses.

Cardiorespiratory fitness was evaluated using the submaximal modified Bruce protocol.25 Participants were encouraged to reach 85% of the age-predicted maximum heart rate (85% MHR), which was used as a proxy for cardiorespiratory fitness, as demonstrated previously.25,33 Heart rate was monitored using a Polar V800 device (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland). A safety harness was worn by all participants to prevent falls.

Laboratory methods

Midwives from hospitals and physiologists responsible for processing placental samples were blinded to the treatment allocation of the participants.

Placenta collection

Placental term samples were collected immediately after vaginal birth or caesarean section by midwives. Within a maximum of 2 h, a trained researcher on call attended the hospital to process the placenta, which was kept in the fridge meanwhile. Only those placentas that were characterized by completeness and did not present abnormalities related to the shape, maternal or fetal placental surface (e.g., placenta infarcts), or fetal membranes were processed. The amniotic membrane and umbilical cord were extracted, and placentas were washed with saline solution (sodium chloride 0.9%, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). Placental weight was recorded using a precision gram scale (Ohaus Compass; Ohaus, Parsipanny, NJ, USA). Placental efficiency was calculated as the ratio of birthweight to placental weight. Subsequently, two 2 × 2 cm placental biopsies, consisting of the basal plate (including decidua basalis) and chorionic villi, were obtained in two of the 4 quadrants from the central part in relation to the cord insertion. All samples were collected in cryotubes, transported to the lab, and stored at −80 °C until analyses.

Samples processing

Placenta samples were defrosted, and small pieces totaling approximately 100 mg from one biopsy/placenta were homogenized with the T-10 basic ULTRA-TURRAX® (IKA, Staufen, Germany). The lysis was conducted on ice in 1 mL radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA, R0278; Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) together with the protease inhibitors phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, 1:200, P7626, Sigma-Aldrich) and protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC, 1:100, P8340, Sigma). The lysates were then kept at 4 °C in a cold room in a rotator. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged (13000g, 15 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was collected and frozen at −80 °C, except for 10 uL of supernatant, which was diluted with 10 uL of miliQ water and used to determine total protein concentrations with the Bradford assay. The Bradford assay was conducted following manufacturer’s instructions, using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay Dye Reagent Concentrate (5000006; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The standard curve was determined with duplicates, and each sample was tested in triplicate for total protein quantification. Fluorescence was determined using the Nanoquant Infinite M200 Pro multi-plate reader (Tecan, Zurich, Switzerland), with absorbance at 595 nm. The Beer-Lambert law was applied to calculate total protein levels in each sample, considering dilution factors. All samples were diluted to achieve a mean protein concentration of 2 µg/mL.

Placental cytokines analyses

Placenta supernatants were defrosted, and RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each supernatant until equaling a volume of 100 µL. A few of these samples were then used in a pilot immunoassay to explore whether cytokines were within the standard range, or if dilutions were required. Consequently, 400 µL of RIPA buffer were further added to each sample (1:5).

Cytokines concentrations (G-CSF, GM-CSF, PDGF-AA, EGF, MCP1, fractalkine, IL-8, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-1ra, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) were determined with a customized 19-plex Human Cytokine Magnetic Bead Panel immunoassay (SPR1630; MilliporeSigma, Darmstadt, Germany), using Luminex xMAP technology. Manufacturer’s instructios were followed. The 96-well plate was read on the LuminexTm 200 analyzer (Luminex Corporation, Austin, Texas, USA) and data acquisition was performed with the xPONENT 3.1 software (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA).

Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values for each standard and sample were quantified. Standard curves (one blank and 7 standards) were generated by plotting known cytokines concentrations vs. observed MFI using 5-parameter logistic curve-fit models. Mathematical equations derived from these curve-fitting models were used to predict cytokines concentrations based on samples’ net MFI values. Dilution factors were considered for the calculation of cytokines concentrations. Several non-detectable values (below the limit of blank) and out-of-range values (below the standard one) were identified. Non-detectable values and out-of-range values were estimated following recommendations from previous authors34,35 to avoid biased statistics and misleading regression models; excluding them is considered inadequate practice. Simple imputation (regression-based extrapolation) was used for those cytokines where <15% of cases were below the standard one. When 15%–50% of the cases were below the standard one, multiple imputation was used (data were missing at random) to replace values within this range. No method was used to estimate cytokines concentrations when censoring was >50%. The percentage of left-censored data has been summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Only pregnant women who attended >75% of the exercise sessions and had placental data available were considered for per-protocol analyses, which prioritized treatment efficacy and minimized confounding. Intention-to-treat analyses were dismissed due to the loss of the random component. Descriptive statistics were used to show women’s clinical characteristics, lifestyle factors, and levels of placental proteins (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2). The independent samples Student’s t test, Welch’s t test, Mann–Whitney U test, and χ2 test were used to detect differences between intervention groups. Relevant confounders, identified based on previous evidence and shown to influence the relationship between the independent and dependent variables (a change in B ≈ 15%), were included in the regression models: Mediterranean diet score at Week 34, gestational age at birth, and percentage of MVPA at baseline ((MVPA/accelerometer wearing time) × 100).

All assumptions related to the generalization of the results were met (e.g., normal distribution of errors). The analyses were conducted using the SPSS software (Version 22.0; IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA), and moderation and mediation analyses were performed with the PROCESS macro (v3.5, University of Calgary, Calgary, Canada). Significance was set at p < 0.05. Post hoc power analyses were conducted using the G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.4; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). The Benjamini–Hochberg step-up procedure was used in the main analyses to correct for false discovery rate.

Results

A total of 76 Caucasian southern Spanish pregnant women (age 33 ± 4 years, mean ± SD; pre-pregnancy BMI = 23.7 ± 3.9 kg/m2) without diagnosed cardiometabolic diseases and with placental data available were included in the analyses. Women were divided into control (n = 36) and exercise (n = 40) groups. Additional information on dropouts is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. Women’s clinical characteristics, lifestyle factors, and placental cytokines levels are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2. Women in the exercise group had an average attendance of 86% to exercise sessions and were characterized by higher energy intake than women in the control group at baseline (p < 0.05). Overall, no clinically significant infections were reported in the medical records. The correlations between placental proteins are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Effects of exercise on placental cytokines

The effects of exercise on placental cytokines are shown in Table 2. After adjusting analyses for gestational age at birth and baseline percentage of MVPA, the exercise group showed higher levels of G-CSF (B = 43.79, 95% confidence interval (95%CI): 14.05–73.53, R2adj = 0.09), and lower concentrations of EGF (B = −7.35, 95%CI: −13.73 to −0.96, R2adj = 0.14), IL-6 (non-significant trend, B = −0.43, 95%CI: −0.90 to 0.01, R2adj = 0.04), and IL-1ra (B = −0.49, 95%CI: −0.87 to −0.11, R2adj = 0.10) than the control group. No other significant effects or trends were observed with exercise (p > 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses showed that the results remained similar after additionally adjusting the models for the following variables one at a time: maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity status, type of birth, use of antibiotics for group B streptococcal infections, use of oxytocin, epidural anesthesia or another anesthetic at birth, duration of active and expulsive phase, season of the year, anxiety, tobacco use, sun exposure, sleep duration and quality at baseline, energy intake and relative percentage of sedentary time at baseline, and changes from Week 16 to Week 34 in energy intake, upper-body flexibility, and lower-body muscle strength. When controlling for false discovery rate, the associations shown between exercise and G-CSF, EGF, and IL-1ra levels remained statistically significant. Post hoc power analyses showed an ∼80% statistical power to detect associations with small-to-medium effect sizes (minimum detectable f2 = 0.1) in Table 3. The associations with GM-CSF, fractalkine, IL-6, and TNF-α showed lower statistical power (∼50%). When considering only non-censored data, the results remained similar except for the association between exercise and EGF, which showed the same trend but was not significant.

Moderation by fetal sex and maternal weight status

When analyses were conducted separately by fetal sex (male n = 40 vs. female n = 36; Supplementary Table 4), the effects of exercise on placental G-CSF (B = 75.43, standard error (SE) = 21.00, p < 0.001) and TNF-α (B = −12.32, SE = 5.94, p = 0.04) were significant only in female placentas. Similarly, the exercise group showed a trend toward lower levels of IL-6 compared to the control group only in female placentas (B = −0.60, SE = 0.34, p = 0.08).

When analyses were conducted separately by pre-pregnancy BMI (i.e., healthy weight n = 51 vs. overweight/obese n = 25; Supplementary Table 5), the effects of exercise on placental proteins remained generally similar. A significant effect of exercise on IL-6 levels was only observed in pregnant women with overweight/obesity (Model 2: B = −1.05, SE = 0.45, p = 0.02).

Mediating role of gestational weight gain, lifestyle, and physical fitness

The role of lifestyle and physical fitness changes (Weeks 16–34) as mediators of the exercise-induced effects on placental cytokines is addressed in Table 3. Mediation analyses showed that exercise exerted an indirect effect on placental G-CSF levels through changes in gestational weight gain (indirect effect B = 17.83, 95%CI: 2.09–40.37). Notably, lower gestational weight gain was correlated with a lower postpartum fat percentage (r = 0.3, p = 0.03). No other lifestyle or physical fitness component mediated the effects of exercise on G-CSF, EGF, or IL-1ra levels (p > 0.05).

Mediation analyses also provided insights into the role of these variables as confounders. For instance, they showed that the effects of exercise on G-CSF were independent of changes in dietary patterns, sleep duration, muscle strength, and cardiorespiratory fitness (c’-path, p < 0.05). Additionally, the effects of exercise on EGF and IL-1ra remained independent of changes in dietary patterns and muscle strength; the associations of EGF were also independent of gestational weight gain and MVPA (all, p < 0.05).

Discussion

Evidence regarding the impact of exercise on placental phenotype remains almost inexistent despite its relevance for maternal-fetal health. This is the first study to explore the influence of a supervised concurrent exercise intervention on cytokines that have a pivotal role in placental development and function.

Our main findings showed that exercise was related to increased concentrations of G-CSF and lower levels of EGF, IL-1ra, and IL-6 (trend) in term placenta samples. However, the most striking finding was that some exercise-induced effects on placental proteins, such as higher G-CSF, lower TNF-α levels, and a trend toward reduced IL-6 concentrations, were only observed in female placentas. In other words, fetal sex appeared to influence some of the intrauterine effects of exercise.

G-CSF and its downstream receptor (G-CSFR), both of which are expressed in placental trophoblast and decidual cells, play crucial roles in embryonic implantation, placental development, and immune modulation.10,36 For instance, mice models of recurrent spontaneous abortion, which are characterized by lower levels of G-CSF, have higher abortion rates. This is partially explained by an impaired proliferative capacity of trophoblasts due to suppressed G-CSF.10 Notably, the intraperitoneal administration of recombinant G-CSF to these mice mitigated abortion rates.10 Similarly, pregnant women with recurrent abortions also showed lower levels of G-CSF in the placental villi,10 and the treatment with recombinant human G-CSF was proven to decrease abortion rates, postulating a key role for G-CSF in placental development.36 Based on our findings, it can be speculated that exercise upregulates placental levels of G-CSF, potentially enhancing placental development without the need for medications and their associated adverse effects. Unfortunately, there are no previous exercise studies available with which to compare our results. Of note, the results regarding G-CSF were only observed in female placentas, which is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that the female placenta exhibits a better adaptive response than the male placenta.37 For instance, the female placenta appears to undergo intrauterine adaptations in protein and gene expression that help attenuate chronic inflammation and mitigate associated histopathological lesions in response to the same maternal stimuli or adverse conditions.37

Importantly, we also found that the exercise-induced increase in placental G-CSF levels was paralleled by a lower gestational weight gain among women who exercised. Notably, this reduced weight gain was reflected in a lower postpartum fat percentage with no significant changes in lean mass. This aligns with our previous findings showing that exercise limits gestational weight gain and may contribute to improved placental function and development3,38; this is also supported by previous studies4,39,40 reporting observations such as increased villous density and enhanced oxygenation.

EGF is an endogenous placental growth factor that, together with its receptor (EGFR), plays a critical role in placental-fetal growth.41 For instance, physiological concentrations of placental EGF improve in vitro rates of blastocyst formation and trophoblasts proliferation,41 reduce trophoblasts apoptosis induced by reactive oxygen species,13 and enhance amino acid transport,14 as evidenced by studies in humans and rodents.13,14,41 However, aberrant levels of EGF (i.e., deficient or excessive EGFR signaling) are related to abnormal placental development and pregnancy outcomes (e.g., intrauterine growth restriction and lower neonate birthweight).13,41 In our study, we observed that exercise reduced placental levels of EGF independently of fetal sex and maternal weight status, suggesting a potential mechanism by which exercise may influence placental development.6 These findings should be interpreted cautiously since the trend was non-significant when left-censored data were excluded (likely due to lower power). Further research is necessary to clarify the role of EGF in exercise-modulated placental adaptations.

IL-1ra is a major anti-inflammatory cytokine that inhibits the effects of IL-1β and IL-1α by binding to their receptor (IL-1R).42 During pregnancy, IL-1ra is often associated with attenuated placental inflammation in both humans and rodents,19 although aberrantly high levels have been observed in women with pregnancy complications, such as preeclampsia.43 In response to exercise, circulating IL-1ra levels are usually increased, at least in non-pregnant populations.42 Hence, it is intriguing that in our study, exercise reduced placental IL-1ra levels. Of note, other researchers have discussed that in specific conditions such as preeclampsia, the human placenta secretes higher levels of IL-1β to maternal circulation, and this response is accompanied by an increase in IL-1ra levels as a protective mechanism to limit inflammation.43 In other words, placental IL-1ra levels could reflect IL-1β levels. Thus, a possible interpretation in our study may be that exercise induced a placental IL-1β downregulation along with a decrease in IL-1ra levels. In support of this speculation, we previously observed in this cohort that maternal exercise reduced maternal systemic IL-1β.5 Unfortunately, placental IL-1β could not be analyzed here due to the high percentage of censored data.

Moreover, we observed that exercise was related to lower TNF-α in female but not male placentas (maybe due to a lower adaptative capacity in male placentas37,44). Although TNF-α has pleiotropic effects on the human placenta (depending on the concentrations and receptor signaling),21 it has been usually considered a potent pro-inflammatory agent, especially in those conditions where its expression is exacerbated.18 Thus, if confirmed by future studies, exercise may be a useful tool to attenuate the exacerbated inflammatory response associated with TNF-α, at least in female placentas. This speculation is supported by our previous studies3,5 showing that exercise is associated with lower levels of TNF-α in maternal and venous cord serum, and by other authors.45

Similarly, we found that exercise was related to lower placental IL-6 in pregnant women carrying female fetuses (non-significant trend) or women with obesity. Human IL-6 is involved in multiple physiological processes such as embryo implantation, trophoblast invasion, and immunometabolic modulation.46 However, at supraphysiological levels, particularly in conditions such as maternal obesity47 or gestational diabetes,17 IL-6 may play a pathological role related to maternal-fetal complications.17,47 In this regard, exercise may be beneficial for decreasing circulating levels of IL-6. This has been demonstrated in non-pregnant women42; however, evidence is scarce in pregnant women. Our previous study in pregnant women showed that maternal exercise did not induce reductions in circulating maternal IL-6 levels, though it did do so in cord arterial serum.5 These findings tempt us to speculate that exercise might downregulate placental and fetal synthesis of IL-6, which may be involved in placental adaptions and function, as suggested by previous evidence and unpublished data from the DALI study (Vitamin D And Lifestyle Intervention for Gestational Diabetes Mellitus Prevention).9 Given the clinical relevance, especially for pregnant women with obesity and female fetuses, future studies should try to confirm these findings.

The other cytokines studied were not related to exercise or could not be analyzed due to the high number of censored cases (often observed in similar studies that require high sensitivity).

Importantly, conclusions about the effects of exercise on the functions of placental cytokines earlier in pregnancy cannot be drawn at this stage, as the exercise regimen started at Week 17 and placental samples were collected only at birth. Moreover, the interpretation of the results is limited to the pooled analysis of the basal plate and chronic villi (i.e., to the maternal-fetal interface). Therefore, conclusions about the influence of exercise on individual maternal or fetal tissues should be avoided until future studies specifically target these tissues.

Selection bias might be present as the random component could not be sustained due to difficulties in retaining participants within the control group. For instance, self-selection of participants into the exercise group may have biased the results toward individuals who were more health-conscious or had healthier maternal profiles. However, baseline characteristics were comparable between the intervention groups except for energy intake, which was considered to be a potential confounder and did not influence the results. Although we cannot fully exclude the influence of labor on placental cytokines, both intervention groups underwent similar types of birth, and we considered this variable in our analyses. Moreover, only women with complete placental data were included in the analyses, which might have affected the representativeness of the cohort. Nonetheless, the groups for analysis were distributed approximately evenly (controls = 47%, exercisers = 53%; female fetuses = 48%, male = 52%; healthy-weight = 67%, overweight/obese = 33%). Lastly, only single placental biopsies were analyzed, and cytokines could not be measured in duplicates due to funding limitations. Additionally, the associations of exercise with placental IL-6, TNF-α, and GM-CSF did not have enough statistical power to detect small effects sizes. Of note, the study also has relevant strengths. Our exercise intervention is a uniquely tailored program grounded in the most recent evidence and developed by an expert multidisciplinary team adhering to the latest pregnancy guidelines.28 All sessions were closely supervised, with regular monitoring of intensity, attendance, and other key parameters. Notably, this is the first comprehensive study to explore the role of supervised exercise on these placental cytokines and the mediating roles of weight gain, lifestyle behaviors, and physical fitness. Additionally, we considered sexual dimorphism and maternal BMI in the analyses, and we thoroughly accounted for relevant confounders such as dietary patterns and objectively measured sedentary time, MVPA, and sleep duration, among others.

Conclusion

Maternal exercise during pregnancy is related to increased placental levels of G-CSF and lower levels of IL-1ra and EGF. Remarkably, some effects of exercise are sex-dependent and are primarily observed in female placentas, including increased G-CSF and lower TNF-α and IL-6 concentrations. Moreover, the increased levels of placental G-CSF observed with exercise are related to a more adequate gestational weight gain. Altogether, it seems plausible that exercise-induced changes in placental cytokines may explain some of the previously documented beneficial adaptations in the placenta. Future studies are warranted to better understand the molecular mechanisms linking exercise to placental development, identify novel therapeutic targets, and develop more effective interventions for pregnant women.
