How is the mental health of teachers faring? A 10-year follow-up study of depressive symptoms and job dissatisfaction in the CONSTANCES cohort
Authors: Justine Wenta, Nathalie Billaudeau, Sofia Temam, Sofiane Kab, Marcel Goldberg, Cécile Vuillermoz, Marie-Noël Vercambre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTeachers’ mental health, an important asset for society, may be impacted by security or health crises alongside more structural changes. Our primary aim was to assess 2012–2022 trends in depressive symptoms among French teachers compared to similar employees. We further examined concomitant trends in job dissatisfaction.MethodsWithin the ongoing French national CONSTANCES cohort, depressive symptoms were regularly assessed (up to four times between 2012 and 2022) using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression scale (CES-D, score 0–60). We used mixed models adjusted for sociodemographic factors to estimate mean parameters (95% CI) of CES-D variations between 2012 and 2022 among teachers (n=15 022) compared with other intermediate or managerial/professional occupations (n=21 361). We similarly studied changes in job dissatisfaction (score 1–8) from 2018 (first occurrence in questionnaires) to 2022.ResultsIn 2012, teachers’ CES-D was slightly lower on average compared with that of non-teachers (−0.66 point (−1.19 to −0.12)), but tended to increase more rapidly over the decade, particularly in the years concomitant to the COVID-19 pandemic (+0.14 point/year (0.01 to 0.27)). By 2022, the gap had closed. Job dissatisfaction followed a somewhat different pattern: slightly lower among teachers in 2018, it increased at first more rapidly compared with non-teachers, but then stabilised, in parallel with the waning of the pandemic.ConclusionsOur study highlights unfavourable long-term trends in the mental well-being of French teachers. Although clinical changes at individual levels would be mostly imperceptible, our findings concern a large population of key professionals, suggesting their growing needs for mental health support in the long run.Keywords: Teachers, Mental Health, COVID-19, Violence, Longitudinal studies

Introduction

Teachers—more than 90 million individuals worldwide—represent a significant portion of the workforce and play a vital role in shaping societies.1,3 Teachers’ health is therefore an important asset for public health, from an individual point of view and also as a major factor of education quality, system effectiveness and, in turn, social welfare.

Considering these strategic implications, the scientific literature on teachers’ occupational health has developed since the 1970s, alongside the growing general interest in occupational health. Primarily rooted within the field of social sciences,4 it largely focused on issues such as stress, job satisfaction, burnout or subjective well-being, but somewhat less on depression or anxiety disorders. The few epidemiological studies available in the pre-COVID-19 pandemic period suggested, in fact, that teachers were overall in good health compared with other professions,5 6 in line with the more holistic approach considering teacher well-being.7 Regarding the mental dimension of teachers’ health, specifically, results were more mixed. Some studies reported that teachers had better or no worse mental health outcomes than other workers,6,8 but some studies argued that the high work-related demands and stresses specific to the teaching profession9 10 may place teachers at greater risks of developing mental troubles over time.11 12 So far, prevalence estimates of depression among teachers have been highly heterogeneous.13 In addition to differences in assessment tools, samples of teachers varied widely in terms of socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds, but also in observation timing. As depressive symptoms can fluctuate in intensity, in relation/interaction with personal, work-related but also societal/historical factors, it is important to monitor them in the long term.

In recent years, besides structural changes in the education sector such as teacher shortage, class size increase, new digital tools integration and shifts in curriculum requirements,3 14 teachers around the world have faced various work-related challenges that may have particularly weighed on their mental health. Insecurity has been on the rise in schools,15 with a few studies highlighting deleterious repercussions, but from the youth’s perspective mostly.16 17 In France specifically, the school security crisis, directly linked to terrorist attacks, intensified in 2015 while major attacks occurred between January and November of this year, leading to reinforcement of safety plans in schools and consequently, a certain isolation of education staff from the families. Also, from spring 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced worldwide unprecedented disruptions, such as transitioning to remote teaching and managing students’ pandemic-related challenges, which have been consistently linked to heightened stress and mental troubles among teachers.18,23

Mirroring the increased focus on healthcare workers and other key professions during the COVID-19 pandemic, teachers’ mental health research has grown significantly in recent years.24 However, available data remain limited in several key aspects. The predominance of cross-sectional designs—the few longitudinal studies available are all short-term25—prevents investigation into the long-term dynamic nature of depression. Also, findings relying on geographically localised samples (eg, teachers from a specific school, a particular district) hinder to generalise them at a higher scale. Moreover, studies that focus primarily on work-related factors among teachers may have introduced selection bias, such as underestimation through healthy worker effects.26 Finally, the frequent lack of a comparison group makes it challenging to determine whether observed trends in teacher mental outcomes are unique to the profession or reflect broader societal/collective trends.

Given the evolving and multi-faceted nature of teachers’ stressors and the importance of teachers’ mental health for society, we aimed to monitor its long-term evolution in the French national context to contribute to quantifying and prioritising needs for this profession. We primarily investigated how teachers’ depressive symptoms changed between 2012 and 2022, taking similar non-teaching employees’ evolution as a benchmark, to capture the specific impact associated with the teaching activity and context on mental health. To complete this overall approach of mental health through depressive symptoms, we further examined a critical component of mental well-being at work: changes in teachers’ job satisfaction.27 Although job satisfaction has been linked with overall mental health,28 including among teachers,29 more complex mechanisms have also been highlighted, particularly in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic.25

Materials and methods

CONSTANCES cohort

CONSTANCES30 is a large French general population-based epidemiological cohort involving nearly 220 000 adults who were randomly selected from the National Health Insurance Fund and gave informed consent to participate (doi.org/10.13143/inserm_constances). The inclusion phase began in 2012 and lasted several years, with most participants enrolled in the first 7 years, through an extensive medical examination and various questionnaires. Every year after inclusion (except in 2021 due to logistical problems related to the COVID-19 pandemic), participants received a self-reported follow-up questionnaire.

ESTER subproject and study sample

Within CONSTANCES, the present study is part of the ESTER project (French acronym for ‘Occupational health study in education and research’)31 which focuses on health outcomes and risk factors of education and research staff specifically. The ESTER sample is composed of all education or research professionals enrolled in CONSTANCES from 2012 to 2020 (n=19 184) and, for comparison purposes, a two times as large sample of workers from other sectors (n=38 504) selected at random among the remaining CONSTANCES participants in employment. The identification of education/research occupations was chiefly based on the national nomenclature of occupations and socio-professional categories developed in 2003 (PCS2003) by the National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (https://www.insee.fr/en/metadonnees/definition/c1493). Among the education/research professionals, the present study focused specifically on the 15 184 teachers. In ESTER, they were included regardless of their teaching level, thus working from preschool to higher education, either in the public or private sector. Indeed, the French education system is divided into ‘École Maternelle’ (preschool), ‘École Élémentaire’ (primary education), ‘Collège’ (lower secondary education), ‘Lycée’ (upper secondary education) and higher education (including universities and higher education schools/institutes). The system is mostly public and centrally managed by the Ministry of National Education.

To ensure a minimum of socioeconomic comparability of the comparison sample of workers from other sectors,32 we selected, according to the first level of aggregation of the PCS2003 nomenclature, the same socio-professional group as teachers, that is, intermediate occupations (group ‘4’: primary school teachers’ majority socio-professional group) and managers and professional employees (group ‘3’: secondary school and higher education teachers’ majority socio-professional group) and excluded n=16 946 employees from other socio-professional groups (eg, group ‘6’: manual workers) (figure 1).

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the repeated individual measures of the French version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression (CES-D) scale.33 This self-administered questionnaire of 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (score range: 0–60) assesses the frequency of depressive symptoms during the previous week. Higher scores reflect greater depressive symptomatology. It has shown good psychometric properties in French clinical and non-clinical adults, including high internal consistency in the CONSTANCES setting.34 To assess trends of depressive symptoms over time, we modelled absolute changes in the CES-D score over follow-up. Considering that the CES-D scale was part of the baseline, 2015, 2018 and 2022 follow-up questionnaires and given the sequential inclusion phase of the participants and their potential non-responses to one or the other follow-up questionnaires, the number of available CES-D per individual varied from one to four (online supplemental figure S1). We retained only those with at least one available CES-D assessment during follow-up (n=359 individuals excluded). In total, our analyses included 15 022 teachers and 21 361 other employees with similar socio-professional backgrounds, hereafter named ‘non-teachers’ (figure 1).

Job dissatisfaction

Job dissatisfaction was assessed using the question ‘How satisfied are you with your work?’ with responses ranging from ‘A—very satisfied’ to ‘H—not at all satisfied’. Responses were coded from 1 to 8 so that a higher score indicated a lower job satisfaction and could be interpreted in the same way as CES-D (the higher, the worse). As the job satisfaction item was part of the annual follow-up questionnaire from 2018, the number of available job dissatisfaction measures per individual varied from one to four.

Time and covariates

To model the evolution over time of repeated individual outcome measures with varying measurement time and considering that certain events in historical time would affect individual mental well-being (eg, pandemic lockdown collectively affecting a whole society, etc), we anchored assessments in calendar time rather than relative to the inclusion of each participant. The time origin was set to the first available outcome measurement in the whole sample, which was 5 January 2012 for CES-D and 6 January 2018 for job dissatisfaction, respectively. To improve the meaningfulness of mean rates of change, we chose the year as the time scale in dividing the exact number of days since the reference date divided by 365.

To isolate work-related effects when comparing teachers and non-teachers, we adjusted the models for several potential sociodemographic confounders that have been consistently associated with both teaching status and depressive symptoms or job dissatisfaction.32 We considered gender, birth generation (born before 1960; in the 60s or 70s/after 1980), education level (undergraduate degree or less/postgraduate degree), relationship status (in a relationship/single), and parental status (children/no children).

Statistical analysis

We first gauged the representativeness of our samples of workers in comparing their key demographic characteristics with corresponding statistics at the national level. Second, we described baseline characteristics of teachers compared with other intermediate or managerial/professional occupations involved in our study. Next, as a first illustrative approach, we plotted ‘trend graphs’ as the mean CES-D scores of these two occupational groups over the years 2012–2022 (alternatively: the mean job dissatisfaction scores from 2018 (when the corresponding question was first asked)). The period over which measurements were averaged (by 2-year periods for the CES-D and 1-year periods for the job dissatisfaction) depended on the number of measurements available each year in our sample.

Then, we formally assessed changes in CES-D score (alternatively: in job dissatisfaction) over follow-up using mixed models for repeated measures. Such generalised linear models allow for the inclusion of both fixed and random effects, accounting for the correlation structure of the outcome data, here, the repeated scores for the same individual. It enables the estimation of the mean score trajectory as a function of time and to detect any significant deviation from it, related to the covariables, in terms of initial score levels and/or rates of change. In this study of active workers, individuals were gradually censored as they retired during follow-up. To evaluate to what extent the trends differed between teachers and non-teachers as groups, we first evaluated a simple model accounting for time, occupation and their respective interaction. Then, we evaluated a multi-adjusted model further accounting for all covariates previously described, including their respective interactions with time. We evaluated various approaches to modelling time: linear, quadratic and piecewise linear. Based on the Bayesian information criterion, we selected two models among the most efficient: a basic linear model and a piecewise linear model. For the latter, we divided the study period into three phases to account for major national phenomena with potential impacts on mental health and the teaching profession. The first phenomenon was the series of terrorist attacks in 2015, which had a profound psychological impact35 and resulted in reinforced security protocols in schools. The second was the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, which began in France with the first national lockdown on 17 March 2020. This later period was also marked by the terrorist murder of the teacher Samuel Paty (16 October 2020), which further impacted the profession. Accordingly, our time periods were defined as: T1 (cohort initiation–31 December 2015), T2 (1 January 2016–16 March 2020) and T3 (17 March 2020 onwards). Since data collection on job dissatisfaction began in 2018, the a priori periods were adapted as follows: T2′ from early 2018 to 16 March 2020, and the same T3 as previously defined.

To assess the robustness of our results, we conducted sensitivity analyses, (1) in implementing a case–control matching on all sociodemographic covariates as an alternative approach to control for confounding, (2) in moving period limits a few months in either direction in the piecewise linear models or (3) by excluding individuals with only one CES-D measure over follow-up (those for which evolution was most uncertain). We also examined the main results in stratified samples according to gender.

All models were fitted by the maximum likelihood method using Stata V.18.0. Figures were designed using Excel V.2402.

Missing data

Statistical models implemented here allowed for missing outcome data during follow-up, optimising the use of available outcome information. Regarding the covariates, there was no missing data for gender and generation. For education, relationship status and parental status, the proportion of missing data represented less than 5%, all were assumed to be missing at random. In the adjusted models, we imputed missing data using a multiple imputation procedure (multiple imputation by chained equations) in 10 different datasets and presented combined estimations.

Results

Age and gender characteristics of the three samples of workers (ie, of teachers, of other intermediate occupations and of other managerial/professional occupations) included in the present study did not strongly differ from those at the national level (online supplemental table S1).

Regarding the specificities in mental health trend among teachers, although the exact values differed slightly between the basic and adjusted models and between linear versus piecewise approaches, the overall pattern remained the same: compared with non-teachers, the mean level of depressive symptoms was lower among teachers in the early 2010s, but underwent a comparatively faster increase over the decade. Specifically, the multivariable-adjusted piecewise linear model (table 2) suggested that at first, teachers’ CES-D score was on average significantly lower compared with non-teachers (−0.66 (−1.19 to −0.12)), with no difference in annual rate of change both during T1 and T2 but then significant difference during T3 (+0.14 point/year (0.01 to 0.27) for teachers vs non-teachers).

Regarding job dissatisfaction (table 3), the multivariable-adjusted piecewise linear model indicated that around 2018, teachers’ job dissatisfaction score was on average significantly lower compared with non-teachers (−0.44 (−0.50 to −0.37)), with a significant positive difference in rate of change of 0.17 point/year (0.13 to 0.21) during T2′ (meaning a marked relative increase of teachers’ job dissatisfaction over the period), but thereafter with a borderline-significant negative difference in rate of change (−0.02 point/year (−0.05 to 0.00)) during T3, suggesting at term a slight relative decrease of teachers’ job dissatisfaction.

Results did not substantially change in the sensitivity analyses involving case–control matching, minor changes in period limits in the piecewise linear models, or sample restrictions to limit outcome trajectory uncertainties. In the stratified models by gender, parameters of interest were not always significant, likely due to the loss of power inherent to stratification, but they remained close in absolute value to that of the global model. More specifically, the teachers’ negative trend appeared slightly more marked among men than among women, both in terms of depressive symptoms (online supplemental table S2) and job dissatisfaction (online supplemental table S3).

Discussion

In this longitudinal study of 36000+ employees with rather homogeneous socio-professional backgrounds (intermediate or managerial/professional occupations) participating in the CONSTANCES cohort, levels of depressive symptoms in the early 2010s were lower on average among teachers, but increased more rapidly between 2012 and 2022 as compared with non-teachers, particularly the last few years corresponding to the COVID-19 pandemic, so that the teachers’ depressive symptoms advantage had completely closed at the end of the decade.

In our large sample of employees, we observed that overall, parallel to ageing, depressive symptoms tended to increase. As our sample was likely selected regarding health status by both healthy cohort and healthy worker effects, the pejorative trend at the population level is certainly underestimated, highlighting how mental health has become an increasingly important issue and challenge in a post-pandemic and more and more unsecured world, including among workers.36 Teachers appear particularly concerned by this worrying trend. Consistent with previous studies on teacher well-being,6 7 we confirmed that in the first part of the 2010s, teachers showed slightly less depressive symptoms than non-teachers, but our longitudinal study enables us to go beyond outdated data, in formally showing a long-term negative dynamic.

Considering that we limited socio-professional heterogeneity of the comparison group and that we further adjusted for sociodemographic factors, our study points to factors specific to the teaching activity and/or the teaching context as underlying causes for the unfavourable mental health trend among teachers. Potential factors that have been highlighted in the French context include rising violence/insecurity in schools, the eroding of social recognition/support of the teaching profession37 and the rising feeling of lack of institutional support,38 all of which warrant further examination.

More specifically, piecewise model selections indicated that depressive symptoms increased at an especially faster rate among teachers as compared with non-teachers in the last few years of follow-up, namely from 2020. In addition to structural factors specific to the education context, two events a few months apart, but related to two entirely different crises—health and security crises, respectively—may have been particularly impacting. First, from March 2020, the unique and specific challenges related to the COVID-19 pandemic18 23; second, on 16 October 2020, the murder of the teacher Samuel Paty by a terrorist, raising lasting concerns about freedom of expression, school safety and personal risks associated with teaching. More globally, the education world has remained under long-term security pressure in France. Although the tragic 2015 terrorist attacks did not occur in schools, they did prompt enforcement of strict security protocols there: from that time, schools have closed themselves off to ensure greater safety, with long-term psychosocial impact through a certain estrangement between education professionals and families. Now that the pandemic threat and related stress have receded, it will be interesting to continue to follow the dynamics of French teachers’ depressive symptoms to distinguish the respective effects of the health crisis, the still ongoing security crisis (tensions flared again 3 years later when Dominique Bernard, another teacher on duty, was murdered), and more structural/systemic factors. In addition, replicating the study in other socio-cultural contexts would be instructive as both security and health crises have an impact on education systems worldwide, but to different degrees.

Although statistically significant, the magnitude of the mean CESD-D change parameters estimated here is modest, indicating that clinical changes at individual levels would be mostly imperceptible. Yet, the pejorative trend concerns a large population of workers—around 1 million in France—and developed in the long run, with a worrying acceleration in recent years, as highlighted by the piecewise linear mixed model. Therefore, we believe that our results represent a warning that should not be ignored.

When further exploring how job satisfaction evolved during available follow-up, we observed a partially decoupled evolution between job satisfaction and depressive symptoms, suggesting a more complex and probably asynchronous mechanism linking both. Possibly, work-life balance should be considered in the equation, as it was clearly disrupted when the COVID-19 pandemic forced the entire country into a lockdown that lasted several months, and notably so among teachers.39 In that line, time spent on distance learning could be a critical factor to explain the divergence in trends between depression and job dissatisfaction.25 Further longitudinal studies among teachers that thoroughly investigate the network of associations between occupational and private factors as well as work-life balance regarding job satisfaction and depressive symptoms are warranted to contribute to disentangling underlying mechanisms and contribute to identifying the most effective levers to promote teacher well-being in turn.37

This study benefits from a large, population-based sample of employees, with detailed data collected prospectively over more than 10 years in the CONSTANCES epidemiological cohort.30 The use of mixed models for repeated measures, taking into consideration the complex structure of longitudinal outcome data related to individuals included progressively in the cohort, and adjusting for certain personal/family factors, enables us to formally evaluate differences in trends in key mental health indicators that could here be related more specifically to the teaching occupation/context. Several limitations should nonetheless be noted. First, while we observed significant associations between occupation and mental health trends or breaks in trends at a given major event, causality cannot be established due to the observational nature of the data. Second, in the piecewise mixed models, we fixed the limits of the periods a priori. The choices were guided by hypotheses, and we ensured afterwards that a minor change did not affect the broad outlines of the results; nonetheless, the period limits should be considered as indicative. Further, job dissatisfaction was assessed by a single question, a less robust approach than a measure based on multiple items. Finally, absolute average changes in depressive symptoms detected here were modest, indicating that there would be mostly no clinical implications at the individual levels. However, over the years and at the population level, a consistent differential increase in average burdens of depressive symptoms among teachers compared with other similar employees—especially during recent years—should warrant attention, as it suggests cumulative stressors impacting this key occupation, with potential disastrous long-term societal consequences.

In conclusion, our findings contribute to illustrating the rising mental health burden among teachers over the past years. The deterioration was only partly coupled with job dissatisfaction trends, highlighting the importance of monitoring complementary indicators of well-being in the teaching profession, especially during periods of crisis. Efforts to support teachers’ mental well-being should be prioritised, as they continue to face challenges in a fast-changing educational landscape. Understanding and addressing underlying risk factors through coordinated interventions at the workplace would be important to effectively mitigate the ongoing malaise in this essential workforce.
