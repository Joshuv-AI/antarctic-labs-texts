Immunotherapy impact of macrophage glycosylation on cholangiocarcinoma and its prognostic and immune microenvironment significance
Authors: Yufen Xu, Xiaofang Xu, Yan Xu, Jianwen Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCholangiocarcinoma (CCA) has a complex tumor microenvironment that critically influences tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Glycosylation abnormalities have been linked to cancer growth and progression. This work was designed to develop a prognostic model based on glycosylation-related genes (GRGs) for predicting CCA outcomes and immunotherapy responses. Glycosylation patterns in macrophage subsets of CCA were analyzed via scRNA-seq. Key genes were identified by integrating module genes from WGCNA and DEGs. A risk model for CCA was established utilizing LASSO Cox regression. In vitro tests were conducted to validate the function of PGK1. The immune checkpoint blockade group exhibited elevated M1 signature scores and higher glycosylation levels. A risk model incorporating five genes (ANXA3, PGK1, PLAUR, CREB5, SPP1) for CCA was established. The high macrophage glycosylation-related risk score group had a considerable infiltration of M0 macrophages. In vitro experiments confirmed that PGK1 advanced glycation end products accumulation, drove M2 polarization of macrophages, and increased CCA cell proliferation and migration. This work proposes a glycosylation-based risk model for predicting CCA prognosis and directing therapeutic strategies. PGK1 is highlighted as a potential therapeutic target in CCA.KEYWORDS: Cholangiocarcinoma, macrophage, glycosylation, immunotherapy, prognosis

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a malignant epithelial tumor originating from various parts of the biliary tree, with an increasing global incidence. CCA takes up roughly 3% of gastrointestinal malignancies and 15% of all primary liver cancers.1,2 Due to its asymptomatic presentation, high invasiveness, and frequent resistance to treatment, the mortality rate of CCA is concerning, accounting for around 2% of all cancer-related fatalities worldwide.2,3 In recent years, immunotherapy has demonstrated therapeutic promise in the treatment of advanced CCA. For example, the most recent clinical study reported that the sensitivity analysis results of the BILCAP phase III clinical trial showed that capecitabine can bring survival benefits to patients.4 Despite these advances, the overall clinical benefit for CCA patients remains quite low, with 5-y survival rates ranging from 7% to 20%.2,5,6 Related treatment methods remain imperfect. For example, combining epidermal growth factor receptor monoclonal antibodies (EGFR-mAbs) with gemcitabine-based chemotherapy regimens not only failed to significantly improve overall survival, progression-free survival, and objective response rate in patients with advanced CCA, but also increased the risk of related adverse events.7 Recent research has highlighted the crucial role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in modulating the response to targeted therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors, as well as drug resistance, in patients with biliary tract cancer.8,9 As a result, evaluating the TME may contribute to the development of novel and effective personalized treatments.

Within the TME, macrophages serve as crucial cellular components that perform diverse effector functions and adapt to the dynamic changes of the TME by adjusting their metabolic state.10,11 Glycosylation is a multi-step post-translational modification process, involving the addition and removal of carbohydrates to proteins and lipids, utilizing approximately 200 glycosyltransferases.12,13 The functional polarization of macrophages is closely linked to aberrant glycosylation modifications.14 For example, carbohydrate sulfotransferase 8 (CHST8)-mediated PD-L1 sulfation boosts the recruitment of CD206 M2-type macrophages, changing the tumor immune microenvironment toward a more immunosuppressive state.15 Furthermore, inhibition of C1GalT1 strongly enhances MGL-mediated interactions between macrophages and tumor cells, as well as macrophage phagocytosis of tumor cells and cytokine secretion.16 Consequently, illuminating the impact of glycosylation on macrophages within the TME is essential to unveil new biological mechanisms driving tumor progression and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Our study, based on an in-depth analysis of glycosylation characteristics in CCA macrophages using scRNA-seq technology, built a prognostic risk model for CCA patients utilizing bulk RNA sequencing data. Additionally, this research looked into the characteristics of immune infiltration within the CCA microenvironment. The functional roles of macrophage glycosylation-related genes (GRGs) in CCA were validated through in vitro experiments. In summary, this study can provide novel potential targets for predicting prognosis and improving treatment outcomes in CCA.

Materials and methods

Data download

Gene expression profiles and corresponding clinical data of 255 CCA samples were obtained from the NGDC database (https://www.biosino.org/node/project/detail/OEP001105) to serve as the training set for prognostic model construction. The CCA-related dataset from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/; tumor sample: 35; normal adjacent tissue sample: 9) was downloaded. The GSE107943 dataset, comprising 27 control samples and 30 tumor samples, was acquired from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Additionally, the single-cell CCA dataset GSE253627 was downloaded, including five mouse samples: two treated with gemcitabine/cisplatin chemotherapy (GC) and three treated with chemotherapy combined with immune checkpoint blockade (anti-PD1 plus anti-CTLA-4) (ICBs).

A systematic search using the keyword “Glycosylation” in the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/collections.jsp, accessed on 09 September 2025) yielded 674 GRGs. The intersection of these genes with those expressed in the NGDC training set resulted in 645 genes, which were defined as GRGs for subsequent research (Table S1).

WGCNA

In the NGDC dataset, the ssGSEA algorithm was implemented to calculate enrichment scores for the GRGs. WGCNA was employed to identify gene modules related to GRGs. By using the “WGCNA” R package,17 the optimal soft threshold power (β) for a scale-free co-expression network was set to 6. The adjacency matrix was then converted into a TOM to quantify gene-gene interconnections. Hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted using a distance metric defined as 1-TOM. Modules were identified with a minimum gene count threshold of 50 and merged at a height cutoff of 0.2. Four gene modules exhibiting the highest absolute correlation coefficients with GRGs and statistical significance (P < .05) were selected for downstream analysis.

Human genes within these modules were mapped to their mouse orthologs using information from http://www.informatics.jax.org/downloads/reports/HOM_MouseHumanSequence.rpt. A total of 6,393 unique genes were retained after deduplication.

Differential expression analysis between normal and tumor samples was performed (|log2FC|>1, FDR < 0.05) to obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs). These DEGs were similarly mapped to mouse orthologs and deduplicated, yielding 11,502 unique DEGs.

scRNA-seq data analysis

Quality control processing of the scRNA-seq data was conducted through the “seurat” R package.18 Cells that met the following criteria were retained: mitochondrial gene ratio ≤ 20%, 500 < nFeature RNA < median + 5 × MAD, and 1000 < nCount RNA < median + 5 × MAD. Data was then normalized, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were identified. Principal component analysis was conducted for dimensionality reduction by RunPCA, with the RunHarmony function applied to correct for batch effects. Finally, cell clustering was performed utilizing the FindNeighbors function with 40 dimensions of Harmony-corrected data to construct an SNN Graph and the FindClusters function (resolution = 1.0). The identified 26 cell clusters were visualized by t-SNE plots.

The FindAllMarkers function was applied to identify cluster-specific DEGs with parameters set as logfc.threshold = 0.585, min.pct = 0.3, and only.pos = T. Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes by the “singleR” package,19 and these annotations were subsequently manually validated against published literature.20

Macro/Mono cells subset analysis

The Macro/Mono cell subset was extracted from the complete single-cell dataset utilizing the subset function. The identical processing pipeline described in scRNA-seq data analysis was applied to this subset. Using FindNeighbors with 30 harmony dimensions to generate SNN Graph and FindClusters with a resolution of 0.3, 9 distinct cell clusters were identified. These clusters were annotated based on established marker genes, with detailed visualization through a comprehensive bubble plot displaying marker expression patterns.

Spatial distribution analysis of macrophage subsets

The Ro/e analysis, also known as STARTRAC-distribution,21 was implemented to examine the preferential distribution of macrophage subsets across different treatment groups. This approach computes the ratio between empirically observed cell numbers and theoretically expected numbers (derived through chi-square test). If Ro/e > 1, it indicates that within the given tissue, the cell count of a specific cluster is higher than the random expectation, demonstrating enrichment. Ro/e < 1 indicates depletion. This study employed Ro/e analysis to identify the proportional differences of macrophage subsets between two sample groups. The AddModuleScore function was applied to calculate the glycosylation scores of macrophage subsets, thereby comparing the differences in macrophage glycosylation levels between the two groups.

Prognostic model development

Based on scRNA-seq data, DEGs between the ICBs and the GC groups were identified in the Macro/Mono cell subsets with thresholds of |avg_log2FC| > 0.25 and p_val_adj < .05. The intersection of these DEGs with GRGs screened by WGCNA and with DEGs from the TCGA dataset was obtained to form the input gene set for model construction. The NGDC dataset served as the training set to build the prognostic model, with the random seed set to 518. Initially, univariate Cox regression analysis was utilized to screen genes strongly linked with patient overall survival (OS) (p < .05). LASSO regression analysis was then followed with the “glmnet” R package22 used to further refine the candidate genes, where the optimal lambda value was determined through ten-fold cross-validation based on the minimum mean error. Finally, multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted on the filtered genes to establish the final prognostic model. The risk score for each patient, termed the macrophage glycosylation-related risk score (MGRS), was generated based on the gene expression levels and the regression coefficients derived from the multivariable Cox model. With the median MGRS as the cutoff value, patients with CCA were sorted into two groups: the high-MGRS group and the low-MGRS group.

The Kaplan-Meier (K-M) method was employed to generate survival curves for both the high- and low-MGRS groups. The predictive performance of the model was assessed using time-dependent ROC curves implemented in the “timeROC” R package.23 An AUC value greater than 0.6 was defined as indicative of strong performance. The model’s accuracy in predicting outcomes was further validated in an independent dataset (GSE107943) through survival analysis and AUC evaluation.

Nomogram construction and validation

Through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, the independent prognostic value of the MGRS based on clinical characteristics (Age, TNM stage, Liver Fluke infection, Tumor Size, Gender, CEA, and CA19-9 levels) was assessed. A comprehensive prognostic nomogram integrating the MGRS and clinical factors was developed utilizing the “rms” R package.24 Calibration curves were generated to analyze the concordance between predicted and observed survival probabilities. The predictive potential of the nomogram was evaluated through time-dependent ROC analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA).

TME analysis and drug prediction

Immune cell infiltration patterns in high- and low-MGRS groups were calculated by the CIBERSORT algorithm implemented in the “IOBR” R package.25 Expression levels of common immune checkpoint genes were evaluated in different risk groups. Based on the GDSC2 database, the “oncoPredict” R package26 and Wilcoxon rank-sum test were utilized to predict their responses to different drugs.

Pseudotemporal and signature gene expression analyses of Macro/Mono cells

Pseudotemporal analysis of Macro/Mono cell subsets was continued by utilizing the “Monocle” R package.27 The dynamic expression patterns of signature genes along the pseudotime axis were analyzed. Furthermore, based on the classic M1 and M2 polarization gene sets, the AddModuleScore was applied to get the M1 and M2 signature scores of cells. The M1 and M2 scores from the GC and ICB groups were compared.

Cell culture

The human CCA cell line (QBC-939) and normal liver cell line (L02) were obtained from Yubo Biology (Shanghai, China). All cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 100 μg/mL penicillin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. ShRNA plasmids targeting PGK1 and corresponding negative control shRNA from GeneChem (Shanghai, China) were utilized for gene knockdown experiments.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA extraction from the cultured L02 and QBC-939 cells was carried out using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan). A Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was applied in synthesizing cDNA. The SYBR GreenTM Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was involved to implement qRT-PCR. The results were analyzed with the 2−ΔΔCt method (GAPDH: the endogenous control). Table 1 is a list of primer sequences.

Glycosylation level quantification

Quantitative analysis was performed on sh-PGK1 and sh-NC cells. The AGEs content in each group of samples was detected using the Human AGEs ELISA Kit (ml105237, Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Transwell co-culture of CCA cells with M0 macrophages

To evaluate the impact of sh-PGK1-mediated CCA cells on macrophage polarization, QBC-939 cells transfected with sh-NC or sh-PGK1 and THP-1 cells were seeded in the upper and lower chambers, respectively. Both chambers were filled with identical RPMI-1640 medium. After 48 hours of co-culture, the relative mRNA levels of CD206 (a marker for M2 macrophages) and INOS (an indicator for M1 macrophages) in the lower chamber cells were measured by qRT-PCR.

To investigate the influence of PGK1 on macrophage migration, THP-1 cells were placed in the upper chamber, while CCA cells transfected with sh-NC or sh-PGK1 were seeded in the lower chamber. Following 24 hours of co-culture, the upper chamber was rinsed twice with PBS, immobilized with 4% formaldehyde for 30 minutes, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 minutes. Finally, cell counting was conducted with a microscope and ImageJ software. Similarly, to examine the function of PGK1 in CCA cell migration, QBC-939 cells were placed in the upper chamber with 200 μL of serum-free RPMI-1640. And 500 μL of RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS was introduced into the lower chamber as a chemoattractant.

Cell proliferation and migration assays

Cell proliferation was evaluated by the CCK-8 assay kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Cells were put in 96-well plates (2 × 103 cells/well). When cell confluence reached 40%, the cells were transfected with sh-PGK1. At indicated time points (0, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h), 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was directly added to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1.5 hours. The OD was determined at 450 nm via a microplate reader.

A wound healing assay was implemented to assess the migratory capacity of CCA cells. After transfection, QBC-939 cells were seeded into 6-well plates. Once the cells grew to 90% confluence to form a monolayer, a scratch was created on the cell surface with a 200 μL pipette tip. After removing detached cells and debris with PBS, the cells were cultivated in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium at 37°C for 24 hours. The lateral distance of migrated cells was captured by microscopy and quantified with ImageJ software. The results were presented as the cell migration rate: (initial scratch width – final scratch width)/initial scratch width × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were implemented with GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2, and presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were carried out through the Student’s t-test. Statistical tests were two-sided tests (significance threshold: P < .05).

In TCGA cohort, differential expression analysis between normal and tumor samples yielded 11,502 DEGs (Figure 1A). WGCNA analysis was performed on the NGDC dataset to screen for gene modules linked to GRGs. The soft threshold power β was 6, which can satisfy the scale-free topology criterion (R2 > 0.8) (Figure 1B). The modules initially identified by the dynamic tree cut method were then merged, yielding a total of 21 gene modules (Figure 1C). Notably, the MEpink (cor = −0.56), MEtan (cor = −0.42), MEbrown (cor = 0.45), and MEturquoise (cor = −0.4) modules exhibited the strongest associations with GRGs. The relations between module membership (MM) and the significance of GRGs (GS) are shown in Figure 1D. These four modules collectively contained 6,393 genes.

Immune microenvironment characteristics and drug prediction

Analysis of immune cell infiltration between the different MGRS groups revealed that the high-MGRS group had a substantially higher infiltration proportion of M0 macrophages compared to the low-MGRS group. In contrast, activated mast cells, resting dendritic cells, resting memory CD4+ T cells, and naive B cells were more abundant in the low-MGRS group (p < .05, Figure 8A). We also probed into the expression differences of common immune checkpoint genes between the two MGRS groups and uncovered that CD40LG was upregulated in the low-MGRS group (p < .05, Figure 8B). Analysis of potential drugs for the high- and low-MGRS groups indicated that patients in the low-MGRS group might be more sensitive to drugs such as SB505124, Rapamycin, Tozasertib, and Dactolisib, whereas patients in the high-MGRS group showed greater sensitivity to SCH772984, Trametinib, AZD5582, and Selumetinib (p < .0001, Figure 8C).

Pseudotime analysis of Macro/Mono cells and expression of GRGs

Pseudotime analysis was applied to Macro/Mono cells to construct a differentiation trajectory. The analysis showed that State1 appeared early in pseudotime, while State8 appeared at a later timepoint (Figure 9A). Analysis of the Macro/Mono differentiation trajectory between the GC and ICBs groups revealed that cells from the ICBs group were primarily located in the early stage of the trajectory, whereas cells from the GC group were more prevalent in the mid-stage (Figure 9B). Specifically, Spp1+Mac was more enriched in the early differentiation stage, while Thbs1+Mac was predominantly in the mid-differentiation stage (Figure 9C). Furthermore, along the pseudotime trajectory, the expression of PGK1 and SPP1 gradually decreased; conversely, CREB5 and PLAUR expression gradually increased; and ANXA3 exhibited a pattern of initial decrease followed by increase (Figure 9D). These suggested that the expression of these signature genes is linked with the macrophage differentiation process. Subsequently, in the TCGA dataset, the expression of ANXA3, PGK1, PLAUR, CREB5, and SPP1 was substantially elevated in the tumor group compared with the normal group (p < .0001, Figure 9E). In the NGDC cohort, high levels of ANXA3, PGK1, and PLAUR were associated with poorer OS, while CREB5 and SPP1 were not (p < .05, Figure 9F). Overall, these results indicated that ANXA3, PGK1, and PLAUR were linked with poor prognosis in CCA. Notably, among these five genes, Pgk1 exhibited the highest expression level in Macro/Mono cells (Figure 9G,H), with its expression notably negatively correlated with the immune checkpoint PD-L1 (p < .0001, Figure 9I).

PGK1 influences glycosylation levels, CCA cell proliferation, and migration, and macrophage polarization

Regarding the above findings, the functional role of PGK1 in CCA progression were further investigated. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that PGK1 expression was greatly upregulated in CCA cell lines, and successful knockdown of PGK1 was achieved (p < .001, Figure 10A,B). ELISA detection revealed that PGK1 knockdown suppressed the expression level of AGEs in CCA cells (p < .01, Figure 10C). The CCK-8 assay confirmed that inhibiting PGK1 markedly suppressed the proliferation of QBC-939 cells (p < .05, Figure 10D). Transwell and scratch assays demonstrated that PGK1 knockout considerably inhibited the migration ability of QBC-939 cells (p < .01, Figure 10E,F). Next, human CCA cells (QBC-939) transfected with PGK1 shRNA were co-cultivated with THP-1 cells for 24 hours. PGK1 knockdown upregulated the level of the INOS and downregulated the level of the M2 macrophage marker (CD206) (p < .001, Figure 10G). The knockdown of PGK1 also strongly inhibited the migration ability of macrophages (p < .001, Figure 10H).

Discussion

CCA’s refractory character is primarily due to its unique TME.30,31 Abnormal glycosylation, as an important characteristic of cancer, can further promote tumor progression, metastasis, and immune escape by regulating the TME, thereby affecting treatment.32–36 This study, using scRNA-seq technology, found that macrophage glycosylation levels were significantly increased in CCA tumor tissue than in normal tissue. Based on scRNA-seq data, we identified several key macrophage subsets and focused on analyzing macrophage glycosylation patterns, speculating that high glycosylation levels may promote macrophage polarization toward the M1 type, thereby altering the TME of CCA. By further combining scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data, this study identified five key GRGs: ANXA3, PGK1, PLAUR, CREB5, and SPP1, and constructed a risk model for CCA.

The five main DEGs identified in this study each play an important role in tumorigenesis and development. ANXA3 not only functions as a structural protein but also promotes tumor progression, invasion, and treatment resistance by regulating multiple signaling pathways, and its molecular mechanism has become an important target for anti-cancer research.37 In colorectal cancer, ANXA3 expression is significantly upregulated and synergistically responds to hypoxia with HIF-1α to promote tumor proliferation.38 Furthermore, ANXA3 is enriched in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and can induce their polarization toward the immunosuppressive M2 type; ANXA3+ exosomes derived from TAMs can inhibit tumor cell ferroptosis and promote lymph node metastasis, suggesting that it also plays a crucial role in the regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment.39 This study further found that ANXA3 expression was significantly increased in the CCA immunotherapy response group, and single-cell analysis showed that its glycosylation characteristics were related to the M1 polarization state of macrophages, which echoes and complements existing research.

PLAUR-encoded proteins participate in extracellular matrix remodeling and mediate downstream signal transduction to promote tumor migration and invasion through interactions with integrins, EGFR, and other receptors.40 A study that combined spatial transcriptomics and machine learning showed that PLAUR was connected with immune invasion and prognosis in CCA and can serve as an important component of predictive indicators for immunotherapy response,41 which is consistent with our findings.CREB5 belongs to the CREB/ATF transcription factor family and promotes tumor invasion and metastasis in colorectal cancer by activating MET expression. However, current research on the involvement of CREB5 in glycosylation or CCA remains limited, suggesting it may be a direction worthy of further exploration.SPP1-encoded osteopontin is a highly glycosylated acidic protein whose glycosylation modification facilitates binding to CD44/integrin and regulates downstream signaling pathways, thereby playing multiple functions in the TME.42 In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), high expression of SPP1 can serve as a marker to distinguish different tumor subtypes and immune microenvironment status. The high expression group has different clinical and molecular characteristics, suggesting that it plays an important role in the progression of CCA.43

From a scientific standpoint, macrophage glycosylation is an important but underappreciated immune regulation mechanism in CCA, playing a key role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment and influencing immunotherapy responses. This study focuses on PGK1, a key enzyme in glycolysis, whose high expression has been shown to be significantly associated with poor prognosis in CCA patients.44 PGK1 was chosen as the research subject not only because it exhibits a significant prognostic weight in risk models, but also because it has been reported to potentially participate in both cellular metabolic regulation and macrophage functional reprogramming.45,46 Herein, CCK-8, scratch assays, and Transwell assays demonstrated that PGK1 knockdown effectively inhibited the proliferation and migration of CCA cells. Notably, PGK1 is highly expressed in the macrophage/monocyte (Macro/Mono) subset. Knockdown of PGK1 reduced the expression of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), thereby inhibiting tumor progression. Multiple studies have shown that aberrant PGK1 expression can affect macrophage function, encouraging M2 polarization and enhancing migration ability, which may be an important mechanism for shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment.45,46 The key genes screened in this study are widely involved in tumor progression, with several being demonstrated to be directly associated to the occurrence, development, immunological modulation, and metabolic remodeling of CCA. The association of these genes with glycosylation modification further highlights their potential role in shaping the CCA microenvironment and validates the biological significance of the model screening results. In summary, the multiple pieces of evidence regarding PGK1 in prognosis, cellular function, and immune regulation provide strong support for its potential therapeutic target in CCA.

The TME, related to tumorigenesis, generates signals relevant to tumor prognosis and response to immunotherapy.47,48 ICIs are a class of monoclonal antibodies (mAb) that stimulate the immune system by inhibiting the delivery of co-inhibitory signals.49 The primary targets of ICIs cover PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA4. PD-1 is a receptor primarily expressed on the surface of activated T cells. The binding of PD-1 to its ligand PD-L1 induces T cell anergy, a key mechanism underlying immune evasion when tumor cells overexpress PD-L1.50,51 CTLA4 is a negative immune regulator that constrains T cell activation through competitive binding with CD28 and various suppressive functions, including the modulation of Treg cells.52,53 Patients with higher levels of PD-1+CD8+ T cells and PD-1+FoxP3+ cells in the TME demonstrated better responses to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.54 Similarly, Dutta et al.55 indicated that high PD-1+CD8+ T cell levels were markedly correlated with a positive response to PD-1 blockade therapy. Therefore, CD8+ T cell infiltration and ICI-related gene expression are reliable indicators for assessing the response to ICI treatment. This study observed that patients in the low-risk group were enriched for the infiltration of most immune cell types, while the high-MGRS group exhibited infiltration of M0 macrophages. Furthermore, a negative linkage between PGK1 expression and the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 was discovered. Consequently, the expression of PGK1 may serve as an important biomarker for CCA prognosis and a potential indicator for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy. In summary, high expression of PGK1 in CCA could be a key factor contributing to poorer prognosis.

In conclusion, this work developed a robust risk model with five GRGs and identified distinct immune infiltration patterns and chemotherapeutic drug sensitivities between the two MGRS groups. PGK1 was validated as a potential therapeutic target for CCA through in vitro experiments, serving as a critical reference for developing targeted therapeutic strategies to refine the prognosis and treatment for CCA patients. First, this study used a mouse immunotherapy model for single-cell transcriptome analysis, while the risk scoring model and in vitro experiments relied on human cell lines. This cross-species research design may introduce some biological differences, potentially limiting the translational applicability of some findings. Although this study attempted to validate the results through various methods, further validation in more human immunotherapy cohorts is needed to ensure better clinical application. Second, the glycosylation risk scoring model constructed in this study was only validated on a limited number of public datasets with a relatively small sample size. Therefore, the model’s stability and broad applicability remain to be verified. To validate the model’s generality and accuracy, future studies should validate it on more datasets, especially in different tumor types and under different immunotherapy contexts. Furthermore, this study primarily focused on the impact of PGK1 on macrophage function and tumor prognosis, while the exploration of other GRGs was relatively limited. The complexity of these key genes and their differences from human physiology may still affect the translatability of experimental results. For example, the conditions of in vitro cell experiments may differ significantly from the clinical TME. Future research can further validate these findings through more types of in vivo trials (such as immunodeficient mice, orthotopic transplantation models, etc.) and, in combination with human tumor samples, provide evidence that is closer to clinical application.
