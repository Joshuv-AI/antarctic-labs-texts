Outcomes of Bisphosphonate‐Related Osteonecrosis Following Head and Neck Free‐Flap Reconstruction
Authors: Taylor E. Freeman, Ericka L. Erickson, Nolan B. Seim, Matthew O. Old, Lauren E. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBisphosphonate‐related osteonecrosis of the jaw (BRONJ) can be refractory to conservative management. Definitive segmental resection with microvascular free‐flap reconstruction is increasingly used, yet outcomes remain underreported. We conducted a single‐institution retrospective review (2014‐2022) of patients with BRONJ undergoing free‐flap reconstruction. Exposures included inciting medication and smoking history. Primary outcomes were recurrence and orocutaneous fistula. Ten patients met criteria; zoledronic acid was the most common exposure (60%). Reconstructions included fibular (n = 8) and scapular (n = 2) free flaps. Eight patients (80%) did not have recurrence of osteonecrosis after a single operation over the span of follow‐up. Two had recurrent osteonecrosis, one of which developed a postoperative fistula requiring reoperation. In our cohort of patients with advanced BRONJ, segmental resection with immediate free‐flap reconstruction achieved high single‐stage cure with low complication rates.

Bisphosphonate‐related osteonecrosis of the jaw (BRONJ) is a serious complication occurring most frequently with high‐dose intravenous bisphosphonates. 1 , 2 Current guidelines recommend escalating from conservative management to surgical resection for advanced disease. 1 , 2 , 3 Definitive resection with immediate microvascular reconstruction can restore mandibular continuity and oral function in a single procedure, yet outcomes for free‐flap reconstruction in BRONJ remain poorly characterized. 4 , 5 This paper sought to evaluate trends and outcomes of BRONJ following free‐flap reconstruction.

Methods

We performed a retrospective review at a single tertiary cancer center of patients with BRONJ who underwent segmental resection with microvascular free‐flap reconstruction from 2014 to 2022. This study was approved by the Ohio State University Institutional Review Board. Variables of interest included inciting medication exposure, conservative management utilized, and smoking history. Primary outcomes were (1) recurrence of osteonecrosis, (2) occurrence of orocutaneous fistula, and (3) need for additional surgeries. Patients with prior head and neck cancer treatment were excluded. Given a small sample size, we performed descriptive statistics of demographic information (mean, standard deviation [SD]) to understand the development of osteonecrosis or orocutaneous fistula over time.

Results

Ten patients met the inclusion criteria. Zoledronic acid was the most common inciting exposure (6, 60%), followed by alendronate (30%). The average length of time patients were on bisphosphonates prior to diagnosis of BRONJ was 3.21 years (±1.41 years). Seven patients had a history of osteoporosis/osteopenia requiring treatment, while three patients were taking bisphosphonates secondary to bone metastases and associated hypercalcemia from malignancy. Four patients had a current (n = 2) or former (n = 2) smoking history.

All patients had undergone conservative management (debridement, antibiotics, Vitamin E, pentoxifylline, and hyperbaric oxygen) prior to free‐flap reconstruction. In total, 90% of patients underwent prior bony debridement, and 100% of patients trialed antibiotics prior to definitive resection and reconstruction. Multiple patients used adjuvant medical therapies including vitamin E (n = 2, 20%), pentoxifylline (n = 2, 20%), and hyperbaric oxygen (n = 2, 20%). Start dates for these medical therapies ranged from 1 to 6 months prior to surgery. Repeated debridements and antibiotics were trialed for 1 or more years prior to definitive surgical intervention. Length of conservative management varied based on the severity of symptoms and exam findings that had the potential to expedite time to surgery. The most frequently used reconstructive method was the fibular free flap (n = 8), followed by the scapular tip free flap (n = 2). One patient had a scapular free flap in the setting of significant Andy gump deformity secondary to previous resections and persistent osteonecrosis. A scapular free flap was chosen for the second patient due to a large anterior mandible defect. Surgical procedure was uncomplicated in all cases. Mean surgical time was 7.99 hours (SD 1.61 hours). Similarly, there were no major postoperative complications with a mean hospital length of stay of 8.25 days (SD 3.61 days). The average mean follow‐up time was 1.40 years (SD 1.05 years). Eight patients (80%) achieved a cure after a single free‐flap operation, while two experienced recurrent osteonecrosis during follow‐up. One patient developed an orocutaneous fistula after 2.36 years, requiring operative management involving removal of hardware with adjacent tissue transfer. The second patient developed exposed hardware and bone after 0.66 years, requiring mandibular debridement and adjacent tissue transfer (Table 2). Of these two patients, one was a former smoker with a 35‐pack‐year smoking history.

Discussion

This single‐center experience demonstrated moderate success with definitive resection via immediate free‐flap reconstruction for advanced BRONJ. The single‐stage cure rate in 8 of 10 patients and low complication burden align with broader reconstructive experience. Fibular flaps predominated due to their ability to restore mandibular length, provide cortical stock for osseointegration, and accommodate dental rehabilitation 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ; scapular flaps addressed complex three‐dimensional defects or soft‐tissue dominant reconstructions, or in patients where the fibular bone is not an option. 9 While not demonstrated in this study, anterolateral thigh vascularized free flaps may provide an option for patients with earlier stage BRONJ, given its previous success in radiation‐related osteoradionecrosis. 10

Studies have shown that conservative intervention is particularly beneficial in early‐stage BRONJ. 11 However, in more advanced stages, conservative debridement may be less effective. Prompt transition from failed conservative therapy to definitive surgery may reduce repeated debridements and prolonged morbidity. Free‐flap reconstruction that restores mandibular continuity can enable mastication and speech, outcomes rarely achievable in patients with severe osteonecrosis and conservative management alone. 12 , 13

Additionally, free‐flap reconstruction for BRONJ offers the potential for implant‐based dental rehabilitation. Previous studies have demonstrated that dental rehabilitation following free‐flap reconstruction for BRONJ can typically be achieved in 51% to 69% of patients if completed in a multi‐stage fashion, 6 to 12 months after reconstruction. 14 , 15 This offers a promising option as patients with BRONJ frequently experience poor dental sequelae secondary to exposed and necrotic bone.

This study addresses a persistent gap in literature by examining how prior medical therapy, preoperative diagnosis, and surgical characteristics influence outcomes of free‐flap reconstruction for BRONJ. Limitations include a small sample size, a single‐center design, and the absence of a non‐surgical comparator group. Selection bias toward patients eligible for major reconstruction, variability in pre‐referral medical management, and incomplete long‐term follow‐up may limit generalizability.

Conclusions

In advanced BRONJ, segmental resection with immediate free‐flap reconstruction achieved high rates of single‐stage cure and acceptable morbidity in this series. Multicenter studies with standardized definitions and longer follow‐up are warranted to refine patient selection, quantify functional outcomes, and benchmark complication profiles across reconstructive strategies.
