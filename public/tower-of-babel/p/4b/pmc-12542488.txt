Clinicopathological analysis of three cases of breast desmoid-type fibromatosis
Authors: Qiong Li, Cancan Zou, Yanlin Chen, Muzi Li, Shuang Liang, Tianjing Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast desmoid-type fibromatosis is a rare, locally aggressive but non-metastatic mesenchymal tumor according to WHO classification, characterized by spindle cell proliferation with irregular infiltrative borders. Its morphological overlap with other spindle cell lesions necessitates careful differential diagnosis. It’s worth noting that multifocal breast desmoid-type fibromatosis has not been reported.Case presentationWe present three breast desmoid-type fibromatosis cases diagnosed at Women and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Oct 2022-Sep 2024) in females aged 15–42 years (mean ± standard deviation: 31.3 3 ± 11.73 years; median 37 years). All patients were asymptomatic. A 37-year-old female who was diagnosed with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) 5 years ago presented with multifocal lesions. One case co-occurred with low nuclear grade ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and fibroadenoma. Ultrasonography revealed circumscribed margins in two instances and ill-defined margins in one case. Surgical management included vacuum-assisted excision (two cases) and combined breast-conserving surgery for the carcinoma-associated case.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first report of multifocal breast desmoid-type fibromatosis and its co-occurrence with fibroadenoma and low nuclear grade DCIS. Through integrated clinicopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses supplemented by a systematic literature review, we delineate diagnostic criteria, therapeutic approaches, and prognostic indicators. These findings provide critical insights to reduce diagnostic errors and enhance precision in managing this challenging entity.

Background

Desmoid-type fibromatosis, a locally aggressive fibroblastic/myofibroblastic tumor [1], commonly affects the abdominal wall (30–40%), extra-abdominal sites (50%), and intra-abdominal and mesenteric sites (10–20%) [2]. Breast involvement is rare (2–5% of desmoid cases, < 0.2% of breast tumors) [3–5]. Breast desmoid-type fibromatosis usually appears as solitary masses, with histological features of spindle cell fascicles and infiltrative margins, and can mimic breast cancer on imaging [2, 6, 7].​

Notably, multifocal breast desmoid-type fibromatosis was unreported previously. This study presents, for the first time, three cases, including one with multifocal disease and fibroadenoma, and another with low-grade intraductal carcinoma and fibroadenoma. We summarize its pathogenesis, clinicopathological features, immunohistochemical and molecular profiles, differential diagnosis, and management to aid accurate diagnosis and treatment.​

Case presentation

Case no. 1

A 15-year-old girl was detected with a mass in the left breast’s inner upper quadrant during a school physical examination and was then admitted to the breast department of the Women and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Breast color Doppler ultrasound showed an irregular, 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.5 cm abnormal echo with blurred margins in the glandular layer at the 11 o’clock position of the left breast, classified as BI-RADS 4 A (2–10% malignancy risk) (Fig. 1a). The mass was non-tender, with no skin changes or nipple discharge. The patient had no history of trauma or family history of breast cancer. Vacuum-assisted excision was performed, and specimens were sent for histopathological examination.​

Grossly, the tumor had a grayish white, medium-textured cut surface. Microscopically, it consisted of spindle cells and collagen fibers in bundles or a staggered pattern, with no nuclear mitosis (Fig. 1b). The tumor infiltrated the surrounding mammary parenchyma in a finger-like pattern (Fig. 1c). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed diffuse nuclear positivity for β-catenin (Fig. 1d), diffuse positivity for vimentin, focal positivity for smooth muscle actin (SMA) and desmin, negativity for AE1/AE3, P63, CD34, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), androgen receptor (AR), and Ki67 positivity in about 5% of tumor cells. With proper internal/external controls, the final diagnosis was breast desmoid-type fibromatosis (Table 1).​

Case no. 2

A 37-year-old female patient was diagnosed with two masses in the left breast during a physical examination. Breast color Doppler ultrasonography revealed abnormal glandular echo areas at the 2 o’clock and 10 o’clock positions in the left breast, measuring 1.5 cm and 0.6 cm in maximum diameter, respectively. Both areas exhibited a regular shape, uneven internal echoes, and a slightly hypoechoic appearance. The BI-RADS classification was category 3 (2% malignancy risk). There was no tenderness upon palpation, and neither covering skin changes nor nipple discharge were observed. Axillary and supraclavicular lymph nodes were not palpable. Notably, 5 years prior to the current presentation, the patient had been diagnosed with FAP. Detailed clinical records on extramammary (e.g., colonic) tumors were unavailable as the patient was diagnosed and followed up at another institution.

Subsequently, vacuum-assisted excision of the breast masses was performed, and the specimens were sent for routine histopathological examination. Microscopically, the majority of the areas exhibited typical fibroadenoma structures (Fig. 2a). However, within the fibroadenoma, small foci of spindle cell proliferation were detected at the 2 o’clock and 10 o’clock positions. These foci measured 0.4 cm and 0.3 cm in diameter, respectively. These spindle cells, arranged in bundles or in a staggered pattern, presented a mild morphology. No nuclear mitosis was observed (Fig. 2b). The detailed IHC staining results were as follows: β-catenin manifested as diffuse nuclear positivity (Fig. 2c). Vimentin presented diffuse positivity. SMA exhibited focal positivity. AE1/AE3, P63, desmin, CD34, ER, PR and AR were all negative. Ki67 was positive in about 1% of tumor cells. All immunohistochemical assays included validated internal/external controls. Based on the clinical history, morphological features, and immunohistochemical findings, this case was ultimately diagnosed as desmoid-type fibromatosis (Table 1).

Case no. 3

A 42-year-old female patient incidentally discovered a small nodule in the upper inner quadrant of her right breast while taking a shower. On palpation, the nodule was immobile and painless. Subsequently, she visited the outpatient clinic of the breast department at the Women and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Breast color Doppler ultrasound examination revealed an abnormal echo area in the glandular layer at the 2 o’clock position of the right breast, measuring approximately 2.7 × 1.6 × 1.0 cm in size. The lesion exhibited a regular shape, intact margins, and heterogeneous internal echoes, presenting as low-to-moderate echoes. The BI-RADS classification is category 3 (2% malignancy risk). The patient had no significant clinical history. Subsequently, vacuum-assisted excision of the breast mass was performed. The postoperative pathological examination revealed a diagnosis of low nuclear grade DCIS (Fig. 3a), accompanied by fibroadenoma (Fig. 3b). One week after the initial diagnosis, the patient underwent a type of breast-conserving surgery for the treatment of right-sided breast cancer. Upon postoperative pathological examination, it was clearly revealed that there was no residual ductal carcinoma in situ. Nevertheless, an area of spindle cell hyperplasia (Fig. 3c), measuring 0.5 cm in diameter, was identified. Microscopic examination revealed that this area had an ill-defined boundary with the adjacent tissues. The IHC staining results of this spindle cell region were as follows: β-catenin manifested as diffuse nuclear positivity (Fig. 3d). Vimentin presented diffuse positivity. SMA exhibited focal weak positivity. AE1/AE3, P63, desmin, CD34, ER, PR and AR were all negative. Ki67 was positive in about 1% of tumor cells. All immunohistochemical assays included validated internal/external controls. Genetic testing was conducted at an external medical institution, and the results identified CTNNB1 c.121 A > G (p.Thr41Ala, T41A) missense mutation by NGS panel testing. Therefore, despite the small size (0.5 cm), the lesion’s infiltrative pattern, β-catenin nuclear positivity, and CTNNB1 T41A mutation collectively confirm desmoid-type fibromatosis rather than reactive change (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusions

Desmoid-type fibromatosis, also referred to as ligamentoid fibromatosis or invasive fibromatosis, is a locally infiltrative lesion. Initially described by Stout [8] in 1954, desmoid-type fibromatosis was classified as an intermediate neoplasm owing to its propensity for recurrence following extensive resection of the primary tumor [9]. However, desmoid-type fibromatosis does not exhibit metastatic potential [5, 7]. According to the 2003 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of breast tumors, desmoid-type fibromatosis originating from mammary parenchyma must be distinguished from that involving the chest wall fascia, which may extend into the breast tissue. However, in the subsequent WHO classifications of breast tumors issued in 2012 and 2019, both types of desmoid-type fibromatosis—whether originating in the breast parenchyma or the thoracic fascia—are categorized under breast desmoid-type fibromatosis without specific reference to their anatomical origins. Typically, breast desmoid-type fibromatosis presents unilaterally, although there are instances where it affects both breasts [10]. The age of onset for this condition spans a broad range, predominantly affecting females between the ages of 13 and 80 years [11].

From the diagnostic records of our department (Oct 2022–Sep 2024), we identified 3,030 cases related to breast diseases, with breast desmoid-type fibromatosis representing only approximately 0.1% of these cases, which aligns with previous findings [3–5]. The incidence of multifocal breast desmoid-type fibromatosis remains uncertain due to its rarity. While current diagnoses adhere to WHO criteria, larger cohorts are required for accurate epidemiological assessment.

The pathogenesis of breast desmoid-type fibromatosis remains poorly understood. The majority of cases are sporadic and may be associated with a variety of factors, including previous trauma, surgery, silicone implants, genetic predispositions, hormonal influences, and pregnancy [12–18]. A minority of cases have been associated with hereditary conditions such as Gardner syndrome or FAP [19, 20]. In sporadic and familial instances, approximately 80% of cases display nuclear expression of β-catenin, which is a critical factor in tumorigenesis [19, 21–23]. Specifically, about 20% of patients with sporadic breast desmoid-type fibromatosis exhibit mutations in the APC gene, whereas 40–50% harbor mutations in exon 3 of CTNNB1 [3, 24]. These mutations can result in the excessive accumulation of β-catenin in the nucleus. Elevated levels of β-catenin stimulate aberrant activation of the Wnt signaling pathway, which promotes the uncontrolled proliferation and differentiation of fibroblasts; this contributes to tumor development [23, 25, 26]. In extramammary desmoid-type fibromatosis cases, CTNNB1 mutations have been identified in as many as 94% of instances [3, 27].

CTNNB1 mutations cluster in the N-terminal region, within codons 32–45 encoded by exon 3. The T41A and S45F mutations are the most prevalent, detected in 50% and 25% of cases respectively, while the S45P mutation ranks third, present in 9% of cases [1]. Some studies have revealed an association between the S45F mutation and a higher risk of postoperative recurrence. Additionally, this mutation is linked to an increased likelihood of disease progression and a poor response to meloxicam treatment [28].

Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) show irregular, invasively growing masses. On MRI, T1-weighted images typically present isointense or slightly hyperintense signals relative to surrounding tissues, while T2-weighted images (T2WI) display heterogeneous, slightly hyperintense signals, aiding preoperative diagnosis [29, 30]. Notably, approximately 86% of breast desmoid-type fibromatosis cases exhibit low-signal bands on T2WI, consistent with histological collagen fibers. Ultrasonography often detects hypoechoic, irregular lesions with reduced posterior echoes [31]. Given these imaging features’ similarity to breast cancer, misdiagnosis risks are high.

Clinically, breast desmoid-type fibromatosis typically manifests as a solitary, palpable mass characterized by well-defined margins and no associated pain. Additional clinical signs include skin and nipple retraction and dimpling [13, 32, 33]. The tumor usually presents as firm, pale, and unencapsulated, with sizes ranging from 0.3 to 15 cm in diameter [34]. In the current report involving three patients diagnosed with desmoid-type fibromatosis, the lesions ranged in diameter from 0.3 to 0.8 cm. Notably, one case presented with two small foci, measuring 0.3 cm and 0.4 cm in diameter, respectively.

Breast desmoid-type fibromatosis histologically mirrors lesions at other sites, featuring uniform spindle cells (fibroblasts/myofibroblasts) in collagenous stroma arranged in parallel bundles or wavy fascicles, with rare mitotic figures. The tumor demonstrates variable architecture, alternating between hypocellular collagen-rich zones with keloid-like hyalinization and moderately cellular areas with interlacing patterns. Its characteristic infiltrative growth shows finger-like extensions into mammary parenchyma and adipose tissue, resembling phyllodes tumors. Stromal variations may include peripheral lymphocytic infiltration or focal myxoid changes mimicking low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma [2, 6, 35].

The immunoprofile demonstrates nuclear β-catenin expression in 76–100% of cases [35], though this finding is nonspecific as weak positivity may also occur in metaplastic carcinoma, phyllodes tumors, and fibrosarcoma [23–26]. Tumor cells uniformly express vimentin with variable α-SMA, actin, and desmin reactivity, while consistently lacking epithelial (AE1/AE3, p63), neural (S100), and CD34 markers. Hormone receptors (ER, PR) and HER2 are typically negative [35].

Myofibroblastic stromal hyperplasia (MSH) is a benign spindle cell proliferation of the breast stroma. Histopathologically, it is characterized by the proliferation of benign-appearing spindle cells within the intralobular stroma, typically manifesting as a diffuse, compact spindle cell proliferation with perilobular/periductal accentuation. Notably, MSH lacks mitotic activity and atypia. Immunohistochemically, tumor cells in MSH are positive for CD34 and SMA but negative for ER, β-catenin and p63 [36]. These features distinguish MSH from fibromatosis, as fibromatosis often exhibits infiltrative growth patterns, may show nuclear β-catenin positivity, and has distinct genetic alterations such as CTNNB1 mutations, which are absent in MSH.

Fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma (FLMC) is a distinct histopathologic entity defined by biphasic proliferation of mildly atypical spindle cells with epithelial elements. Neoplastic spindle cells show fascicular growth in interlacing whorls, histomorphologically mimicking desmoid-type fibromatosis. Key diagnostic features include epithelial components and immunohistochemical evidence of epithelial differentiation (strong AE1/AE3 cytokeratin and nuclear P63 reactivity). Notably, FLMC demonstrates low-grade morphology while maintaining triple-negative status (negative for ER, PR, and HER2), a paradoxical feature distinguishing it from conventional metaplastic carcinomas that typically display high-grade nuclear atypia and prominent sarcomatoid differentiation. This distinctive clinicopathologic profile facilitates differentiation from more aggressive variants within the metaplastic carcinoma spectrum [35, 37].

Phyllodes tumors demonstrate mesenchymal-predominant biphasic morphology comprising epithelial and stromal components. While sharing stromal similarities with desmoid fibromatosis, they are distinguished by epithelium-lined clefts and absence of organized fascicular stromal patterns. Immunohistochemically, diffuse CD34 positivity in most cases [38] contrasts with negative nuclear β-catenin expression, unlike the characteristic β-catenin nuclear positivity typically seen in fibromatosis.

Nodular fasciitis is characterized by rapid growth, tenderness, or pain, presenting with relatively distinct but non-encapsulated margins and lacking finger-like invasive fronts. Increased mitotic activity is a notable feature. Unlike desmoid-type fibromatosis, which exhibits focal, clustered inflammatory cell infiltrates, nodular fasciitis shows dispersed inflammatory cells throughout the lesion. In histomorphologically ambiguous cases, USP6 rearrangement detected by FISH can aid in make a definite diagnosis, as this molecular hallmark distinguishes nodular fasciitis from morphologic mimics lesions [6].

This tumor is also characterized by significant infiltration of inflammatory cells, predominantly plasma cells, alongside occasional lymphocytes or neutrophils. The myxoid degeneration in desmoid-type fibromatosis may mimic low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma (LGFMS); however, key histological and molecular differences aid in differentiation. Desmoid-type fibromatosis typically exhibits elongated, uniform spindle cells arranged in broad fascicles, whereas LGFMS demonstrates short, plump spindle or oval cells with a characteristic swirling growth pattern. Immunohistochemically, MUC4 expression strongly supports LGFMS, while molecular analysis for FUS rearrangements by FISH provides definitive diagnostic confirmation [2, 6].

Our three cases of breast desmoid-type fibromatosis demonstrated distinct clinicopathological presentations: fibroadenoma with multifocal desmoid-type fibromatosis, pure desmoid-type fibromatosis, and a complex case in which low nuclear grade DCIS coexists with both fibroadenoma and desmoid-type fibromatosis. These findings emphasize the diagnostic challenges posed by breast spindle cell lesions and highlight the importance of comprehensive pathological assessment. While β-catenin IHC often suffices for diagnosis, CTNNB1 mutation analysis remains valuable in diagnostically ambiguous cases or clinically complex tumors. However, routine genetic testing for all spindle cell lesions is currently impractical given the rarity of breast fibromatosis (< 0.2% of breast tumors) and the satisfactory performance of IHC in most cases [35].

Local recurrence has been reported in 25–29% of breast lesions following local excision [35]. Colombo et al. [41] found that the CTNNB1 S45F mutation was associated with an elevated risk of local recurrence, and incomplete resection has been linked to recurrence [4]. In contrast, Crago et al.‘s 29-year follow-up of 382 surgical cases [42] showed that margin status and sex did not predict recurrence, while younger age and larger tumor size were significant predictors. These contradictory results underscore the necessity of further research to identify reliable recurrence predictors.

Surgical resection is the standard treatment for fibromatosis. However, multiple studies report high recurrence rates, especially in young women undergoing extensive surgery [43]. As a result, management strategies have shifted towards conservative strategies, including observation, pharmacotherapy, and radiotherapy [44–46]. Radiotherapy achieves approximately 80% local control, though rare radiation-induced sarcomas require careful differentiation from tumor recurrence [47]. The former features cellular atypia, high mitotic activity, and abnormal architecture (e.g., vascular anomalies), while recurrent desmoid-type fibromatosis comprises uniform spindle cells with low mitotic activity and minimal cytological atypia. IHC facilitates discrimination, as desmoid-type fibromatosis typically exhibits nuclear β-catenin positivity, unlike radiation-induced sarcomas with subtype-specific profiles. Although our cases lacked radiotherapy history, this distinction is crucial for managing irradiated patients.

In this cohort, two patients underwent vacuum-assisted excision of breast masses, while one patient received additional breast-conserving surgery for concurrent breast cancer. All resections included tumor and margin tissue without adjuvant therapy. Outpatient follow-up was conducted every six months, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up (median 27 months, range 18–30 months).

In conclusion, the histological features of breast desmoid-type fibromatosis overlap significantly with fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma and other mesenchymal spindle cell tumors. Limited sampling or small lesion size may lead to misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis. Historically, all reported breast cases have been unifocal; however, this study describes three cases of breast desmoid-type fibromatosis, including the first documented multifocal case and coexisting breast neoplasms. These findings emphasize the need for thorough evaluation of breast fibromatosis in any spindle cell lesion, whether solitary or multifocal, and with or without concomitant pathologies. In diagnostically challenging cases, we recommend a combined approach of β-catenin IHC and genetic testing to enhance diagnostic precision and minimize misdiagnosis risks.
