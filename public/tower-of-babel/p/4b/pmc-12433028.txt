Age at disease onset is an independent risk factor for physical growth of children with idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis
Authors: Siyun Li, Tingting Li, Lanzhi Zhou, Dandan Mou, Meng Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (IPH), a pediatric pulmonary disorder requiring prolonged corticosteroid therapy raises concerns about growth impairment. While corticosteroid-induced stunting is well-documented, its specific impact on the height of IPH patients remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of corticosteroid exposure on height outcomes and identify key predictors of growth restriction in children with IPH.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 45 IPH patients [2018–2023] were stratified into normal-stature (n=37) and short-stature (n=8) groups based on anthropometric criteria. Multivariable Cox regression and comparative analyses assessed age at onset, diagnostic delay, corticosteroid duration, nutritional parameters, pulmonary function, and growth hormone (GH) therapy efficacy.ResultsShort-stature patients demonstrated an earlier disease onset (P=0.004) and prolonged diagnostic delay (P=0.002). A younger age at onset independently predicted impaired height growth [hazard ratio (HR) =0.435, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.206–0.920, P=0.02], while corticosteroid duration showed no association (P=0.45). Secondary analyses identified synergistic effects of anemia (P=0.02), malnutrition (P=0.045), and pulmonary fibrosis (P=0.008). GH therapy significantly improved height velocity (P<0.001) and bone metabolism markers (P<0.05).ConclusionsEarly IPH onset, rather than corticosteroid exposure, drives growth impairment in children. Timely diagnosis, nutritional optimization, and adjunctive GH therapy may mitigate height deficits, underscoring the significance of multidisciplinary management strategies.Keywords: Idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (IPH), corticosteroids, height, etiology, children

Highlight box

Key findings

• Early disease onset is an independent risk factor for growth impairment in pediatric idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (IPH), and delayed diagnosis, anemia, malnutrition, and pulmonary fibrosis have synergistic effects.

• Prolonged corticosteroid exposure showed no significant association with height deficits.

What is known and what is new?

• Prolonged corticosteroid use in pediatric populations will impair physical growth, but its specific impact on growth trajectories in IPH patients remains underexplored.

• This study assesses the association between corticosteroid therapy duration and height impairment in children with IPH, and identifies independent risk factors for growth restriction, including age at disease onset, diagnostic delay, anemia severity, and pulmonary function decline.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Early diagnosis and adjunctive growth hormone therapy significantly improved height velocity and bone metabolism, highlighting interventions to mitigate developmental deficits.

• Future studies should explore targeted interventions for physical growth in children with IPH and their underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis (IPH), a rare and life-threatening pulmonary disorder characterized by recurrent alveolar hemorrhage, diffuse parenchymal infiltration, and iron-deficiency anemia, predominantly affects children under 10 years of age (1-3). Its clinical course is marked by unpredictable exacerbations, chronic progression, and potential complications such as pulmonary fibrosis and respiratory failure, contributing to a mortality rate as high as 50% (4-6). Despite advances in diagnostic imaging and histopathological techniques, IPH pathogenesis remains elusive, and its heterogeneous clinical manifestations often delay diagnosis, complicating therapeutic interventions.

Corticosteroids, frequently combined with immunosuppressants like azathioprine, represent the cornerstone of IPH management (7,8). However, prolonged corticosteroid use in pediatric populations is well-documented to impair physical growth through mechanisms involving growth hormone (GH) suppression, reduced insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) activity, and disrupted bone metabolism (9-12). While these adverse effects are established in other chronic inflammatory conditions, their specific impact on growth trajectories in IPH patients remains underexplored. Existing studies have primarily focused on acute clinical outcomes, leaving a critical gap in understanding long-term growth impairment.

Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that disease-related factors such as early-onset IPH, prolonged anemia, and pulmonary dysfunction may synergistically exacerbate growth restriction independent of corticosteroid exposure. Early disease manifestation, often accompanied by diagnostic delays, could disrupt critical phases of skeletal development, while chronic hypoxia secondary to pulmonary fibrosis may further impair growth potential (13,14). Despite these hypotheses, no comprehensive analyses have systematically evaluated the interplay between corticosteroid therapy, disease severity, and nutritional status in shaping height outcomes in pediatric IPH.

This study aims to address these knowledge gaps by assessing the association between corticosteroid therapy duration and height impairment in children with IPH, and identifying independent risk factors for growth restriction, including age at disease onset, diagnostic delay, anemia severity, and pulmonary function decline. Our findings aim to inform tailored management strategies to optimize both survival and developmental outcomes in this high-risk population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-263/rc).

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively collected and analyzed clinical data and treatment information of patients with IPH in Qingdao Women and Children’s Hospital from January 2018 to December 2023. The collected clinical data included gender, age at disease onset, age at diagnosis, follow-up height, clinical symptoms, duration of corticosteroid therapy, and diagnostic criteria for IPH. All cases met diagnostic criteria for IPH, with secondary causes of diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (e.g., vasculitides, coagulopathies, connective tissue disorders) rigorously excluded per established guidelines (13). All patients underwent baseline height measurement at diagnosis using wall-mounted stadiometers. Height was subsequently tracked at 3-month intervals during follow-up visits. Corticosteroid regimens were individualized: initial induction dose (1–2 mg/kg/day methylprednisolone), tapered to maintenance (0.25–0.5 mg/kg/day). This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao Women and Children’s Hospital (approval No. 202305012). Due to the retrospective design using anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. All patient identifiers were removed to ensure confidentiality.

Measurement of height

Currently, height measurements of children in China have been carried out by several studies (15-17). In this study, the patients’ height data were compared and analyzed against the average height data of Chinese rural children. Children were classified as having short stature if their height was more than two standard deviations below the mean for rural children in China, based on nationally representative growth standards from the National Rural Children Growth Standard Survey (18-20).

Treatment regimens

There is no standardized treatment protocol for pediatric IPH, but corticosteroids (oral or intravenous methylprednisolone) are typically the first-line option as induction therapy. In this study, all pediatric patients received regular corticosteroid treatment, with some additionally prescribed other immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine based on disease severity.

Observation indicators

To further investigate the impact of corticosteroid therapy on growth and development in pediatric IPH patients, we measured and compared the levels of calcium (Ca2+), phosphorus (P3+), IGF-1, insulin-like growth factor binding protein-3 (IGFBP-3), and osteocalcin (BGP) among children during different maintenance phases of corticosteroid therapy.

To investigate the impact of additional factors, such as nutritional deficiencies and anemia, on height outcomes in children with IPH, we further analyzed and compared hemoglobin, serum ferritin, transferrin saturation, body mass index (BMI), and pulmonary function parameters between the normal-height group and the short-stature group.

Pulmonary function evaluation was rigorously adapted to pediatric developmental stages in accordance with American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) guidelines (21). Standard spirometry including forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) measurements was performed in cooperative children aged ≥6 years. For preschool children (3–5 years), we employed play-based incentive spirometry with interactive biofeedback systems to enhance engagement, achieving technically acceptable maneuvers in >90% of sessions through repetitive trials with visual rewards. In infants and toddlers under 3 years—where conventional pulmonary function testing proves challenging—we implemented a comprehensive surrogate protocol combining continuous pulse oximetry monitoring during natural sleep, standardized clinical respiratory scoring (assessing respiratory rate, retractions, and auscultation findings), quantitative high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) analysis of fibrosis burden using validated densitometric thresholds, and serum biomarker profiling (Krebs von den Lungen-6/Mucin 1, KL-6/MUC1) reflecting alveolar epithelial integrity. All assessments were conducted during clinical stability phases (≥4 weeks after acute hemorrhage episodes) under the supervision of certified pediatric pulmonologists, ensuring methodological consistency across the cohort while addressing age-specific technical limitations through multimodal physiological characterization.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0. Categorical variables were assessed using Chi-squared tests, and continuous variables were analyzed using parametric tests: independent samples t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multi-group analyses. Cox proportional hazards regression modeling was used to identify potential risk factors for height in children with IPH. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05, with hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) reported.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The study included 45 children with IPH, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1.3:1. All children presented with idiopathic iron-deficiency anemia at the time of admission. The median age at disease onset was 3 years (range, 0.5–16 years), and the median age at the end of follow-up was 6 years (range, 2–17 years). The median diagnostic delay was 4.67 months (range, 0.03–28.00 months), and the median duration of corticosteroid treatment was 15 months (range, 0.3–95 months).

Imaging findings and analysis

All pediatric patients with IPH in this study underwent chest X-ray and HRCT during diagnosis and follow-up. Acute-phase imaging predominantly revealed bilateral diffuse ground-glass opacities (76.7%, Figure 3) and patchy consolidations (64.4%), with interlobular septal thickening observed in 31.1% of cases, consistent with the classic presentation of alveolar hemorrhage. Chronic-phase follow-up demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of pulmonary fibrosis in the low group compared to the normal group (62.5% vs. 18.9%, P=0.008). HRCT findings in the chronic phase included reticular opacities (58.3%, Figure 4), honeycombing (25.0%), and traction bronchiectasis (16.7%), indicating structural lung damage associated with prolonged disease activity.

A 9-year-old male patient presented with a 14-day history of cough and three episodes of hemoptysis. Laboratory findings revealed a normal white blood cell count, alongside reduced red blood cell count and hemoglobin levels. High-resolution computed tomography demonstrated bilateral diffuse irregular ground-glass opacities, consistent with alveolar hemorrhage.

A 6-year-old male patient from the low group, diagnosed with idiopathic pulmonary hemosiderosis 2 years prior, presented for follow-up evaluation. The patient is currently asymptomatic. High-resolution computed tomography revealed bilateral diffuse reticular opacities in both lungs.

Comprehensive analysis revealed significant correlations between imaging severity and pulmonary function parameters: patients with pulmonary fibrosis exhibited lower DLCO (% predicted) (62.1±8.9 vs. 73.8±9.5, P=0.006) and reduced FEV1/FVC ratios (77.2±6.8 vs. 86.1±6.1, P=0.003) compared to non-fibrotic patients. Furthermore, delayed diagnosis (>12 months) was associated with a higher proportion of fibrotic changes on HRCT (75.0% vs. 12.0% in the early diagnosis group (<6 months), supporting the hypothesis that delayed intervention exacerbates pulmonary structural deterioration (P<0.001).

Notably, patients with disease onset <2 years exhibited more extensive baseline ground-glass opacities (90.0% vs. 53.8% in the >4-year onset group, P=0.02) and accelerated fibrotic progression during follow-up (annual increase in reticular opacity area: 1.2±0.4 vs. 0.5±0.3 cm2, P=0.007). These findings align with clinical observations of heightened pulmonary functional decline and growth impairment in early-onset cases, underscoring the importance of dynamic imaging monitoring for assessing disease activity and prognostic stratification.

Risk factors for height

Multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that prolonged corticosteroid exposure did not independently predict height impairment (P=0.45). Notably, younger age at disease onset emerged as a significant independent predictor of growth restriction (HR =0.435, 95% CI: 0.206–0.920, P=0.02), indicating that each year of later disease manifestation corresponded to a 43.5% decreased risk of compromised height growth in the IPH cohort. Besides, in addition to age at disease onset, both anemia and pulmonary fibrosis independently influenced height growth. Furthermore, follow-up observations demonstrated that a younger age at disease onset was associated with slower annual height gain (P=0.002), supporting early disease onset as an independent risk factor for growth impairment (Tables 5,6).

To delineate the respective contributions of disease onset age versus therapeutic timing, we analyzed the early-onset IPH cohort (<2 years; n=10) stratified by corticosteroid initiation latency. As presented in Table 7, patients receiving early corticosteroid intervention (≤3 months post-onset) demonstrated comparable growth outcomes to those with delayed initiation (>3 months), with no significant difference in final height Z-scores (−1.8±0.4 vs. −1.6±0.5, P=0.41) or annual height velocity (3.5±0.6 vs. 3.7±0.5 cm/year, P=0.53). These findings collectively indicate that the developmental stage at disease manifestation—rather than corticosteroid exposure chronology—constitutes the principal determinant of growth failure in pediatric IPH.

The effect of pulmonary fibrosis on height growth in children with IPH

Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant inverse relationship between pulmonary fibrosis severity and height velocity (P<0.001). Patients without radiological evidence of fibrosis maintained near-normal growth trajectories (5.2±0.4 cm/year), whereas those with mild fibrosis exhibited 17.3% reduction in annual height gain (4.3±0.5 cm/year, P=0.008). Most notably, severe fibrosis—defined as >30% lung involvement on HRCT—correlated with profoundly compromised growth velocity (3.4±0.6 cm/year), representing a 34.6% deficit compared to non-fibrotic counterparts (P<0.001). This dose-dependent impairment persisted after adjusting for age and nutritional status in multivariable modeling (β=−0.7, 95% CI: −1.1 to −0.3, P=0.001), indicating that each incremental severity grade reduced height velocity by approximately 0.7 cm/year independent of confounding factors (Table 17).

Discussion

IPH, first characterized in 1931 as a pediatric diffuse alveolar hemorrhage syndrome, represents a life-threatening respiratory disorder primarily affecting children under 10 years of age. Our cohort (n=45) validated this epidemiological pattern, demonstrating a median disease onset at 3 years aligning with landmark epidemiological studies (2,22), while revealing novel insights into growth impairment correlates. Our study elucidated the complex interplay between IPH and growth impairment in pediatric patients, identifying early disease onset as a critical independent risk factor for compromised height trajectories.

Corticosteroids remain a mainstay of treatment for IPH, but prolonged use is demonstrated to have adverse effects on pediatric growth (9,11,12). Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, corticosteroids may impair growth by inhibiting the secretion of GH, reducing IGF-1 activity, or affecting bone formation and collagen synthesis (23,24). Contrary to prior assumptions, prolonged corticosteroid exposure and corticosteroid dose demonstrated no significant association with growth restriction, challenging conventional perspectives on glucocorticoid-induced stunting in chronic pediatric conditions. Furthermore, our analyses revealed preserved IGF-1, IGFBP-3, and bone turnover markers across corticosteroid treatment durations, suggesting that IPH-related pathophysiological mechanisms rather than iatrogenic factors, may predominantly disrupt growth. This discrepancy highlights disease-specific variability in glucocorticoid impacts and necessitates tailored therapeutic strategies. Notably, combination therapy with immunosuppressants reduced relapse rates without exacerbating growth impairment, supporting its utility in balancing disease control and developmental preservation.

Instead, younger age at disease manifestation, prolonged diagnostic delays, and synergistic effects of anemia, malnutrition, and pulmonary fibrosis emerged as dominant contributors to impaired growth. Early disease onset (<2 years) was strongly associated with prolonged diagnostic delays, severe anemia, and reduced height velocity, aligning with developmental vulnerability during critical growth phases. Chronic hypoxia secondary to pulmonary fibrosis and malnutrition likely exacerbates growth restriction by impairing metabolic and skeletal homeostasis (22,25). The correlation between lower ferritin, BMI, and height Z-scores further emphasizes the role of nutritional optimization in mitigating growth deficits. Findings corroborate emerging hypotheses that disease severity and systemic complications synergistically drive growth impairment in IPH, independent of corticosteroid exposure, also underscoring the necessity of prioritizing early diagnosis and multidisciplinary interventions to mitigate developmental deficits in children with IPH.

The efficacy of adjunctive GH therapy in improving height velocity and restoring IGF-1 levels offers a promising avenue for addressing stunting. While GH therapy has shown benefits in other steroid-dependent conditions (26-28), its application in IPH warrants further exploration, particularly in patients with early-onset disease and persistent growth failure.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including potential selection bias and a small cohort size, which may limit generalizability. Furthermore, the observational nature precludes definitive causal inferences. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal follow-up are needed to validate these findings and elucidate mechanistic pathways linking IPH pathogenesis to growth disruption.

In conclusion, our findings redefine the etiological framework of growth impairment in pediatric IPH, emphasizing early disease onset and systemic complications as primary drivers. Timely diagnosis, nutritional support, and adjunctive GH therapy may optimize growth outcomes, underscoring the imperative for integrated management strategies in this high-risk population.

Conclusions

This study identified early disease onset as an independent risk factor for growth impairment in pediatric IPH, highlighting the synergistic roles of diagnostic delays, anemia, malnutrition, and pulmonary fibrosis. Prolonged corticosteroid exposure showed no significant association with height deficits. Multidisciplinary strategies including timely diagnosis, nutritional optimization, and adjunctive GH therapy, may mitigate developmental deficits.
