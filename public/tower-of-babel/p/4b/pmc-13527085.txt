Nanozymatic platform for dual colorimetric and fluorimetric sensing of H₂O₂ and glucose using Ni-CoWO₄ nanoparticles
Authors: Kiarash Jodat Asl, Abdolhossein Naseri, Mohammad Amjadi, Seyyed Morteza Seyyed Alavi, Aref Pariz, Farida Tatardar, Farzaneh Rasoulzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) plays a crucial role in both biological and industrial systems. Excessive H₂O₂ can lead to oxidative stress, cellular damage, and process-related issues in manufacturing. For this reason, fast and sensitive detection methods are needed. In this study, Ni-CoWO₄ nanoparticles, belonging to the bimetallic nanozymes family, were used as catalysts to promote the oxidation of ortho-phenylenediamine (OPD) by H₂O₂. Polyoxometalates are renowned for their structural diversity, large surface area, and robust redox activity, making them exceptional candidates for catalytic applications. Ni-CoWO₄ was prepared using a hydrothermal method and characterized using SEM-EDX, XRD, and FT-IR techniques. The nanoparticles exhibited an even distribution of nickel and cobalt within a multi-metallic framework, resulting in numerous active sites and a high surface-to-volume ratio. Compared to the system without the catalyst, Ni-CoWO₄ significantly improved the reaction rate, produced a stronger yellow color, and reduced the oxidation time. The reaction was monitored by visible spectrophotometry, spectrofluorimetry, and smartphone-based colorimetry. A linear range of 0.5–150 µM and a limit of detection of 485 nM for H2O2, and a linear range of 1-125 µM for glucose were obtained by the spectrophotometry. In spectrofluorimetry, it showed a linear range of 0.1–50 µM, and LOD was 64 nM. In the smartphone-based method, RGB (Red, Green, Blue) and CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key (Black)) were used for colorimetric and fluorimetric determination. Overall, the results demonstrate that Ni-CoWO₄ is a low-cost and effective nanocatalyst for H₂O₂ and glucose sensing, with significant potential for both biological and industrial applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58001-3.Keywords: H₂O₂, Ni-CoWO₄, Bimetallic nanoparticles, Nanozymes, Glucose, Colorimetric/fluorimetric sensing, SmartphoneSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is an important chemical widely used in healthcare, textile processing, water treatment, and chemical synthesis due to its strong oxidative properties1,2. However, it’s high reactivity also poses significant risks, as excessive exposure can lead to oxidative stress, cellular damage, and various health issues, including skin and eye irritation3. Therefore, the precise and highly efficient detection of H₂O₂ is crucial to ensure both safety and environmental protection4.

Among various analytical approaches, including electrochemical and fluorescence-based methods, colorimetric techniques have attracted considerable attention owing to their simplicity, rapid response, and minimal instrumentation requirements5. These methods typically rely on nanomaterial-based catalysts that promote colorimetric reactions proportional to H₂O₂ concentration, enabling straightforward and sensitive detection6. In addition, H₂O₂ detection plays a central role in glucose sensing, as it is a key byproduct of enzymatic glucose oxidation reactions7.

Fluorescence spectroscopy is another powerful analytical technique known for its high sensitivity and selectivity8. It operates by measuring the emission of light from excited molecules, allowing detection at very low concentration levels, often in the nanomolar or even picomolar range. Owing to its rapid response and capability for real-time monitoring, fluorescence analysis has been widely applied in chemical and biological systems9–11.

Smartphones, equipped with cameras, sensors, and fast processors, are increasingly used in analytical chemistry as cost-effective alternatives to traditional instruments. With simple apps or add-ons, they can perform spectroscopic, electrochemical, and colorimetric tests by recording and analyzing color changes in reactions12,13. By capturing and processing color changes in chemical reactions, smartphone-based platforms enable on-site and real-time analysis without the need for sophisticated instrumentation. Such systems have been successfully applied in medical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and food safety analysis14,15. A good example is the colorimetric detection of hydrogen peroxide. The phone camera captures the color shift that happens when H₂O₂ reacts with chosen indicators. Simple apps then measure the color intensity and link it to concentration. This makes the process cheaper and more practical than standard instruments. Small chips or add-ons can increase accuracy and sensitivity. Since phones are portable, they allow on-site detection of H₂O₂ in medical samples, food, and water16.

In this work, an integrated sensing strategy based on Ni-CoWO₄ nanozymes and smartphone-assisted colorimetric/fluorimetric readout is explored for the detection of H₂O₂ and glucose. The use of a smartphone as a signal acquisition tool provides a portable and relatively low-cost alternative to conventional laboratory instrumentation and enables simple visual monitoring of the reaction through digital color analysis. Such an approach may facilitate rapid and accessible measurements, which can be advantageous for preliminary screening and potential on-site applications.

Glucose serves as the primary energy source for cellular metabolism and is essential for maintaining normal physiological functions; however, abnormal levels can lead to diseases such as diabetes17. Therefore, precise and continuous monitoring of blood glucose levels is essential for effective disease management, early diagnosis, and prevention of diabetes-associated complications18,19. In enzymatic glucose sensing systems, glucose oxidase (GOx) catalyzes the oxidation of β-D-glucose to D-glucono-δ-lactone, producing H₂O₂ as a detectable byproduct. This generated H₂O₂ can then be quantified using colorimetric or fluorimetric methods, typically through the catalytic oxidation of chromogenic substrates such as o-phenylenediamine (OPD) or 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), resulting in measurable optical signals20,21. Colorimetric techniques offer high accuracy, speed, and portability, they are highly suitable for point-of-care diagnosis22,23. Nanoparticles are crucial in optimizing these detection strategies due to remarkable physicochemical characteristics, namely elevated surface-to-volume ratios, controllable optical as well as electronic properties, and enhanced catalytic activity24–26. In particular, nanozymes—nanomaterials with enzyme-like activity have been widely explored as stable and cost-effective alternatives to natural enzymes. Recent studies have demonstrated their broad applicability in biosensing, environmental analysis, and food safety monitoring27–29. Despite these advances, challenges such as limited catalytic efficiency and selectivity in complex matrices remain, necessitating the development of improved nanozyme systems30–33.

Bimetallic nanozymes have recently emerged as a highly efficient class of enzyme-mimicking nanomaterials due to their superior catalytic activity, structural versatility, and enhanced electron-transfer properties. Compared to monometallic nanozymes, bimetallic systems exhibit pronounced synergistic effects arising from the interaction between two metal centers, which can significantly improve catalytic efficiency, substrate affinity, and stability. Previous studies have demonstrated that such synergistic interactions facilitate rapid redox cycling and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, thereby enhancing peroxidase-like activity34–38. For instance, Fe–Ni and Co-based bimetallic nanozymes have shown markedly improved catalytic performance and sensitivity in biosensing applications due to optimized electronic structures and increased active site density39–41. Among various candidates, transition metal tungstates have attracted increasing attention as promising nanozyme platforms owing to their robust crystal structures, favorable redox characteristics, and chemical stability. In particular, Ni-CoWO₄ combines the advantages of both nickel and cobalt, enabling efficient redox cycling between Ni²⁺/Ni³⁺ and Co²⁺/Co³⁺, which plays a critical role in catalytic reactions involving H₂O₂ decomposition. The presence of tungsten further contributes to structural stability and modulates the electronic environment, facilitating charge transfer processes. Although several bimetallic nanozymes have been explored for colorimetric sensing, reports on Ni-CoWO₄ as a dual mode (colorimetric/fluorimetric) nanozyme platform remain limited. Therefore, the development of Ni-CoWO₄-based nanozymes offers a promising strategy to achieve enhanced catalytic performance and improved analytical sensitivity for practical sensing applications. These attributes, combined with their extraordinary reactivity alongside chemical as well as thermal stabilities, make Ni-CoWO₄ nanomaterials optimal leads for sensing chemical applications, energy storage, photocatalysis, as well as medical diagnosis42–44.

In this study, colorimetric sensing based on novel Ni-CoWO₄ nanomaterials as nanocatalysts is introduced to enable H₂O₂-assisted OPD oxidation sensing to measure glucose concentrations using GOx activity. Visibly apparent yellow shift as color change is monitored using UV-Vis spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, and smartphone-based colorimetry, offering a quick and reliable measure for glucose concentrations. Colorimetric sensing with Ni-CoWO₄ nanomaterials has significant potential for developing analytical chemistry as well as biosensing technologies, particularly in resource-poor settings.

Experimental

Materials and instrumentation

All reagents used were of analytical grade and primarily sourced from Merck (Germany), Sigma-Aldrich, or Fluka. UV-Vis absorption spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Japan), with 2 mL quartz cuvettes. FT-IR spectra were acquired with a Tensor 27 spectrophotometer (Bruker, Germany). Elemental composition was analyzed via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using a MIRA III system. Morphological and structural characterization of the synthesized nanomaterials was conducted using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, MIRA 3 FEG, Tescan Co., Czech Republic). X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using a Siemens D500 diffractometer (Germany). Centrifugation was performed using a Hettich centrifuge (D-7200 Tuttlingen, Germany) at 6000 rpm. A Shimadzu AW220 digital balance (Japan) was employed for weighing all solids. Solution heating was carried out using a laboratory hot plate. The pH of solutions was controlled using a Metrohm 827 pH meter. A Carbolite chamber furnace was utilized for high-temperature drying and calcination of the synthesized materials.

Synthesis of nickel-cobalt tungstate polyoxometalate nanoparticles

Nickel-cobalt tungstate polyoxometalate nanoparticles were synthesized based on Alharthi et. al.‘s report45. Briefly, 1.0 g each of nickel nitrate (Ni(NO₃)₂) and cobalt nitrate (Co(NO₃)₂) was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring on a heater-stirrer at 50 °C for 30 min. Without removing the solution from heat, 4.6 g of sodium tungstate (Na₂WO₄) was added, resulting in a visible color change from green to purple. Heating was maintained for an additional 30 min. The mixture was then transferred to an autoclave and heated at 150 °C for 7 h. The product was allowed to cool to room temperature, then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the solid residue was washed three times with distilled water and ethanol. The resulting product was calcinated at 600 °C for 5 h using a heating rate of 10 °C min⁻¹. Calcination at 600 °C for 5 h was performed to achieve the required crystallinity and phase stability of the Ni-CoWO₄ nanoparticles. The resultant nickel-cobalt tungstate polyoxometalate powder was stored for subsequent use.

Procedure for hydrogen peroxide determination by colorimetry

For the preparation of the H2O2 assay, 2500 µL of 0.1 M OPD solution in phosphate buffer at pH 6.0, and 450 µL of the nanoparticle suspension were added to a 5 mL volumetric flask. Then, standard or real samples were added to it, and the final volume was adjusted to 5 mL with double-distilled water. The solution was incubated in a water bath at 40 °C for 25 min. Absorbance was measured at 480 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, and the increase in absorbance was used to determine the hydrogen peroxide concentration.

Procedure of hydrogen peroxide determination by fluorimetry

For the preparation of the H2O2 assay, 2500 µL of 0.1 M OPD solution in phosphate buffer at pH 6, and 450 µL of the nanoparticle suspension were added to a 5 mL volumetric flask. Then, standard or real samples were added to it, and the final volume was adjusted to 5 mL with double-distilled water. The solution was incubated in a water bath at 40 °C for 25 min. Fluorescence intensity at 620 nm (λexcitation = 425 nm) was used as an analytical signal for determination.

Glucose determination

For glucose detection, 200 µL of glucose oxidase solution (1 mg/mL), 200 µL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH = 7), and 1400 µL of deionized water were added to test tubes. Subsequently, 200 µL of glucose standard solutions of varying concentrations were introduced. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Following the enzymatic reaction, 500 µL of each sample was subjected to the same protocol as hydrogen peroxide detection. The corresponding absorbance spectra were recorded accordingly.

Smartphone procedure

A 1000 µL aliquot of 0.1 M OPD prepared in phosphate buffer (pH 6) was added to a test tube, followed by 180 µL of NiCoWO₄, H₂O₂ standard solution, or a real sample added to the tube. The final volume was adjusted to 2 mL using deionized water (DIW). The mixture was immediately placed in a water bath at 40 °C and incubated for 25 min. After incubation, the reaction mixture was transferred into a 2 mL glass tube and positioned in the cell holder of a smartphone-based detection box (Fig. S1, ESI), where the RGB and CMYK values were recorded using the mobile phone’s (Samsung Galaxy A71) camera with color palette: pick and generate (core apps creation).

Preparation of real sample

Apple juice: A 10 mL aliquot of apple juice was placed in a centrifuge tube and spun at 5000 rpm for 15 min to remove pulp and coarse particles. The supernatant was decanted into a fresh centrifuge tube, and two concentrations of H2O2/glucose standards were spiked into the sample. For H2O2 detection, the sample was diluted 10-fold and analyzed directly by the proposed method. For glucose detection, the sample was pre-treated according to the established method and appropriately diluted before analysis.

Soft drink: A 10 mL sample of soda was transferred to a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min to separate coarse particles. The supernatant was collected in a new centrifuge tube, and two concentrations of H2O2/glucose standards were added. For H2O2 determination, the sample was diluted 10-fold and used directly by the proposed method. For glucose analysis, the sample was processed using the proposed method and diluted appropriately before detection.

Human blood serum: A 0.5 mL sample of human blood serum was transferred to a centrifuge tube, spiked with H2O2/glucose standards, and mixed with 1.5 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid solution. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to precipitate serum proteins and excess lipids. The supernatant was transferred to a 5 mL volumetric flask and brought to volume with deionized water. For H2O2 detection, the sample was diluted 10-fold and analyzed directly by the proposed method. For glucose detection, the sample was prepared following the proposed method and diluted appropriately before analysis.

Results and discussion

Characterization

The synthesized Ni-CoWO₄ nanomaterial was comprehensively characterized to elucidate its structural, morphological, and compositional properties using a combination of analytical techniques. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy provided insights into the functional groups and chemical bonding present within the material. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, a broad and low-intensity absorption band observed at 3427.72 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the O-H stretching vibrations, typically attributed to adsorbed moisture or surface hydroxyl groups. In addition, the distinct absorption peaks located at 868.75 cm⁻¹ and 622.57 cm⁻¹ are characteristic of W = O terminal vibrations and M-O-M bridges (where M = Ni or Co), supporting the formation of a polyoxometalate-like structural framework. Furthermore, the signals at 616.06 cm⁻¹ and 526.27 cm⁻¹ are associated with Ni-O and Co-O bonds, respectively, confirming the bimetallic nature of the tungstate phase. A notable feature at 466.91 cm⁻¹ is indicative of bending vibrations within the WO₆ octahedra and serves as a fingerprint band for the polyoxometalate structure, corroborating the successful integration of tungsten-oxygen coordination environments.

(a) FT-IR spectroscopy, (b) X-ray diffraction pattern, and (c) EDS analysis of Ni-CoWo4.

The crystalline structure and phase composition of the synthesized material were examined using X ray diffraction (XRD) analysis over a 2θ range of 5°−70°. As shown in Fig. 1b, the diffraction pattern exhibits sharp and well-defined peaks, confirming the formation of a highly crystalline monoclinic structure. The diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of 23.9°, 24.7°, 30.78°, 36.4°, 41.3°, 54.2°, and 65.3° are in good agreement with the standard patterns of wolframite type NiWO₄ and CoWO₄ (JCPDS 00–015-0755 and 00–015-0867). The absence of any additional diffraction peaks indicates that the synthesized material is phase pure within the detection limit of the technique. Minor shifts in peak positions compared to the reference patterns are attributed to the partial substitution of Ni²⁺ by Co²⁺ ions within the crystal lattice, leading to slight lattice distortion. Accordingly, the material can be more accurately described as a Ni-Co substituted tungstate solid solution with a wolframite type structure, rather than a distinct NiCoWO₄ crystalline phase.

To validate the elemental composition and purity of the synthesized product, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 1c, the EDX spectrum confirms the presence of Ni, Co, W, and O as the principal elements, with no detectable signals from impurities or unintended elements, thereby indicating a high level of chemical purity. The quantitative elemental analysis revealed that tungsten is the predominant element, with a weight% of 72.92%, followed by oxygen (11.48%), nickel (9.42%), and cobalt (6.18%). These values are in line with the expected stoichiometry of Ni-CoWO₄. Corresponding atomic percentages also reflect a logical elemental distribution, with oxygen being the most abundant due to its role in the tungstate lattice. To assess the spatial homogeneity of elemental distribution, elemental mapping was conducted, and the resulting maps are presented in Fig. 1c. The elemental maps confirm a uniform distribution of Ni, Co, W, and O across the material surface.This uniformity is critical for ensuring consistent performance in catalytic and electrochemical applications.

The morphology and microstructure of the synthesized nanomaterial were further examined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM images captured at various magnifications, shown in Fig. 2, revealed that the particles possess a hybrid morphology composed of spherical and cauliflower-like aggregates. These hierarchical structures appear to result from spontaneous clustering of individual nanoparticles, likely driven by van der Waals forces and minimization of surface energy. The aggregates were uniformly distributed across the substrate, suggesting a consistent and controlled growth mechanism. From high-resolution images, the average diameter of the clusters was estimated to be between 10 and 12 μm. The observed porous and textured surface is indicative of a high surface area, a desirable feature for applications involving catalysis, adsorption, or sensing. The cauliflower-like texture, in particular, is often associated with increased diffusivity and surface reactivity.

Study of nanozymic properties of Ni-CoWO₄

Nanozymes are nanoparticles or nanostructures that mimic natural enzymatic activity. The advantages of nanozymes include high stability under different thermal and chemical conditions, low production cost, tunability in terms of size and structure, and applicability in complex matrices46. These features improve nanozymes compared to natural enzymes and have proposed them as suitable alternatives in biological, sensing, and catalysis applications. Nanozymes can perform activities such as peroxidase, catalase, and oxidase, and play an important role in the detection of biomolecules, pollution control, and medical treatments47. Ni-CoWO₄ nanoparticles represent a class of bimetallic tungstate nanomaterials in which the coexistence of Ni²⁺ and Co²⁺ ions can influence the electronic structure and catalytic behavior. The nanoscale morphology of these materials may contribute to an increased surface area and improved accessibility of active sites, which can facilitate mass transfer during catalytic reactions. As shown in Fig. 3, Ni-CoWO4 exhibits a great nanozymetic activity toward the oxidation of chromogenic substrates. The observed catalytic behavior can be tentatively attributed to the presence of mixed transition metal centers, which may enable redox interactions during the reaction process. In the presence of H₂O₂, the material is likely to promote the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which subsequently oxidize colorimetric substrates such as TMB or OPD. In many studies, these types of nanozymes are utilized in colorimetric biosensors for the detection of H₂O₂ and glucose, serving as a substitute for natural peroxidase enzymes with high sensitivity, a low detection limit, and greater stability48,49. For the detection of efficient ROS species in the OPD substrate reaction, some radical scavengers have been tested. During the catalytic decomposition of H₂O₂, highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH) are generated, which subsequently oxidize o-phenylenediamine (OPD) to produce a yellow-colored product with a strong absorbance peak at 450 nm. Ascorbic acid significantly inhibited the catalytic activity by 99.5%. Sodium azide reduces the absorbance by 12.5%. The presence of thiourea resulted in a 5.6% reduction in absorbance. These results demonstrate that ·OH radicals play a major role in OPD oxidation, while singlet oxygen and other ROS have minor contributions. Nonenzymatic behavior of Ni-CoWO4.

The catalytic behavior of enzymes and enzyme-like nanomaterials is often studied using Michaelis-Menten kinetics. This model relates reaction rate to substrate concentration. Two key parameters are obtained: the Michaelis constant (Km), which indicates how well the catalyst binds to the substrate, and the maximum reaction rate (Vmax), which reflects the turnover rate when the substrate is plentiful. This approach is useful for comparing nanozymes with natural enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase (HRP). Ni-CoWO₄ nanoparticles, acting as peroxidase mimics, are typically tested with chromogenic substrates like TMB or OPD in the presence of hydrogen peroxide. They usually display a low Kₘ, indicating strong substrate binding, and a high Vₘₐₓ, demonstrating efficient catalytic activity. As shown in Fig. 3, the Vmax value is 2.37 × 10− 8Ms− 1, and the Km is 15.9 µM with a standard error of 2.51. These findings confirm that Ni-CoWO₄ nanozymes exhibit strong peroxidase-like activity, highlighting their potential as stable, cost-effective, and highly efficient alternatives to natural enzymes for biosensing and analytical applications. Preparation of a sensor for H2O2 and glucose based on Ni-CoWO4

At optimum condition, OPD is 50mM in phosphate buffer pH = 6, temperature of 40 °C, concentration of Ni-CoWO4 nanoparticles 0.5mM, and time 30 min (Fig S2-S6 in supplementary), different concentrations of H2O2 and glucose were tested, and the absorbance and fluorescence intensity were determined by UV-Vis spectrophotometry and spectrofluorimetry, and the calibration curves are shown in Fig. S7-S8 (ESI). The colorimetric probe exhibited a linear response in the range of 0.5 to 150 µM, with the equation ΔA = 0.0006 C + 0.1475, where ΔA is the difference between absorbance in λ = 450 nm in the absence (A0) and presence of H2O2 (A), and C is the concentration of H2O2. The detection limit calculated as 3Sy/x/b (where Sy/x is the standard deviation of regression and b is the slope of the calibration graph) was 485 nM. Table S4 (ESI) presents the reproducibility of the probe response, evaluated by calculating the relative standard deviation (RSD%) at three points (starting, middle, and ending) of the calibration curve. These measurements were repeated three times on the same day and on three different days. For glucose, as shown in Fig. 4a, b, the UV-Vis method demonstrated a linear range of 1–125 µM with the line equation of ΔA = 0.0038 C + 0.1145, where ΔA is the difference of absorbance in the presence and absence of glucose, a LOD of 0.96 µM. In contrast, the spectrofluorimetric method provided a superior sensitivity for H₂O₂, achieving a broad linear range of 0.1–50 µM and an ultra-low LOD of 64 nM Similarly, for glucose detection, as exhibited in Fig. 4c, d, this method exhibited a linear range of 0.1–50 µM with an LOD of 87 nM. The repeatability and reproducibility of the techniques were examined within-day and between-day, respectively, and the results are shown in Table S4 (ESI).

The development of efficient and selective detection systems for H₂O₂, a key biomarker in various physiological and pathological processes, has attracted significant attention in recent years. Several strategies have been proposed based on metal based nanocatalysts, POMs, and hybrid nanostructures, each employing different methodologies and demonstrating varied sensing performances. In the colorimetric mode, the GOx/Ni-CoWO₄ system demonstrated a linear range of 0.05–1.5 mM with excellent selectivity. While highly versatile, the reliance on enzyme immobilization increases operational complexity and reduces long-term stability under varying conditions. Compared to previous approaches Table S2 (ESI), our proposed Ni-CoWO₄ based nanozyme combines the advantages of polyoxometalates and transition-metal oxides while overcoming several limitations. The synergistic effect between Ni and Co enhances electron transfer and active site density, resulting in significantly faster oxidation of OPD and improved colorimetric response for H₂O₂ detection. Additionally, our system demonstrates superior sensitivity, broader linear ranges, and enhanced selectivity against common interfering species, making it more suitable for real time applications in complex biological and environmental matrices. This comparative analysis highlights the evolution of methodologies and performances across existing detection strategies while underscoring the need for a nanozyme with high catalytic efficiency, robust stability, and simplified operational requirements. The long-term stability of the Ni–CoWO₄ nanozyme was evaluated over 15 days, shown in Fig. S9. during which it retained a high proportion of its initial catalytic activity, indicating good stability under storage conditions. A slight decrease in activity at extended times may be attributed to surface oxidation and partial nanoparticle aggregation. The Ni-CoWO₄-based approach introduced in this work addresses these challenges and provides a promising platform for next generation colorimetric and spectrofluorometric sensing.

The interference studies were conducted with respect to hydrogen peroxide detection. Glucose was not directly determined by the proposed sensing system. These results show that these chemicals do not interfere with optimal reaction at their typical concentrations in real samples.

Smartphone-based detection of H2O2 and glucose

The distinct color change in the OPD-H2O2 colorimetric sensor served as the basis for designing a smartphone-based sensor for in situ, fast, and simple detection of H2O2/Glucose. In detection by smartphone color change in the system track by smartphone camera, and the photo’s color is resolved into its main components (CMYK or RGB). Then, the average value of these parameters or the sharpest change in the parameter is chosen as the analytical signal. Figure 5 represents the response range of H2O2/glucose in both the CMYK and RGB systems. Both systems assisted in the development of colorimetric and fluorimetric sensors. Based on RGB data, the best linear response was in blue (B) color change, while the H2O2 changed; the green (G) change had a minor response, and the red (R) remained unchanged. As shown in Fig. 5A and C, the linear range of the RGB method based on B value was 1–100 µM for both the colorimetric and fluorimetric assays. In the CMYK method, only the yellow color (Y) change was distinct, and the other parameters’ response was not consistent or remained unchanged. The linear range of H2O2 in the CMYK method was 0.1 to 100 and 20 to 100 µM for colorimetric and fluorimetric determination, respectively (Fig. 5B and D). Interferences of the 425 lamp and its reflection limit the fluorimetric method’s LOQ. In the smartphone-based fluorimetric configuration, residual stray reflections from the 425 nm excitation source can introduce baseline fluctuations and thereby influence the LOQ. While optimization of the optical geometry mitigates part of this effect, future implementations incorporating narrow band optical filtering or improved excitation emission spatial separation are expected to substantially enhance signal fidelity and analytical performance. The oxidized OPD product (DAP) exhibits dual chromophoric and fluorophoric behavior, with well-defined absorption and emission bands that contribute to its high analytical sensitivity.

Real samples analysis

To evaluate the practical applicability of the developed Ni-CoWO₄ based colorimetric method for H2O2 and glucose detection, three different real samples, such as human serum, orange soda, and apple juice, were analyzed using both spectrophotometric and fluorescence methods, as summarized in Table S3 (ESI) & Table 2. Recovery experiments were performed by spiking known concentrations. The results indicated that these methods are simple, rapid, cost effective, and user friendly platforms. The concentration of analyte in unspiked real samples was calculated by interpolating the measured response on the external calibration curve, and the accuracy of the results was further verified using a single-point standard addition method to minimize potential matrix effects. The serum samples were analyzed in both spiked and unspiked conditions to evaluate the accuracy of the proposed method. Statistical analysis using the t-test showed no significant difference between the measured and expected values for the spiked samples, confirming the reliability of the method for glucose determination in real serum matrices. A slight negative bias was observed in the H₂O₂ recovery results, which may be attributed to matrix effects and diffusion-related limitations in real sample environments. However, the recovery values remained within an acceptable range, confirming the reliability of the proposed method for practical applications (Table 2).

Conclusion

In summary, Ni-CoWO₄ nanoparticles were successfully synthesized through a facile hydrothermal method and exhibited pronounced peroxidase like catalytic activity toward the oxidation of OPD in the presence of H₂O₂. The incorporation of both Ni and Co within the tungstate framework provided synergistic effects that enhanced electron transfer efficiency and catalytic performance. The developed dual mode sensing platform enabled the sensitive and selective detection of H₂O₂ and glucose using colorimetric, fluorimetric, and smartphone-assisted approaches, offering both analytical reliability and practical applicability. The method demonstrated excellent analytical characteristics, including wide linear detection ranges, low limits of detection, good repeatability, and satisfactory stability. In addition, its applicability was validated through the successful analysis of real samples, confirming its robustness in complex matrices. Overall, these results highlight the significant potential of Ni-CoWO₄ nanozymes as efficient, low cost, and versatile candidates for practical sensing applications in biomedical diagnostics, food safety, and environmental monitoring.
