Glucocorticoid exposure and the risk of serious infections in rheumatoid arthritis: a marginal structural model application
Authors: Andrei Barbulescu, Arvid Sjölander, Bénédicte Delcoigne, Johan Askling, Thomas Frisell
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveObservational studies have reported an increased risk of infections associated with glucocorticoids in RA, not supported by evidence from randomized controlled trials. Inappropriately accommodating time-varying exposure and confounding in observational studies might explain the conflicting results. Therefore, we compared the incidence of serious infections between different oral glucocorticoid dose patterns over three years in a prospective inception cohort, adjusting for time-varying confounders in marginal structural models.MethodsWe included 9654 newly diagnosed RA patients from the Swedish Rheumatology Quality Register between 2007–2018 and followed them for three years after the first rheumatology visit. Follow-up was divided into 90-day periods. A mean oral prednisone daily dose was calculated for each period and categorized into ‘no use’, ‘low’ (≤10 mg/day) and ‘high’ (>10 mg/day) doses. The incidence of serious infections (hospitalization for infection) over follow-up periods was modelled by pooled logistic regression allowing separate effects for recent and past exposure.ResultsAn increased incidence of serious infections was associated with higher compared with lower doses and with more recent compared with past glucocorticoid exposure. Over 3 years of follow-up, the marginal structural models predicted one additional serious infection for every 83 individuals treated with low GC doses for the first 6 months, and for every 125 individuals treated with high GC doses for the first 3 months, compared with no GC use.ConclusionOur results broadly agree with previous observational studies showing a dose dependent increased risk of infection associated with (recent) use of oral glucocorticoids.Keywords: RA, glucocorticoids, infections, marginal structural models

Introduction

More than 70 years have passed since the first administration of a glucocorticoid (GC) to a patient with RA [1], and conclusive evidence has since accumulated showing that these inexpensive drugs provide strong symptomatic relief and may reduce joint damage [2–4], making GC a mainstay of RA therapy [5, 6].

Despite this, uncertainty remains regarding the risk–benefit balance of GC use in rheumatology [7, 8]. Although generally accepted to cause a series of adverse outcomes, including infections, diabetes and osteoporosis, GC may also counteract RA disease-related processes involved in inducing such outcomes [9, 10], and there is little consensus on how large these risks are in clinical practice, or how they depend on patterns of changing doses over time [11]. Reflecting this uncertainty, current treatment guidelines recommend short-term use of low-dose GC [12–14] whereas some authors maintain that this practice may deprive patients of an affordable and effective treatment based only on weak evidence [8, 15].

A comprehensive review and meta-analysis of studies addressing the risk of infections associated with GC use in RA found conflicting evidence [16]. Comparing GC use with no-use, the pooled risk-ratio from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was 1.0 (0.7–1.4) while those from cohort and case-control studies were 1.6 (1.4–1.7) and 2.0 (1.6–2.4), respectively [16]. It has been noted that RCTs, primarily designed to study efficacy, suffered from non-systematic and incomplete reporting of infection events, which combined with small sample size and short follow-up would reduce power [16, 17]. Additionally, RCTs are known to include selected populations to maximize the benefit–risk ratio of interventions. They may include individuals younger than the general RA population [18], and without important infection risk factors such as neoplasms, diabetes or renal insufficiency [19, 20]. The average effect of GC on the risk on infections could be different in such selected populations compared with patients treated in clinical practice and included in observational studies. Alternatively, the difference may be a result of the inadequate handling of the biases present in non-randomized studies.

In rheumatology clinical practice, GC are initiated as ‘bridging therapy’ with slow-onset conventional-synthetic DMARDs or at inflammation flares and are meant to be tapered to discontinuation within 3–6 months (longer in the Swedish guidelines) and restarted when necessary [21–23]. Observational studies have responded to this complex dose pattern with a great heterogeneity in how exposure was defined, including: time-fixed exposure measured using information before baseline [24, 25] but also after baseline [26, 27]; time-varying exposure considering current dose alone [28, 29], or including historical exposure measured within various time windows [30–33]. Allowing exposure to vary over time may reflect real-world GC use more accurately but it requires careful confounding adjustment. Adjusting only for baseline confounding values may be insufficient if GC dose changes after baseline are influenced by time-varying patient characteristics. Less generally recognized, adjusting for time-varying covariates by including them in a conventional regression model may also induce bias when they are affected by past exposure [34]. Marginal structural models (MSM) estimated using inverse probability weighting (IPW) have been proposed to correctly estimate exposure effects in the presence of time-varying confounding, exposure feedback [35].

We explored whether, by appropriately accommodating time-varying confounding, we would estimate an effect of GC use on the risk of serious infections in RA closer to the null effect reported in most RCTs. Therefore, we aimed to contrast the incidence of serious infections between early RA patients exposed to different GC dose histories, adjusting for a broad range of time-fixed and time-varying potential confounders using MSM, and to compare results with adjustments in standard regression models.

Patients and methods

We conducted a cohort study of RA patients with data prospectively collected in the Swedish Rheumatology Quality register (SRQ) [36]. Additional data were obtained from other Swedish national registers, linked via the personal identification number of each patient [37].

All RA patients whose first recorded visit in the SRQ was less than one year from RA debut and contained data on Disease Activity Score 28 using CRP protein (DAS28-CRP) and HAQ Disability Index (HAQ) were considered for inclusion in the study. Patients who had experienced serious infections within 90 days before the first SRQ visit or between this visit and baseline (defined below) were excluded from the study. The recruitment period was from January 2007 to February 2018, to ensure a minimum of 18 months before study-entry (data available from all sources starting July 2005) and a follow-up of 3 years (data availability ended February 2021).

Follow-up

Baseline was set 90 days after the initial SRQ visit, to allow assessment of exposure as outlined below. Each participant was followed from baseline to the first outcome event, death, emigration or to the end of the 3-year follow-up. To enable weighted modelling over time, the follow-up of each participant was split into 90-day periods. Thus, the unit of observation was the contribution of a participant to a 90-day risk-set; each participant could contribute with a maximum of 12 such observations (study design described in Supplementary Fig. S1, available at Rheumatology online).

Outcome

The outcome event was ‘serious infections’ defined as hospitalization with a main diagnosis of infection recorded in the National Patient Register [38] or death with infection as contributory cause, as identified in the Causes of Death Register [39] (list of ICD-10 codes in Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology online).

Exposure

A time-varying GC mean daily dose was estimated by dividing the total quantity of GC collected during a 90-day window before the start of each follow-up period by the length of the window. Prescriptions for systemic GCs (ATC: H02AB) were extracted from the Prescribed Drugs Register [40], which contains the dispensation date and drug quantity for all prescriptions dispensed by community pharmacies in Sweden. As the great majority of GC was administered orally, exposure was restricted to oral GC. All GC quantities were transformed into prednisone equivalents (PEQ) and daily doses were categorized into: ‘no GC’, ‘low GC dose’ (≤10 mg/day) and ‘high GC dose’ (>10 mg/day).

Potential confounders

Covariates selected for confounding adjustment were risk factors for infection, based on previous literature [7, 41] or other determinants of GC treatment decision associated with the outcome. The following time-fixed and time-varying confounders were measured before each GC assessment window: age, sex, country of origin, baseline education level, baseline rheumatoid factor positivity, GC exposure before baseline, RA duration at GC evaluation, current disease activity (DAS28-CRP), current disability (HAQ-disability index), co-medication (with biological or synthetic DMARDs), a history of several comorbid conditions and the days spend in hospital within one year before each GC assessment window. Detailed definitions of all covariates are presented in Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology online.

In the main analysis, the latest available values of DAS28-CRP and HAQ were used at each period (last observation carried forward) to avoid missing data (Supplementary Table S2, available at Rheumatology online).

Statistical analysis

Pooled logistic regression was used to model the incidence of serious infections in each period (discrete time hazard) as a function of GC exposure history [42]. Briefly, we treated each 90-day follow-up period as a separate observation and modelled the log-odds of infection over all periods as a function of exposure history, and of follow-up time itself (categorical indicator of period, 1 through 12) to allow for a time-varying baseline hazard. The odds-ratios (OR) obtained from the pooled logistic regression model can be interpreted as hazard ratios (HR) from an equivalent Cox regression. Confidence limits for HR were estimated using robust standard errors.

Exposure definition and parametrization

To specify one outcome model over all follow-up periods, we fixed the length of exposure history to three years and set the value of GC exposure before the study to zero, adjusting for actual GC use before baseline in a separate variable (details in Supplementary Data S1, available at Rheumatology online).

We explored several exposure history parametrizations. First GC exposure history was summarized in three parsimonious models. Then, independent effects for categorical exposure in the current plus 11 past periods were allowed in a more flexible model.

Bias adjustment

First, three progressive ‘conventional’ adjustments were made by including confounders directly in the outcome regression models together with exposure history. A crude (unadjusted) model was followed by adjustments for baseline confounder values and finally for updated values of time-varying confounders.

Because conditioning on time-updated confounder values could introduce bias in the presence of exposure-confounder feedback, we subsequently adjusted for confounding and selection bias (via informative censoring) in MSM using stabilized IPW (details in Supplementary Data S2, available at Rheumatology online) [35, 42].

The distribution of IPW is presented in Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology online. IPW were truncated to the first and 99th percentiles of their distribution in each period.

Cumulative incidence

The flexible exposure model was used for scoring GC dose patterns of interest in order to predict the corresponding probabilities of not having experienced an infection in each period of follow-up. Survival from baseline up to and including each period was then calculated by multiplying these probabilities over periods, and finally the cumulative incidence of serious infection was calculated as 1–survival [43].

Confidence limits around cumulative incidence curves were estimated using non-parametric bootstrapping, in 1000 randomly re-drawn samples, with each patient as the sampling unit. The IPW estimation and outcome model were reproduced in each bootstrap sample obtaining a distribution of estimates of which the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles were used as 95% confidence limits.

Supplementary analyses

In a supplementary analysis, missing DAS28/HAQ values were handled according to the ‘missingness patterns’ approach [44]. Briefly, it was assumed that only measured (non-missing) covariates influenced treatment (and censoring) for each patient, thus only measured covariates were included in each subject’s exposure (and censoring) model. This entails different exposure models for different groups of patients, according to the available data. The method is described in more detail in Supplementary Data S3, available at Rheumatology online.

In a second supplementary analysis, hazard ratios of serious infections were presented stratified over three calendar periods: before 2012, between 2012 and 2015, and after 2015.

Ethics

The study was conducted in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Ethical Review Board in Stockholm (DNR: 2015/1844–31/2, amended 2016/1986–32, 2017/2473–32, 2020–01756). In accordance with the Swedish law, participant consent is not necessary for register-based studies with pseudo-anonymized data.

In 62% of the 110 327 periods, patients did not use GC, in 28% of periods they used low GC doses and in 10% of periods they used high GC doses. Over time, there was a clear trend towards GC discontinuation and dose reduction (Supplementary Table S3, available at Rheumatology online). The median age at GC assessment was 2–3 years higher among patients exposed to GC than among patients not exposed and patients using high GC doses were more likely male. GC exposure was associated with an increased prevalence of various comorbidities, with the exception of diabetes where the association was reversed, and a dose-response relationship was observed. Current disease activity and disability (DAS28-CRP and HAQ) were also positively correlated with the GC dose used. There was a ‘U-shaped’ association between the use of biological DMARDs around GC-assessment window start and the current GC dose, with higher biological DMARD use among patients not treated with GC or among those treated with high doses.

Over the 3 years of follow-up, 740 (∼8%) of the 9654 patients suffered serious infections, 208 patients died from other causes than infections and were censored and 17 patients were lost due to emigration.

A dose–response relationship was observed through exposure models and adjustments, with higher HRs for high (>10 mg/day) compared with low (≤10 mg/day) doses vs no GC. Current use was more strongly associated with the risk of infection than past use, but including the accumulated past GC use in the outcome model alongside current GC use decreased the association with current use (models 2 vs model 1, in Table 2). The attenuation was stronger when dividing past use into recent and distant use (model 3). After adjusting for accumulated past use, current high (but not low) dose remained significantly associated with an increased risk of infection. Over exposure models 1, 2 and 3, the MSM (IPW adjusted) HR for current GC exposure changed from 1.42–1.16 and 1.10 for low dose, and from 1.99–1.59 and 1.46 for high dose (vs ‘no GC’).

The cumulative exposure (to either low or high GC doses) during the most recent year was also associated with an increase in the current hazard of infection while earlier exposure was not (model 3, Table 2).

HRs were substantially reduced by confounding adjustment, but baseline covariate values accounted for most of this attenuation, with only a small additional impact of adjustment for time-updated covariate values (adjustment 2 vs adjustment 1 in Table 2). Moreover, the method used to adjust for time-varying covariates had little impact: ‘conventional confounding adjustment’ by adding time-updated confounder values to the regression model produced identical or slightly lower HRs compared with IPW adjustment in MSM (adjustment 2 vs MSM, in Table 2).

Supplementary analysis

There were 69 231 observations with both DAS28 and HAQ missing, 38 855 observations with both DAS28 and HAQ observed, 1054 observations with DAS28 observed and HAQ missing and 1187 observations with DAS28 missing and HAQ observed. Applying the ‘missingness patterns’ approach, different exposure/censoring models (used for estimating IPW) were estimated within each of these subsamples. The logistic regression likelihood maximization algorithms had problems converging in the smaller data sets. Nonetheless, IPW-adjusted HRs were similar to those obtained via the ‘last observation carried forward’ approach (Supplementary Figs S3C vs S3A, available at Rheumatology online).

Results remained consistent across calendar periods (Supplementary Table S4, available at Rheumatology online), though the increased risk of infection was seemingly shifted from ‘current’ to ‘recent’ exposure among patients who entered the study after 2015. This is probably a statistical artefact as the two exposure periods are highly co-linear.

Discussion

In the current study we used nationwide, prospectively collected Swedish register data on patients with recent onset RA and estimated the association between GC use over a follow-up of 3 years and the risk of developing serious infections. We expanded on earlier studies by adjusting for confounding and selection bias using longitudinal data on a range of covariates, including disease activity and disability, in MSM.

Our results confirm an association between GC treatment and an increased risk of serious infections, with a magnitude dependent on dose and temporal pattern of administration, broadly in line with previous observational studies. One recent cohort study assessing the effect of a time-varying oral GC exposure on the risk of infection (any cause) in patients with polymyalgia rheumatica or giant cell arteritis patients reported HRs between 1.4, for current dose <5 mg prednisone/day and 2.3 for current dose >25 mg prednisone/day compared with no use [30]. A recent case-control study of RA patients reported an overall OR for serious infections of 1.6 for any current GC use within 180 days before index date vs no use, with a dose–response relationship, and a null association for earlier GC use [32]. In another case-control study, Dixon et al. showed that the risk of serious infection is associated with GC exposure within one year before index date, the association diminishing for earlier exposure [33]. Our results (Table 2, Model 3) agree that the association approaches the null for GC exposure more than one year before risk assessment. However, HRs for past exposure should be interpreted cautiously as they may be affected by selection bias [45], a problem that does not impact the cumulative incidence.

An increased risk of infection for patients treated with glucocorticoids even at low doses was confirmed in the recent GLORIA pragmatic trial where elderly patients were treated with prednisolone 5 mg/day or placebo over two years [46].

We observed a gradual increase in the cumulative incidence of serious infections with increased duration of exposure to a low GC dose. However, the increments were small, the cumulative incidence at three years increasing from 5.5% under no GC exposure (Fig. 1), to 6.2% under low dose in the first three months, 6.3% under high dose in the first three months, 6.7% under low dose exposure in the first six months, 7.1% under low dose in the first nine months (Fig. 2) and 7.4% under low dose in the first year (Fig. 1).

Conventional adjustment for baseline confounder values yielded similar results to adjustment for time-updated confounder values via IPW (i.e. MSM). Conventional adjustment for time-updated covariate values resulted in lower HRs compared with IPW adjustment, potentially implying unintentional blocking of mediated effects or opening collider pathways. However, the differences were minor. This may be considered comforting with regard to previous studies that used conventional adjustment methods.

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. Accurately measuring the current use of GC is difficult, as in clinical practice GC doses are adjusted at irregular intervals, and we lacked reliable data on the intended dosing. For each 90-day follow-up period we used GC quantities dispensed within the previous 90 days to estimate mean daily doses. The 90-day window matches the pattern of filling prescriptions for prolonged treatments in Sweden (3-month supply per dispensation), and this window length produced the most credible dose estimates after testing 30 and 120 days. Due to uncertainty in the estimated mean daily doses, these were categorized into three levels.

Shorter follow-up periods (e.g. 30 instead of 90 days) would allow more frequent GC dose updating and would minimize the period between current dose and outcome events. However, this comes at the price of potentially more extreme IPWs (due to accumulation of inverse probabilities over many time points) which would require more truncation, and longer processing times (due to more observations). We observed stabilized weights as high as 483 at later time points (Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology online) but extreme weights were rare and truncation to the 1 and 99 percentiles of the IPW distribution at each time produced similar (but more precise) estimates compared with using non-truncated IPWs (Supplementary Figs S3A vs S3B, available at Rheumatology online).

Another limitation was the incomplete longitudinal data on DAS28-CRP and HAQ-DI (Supplementary Table S2, available at Rheumatology online). Multiple imputation would have been the ideal method for handling missing data [44] but it proved too computationally demanding in our study with large longitudinal data. Therefore, in the main analysis we carried forward the latest available DAS28/HAQ value, regardless of how early it was measured. This imputation is valid under the assumption that the latest available DAS28/HAQ values was used at each treatment decision, no matter how long in the past these were measured. In a sensitivity analysis we investigated the ‘missingness patterns’ approach, where it was assumed that treatment decision was only influenced by variables measured recently (Supplementary Data S3, available at Rheumatology online). Despite the distinct assumptions, the ‘missingness patterns’ approach generated similar results to those obtained by ‘last observation carried forward’. If imputation assumptions were incorrect, the resulting measurement error would leave residual unadjusted confounding. Moreover, as in all observational studies, unmeasured confounding is possible despite adjusting for a diversity of variables and could possibly account for the weak association between low GC doses and increased risk of infection.

The main strengths of our study are access to a broad range of prospectively recorded longitudinal variables (including RA activity/disability) via several linked Swedish national registers and using a methodology which allows (under certain assumptions) to estimate the joint effect of a time-varying exposure history without bias. Additionally, by modelling independent effects of exposure at different times we allowed more recent exposure to have a different effect on the outcome than past exposure. Using this flexible marginal structural model of exposure history, we estimated contrasts between potential cumulative incidences under specific GC dose patterns of clinical relevance.

In conclusion, our results indicate a dose-dependent effect of GC treatment on increasing the risk of serious infections in RA, in line with previous findings from observational studies. We also observed that the accumulated dose in the past year, in addition to the current dose, was associated with the increased infection risk, while doses administered earlier than one year ago were not.

In our study the theoretically more appropriate adjustment through MSMs for various covariates including RA activity/disability did not reduce the association between GC use and infection risks. Together with the increased infection risk seen among senior patients in the recent GLORIA trial, this could suggest that the difference in findings between earlier RCTs and observational studies may be more impacted by differences in the patient population than by confounding or the statistical methods used. Although this seeming disagreement remains to be fully explained, our study adds further evidence that supports the current guidelines of ‘lowest effective dose for the shortest duration’ when using GC in RA. It must also be recognized that a modest increase in the risk of serious infections, especially under low doses for less than one year, may be counterbalanced by the benefits of treatment.
