Robotic mini- or less open sublay for anterior abdominal wall hernia repair using the da Vinci single port robotic platform (SP MILOS): a preclinical feasibility study
Authors: Alexander D Schroeder, Jorge L Florin, Christopher J Johnson, David C Chen, Conrad Ballecer, Michael Schroeder, Clark Gerhart, Wolfgang Reinpold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEndoscopic Mini or Less Open Sublay (eMILOS) is a transhernial endoscopically assisted approach for anterior abdominal wall hernia repair that has demonstrated excellent long-term outcomes. Performed through a small skin incision directly over the hernia, the eMILOS technique minimizes additional access trauma, but remains technically demanding with conventional instruments. This study investigates the preclinical feasibility of adapting the da Vinci SP system to perform the transhernial SP MILOS technique.MethodsThis preclinical study evaluated the technical feasibility of performing daVinci SP MILOS hernia repair using human cadavers. The da Vinci SP robotic platform and its standard instruments were employed. Procedural steps, technical considerations, limitations, and potential advantages were systematically documented.ResultsThe procedure was completed through a 1.8-cm skin incision. Correct placement of the Access Port (AP) and defining the custom remote center (CRC) at the posterior fascial edge was essential for optimal trocar orientation and to limit collisions with the incision. The AP can be positioned below the skin or below the fascia depending on work flow and surgeon preference. The retrorectus space was developed robotically from xiphoid to pubis, with full 360° transhernial exposure. Transversus abdominis release and lateral dissection into the preperitoneal and pretransversalis planes were successfully achieved.ConclusionIn this preclinical cadaveric study, the SP MILOS technique provided 360° transhernial access to the retrorectus space without platform repositioning. Our initial experience supports its feasibility as a new single-port robotic option for ventral hernia repair, eliminating access trauma to the abdominal wall through a single, minimal skin incision.Keywords: Incisional hernia, Ventral hernia, MILOS, Robotic single port

Introduction

Ventral and incisional hernia repair is among the most frequently performed procedures in general surgery, with a lifetime risk approaching 20% following laparotomy [1]. Recently, minimally invasive approaches for extraperitoneal mesh placement have gained popularity. One of the first reported minimally invasive techniques for retromuscular mesh placement is the endoscopic mini- or less-open sublay (E/MILOS) operation, described by Reinpold et al. [2]. This technique combines a transhernial approach with endoscopic visualization and dissection, allowing mesh placement in the favorable retromuscular or preperitoneal planes while minimizing soft-tissue trauma. Comparative studies have demonstrated low recurrence rates and reduced postoperative chronic pain following E/MILOS relative to open sublay and laparoscopic intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) techniques, with durable outcomes up to 5 years postoperatively [3, 4]. However, E/MILOS can require a slightly larger skin incision than traditional multi-port laparoscopic or robotic techniques and is technically demanding when performed with conventional instruments. In the United States, robotic minimally invasive techniques have been adopted more rapidly due to the widespread availability of multi-port robotic platforms, whereas in Europe—where robotic access is more limited—E/MILOS is more commonly utilized.

The da Vinci SP robotic platform represents the latest evolution in minimally invasive technology, offering a unique single-incision system with four working channels for a 3DHD endoscope and instruments that have both wrists and elbows that allow separation to prevent internal collisions common with single incision procedures. Currently approved for urologic, colorectal, thoracic, transanal and transoral procedures, the SP platform has shown increasing promise in general abdominal surgery [5, 6]. Although clinical data for ventral hernia repair remain limited, De Troyer et al. reported the preclinical feasibility of SP-assisted suprapubic extended totally extraperitoneal (eTEP) repair, establishing proof of concept for extraperitoneal applications [7]. Kim and Han described the first SP-assisted eTEP with transversus abdominis release (TAR) for a lateral ventral hernia, indicating technical feasibility and favorable clinical short-term outcomes [8].

To date, however, no published studies have described the application of the da Vinci SP Surgical System for mini- or less-open sublay (SP MILOS) hernia repair.

We hypothesize that adapting the da Vinci SP platform for the MILOS technique can deliver the benefits of a fully robotic, minimally invasive extraperitoneal repair while maintaining optimal visualization and dexterity. Specifically, we expect the SP MILOS approach to:

Eliminate additional port-site and fascial trauma by using a single, small skin incision for a transhernial approach;

Provide full robotic articulation and 3D visualization within the retromuscular plane;

Enable continuous 360° access to the retromuscular planes without repositioning of the robotic system.

Together, these features may enhance ergonomics, minimize tissue trauma, and expand the applicability of minimally invasive retromuscular hernia repair with its associated benefits in patient recovery.

Methods

Study design

A preclinical feasibility study was conducted using the da Vinci SP Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA, February 3rd, 2024) to evaluate the technical application of the robotic single port mini- or less-open sublay (SP MILOS) technique for anterior abdominal wall hernia repair. The study was performed in a human cadaver laboratory under the guidance of Intuitive Surgical clinical specialists. Prior to cadaveric dissection, participating surgeons completed a structured didactic and hands-on orientation session focused on system setup, docking workflow, and single-port instrument control.

Cadaveric dissections

Two human cadavers were used for this study. Each dissection was performed sequentially by the same surgical team to ensure procedural consistency. Observations and technical findings were documented systematically. Key procedural domains assessed included:

Initial SP port placement and ergonomics of access site selection,

Dissection maneuverability with particular attention to 360° visualization and instrument range,

Dissection maneuverability with particular attention to 360° visualization and instrument range,

Extension of the dissection into the pretransversalis and preperitoneal planes during transversus abdominis release (TAR), and.

Extension of the dissection into the pretransversalis and preperitoneal planes during transversus abdominis release (TAR), and.

Assessment of external and internal collisions during full instrument articulation.

Robotic platform and instrumentation

The da Vinci SP Surgical System is a single-port robotic platform designed to facilitate complex minimally invasive procedures through a single small incision. The system comprises three primary components:

Surgeon console, providing 3-dimensional high-definition visualization and wristed instrument control;

Surgeon console, providing 3-dimensional high-definition visualization and wristed instrument control;

Vision cart, integrating the imaging and energy subsystems; and.

Vision cart, integrating the imaging and energy subsystems; and.

Patient cart, which houses a robotic arm with an instrument guide that accommodates a camera and up to three wristed instruments with both wrists and elbow articulation.

Patient cart, which houses a robotic arm with an instrument guide that accommodates a camera and up to three wristed instruments with both wrists and elbow articulation.

The da Vinci SP Access Port is a balloon-type system that can be placed either subcutaneously or below the anterior rectus sheath, enabling secure extra-abdominal fixation of the SP cannula. A variety of SP instruments are available, including atraumatic graspers, high grip strength graspers, scissors, and monopolar and bipolar cautery devices and a Sureform 45 mm stapler. For this procedure the fenestrated bipolar forceps and monopolar scissors, were utilized. Carbon dioxide insufflation through the AP was used to establish and maintain the extraperitoneal workspace.

Data collection and analysis

Technical feasibility was evaluated qualitatively based on procedural completion, freedom of movement, and quality of exposure in each anatomic plane. Limitations and ergonomic challenges were recorded in real time. Representative photographs and videos were obtained to document key procedural steps and spatial relationships.

Results

Access and setup

In the first cadaver, a 1.8-cm skin incision was made (Fig. 1), followed by a 9-cm circumferential subcutaneous dissection to simulate a hernia sac. A 5-cm fascial defect was then created to represent the hernia opening. In the second cadaver, a pre-existing 3-cm umbilical hernia was present; a 2.0 cm skin incision was carried out, the hernia sac was dissected free and reduced below the fascia. In both models, the internal ring of the da Vinci SP AP was initially placed in the subcutaneous space (Fig. 2).

AP ring placed in subcutaneous tissue

Open dissection through a 2-cm incision offers limited visibility but allows familiar tactile identification of the retrorectus plane, which can then be expanded digitally for AP placement. Robotic subcutaneous dissection improves visualization and dexterity but relies on visual cues to distinguish the peritoneal sac (Fig. 3), subcutaneous fat, and fascial edges, with some restriction from single-port geometry. In practice, surgeons will likely combine efficient mini open and robotic retromuscular dissection depending on experience and hernia characteristics.

Dissection of subcutaneous space

When the retrorectus space is initially developed in open fashion, the internal ring of the AP can be placed directly beneath the fascia, minimizing instrument clashing. With robotic subcutaneous dissection, the ring may remain in the subcutaneous plane or be advanced behind the rectus muscle, though this increases instrument angulation. In large defects exceeding ring size, subcutaneous placement is required but provides favorable working angles due to the expanded space.

Optimal - positioning of the custom remote center (CRC) at the posterior skin edge was essential for optimal trocar orientation and to limit collisions with the incision allowing instrument freedom of movement in the subcutaneous space. Flexing the operating table improved spatial alignment between the robotic arm and the abdominal wall, helping to prevent both internal and external collisions, as well as patient clearance for safety (Fig. 4). Awareness of the robotic arm’s spatial movement relative to the patient is essential and initially required feedback from a second bedside surgeon.

Patient and daVinci SP positioning

Retrorectus access and dissection

Entry into the retrorectus space was achieved by grasping the anterior rectus sheath with the fenestrated bipolar forceps and incising the posterior lamella under direct three-dimensional visualization (Fig. 5). The custom remote center (CRC) was repositioned to the posterior fascial edge for optimal trocar orientation and to limit collisions with the incision in the retrorectus space. Triangulation of the instruments can be challenging initially in a confined working space. Using the da Vinci SP AP is essential to overcome this limitation. The robotic arm transitioned from an initially vertical orientation at entry to a more horizontal working configuration, when beneath the fascia. This maneuver required knowledge of the daVinci SP system’s control modes but became intuitive after brief familiarization.

Entering the retrorectus space

The retrorectus plane was developed smoothly using primarily blunt dissection, with selective use of sharp and energy-assisted techniques. The da Vinci SP camera’s “Cobra pose”, helps retract muscle upward. Using the camera intentionally for retraction purposes is unique to this approach and required some practice. Critical anatomic landmarks—including the arcuate line (Fig. 6), EIT ambivium/junction of external oblique, internal oblique, and transverse abdominis muscles (Fig. 7), and pubic bone (Fig. 8) —were clearly identified throughout the dissection. The daVinci SP system’s high-definition visualization and wristed instrumentation allowed consistent identification and preservation of the lateral neurovascular bundles supplying the rectus muscle.

Closing of fascia and mesh placement

Any posterior peritoneal defects are closed. Closure of the posterior sheath is optional when tension-free. Because the da Vinci SP port is centered within the defect, closure must be performed on the inferior half with the robotic arm oriented inferiorly, and vice versa for the superior half (Fig. 9). Applying tension to the suture during fascial reapproximation was modestly more challenging compared with the conventional multi-port robotic platform and appeared to generate slightly less forceful traction. Most sutures and mesh implants can be inserted through a 12-mm trocar via the AP (Fig. 10), with the mesh typically rolled lengthwise for easier passage and deployment. The AP may be detached and placed directly through the fascial opening at this point. Mesh fixation is generally unnecessary, as decompression secures the mesh within the well-defined retrorectus space. Closure of the anterior sheath (Fig. 11) requires the internal ring of the AP to be positioned subcutaneously, as it cannot remain within the fascial defect during closure. Alternatively, surgeons may close the superior and inferior portions robotically up to the AP’s circular wound retractor, then complete the remaining closure in open fashion.

Range of motion and spatial access

The da Vinci SP platform provided excellent dexterity and a wide range of motion within the retromuscular compartment, permitting complete bilateral retrorectus dissections in both cadavers from the symphysis pubis all the way to the xiphoid. Optimal camera positioning and instrument triangulation are more challenging at the beginning of the procedure but become easier as the extraperitoneal space is developed. In one specimen, the dissection was extended laterally into the transversus abdominis release (TAR) plane using a top-down approach, demonstrating the ability to extend the extraperitoneal dissection through the same single-port approach.

The SP system provided 360° circumferential access to the retromuscular space allowing you to work in all four quadrants from one incision. The most efficient technique to achieve complete circumferential access was beginning in the clockwise direction, completing > 10% of the dissection circumference and then in counterclockwise dissection, until reaching the initial > 10° dissection.

Incision and integrity

Upon completion, both retrorectus spaces and preperitoneal space behind the linea alba were fully developed, and the skin incision remained uncompromised, with no evidence of stretch or trauma from cannula torque (Fig. 12).

Discussion

In this preclinical cadaveric evaluation of the SP MILOS technique for anterior abdominal wall hernia repair, the findings illustrate a promising intersection between the established endoscopic mini- or less-open sublay repair (E/MILOS) and the latest robotic single-port platform, da Vinci SP. Our findings support the feasibility of complete transhernial access to the extraperitoneal anatomic planes while minimizing additional fascial access trauma. Although E/MILOS is a technically demanding procedure, SP MILOS may streamline its execution by leveraging the enhanced dexterity and three-dimensional visualization of the da Vinci SP system.

A recent scoping review by Celotto et al. identified 56 published studies on the SP platform in general surgery as of March 2024, encompassing colorectal, cholecystectomy, and abdominal wall applications [9]. Within the domain of hernia and abdominal wall reconstruction, available literature is still limited. Bianco et al. reported the first general-surgery experience using the SP system—including inguinal and ventral hernia cases—demonstrating procedural feasibility [5]. Kim et al. described the first SP-assisted eTEP with TAR for lateral ventral hernia repair, confirming technical feasibility and short-term safety [8]. Most recently, De Troyer et al. evaluated a suprapubic single-port approach for the SP-eTEP technique, demonstrating that suprapubic access enables effective dissection of the retrorectus and preperitoneal spaces—including bilateral component separation via TAR—with adequate instrument range and ergonomic control [7]. Collectively, these studies support the feasibility of SP extraperitoneal access for hernia repair and abdominal wall reconstruction. Overall, clinical outcomes data remain sparse, reflecting the early phase of SP system adoption.

Cost-effectiveness remains an important consideration when investigating robotic approaches for ventral hernia repair. The PROVE-IT randomized controlled trial by Petro et al. showed higher cost with robotic intraperitoneal mesh placement, when compared to standard laparoscopic technique without any difference in outcomes [10], raising valid economic and ethical questions. However, the field of hernia surgery has rapidly evolved with the introduction of advanced robotic techniques that enable mesh placement in all extraperitoneal planes of the abdominal wall. These techniques have gained popularity primarily on the robotic platform, as performing them laparoscopically would be technically extremely demanding for most surgeons. For example, there is significant clinical benefit when comparing robotic TAR to open TAR (a technique not commonly performed laparoscopically) [11]. Henriksen et al. conducted a cost analysis of open vs. robot-assisted incisional hernia repair (most with component separations) and showed cost benefit of the robotic approach due to reduced length of stay and a 4-fold decrease in readmissions and re-operations [12]. The true downstream cost of the robotic approach and its indications remains an important question for future investigations. In the case of SP MILOS, the authors hope to reproduce the long-term benefits to patients shown by Reinpold et al. in terms of extremely low recurrence rates, less complications, and less chronic pain than laparoscopic IPOM or open sublay technique [4]. While E/MILOS is currently performed mostly in expert hands due to its technical difficulty, the da Vinci SP system may be an opportunity to expand its benefits to more patients. Future clinical investigations of SP MILOS should incorporate downstream cost analyses.

As robotic surgery adoption continues to grow and expand the proportion of procedures performed minimally invasively [13], it is essential to consider the implications for surgical educators and trainees. Using a questionnaire for fellows and surgeon educators, Turner et al. reported that teaching and learning of robotic surgery was perceived as superior to laparoscopy, but as disadvantageous compared to open surgery [14]. This disadvantage was mostly attributed to surgeons having to give full control to the trainee during robotic surgery vs. open surgery. The rapid adoption of robotic surgery has also prompted discussion regarding the ongoing requirements for comprehensive training in open and laparoscopic approaches. In a retrospective cohort study by Farah et al., general surgery residents performed better on robotic skills drills when they had prior open and laparoscopic training in foundational tasks such as knot tying, needle driving, and tissue handling, underscoring the importance of continued training across all surgical approaches [15]. In our opinion, mastery of fundamental surgical principles and stepwise execution of an operation remains the cornerstone of surgical training, irrespective of whether it is performed via an open, laparoscopic, or robotic approach. In the case of SP MILOS, there is a unique opportunity to integrate open surgical training with robotic skill development. The initial mini-open access phase allows trainees to practice foundational open techniques, followed by a robotic portion that reproduces the principles of the traditional retromuscular Rives–Stoppa repair. Depending on the experience and comfort level of both the surgical educator and the trainee, the robotic platform can be introduced at different procedural steps. In contrast to common multiport robotic approaches, SP MILOS offers a pathway to combine open and minimally invasive skill sets within a single operation.

Limitations of the SP MILOS approach may include its deployment in ventral hernias with smaller fascial defect sizes (< 2.0 cm), as those might not accommodate transhernial access with multiple robotic instruments. Very large anterior abdominal wall hernias may require a larger skin incision to facilitate efficient hernia sac reduction and optimal exposure, raising the question of whether the da Vinci SP platform offers a meaningful advantage in these cases, when deployed in transhernial fashion over standard endoscopic or hybrid less-open techniques.

Future clinical evaluation is essential, and prospective studies should assess optimal patient selection criteria and perioperative safety outcomes. Initial clinical implementation should focus on smaller, less complex hernias to minimize patient risk. We propose a pilot series evaluating intraoperative metrics, early postoperative recovery, long-term outcomes, and cost-analyses to define the role of SP MILOS within the broader spectrum of minimally invasive abdominal wall reconstruction.

Conclusion

The SP MILOS technique proved technically feasible in a cadaveric model, allowing safe and efficient transhernial access to the retrorectus and TAR planes through a minimal skin incision. The system provided excellent visualization, dexterity, and 360° workspace control without additional ports or re-docking. These findings support further clinical evaluation of SP MILOS as a new option for ventral and incisional hernia repair.
