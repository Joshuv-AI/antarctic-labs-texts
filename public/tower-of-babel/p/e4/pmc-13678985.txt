Herpes zoster risk among female survivors of breast cancer in Japan
Authors: Chitose Kawamura, Kensuke Shimada, Krishnan Bhaskaran, Takaaki Konishi, Yasuaki Sagara, Hiroko Bando, Tomohiro Shinozaki, Shuko Nojiri, Motohiko Adomi, Angel Y. S. Wong, Nanako Tamiya, Masao Iwagami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurvivors of breast cancer (BC) have a higher risk of developing herpes zoster (HZ), but it remains unclear how this risk is affected by time since BC diagnosis and treatment regimen.MethodsThis matched cohort study used a large health-insurance claims database including Japanese employees and their families. Between January 2005 and December 2019, women aged 18–74 years with and without incident BC were matched 1:4 by age and timing of database entry, in the month of BC diagnosis. This study aimed to evaluate the risk of developing HZ in female BC survivors compared with women without BC, to examine temporal variations in risk during survivorship, and to assess the impact of different BC treatments on this risk. We compared the first HZ incidence using stratified Cox regression analyses over the study period, divided by follow-up length. We then estimated HZ risk by treatment regimen using unstratified Cox regression analysis, with follow-up beginning 1 year after matching.ResultsComparing 23,169 BC survivors vs 92,801 matched women without BC (median age: 49.4 years, interquartile range: 44.7–56.0 years), the HZ incidence rate (95% confidence interval) was higher in the survivors (16.5 [15.6–17.5] vs 10.6 [10.3–11.0]/1000 person-years), with an adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of 1.57 (1.46–1.68) over the whole period. This aHR decreased over time but remained elevated up to 10 years (0–1 year: 1.90 [1.68–2.16]; 1–2 years: 1.64 [1.42–1.89]; 2–3 years: 1.30 [1.08–1.57]; 3–10 years: 1.37 [1.20–1.56]). The aHRs were 1.15 (0.98–1.36), 1.64 (1.16–2.30), 1.23 (0.94–1.62), and 1.60 (1.30–1.97), respectively, for survivors who received no chemotherapy, anthracycline-only, taxane-only, and anthracycline-plus-taxane, compared with women without BC.ConclusionsFemale BC survivors in Japan had a higher HZ risk compared with the general age-matched female Japanese population. HZ vaccination should therefore be promoted for BC survivors.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02345-1.

Background

Breast cancer (BC) is currently the most common cancer among women worldwide [1]. Patients with BC have higher risks of developing certain diseases, highlighting the importance of monitoring BC alongside other conditions [2–4]. Among these, herpes zoster (HZ), with or without postherpetic neuralgia, can significantly reduce the quality of life [5, 6]. Mitigating the incidence and severity of HZ is therefore particularly important among BC survivors.

BC is a known risk factor for HZ incidence [7–9]. Patients treated with immunosuppressive drugs also have a higher risk of developing HZ [9–14]. However, it remains unclear whether: (i) HZ risk changes over time after BC diagnosis; (ii) this risk varies by treatment regimen (chemotherapy and radiotherapy); and (iii) this risk extends to experiencing postherpetic neuralgia after developing HZ, among BC survivors, compared with the general population.

To clarify these knowledge gaps, we examined the risk of HZ and subsequent postherpetic neuralgia among female BC survivors compared with age-matched women without BC, overall, by follow-up time, and by treatment regimens.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a matched cohort study using data from the JMDC claims database spanning January 2005 to June 2022.

Data source

JMDC Inc. records individual medical claims and annual health checkup data from participating health insurance associations for Japanese employees of large- and medium-sized companies, as well as their family members aged < 75 years. Their database contains data from a cumulative total of > 14 million insured individuals logged since 2005 [15]. In addition to demographics and eligibility information, the JMDC database also includes monthly data concerning inpatient and outpatient diagnoses by International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code; prescribed and dispensed medications (in both hospitals and clinics) by World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical or European Pharmaceutical Market Research Association classification; and performed medical procedures by Japanese procedure code [16, 17].

Participants

The exposed group (hereafter referred to as BC survivors) included women with incident BC, defined as a new BC diagnosis (ICD-10: C50) between the database’s inception (January 2005) and December 2019, who underwent surgical treatment for the condition within 1 year of diagnosis. In this study, the term “BC survivors” refers to women from the time of BC diagnosis, consistent with the contemporary concept of cancer survivorship, in which survivorship begins at cancer diagnosis [18, 19]. We selected December 2019 to ensure a sufficient follow-up period until June 2022, which marked the latest JMDC data available, and to avoid confounders related to diagnostic delays and disease progression exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan after January 2020. To ensure that every BC diagnosis was novel, we excluded any patients diagnosed within 3 months of their entry into the database. We also excluded patients with metastatic or recurrent BC, sarcoma, or malignant phyllode tumors using the original Japanese diagnoses, as such survivors often experience different clinical courses from primary BC survivors. We also excluded patients whose BC diagnoses and JMDC withdrawals occurred in the same month because of insufficient follow-up. The month of each patient’s first BC diagnosis was designated as the index month, indicating the start of follow-up.

For each BC survivor, we randomly selected four women with no BC history during the index month, of similar age (± 1 year) and timing (year and month) of initial entry into the JMDC database, to ensure comparable lengths of covariate definitions. Sampling was conducted without replacement, meaning that each woman in the matched cohort could only be selected once. Finally, women with a history of HZ, defined as a recorded diagnosis between entry into the database and the index month, were excluded from both groups.

Outcomes and follow-ups

We defined the first incidence of HZ between the month following each patient’s index month and June 2022 as a new diagnosis, using ICD-10 code B02 and claims for antiviral drugs targeting HZ (i.e., aciclovir, vidarabine, famciclovir, valaciclovir, or amenamevir; eTable 1).

Both groups were followed from the index month to the earliest of the first incidence of HZ, withdrawal from the JMDC database, June 2022, or 10 years after the index month. Any matched women without BC who were subsequently diagnosed with BC were censored at the month of initial BC diagnosis, allowing them to be included in the BC survivor group from that time onward.

As a secondary outcome, we defined postherpetic neuralgia as including: (i) HZ incidence as described above; (ii) prescription of medications recommended for neuropathic pain between 3–12 months following HZ onset, with the month of the first prescription considered to indicate the development of postherpetic neuralgia [20]; and (iii) no prescriptions related to neuropathic pain within 3 months before HZ incidence, to exclude prevalent cases with other neuropathic pain conditions (e.g., chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy; eFig. 1).

Covariates

Potential confounding factors between BC survivorship and the study outcomes included body mass index (BMI) (classified as < 18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2, or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2), smoking history (non-smoker/ex-smoker or current smoker), and drinking habits (none, sometimes, or every day), which were obtained from each participant’s most recent available annual health examination data at or before their index month, and any comorbidities known to represent HZ risk factors (i.e., hypertension, diabetes, asthma, anxiety/depression, and dialysis), which were defined based on any history of treatment for these conditions between each patient’s entry into the database and their index month (eTable 1). We also adjusted for any immunosuppressants/chemotherapy, or steroids (injectable or oral) administered during the 3 months before the index month for conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and various immunosuppressed states [7, 21, 22].

Statistical analysis

We first summarized the baseline characteristics of the BC survivors and matched women without BC, as well as the treatment details of the BC survivors.

We plotted Kaplan–Meier survival curves and calculated the crude HZ incidence rates for HZ in both groups. We then estimated HRs using four stratified Cox regression models to account for the matched cohort: model 1 was not adjusted; model 2 was adjusted for comorbidities, immunosuppression/chemotherapy, and steroid therapy (only for variables without missing data); model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, smoking history, and drinking habits, as a complete case analysis; and model 4 used multiple imputation for BMI, smoking history, and drinking habits. Any missing BMI, smoking history, and drinking habit values were considered missing at random and imputed using binary or multinomial logistic regression imputation. For multiple imputations, we used a chained equation method and created 50 imputed datasets using information from other variables in the imputation model (i.e., exposure group, age, comorbidities, immunosuppressant/chemotherapy/steroid therapy, and outcome status) [23].

We conducted a subgroup analysis by age, dividing the participants into < 50 and ≥ 50 years, since the recommendation for the HZ vaccine differs between these categories in Japan [24].

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated our analyses for the period before the HZ vaccine was introduced in Japan, targeting only participants with index months before February 2016 and ending the observation period in March 2016 (when the vaccine was first administered to women aged ≥ 50 years) [25]. To account for potential immortal time bias, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed wherein both groups were followed from the month of the first BC surgery (with the same month used for the matched women without BC), rather than the index month.

After observing the spline curves for the HR as a function of time since BC diagnosis using flexible parametric models, we estimated separate incidences and HRs for 0–1, 1–2, 2–3, and 3–10 year intervals [26, 27].

As a secondary outcome, we analyzed the incidence of postherpetic neuralgia. We calculated the crude incidence rates in the two groups and estimated aHRs using the same approach used for the main HZ outcome.

Next, we estimated the aHRs using chemotherapy and radiotherapy. The treatment regimen was classified during the first year following each index month, after which the participant was considered to be in follow-up. Those who developed HZ or were lost to follow-up within the first year were excluded. For chemotherapy analysis, we categorized BC survivors into four groups: no-chemotherapy, anthracycline-only, taxane-only, and anthracycline-plus-taxane. We compared the BC survivors who received treatment (by type) with women without BC via simple Cox regression, adjusting for comorbidities, in addition to treatment type during the first year (axillary dissection, radiotherapy, tamoxifen, and aromatase inhibitors). In this analysis, we did not adjust for immunosuppression/chemotherapy and steroid therapy because these were considered to be highly correlated with chemotherapies. We also directly compared the various BC survivor groups using the no-chemotherapy group as the reference. For radiotherapy analysis, we categorized the survivors into three groups—no-radiotherapy, radiotherapy with breast-conserving surgery, and radiotherapy with mastectomy—as the radiotherapy type often correlates with the surgery type. We excluded participants with concurrent bilateral BC and those who underwent only axillary surgery. The analysis was conducted similarly to the chemotherapy analysis, with the anthracycline and taxane adjustments replaced by those for radiation. Furthermore, we estimated the aHRs for the combination of chemotherapy and radiotherapy. We categorized BC survivors into four groups: no-chemotherapy/radiotherapy, chemotherapy-only, radiotherapy-only, and chemotherapy-plus-radiotherapy. The analysis was conducted similarly to the radiotherapy analysis.

All analyses were performed using STATA version 15.1 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

Risk of herpes zoster over the entire follow-up period

The median follow-up period (maximum 10 years), if not censored for the outcome, was 3.33 years (IQR: 1.33–4.67) in the survivors and 3.30 years (IQR: 1.25–4.67) in the women without BC. Death occurred in 0.9% (200/23,169) and 0.2% (181/92,801) of women with and without BC, respectively. Discussion

By analyzing data from a large Japanese health insurance claims database, this study showed that BC survivors in Japan had a higher risk of developing HZ than age-matched women without BC. The risk of HZ remained high in both groups when they were age-stratified (< 50 vs. ≥ 50 years). Notably, the risk of HZ among BC survivors changed over time and varied by treatment.

The elevated HZ risk was most pronounced within 1 year of BC diagnosis, but remained high thereafter, mirroring the time-specific odds ratios reported in a related UK-based case–control study [7]. Notably, our BC survivors showed a higher risk of developing HZ not only during the first year post-diagnosis (i.e., while undergoing intensive multidisciplinary treatments) but also beyond it. Similar results were observed when the data were analyzed for infectious pneumonia and urinary tract infections, suggesting that BC survivors may remain susceptible to various infections even beyond the first year post-diagnosis [4]. Previous studies have shown that BC survivors face an increased risk of anxiety and depression over the long term [3, 4, 28], suggesting that survivorship-related disease burden may persist beyond the initial treatment period. Long-term treatment (hormone therapy typically spans 5–10 years) and disease burden may contribute to the persistent elevation in HZ risk, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. It is also possible that immune function does not fully recover following chemotherapy.

We also found that BC survivors had a higher risk of developing postherpetic neuralgia than women without BC. Among patients who developed HZ, the proportion who subsequently developed postherpetic neuralgia was slightly higher in BC survivors than in women without BC (4.2% vs. 3.4%), suggesting that the increased burden of postherpetic neuralgia may be attributable not only to the higher incidence of HZ but also to a modestly higher risk of developing postherpetic neuralgia after HZ. This analysis was restricted to those who did not use neuropathic pain medications within 3 months of HZ onset, because we could not reliably separate postherpetic neuralgia from other (e.g., cancer-related) neuropathic pain in our dataset. The risk, therefore remains unclear in BC survivors who use neuropathic pain medications alongside chemotherapy. Furthermore, postherpetic neuralgia is a chronic pain syndrome that is difficult to identify accurately using claims data alone. Our prescription-based definition may have missed untreated or unrecognized cases, potentially resulting in an underestimation of its incidence. While chemotherapy-related immunosuppression is a risk factor for developing postherpetic neuralgia [29], neuropathic pain medications may prevent HZ from progressing to postherpetic neuralgia. Beyond postherpetic neuralgia, HZ can cause a wide range of severe complications, including disseminated HZ and bacterial skin superinfection, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [12, 20, 30]. Although these outcomes could not be evaluated in our data based on ICD-10 codes, preventing HZ in BC survivors may provide benefits beyond reducing HZ incidence alone.

When analyzed by chemotherapy type, the aHRs of the anthracycline-only and anthracycline-plus-taxane groups were higher than those of women without BC. The anthracycline-only and anthracycline-plus-taxane groups showed strong evidence of a higher HR even when the BC survivor subgroups were compared with each other. Both anthracycline and taxane would have been completed within 1 year of the index month, suggesting that the increased risk is not due to immunosuppression caused by myelosuppression during treatment [10]. The continued high risk of HZ beyond 1 year post-diagnosis may reflect weakened immune function caused by chemotherapy. Chemotherapy among BC survivors has been associated with decreased absolute lymphocyte count (e.g., B and CD4+T cells) at 6, 9, and 12 months post-chemotherapy [31–33]. These findings support the biological plausibility that incomplete immune recovery after chemotherapy may contribute to the persistent risk of HZ. Although the situation beyond 1 year post-diagnosis remains unclear, incomplete immune function recovery may increase susceptibility to infections. Immunosuppression may also persist if additional chemotherapy is administered. By June 2022, a small number of BC survivors with higher risks of recurrence received further treatment with capecitabine (an oral chemotherapy) for 6 months, in addition to anthracycline-plus-taxane.

Although not statistically significant, patients who underwent radiation therapy showed a tendency toward a higher risk. Radiation therapy may trigger herpes reactivation by creating areas of immunodeficiency and inhibiting the development of acquired immunity during the reactivation phase [34, 35]. This risk increased within 1 year [36], and it may persist for a longer duration.

In the analysis of the combination of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, the association in the no-chemotherapy/radiotherapy group was modest and not statistically significant. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution. The slight elevation of the point estimate may reflect factors other than chemotherapy or radiotherapy, such as cancer-related immune alterations, psychosocial stress after cancer diagnosis, or residual confounding.

The recombinant zoster vaccine is effective in preventing HZ and postherpetic neuralgia onset [37–41], and its administration is recommended according to regional guidelines. The recommended vaccination age for immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals varies by country [42]. Immunocompromised individuals include those with immunodeficiencies or under immunosuppression, scheduled to undergo immunosuppressive therapies, and with chronic comorbidities such as rheumatoid arthritis, asthma, or diabetes [43–49].

The recombinant zoster vaccine became available in January 2020 for those aged ≥ 50 years in Japan, and its indication was expanded in June 2023 to include individuals aged ≥ 18 years at high risk of developing HZ. Our study (showing an increased risk of HZ among BC survivors, including those aged ≥ 50 years and those who underwent chemotherapy) supports the current Japanese guidelines recommending the administration of the recombinant zoster vaccine to BC survivors aged ≥ 50 years and those aged < 50 years scheduled to undergo chemotherapy. However, there are no clear guidelines for BC survivors aged < 50 years who require neither chemotherapy nor radiotherapy [24]. Further studies are warranted to establish future guidelines for these individuals.

Regarding the timing of vaccination, it is generally recommended to review a patient’s overall vaccination history and to administer the recombinant zoster vaccine before the patient’s immune system is affected by the cancer treatment [45, 50]. While the optimal timing for vaccination in patients undergoing active treatment remains unclear, vaccination should ideally be scheduled 2–3 weeks before the start of cancer treatment, if possible, to avoid periods of leukopenia [50]. Furthermore, while live-attenuated herpes zoster vaccination is available in Japan, caution is required because the live vaccination is contraindicated for individuals with clearly compromised immune function or those undergoing immunosuppressive therapy [51].

This study had some limitations. First, because the JMDC database mainly includes individuals aged < 65 years (up to 74), the generalizability of our results to older individuals remains uncertain. Because aging is a risk factor for HZ, older BC survivors have a higher risk of developing HZ. Therefore, the HR between patients with and without BC may be lower in this population. Second, our database did not include the HZ vaccination status, and some of our participants aged ≥ 50 years may have previously received the HZ vaccine. However, we found similar results when we considered only the pre-vaccine period (until March 2016) in our sensitivity analysis, meaning our interpretation remained unaffected. Third, no information was obtained regarding BC stages. Although this can significantly affect stress, insurance claims data cannot identify patients’ stress levels. Furthermore, although other unmeasurable factors (e.g., negative life events [52]) may have also affected our participants, we adjusted for those who had previously been treated for anxiety and depression. Finally, this study focused on overall and period-specific (< 1, 1–2, 2–3, and 3–10 years) HRs over several years of follow-up. These should be interpreted cautiously, because if HRs vary over the follow-up period (as suggested by our supplementary analyses using flexible parametric models), the results may be particularly affected by the follow-up length. Latter period-specific HRs may also suffer from “built-in survivor bias” [53].

In conclusion, in the large health insurance claims database we analyzed, female BC survivors living in Japan showed higher risks of developing HZ and postherpetic neuralgia than similarly aged women without BC. This risk persisted 1 year after BC diagnosis and was particularly high among women who received anthracycline-based chemotherapy. HZ vaccination should be recommended for patients with BC at the time of diagnosis, in accordance with the relevant guidelines of each country.
