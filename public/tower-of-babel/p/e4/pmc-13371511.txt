Facilitators of and barriers to high-quality care at end-of-life: experiences of terminally ill patients and their relatives
Authors: Hanne Irene Jensen, Lene Jørgensen, Lisbeth Bertelsen, Helen Bruun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo ensure coherence and continuity of care for individual patients, it is necessary to improve collaboration between healthcare systems as well as between healthcare systems and patients. The objective of the study was to gain knowledge about the experiences of terminally ill patients and their relatives with the healthcare system to identify facilitators of and barriers to high-quality care in the last stage of patients’ lives.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with terminally ill patients and their relatives. The data were analysed using content analysis.ResultsSix patients and four relatives participated in the study. The analysis identified five themes: (1) continuity, coherence and timing: significance and challenges; (2) conversations about end-of-life: the need for an individual approach; (3) being seen and trusted; (4) “small” things may have a great significance; and (5) relatives have their own needs.Among patients and relatives who felt that the healthcare system had the overall overview and was present with the necessary help at the right time, the healthcare system provided a secure frame within which they could devote their energy to dealing with the impending farewell and trying to foster a high quality of life every day. One of the most important things for patients and relatives was that there was clear agreement regarding who was responsible for what and where they could call for help.ConclusionThe findings highlight the importance of continuity of care, an individual approach and the need to trust the patients. Background

A high quality of life is associated with the perception of living a meaningful life [1]. Illness and age-related impairment can challenge lining a meaningful life, but quality of life is, among other things, dependent on whether the treatment and care offered are tailored to individuals’ needs and wishes [2].

The number and proportion of older people is increasing worldwide, and more people are living with the consequences of chronic illness in the last stage of their lives. Meeting patients’ needs and wishes for end-of-life care is an important aspect of healthcare [3]. However, conversations with patients about their wishes for end-of-life care are often not conducted [4]. Patients who have such conversations find them valuable and that they let them have a say, and their relatives also consider it important that their wishes are known [5–7]. In a randomised controlled trial about advanced care planning, secondary analyses indicated that patients who have conversations about their wishes and receive customised care as a result live longer than patients who do not [8].

Even if a conversation about wishes for end-of-life care has occurred, one of the major challenges in healthcare is ensuring knowledge and continuity of care when patients change healthcare sectors [9–12]. In Denmark, as in many other countries, there are limitations in the exchange of health information between hospitals, home care, general practice and the pre-hospital acute system [13]. The lack of knowledge across sectors can also entail inadequate care in the last part of life. To ensure coherence and continuity of care for individual patients, it is necessary to improve collaboration between healthcare systems.

To be able to identify areas in need of improvement, users’ perspectives are needed. Integrating patient experiences represents a significant advancement in the endeavour to more accurately assess value within the healthcare system [14, 15].

The aim of the study was to gain knowledge about the experiences of terminally ill patients and their relatives with the healthcare system to identify organisational facilitators of and barriers to high-quality care in the last stage of patients’ lives.

Methods

Design

The study was a cross-sectional qualitative study using semi-structured interviews.

Study population

Eligible participants were patients 18 years or older who recently (within the last 5–45 days) received a ‘terminal declaration’, a Danish medical declaration stating that the patient is terminally ill. The declaration entails access to extended home care, free palliative medication, etc. In addition, one relative aged 18 years or older could participate if the patient had a close relative and the patient consented to the relative’s participation. The relative was identified by the patient and could be e.g. a partner, son/daughter, sibling or friend.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria were patients and relatives who could neither read nor speak Danish, patients and relatives who did not have decision-making capacity (e.g. due to illness or treatment), and patients with expected death within two weeks.

Recruitment of participants

Two project contact persons (a nurse and a physician) from a palliative care team at a Danish hospital assessed inclusion and exclusion criteria when they met patients with a recent terminal declaration. Expected death within two weeks was assessed based on prognostic judgement. If the patient was assessed as eligible to participate, the study was briefly introduced orally by the contact persons, and written information was provided. If the patient expressed a wish to know more or participate in the study, the patient’s name and phone number was passed on to the first and last author. One of them then contacted the patient, explained the study in detail and answered their questions.

Interviews

If the patient agreed to participate and written consent was obtained, a time was arranged for the interview. The interviews were carried out via telephone or face to face, according to the patient’s preference. If a relative participated, a separate telephone interview was arranged. The interviews were conducted by the first and last author. The first author is a critical care nurse and professor in end-of-life care with substantial experience in qualitative research and end-of-life issues. The last author is a registered nurse with a master’s degree, experience in qualitative research and in end-of-life issues. None of the interviewers had any clinical relationship with the participants. Their end-of-life knowledge equipped them with an in-depth understanding of the ethical, emotional, and clinical complexities surrounding end-of-life scenarios. However, this could potentially introduce biases, as certain aspects of care based on personal beliefs and experiences could be prioritised. The semi-structured interviews provided a format which encouraged participants to share their authentic experiences and opinions without being guided too closely by the interviewers’ preconceptions. Likewise, the interview method made it possible to check understanding of statements, ensuring that the participants’ voices were accurately understood and represented.

At the beginning of each interview, the aim of the study and the conditions for participation were repeated. Based on end-of-life and patient/family involvement literature [5, 10, 11, 16] and praxis experiences, a semi-structured interview guide was developed. It included questions about the patients’ experiences and contacts with the healthcare system within the last year, satisfaction with the care they received, whether something had been lacking or it should have been handled differently, whether the conversation about approaching the last part of life had occurred at the right time and in the right way, and whether the collaboration between hospital, home care and general practitioners had been satisfactory. The interview guide was pilot tested with two patients (not included in the study), and a few changes were made. Please see Additional file 1 for an English translation of the final interview guide.

Data analysis

All the interviews were recorded digitally and transcribed. The transcripts were read repeatedly and analysed inductively with qualitative content analysis [17] beginning with basic descriptive coding that labeled the manifest content of each comment. The first and last authors individually coded the interviews. The codes were then compared and discussed and moved to interpretive analysis and categorisation by identifying the underlying and intended meaning of the text. Based on the categories, central themes were identified. Table 1 provides an example of the analysis process.

The reporting followed the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) guideline [18].

Ethics

All interviewees received written and oral information about the study and had the option of at least 24 h of consideration time. They were also informed that participation would be voluntary, that all collected data would be handled such that confidentiality and anonymity would be maintained, and that they could withdraw from the study at any time prior to the data analysis. The participants received a consent form and a prepaid envelope together with the written information. If the patient (and relative) agreed to participate, they either, according to the individual participant’s preference, signed the consent form and returned it by mail or used the link provided to them to complete an online consent form via e-Boks (a Danish digital secure public information system).

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Southern Denmark (22-77189) and was registered with the Danish Data Protection Agency (22-55484). The data were stored on a secure SharePoint site.

Results

From September 2023 to November 2024, six patients – three women and three men, with a median age of 71 years (range 62–80) – participated in the study. Four were cancer patients, and two were lung disease patients. Five interviews were conducted by phone, and one was conducted in the patient’s home. Four relatives – two women and two men, with a median age of 75 years (range 67–81) participated; all four of their interviews were conducted by phone. One patient who had expressed a desire to participate in the study died before they got the chance. Their relative participated of their own accord after the patient’s death.

The interviews lasted a median of 31 min (range 7–48) for patients and a median of 24 min (range 13–46) for relatives.

The analysis identified five themes: (1) continuity, coherence and timing: significance and challenges; (2) conversations about end-of-life: the need for an individual approach; (3) being seen and trusted; (4) “small” things may have great significance; and (5) relatives have their own needs.

Continuity, coherence and timing: significance and challenges

Most patients and relatives elaborated on the importance of meeting the same staff and the need for knowledge sharing between different sectors of the healthcare system. The patients and relatives who had experienced very satisfactory processes highlighted continuity as one of the important factors for a good experience:

It’s nice to be met by the same people every day because they know how you feel and how you should feel and how it should be done and all that.. . And if there were different doctors, they were well prepared,. . but I think they start by telling and talking, and you feel that they have read and seen the papers that have been written. It’s not just something they’re catching out of thin air. (P1)

The satisfied patients used the word security to describe how they felt. Their experience of continuity of staff meant that, despite the challenges their illness entailed, they felt secure in the knowledge that the healthcare system had their illness trajectory under control. The relatives also used the word secure several times when describing the patients’ experiences of the healthcare system:

She’s not well. But she is… she is secure. (R6)

By contrast, patients who had experienced very unsatisfactory processes emphasised the lack of continuity of staff as one of the biggest challenges. The lack of continuity meant that the patients felt lost in the system, they felt they were just a number, that they were personally responsible for their process and perceived that everybody denied responsibility:

I think it’s a bit scary as a patient to feel that the main responsibility is mine, right?. . It’s a bit like, ‘Well, it’s not our fault, it’s the others’ [fault]. It’s the other hospital, or it’s the other regime…. which hasn’t been able to figure out how to do it right or something.’ (P3).

Patient 3 described their wish for the healthcare system as follows:

My big dream in this is like when you build a house, you have a contractor or someone who has complete oversight over your construction. . Can’t we just have one fixed person who at least doesn’t… it’s not certain that this person has all the answers. But someone you can call and say, you know, just get in touch, or I’ll get in touch or something. So it’s coordinated, so you know that, regardless of what this is about, this is the person. (P3)

For some patients and relatives, continuity also involved having an ‘open admission’, meaning that they could get admitted to a specific hospital ward directly without having to go through the normal admission process.

The terminal declaration meant, for most, that a large machinery was set in motion. Some of the patients described the first 14 days as chaotic. Their desire was clear: that everything should not happen at once but that contacts should be regulated so that they would not be contacted by everyone, i.e. the palliative care team, general practitioners, home care, home nursing, palliative nurses, etc., simultaneously:

Now I can’t remember how many have called and been here, but it’s at least four to six people. Two might have been enough to explain and tell how it has been or how it should be and how the process. . It’s just a lot of things all at once. It was right in the first 8–14 days when you just got the message, and it’s not exactly the nicest message to receive, and then you get all the other impressions on top of that. (P1)

They experienced losing control over their lives and that they only began to regain it after the first weeks. Their relative said,

It’s the same thing you have to say again and again and again. It can’t be any different because they are new people, but it’s hard. . The only thing I want to say is that when you get that message, you should also be allowed to breathe. It was as if it went incredibly fast, not because they did anything wrong, but one after the other just came crashing down. I think maybe you should figure out how to take it at the patient’s pace, if you can say it that way. . Now we have put all that at a distance, so now we feel we can function as ourselves again. (R1)

Another patient said,

It’s almost overwhelming with all the appointments and people running around, keeping track of who is what, whether it’s a nurse, whether it’s home help or whatever it is. (P2)

Conversations about end-of-life: the need for an individual approach

All patients had received a terminal declaration and had had a conversation about their illness and the fact that no life-saving treatment options were available. These conversations were experienced very differently by the participants. Most of them experienced it as a hard but good conversation. Some had thought about it beforehand, knowing that they had now reached this stage, and they appreciated the honesty with which they were met:

Yes, I think it was a proper conversation because I say they better spit it out instead of sugarcoating it. I say ‘spill the beans’ because then you know what you have to deal with. (P1)

Their relative had the same experience of the conversation:

No [there was nothing that should have been different] because we asked to get the facts and that’s how it is. There is no sense in sugarcoating it, so, of course, it’s hard to be told, but it is what it is, whether it’s sugarcoated or not. (R1)

For one patient, the conversation was a violent, negative experience. The patient had understood that she would recover from her cancer and was completely unprepared for the assessment that no more life-saving treatment was available.

So… it was just aired that I probably shouldn’t have more treatment, that I… that they were probably moving towards me being terminal. . I was so shaken to my core. . I think what weighs most on my mind… is that I was just told that I wouldn’t be resuscitated. That hit me. . And then the way they come in and tell me that they have decided that I should be declared terminal… there… I think… do they not know what that can do to a person? (P5)

She was also not asked if it was okay for an extra person (a trainee staff member) to be present during the conversation:

But they didn’t come in and ask if it was okay. Is it okay for her to be present… they didn’t ask anything; they just came in and sat down… with someone who was to receive a death declaration. Listen, that’s not okay. (P5)

For another patient, although they had opted out of more chemotherapy, living was still very important:

But it’s not like I’m wallowing in life and death; it’s not something I think about. Yes, I know I have to leave here, but it may well be that I still see the apple trees bloom next year or something. But you also have to have hope all the time. (P6)

A patient and their relative were offered a family conversation, which they both described as very valuable:

And they [the children] have also really appreciated those conversations where we also talk in depth about many things. And we talked about some things that you might not otherwise talk about, like death… But it has also been good for my family to be part of those conversations and to be able to ask about some things and such. (P6)

Being seen and trusted

A lung disease patient had experienced increasing problems with breathing for a long time but was repeatedly dismissed by their own doctor with the explanation that it was because they were a smoker:

I tried to tell my doctor that something is wrong with my diaphragm because I’m not breathing the right way. But I was brushed aside; they didn’t listen: ‘You’re just a smoker.’ I tried to talk about my profession throughout my life and my experiences. . ‘No, you’re a smoker.’ (P2).

Treatment for their breathing problems was therefore initiated late.

Another patient had gone to the doctor due to increased fatigue and had been seen by a nurse who did not refer them to a doctor when the blood tests did not show anything specific:

And then she says to me that she really doesn’t know why I feel so flat. And then I should have said that I would like to see a doctor. But I didn’t. And she doesn’t refer me to a doctor. (P5)

The patient, who turned out to have cancer, later thought about whether the process would have been different if they had insisted on seeing a doctor.

“Small” things may have great significance

The relatives were generally very satisfied with the treatment both they and the patients had experienced. However, two relatives recounted something they called trivialities, but which they returned to several times during the interviews as negative experiences.

For a relative, the patient’s personal belongings (toiletries, etc.) had been thrown away after her death. This was important for the relative, as they did not have the opportunity to assess what should be kept:

It wasn’t many things; it didn’t matter, but the few things that there were, I think, belonged to my wife. You don’t just clear the living room and throw it in the trash. (R7)

Another relative talked about how the lack of information about whom they could contact when in need created a very insecure situation one night when they were sent around in the system:

That little clumsiness with us as patients and relatives wanting to know what we do if it’s half past three in the night. (R6)

Subsequent information and clear guidelines on who they could call and when, ensuring that all 24 h of the day were covered, provided great security, but the experience remained as a negative and unnecessary one that the relative returned to several times.

Relatives have their own needs

The participating relatives described how being a relative of a terminally ill patient was a burdensome role, and that they as relatives also needed support. One relative had an experience in which two staff members from the palliative care unit had split during a home visit so that the patient was examined in another room by one, while the other staff member asked how the relative was doing. This meant a lot to them.

Another relative felt that they were asked about their needs, but this happened while the patient was present:

Well, the nurses who come here and are supposed to check on my husband and such, they are very nice at asking how you are, but… you’re not honest sometimes [when your husband is sitting next to you]. (R4)

This relative of a non-cancer patient wanted more support and a forum for relatives to share what was difficult as a relative.

One relative had greatly benefited from receiving practical recommendations from the hospital staff about not taking on a caregiver role:

We’re sending her home to you now because you benefit from her coming home in a hospital bed, but you shouldn’t run around taking care of her; you should be there for her and accept all the help you get from outside. (R7)

Similarly, recommendations to make arrangements for ongoing updates to friends so that everyone was not calling all the time and getting psychological help through the Cancer Society, for example, were experienced as very helpful.

One relative talked about anticipatory grief and another relative described how the awareness that it would soon be over was constant:

But it’s, it’s, I wouldn’t say I fear it, but that experience, if she suddenly is no longer here, in connection with stopping breathing… It’s, it’s a bit hard, it is. (R6)

The interviews also showed differences in opportunities for cancer patients and non-cancer patients. One relative talked about the benefits they had received, such as psychological conversations to which they had free access through the Cancer Society. Meanwhile, a relative of a lung disease patient needed support that was unavailable:

So now I know that if it had been cancer, it would have been a completely different… then you would have been in some kind of package, with different solutions, right? (R4)

Discussion

Being terminally ill with the knowledge that death is near can be overwhelming for both patients and relatives. The interviews showed that among patients and relatives who felt that the healthcare system had the overall overview and was present with the necessary help at the right time, the healthcare system provided a secure framework within which the patient and relatives could devote their energy to dealing with the impending farewell and trying to foster a high quality of life every day. However, the interviews also showed that in cases in which the healthcare system was not perceived to have an overall overview, the patient needed to expend energy and effort to navigate the healthcare system themselves.

One of the most important things for patients and relatives was that there was clear agreement regarding who was responsible for what and where they could call for help at any time of day. This aligns with the findings of other studies [19, 20]. Healthcare consists of multiple, different care settings, and with many different parties involved in the patient’s treatment, especially when it comes to palliative care, navigating this situation can become overwhelming for patients and their relatives. Professionals designated for care coordination could play a critical role in facilitating the effective integration of healthcare services, thereby enhancing the overall efficiency of care delivery and lessen the burden of patients and relatives [21–23]. The care coordinator could for example be a registered nurse employed both at the hospital and in primary care. Furthermore, continuity of care is warranted to decrease the number of different healthcare professionals involved [24]. The interviews also demonstrated that patients and relatives may have vastly different needs and that offers and conversations must be tailored to individual patients [20, 25, 26].

Substantial evidence supports the need for conversations about end of life, as it helps patients and families prepare [27], gives peace of mind to know that treatment offered is in accordance with patients’ wishes [28] and entails less aggressive treatment at end-of-life [29]. The findings of this study support the notion that clear and honest end-of-life conversations are needed; however, these conversations need to be tailored to individual patients, as honesty without understanding and empathy can be experienced as a violation [26].

There is a substantial amount of literature about patients’ trust in healthcare professionals [30–32], but there is limited research on the effect of healthcare professionals’ trust in patients [30]. The findings of this study show that a lack of trust in patients’ perceptions may have serious consequences for patients’ trajectories and will most likely also affect their general trust in healthcare.

The study included both cancer and non-cancer patients and provided examples of inequality in the Danish healthcare system. The cancer patients and their relatives had access to several palliative support functions that were unavailable to the non-cancer patients. This inequality also exists in other healthcare systems [33–36]; thus, an increased focus on palliative care for non-cancer patients is warranted.

The participating relatives, who were interviewed separately, i.e. without the patients present, shared their experiences of both the practical and emotional impacts of being a relative of a terminally ill patient and stressed the need for being seen and supported. This aligns with a review that identified support for informal caregivers in dealing with end-of-life challenges as one of five main themes when exploring concerns and potential improvements in end-of-life care [26]. As experienced by one of the participating relatives, structured family meetings may represent an effective strategy for addressing the diverse needs of family members. Such meetings can create an environment conducive to articulating the specific requirements of each individual, thereby facilitating the alignment of expectations among all parties involved [36–38].

One of the strengths of this study is the inclusion of both patients’ and relatives’ perspectives, which added nuance to the results. The study sample was small. It was difficult to identify potential participants and inform them about the study. This was likely because healthcare professionals are busy and due to the vulnerable situations patients find themselves in after a terminal declaration. The study does not provide an exhaustive overview of facilitators and barriers for high-quality care at end-of-life. However, it provides insight into some patients and relatives experiences which to a certain extent may be generic but also shows that to provide high quality end-of-life care, you need to have an individual approach. All patients were recruited via the hospital, which may limit the extent to which the findings can be transferred to other settings, as patients and relatives recruited from other healthcare sectors may have had other experiences. Future studies could with benefit include patients and relatives from different healthcare settings to achieve larger and more diverse samples. The patients and relatives who participated found it very meaningful to contribute with their experiences, both the good and the bad.

Conclusion

For patients and relatives who felt that the healthcare system had the overall overview and provided the necessary help at the right time, the healthcare system provided a secure frame within which the patient and relatives could devote their energy to dealing with the impending farewell and trying to foster quality of life every day. However, in cases in which the healthcare system was not perceived to have the overview, patients and relatives became insecure and had to exert energy and effort to navigate the system themselves. One of the most important things for patients and relatives was that there was clear agreement on who was responsible for what and where they could call for help at any time of day.

Based on the findings, recommendations for improvement of care include designations of care coordinators, focus on continuity of care, clear and honest end-of-life conversations tailored to the individual patient, increased focus on palliative care for non-cancer patients, and structured family meetings.
