Impact of altitude and temperature variation on microbial communities and fermentation quality of natural oat and maize silage on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
Authors: Haiping Li, Jiamin Zhang, Zhifeng Jia, Wenhui Liu, Qingqing Yang, Songyu Fang, Qingping Zhou, Hao Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the microbial community dynamics and climatic responsiveness of naturally fermented silage in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, 51 silages from 17 distinct geographical locations across Qinghai Province were collected and analyzed. Bacterial community profiling was conducted through high-throughput 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing, while fermentation characteristics and nutritional composition were systematically evaluated. Significant variations were observed in both fermentation parameters and nutritional quality across samples (P < 0.05). Notably, maize silage from the BHJ.M site exhibited significantly higher acetic acid (AA) content compared to other samples (P < 0.05). Moreover, BMG.O oat had better fermentation quality, indicated by the lowest pH (3.57) and higher lactic acid (LA) content (4.50%DM). Unlike oat silage, maize silage had a lower relative abundance of Lactobacillus and a higher abundance of Lentilactobacillus. Except for the Hainan (HN), the bacterial community in all regions was dominated by Lactobacillus, but the abundance of Lactobacillus was less than 75% in all samples except BMG.O. Correlation analysis indicated that high altitude and large diurnal temperature fluctuations jointly constrained Lactobacillus dominance. This environmental filtering not only shaped the Lactobacillus community structure but may also limit the metabolic functionality of certain strains, contributing to the inconsistent fermentation quality observed in naturally fermented silage on the plateau.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-026-02077-7.Keywords: Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, natural fermented silage, microbial community, oat, maize

Introduction

Qinghai, located on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, is one of China’s five major pastoral regions. However, the escalating pastoral population, reliance on traditional husbandry practices, and the region’s high-altitude harsh climate have intensified land-use conflicts between humans, forage cultivation, and livestock grazing (Yuan et al. 2015). Silage technology, which relies on microbial fermentation of water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) into lactic acid (LA), offers a critical solution to seasonal forage shortages and low utilization efficiency of crop residues. The technology is also of great significance in addressing the challenge of limited livestock feed sources while ensuring cost savings (Coblentz Akins 2018).

Silage production has been widely used as a common forage processing method in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. However, silage fermentation is greatly affected by environmental climate, with the suitable temperature range for silage fermentation being 20–30℃. Low temperatures can limit silage fermentation and inhibit pH reduction and LA production (Wang et al. 2018), while high temperatures can easily lead to clostridial fermentation and transformation from homofermentative to heterofermentative fermentation (Chen et al. 2013; Guan et al. 2020). Some studies also suggested that forage grown in colder regions may limit the growth and reproduction of microbial communities and reduce microbial diversity, eventually affecting silage quality (Wang et al. 2019a). Therefore, the microbial community is crucial for silage fermentation, and the presence of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) as the dominant bacterial group during the process is the main factor determining silage fermentation quality.

The animal husbandry sector on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau has experienced rapid expansion in recent decades. However, critical gaps remain in understanding silage fermentation dynamics under its unique climatic extremes, characterized by drastic diurnal temperature fluctuations (20 °C daytime vs. subzero nocturnal temperatures during September harvests) and cyclical freeze-thaw stress during the critical initial ensiling phase. Moreover, an effective and easily scalable silage processing system has not yet been established in this area (Kim et al. 2006; Weinberg Zwi 2013), greatly hindering the upgrading of the forage industry in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. In this study, 30 oat and 21 maize silage samples from natural fermentation were selected at 17 sampling points in Qinghai Province to determine their characteristics and quality. The microbial communities were determined using high-throughput sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene. Moreover, correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between silage quality, microbial community, and climatic factors, aiming to explore how extreme climatic constraints (high altitude and diurnal temperature fluctuations) uniquely shape microbial community assembly and limit Lactobacillus functional dominance in this extreme environment, distinct from temperate-region silage systems. The study provided a theoretical foundation for establishing a high-quality silage processing technology in this region, thereby promoting the transformation and upgrading of forage processing in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

In March 2021, a total of 51 silage samples (30 for oats and 21 for maize) were collected from 17 sites across Qinghai Province. Three replicates were taken at each site. The collection sites spanned four administrative regions in Qinghai Province, namely XiNing (XN), HaiBei (HB), HaiNan (HN), and HaiDong (HD), which are located in high-altitude regions between 2400 and 3300 m and represent distinct local climatic regimes. The selection includes both oat and maize—the two most commonly ensiled forage crops in the region—enabling comparison of crop-specific fermentation characteristics under the same climatic conditions.The samples were aseptically collected at a depth of 30 cm beneath the surface. For each replicate, 300 g of silage were separately gathered from the upper, middle, and lower sections of each silo. After thorough mixing, each sample was divided into three portions and promptly placed in an ice box for transportation to the laboratory. Upon arrival, the samples were stored at −80 °C until further use. All the silages were prepared in September 2020. The detailed information regarding the experimental materials is presented in Table S1.

Nutrient content determination

The samples were oven-dried (DHG-9053 A, Weiling brand, Shanghai, China) at 65℃ to a constant weight, and the dry matter (DM) content was determined. The samples were then ground and passed through a 1 mm sieve to measure other nutrient components. The crude protein (CP) content was determined using the Dumas combustion method (Lee 1995), while neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were measured using the method described by Van Soest et al. (Van Soest et al. 1991). WSC content was estimated using a soluble sugar content kit (Suzhou Ke Ming Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China).

Determination of the fermentation characteristics

Silage samples (20 g) were mixed with 180 mL of distilled water, sealed, and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. The pH value of the silage extract was measured using a pH meter (Light Magnetic pHS-3 C, Shanghai Instrumental Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and the extracted solution was filtered through four layers of gauze and stored at −20 °C for the determination of ammoniacal nitrogen (NH3-N, AN) and organic acid contents. The concentration of ammoniacal nitrogen was determined using the phenol-hypochlorite colorimetric method (Broderick and Kang 1980). The LA, acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA), and butyric acid (BA) contents in the silage extract were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Thermo Fisher UltiMate3000, Thermo Scientific, United States). The HPLC analysis was conducted using an RSpak KC-811 column (Shimadzu Co. Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), with 0.1% (w/w) H3PO4 as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.5 mL·min− 1 and a column temperature of 55 °C (Li et al. 2017).

Microbial diversity analysis based on high-throughput sequencing

Silage samples (20 g) were weighed into 180 mL of sterile distilled water and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min with shaking. The mixture was filtered through two layers of sterile gauze, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and 3 mL of sterile distilled water was added to resuspend the bacterial cells for DNA extraction. Total DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Bacterial DNA Kit (DP302, Tiangen, Beijing, China), and the purity and concentration of DNA were detected using agarose gel electrophoresis. The extracted DNA was diluted with sterile water to a concentration of 1 ng·µL− 1 in centrifuge tubes. The V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using specific primers, 515 F (5’-GTTTCGGTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3’) and 806R (5’-GCCAATGGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The PCR products were separated on 2% agarose via gel electrophoresis, and the qualified PCR products were purified using magnetic beads, quantified using enzyme labeling, mixed equally according to the concentration of PCR products, and detected via gel electrophoresis using 2% agarose. The target band was recovered using the gel recovery kit provided by Qiagen, and the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit was used for library construction. The constructed library was quantified using Qubit and qPCR and then sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform after the quality control test.

The reads of each sample were spliced using FLASH (v1.2.7) (Magoc and Salzberg 2011), and the final valid sequences were obtained using the quality control process of Qiime (v1.9.1). All valid sequences from all samples were clustered using the Uparse algorithm (Uparse v7.0.1001) (Edgar 2013), which clustered sequences into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with 97% concordance by default. Multiple sequence comparison was performed using the MUSCLE (Version 3.8.31) software (Edgar 2004) to obtain all the OTUs representing the phylogenetic relationships of the sequences. Alpha and beta diversity indices were calculated using Qiime software (Version 1.9.1) and displayed using R software (Version 2.15.3). For beta diversity, Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed using QIIME v1.9.1 based on weighted UniFrac distances. PERMANOVA with 999 permutations and Benjamini‑Hochberg correction was used to test for differences among regions. After quality filtering and chimera removal, a total of 3,566,787 high-quality effective tags were obtained across all 51 samples, with each sample containing 69,937 effective tags. To account for uneven sequencing depth, all samples were rarefied to 62,942 tags for downstream alpha and beta diversity analyses. Good’s coverage estimates and rarefaction curves were calculated using QIIME (v1.9.1) to assess sequencing depth sufficiency.The sequence data reported in this study have been deposited in the NCBI database (Accession NO. PRJNA884491).

Data processing

Data were organized using EXCEL software, and statistical analysis was performed using the GLM procedure of SPSS Statistics 26.0 to test for the differences between samples. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the silage samples collected from different locations, and multiple comparisons of the different samples were performed using Duncan’s method (P < 0.05). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed using R software (version 4.0.3) with the ‘psych’ package to evaluate correlations between environmental factors (altitude, Htemp, Ltemp, Difftemp) and dominant bacterial genera, as well as between fermentation characteristics and bacterial genera. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Microbial diversity of silage samples

Sequencing depth and coverage

Good’s coverage values for all 51 samples ranged from 0.993 to 0.998 (mean = 0.996), indicating that > 99% of the microbial species were detected (Table S2). Rarefaction curves approached a plateau for all samples, confirming sufficient sequencing depth (Figure S1).

Microbial community structure analysis of silage

The bacterial community of silage on the genus level was presented in Fig. 1. Most samples from the 17 sampling points (Fig. 1a), except for BMD.O, NGX.M, NGC.M, and NGZ.O, were dominated by Lactobacillus, but the relative abundance of Lactobacillus was less than 75%, some even below 50%. Based on the raw material source (Fig. 1b), the total abundance of Lactobacillus and Latilactobacillus in maize silage was far below 50%, but the abundance of Lactobacillus, Latilactobacillus, Acetobacter, and Leuconostoc was higher in oat silage than that in maize silage. However, the abundance of Lentilactobacillus was higher in maize silage than in oat silage. When oat silage samples were grouped by geographical region (Fig. 1c), the bacterial community composition in HN showed a distinct pattern compared to the other three regions. Specifically, HN showed lower relative abundances of Lactobacillus and Acetobacter, while demonstrating higher proportions of Latilactobacillus and Loigolactobacillus compared to XN, HD, and HB. The top 10 abundant bacteria in XN and HD were similar in abundance and structure. The abundance of Leuconostoc in HN and HB was higher than that in XN and HD, and the abundance of Prevotella_7 in HB was higher than that in the other three regions. When maize silage was grouped by region (Fig. 1d), many similarities with oat silage were found. The abundance of Lactobacillus and Acetobacter in the HN samples was lower than that in the other three regions, while the abundance of Latilactobacillus and Loigolactobacillus was higher than that in the other three regions. The top 10 abundant bacteria in XN and HD had similar abundance and structure, and the abundance of Prevotella_7 in HB maize silage was also higher than in the other regions. However, the top 10 abundant bacteria in maize silage did not include Leuconostoc, and the relative abundance of Lactobacillus in maize silage in HB was lower, while the abundance of Acetobacter was higher.

Figure 2 shows the PCoA plots for maize (Fig. 2a) and oat (Fig. 2b) silage based on weighted UniFrac distances. For maize silage (Fig. 2a), samples from Xining (XN) and Haidong (HD) clustered closely together, indicating similar microbial community structures. Discussion

Characteristics of silage fermentation quality

All samples in this study were ensiled approximately 6 months (180 ± 10 days). Previous research has shown that silage microbial communities typically reach a relatively stable state after 28–90 days, with LAB dominating and fermentation parameters stabilizing (Oude Elferink et al. 2000; Wang et al. 2021). The uniform fermentation duration across samples ensures that comparisons among regions and crop types are not confounded by differences in storage time.

In this study, maize silage exhibited a relatively high pH and a low abundance of LAB, which contrasts with previous findings (Guan et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2021). Such inconsistencies may be attributed to differences in the epiphytic microbial populations or the chemical composition of the raw materials. Indeed, it has been reported that the microbial flora of whole-plant maize silage varies across climatic regions (Huang et al. 2021). In our study, maize from Hainan (HN) was cultivated under unique valley climatic conditions that strictly limit its growth to this region. These specialized growth conditions are likely to have shaped a distinctive epiphytic microbiota, thereby hindering LAB from achieving dominance after direct ensiling (Sun et al. 2021). In contrast, oat silage generally exhibited lower pH and higher LA content than maize silage, with Lactobacillus comprising > 75% of the bacterial community in BMG.O, suggesting that oat epiphytic microbiota are better suited to the local fermentation environment. A noteworthy case is NGY.M, which simultaneously exhibited the lowest pH, the highest LA content, and AN level exceeding the threshold for well-fermented silage (Henderson 1993). This co-occurrence of effective acidification and extensive proteolysis suggests a temporal decoupling between acid production and proteolytic suppression. Low nocturnal temperatures during the September ensiling period on the plateau can retard the initial growth of homofermentative Lactobacillus (Wang et al. 2017), delaying the pH decline necessary to inhibit plant proteases and enterobacterial activity. Consistent with this, LEfSe analysis identified Enterobacteriaceae as a significant biomarker in HN maize silage (Fig. 3b). Once Lactobacillus dominance is eventually established—likely facilitated by warmer daytime temperatures—LA accumulates and terminal pH declines, but the proteolytic damage incurred during the initial window is irreversible. This combination signals that while lactic acid production was effective, protein preservation was compromised, illustrating the importance of evaluating multiple indicators collectively when assessing silage quality. The AA content of the DPC.M sample was higher than that of LA, indicating that the fermentation pattern of the DPC.M silage tended to heterofermentation, consistent with the study by Ren et al. (Ren et al. 2019). BHJ.M exhibited favorable fermentation characteristics, indicating that acceptable fermentation quality is achievable in maize silage from HB under certain conditions. PA is typically associated with clostridial fermentation (Kung et al. 2018). Studies showed that both AA and PA can inhibit fungal activity and improve the aerobic stability of silage (Schmidt & Kung. 2010). The higher the content of BA, the lower the feed intake of livestock (Aisan et al. 1997), indicating that BA content is also an important indicator for evaluating the fermentation quality of silage. In this study, no BA was detected in any sample, indicating that no BA fermentation occurred. In oat silage, the pH value was lower, and the LA content was higher in HB, while HD (DPW.O) and HN (NGZ.O) had higher pH values and lower LA contents. This was consistent with the findings of Zi et al. (Zi et al. 2021), which suggested that the pH decreased as the LA content increased. These results indicated that the quality of oat silage in HB was superior to that of the other three regions.

Nutrient differences among different silage

In maize silage, the DPC.M sample from HD exhibited the lowest WSC content. Additionally, it had the highest pH value, the lowest LA content, and the highest AA content. These results suggest that during the fermentation process of maize silage from HD, more WSC was consumed as a fermentation substrate. The reason for the lowest WSC content of the DPC.M sample and poor fermentation quality may be that other miscellaneous bacteria competitively consumed the produced LA during fermentation, leading to an increased AA content. Other studies showed that CP, NDF, and ADF are important indicators reflecting the nutritional quality of silage, and animal digestibility negatively correlates with ADF. The lower the ADF content, the higher the digestibility and the feeding value (Mustafa and Seguin 2003). In this study, the same silage raw material had significant differences in nutrient composition among different regions, probably due to regional or variety differences. The low WSC and high NDF and ADF contents of the DPC.M sample from HD might be attributed to over-maturity of the forage at harvest. The AN content in NGY.M and BMB.O samples is significantly higher than that in other samples. Both sampling sites are located at approximately 3200 m, correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between AN and Loigolactobacillus, while Loigolactobacillus showed a positive correlation with altitude, which might explain the difference. Concurrently, these two groups demonstrated higher lactic acid content, potentially linked to deamination processes, which needs further investigation.

The structural characteristics of microbial community

In this study, the relative abundance of the Lactobacillus in the BMG.O sample was as high as 85.96%, resulting in a high LA content and low pH. In contrast, the dominant bacterial genera in BMD.O, NGX.M, NGC.M, and NGZ.O samples were not Lactobacillus, resulting in lower LA content. In both oat and maize silage from HN, the relative abundance of Loigolactobacillus was high and was associated with high pH and low LA content. The PCoA analysis results and relative abundance plots of the two types of silage samples were consistent and exhibited closer distances in the principal coordinate analysis, indicating higher microbial community similarity between the samples. The microbial community structure of maize silage from HN and HB was different from that of other regions, possibly because HN and HB are located in the agricultural and pastoral areas of Qinghai province with high altitudes, lower temperatures, and greater temperature differences. A previous study showed that Lactilactobacillus strains are psychrophilic and can grow at 2–4 °C (Klein et al. 1996). Another study reported that Leuconostoc abundance was higher in low-temperature whole-maize silage (Zhou et al. 2016), consistent with the results of this study, where higher relative abundance of the Lactilactobacillus and Leuconostoc were observed in samples from HN and HB. Wang et al. (Wang et al. 2019b) found that Enterobacteriaceae in silage may compete with LAB for fermentation substrates, resulting in AN production and nutrient losses. The LEfSe analysis revealed that Enterobacteriaceae were significantly different in HN, possibly contributing to the higher AN content in this region. Significant differences in Acetobacter were observed in maize silage samples from HB, and studies have shown that these gram-negative bacteria can oxidize ethanol into AA (Nie et al. 2017; Cheng et al. 2018). This may be the reason for the higher AA content in the BHJ.M sample from HB. Moreover, the presence of Acetobacter may be related to the use of maize as a raw material because it has a strong specificity for maize (Li and Nishino 2013). Based on the investigation of wet-hot area maize silage, Guan et al. (Guan et al. 2018) speculated that Acetobacter are associated with high temperature and humidity, which is contrary to the results of this study. The high abundance of Acetobacter in maize silage on the cold Qinghai-Tibet Plateau may be more related to the characteristics of the raw material.

Correlation analysis of microbial communities and environmental factors

This study found that different regional distributions significantly impacted the silage quality, and a correlation analysis was conducted between the microbial community and fermentation indicators. Spearman correlation analysis showed that Lactobacillus abundance was positively correlated with LA and AA, and negatively correlated with pH. These associations are consistent with the well-documented ability of Lactobacillus to produceLA and, in the case of heterofermentative species, AA from pentoses (Muck et al. 2018). However, experimental confirmation is required to substantiate these functional links. As the main LAB in silage fermentation, Weissella and Leuconostoc were negatively correlated with fermentation quality in this study, which may be because this study was conducted during the late stages of fermentation, and these two bacteria are more active in the initial stages of fermentation (Guo et al. 2022). Lactobacillus (Loigolactobacillus) coryniformis can improve silage fermentation quality at 10℃ and 15℃ (Wang et al. 2017), indicating it may undergo characteristic changes associated with the climatic changes in cold areas, that is, they can grow without fermentation. The correlation between Lactobacillus and altitude and temperature difference further indicated that the poor quality of silage samples in HN might have been mainly due to the high altitude and large temperature difference, which led to low abundance of Lactobacillus and increase number of miscellaneous bacteria, making the rapid completion of fermentation difficult. Correlation analysis identified temperature difference and altitude as important climatic determinants of major LAB distribution, microbial community structure, and silage quality—a relationship that may be mechanistically attributed to the plateau’s low nocturnal temperatures during the September ensiling period (frequently dropping below 5 °C). The synergistic effect of low mean temperature and high diurnal thermal variability likely suppresses the initial colonization and growth rate of epiphytic homofermentative Lactobacillus species (e.g., L. plantarum), which typically exhibit optimal growth between 30 and 37 °C and markedly reduced metabolic activity below 10 °C (Wang et al. 2017). The Limosilactobacillus and Liquorilactobacillus were positively correlated with PA, and were among the top 10 abundant genera in maize and oat silage, respectively. The production of PA may be due to the different strains of bacteria from different raw materials. Additionally, most strains of Limosilactobacillus can produce extracellular polysaccharides from sucrose (Zheng et al. 2020), which can enhance their survival in stressful environments (Ikai et al. 2020). Thus, further studies on bacterial characteristics could facilitate the development of superior strains for fermentation under adverse conditions. In April 2020, the Lactobacillus was reclassified into 25 genera (including 23 new genera and a revision of Paralactobacillus), and the taxonomic changes of the species were described (Zheng et al. 2020). The Latilactobacillus, formerly known as Lac. sakei, is a widely distributed LAB. Moreover, the Limosilactobacillus corresponds to L. reuteri, Lentilactobacillus (formerly L. buchneri group) is an obligate heterofermentative LAB known to anaerobically convert LA to AA and 1,2-propanediol (Oude Elferink et al. 2001; Gomes et al. 2021). Acetic acid has well-established antifungal activity, and its accumulation is generally associated with improved aerobic stability upon silo opening (Driehuis et al. 1999; Schmidt and Kung 2010). In our study, Lentilactobacillus was significantly enriched in maize silage compared to oat silage. This higher relative abundance raises the possibility that maize silage on the plateau may harbor a naturally greater inherent capacity for aerobic stability via acetic acid production, though this functional inference remains to be directly tested. From a practical perspective, Lentilactobacillus-based inoculants could be particularly suited to maize silage in high-altitude regions, where aerobic spoilage is a concern. Collectively, the negative correlation between Lactobacillus abundance and altitude and diurnal temperature difference, together with the enrichment of Loigolactobacillus and Latilactobacillus in high-altitude samples, indicates that altitude and thermal variability act as environmental filters shaping silage microbial community structure on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau.

Conclusion

This study reveals that on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, high altitude and large diurnal temperature differences constrain Lactobacillus dominance and enrich Loigolactobacillus as well as the psychrotolerant genus Latilactobacillus, leading to inconsistent fermentation quality. These findings highlight the potential for developing locally adapted LAB inoculants to improve silage production in this environment.
