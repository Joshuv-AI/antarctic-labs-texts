Integrating mortality surveillance as a routine component of national essential public health functions: a blueprint for action
Authors: Erin Nichols, Kingsley Arhin-Wiredu, Shelly Bratton, Emily Cercone, Stephen Longa Chanda, Daniel Cobos Muñoz, Joy Ebonwu, Andres Espinosa-Bode, Endang Handzel, Priscilla Kapombe, Daniel Martin, Ronald Moolenaar, William Muhwava, Emily Barigye Atuheire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic and other recent public health responses have highlighted significant challenges in global mortality data collection, with approximately 40% of deaths unregistered and fragmented reporting systems hampering public health responses. This paper supports the establishment of routine mortality surveillance as a core function of essential public health services, emphasising the need for integration with civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) systems. By leveraging existing frameworks and proposing a Theory of Change (ToC), we outline critical linkages between CRVS and mortality surveillance that enhance data accuracy, timeliness and utility for public health decision-making. The paper provides examples across key enablers—policies, agreements and legislation; infrastructure; governance, funding and resources; and people and organisational culture—that facilitate these linkages. We also present examples from initiatives like the Africa CDC’s Mortality Surveillance Programme to illustrate effective strategies in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The ToC serves as a blueprint for stakeholders to implement strategic interventions aimed at overcoming barriers to linking systems and data integration and utilisation. Ultimately, this paper underscores the importance of coordinated efforts among national public health agencies, civil registration authorities and other stakeholders to improve mortality data systems globally. By addressing these systemic challenges through enhanced collaboration and technological innovation, we can significantly advance public health outcomes and policy formulation in response to future health crises.Keywords: Public Health, Epidemiology, Population Surveillance

Introduction

During recent global public health responses, particularly the COVID-19 pandemic, countries have faced significant challenges in producing timely and useful mortality statistics.1 One major issue is that there is a lack of organisational structure and comprehensive data-sharing agreements necessary for timely information exchange critical to public health needs. Various mortality data collection efforts are underway globally, such as civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS), household surveys, health and demographic surveillance sites, health management information systems and sample-based mortality surveillance. However, many countries lack a designated government entity responsible for coordinating these efforts and ensuring they can be leveraged for public health use. This absence hampers the ability to effectively harness available mortality data for improving statistics and informing public health initiatives.2 3

In their call to ‘reimagine mortality surveillance,’ Rao et al emphasise the importance of coordinated linkages between data producers—such as CRVS authorities—and national public health entities.4 To meet this call, there is a pressing need for a routine mortality surveillance function that can compile, harmonise and curate mortality statistics for public health use. Ideally, this function can be integrated into a country’s first essential public health function (EPHF), specifically public health surveillance and monitoring (box 1).5

The United Nations recommends that CRVS systems provide universal birth and death registration coverage guided by extensive international standards. Fulfilling part of their statistical function, such systems can therefore be the optimal data source for timely and reliable mortality surveillance.3 6 Unfortunately, less than one-quarter of countries currently meet standards for a functioning CRVS system, which include provisions for registration in rural and hard-to-reach areas.7 Many efforts are underway to strengthen CRVS systems, particularly those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Efforts include digitising death reporting processes, using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) system for mortality coding, expanding electronic medical records, employing health information management tools like the District Health Information System 2 also known as DHIS2, simplifying verbal autopsy methods for ascertaining probable causes of death when no physician is present and integrating CRVS functions with the health sector.8,10

However, a complex set of factors is required to leverage modern CRVS advancements and establish a routine mortality surveillance function that can readily support countries in using these critical data. This article uses a framework proposed by Zuber et al to outline the necessary linkages and key enablers needed to integrate routine mortality surveillance with CRVS functions and to establish this integration as a routine component of EPHF.11 This article also presents a Theory of Change (ToC) as a blueprint to develop a robust and integrated programme that provides timely and accurate mortality data that are easily accessible and used to inform public health decisions and policies.

The United Nations recommendations call for the release of provisional data through monthly/quarterly bulletins to those with an interest or need for this information.6 Where CRVS systems are recognised as a reliable source of data, appropriate linkages are well established and valid mortality data can be made available in real or near-real time, they can fill the critical role of mortality surveillance in public health preparedness and response.3 6 This role has been demonstrated by recent advancements in the USA, Australia and South Africa. These countries now provide publicly available, near-real-time mortality data for public health use.13,15 Many initiatives are now underway to improve the quality and completeness of CRVS systems in LMICs. However, accompanying efforts to ensure sufficient linkage between CRVS systems and health authorities to support routine mortality surveillance for public health purposes have been limited.4 A redesigned CRVS system that integrates relevant data sources and is agile in producing information can be at the centre of a national routine mortality surveillance function.

Leading the way

The ACDC has been actively working to strengthen public health systems through its Mortality Surveillance Programme. Since 2019, this programme has helped Member States accelerate their efforts to monitor mortality and causes of death.3

To support these initiatives, ACDC has introduced the Continental Framework for Strengthening Mortality Surveillance Systems, accompanied by an operational guide.3 16 This framework outlines key principles and a long-term vision to assist African Union Member States in coordinating and planning effective systems to inform public health decisions and save lives. Notably, it is the first framework of its kind in the world, encouraging national public health institutes and other relevant government entities to establish routine mortality surveillance functions.3

As of 2024, the programme has engaged 55 member states, with 43 drafting action plans. Additionally, 12 member states have begun implementing the Continental Framework through national-level partnerships supported by ACDC.3

Early experiences in establishing and optimising routine mortality surveillance in lower-resource settings are starting to emerge, particularly as they relate to improving CRVS systems. The following sections will highlight observations from these early experiences, showcasing examples of critical linkages and enabling factors essential for developing effective routine mortality surveillance programmes. Complementing the Continental Framework, this article draws on these early experiences to introduce a ToC for integrating mortality systems. The ToC can be used as a blueprint to guide the complex task of efficiently aligning new or improved CRVS and mortality surveillance processes within the robust landscape of existing public health functions and programmes.

Fostering linkages

Zuber et al introduced a new typology to describe five types of linkages for successful public health programming: functional, multifunctional, multisectoral, multilevel and international.11 Such linkages can ensure that mortality data are accurately captured and harmonised, routinely analysed and effectively integrated into public health functions, ultimately improving health outcomes and policy formulation. Examples of each of these linkage types are described below in the context of integrated mortality surveillance.

Functional linkages are ‘linkages between two public health functions.’11 An example includes linkages between CRVS and health functions, whereas death notification and certification of the cause of death are typically mandated activities carried out by health institutions but administered by civil registration authorities. Cross-institutional coordination is needed to ensure alignment in these foundational functions. A second example is linkage between CRVS and public health mortality surveillance functions—a foundational linkage for routine mortality surveillance. Information on fact and causes of death reported through the CRVS system can be used to monitor excess mortality trends and identify deaths of public health concern. Practical applications include using one death report for multiple purposes and harmonising CRVS data management processes (eg, coding) to facilitate timely sharing of data, provisionally, for public health use.6

Multifunctional linkages are ‘linkages between three or more public health functions.’11 An example includes linking CRVS and mortality surveillance with epidemiologic and laboratory functions—for example, where an integrated information platform can connect mortality outcomes and diagnostic laboratory findings. This integration supports epidemiologic analysis with mortality data and diagnostic laboratory capabilities. It also supports important public health research to identify risk factors, determine outbreak severity or evaluate intervention impacts. A second example includes linking CRVS and mortality surveillance with public health response. Through this linkage, routine monitoring can connect public health response functions to trigger appropriate actions based on excess mortality reports, unexplained clusters of deaths, and deaths due to highly pathogenic causes, among other phenomena. These surveillance and response functions can agree on the processes for routine data monitoring, the data signals that will trigger further review or response, and the processes for alerting the response function when signals are detected.

Multisectoral linkages are those ‘between government, private, academic, non-governmental sectors or between sectors within the government.’11 Linkage between civil registration, health, public health and statistics is a core example of this linkage type. As national statistics authorities generate official statistics necessary for mortality measures, including population denominators and population projections, effective linkages among these entities are essential for leveraging CRVS outputs for public health purposes.6 A second example includes linkage between civil registration, health, public health, statistics and medicolegal death investigation systems. Medicolegal death investigation is required when cause of death is unknown or when non-natural causes of death are suspected. Thus, establishing effective linkages between civil registration, health and medicolegal processes ensures integration of non-natural cause data into mortality statistics. Additionally, non-governmental organisations and academia provide technical assistance for many public health efforts, including surveillance, particularly in the areas of capacity development, data collection and system evaluation.17 18

Multilevel linkages are those ‘between entities at national and subnational levels of the health system.’11 An example in the context of integrated mortality surveillance includes engagement of subnational entities at the provincial, district and sub-county level. The detection and reporting of deaths, as well as ascertainment of cause of death, rely on health facilities and community entities managed subnationally. As most deaths in LMICs occur in settings where medical certification of cause of death is unlikely, effective engagement with subnational partners is particularly important for verbal autopsy implementation. Furthermore, the engagement of subnational staff in supervisory and quality control activities is critical for ensuring reliable and timely data from the point of collection.6 The lead public health entity supporting mortality surveillance, in collaboration with CRVS partners, can adapt international guidance to define national data standards, provide electronic data platforms and provide training for staff operating at subnational levels.6 9

Finally, international linkages are those ‘between national entities and foreign governments, companies, associations, donors, or multilateral partners.’11 A relevant example includes international standards and guidelines, whereas international standards from United Nations entities and guidelines from organisations like the US CDC and Africa CDC inform programme implementation and quality improvement. A second example includes development indicator reporting and International Health Regulations (IHR). Global goals such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) have increased demand for improved mortality reporting.19 Eight SDG targets require information on deaths and causes of death (SDGs 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.6, 3.9, 11.5 and 16.1). A routine mortality surveillance programme can help compile, harmonise and curate a country’s mortality data for public health use.6 Improved mortality surveillance also fulfils IHR obligations by strengthening national surveillance and response capacities.20 A third example is linkage with funding and technical support. Organisations like the World Bank, Global Fund, the Gates Foundation and Bloomberg Philanthropies fund initiatives to improve CRVS and health data systems. Various academic institutions, bilateral partners and non-government organisations support implementation research and provide technical assistance. Global efforts like the Health Data Collaborative can coordinate partner agendas to minimise the burden on public health/mortality surveillance programmes.4

The Zuber et al linkage framework is particularly valuable because it highlights key elements in navigating the political and power dynamics inherent in interagency work. The different types of linkages offer insight into what must be considered when aligning stakeholders who bring diverse purposes and interests. Their contributions must be coordinated around mutual benefit and shared objectives in order to overcome institutional silos. Though a full description of the methodology is beyond the scope of this work, we highlight the usefulness of CRDM (Collaborative Requirements Development Methodology) for building the institutional support needed to integrate mortality systems for effective routine mortality surveillance.21 Partners will need to collectively identify common objectives and design systems and processes that are suitable, feasible and acceptable to all contributors, including establishing terms for data sharing, process alignment, financing and incentives for collaboration, among other considerations.

A Theory of Change (ToC)

To support these five linkage types, Zuber et al further describe four types of enabling factors that strengthen the connections between public health functions.11 These include policy, agreements and legislation; infrastructure; governance, funding and resources; and people and organisational culture. Building on reimagined efforts to establish routine mortality surveillance operations as a function of EPHFs, we incorporate these enabling factors into a ToC aimed at significantly enhancing the integration and use of CRVS and mortality data for public health decision-making and interventions.

The ToC serves as a blueprint, helping to navigate existing challenges through strategic interventions that align with the desired future state. It can guide both monitoring and evaluation processes, as well as implementation, to assess progress and make necessary adjustments as needed.22 Given the complex nature of CRVS, mortality data and public health information systems, this ToC also outlines the interrelationships among the interventions needed to achieve the desired outcomes. Since these interventions require a diverse range of skills and expertise, the ToC serves to orient various contributors regarding their specific roles within the broader context of the changes at hand. A pathway to impact can be supported by a collaborative requirements development process that engages the various contributing stakeholders.21

Looking ahead

Although mortality data have historically informed even the first instances of public health surveillance, many countries are establishing new, routine mortality surveillance functions.23 24 It is crucial that these new processes are integrated within the context of existing systems and functions. The steps of the Africa CDC Continental Framework can assist countries in navigating the strategic interventions necessary to achieve a well-integrated mortality surveillance function. These steps include partner engagement, establishment of governance mechanisms, assessing the current state of existing systems and subsystems, identifying opportunities for strengthening mortality surveillance, developing harmonised strategic action plans and evaluating resource needs.3

The ToC presented here serves as a further guide for countries on the steps required to reach their desired future state of an integrated routine mortality surveillance function. The ToC is grounded in a well-researched framework for linking public health functions that, in this case, has been applied to systems supporting CRVS and mortality surveillance.11 The linkage examples provided in this paper, along with the proposed interventions from the ToC, draw on experiences of the past decade of CRVS improvements and emerging practices in establishing routine mortality surveillance systems.

Early ToC implementation examples are beginning to come to fruition, as Chanda et al explored in a retrospective analysis of lessons learnt on expanding verbal autopsy implementation in Zambia.25 In 2022, Zambia began operationalising an integrated mortality surveillance approach by establishing a National Mortality Surveillance Subcommittee to coordinate CRVS, health, public health and statistical institutions. This integrated approach sought to expand an initial sentinel verbal autopsy implementation platform introduced in partnership between Zambia’s health and civil registration authorities to population representative verbal autopsy implementation. The approach brought an enhanced focus on the representativeness and usability of existing mortality data sources. To ensure the production of routine mortality surveillance data products, the Zambia National Public Health Institute established a Mortality Surveillance Unit and began coordinating stakeholders through the Subcommittee. The Subcommittee has assessed the current status and established future plans for more sustainable financing and governance structures, electronic integration and infrastructure improvements, and enhanced design and quality improvements of mortality systems. This integrated mortality surveillance approach aims to consolidate and harmonise data collection and processing, including routine quality checks, to improve timeliness and subnational visibility. These actions reflect the components of the ToC by strengthening governance, improving data acquisition, modernising ICT systems and infrastructure, and embedding sustainable mortality surveillance practices.25 Further validation of the ToC and compilation of country-specific experiences is underway; an example illustrating how these linkages have been implemented in the USA, Colombia and Zambia is forthcoming.

While this work supports the establishment or improvement of a critical public health function, it is important to acknowledge the challenges and trade-offs when implementing new public health programming. For example, in pursuit of sustainable solutions, many mortality data improvement efforts have been designed to be implemented by existing government or community cadres. Accordingly, and new tasks pose an increased burden on these cadres, especially frontline health workers.26 Also, while improved processes have been grounded in updated legal frameworks and enhanced governance, these efforts generally start with centralised or national-level functions. Concerted effort is needed to ensure benefits reach the local level—a particularly critical feature for effective public health programming.27 Furthermore, public health data can be politically sensitive and may be subject to misuse.28 Attention must be given to building and maintaining trust and security in the data and the organisations that generate it.

Given the complexity of the mortality data landscape—especially in LMICs—siloed, vertical programmatic approaches to mortality surveillance will not adequately address urgent public health needs moving forward. The blueprint for change presented here illustrates how various programmatic areas—including CRVS, public health programming, health informatics, and public health surveillance and response—can be effectively interconnected. By observing, supporting and learning from countries that are beginning to adopt emerging guidance for routine mortality surveillance, we can reshape the fundamental role of such functions within public health to better inform future action.

Conclusions

The COVID-19 pandemic has prompted a renewed focus on the fundamental role of mortality data in supporting public health action globally. Addressing gaps in critical linkages and enabling factors allows for better integration of CRVS and mortality data within public health systems, particularly in low-resource settings. Political commitment is essential for bridging these gaps. Such commitment empowers leadership to convene and guide partners, fostering the coordination and collaboration necessary for enhancing system and data integration. Additionally, tackling challenges related to data ownership, sharing and confidentiality requires updating outdated legislation to promote transparency while safeguarding sensitive information. Realising the enabling factors that establish connections between CRVS, mortality surveillance and public health response functions helps ensure that CRVS and mortality data are accurately captured, harmonised, routinely analysed and effectively integrated into public health initiatives. This comprehensive approach will ultimately lead to improved health outcomes and more informed policy formulation.
