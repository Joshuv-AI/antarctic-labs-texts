Effect of a mixture of peppermint and clove oils on growth performance, carcass traits, blood parameters, and gut microbiota in broiler chickens
Authors: Fayiz M Reda, Mahmoud Kamal, Hemat K Mahmoud, Hassan A Rudayni, Ahmed A Allam, Asmaa F Khafaga, Mohammad MH Khan, Reda S Mohamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to assess the effect of supplementing Arbor Acre broiler chick diets with a blend of peppermint and clove oils on growth efficiency, blood indicators, and intestinal microbial communities. In a randomized experimental design, 250 unsexed, one-day-old Arbor Acre broiler chicks were allocated into 5 groups. Each group included 5 replicates, each containing 10 birds. The control group administered a basal diet devoid of oils, whereas the remaining groups were provided with meals enriched with 150, 300, 450, or 600 mg/kg of the oil blend. The findings indicated significant variations (P ≤ 0.05) among the treatment groups during weeks 3 to 6—supplementation with the oil blend led to increased body weight (BW) and body weight gain (BWG). Compared to the control, birds receiving the oil consumed less feed intake (FI) and exhibited an improved feed conversion ratio (FCR). Additionally, the oil-supplemented diets significantly (P ≤ 0.05) decreased abdominal fat and altered the relative weights of the gizzard, giblets, spleen, thymus, and bursa. Except for hemoglobin, all hematological indicators were substantially influenced by the oil supplementation. Biochemical analysis revealed significant reductions (P ≤ 0.05) in serum levels of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), urea, creatinine, cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and glucose. Conversely, total protein, albumin, globulin, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels significantly increased (P ≤ 0.05). Oil supplementation also enhanced immune response and antioxidant enzyme activities, as evidenced by significant increases (P ≤ 0.05) in total oxidant capacity (TAC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), reduced glutathione (GSH), and catalase (CAT), along with a notable decrease in malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Moreover, birds fed diets containing the oil blend exhibited changes in digestive enzyme activity and blood mineral levels. Microbiological assessments showed decreased counts of Salmonella and E. coli in the oil-treated groups. In conclusion, dietary supplementation with peppermint and clove oils improved growth performance, enhanced gut microbiota balance, and positively influenced carcass traits and biochemical markers in broiler chickens.Keywords: Peppermint and clove oils, Growth performance, Carcass features, Physiological traits, Microbiota, Broilers

Introduction

The global demand for affordable and highly palatable sources of protein has driven substantial growth in the poultry industry (Bist et al., 2024; El-Abasy et al., 2025). For many years, antibiotics were commonly used to promote growth and protect poultry from pathogenic microorganisms (Yang et al., 2024). However, the European Union has banned the use of antibiotics due to the significant risks of the emergence of resistant bacteria and the presence of their residues in livestock products, which lead to negative effects (Schmerold et al., 2023). In response, natural alternatives such as plant-derived supplements have gained increasing attention for their ability to enhance immunity, growth performance, and carcass traits in poultry (Kamal et al., 2023a; Abd El-Hack et al., 2024a, b).

Essential oils have emerged as potential alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters in broiler diets owing to their intrinsic antibacterial and anti-inflammatory characteristics (Zhang et al., 2022). The use of these natural compounds not only supports better performance and gastrointestinal health but also improves meat features and oxidative stability, while reducing the risks linked with antibiotic residues and resistance (Ashour et al., 2025a, b). Among these natural additives, peppermint essential oil (PEO) has been widely studied and shown to produce favorable effects in broiler production (El-Hack et al., 2023). PEO has demonstrated potential as both a growth promoter and a natural antimicrobial agent, supporting the proliferation of beneficial gut bacteria (Aguiar et al., 2023; Ashour et al., 2025b; El-Abbasy et al., 2025). It contains a complex mixture of bioactive secondary metabolites with diverse pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, immunomodulatory, anticancer, neuroprotective, and antioxidant activities. Key components of PEO include menthol, menthone, neomenthol, and iso-menthone (Bardaweel et al., 2018). Various species of mint and clove oils are also recognized for their rich profiles of active constituents like carvone, limonene, menthone, β-pinene, α-pinene, geraniol, and menthol, contributing to their medicinal value (El-Maati et al., 2016; Aydın and Barbas, 2020).

Studies have reported that peppermint oil improves feed efficiency and weight gain in poultry, largely due to its bioactive compounds, particularly menthol (Abdel-Wareth et al., 2019). Its antimicrobial action has been shown to reduce intestinal infections caused by pathogens such as E. coli and Salmonella (Zhao et al., 2022), while also enhancing nutrient absorption by modulating the gut microbiota in favor of beneficial species (Salinas-Chavira and Barrios-García, 2024). Copper exposure caused injury to chicken livers, disturbed metabolic processes, and mediated autophagy primarily through the AMPK-mTOR axis (Chen et al., 2024). PEO has also been reported to boost immune function, reduce inflammation, and potentially lower ammonia emissions in poultry houses by improving nitrogen utilization (Abdel-Wareth et al., 2012). Its natural origin makes it a safer alternative to synthetic antibiotics, aligning with the global shift toward antibiotic-free animal production systems (Jabbar et al., 2024).

Clove and its essential oil have also been investigated for their growth-promoting potential in poultry, particularly due to their capacity to enhance gut microbial equilibrium (Valdez et al., 2023). Furthermore, Mohammadi et al. (2014) found that dietary clove oil enhanced feed intake, growth rate, and weight gain without adversely affecting serum lipid levels or gut microflora. Similarly, Suliman et al. (2021) suggested that diets enriched with clove seeds may function as potent natural substitutes for antibiotics, resulting in safe, health-promoting poultry products for consumers. So, this study aimed to assess the impact of adding nutrients with a natural blend of peppermint and clove essential oils on growth efficiency, carcass traits, blood criteria, and bacterial populations in broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and ethical approval

The experiment was conducted at the Poultry Research Farm, Faculty of Agriculture, Zagazig University. All experimental protocols received approval from the Department of Poultry Ethics Committee and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Zagazig University. These approvals ensured that all procedures adhered to ethical standards for the care and use of animals in scientific research.

Birds, diets, and experimental design

A total of 250 one-day-old Arbor Acre chicks with uniform initial body weights were randomly allocated into five treatment groups using a completely randomized design. Each treatment included five replicates, with ten unsexed chicks per replicate. The investigation continued for six weeks following hatching. All diets were formulated according to NRC, National Research Council, 1994 guidelines to meet the birds' nutritional requirements. During the testing period, birds were administered pelleted meals (days 1 to 42), as illustrated in Table 1. The feeding initiative was segmented into two stages: starter (1–3 weeks) and finisher (4–6 weeks).

The groups were organized as follows: the control group received a basal diet with no additives, while the remaining four groups were supplemented with 150, 300, 450, or 600 mg/kg of a 1:1 mixture of peppermint and clove essential oils. All chicks were housed in conventional cages (100 × 100 × 50 cm), provided with ad libitum access to feed and fresh water. Environmental conditions were controlled, and a lighting schedule of 23 h light and 1 h dark was maintained throughout the study.

Growth efficiency and carcass evaluation

Body weight (BW) and body weight gain (BWG) were recorded weekly. Average daily feed intake (FI) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated accordingly. At the end of the trial (week 6), five birds from each group were selected, based on the group’s average body weight, and slaughtered after a 12-hour feed withdrawal period to assess carcass traits.

To minimize transport stress, slaughter was carried out at a facility adjacent to the experimental site. Birds were weighed pre-slaughtered, and after exsanguination, feathers, heads, feet, tails, and internal organs were removed. Carcasses, abdominal fat, liver, gizzard, spleen, and the bursa of Fabricius were individually weighed, and their relative weights were calculated as percentages of BW to determine carcass traits.

Blood sampling and biochemical analysis

At six weeks of age, we took blood specimens from the wing veins of five randomly selected birds per group before slaughter. Two types of tubes were used: heparinized tubes for hematological analysis and plain tubes for serum separation. Hematological variables, including red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin concentration (Hb), and white blood cell (WBC), were measured using an NIHON KOHDEN automated hematology analyzer, following the protocol of Abd El-Hack et al. (2017).

The blood in plain tubes was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 minutes to separate serum. Serum samples were analyzed for total protein (TP), albumin, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), creatinine, uric acid, and liver enzymes (ALT and AST), using standard biochemical kits according to Young and Friedman (2001). Serum globulin was determined by deducting albumin from TP. Immunoglobulins IgA, IgY, and IgM were quantified utilizing commercial ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

To assess oxidative stress and antioxidant activity, serum concentrations of total antioxidant capacity (TAC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA), and reduced glutathione (GSH) were measured. TAC was evaluated utilizing a spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) and Randox commercial kits, as described by Erel (2004). Antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT) were determined based on the method of Wang et al. (2011), while MDA and GSH levels were assessed using protocols modified from Miranda et al. (2001).

Serum concentrations of K, Na, Ca, and P are quantified using automated clinical chemistry analyzers employing standard colorimetric methods. Serum Iron (Fe) levels are determined using colorimetric assays (ferrozine method).

Digestive enzymes

The digestive enzymes (amylase, lipase, and protease) were assessed in the ileal digesta of broilers after the study period. Researchers dissected the broilers' ileum, which extends from Meckel’s diverticulum to 2 cm above the ileocecal junction, and aseptically collected the ileal contents in sterile specimen vials with screw caps. The activities of amylase, lipase, and protease were assessed in the digestive samples following the methodology established by Najafi et al. (2006).

Microbiological analysis

At the end of the trial, cecal content specimens were collected from five chickens per group under sterile conditions and stored at –20°C until analysis. Total bacterial count (TBC), E. coli, Salmonella spp., and coliforms were determined following standard microbiological methods. Colony-forming units (CFU) were quantified utilizing a digital colony counter (Scan 300, Interscience, France) and reported as CFU per gram of cecal material.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) under a completely randomized design utilizing the General Linear Model (GLM) procedures of SPSS (version 27). Duncan’s multiple range test was used to compare means at a substantial level of P < 0.05, unless otherwise stated. Linear and quadratic effects of the EO doses were also assessed using orthogonal contrasts. All graphical representations were generated utilizing GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Carcass traits

Table 3 summarizes the influence of peppermint and clove oil on carcass characteristics. Supplementation significantly influenced (P ≤ 0.05) the relative weights of the gizzard, giblets, spleen, thymus, bursa of Fabricius, and abdominal fat, while liver and heart weights were unaffected. The 300 and 450 mg/kg treatments resulted in significantly higher (P ≤ 0.05) percentage weights of the gizzard, giblets, spleen, thymus, and bursa compared to the control. In contrast, birds in these same groups showed a marked reduction in abdominal fat percentage. The results of the linear and quadratic effects of essential oil doses were not different from the results of the overall treatments.

Immune parameters

As demonstrated in Table 5, dietary inclusion of the peppermint and clove oil mixture significantly (P ≤ 0.05) enhanced immune-related markers, including IgY, IgA, IgM, complement component 3 (C3), and lysozyme activity. A dose-dependent increase was observed, with the best IgM levels in birds fed 450, 300, and 600 mg/kg, respectively. IgY levels were highest in the 300, 450, and 600 mg/kg groups, while IgA, C3, and lysozyme concentrations peaked in birds receiving 600, 450, and 300 mg/kg, respectively. The findings regarding the linear and quadratic effects of essential oil dosages were consistent with the overall treatments. These findings suggest enhanced humoral and innate immune responses associated with oil supplementation.

On the other hand, the results of the overall treatment and linear effects were significant on TAC, while the results of the quadratic effects were insignificant.

Discussion

The growing concern over antibiotic resistance and residue contamination in poultry products has prompted researchers and producers to explore natural alternatives to in-feed antibiotics. Among these, essential oils such as peppermint and clove have garnered attention for their bioactive properties, involving antimicrobial, antioxidant, and growth-promoting effects (Abd El-Hack et al., 2024c; Mahgoub et al., 2024). Herbs, spices, and other naturally occurring plant chemicals are under evaluation as substitutes for antibiotics and almost certainly have growth-promoting effects, antibacterial action, and other health-related benefits (Naiel et al., 2020; Abd El-Hack et al., 2025).

In the current study, dietary supplementation with a peppermint and clove oil mixture at 300 mg/kg significantly enhanced BW and BWG, particularly during the third and sixth weeks of growth. These improvements were accompanied by reduced FI and improved FCR, suggesting better feed efficiency. Similar outcomes were reported by Suliman et al. (2021) and Dong et al. (2024), who demonstrated improved growth performance and profitability in broilers receiving clove-based or mixed essential oil diets. Moreover, Shil et al. (2023) found that the broiler chicken diet's nutritional supplements of probiotics and clove oil might raise BWG and FCR. Additionally, Ghazanfari et al. (2024) found that incorporating 150 ppm peppermint essential oil and 300 ppm artifier in a low-energy diet for broiler chickens improved growth. This not only improved meat quality but also improved growth performance and efficiency, indicating the potential benefits of these additives. In another study, Hussein et al. (2023) found that mixed peppermint and clove oils considerably reduced BWG, FCR, and performance index in chickens. The presence of compounds such as menthol, eugenol, cineole, and linalool may explain these outcomes through their positive influence on appetite, gut health, and nutrient absorption (Abdel-Wareth et al., 2019; Meligy et al., 2023).

However, inconsistencies in the literature exist. For instance, Kahiel et al. (2025) and Mohammadi et al. (2014) found no adverse effects on growth performance at higher essential oil concentrations. These discrepancies could be attributed to variations in dosage, oil composition, and environmental conditions.

The improved carcass traits observed in this study, including increased gizzard, spleen, thymus, and bursa weights, and reduced abdominal fat, are in line with findings by Abd El-Hack et al. (2015) and Hussein et al. (2023). The increase in abdominal fat found in this study was like what Dalkiliç and G€uler (2009) reported, where they saw a significant (P < 0.05) reduction in abdominal fat when broilers were fed a diet with a lot of clove extract. Conversely, the features of the carcass and nutritional supplements of essential oils at 300 mg/kg revealed a non-significant impact (Alp et al., 2012). The reduction in abdominal fat may be associated with enhanced lipid metabolism due to the bioactive components of the oils. Conversely, some studies reported no significant effect of essential oils on carcass yield, which may relate to variation in plant sources or extract concentrations (Cabuk et al., 2006; Soltani and Nobakht, 2016).

Hematological and biochemical parameters serve as reliable indicators of animal health (Adeyeye et al., 2020; Kamal et al., 2023b). In this study, peppermint and clove oil supplementation led to increased RBC and WBC and slightly reduced glucose levels, reflecting improved immune function and metabolic balance. These findings support earlier research suggesting essential oils enhance nutrient absorption and hematopoiesis (Wakenell, 2010; Awad et al., 2022). Additionally, serum protein and globulin levels increased, while ALT, creatinine, uric acid, and lipid profiles (TC, TG, LDL) improved, with a concurrent rise in HDL. These biochemical enhancements mirror those observed by Jin and Cho (2011) and Hussein et al. (2023), who attributed lipid-lowering effects to eugenol-mediated inhibition of hepatic cholesterol synthesis.

Bioactive constituents such as eugenol, which influence lipid metabolism, can explain the noted decrease in total cholesterol and triglycerides in broiler hens treated with clove oil extract. Eugenol inhibits hepatic cholesterol synthesis by downregulating HMG-CoA reductase activity, a crucial enzyme in the mevalonate pathway (Al-Okbi et al., 2014). Moreover, clove oil promotes bile acid excretion, hence decreasing cholesterol absorption (Srinivasan, 2013). The antioxidant capabilities of clove polyphenols may alleviate dyslipidemia associated with oxidative stress and enhance lipid profiles (Khalil et al., 2017). These processes collectively elucidate the hypolipidemic effects observed in avian investigations.

The immune-enhancing effects of the peppermint and clove oil mixture were also evident. Elevated levels of immunoglobulins (IgA, IgY, IgM), complement (C3), and lysozyme indicate enhanced humoral and innate immune responses. This immune stimulation may be owed to the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory characteristics of menthol and eugenol, which support immune organ development and modulate cytokine activity (Elbaz et al., 2023). Peppermint and clove oils in broiler feed enhance immunity levels, improve health, and support immune responses by containing antioxidant, antimicrobial, and immunomodulating compounds, enhancing appetite and nutrient absorption (Hussein et al., 2023). The oils may also contribute to gut health by promoting beneficial microbes and reducing pathogenic bacterial load.

Notably, the antioxidant capacity of broilers improved with increasing oil supplementation, as evidenced by elevated levels of TAC, SOD, CAT, and GSH, and reduced MDA. These results confirm previous findings that essential oils enhance the oxidative defense system, reducing cellular damage and supporting overall health. Hussein et al. (2023) found that adding clove oil to poultry feed reduces serum cholesterol levels, total lipids, and LDL, in addition to the concentration of MDA, while increasing the levels of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activities. The findings of the current experiment align with those of Al-Kassie (2010), who indicated a significant rise in serum levels of glutathione peroxidase activity in broiler diets supplemented with cinnamon oils. Serum MDA levels were considerably reduced in the cinnamon oil groups compared to the control group. Yang et al. (2019) found analogous findings, demonstrating that broilers treated with 50, 100, 200, 400, or 800 mg of cinnamon oil per kg of feed significantly affected liver MDA levels at 21 days of age. Furthermore, Sarica et al. (2009) argued that quail meals supplemented with essential oils led to a decrease in plasma TC levels compared to the basal diet.

The study indicates that the essential oil mixture markedly affects serum mineral concentrations in broilers, particularly K, Fe, Ca, and P, while having little impact on Na. The greatest potassium concentrations were noted in the 450 mg/kg treatment group, indicating a dose-dependent regulation of potassium homeostasis, potentially attributable to increased absorption or decreased excretion facilitated by bioactive chemicals in the essential oils (Brenes and Roura, 2010). Iron levels reached their zenith at various dosages (300, 450, and 600 mg/kg), suggesting that the essential oil mix may augment Fe bioavailability, either through the chelation of inhibitors or enhancement of intestinal absorption (Surai, 2020). The absence of impact on sodium indicates that the essential oils do not disrupt sodium regulation, which is predominantly governed by renal and hormonal processes (Shirreffs, 2001).

The results indicate that calcium and phosphorus levels were elevated in the 450 and 600 mg/kg groups, implying that the essential oil mix may enhance mineral metabolism, potentially by improving intestinal absorption or decreasing mineral sequestration. This conclusion corresponds with prior research indicating that phytogenic additions can enhance the expression of calcium-binding proteins and phosphate transporters in the gastrointestinal tract (Platel and Srinivasan, 2016). The varying effects among minerals underscore the complex relationship between essential oils and mineral balance, necessitating additional investigation into the fundamental mechanisms, including influences on gut flora or enzyme function (Windisch et al., 2008).

The increase in digestive enzyme activity (amylase, lipase, and protease) in broilers treated with a blend of peppermint and clove oil corresponds with before research on essential oils (EOs) as digestive stimulants. The dose-dependent elevation in amylase activity, peaking at 600 mg/kg, indicates enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, potentially attributable to EO-induced stimulation of pancreatic secretion or synthesis of intestinal brush-border enzymes (Platel and Srinivasan, 2004; Hashemipour et al., 2013). The simultaneous elevations in lipase activity at various dosages (300–600 mg/kg) may indicate the emulsifying characteristics of the oils or the manipulation of bile acid secretion, as evidenced by Zeng et al. (2015) with analogous phytogenic chemicals. The peak protease activity at 300 mg/kg suggests a lower optimum threshold for enhancing protein digestion, likely attributable to less oxidative stress on proteolytic enzymes at moderate dosages (Hong et al., 2012). The findings collectively substantiate the hypothesis that essential oils improve nutrient hydrolysis efficiency through various mechanisms; however, excessive concentrations may reduce efficacy due to bioactive compound saturation or antimicrobial effects on gut microbiota that regulate enzymes (Brenes and Roura, 2010). Moreover, essential oils enhance villus height and crypt depth, hence augmenting enzyme production sites (Yang et al., 2016).

Cloves, abundant in eugenol, predominantly enhance total nutritional digestibility (Suliman et al., 2021). This effect is predominantly regarded as indirect, originating from the favorable regulation of gut microbiota. Eugenol can enhance beneficial microorganisms such as Lactobacillus (Giannenas et al., 2012). An optimized gut microbial equilibrium promotes the efficacy of the digestive milieu and its associated activities (Arif et al., 2022). Consequently, peppermint and cloves facilitate digestion mostly by promoting intestinal health.

The antimicrobial characteristics of peppermint and clove essential oils are extensively documented, with their active constituents, menthol and eugenol, demonstrating potent bactericidal activity against Gram-negative pathogens, such as Salmonella, coliforms, and E. coli, through the disruption of bacterial cell membranes and the inhibition of critical enzymatic functions (Burt, 2004). Their incorporation into broiler diets has been demonstrated to selectively alter the cecal microbiota, inhibiting pathogenic bacteria while promoting beneficial lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus spp.), likely attributable to the oils' capacity to foster a conducive gut environment for probiotic proliferation (Omonijo et al., 2018). This transition fosters gut homeostasis by enabling Lactobacillus spp. to facilitate pathogen exclusion, regulate immunological responses, and generate short-chain fatty acids, thereby enhancing overall intestinal health (Ricke, 2015). The targeted antibacterial properties of these essential oils underscore their potential as natural substitutes for antibiotics in chicken production. Healthier gut microbiota contributes to improved nutrient utilization, immune function, and disease resistance. Additionally, Peppermint and clove oil additions to broiler chicken diets can drastically change the gut microbiota composition, lower harmful bacteria, and increase helpful ones (Elbaz et al., 2023). The rise in LAC also points to a proliferation of lactic acid-producing bacteria, which are beneficial for gut health. Often used in conjunction with essential oils, probiotics also help boost Lactobacillus, restoring gut microbial balance.

Conclusion

This study illustrated that dietary supplementation with a combination of peppermint and clove oils, particularly at a level of 300 mg/kg, significantly enhanced growth efficiency, feed efficiency, and physiological well-being in broilers. This enhancement was accompanied by favorable modulation of hematological and biochemical parameters, enhancement of antioxidant enzyme activity, and a beneficial shift in cecal microbiota characterized by a reduction in pathogenic bacteria such as E. coli and Salmonella. Collectively, these outcomes indicate that peppermint and clove oils can serve as effective natural alternatives to growth promoters, supporting not only improved performance but also better immune status and intestinal health in broiler production systems.
