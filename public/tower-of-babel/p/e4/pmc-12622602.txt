Association between red blood cell distribution width-hematocrit ratio and 28-Day all-cause mortality in patients with acute pancreatitis in ICU: A retrospective study
Authors: Qianyi Ma, Yuanmeng Wu, Fengming Niu, Liping He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a sudden inflammatory condition of the pancreas that can lead to severe systemic complications and high mortality, especially in critically ill patients. Red cell distribution width-to-hematocrit ratio (RDH) has emerged as a potential prognostic biomarker in critical care. This study aimed to explore the association between RDH and 28-day mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with AP. This retrospective observational cohort study utilized data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV, v3.1) database. Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to assess the relationship between RDH and 28-day all-cause mortality. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, along with area under the curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement, and integrated discrimination improvement, was performed to evaluate the incremental prognostic value of RDH beyond sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) and SAPSⅡ scores. Survival curves and subgroup analyses were performed based on pertinent covariates. A total of 1126 ICU patients with AP were included. RDH were significantly associated with 28-day all-cause mortality. In multivariable Cox models, the highest RDH quartile exhibited a 5.52-fold increased mortality risk (heart rate: 6.52; 95% confidence intervals: 2.88–14.7; P <.05) compared to those in the lowest quartile. The relationship was linear (P for nonlinearity >.05). Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated low survival rates in the highest RDH quartile (P <.001). RDH alone showed modest discrimination (AUC = 0.640), while SOFA (AUC = 0.724) and SAPSⅡ (AUC = 0.768) performed better. Adding RDH improved model performance (SOFA + RDH AUC = 0.743; SAPSⅡ+RDH AUC = 0.792) with significant net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement gains. RDH is an independent predictor of 28-day mortality in ICU patients with AP. Higher RDH values are linked with increased clinical severity and mortality risk.

1. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a necroinflammatory pancreatic disorder caused by autodigestive injury to acinar cells.[1] The global incidence of AP is approximately 34 per 100,000 individuals, with an increasing trend.[2,3] While most cases are mild, 20–30% of patients develop severe AP, and approximately 2.9% require intensive care unit (ICU) admission.[4] Early identification of biomarkers for AP severity is critical for timely intervention and improved outcomes in critically ill patients.

Red cell distribution width (RDW), a measure of variation in red blood cell size, is a routine component of complete blood counts.[5] Recent studies have linked elevated RDW to inflammation and poor prognosis in various conditions, including hypertension,[6] coronary artery disease,[7] cerebral hemorrhage,[8] pulmonary arterial hypertension,[9] and acute kidney injury (AKI).[10] RDW has also been identified as a prognostic indicator in severe AP.[11] Hematocrit (HCT), which reflects the proportion of red blood cells in whole blood, is associated with blood viscosity and oxygen-carrying capacity. HCT levels have been correlated with adverse outcomes in AP and other diseases, such as cardiovascular and lung disorders.[12–14]

The red blood cell distribution width-hematocrit ratio (RDH) has shown superior predictive power in certain conditions, such as acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.[15] However, its prognostic value in AP remains unexplored. This study aims to evaluate the relationship between RDH and 28 days mortality in ICU patients with AP, providing a scientific basis for early risk stratification and optimized treatment strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the MIMIC-IV database, including more than 70,000 patients admitted to the emergency department or ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, MA, USA) between 2008 and 2022.[16,17] The Institutional Review Board of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center approved the creation of the database and waived informed consent for data sharing. The primary author, Qianyi Ma, obtained access to the database (Certification No. 67704674) and performed data extraction.

2.2. Study population

Patients were chosen based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9) codes for AP. Exclusion criteria included: non-first admissions and ICU survival ≤ 24h; missing values of RDW and HCT; age <18 years old.

2.3. Data extraction

The clincal data included: demographics information such as age, sex, race. Vital signs, including heart rate (HR), mean blood pressure, respiratory rate (RR). Disease severity scores, including sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA), simplified acute physiology score II (SAPS II). Comorbidities such as hypertension, heart failure (HF), sepsis, AKI. Laboratory parameters, including platelet count, RDW, HCT, white blood cell count (WBC), potassium, Aspartate Aminotransferase(AST), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), Prothrombin Time(PT), Partial Thromboplastin Time(PTT). Treatments including vasopressin, octreotide, fibrates, vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy.

2.4. RDH calculation and outcomes

RDW and HCT were calculated as the mean of measurements taken within 24h of ICU admission. RDH was calculated as RDW (%) divided by HCT (%). The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality in the ICU.

3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using DecisionLinnc software (version 1.0).[18] Variables with >20% missing data were excluded, while those with <20% missing data were imputed using multiple imputation. Continuous variables were reported as medians (P25-P75) and analyzed using t-tests (normal distribution) or Kruskal-Wallis tests (non-normal distribution). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%) and analyzed with chi-square tests.

RDH values were divided into quartiles. Univariate Cox regression identified risk factors for 28-day mortality. Variance inflation factor was calculated to assess multicollinearity, with variance inflation factor <5 indicating acceptable levels. Multivariable Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Four models were used for regression analyses: Model 1 included no adjustments; Model 2 was adjusted for baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and race; Model 3 further adjusted for vital signs (HR, mean blood pressure, RR), disease scores (SOFA, SAPSII), comorbidities (hypertension, HF, sepsis, AKI), laboratory parameters (platelet count, RDW, HCT, WBC, potassium, AST, creatinine, BUN, PT, PTT); Model 4 added treatments (asopressin, octreotide, fibrates, vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy) to model 3. Restricted cubic spline analysis assessed nonlinearity between RDH and mortality. Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests compared survival across RDH quartiles. Subgroup analyses explored interactions by age, gener, race, hyperlipidemia, vasopressin and octreotide. To further evaluate the incremental prognostic value of RDH beyond established severity scores, model performance metrics were assessed. Receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for RDH, SOFA, SAPSⅡ, and their combinations. In addition, net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement were computed to quantify the added predictive value of RDH when incorporated into these models.

4.2. Clinical outcomes

In Cox regression analyses, RDH was significantly associated with 28-day mortality. When modeled as a continuous variable per 1 standard deviation increase, the risk of death remained robust across different levels of adjustment. In the unadjusted model, the HR was 1.62 (95% CI: 1.45–1.81, P <.001). After sequential adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory tests, and treatment variables, the association persisted (fully adjusted model: HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.15–1.49, P <.001) (Table 2). Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed the lowest survival in Q4 (P <.001; Fig. 2). A significant linear association was observed between RDH and 28-day all-cause mortality (P for nonlinearity >.05; Fig. 3 and Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q585). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated that RDH alone had modest discriminative ability for 28-day mortality prediction (AUC = 0.640, 95% CI: 0.596–0.684). SOFA and SAPSⅡ performed better, with AUC of 0.724 (95% CI: 0.687–0.762) and 0.768 (95% CI: 0.733–0.802), respectively. Importantly, the addition of RDH improved model performance, increasing the AUC to 0.743 (95% CI: 0.707–0.780) for SOFA + RDH and 0.792 (95% CI: 0.759–0.826) for SAPSⅡ+RDH (Fig. 4). Table 3 further shows that adding RDH significantly improved risk stratification, with positive net reclassification improvement [SOFA + RDH: 0.355 (95% CI: 0.207–0.504); SAPSⅡ+RDH: 0.392 (95% CI: 0.244–0.540), both P <.001] and integrated discrimination improvement [SOFA + RDH: 0.033 (95% CI: 0.018–0.048); SAPSⅡ+RDH: 0.044 (95% CI: 0.026–0.062), both P <.001]. These results indicate that RDH provides incremental prognostic value beyond SOFA and SAPSⅡ scores.

4.3. Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses showed that the predictive value of RDH was largely consistent across categories of sex, race, comorbidities, and treatment use. Notably, the association appeared stronger in patients aged <60 years (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.79–2.41) compared with those aged ≥ 60 years (HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.10–1.53; P for interaction <.001). Similar trends were observed in other subgroups, although no significant interactions were detected except for age and vasopressin use (Fig. 5).

5. Discussion

This study provides robust evidence for the prognostic value of the RDH in predicting 28-day all-cause mortality in ICU patients with AP. The RDH integrates RDW, which reflects inflammatory status, and HCT, which indicates hemodynamic status, offering a comprehensive assessment of AP severity. RDH can be calculated upon admission without additional testing, facilitating early identification of high-risk patients and guiding clinical decision-making.

Notably, in subgroup analysis, a significant interaction was observed between age and the association of the red blood cell distribution RDH with 28-day all-cause mortality in ICU patients with AP(P for interaction <.05), with RDH showing a stronger prognostic effect in patients younger than 65 years (HR: 15.52, 95% CI: 4.08–59.05; P <.05) compared to those aged 65 years or older (HR: 1.43, 95% CI: 0.41–5.01; P >.05). This suggests that RDH is a more potent predictor of mortality in younger patients, possibly due to lower baseline RDW levels in this group, which enhance RDH sensitivity to acute inflammation, or due to differences in AP etiology, such as alcohol-related AP being more common in younger individuals, eliciting a stronger inflammatory response reflected by RDH. In contrast, older patients’ higher baseline RDW, comorbidities, or blunted inflammatory responses may reduce RDH’s specificity, with competing mortality risks overshadowing its prognostic value. Clinically, RDH’s enhanced utility in younger patients underscores its role in guiding aggressive monitoring, while in older patients, it should be interpreted alongside other factors. Future studies should elucidate the mechanisms driving this interaction and validate age-specific RDH thresholds to optimize risk stratification in AP.

The prognostic value of RDW and HCT has been extensively explored in various medical conditions. RDW, a measure of erythrocyte volume heterogeneity, has been associated with adverse outcomes in cardiovascular diseases, including HF, coronary heart disease, and myocardial infarction.[19] In respiratory diseases, elevated RDW predicts worse outcomes in COPD and pulmonary hypertension. Furthermore, RDW has shown prognostic significance in rheumatoid arthritis, progressive inflammatory states, and cancer. In the context of AP, Yao et al.’s[20] cross-sectional study demonstrated that RDW values were significantly higher in AP non-survivors compared to survivors and healthy controls, with a cutoff value of 14.2 yielding 75.0% sensitivity and 89.8% specificity for predicting mortality. RDW is also linked to AP severity, with significantly higher values in severe AP compared to mild AP and healthy controls.

RDW and HCT play distinct yet interconnected roles in the pathophysiology of AP. RDW reflects multiple mechanisms driven by the systemic inflammatory response in AP. Inflammatory mediators, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, inhibit erythrocyte production and maturation, increasing erythrocyte volume heterogeneity.[21] Oxidative stress, a key factor in AP pathogenesis, may damage erythrocyte membrane integrity, shortening erythrocyte lifespan. Additionally, proinflammatory factors can reduce endothelial nitric oxide production, which normally stimulates erythroid progenitor cell proliferation, leading to abnormal erythrocyte production.[22] HCT, on the other hand, is closely tied to fluid dynamics in AP. Early in the disease, inflammation-mediated vascular permeability and “third space” fluid extravasation reduce effective circulating blood volume, elevating HCT. As fluid resuscitation progresses, HCT values may decrease, making HCT monitoring critical for guiding therapy to prevent exacerbation of AP severity and organ dysfunction.

HCT, an indicator of blood viscosity and oxygen-carrying capacity, has been studied in AP for its role in fluid status and circulatory function. Wu et al.[23] identified early HCT elevation, alongside BUN, as an independent risk factor for AP mortality. Early fluid resuscitation, guided by dynamic HCT changes, can mitigate systemic inflammatory response syndrome and organ failure, underscoring the importance of monitoring HCT to assess the adequacy of fluid therapy.

Despite these findings, this retrospective study has limitations. First, the identification of AP cases relied solely on ICD-9 codes. Although this approach is widely used in studies based on the MIMIC-IV database and other electronic health record datasets, the accuracy of coding for AP in ICU populations may vary, potentially leading to misclassification. Unfortunately, uniformly available laboratory or imaging markers (e.g., serum amylase/lipase levels, CT findings) were not consistently recorded in MIMIC-IV, precluding retrospective validation. Therefore, future studies incorporating prospectively collected laboratory and imaging data are necessary to further confirm and refine our findings. Second, RDH was calculated from single 24-hour averages of RDW and hematocrit obtained at ICU admission. Given that AP is a dynamic disease in which hematocrit levels may fluctuate significantly with fluid resuscitation and disease progression, reliance on 1-time measurement may limit clinical applicability. Repeated or longitudinal assessments of RDH could provide more informative prognostic value, which warrants investigation in future prospective studies. Third, several potentially important confounding factors, such as the etiology of AP (e.g., alcohol, gallstones), timing of ICU admission, and detailed treatment strategies (including fluid resuscitation protocols, nutrition, and antibiotic use), were not uniformly available in the MIMIC-IV database. These factors can substantially influence patient outcomes, and the inability to account for them represents a limitation of our study. Although we adjusted for a broad range of demographic, clinical, and treatment covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, the MIMIC-IV database is derived from a single tertiary U.S. center. Therefore, our findings may not be fully generalizable to Chinese ICU patients or other healthcare settings, and external validation in diverse populations is warranted.

Future research should explore RDH’s role in early AP diagnosis and treatment monitoring, aiming to develop a comprehensive scoring system incorporating RDH for more accurate prognostication. Further elucidation of RDH’s biological mechanisms, especially its relationship with inflammatory responses and microcirculatory disorders in AP, is essential to enhance its clinical utility.

6. Conclusions

RDH was independently associated with 28-day mortality in ICU patients with AP. While RDH alone showed modest discrimination, its addition to SOFA and APACHE III scores improved model performance. These results indicate that RDH provides incremental prognostic value, though causality cannot be inferred and prospective validation is needed.
