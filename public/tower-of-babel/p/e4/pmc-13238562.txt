Life‐threatening acute complications persist among individuals with type 1 diabetes in Japan: A nationwide registry‐based cross‐sectional study
Authors: Daisuke Chujo, Akihisa Imagawa, Junnosuke Miura, Kazuma Takahashi, Toru Kikuchi, Ichiro Horie, Daisuke Yabe, Akira Shimada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDespite advances in insulin therapy, acute diabetic complications continue to pose a significant and potentially life‐threatening risk in type 1 diabetes. Since there have been no nationwide studies regarding acute diabetic complications in individuals with type 1 diabetes in Japan, we investigated the incidence of those complications and examined the association between the incidence and clinical factors in the patients.Materials and methodsRegistry‐based cross‐sectional analyses were conducted using data from patients with type 1 diabetes from 125 medical institutions across Japan.ResultsA total of 4,405 cases were registered in the database, of which 2,679 cases with confirmed diagnosis of type 1 diabetes subtypes were included in the analyses. Following insulin therapy, 18.8 and 7.8% of the patients experienced diabetic ketosis and diabetic ketoacidosis, respectively. Furthermore, 12.7 and 28.9% of the participants experienced severe hypoglycemia and hypoglycemic unawareness, respectively. C‐peptide levels were significantly lower in patients with these complications than in those without the complications. Notably, the incidence of these complications in patients with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes “definite” was comparable with those in patients with acute‐onset or fulminant type 1 diabetes.ConclusionsA substantial proportion of patients with type 1 diabetes in Japan experience potentially life‐threatening acute diabetic complications despite receiving insulin replacement therapy. Advanced medical care for type 1 diabetes should be further developed in this country.Keywords: Ketoacidosis, Severe hypoglycemia, Type 1 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes results from the autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β‐cells, resulting in absolute insulin deficiency and requiring lifelong exogenous insulin therapy 1 . Despite advances in insulin delivery systems and glucose monitoring technologies, acute metabolic complications, including diabetic ketosis (DK), diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and severe hypoglycemia, continue to pose significant risks to individuals with type 1 diabetes. DKA is a major cause of morbidity and hospitalization worldwide, and its incidence remains a clinical concern even in high‐income countries 2 . The prevalence of DKA from the 2000s to 2010s was 30–35% at onset 3 , 4 and 5–7% after onset 5 of type 1 diabetes in Western countries. Furthermore, the mortality rate increased from 15 to 25% with increasing age (>45 years) following hyperglycemic emergencies in type 1 diabetes 6 . Severe hypoglycemia, defined as a hypoglycemic event requiring third‐party assistance, is another potentially life‐threatening complication in insulin‐treated individuals with type 1 diabetes and is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality, including sudden death 7 , 8 , 9 . Retrospective cohort studies in the United States (US) showed that approximately 20% of people with type 1 diabetes experienced severe hypoglycemic events 10 , 11 , indicating that many individuals continue to experience severe hypoglycemia despite the use of advanced diabetes technologies. Although Western countries and some Asian countries 12 , 13 have conducted nationwide surveys, there are no nationwide studies regarding such acute diabetic complications in Japan; however, single institutional studies have examined the incidence of severe hypoglycemia 14 , 15 .

Given these concerns, it is essential to investigate the current burden of acute diabetic complications in patients with type 1 diabetes and to identify associated clinical factors by conducting a nationwide study in Japan. To address this, the Japan Diabetes Society (JDS) initiated the “Project for research into eligible treatment using evidence from the real‐world for T1D in Japan (PRETER‐T1DJ).” Using this registry, which compiles clinical data on type 1 diabetes from 125 medical institutions across Japan, we conducted a nationwide, cross‐sectional study to investigate the incidence of acute diabetic complications and their associated clinical factors among individuals with type 1 diabetes in Japan.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and ethics

This was a retrospective, multicenter, and cross‐sectional study designed using the clinical data of patients with type 1 diabetes, and conducted as a part of the PRETER‐T1DJ, a research initiative affiliated with the JDS. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments, and the “Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects” published by the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare of Japan. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the JDS (ID: 02–014‐3b). Individuals who participated in the study were given the opportunity to decline use of their data for scientific research after reviewing the documents.

Study participants

Individuals with type 1 diabetes who received medical care at one of the 125 hospitals across Japan and had at least one visit to the hospitals between November and December 2019 were included in this study. Among 4,405 registered individuals, only those with a confirmed diagnosis of a specific type 1 diabetes subtype were included in the present analyses. Participants with unclassified or missing subtype information were excluded. Each type 1 diabetes subtype, acute‐onset, slowly progressive, and fulminant type 1 diabetes, was diagnosed according to the criteria published by the JDS Committee on T1D Research 16 , 17 , 18 . Individuals who objected to the use of their data were excluded from the study.

Clinical data collection

This registry collected clinical data from patients receiving routine diabetes care at educational medical institutions certified by the JDS across Japan. Clinical data between November and December 2019 were collected using an electronic data collection system developed by the Joint Center for Researchers, Associates, and Clinicians (JCRAC) data center at the Japan Institute for Health and Security. Representative physicians at each hospital directly registered patients' data online using this system. For continuous variables, data were not entered as raw numerical values; instead, we adopted a format in which the ranges of values were predefined and categorized into multiple options from which the researchers selected the appropriate category (Figure S1). Registered data from each hospital were aggregated and stored on the JCRAC data center server. Stored data could be extracted in appropriate formats for analysis upon request from the study's investigators.

Measurements

Collected data included demographic characteristics, body mass index (BMI), type 1 diabetes subtype, family history of diabetes, information on medical subsidies, age at onset or diagnosis, year of onset or diagnosis, duration of insulin therapy, history of DK and DKA, severe hypoglycemia (entire history and history within the past year), and hypoglycemia unawareness. DK and DKA were recorded based on physician‐documented clinical diagnoses. Severe hypoglycemia was defined as a hypoglycemic episode requiring assistance from another person. Hypoglycemia unawareness was defined as asymptomatic hypoglycemia detected by venous blood testing, self‐monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), or continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). In addition, data related to complications were collected, including retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, macrovascular diseases, foot lesions, autoimmune thyroid diseases, and malignancies (hepatic, pancreatic, colorectal, and other cancers). Laboratory data included glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) at onset or diagnosis and at the time of data collection; islet autoantibodies, including glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody and insulinoma‐associated protein‐2 antibody; and fasting serum C‐peptide levels. Physicians at each participating institute were instructed to enter C‐peptide values only when they were measured under fasting conditions (before the first meal of the day). Therapeutic information included insulin administration methods; use of oral hypoglycemic agents, such as sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, biguanides, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP4) inhibitors, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, and alpha‐glucosidase inhibitors; pancreas or islet transplantation; glucose monitoring methods; carbohydrate counting; and prior treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Insulin administration methods were classified into three categories: multiple daily insulin injections (MDI), continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion without integration with a glucose sensor (CSII), and sensor‐augmented insulin pumps (SAP). Cases receiving fewer than three times insulin injections per day accounted for less than 1.0% of the total and were therefore excluded from the analysis. When available, indicators of glycemic variability from CGM, including time in range, time above range, and time below range (TBR), were also collected.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using available data for each variable, and no imputation was performed for missing values. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. For continuous variables collected as categorical ranges (such as age), the midpoint of each predefined interval was assigned to each participant and used in subsequent analyses. Comparisons of continuous data between the two groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, and comparisons of the data among three groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐squared test. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of the patients with each type 1 diabetes subtype

Out of 4,405 patients with type 1 diabetes who were registered in this study, 2,679 cases (age 50.0 ± 20.1 years; 42.0% male), with confirmed diagnosis of any type 1 diabetes subtype, were included in the analyses. Clinical characteristics of the participants with each type 1 diabetes subtype are given in Table 1. Among the participants, 572 (21.3%) had an onset of type 1 diabetes before 20 years of age, while 149 (5.5%) had disease onset at 70 years of age or older (Figure S2). The age at onset was the youngest among participants with acute‐onset type 1 diabetes. Of note, BMI and fasting C‐peptide levels (at onset and at the latest measurement) were highest in participants with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes. The number of patients with fulminant type 1 diabetes who had islet‐related autoantibodies and autoimmune thyroid diseases was lowest among patients with the three type 1 diabetes subtypes.

Incidence of DK/DKA and its association with other clinical factors

As given in Tables 2 and 3, at the onset of type 1 diabetes, 53.1% and 33.1% of the participants had DK and DKA, respectively. Regarding clinical background related to the onset of DK and DKA, the age at onset was significantly younger (P < 0.001), and fasting C‐peptide levels at the onset of type 1 diabetes were lower (P < 0.001) in the patients with DK and DKA than in patients without both conditions. In addition, >70% of the patients with acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes had DK (P <0.001), and 85.3% of patients with fulminant type 1 diabetes had DKA at the onset of type 1 diabetes (P < 0.001).

Notably, following the onset of type 1 diabetes, despite insulin therapy, 18.8 and 7.8% of the patients had DK and DKA, respectively. Latest HbA1c values were significantly higher (P < 0.001), while the latest C‐peptide levels were significantly lower (P < 0.001) in patients with DK and DKA than in patients without both conditions. Furthermore, 21.3 and 18.2% of participants with acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes, respectively, developed DK; and 11.3 and 7.8% of the participants with acute‐onset type 1 diabetes and fulminant type 1 diabetes, respectively, developed DKA after type 1 diabetes onset (DK, P = 0.001; DKA, P < 0.001). Although the incidence of DK and DKA were lower in patients with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes than in patients with acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes, the incidence rates were notably higher in patients with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes who had insulin deficiency, defined as <0.6 ng/mL of fasting C‐peptide (Tables S1 and S3), and the incidence rates (22.8% for DK and 8.2% for DKA) were almost comparable to those in acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes. Regarding the effect of the administration of SGLT2 inhibitors, although the incidence of DK and DKA was not significantly different between patients who were administered SGLT2 inhibitors and those who were not, the incidence was significantly higher in patients who were administered SGLT2 inhibitors than in those who were not administered (DK, P = 0.005; DKA, P = 0.009) when focusing on patients with acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes (Tables S2 and S4). Regarding insulin administration methods, the incidence of DK and DKA was relatively higher in patients who received CSII, and the incidence improved when using SAP (P = 0.001; among the three methods: multiple daily insulin injections [MDI], CSII, and SAP).

Incidence of severe hypoglycemia and its association with other clinical factors

As given in Table 4, 12.7% of the participants experienced severe hypoglycemia with insulin therapy. The duration of insulin therapy was significantly longer (P < 0.001) and the latest C‐peptide levels were lower (P < 0.001) in participants who experienced severe hypoglycemia than in those who did not experience it. In addition, the latest HbA1c values were significantly higher in participants who experienced severe hypoglycemia within the past 1 year than in those who did not experience it (P = 0.025). Notably, TBR, evaluated with CGM, was significantly longer in the individuals who experienced severe hypoglycemia than those who did not experience it (7.4 ± 6.7 vs 11.3 ± 8.2%, P < 0.001 for past episodes; 7.5 ± 6.6 vs 11.0 ± 7.7%, P < 0.001 for episodes within the past 1 year). Regarding the incidence of each type 1 diabetes subtype, 15.3% of patients with acute‐onset type 1 diabetes and 12.4% of patients with fulminant type 1 diabetes experienced severe hypoglycemia during insulin therapy (P < 0.001). Although the incidence of severe hypoglycemia was lower in patients with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes than in acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes, the incidence was notably higher in patients with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes with insulin deficiency, which was defined as <0.6 ng/mL of fasting C‐peptide (Table S5), and the incidence rates (14.2% for past episodes and 5.1% for episodes within past 1 year) were almost comparable to those in acute‐onset type 1 diabetes. The incidence of severe hypoglycemia was not significantly different among participants who received the three insulin injection methods (MDI, CSII, and SAP).

Incidence of hypoglycemia unawareness and its association with other clinical factors

As given in Table 5, 28.9% of participants experienced hypoglycemic unawareness. The age at which the data were registered was significantly older (P < 0.001), the duration of insulin therapy was longer (P < 0.001), and the latest C‐peptide levels were lower (P < 0.001) in the patients who experienced hypoglycemia unawareness than in those who did not experience it. Furthermore, TBR was significantly longer in the patients who experienced hypoglycemia unawareness than those who did not experience it (6.4 ± 5.5 vs 9.8 ± 8.0%; P < 0.001). Regarding the incidence of each type 1 diabetes subtype, 31.2% and 35.9% of the participants with acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes, respectively, experienced hypoglycemia unawareness during insulin therapy (P < 0.001). Although the incidence of hypoglycemia unawareness was relatively lower in patients with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes, the incidence was significantly higher in patients with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes with insulin deficiency, defined as <0.6 ng/mL of fasting C‐peptide, than those without insulin deficiency (Table S6), and the incidence rate (34.8%) was almost comparable to those in acute‐onset and fulminant type 1 diabetes. The incidence of hypoglycemia unawareness was not significantly different among individuals who received the three methods of insulin injection.

DISCUSSION

Notably, this is the first study to comprehensively investigate the incidence of acute diabetic complications using a nationwide type 1 diabetes registry in Japan. Our study showed that the incidence of DKA at type 1 diabetes onset was 33.1%, comparable to reports from the US, Europe, and Australia 3 , 4 , 19 . In addition, we identified that younger age and lower serum levels of C‐peptide at type 1 diabetes onset were associated with the incidence of DKA. In contrast, higher HbA1c levels were associated with its incidence in a study from Australia and New Zealand 19 , and the incidence of DKA in fulminant type 1 diabetes patients was extremely high (85%). These findings indicate that an abrupt deterioration in pancreatic β‐cell function may precipitate the development of DKA at type 1 diabetes onset.

Our study showed that the incidence of DKA following type 1 diabetes onset was 7.8% despite insulin therapy, which is comparable to studies in the US and Europe (5–7%) 5 , and in an Asian region, Hong Kong (6–12%) 12 . Furthermore, our study demonstrated that higher HbA1c levels and a longer duration of insulin therapy were associated with the incidence of DKA following type 1 diabetes onset. These results were comparable to studies from the US, Europe 5 , and Saudi Arabia 13 , whereas a study from Hong Kong reported that younger age was associated with a higher incidence of DKA 12 . The incidence of DKA increases as glycemic control worsens, attaining 15.8% in individuals with an HbA1c level of 9% or higher 20 , highlighting the importance of glycemic management in the prevention of DKA. Similarly, lower C‐peptide levels following type 1 diabetes onset were associated with the development of DKA even in participants with slowly progressive type 1 diabetes. The diagnostic criteria for slowly progressive type 1 diabetes define slowly progressive type 1 diabetes (definite) as a fasting C‐peptide level <0.6 ng/mL, and slowly progressive type 1 diabetes (probable) as ≥0.6 ng/mL 21 . From these observations, it is important to note that patients classified as “definite” may have a higher risk of developing DKA after diagnosis compared with those classified as “probable.” In clinical practice, while the usefulness of selected oral glucose‐lowering agents such as DPP4 inhibitors and biguanides has been demonstrated in slowly progressive type 1 diabetes (probable), in cases of slowly progressive type 1 diabetes (definite), insulin therapy is recommended, taking into account the risk of DKA and considering the use of advanced devices 22 .

In our study, the incidence of DKA was higher in the CSII group than in the MDI group. The FinnDiane study reported that the use of CSII was a predictor of DKA 23 , and Madsen et al. reported that insulin pump therapy marginally increased the risk of hospitalized DKA while reducing HbA1c levels 24 . In contrast, insulin pump therapy was associated with a lower risk of DKA than MDI 25 . The elevated risk of DKA in patients treated with CSII is largely ascribed to infusion set failures and inadequate management of pump‐related interruptions 26 . Given that CSII employs only rapid‐acting insulin, any disruption in insulin delivery may result in the rapid development of ketosis and progression to DKA. These intrinsic vulnerabilities of pump therapy have been recognized as major contributors to DKA episodes in this population. Nevertheless, advances in pump technology, infusion set design, and integrated safety mechanisms over the past decades have progressively reduced the likelihood of such adverse events, which may account for the decline in reported DKA incidence in recent studies. In contrast, our study showed that the introduction of SAP therapy reduced the incidence of DKA compared with the CSII group, suggesting that the use of advanced medical technologies may contribute to the reduction of life‐threatening acute complications. A Japanese study reports that poor medication compliance and excessive consumption of sugary soft drinks are associated with the onset of DKA 27 , suggesting that strengthening medication adherence and lifestyle modifications may play an important role in preventing the onset of DKA.

Our study also showed that the incidence rates of severe hypoglycemia and hypoglycemia unawareness were 12.7% and 28.9%, respectively. Although the frequency of hypoglycemia unawareness in this study was comparable to that reported in the US 11 , the incidence of severe hypoglycemia was lower in the US (20%) 11 and the United Kingdom (UK) (16%) 28 . This finding was similar to the results obtained from a single‐center study conducted in Japan 14 . Regarding the factors associated with hypoglycemic events, a longer duration of insulin therapy and lower C‐peptide levels were associated with the incidence of severe hypoglycemia and hypoglycemia unawareness in our study. Similar to the results regarding the onset of DKA, lower C‐peptide levels were associated with the incidence of severe hypoglycemia, even in slowly progressive patients with type 1 diabetes. These findings are consistent with reports from the UK showing that higher C‐peptide levels reduce the risk of severe hypoglycemia 28 , suggesting that a progressive decline in pancreatic β‐cell function is associated with hypoglycemic events. Notably, in our study, a lower TBR was associated with the incidence of severe hypoglycemia and hypoglycemia unawareness, with the average TBR among those who experienced such events being 10–11%. Deshmukh et al. reported that the cut‐off values of TBR for predicting severe hypoglycemia and hypoglycemia unawareness were 3.95 and 3.35%, respectively, proposing that maintaining the TBR below 4% may help prevent these hypoglycemic events 29 .

Our study found no differences in the incidence of severe hypoglycemia or hypoglycemia unawareness among patients using MDI, CSII, or SAP. In contrast, randomized controlled trials involving individuals with type 1 diabetes with impaired awareness of hypoglycemia have reported that the use of CSII or CGM could reduce the incidence of severe hypoglycemia compared with treatment with MDI or SMBG 30 ; hence, the effectiveness of these devices could not be dismissed. Sonoda et al. demonstrated that alcohol consumption and the requirement for family assistance at the time of insulin injection were associated with hypoglycemic events 31 . Fan et al. showed that the incidence of severe hypoglycemia increased with advancing age in patients with type 1 diabetes 12 , suggesting that lifestyle and the need for social support may also contribute to the development of such hypoglycemic events.

Our results demonstrate that impaired pancreatic β‐cell function is associated with the development of acute diabetic complications and that treatments aimed at preserving β‐cell function may help reduce the risk of these complications. If the long‐term effects of immunomodulatory therapies, as reported in recent years 32 , 33 , 34 , are proven and these therapies become widely adopted in clinical practice, it may be possible to prevent such potentially life‐threatening complications.

Our study has several limitations. First, given the cross‐sectional nature of this study, careful interpretation is warranted regarding prior use of alternative insulin delivery methods. Acute complications, including DK, DKA, and severe hypoglycemia, may have occurred while patients were using a different insulin delivery modality than that captured at the time of the survey. Second, the number of cases with available data varied across items, which may have influenced the statistical outcomes. Third, our study does not present its data in a manner that accounts for potential confounding factors and this point should be addressed in future analyses. Fourth, the cases registered in the registry were predominantly adult patients treated by adult endocrinologists, and the proportion of pediatric cases was limited; therefore, the age distribution does not fully reflect that of the Japanese population. Fifth, because participation was limited to institutions with diabetes specialists, selection bias cannot be excluded. Therefore, caution is required when generalizing these findings to patients managed exclusively in primary care settings or to pediatric populations. To overcome these limitations, a large‐scale prospective longitudinal study, including a substantial population of pediatric patients, would be desirable.

In conclusion, our study reveals that a substantial proportion of individuals with type 1 diabetes in Japan experience acute complications with potential life‐threatening consequences, including DKA and severe hypoglycemia. Moreover, the suggested association between the progressive loss of pancreatic β‐cell function and the occurrence of these complications warrants particular attention. To improve these circumstances, it is desirable to develop an environment that facilitates a more proactive implementation of advanced device‐based therapies and therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving pancreatic β‐cell function. Socioeconomic support is also expected to play a vital role in addressing these challenges.
