The influence of consent models on recruitment rates in randomised trials in critical care: A systematic review
Authors: Mahesh Ramanan, Aashish Kumar, Laurent Billot, John Myburgh, Balasubramanian Venkatesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine whether type of consent model was associated with recruitment rate in critical care randomised clinical trials (RCT).Data sourcesPubMed was searched for relevant articles.Study selectionIndividual patient RCTs in critical care with a primary outcome of mortality published between 1990 and 2020 were included.Data extractionTwo authors independently reviewed titles, abstracts, and full-text articles for eligibility, and data was entered into a structured custom database.Data synthesis186 RCTs were included, of which 141(75.8%) used a priori consent, while 45 (24.2%) used alternative consent models, including consent waiver and consent-to-continue. The alternate consent RCTs recruited significantly larger sample sizes (median 680 patients, IQR 300–2410) compared to the a priori group (350 patients, IQR 118–725) over similar recruitment periods (mean 3.06 years for both). The unadjusted mean weekly recruitment rate was significantly higher in the alternate consent group (mean difference +8.57 patients per week, 95% CI: 5.02–12.12). After adjustment for number of recruiting sites, diagnostic group of patients included, intervention type, investigator-initiated trial, and continent of primary trial sponsor, the alternate consent group still had a significantly higher mean weekly recruitment rate (mean difference +6.78 patients per week, 95% CI, 3.30–10.26). The proportion of RCTs that were ceased early and that reached target recruitment were similar between the two groups, as were rates of withdrawn consent.ConclusionAlternate consent models for critical care RCTs were associated with higher recruitment rates compared to a priori consent. A study-within-a-trial analysis may be required for definitive evaluation.RegistrationPROSPERO Record ID: Record ID: CRD42020215950.Keywords: Consent, Informed consent, Critical care, Randomised trial, A priori, Consent to continue

Introduction

Deficiencies in ethical recruitment of participants in medical research (Tuskegee) led to the enshrinement of informed consent as a key pillar of medical research,[1], [2], [3] particularly for the conduct of randomised clinical trials (RCT),4 The National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia (NHMRC) requires that consent for participation in medical research should “be a voluntary choice” and “based on sufficient information and adequate understanding” of the proposed research and its implications for the participant.5

For some illnesses, certain components of therapy, such as antibiotics in septic shock,6 are time-critical interventions. This is particularly the case in critical care medicine, where patients present with sudden-onset, life-threatening illnesses and require prompt resuscitation and other treatment, which sometimes may include invasive interventions. Research investigating such illnesses and treatments are thus important to advance the care of patients with critical illnesses. However, unlike chronic and sub-acute illnesses, there are several barriers to obtaining consent in a timely fashion for time-critical interventions in critical care. These include patients being unable to consent due to sedation, neuromuscular blockade, or neurological illnesses, surrogate decision-makers being unavailable; and high levels of distress and anxiety among patients and families due to the life-threatening nature of many critical illnesses.7

In this context, alternatives to a priori consent, such as consent-to-continue (obtained after patient enrolment) and opt-out consent,[8], [9], [10] have been developed to give patients opportunities to participate in and benefit from RCTs, but also have opportunities to participate in informed consent discussions at more suitable times when they can make considered decisions. In the critical care setting, such alternative consent models are broadly acceptable to patients, substitute decision-makers, and clinicians alike.[11], [12], [13] The NHMRC's National Statement also provides guidance for intensive care research (for patients heavily dependent upon medical care who may be temporarily have impaired capacity to consent), which includes situations where alternatives to a priori consent may be applicable in order to promote the general principle of inclusiveness in research regardless of severity of acute illness. The alternatives listed include consent-to-continue (or agreement to continue participation) and waiver of the requirement for consent.

The use of different consent models and their associations with recruitment characteristics of critical care RCTs have not been systematically studied, though individual evaluations have suggested faster recruitment.14 We performed this systematic review to address this evidence gap and evaluate consent models in critical care recruitment.

We hypothesised that critical care RCTs with alternate consent models, including consent waiver, consent-to-continue and opt-out consent, would have faster recruitment rates and lower early cessation rates than critical care RCTs with an a priori consent model.

Methods

We have reported this systematic review according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.15 It was registered on PROSPERO on 22 November 2020 (Record ID: CRD42020215950).

Search strategy

We searched PubMed for articles published between 1 January 1990 and 31 December 2020 that met prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Details of the search strategy are listed in the Supplementary Appendix.

Study selection

The literature search was independently performed by two reviewers. The results of the search were compared and disputes resolved through discussion by a third reviewer if required. The inclusion criteria were articles that presented the results of adult and paediatric critical care RCTs, with a primary outcome of mortality. Only English-language publications were included. RCTs were deemed to be critical care RCTs if they included critically ill patients treated in an ICU. We excluded neonatal ICU RCTs, pseudo-randomised RCTs and RCTs where only abstract or conference proceedings were available without a full-length publication. RCTs which did not specifically report the type of consent used, and cluster-randomised trials were excluded. All titles and abstracts were screened, and selected articles underwent full-text review to determine eligibility. Individual study authors were not contacted for unpublished data.

Quality assessment

All articles selected after full-text review were subjected to independent quality assessment by two reviewers, with a third reviewer available to resolve disputes. The Cochrane risk of bias tool was used to determine quality.16

Data extraction

We custom-designed and pilot-tested a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA) database,17,18 which was used to store all extracted data. Data related to methodological characteristics, consent models, and results of each RCT were extracted. The included RCTs were dichotomised by consent model (Table 1). The a priori group included RCTs where full informed consent was required from either patient or a substitute decision maker before a patient could be randomised into the RCT. The alternative consent group included RCTs which had consent waiver, consent-to-continue, consent not required, and opt-out consent. Terms such as “research without prior consent” and “deferred consent” have been used in the literature to describe the consent-to-continue model. We included studies with “research without prior consent” and “deferred consent” models under the consent-to-continue term.19,20 RCTs with hybrid models, e.g., a combination of a priori consent and consent-to-continue, were also included in the alternative consent group.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterise differences between the a priori and alternative consent groups. Continuous data were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean and standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and proportions. Univariable analyses were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher's exact test for continuous and categorical variables respectively. Differences in recruitment rates and times between the two groups were modelled using multivariable linear regression, with differences reported as mean difference with 95% confidence intervals. The following covariates were prespecified for inclusion based on authors' judgement of potentially significant factors associated with recruitment rate: number of recruiting sites, diagnostic group of patients included, intervention type, investigator-initiated trial, and continent of primary trial sponsor. Early cessation rate and completion of target recruitment were evaluated with multivariable logistic regression models, with results reported as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval. The same covariates as for the primary outcome were used in the logistic regression models. Statistical analyses were performed in Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX-USA).

Results

Search and trial characteristics

There were 186 RCTs which met all inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1, Supplementary Appendix). The majority were investigator-initiated (115/186, 61.8%), multicentre (132/186, 71.0%) RCTs, which recruited a median of 391 patients (IQR 140–802) (Table 2). Most RCTs had their primary sponsor based in Europe (102/186, 54.8%) or North America (38/186, 20.4%). Fifty-five (55/186, 29.6%) RCTs scored low risk of bias in all Cochrane risk of bias categories. Full details of risk of bias assessment are presented in the Supplementary Appendix.

Consent models

There were 141 (75.8%) RCTs which used an a priori consent model and 45 (24.2%) used alternate consent models (Fig. 2). Of the RCTs with alternate consent models, 35 (18.8%) used consent-to-continue, 7 (3.8%) had waiver of consent, and 3 (1.6%) did not require consent. The characteristics of RCTs in the a priori and alternate consent groups are presented in Table 2. There were some differences between the characteristics of the two groups. Most importantly, the alternate consent group had larger planned sample sizes, more investigator-initiated, multicentre RCTs, and had higher rates of prospective trial registration and presence of data safety monitoring committee. The distributions of primary sponsor location, diagnostic subgroups, and type of intervention tested were also different.

Recruitment rates

The median (IQR) target sample size was higher in the alternate consent group compared to the a priori group (1090 [480,3010] vs. 508 [207,1100]), as was the actual number of patients recruited (median 680 [300,2410] vs. 350 [118–725]) (Table 3). The mean recruitment times were similar, 3.06 years (SD 1.60) in the alternate consent group and 3.06 years (SD 1.41) in the a priori group. The proportion of RCTs reaching 90% and 100% of their recruitment target were also similar across the two groups.

Early cessation and target recruitment

The proportion of RCTs which were ceased early were similar between the two groups (Table 3). Multivariable logistic regression modelling (Supplementary Appendix) showed that consent model was not significantly associated with early cessation (OR 1.30, 95% CI: 0.53 to 3.18). Similarly, the proportion of RCTs recruiting at least 90% or 100% of their target sample size was not significantly different between the a priori and alternate consent groups, either on univariable or multivariable analysis (Supplementary Appendix).

Withdrawn consent

The proportion of RCTs which reported the numbers of patients who withdrew consent were 120/141 (85.1%) and 43/45 (95.6%) for RCTs with a priori and alternate consent models, respectively. The median percentage of patients who withdrew consent was 0.72% (IQR 0.41% to 1.27%) for the a priori group and 0.99% (IQR 0.28% to 3.57%) for the alternate consent group. After adjustment using multivariable modelling, the alternaternative consent model was not associated with a significantly elevated proportion of withdrawn consent (mean difference +0.5%, 95% CI: -0.02% to +1.03%, p = 0.061) (Supplementary Appendix).

Discussion

Key findings

Critical care RCTs with a primary outcome of mortality which used alternate consent models had significantly higher recruitment rates compared to those which used a priori consent models. There were systematic differences between these groups of RCTs, with the alternate consent group comprising more investigator-initiated and larger, multicentre RCTs, while also having different distributions in terms of geographical location, diagnostic subgroups of patients recruited, and types of interventions tested.

Comparisons with literature

Analyses of single RCTs have previously been suggestive of such associations,14 with our results providing confirmation across a large sample of critical care RCTs. Alternate consent models have also been associated with a higher chance of consent being obtained compared to a priori consent in settings where both options have been available.11,21

Where consumer engagement has been performed, patients and families have generally been supportive of alternate consent models such as consent-to-continue.[11], [12], [13],[21], [22], [23] There is some evidence that ethics committees (or institutional review boards) are also supportive of alternate consent models.11,14 Further, there have been moves towards simplifying the informed consent process and applying a layered, proportionate approach tailored to individual patient/family needs.24 This is supported by our finding that rates of withdrawal of consent were similar when comparing the two groups, with the median percentage of withdrawn consent less than 1% for both groups.

Implications

Slow recruitment and early cessation have been highly problematic and strategies to improve recruitment rates are highly sought after.[25], [26], [27], [28] Where ethically acceptable, and suitable to clinicians, patients, and families, triallists may explore alternate models of consent, including consent-to-continue and waiver of consent, to increase recruitment rates.

However, it must be noted that there are differences in consent requirements across different jurisdictions. For example, in the European Union, United Kingdom and numerous other jurisdictions, waiver of consent is not included in the legislation.20 Even when there are multiple jurisdictions within a trial with different legal requirements, trials have been successfully conducted using a combination of consent models. For example, the ADRENAL trial, conducted in five countries in three continents, used a combination of a priori consent and consent-to-continue models.10 Similarly, the PEPTIC trial used a combination of waiver of consent and opt-out consent across five countries in three continents.9

Strengths and limitations

This analysis restricted was restricted to RCTs with mortality as the primary outcome, and other outcomes may have different consent model distributions and recruitment characteristics. This is an observational study of data from RCTs, and the findings may be biased by unknown confounders. Recruitment rates were not calculable for all identified RCTs, as recruitment times were not always reported, and authors were not contacted for unpublished data.

Conclusion

Alternate consent models, compared to a priori consent, were associated with higher recruitment rates with similar rates of withdrawn consent in critical care RCTs with a primary outcome of mortality. A prospective study-within-a-trial approach may be required to definitively evaluate the association between consent models and recruitment rate.
