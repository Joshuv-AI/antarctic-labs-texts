Discrepancies in the country versions of the WHOQOL-BREF as a potential source of error in assessing quality of life and a barrier to comparative research
Authors: Stanisław Maksymowicz, Maria Libura, Andrzej Jarynowski, Michał B. Paradowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to determine whether different language versions of the WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire allow for obtaining comparable health-related quality of life measurement results across different countries.MethodsWe analysed 21 country versions published on the World Health Organization website. Each version was compared to the reference English questionnaire in terms of its translation, the timeframe of measurement, the inclusion of instructions for respondents, visual layout, and additional elements. To evaluate the accuracy of the questions and scales, selected versions were reviewed by native speakers, and all translations were subsequently reviewed using a large language model.ResultsThe results reveal substantial discrepancies between the versions across all evaluated criteria. In some cases, translation errors were so severe that responses to certain questions could not be meaningfully compared.ConclusionsWe conclude that the WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire translations should undergo a comprehensive review to ensure conceptual and structural consistency and comparability. Additionally, language versions developed by external institutions should be carefully vetted by the WHO. The common practice of adding a note that a certain version is not an official WHO translation proves insufficient, as these tools are routinely treated by researchers and clinicians as de facto official instruments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02529-5.

Background

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is now recognised as a critical component of many clinical trials and medical experiments supplementing more objective clinical data [1, 2]. Importantly, HRQoL helps understand the impact of medical interventions not only in their physiological aspects, but also across multiple patient-centred dimensions of health—physical, psychological, and social—which are integral to evaluating therapeutic success [3–5]. Thus, measuring HRQoL provides a more complete picture of treatment impact, encompassing psychosocial benefits or drawbacks that purely physiological endpoints would miss.

HRQoL can be assessed in various ways. In principle, one could ask patients open-ended questions about their subjective well-being. However, to obtain reliable, statistically analysable data for comparing treatment outcomes across patients and studies, standardised tools must be used [6]. This role is fulfilled by HRQoL assessment questionnaires [4] designed to capture patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in a systematic way [7, 8], supplementing more objective clinical data [3].

In clinical practice, researchers typically use tools with simplified structures to obtain easily analysable data. One such tool is the WHOQOL-BREF [9], a shortened version of the health-related quality of life assessment questionnaire developed by the World Health Organization. The WHOQOL-BREF consists of 26 questions that address four domains of everyday life functioning: physical, psychological, social, and environmental. It is a shortened version of the WHOQOL-100 [10], making it easier to use. Each question employs a five-point Likert scale. The questionnaire is used in both clinical practice and scientific research. As of March 11, 2026, there were 4,166 references to WHOQOL-BREF in the Title or Abstract in PubMed and over 79,500 in Google Scholar.

The WHOQOL-BREF is a practical tool translated into many languages. However, while using the Polish version in our own research, we identified numerous structural and translation errors. This led us to investigate whether similar issues exist in other language versions, the gravity of the discrepancies, and whether they could compromise the reliability and comparability of responses across countries. The aim of this study is therefore to assess the extent of these discrepancies across selected translations and evaluate the tool’s international comparability.

Methods

For this analysis, we reviewed 21 language versions from the WHO website of the WHOQOL-BREF questionnaires in PDF and Word formats. The languages included Australian English, Bulgarian, Czech, Danish, Dutch (Netherlands), Finnish, French, German, Greek, Italian, Lithuanian, Norwegian, Polish (including a revised Polish version that we had improved), Romanian, Slovak, Spanish, Swedish, Thai, Ukrainian, and Vietnamese. Most represent European countries. The Australian version was added to enable a comparison between Australian English and the reference English version from the official WHO manual (https://www.who.int/tools/whoqol/whoqol-bref/docs/default-source/publishing-policies/whoqol-bref/english_whoqol_bref). The Thai and Vietnamese versions of the questionnaire additionally broaden the analysis by incorporating languages that originate outside of the Western cultural context.

We conducted a qualitative analysis of translation quality for both individual questions and response scales, using two approaches: (1) review by native speakers (for selected questionnaires in Danish, Dutch, Finnish, German, Polish, Spanish, Swedish, Thai, and Vietnamese; the task of the native speakers was to compare the version in their native language with the original English version and point out significant discrepancies in questions and scales), and (2) assessment using a large language model (LLM) for all 21 versions (Google NotebookLM with the same task as for the native-speakers; the exact prompt for the LLM is provided in Attachment 1).

Furthermore, all questionnaires were analysed manually in terms of the timeframe respondents were instructed to consider when answering, the inclusion of essential elements such as instructions for respondents, visual layout, and additional components (see Fig. 1; Table 1).

The degree of deviation from the original translation was assessed on nine levels, each assigned a corresponding score with weighted factors. The discrepancy scoring framework assigns a total maximum of 10 points and is structured to prioritise measurement equivalence over purely formal consistency. 60% of the total score (6 points) is allocated to the measurement core, encompassing item content (0–3 points) and response scales (0–3 points), thereby ensuring that deviations affecting construct representation, semantic meaning, or scaling structure exert the strongest influence on the overall assessment. The remaining 40% (4 points) is attributed to structural and formal elements, including the timeframe for measurement (0–2 points) and other formal components such as visual presentation, demographics, title, instructions, acknowledgment, and additional non-original elements (0–2 points combined). This proportional weighting reflects the assumption that deviations in substantive measurement components have greater implications for validity and comparability than variations in formatting or administrative features.

This permitted the calculation and comparison of the magnitude of deviations across versions (see Table 1). For questions and response scales, we only assessed the general presence of linguistic discrepancies, not deviations due to country-specific adaptation (such as the minor differences in scale questions observed between the reference English version and the Australian English version). A higher score indicates a greater divergence from the reference/original, with the maximum possible overall score being 10 points. The scoring criteria have not been used or validated in previous studies on translation quality evaluation. They were developed specifically for the purpose of this study and have not been formally tested in prior research contexts.

Results

Visual presentation and measurement timeframe

The first analysed aspect concerned the visual presentation of the entire questionnaire and its questions: the general layout, the existence of a demographic section, an appropriate title, the introduction and instructions for the respondent, acknowledgements, and additional elements. This area exhibited the most discrepancies, although it is important to note that, aside from the instructions for the respondent (the absence of instructions in eigth of the analysed language versions could lead to errors from the respondent), these elements generally do not significantly impact the results. However, there are exceptions, such as when a question and its scale are split across two pages, which can greatly hinder completing the questionnaire (this was the case in the Slovak and French versions, where a portion of a question in the PDF file—intended for printing by medical staff—was divided; see Fig. 2). Another example is the Hindi version, which only has a PDF file that is a scanned copy of a low-quality printout (for this reason, it was not included in the analysis as it is not machine-readable).

The most critical error, however, was related to the measurement intervals over which the patient was asked to assess their experiences. The original version of the questionnaire instructs the patient to reflect exactly on the previous two weeks. However, nine of the analysed language versions used different timeframes : four weeks in the Czech, Polish (unrevised), Thai, Ukrainian, and Vietnamese versions, a month in the Lithuanian and Norwegian versions, and 15 days in the Italian version. The French version did not specify a time interval at all. This means that in these cases, respondents were asked to answer based on a different timeframe than originally intended, which could affect the results, undermining the comparability of data, especially in cross-national analyses that assume methodological uniformity.

Translation errors in questions and response scales

The second - and more critical - area of analysis involved discrepancies in the translation of individual questions and response scales. While some differences may reflect appropriate cultural adaptation, many were clearly translation errors that compromise construct validity.

We began with a detailed review of the Polish version (before revision). One major concern was the consistent exclusive use of the masculine formal address (“Pan”, equivalent to “Mr.”), failing to account for non-male respondents. In Polish, respondents and patients are addressed as “Pan” or “Pani” (Mr. or Ms., not by first name, in line with Polish cultural norms). If the tool is intended for both or more genders, a direct form such as second-person singular verbs or a dual-form address such as “Pan/i” (“Sir/Madam”) ought to have been used.

More critically, certain questions in the first Polish version did not correspond to the originally intended domain. For instance, question 15, which in the English original reads “How well are you able to get around?” and refers to Domain 1 (physical – mobility), was translated as “Jak odnajduje się Pan w tej sytuacji?” meaning “How do you find yourself in this situation?”, which is a completely different domain. This difference cannot be explained by cultural adaptation during translation. In this case, the question addresses an entirely different, vague psychological domain, but eventually its score will be counted as mobility, severely skewing the analysis of the patient’s quality of life measurement. A similar mistranslation occurred in the Slovak version, where the question was rendered as “Ako dobre vyjdete?”, which translates to “How well do you get along?” Again, this does not capture the concept of physical mobility, making the item invalid for its intended purpose.

Another illustrative example of a clearly erroneous translation comes from the French questionnaire. Where in the English original question 6 reads “To what extent do you feel your life to be meaningful?”, addressing the subjective sense of life’s meaning and purpose, the French version shifts the focus to philosophical, religious or spiritual belief systems: “Vos croyances personnelles donnent-elles un sens à votre vie ?”, meaning “Do your personal beliefs give meaning to your life?” Such a discrepancy introduces a different conceptual framework and is difficult to justify based on cultural differences considered during validation. Another example is a slight change of concepts in the Dutch version, where the original question referring to the physical environment (question 9: “How healthy is your physical environment?”) has been translated as “de gezondheid van uw omgeving” (“the health of your environment”). This rewording implies ecological or environmental health rather than accessibility, safety, or infrastructure, again leading to a divergence from the intended construct. We encountered a similar problem in the same question in the Thai version, where the word “สุขอนามัย” (s̄uk̄h xnāmạy) refers more to hygiene and sanitation than to the general environment. In the same language version, a meaning shift occurs whereby the English item refers to pain preventing a person from doing required activities (“need to do”), whereas the Thai version (“อยากทำ”; xyāk thả) refers to activities the person wants or desires to do (“want to do”).

Moreover, the Vietnamese translation contains errors that systematically alter key constructs, particularly in the physical domain. The main issues are twofold. First, question 3 narrows the scope of “physical pain”: the original English questionnaire asks how pain affects daily activities, whereas the Vietnamese version refers only to “thương tật về thể xác” (physical injuries or disabilities), excluding broader experiences of pain. Second, question 4 shifts the focus of medical treatment from functioning in daily life to “sustaining life” (“để duy trì cuộc sống”), changing the intended construct from quality of functioning to biological survival.

Unfortunately, similar issues were identified in most language versions. In some cases, the deviations may appear subtle but could nonetheless introduce systematic bias, particularly in large comparative studies.

The analysis also revealed problems in the translation of the response scales. Among others, there were grammatical errors (e.g., in Polish), visual inversion of numbering (e.g., in three questions in the Czech, Ukrainian, and Vietnamese versions), or unnecessary modifications not justified by linguistic norms or validation evidence (e.g., in the Australian version).

These issues collectively undermine the conceptual and semantic equivalence required for meaningful cross-cultural comparison. The extent of discrepancies was highest in the Polish unrevised (9.75), Vietnamese (9,00) and Thai (8.25) versions, and lowest in the Finnish, German, and Polish-revised versions (0.25).

Discussion

The advancement and globalisation of science and medical research have increased the need for culturally adapted quality of life measurement tools. Where available, the adaptation of existing instruments is usually preferred over creating new ones, as this avoids the creation of redundant tools [11], permits the comparability of the results across cultures, and promotes information exchange in the scientific community [12].

However, translating subjective, patient-reported data assessment instruments involves more than simply rendering the text into another language [13–15]. The goal of translation is to ensure that the new version maintains content integrity and conceptual consistency by measuring the target construct as closely as possible to the original [16–19]. This expectation in cross-cultural research is commonly referred to as construct equivalence [20–22], the assumption that the tools employed will measure the same constructs in the same way whether in the original language/culture or in the translation [23–30].

Despite this, numerous researchers have highlighted a shortage of universally accepted, standardised methodological guidelines for questionnaire translation and cross-cultural adaptation [27, 31–34]. While the literature often details the translation process—that is, the transformation of the questionnaire, many studies neglect the validation—or quality assessment—phase, which is critical for assessing translation quality [35]. Both European [23, 36] and U.S. regulatory bodies [37] have likewise expressed concerns over the methodological rigour and validity of translated tools. A methods review of 47 articles in cross-cultural nursing research by Maneesriwongul and Dixon (2004) found that the translation process was often inadequate [38]. Similarly, Hawkins et al. (2020) identified ten common types of translation errors across different versions of the Health Literacy Questionnaire [39]. Such errors may significantly compromise data interpretation. Our findings align with these prior critiques of translation methodologies.

Therefore, to ensure that questionnaire adaptations are reliable, valid, conceptually and semantically equivalent, and culturally relevant for target populations, several experts have advocated for rigorous, robust multi-stage processes, from preliminary translation and cross-cultural adaptation [20, 39–42] to subsequent validation [11, 18, 24, 33, 38, 43–51]. Proper validation is vital for ensuring that the psychometric properties of the new version are not inferior to the original, helps guarantee that the information collected about the target population will be accurate, and enhances the acceptance of the questionnaire by professionals and society, helping reassure respondents’ subjective perceptions, especially in the case of complex or sensitive questions [24, 52, 53].

The development of the WHOQOL-100 was explicitly designed with cross-cultural equivalence as a priority: the full field trial of the instrument was developed collaboratively and administered in testing centres in 15 countries deliberately selected to represent global diversity to provide differences in the level of industrialisation, available health services, and other markers relevant to the measurement of quality of life [54]. Its performance suggested that it was a reliable and universally valid instrument with acceptable psychometric properties for all the 15 diverse cultural settings involved [55]. The validity of the WHOQOL-BREF was in turn analysed using cross-sectional data obtained from a survey carried out in 23 countries, with analyses of internal consistency, discriminant validity, and construct validity through confirmatory factor analysis indicating that the instrument has good to excellent psychometric properties and is a sound, cross-culturally valid assessment of quality of life [9].

To date, numerous translations of various iterations of the WHOQOL-BREF have been produced, spanning languages ranging from Akan [56] and Amharic [57] to Urdu [58] and Yoruba [59]. While most maintain their reliability and validity [60, 61], some report low internal consistency in at least some of the measures [62–66].

This suggests that while the WHOQOL-BREF demonstrates broad cross-cultural utility, certain linguistic and cultural adaptations may negatively impact its psychometric properties, highlighting the need for careful contextual validation in each target population. Our study identifies some of the most critical vulnerabilities in the translation and implementation process and casts light on the scale of the problem, pointing to a need for greater oversight, harmonisation, and quality assurance. Achieving this requires developing a robust translation protocol, providing training for national teams, and validating tools in collaboration with local and international experts, ideally in coordination with the WHO. This would better ensure methodological consistency and facilitate meaningful cross-national comparisons of quality of life outcomes.

Limitations

The linguistic analysis of the selected questionnaires was conducted by native speakers, whose task was to verify the accuracy of the translations. It must be noted that, due to the absence of external funding, these contributors were volunteers, not professional translators, although they included a few linguists. As such, they mostly identified instances of unnatural or awkward phrasing, which were not necessarily direct errors that would affect construct validity or the ability to accurately assess health-related quality of life.

To complement this qualitative evaluation, we employed a large language model to compare each country version with the English original. This approach allowed us to identify several significant semantic discrepancies. Nevertheless, it is still possible that there are more errors that could only be detected by experts in linguistics or psychometrics with specialised training in questionnaire design and validation. In addition, we observed a discrepancy between the LLM’s indications and those of the native speakers. Apart from the characteristics indicated in the previous paragraph, there were cases where the LLM identified different problems from those flagged by human reviewers. For example, in the Vietnamese version, the LLM pointed to two questions that differed significantly from the original (questions 3 and 4). Meanwhile, when asked to analyse these questions, the native speaker initially did not indicate any particular differences. When asked to reanalyse the questions, the LLM, armed with the native speaker’s doubts, continued to insist on its result. Ultimately, when asked about the differences identified by the LLM, the native speaker confirmed their existence, although not to the same extent as the LLM. This example shows that the use of an LLM alone is sufficient for an initial or pilot assessment. In structured, well-defined contexts, AI-assisted tools (such as those combining Artificial Recurrent Neural Networks with Machine Translation and rule-based processes) are gradually closing the gap with human experts and can already identify translation errors with accuracy rates up to 99.96% [67], suggesting strong evidence for their reliability. However, human expertise remains indispensable for nuanced, idiomatic, and high-stakes translations.

In the present study, we primarily focused on the problems in the original Polish version—the questionnaire we eventually revised—and presented the most noticeable translation errors identified during the analysis in other language versions. Although this may appear limited in scope, it still has epistemic value as it highlights the broader issue of inconsistencies between different language versions, which aligns with the central aim of this study.

Ultimately, 21 language versions were included in the comparison. While this may seem restrictive, it should be emphasised that the goal of this work was not an exhaustive audit of all the available translations, which should be the task of the WHO, but to highlight the existing problem of using insufficiently standardised tools for international comparisons. The analysis of these selected tools alone revealed significant challengesthat need to be addressed. In our opinion, a comprehensive review should be initiated by the WHO, and new versions of the tools should be validated not only by country teams, but also by the organisation, encompassing both linguistic and psychometric validation.

Conclusions

Our analysis reveals substantial discrepancies among the national versions of the WHOQOL-BREF tool, undermining the comparability of cross-country results. To address this issue, the instrument must undergo rigorous multilingual validation and standardisation. The WHO should assume active oversight of the translation process to help safeguard linguistic accuracy and structural consistency. Furthermore, the organisation should enforce a uniform layout and formatting standard across all language versions.

Although the WHO mentions on its website and in the questionnaires that the translations were not conducted by the organisation itself, the mere presence of these tools in the official WHO repository gives them the appearance of being formally endorsed. Researchers and clinicians frequently print and hand them out to patients and publish them in medical journals without modification.

Unfortunately, our analysis shows that elements such as layout, instructions, additional elements, question wording, and response scale labels often vary significantly between versions. These translation differences may stem from two factors: (1) appropriate cultural adaptation and localisation, and (2) translation errors. In the case of proper adaptation, supported by appropriate multi-step procedures (methodological rigour, piloting, psychometric validation), variation may be acceptable and documented. However, translation errors can compromise the accuracy and validity of the results.

Importantly, some of the discrepancies identified in this study go beyond minor linguistic variation and involve shifts in the conceptual meaning of specific items. In such cases, the translated question may partially measure a construct different from that intended in the original instrument. This raises concerns not only about cross-national comparability, but also about the validity of measurements within individual language versions. If respondents systematically interpret a modified item in a different way, the resulting domain scores may reflect a different aspect of health-related quality of life than originally intended.

This issue requires urgent attention, as numerous studies based on the current versions of the WHOQOL-BREF may lack cross-national comparability, potentially distorting conclusions in research over many years. For example, a study might conclude that Polish patients have higher mobility than those in other countries, while in reality the item used measured how they assessed their current situation. Similarly, in some translations identified in this analysis, wording changes narrow the scope of the questions or introduce additional contextual constraints, which may systematically influence how respondents evaluate their quality of life. We recommend that the WHO thoroughly review all existing translations, including both linguistic and structural elements, with particular attention to the preservation of instructions for the respondent and conceptual integrity. The organisation’s current abdication of oversight over this important tool negatively affects the quality of global research and its potential use in international contexts.

Additionally, the first author wishes to note that based on the findings of this study, a revised version of the Polish scale has been accepted and published by the WHO on its website. However, this revision addresses only one language version. The broader issue of the lack of comparability of the scales in other languages remains unresolved. We hope that this publication raises greater awareness of the problem and contributes to the advancement of more reliable comparable research in the field of quality of life assessment.
