Functional characterization of tumor-specific CRISPR-directed gene editing as a combinatorial therapy for the treatment of solid tumors
Authors: Kelly H. Banas, Pawel A. Bialk, Natalia Rivera-Torres, Katelynn Owens, Tori N. Reiner, Kristen M. Pisarcik, Nicole Haas, Emily Gielda, Komal Khan, Krishna Priya Narra, Eric B. Kmiec
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As we pursue clinical applications for CRISPR-directed gene editing in overcoming resistance to anticancer drugs, we have focused on genetic disruption of the transcription factor, NRF2, a master regulator of cellular stress and detoxification. The level of NRF2 in tumor cells is often a clear determinant of the effectiveness of standard of care. We began to craft a therapeutic approach using tumor-specific CRISPR editing, building upon our previous work elucidating the effect of NRF2 knockout. We selected a prevalent mutation, R34G, that occurs in the Neh2 domain of NRF2, which has been shown to disrupt KEAP1-mediated degradation, thus impacting the NRF2-KEAP1 pathway. Here, we take a global approach by assessing the genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and phenotypic profile of a CRISPR-targeted population of cells, both in vitro and in vivo. We detail the design and generation of a clinically relevant cell model and its translation into an animal model, characterizing the efficacy of disabling NRF2 concomitant with the restoration of chemosensitivity. We demonstrate that 20%–40% gene editing activity is sufficient to improve response to chemotherapy in animal models. We suggest that understanding the genetic diversity of CRISPR outcomes must be a key consideration in identifying effective CRISPR molecules for clinical application.

Introduction

Despite productive advances in standard care for cancer patients, radiotherapy and chemotherapy remain foundational treatments for solid tumors. The debilitating side effects of both have been well established, and the development of treatment resistance poses a major obstacle. The molecular mechanism of drug resistance is complex and primarily centered on the upregulation or mutations of a variety of genes involved in controlling the efflux of anticancer drugs or transcriptional activation of tumor drivers.1,2,3,4 Currently, there are few effective means of reversing drug resistance in solid tumors, particularly in lung cancer, other than to increase dosing or to modify components of the treatment cocktail. As a result, patient suffering increases and quality of life diminishes.5 Hence, there is a pressing need to develop tools to manage drug resistance and thus enable sustainable, effective treatment protocols.

The development of the novel gene editing tool, CRISPR-Cas, has advanced the prospects of genetic medicine for the treatment of solid tumors. We have been examining the use of CRISPR-Cas complexes to overcome drug resistance in adenocarcinoma and squamous carcinoma of the lung.6,7,8 These studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of such an approach, particularly in the reduction of chemoresistance. The well-known transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (NFE2L2 gene, known as NRF2) is a central gene mediating the evolution of drug resistance in cancer.9,10,11,12,13,14 The upregulation of this master regulator, involved in cellular responses to oxidative and/or electrophilic stress, enhances resistance of cancer cells to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy.15,16,17 Upregulation of NRF2 can occur through various mechanisms; however, mutations in the pathway are most frequent. These mutations in NRF2 destabilize the NRF2-KEAP1 complex, thus impairing proteasomal degradation of NRF2 and in turn leading to protein accumulation and activation of downstream pathways (Figure 1A). NRF2 is composed of five exons encoding the NRF2 protein, comprising seven highly conserved domains referred to as NRF2-ECH (Neh) with important roles in the activity or regulation of NRF2 as a transcription factor (Figure 1B). In particular, exon 2 encodes the KEAP1-binding domain with DLG/ETGE motifs, which foster interaction with KEAP1 protein, responsible for maintaining and tightly controlling ubiquitin-proteasome-based proteolysis of NRF2 under basal conditions in the cell. In some patients suffering from lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), mutations arise at the 5′ end of exon 2, thus disrupting the KEAP1-binding domain and impairing regulation of NRF2 protein within the cell. One of the more prevalent somatic mutations in LUSC is R34G,18 which creates a unique target for CRISPR-Cas9 cleavage. The appearance of this new protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) site affords the opportunity for the CRISPR-Cas complex to cleave the mutated NRF2 genome while leaving the wild-type genome intact (Figure 1C). Hence, it is possible to design a CRISPR-Cas9 complex that disables NRF2 in tumor cells restoring chemosensitivity, while healthy cells are left unmodified. The existence of a tumor-specific CRISPR-Cas target creates an interesting therapeutic possibility, wherein the CRISPR-Cas tool is specifically designed to disable and disrupt mutant NRF2 without impairing normal NRF2 in healthy cells. This approach offers cancer-specific, selective targeting of the genome in a patient. To fully understand and characterize our approach, we describe the genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and phenotypic profiles of a CRISPR-targeted population of tumor cells. Results

Generation of a clinically relevant cell-based model system using a novel CRISPR-Cas9 approach and validation of tumor-specific CRISPR knockout

We decided to take a reductionist approach and examine the onsite activity and downstream consequences of cleaving NRF2 at the R34G PAM recognition site. Since there were no readily available or well-established cell model systems, we engineered the R34G mutation in the clinically relevant NCI-H1703 cell line, using CRISPR-Cas9 and a homology-directed repair (HDR) template. Using the experimental workflow shown in Figure 2A, a two-step engineering approach was designed to maximize HDR efficiency and increase desired clonal outcomes (Figure 2B). The first-step transfection introduced PAM-blocking mutations intended to block re-cleavage while simultaneously creating the R34G point mutation (C>G) and an additional silent mutation that served as a seed-blocking sequence for the second-step transfection. The second-step transfection repaired the blocking mutations back to the original sequence while maintaining the R34G mutation. Once the R34G-mutated cell lines were validated, we wanted to test gene editing activity in the presence and absence of the R34G PAM site. As shown in Figure 3, we achieve specific targeting of the R34G-mutated genomic DNA by CRISPR-Cas9 in the homozygous C44-25 cell line and the heterozygous C64-64 cell line without editing the wild-type genomic DNA. Using optimized conditions, cells were transfected with CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex targeting NRF2 using the R34G mutation as the PAM site for the guide RNA (gRNA). Genomic DNA was collected from the bulk population of transfected cells, sequenced using Next-Generation sequencing (NGS), and gene editing activity was analyzed using CRISPResso2.19 In the presence of the R34G mutation, we achieve overall editing of 91.2% in the homozygous C44-25 cell line and 38.0% in the heterozygous C64-64 cell line (Figure 3A). Figure 3B presents the insertion or deletion (indel) profile after successful editing of NRF2 utilizing the R34G mutation, which remains consistent across both cell lines. The major indel profile at the cleavage site of the R34G gRNA is a frameshifting 1 bp insertion (T), non-frameshifting 9 bp deletion, or frameshifting 4 bp deletion with various other indels contributing to the indel profile at much lower frequencies. To better understand the downstream impact of these indels on NRF2 protein expression, western blot was conducted as shown in Figure 3C. Genotypic and phenotypic characterization of tumor-specific CRISPR-directed gene knockout of R34G-mutant NRF2 in vitro

The data presented in Figure 3 reveal a diverse indel pattern at the R34G target site. While this result is not unprecedented,20,21,22 in general, the contribution of each subpopulation bearing different allelic contents and its impact on function are poorly understood. Our earlier work demonstrated that genetic disruption of critical functional domains in NRF2 could, in fact, restore sensitivity to drug treatment.8 Because we are developing CRISPR-directed gene editing of NRF2 to overcome resistance to drug therapy, we wondered if subpopulations that harbored preferential indel patterns had a greater impact on functional response than others. Due to the nature of CRISPR-induced double-stranded breaks and DNA repair, the heterogeneous profile of indels created cannot be wholly controlled; however, some of the most common genetic profiles found in CRISPR-directed gene editing exhibit genotypes of +1 and −1, consistent with other indel profiles reported in other experimental systems.21,22,23 As such, we found it imperative to characterize and understand each indel created as it would directly impact our understanding of functional outcomes. We isolated individual cells after targeting C44-25 with R34G-specific CRISPR-Cas9 RNP and created an array of clonal cell lines that harbor different indels (Figure S1). Briefly, DNA was isolated from each clonal cell line, Sanger sequenced, and analyzed using DECODR24 to accurately deconvolute the indel profile. From the clonally isolated cell lines, a heterogeneous allelic profile is observed with only a few pairs of alleles having the same genetic footprint and the others being unique.

We strategically selected a series of clones (Figure 4A) that represent the major assortment of indels generated when targeting the clonal cell line C44-25 to better understand the function of each indel as well as the collective impact of indels in a cell. Each clonal cell line selected for further characterization also allowed us to interrogate the potential downstream impact of each indel. Sequencing the isolated mRNA generated by the edited or unedited NRF2 gene (Figure 4B) confirms that the transcripts are in fact produced with the same indel signature seen in the genomic DNA. Although each clonal cell line contains two alleles with distinct indel patterns, NRF2-specific amplification and sequencing of the cDNA reveal preferential expression of certain alleles in each clone, as analyzed by DECODR. For example, clone 3 roughly shows 50/50 allelic expression as opposed to clone 19, which shows more transcript of the allele with the 9 bp deletion. On the other hand, clone 21 shows more transcripts of the allele with the single-base insertion. This can help predict the proteomic and phenotypic outcome of CRISPR targeting. To further understand the impact of each unique indel, we analyzed protein expression by western blot (Figure 4C). Whole-cell lysate was collected from each clonal cell line with and without exposure to a low concentration of cisplatin (3 μM) to ensure the upregulation of NRF2 protein. As expected, the positive control (mock, mock transfection of C44-25) exhibits a strong NRF2 protein band at approximately 100 kDa. Also, as expected, clone 3, which bears a wild-type allele and an allele with a 9 bp in-frame deletion, also produces a fully intact protein. In contrast, clone 8, which has two alleles bearing a single-base insertion, does not produce any detectable protein, whereas clone 11, which contains no genetically modified alleles, produces high levels of NRF2 protein. Clone 19, which contains deletions of 4 and 9 bases on each allele, respectively, does in fact express less basal NRF2. Clone 21, which harbors alleles with a single-base insertion and a 4 bp deletion, is devoid of protein. Lastly, clone 35, which contains a single-base insertion and a 9 bp deletion, also expresses less basal NRF2. Based on these data, cells with either allele bearing a single-base insertion fail to produce NRF2 protein, but cells with allelic content comprising an in-frame 9 bp deletion (even in combination) produce NRF2 protein.

While western blot analyses are instructive in identifying the presence or absence of NRF2 protein, it is much more informative to analyze the functionality of a genetically modified protein. As described earlier, NRF2 is a master regulator of a series of downstream genes that are activated upon oxidative stress or toxic insult. We utilized quantitative PCR (qPCR) to look for the activation or suppression of various downstream genes of NRF2, such as NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), and glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) as a function of gene knockout of NRF2.25 Figure 4D illustrates the relative quantity of respective mRNA, relative to GAPDH, present in each clone as compared to the control (mock, unedited R34G). Under basal cell conditions and with the complete loss of protein, clone 8 and clone 21 show a significant reduction in NQO1, HMOX1, and GCLC mRNA, whereas clones with an allele containing the 9 bp deletion in NRF2 are still likely able to activate the transcription of NQO1, HMOX1, and GCLC, maintaining the transcriptional function of NRF2.

Orthogonal off-target assessment of tumor-specific CRISPR-directed gene knockout of R34G-mutant NRF2

CRISPR-Cas9 executes double-stranded breaks at precise genomic loci, a critical first step for effective genome engineering. However, it also has a well-recognized limitation: a tendency to induce double-stranded breaks at unintended genomic loci with sequence similarity to the intended target site, so-called off-target effects, which can occur across the genome. Efforts to minimize off-target effects start with the design and sequence of the gRNA, which is the key component in CRISPR-Cas9 specificity. However, even with careful consideration of gRNA design, additional measures must be taken for the detection of off-target effects. Many methods have been developed for analyzing and assessing off-target effects; however, there is no gold standard, as each method has its own set of strengths and limitations, thus necessitating that multiple paths be taken.

An orthogonal approach can be employed to overcome the limitations of each method, broken down into two phases: nomination and validation (Figure 5A). For our off-target nomination, our experiments encompassed in silico, biochemical, and cellular methods to detect and identify any potential off-target loci within the genome. Once off-target loci were identified, validation encompassed targeted next-generation sequencing using the RhAmpSeq platform26,27,28 to investigate the presence of valid off-target editing. We used Cas-OFFinder29 for in silico prediction of potential off-target sites across the entire genome. This yielded 478 sites with similar homology to our on-target site. Next, we used SITE-Seq30 for biochemical interrogation of off-target sites, which yielded 29 sites. Lastly, we used GUIDE-Seq31 for cell-based off-target assessment, which yielded 13 sites. In total, 499 unique off-target sites were identified across all the three methods (Figure S3). The nominated potential off-target sites were validated using rhAmpSeq technology through targeted PCR amplification and deep sequencing. Briefly, rhAmpSeq primers for nominated off-target sites were designed and pooled together to perform multiplex amplicon sequencing. Two nominated sites identified by Cas-OFFinder could not be analyzed by sequencing due to the low complexity of the sequence surrounding the sites. Figure 5B presents the on-target editing efficiency of the R34G gRNA and wild-type SpCas9 in NRF2 in each respective cell line. R34G-mutated cells transfected with the R34G gRNA are highly edited, respective to their R34G-mutated allele frequency (on-target: H1703 C44-25 95.43% and H1703 C64-64 38.87%), while the R34G gRNA does not significantly edit the unmutated wild-type site (on-target: H1703 WT, <0.001% and NHBE, 0.02%). The data support that the cleavage activity of the R34G gRNA is highly specific to its intended target. We identified five potential off-target sites with detectable gene editing activity across the cell lines tested, as shown in Figure 5C. When these off-target loci were interrogated in the same cells treated with HiFi spCas9, editing activity fell below 0.2% (Figure S4). A marginal decrease in on-target activity is observed when using HiFi spCas9 in cells with the R34G genotype, whereas a slight increase in on-target activity is observed in cells lacking R34G.

Development and validation of the R34G-mutant NRF2 model system in vivo for intratumoral LNP-mediated CRISPR delivery

Based on recent successes of nonviral vectors, we selected lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) to deliver the gene editing payload into tumor cells. We screened 6 different lipid formulations supplied by Precision NanoSystems (PNI, Vancouver Canada) to determine which LNP had the best expression and biodistribution activity. In the initial analyses, firefly luciferase mRNA was encapsulated to evaluate expression levels and biodistribution of each LNP. H1703 C44-25 cells with the R34G mutation were subcutaneously implanted in female NCG mice. Once tumors reached between 80 and 120 mm3, 2 μg of each respective LNP was directly injected into the tumor and imaged 24 h later using IVIS imaging (Figure S5A). The data show high expression of luciferase from all LNP formulations with much of the signal coming from the tumor (Figure S5B) and some signal presumably from the liver. To achieve a more sensitive measure of expression and biodistribution of the LNP and firefly luciferase mRNA, mice were sacrificed 24 h post intratumoral injection, and organs were collected for RNA isolation and qPCR, presented in Figure S5C. Importantly, the y axis of the tumor data, highlighted in red, reflects a 10,000-fold decrease in luciferase mRNA detection in the outlying tissues in comparison to the tumor.

Based on the in vivo characterization of the various lipid formulations, LNP 3 was selected for further testing with a CRISPR-Cas9 payload. Briefly, 5-methoxyuridine (5moU)-modified wild-type spCas9 mRNA (TriLink) and R34G single gRNA (sgRNA) (IDT) were encapsulated at a 1:1 w/v ratio. The final CRISPR LNP was then tested in vitro in the H1703 C44-25 cell line using increasing concentrations (2–500 ng) to assess gene editing activity. Cells were harvested, Sanger sequenced, and analyzed by DECODR to determine editing efficiency, as displayed in Figure 6A. The data show that as the concentration of CRISPR LNP increases, the overall frameshifting indel and gene editing activity increases. The indel profile of each representative concentration of CRISPR LNP, shown in Figure 6B, remains consistent with the data generated previously. Next, the CRISPR LNP was tested in vivo to assess gene editing efficiency and the indel profile. As depicted in the experimental workflow in Figure 6C, the H1703 C44-25 cells were subcutaneously implanted in athymic nude mice, and once tumors reached 80–120 mm3, 10 μg of CRISPR LNP was injected into the tumor. Mice were sacrificed, and tumors were collected 72 h post intratumoral injection. Genomic DNA was isolated from tumors, PCR amplified, sequenced by NGS, and analyzed using CRISPResso. Overall editing efficiency was assessed (Figure 6D), as well as the indel profile (Figure 6E). The indel profile after CRISPR-Cas9 editing is an important outcome to measure, as different indels result in different downstream consequences. Indel patterns show a significant population of +1 indel formation in H1703 C44-25 cell-based tumors, along with a high proportion of −9 and −4 bp indels of the same set of bases, matching the indel profile from in vitro characterization of the R34G gRNA presented in Figure 3. The other indels present within the indel profile make up a much smaller percentage with each indel showing less than 1% contribution, ranging from −12, −2, −3, and −1 deletions (representative indel profile in Figure S6). Taken together, the R34G-targeting CRISPR LNP shows high editing activity at the NRF2 gene and maintains the same indel profile extensively characterized in vitro. Similarly, we were interested in replicating these results in patient-derived models (Jax244 [TM00244] homozygous at R34G, and JAX 263 [TM00263] and NCI-PDMR [576588-073-R], both heterozygous at R34G, respectively). Mice were implanted with fragments from each model, and once tumors reached 80–120 mm3, they were subsequently injected with CRISPR LNP. The levels of Cas9 mRNA localization and gene editing efficiency were comparable between cell-based and patient-derived models (Figure S7), in support of the idea that expression and activity of Cas9 is achievable in diverse patient backgrounds.

To verify our in vitro functional assessment of NRF2 disruption, we next asked whether the activity of this CRISPR LNP could impact the growth of the tumor through disruption of downstream pathways. Athymic nude mice were injected with H1703 C44-25 cells subcutaneously, and once tumors reached between 200 and 300 mm3, tumors were injected once with either 10 μg of CRISPR LNP or an equivalent volume of PBS. Tumor volume was measured twice weekly for a month, and tumors were collected for genomic DNA and RNA isolation for downstream analysis. Relative expression measured by RT-qPCR of human NRF2, human Ki-67, and human NQO1, normalized to human GAPDH, was conducted in tumors injected with 10 μg CRISPR LNP as compared to PBS-injected controls (average, error bars ±SEM). (D) Experimental workflow for assessment of chemosensitivity after NRF2 targeting in a xenograft model (created with Biorender.com). (E) Tumor volume assessment after PBS or CRISPR LNP intratumoral injections with chemotherapy administration. H1703 C44-25 cells were implanted subcutaneously in mice. Once tumors reached 200–300 mm3 in size, mice were randomized into each respective group, and tumors were injected with 10 μg of CRISPR LNP or PBS, as a control. Both groups of mice were administered 7.5 mg/kg carboplatin and 2.5 m/kg paclitaxel by tail vein injection (as indicated by the arrows) on days 3, 10, and 17, post intratumoral injection. Tumor volume was measured and plotted on the graph over time with days post intratumoral injection as the x axis (average, error bars ±SEM; CRISPR LNP/Chemo, n = 8; PBS/Chemo, n = 5).

Lastly, we wanted to assess if CRISPR-directed knockout of NRF2 could halt tumor growth and restore chemosensitivity in vivo. Athymic nude mice bearing H1703 C44-25 cell-based tumors were injected once with 10 μg of CRISPR LNP followed by systemic administration of chemotherapy for LUSC (carboplatin and paclitaxel32,33,34) once a week, for 3 weeks (Figure 7D). Tumors were measured twice a week, and mean volumes were calculated and plotted over time (PBS/chemo, n = 5; CRISPR LNP/chemo, n = 8) (Figure 7E). The data show that mice injected with CRISPR LNP-treated tumors and treated with chemotherapy have arrested tumor growth over time when compared to PBS-injected mice with chemotherapy. These results confirm that CRISPR-directed knockout of NRF2 is able to restore chemosensitivity in LUSC tumors.

Discussion

In pursuit of a clinical application for CRISPR-directed gene editing in solid tumors, we have focused our efforts on the transcription factor NRF2, a master regulator of cellular stress and detoxification, highly active in promoting tumor cell survival. The level of NRF2 in tumor cells is an indicator of the potential effectiveness of therapeutic agents since it regulates genes that control efflux and metabolism, including key genes and pathways responsible for resistance to chemotherapy, radiation, and even immunotherapy.35,36,37,38,39 Therefore, by designing a sgRNA that successfully disables the functions of this gene, we have demonstrated enhancement of anticancer treatments. In this work, we detail our methodical approach and describe our workflow for assessing novel targets for CRISPR-directed gene editing, even in the absence of clinically relevant or attainable model systems. Importantly, we have detailed the impact of indel profiles in a clinically relevant setting, specifically when the editing events create a heterozygous state inside the targeted cell population. We isolate, expand, and examine many clonal populations (see Figure S1) to understand the nature of each indel profile to be able to translate expected phenotypic outcomes in vivo.

The work described in this manuscript sets the foundation for testing the feasibility of CRISPR-directed gene editing in solid tumors as a therapeutic. Several key evaluations must be part of any developmental plan including the characterization of CRISPR activity at the target site of interest as well as potential off-target sites. Reproducibility of gene editing activity and efficiency regardless of delivery mechanism remains a critical bridging study for translation to in vivo-based assessments. Finally, when targeting a multifunctional transcription factor such as NRF2, the functional outcome needs to be assessed to fully understand the downstream impact on key pathways.40,41

Our in vitro assessment of CRISPR-directed NRF2 knockout reveals that the R34G-mutation sgRNA is highly specific on-target and with little to no off-target activity. Figures 3 and 5 highlight the importance of thoughtful gRNA design and thorough assessment in various cell-based models. We see that even with 100% sequence homology to the NRF2 sequence but in the absence of the PAM-creating R34G mutation, the CRISPR-Cas9 complex is unable to recognize and elicit significant double-stranded breaks for efficient indel formation. Our data support and confirm previous observations of CRISPR-directed gene editing of R34G-mutated NRF2 in cell culture.7,8 To further understand on-target activity in a clinically relevant model system, we methodically characterize each indel at the genomic level and its downstream impact as shown in Figure 4. We see that the combination of indels in a heterozygous population can lead to differential phenotypic outcomes, highlighting the importance of both individual and population-based assessments after gene editing activity. The clonal data show that the degree of protein knockout has a direct impact on the downstream phenotypic outcome, based on the target gene. In our case, loss of NRF2 protein directly correlates with decreased expression of its target genes, such as NQO1, HMOX1, and GCLC. By validating the indel profile of each clonal cell line both at the genomic and transcript level, we can better understand the downstream impacts of CRISPR activity, thus offering a more predictive interpretation of the success of CRISPR-directed gene disruption.

By fully characterizing the impact of CRISPR activity, we were confident moving into an animal model system, testing the feasibility of targeting NRF2 in solid tumors using CRISPR. We validated the use of an LNP encapsulating CRISPR-Cas9 as a robust delivery mechanism and show comparable CRISPR activity and editing efficiency in vitro (Figure 6) before in vivo assessment. When delivered by direct injection into the tumor, we saw that the CRISPR LNP retained high editing efficiency with the same indel profile (+1, −9, and −4 indels) characterized extensively in vitro, suggesting that editing with Cas9 might result in altered protein expression and function (Figure 4). We demonstrate effective gene editing at a level that supports restoration of chemosensitivity and increased survival in both cell-based and patient-derived mouse models. We conclude that the R34G mutation, present only in tumor cells, prevents the effective action of chemotherapeutic drugs, and therefore, the disablement of this mutation by CRISPR-Cas restores chemosensitivity. We show that tumors treated with chemotherapy in the presence of CRISPR-Cas gene editing complex targeting NRF2 are sensitized to chemotherapy, thereby decreasing tumor growth (Figure 7).16,42,43,44 We do recognize that there are some limitations to our studies. Lack of a relevant, readily available cell model system prompted us to engineer our own, thus providing some complexity to the analysis of results. Delivery of the CRISPR-Cas system in vivo remains an ongoing challenge in the field, and while we show promising results using LNPs encapsulating CRISPR-Cas, further optimization of the molecules is needed for translational application.

Our work has always assumed that CRISPR-Cas would be used as an augmentative therapy to support standard of care by facilitating the disruption of genes causing resistance or reducing effective sustainability of targeted therapies. We detail the importance of the complete characterization of CRISPR activity on a target gene and its downstream implications. In the present case, by inhibiting the master regulator of chemoresistance, NRF2, in vivo, we were able to restore chemosensitivity to a level that stalled tumor growth. In a clinical setting, our work could impact treatment regimens in a patient population that is unable to tolerate the escalation of drug needed to achieve a reduction in tumor growth or progression-free survival. Strategically, we feel that by lowering the amount of chemotherapy needed to inhibit tumor growth, a patient will be able to tolerate treatment for longer periods of time supporting more beneficial outcomes. Alternatively, shrinking the size of the tumor may enable surgical resection and, at the very least, reduce the level of suffering endured by patients undergoing anticancer drug treatment. Our results provide important insight as to how these basic observations lead to clinically relevant or irrelevant functional changes and should guide decisions as to treatment options.

Materials and methods

CRISPR-Cas9 design and assembly

The NRF2 gene-coding sequence was analyzed in SnapGene, the R34 amino acid was identified, and the following gRNA was designed: 5′-GATATAGATCTTGGAGTAAG-3′. For control experiments, scramble gRNA (5′-GCACTACCAGAGCTAACTCA-3′) was used. For in vitro studies, synthetic sgRNAs and recombinant SpCas9 protein were ordered from Synthego (Menlo Park, CA, USA). sgRNA and SpCas9 protein were mixed together at a 5:1 molar ratio (250:50 pmol) and set to incubate at room temperature for 15 min before transfections. For in vivo studies, high-performance liquid chromatography-purified synthetic sgRNA was ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA), and SpCas9 mRNA (CleanCap fully substituted with N1-Methyl-Pseudo-U) was ordered from TriLink Biotechnologies (San Diego, CA). sgRNA and SpCas9 mRNA were mixed together at a 1:1 w/v ratio for encapsulation in LNPs purchased from Precision Nanosystems (Vancouver, BC).

Cell line and culture conditions

Human LUSC cells, NCI-H1703 (#CRL-15889, passage number 1–12), were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were thawed, according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and grown in RPMI-1640 (ATCC modification) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ATCC) and grown at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cell lines were tested for mycoplasma upon thawing and before use in experiments using the MycoScope PCR Mycoplasma detection kit (Genlantis, Cat. MY01100). Primary normal human bronchial epithelial (NHBE) cells were purchased from Lonza (NHBE CC-2540). Cells were thawed according to the manufacturer’s protocol and grown in BEGM BulletKit media (Lonza CC-3170) and grown a 37°C in 5% CO2.

Generation of CRISPR-engineered cell lines

To create the R34G-mutated cell line, a two-step approach was optimized to introduce the mutation of interest using CRISPR-Cas9. To generate the R34G mutation in NRF2, Alt-R HDR templates (200 nt) were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, Iowa). NCI-H1703 cells were seeded 48 h prior to transfection and allowed to reach 60%–80% confluency. On the day of the transfection, cells were harvested by trypsinization and washed twice with 1× PBS (−/−). Cells were resuspended in Lonza SF/supplement solution, and RNP complex and HDR template (1) (5′- TTAAAAAACATGAGCTCTCTCCTTCCTTTTTTTGTCTTAAACATAGGACATGGATTTGATTGACATACTTTGGAGGCAAGATATAGATCTTGcAGTAtcTgGAGAAGTATTTGACTTCAGTCAGCGACGGAAAGAGTATGAGCTGGAAAAACAGAAAAAACTTGAAAAGGAAAGACAAGAACAACTCCAAAAGGAGCAAG-3′) (lowercase letters showing intended mutations) were added to each sample. Lonza program CM-130 was used, and after 15 min of rest, cells were transferred to 6-well plates for further experiments. Transfected NCI-H1703 cells were manually sorted into 96-well plates. Clones were expanded and transferred to larger plates as individual clones reached confluence. Clones were screened and Sanger sequenced for identification of the intended mutation. Once identified and expanded, the clonal isolate was transfected following the same protocol with HDR template (2) (5′- TTAAAAAACATGAGCTCTCTCCTTCCTTTTTTTGTCTTAAACATAGGACATGGATTTGATTGACATACTTTGGAGGCAAGATATAGATCTTGgAGTAagTGGAGAAGTATTTGACTTCAGTCAGCGACGGAAAGAGTATGAGCTGGAAAAACAGAAAAAACTTGAAAAGGAAAGACAAGAACAACTCCAAAAGGAGCAAG-3′) (lowercase letters showing intended mutations) to induce the desired mutation. Once the desired clonal cell lines were identified, cells were further expanded to create master and working cell banks for downstream assays.

Transfection and clonal expansion

H1703 parental (WT), C64-64, and C44-25 cells were seeded 24 h prior to transfection and allowed to reach 60%–80% confluency. On the day of the transfection, cells were harvested by trypsinization and washed twice with 1× PBS (−/−). Cells were resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/100 μL in SF/supplement solution, and 5 μL of RNP complex (250 pmols of scramble or R34G gRNA complexed with 50 pmols of SpCas9 protein) or 5 μL of PBS (mock transfection) was added to each sample. Lonza program CM-130 was used, and after 15 min of rest, cells were transferred to T25 flasks for 48 h and collected for downstream experiments.

For single-cell clonal expansion, 48 h after transfection, cells were manually sorted into 96-well plates. Clones were expanded and transferred to larger welled plates as individual clones reached confluence. Clones were screened and Sanger sequenced to identify the indels present at the intended cut site within NRF2.

Sanger sequencing, next-generation sequencing, and gene editing analysis

Cellular genomic DNA was isolated from each clonal cell line using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) or QuickExtract reagent (Biosearch Technologies). The region surrounding the CRISPR target site was PCR amplified using the Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs) or Phusion High Fidelity master mix (exon 2 – FWD primer 5′-CACCATCAACAGTGGCATAATGTGAA-3′, REV primer 5′-AACTCAGGTTAGGTACTGAACTCATCA-3′). The PCR reaction was purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN), and Big Dye Terminator PCR was performed using Big Dye Terminator v.3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). PCR products were purified once more using the Big Dye Xterminator kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then sequenced using the SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Bulk and clonal allelic analysis was conducted using the software program DECODR.24

For NGS, targeted amplicon libraries were prepared using Illumina’s 16S metagenomic sequencing library preparation protocol for the MiSeq. Genomic DNA was extracted from cells using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (QIAGEN) or QuickExtract reagent (Biosearch Technologies). The region surrounding the CRISPR target site in exon 2 (398 bp) of the NRF2 gene was amplified using the 2X Phusion Flash PCR master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Post amplification, samples were cleaned using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA), and DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were run on TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) to verify the presence of the desired amplicon and indexed using the IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD indexes set A (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix PCR Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Florham Park, NJ). Samples were again quantified using Qubit, and average library size was calculated using the Tapestation amplicon size. Once pooled to the loading concentration, the libraries were sequenced using the MiSeq Reagent Nano kit v.2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Only the data that passed data QC in Sequencing Analysis Viewer was analyzed using CRISPResso2.19

Western blot analysis

Total protein was isolated from NCI-H1703 cells (treated with ±3 μM cisplatin for 3 days as indicated) in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail (Pierce), incubated on ice for 30 min with vortexing every 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4°C for 15 min, and the supernatant was saved. Protein extracts were quantified using a BCA assay (Pierce), and 20 μg of protein was loaded onto a 4%–20% polyacrylamide pre-cast gel (Bio-Rad). Protein was transferred onto a 0.2 μM nitrocellulose blot using the Turboblot dry transfer system (Bio-Rad). The membrane was blocked in 5% milk in Tris-buffered saline-T for 2 h at room temperature and stained with primary antibody for NRF2 (1:1,000, Abcam ab62352) and GAPDH (1:10,000, Abcam ab8245) overnight on a shaker at 4°C and secondary antibody conjugated to HRP (1:10,000 dilution) (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were imaged using the Pierce Femto western blotting substrate (Pierce) and detected on the Azure Biosystems Gel Imager.

Reverse-transcription PCR

Total RNA was isolated from clonal cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription of total RNA was conducted using the high capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Standard PCR and amplification of NRF2 transcript was done using cDNA as the template and Phusion High Fidelity PCR master mix (cat. F351, Thermo Fisher Scientific) (FWD primer 5′ CCAACACACGGTCCACAGCTCAT 3′ and REV primer 5′ GAAGACTGGGCTCTCGATGTG 3′). The PCR reaction was purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit, and Big Dye Terminator PCR was performed using Big Dye Terminator v.3.1. PCR products were purified once more using the Big Dye Xterminator kit and sequenced using the SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer.

Quantitative reverse-transcription-PCR

For in vitro experiments, H1703 44-25 clonal cell lines were plated in T25 flasks in duplicate at 1 × 106 cells per well and incubated for 96 h before being collected in triplicate. Total RNA was isolated from clonal cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 15596026) per manufacturer’s instructions, and reverse transcription was conducted using the high capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 4388950). The cDNA was used as the template in the qPCR amplification using the SsoAdvanced universal SYBR Green supermix (Bio-Rad, Cat. 1725272) and the following primers: GAPDH (Fwd 5′-TCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCGAC-3′, Rev 5′-CCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAAATTC-3′), NRF2 (Fwd 5′-TCCAAGTCCAGAAGCCAAACTGAC-3′, Rev 5′-GGAGAGGATGCTGCTGAAGGAATC-3′), NQO1 (Fwd 5′-GGTTTGGAGTCCCTGCCATT-3′, Rev 5′-TTGCAGAGAGTACATGGAGCC-3′), HMOX1 (Fwd 5′-CTTTCAGAAGGGCCAGGTGA-3′, Rev 5′-GTAGACAGGGGCGAAGACTG-3′), and GCLC (Fwd 5′GGACAAGAATACACCATCTCCA-3′, Rev 5′-ATACTGCAGGCTTGGAATGTC-3′). The experiment was conducted twice with five technical replicates per sample. Using the Pfaffl method,45 mRNA levels were calculated from Cq values and normalized to the control.

For in vivo experiments, RNA was extracted from tissue samples using the TRIzol RNA extraction protocol as described by the manufacturer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). cDNA was then synthesized using Applied Biosystems high-capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit at 250 ng per reaction, and qPCR was performed in triplicate with Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City CA and Bio Rad, Waltham, MA) and the following primers: GAPDH (Fwd 5′-TCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCGAC-3′, Rev 5′-CCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAAATTC-3′), hNRF2 (Fwd 5′-CACATCCAGTCAGAAACCAGTGG-3′, Rev 5′-GGAATGTCTGCGCCAAAAGCTG-3′), hNQO1 (Fwd 5′-GGTTTGGAGTCCCTGCCATT 3′, Rev 5′-TTGCAGAGAGTACATGGAGCC-3′), and hKi-67 (Fwd 5′-GAAAGAGTGGCAACCTGCCTTC 3′, Rev 5′-GCACCAAGTTTTACTACATCTGCC-3′). Samples were run on a Bio-Rad CFX384 real-time PCR detection system according to the manufacturer’s conditions: 95°C for 20 s followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 3 s and 60°C for 30 s (Bio-Rad, Waltham, MA). Relative quantification for each transcript was obtained by normalizing against Gapdh transcript abundance according to the formula 2(−Ct)/2(CtGapdh).

On- and off-target editing analysis by rhAmpSeq

H1703 parental and clonal cells were seeded 24 h before transfection. On the day of the transfection, cells were harvested by trypsinization and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/100 μL in SF/supplement solution (Lonza SF Cell Line 4D-Nucleofector X Kit, #V4XC-2032). RNP complex (250 pmols of scramble gRNA or R34G gRNA complexed with 50 pmols of SpCas9 protein) was added to each sample. Lonza program CM-130 was used, and after 15 min of rest, cells were transferred to 6-well plates, incubated for 48 h, and collected for genomic DNA isolation.

NHBE cells were seeded 48 h before transfection. On the day of the transfection, cells were harvested by trypsinization and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/100 μL in P3/supplement solution (P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector X Kit, #V4XP-3032). RNP complex (250 pmols of scramble gRNA or R34G gRNA complexed with 50 pmols of SpCas9 protein) was added to each sample. Lonza program DC-100 was used, and after 15 min of rest, cells were transferred to 6-well plates, incubated for 48 h, and collected for genomic DNA isolation.

Primers were designed to flank the on-target and nominated off-target sites, nominated by Cas-OFFinder, SITE-Seq, and GUIDE-Seq, using IDT’s rhAmpSeq Design Tool. Primers were pooled for multiplex amplification, and amplicon libraries were prepared using the rhAmpSeq Library kit and rhAmpSeq Index primers, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) at a PCR product:bead ratio of 1:1, quantified using Qubit 1X dsDNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sequenced using a 300 bp paired-end cycle on an Illumina MiSeq instrument (v2 chemistry, 300 bp paired-end reads; Illumina). Read depth for each sample was at least 5,000 reads per locus, and data were analyzed using IDT’s rhAmpSeq CRISPR Data Analysis Tool. Editing efficiency was calculated as the percentage of reads containing an indel out of total reads at each target site, and scramble gRNA controls were used to estimate NHEJ% by subtraction

In vitro dose curve and gene editing analysis

H1703 44-25 cells were cultured and plated in a 24-well plate with 50,000 cells and allowed to attach for 24 h. The LNP was diluted to appropriate concentrations and added to each respective well and incubated for 72 h. Cells were collected, genomic DNA was isolated using Quick Extract, and exon 2 of NRF2 was PCR amplified using Phusion High Fidelity PCR master mix (cat. F351, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The PCR reaction was purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN), and Big Dye Terminator PCR was performed using Big Dye Terminator v.3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). PCR products were purified once more using the Big Dye Xterminator kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), sequenced using the SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems), and analyzed using DECODR (https://decodr.org/analyze).

Animal experiments

All experiments with mice conformed to Animal Welfare guidelines and were performed in accordance with protocols approved by University of Delaware’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. H1703 cell-implanted tumors were generated in 6- to 8-week-old athymic nude female mice (Charles River, Wilmington, MA) by subcutaneous implantation of 5 × 106 cells (human lung squamous cell-derived H1703 clone 44-25) in BD Matrigel (volume ratio 1:1). Tumor growth was measured and estimated using a caliper and calculated as volume (mm3) = (length [mm] × (width [mm])2 × 0.5. For experiments following tumor volume over time, mice were humanely euthanized when tumor volumes reached 1,600 mm3. Upon study endpoint, tissues were collected for DNA and RNA extraction. First, tissues were homogenized in PBS on the gentleMacs Octo dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Gaithersburg, MD) and then extracted for downstream analysis.

For efficacy experiments, the H1703 C44-25 cells were subcutaneously implanted and allowed to grow until a minimum of 200 mm3 before intratumoral injections. Cell-based tumors were injected once with 10 μg dose of CRISPR LNP intratumorally. After 72 h of intratumoral injections, mice were humanely euthanized, and tumors were collected for downstream analysis. Tumors were homogenized on the gentleMacs Octo dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA was then isolated using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN) per the manufacturer’s protocol.

For proof-of-concept experiments, the H1703 C44-25 cells were subcutaneously implanted and allowed to grow until a minimum of 200 mm3 before intratumoral injections. Cell-based tumors were injected once with 10 μg dose of CRISPR LNP or PBS (control) intratumorally. After 72 h of the last intratumoral injection, mice received 7.5 mg/kg carboplatin and 2.5 mg/kg paclitaxel once a week for 3 weeks. Tumor volumes were measured twice a week until the end of study.
