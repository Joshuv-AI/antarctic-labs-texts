Nonlinear associations between reported childhood trauma and cognitive impairment in schizophrenia spectrum disorders
Authors: Giancarlo E Denaroso, Jinyuan Liu, Arielle Ered, Neil D Woodward, Stephan Heckers, Julia M Sheffield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Cognitive impairment is a core feature of schizophrenia associated with childhood trauma, but the specific adversity factors underlying this relationship remain unclear. We hypothesized that physical neglect, rather than emotional neglect or abuse (emotional, physical, sexual), would be specifically related to general cognition and memory (verbal learning, working memory), even when controlling for co-occurring traumas, and explain most of the cognitive differences between cases and controls. Data visualization revealed several nonlinear relationships, which were examined through exploratory analyses.Methods:215 individuals with schizophrenia and 213 controls were assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire and Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry. Regression models examined relationships between adversity and cognition. Nonlinear associations were evaluated using segmented regressions. Specificity and proportion of variance explained were tested through sensitivity analyses.Results:In schizophrenia, general cognition was associated with all traumas, except sexual abuse. Verbal learning was related to emotional abuse and physical trauma, while working memory was associated with physical neglect and emotional trauma. Nonlinear relationships with cognition were detected for cumulative trauma, emotional neglect, and emotional abuse. Limited evidence was found for specificity. When compared with controls, cumulative trauma explained more cognitive impairment than any subtype.Conclusions:We provide novel evidence for nonlinear associations between trauma and cognition, which may account for discrepancy among existing studies and suggest sensitivity of neural substrates of memory to particular levels of trauma. Our results also indicate that some of the cognitive deficits in schizophrenia may be attributed to the influence of early environmental factors.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Childhood trauma, Cognitive impairment, Nonlinear relationships

Introduction

Individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders exhibit poorer cognitive performance compared to nonpsychiatric controls across a wide range of domains, including pre-morbid IQ, verbal learning, executive functioning, and processing speed (McCutcheon et al., 2023). Although these deficits are considered a prominent feature of psychosis associated with poor prognoses and functional impairments (Christy et al., 2023), their underlying causes remain unclear and are likely multifactorial. Researchers have identified negative relationships between cognition and factors such as genetic risk (Kuo et al., 2022), prenatal complications (Amoretti et al., 2022), and substance abuse (Ward et al., 2023), albeit with some incongruent results (McCutcheon et al., 2023). In addition, childhood trauma (CT) has also been linked to disrupted cognition in schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Lipner et al., 2023); however, while some report negative effects of trauma on cognitive functioning, particularly working and episodic memory (Mørkved et al., 2020), several describe null and even positive CT-cognition associations (Dauvermann and Donohoe, 2019).

Conflicting reports may result from broad measures masking differential effects of distinct adversity subtypes (Thomas et al., 2023). For instance, in nonpsychiatric samples, early life exposure to neglect—the absence of anticipated cognitive, social, and physical inputs from the environment—is primarily associated with cognitive processes, such as memory and executive function, while abuse—the presence of atypical experiences that impose harm to emotional or physical integrity—is strongly related to emotional processing (Schäfer et al., 2023; Sheridan et al., 2017; Terock et al., 2020). Accordingly, poorer cognition, including verbal learning and working memory, has been observed in individuals with schizophrenia reporting increased childhood neglect, especially physical neglect, but not abuse (Kilian et al., 2018; King et al., 2021; Mørkved et al., 2020; Sideli et al., 2022).

Many of these and related studies, however, fail to control for co-occurring trauma influences, such as other CT exposures (Sheridan et al., 2017) and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which is associated with greater cognitive impairment in schizophrenia (Seow et al., 2016), possibly leading to biased estimates of how specific CT subtypes relate to cognition. Previous research is also limited by not including control participants (Dauvermann and Donohoe, 2019), leaving the extent to which CT and its subtypes account for differences in cognition between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls uncertain. Thus, even with the growing literature on disaggregating childhood adversity, it is still not clear how specific trauma exposures relate to cognition in the context of schizophrenia. Better characterizing these relationships may enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment and aid efforts to develop more effective interventions that improve clinical and functional outcomes.

In the present study, we tested how subtypes of early life adversity relate to cognitive function when controlling for co-occurring traumas in a moderately large sample of individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls. We hypothesized that general cognition and memory domains (verbal learning and working memory) would be negatively associated with all CT exposures, even when controlling for PTSD, but physical neglect, rather than abuse (emotional, physical, or sexual) or emotional neglect, would exhibit specificity and account for most of the cognitive differences between cases and controls. Additionally, preliminary data visualization revealed several unexpected nonlinear relationships between CT and cognition. Although this has not been previously reported, others have shown nonlinear associations between CT and outcomes related to cognition, such as resilience to stress in schizophrenia (Wang et al., 2023) and inhibition deficits (Kira et al., 2023), as well as between cognition and psychosis symptom severity (Does et al., 1993; Kravariti et al., 2012). Therefore, exploratory analyses without a priori hypotheses were also conducted to assess nonlinearity among CT-cognition relationships in schizophrenia with the intention of informing future hypotheses and confirmatory studies.

Materials and methods

Participants

Data on 215 individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (henceforth referred to as schizophrenia) and 213 healthy controls were acquired from the Psychiatric Genotype/Phenotype Project (NCT00762866) repository at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (Table 1). Our patient sample included 82 individuals with schizophrenia, 101 with schizophreniform disorder, and 32 with schizoaffective disorder. Participants were between the ages of 16 and 60 and were excluded if they reported a significant traumatic brain injury, medical ailment, or history of substance dependence and if evidence for intellectual disability was present in their medical records. Parental consent was obtained for participants younger than 18-years-old. Controls that met criteria for a psychiatric illness, had a first-degree relative with a psychotic disorder, or received psychotropic treatment were not included. The Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-IV-TR or 5 (First, 2002; First et al., 2016) was administered to confirm psychosis diagnoses and the presence of comorbid PTSD in cases and rule out psychiatric diagnoses in controls. Premorbid intellect was estimated using the Wechsler Test of Adult Reading (Wechsler, 2001). Written informed consent was obtained for every subject, and all were monetarily compensated. The study was approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Review Board.

Cognitive assessment

Participants were administered the Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP; Purdon, 2005), a valid and reliable measure of cognition in psychiatric illnesses (Rojo et al., 2010). The SCIP consists of five subtests—Verbal Learning Test-Immediate (Immediate Recall), Working Memory Test (Working Memory), Verbal Fluency Test (Verbal Fluency), Verbal Learning Test-Delayed (Delayed Recall), and Processing Speed Test (Processing Speed). Subtest raw scores were converted to z-scores using normative data (Purdon, 2005) and averaged to create a composite z-score (SCIP Total). Following initial group comparisons, only SCIP Total and memory subdomains were included in our analyses.

Childhood trauma

The short form of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ; Bernstein et al., 2003), a 28-item self-report, was used to measure adversity subtypes—emotional and physical neglect (EN, PN), and emotional, physical, and sexual abuse (EA, PA, SA). The CTQ has excellent psychometric properties, including factor structure invariance, criterion-related validity with clinical ratings, convergent and discriminant validity in diverse samples (Bernstein et al., 2003), as well as a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.891 (Badenes-Ribera et al., 2024). Items were rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (Never True) to 5 (Very Often True). CT Total was calculated by combining all five subscales. Severity categories were determined using cut-offs outlined in the CTQ manual (Bernstein and Fink, 1998): None/Minimal (Total: 25–36, EN: 5–9, PN: 5–7, EA: 5–8, PA: 5–7, SA: 5), Low (Total: 37–51, EN: 10–14, PN: 8–9, EA: 9–12, PA: 8–9, SA: 6–7), Moderate (Total: 52–68, EN: 15–17, PN: 10–12, EA: 13–15, PA: 10–12, SA: 8–12), and Severe (Total: ≥69, EN: ≥18, PN: ≥13, EA: ≥16, PA: ≥13, SA: ≥13). A categorical variable separating participants reporting none/minimal levels of CT Total (CT Absent) from those reporting low, moderate, and severe levels (CT Present; Bernstein and Fink, 1998) was created for group comparisons. Otherwise, severity cut-offs were used for interpretation and CTQ variables were treated as continuous.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (v4.2.2; Team, 2020) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Model goodness of fit was measured by adjusted R-squared (Ra2), while the effect size of individual factors was determined by partial eta-squared (ηp2). The criterion for statistical significance was a fixed value of p < 0.05 and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR; Holm, 1979).

Group comparisons

Demographic differences were measured using Mann-Whitney U and chi-square (χ2) tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), controlling for age, sex, parental education, and PTSD, was used to compare cognitive performance and reported childhood trauma between cases and controls. ANCOVA was also used to test whether the effect of CT history on cognition differs between cases and controls (Table S1) and assess differences in cognitive performance between schizophrenia participants with and without a history of CT (Table S2).

Regression models

Regression models using adversity measures as predictors for SCIP performance, while controlling for age, sex, parental education, and PTSD, were developed to elucidate our correlation results (Fig. S1 and Table S3) in participants with schizophrenia. Upon data visualization, several potentially nonlinear CT-cognition associations were observed. Change-points, values marking a significant change in the relationship between the independent and dependent variable, were estimated for each trauma exposure to develop segmented (piecewise) regression models, which ensure continuity at these inflections and preserve statistical power of the entire sample (van den Hout et al., 2013). Preliminary identification of change-points involved visually inspecting cubic splines (Lee and Jhong, 2021; Shepherd and Rebeiro, 2017; Tomal and Rahman, 2021; Yang et al., 2023), followed by formal statistical validation using the segmented package in R (v2.1-3; Muggeo, 2003, 2008). Domain knowledge of CTQ severity cut-offs (Bernstein and Fink, 1998) was also incorporated to assess the plausibility and interpretability of each change-point (Yang et al., 2023). Nonlinearity in these segmented regressions was then verified by comparing model fit with linear regressions using the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc; Table S4). Multivariate linear regressions were used when evidence for nonlinearity was absent. Normality of all regression residuals was supported by Q-Q plots and non-significant Shapiro-Wilk tests (all p > 0.05), and homoscedasticity of all models was assessed through visual inspection of residuals and fitted values and confirmed via non-significant Breusch-Pagan tests (all p > 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses

A sensitivity analysis that controlled for the other CT subtypes, in addition to the covariates mentioned above, was conducted to assess specificity among statistically significant CT-cognition associations in schizophrenia. Since the CTQ subscales correlate significantly with one another (Bernstein et al., 2003), we tested for multicollinearity, which was considered present when variance inflation factor (VIF) values were greater than 4 (Hair Jr et al., 2010). We also performed additional sensitivity analyses to explore the influence of comorbid PTSD on CT-cognition relationships (Tables S5–S8).

Proportion of variance explained

ANCOVA was used to evaluate the proportion of variance in cognitive differences between cases and controls explained by CT. Models controlled for age, sex, parental education, and PTSD; only significant CT-cognition associations from our schizophrenia sample were included; and to evaluate specificity, models for CT subtypes controlled for all other trauma exposures. Lastly, a sensitivity analysis was performed in a subsample (n = 127 per group) matched 1:1 on age, sex, parental education, and CTQ scores (Table S9) using the MatchIt package in R (v4.7.1; Ho et al., 2011), with caliper width set to 0.2 (Austin, 2011).

Results

Group differences in cognition and childhood trauma

The severity of cognitive impairment and childhood trauma in schizophrenia was assessed through group differences. Compared to control participants, individuals with schizophrenia exhibited impairment in all cognitive domains (all p < 0.0001) and reported greater exposure to childhood trauma (all p ≤ 0.0003; Table 2). We also examined the effect of CT history on cognition and tested for differences between cases and controls. CT history had significant main effects on SCIP Total and delayed recall, but no interactions with diagnosis (Table S1). Within the schizophrenia cohort, we found greater impairment in working memory and delayed recall in individuals with a history of CT (Table S2).

Associations between childhood trauma and cognition in schizophrenia

We then treated CT variables as continuous to examine whether early life adversity is related to cognitive functioning in our schizophrenia cohort. SCIP Total was significantly associated with most trauma exposures (all β ≥ −0.016, p ≤ 0.018), while a trend was observed for sexual abuse (β = −0.029, p = 0.053; Table 3). When evaluating the relationship between early life adversity and specific memory functions, we found that immediate recall was significantly related to CT Total and both physical CTs (all β ≥ −0.023, p ≤ 0.018), while delayed recall was related to CT Total, both physical CTs, and emotional abuse (all β ≥ −0.017, p ≤ 0.008). CT Total, both emotional CTs, and physical neglect were significantly associated with working memory (all β ≥ −0.017, p ≤ 0.021).

Nonlinear associations between childhood trauma and cognition in schizophrenia

Nonlinearity was detected in several associations between childhood trauma and cognitive performance in participants with schizophrenia. CT Total, emotional neglect, and emotional abuse exhibited nonlinear relationships with total cognition (all β ≥ −0.016, p ≤ 0.018; Table 3). Immediate recall and delayed recall were nonlinearly associated with cumulative trauma (β = −0.023, p = 0.004) and emotional abuse (β = −0.293, p = 0.001), respectively, while emotional neglect showed a nonlinear relationship with working memory (β = −0.099, p = 0.018). Specificity in the associations between childhood trauma and cognition in schizophrenia

To assess the specificity of our associations in schizophrenia, we included the other trauma subtypes as covariates in our models and found that only the nonlinear relationship between emotional abuse and delayed recall remained significant (β = −0.299, p = 0.017; Table 4). All other associations failed to reach the threshold for statistical significance (all β ≤ −0.130, p ≥ 0.057) and evidence for multicollinearity was absent (all VIF ≤ 2.582).

Impact of comorbid PTSD in schizophrenia

Furthermore, we examined the role of comorbid PTSD in our sample, which was present in 24 participants with schizophrenia. Disproportionately more participants in the comorbid PTSD group reported the presence of CT (low-severe) than those without PTSD (χ2 = 10.91, p = 0.012; Table S5). This was recapitulated in our finding of more severe childhood trauma in schizophrenia participants with PTSD than those without PTSD; however, despite elevated rates of trauma, there were no significant differences in cognition between participants with and without PTSD (Table S6). Within the comorbid PTSD group, none of the CT-cognition relationships were statistically significant (Table S7), and when removing PTSD as a covariate in our full schizophrenia sample models, associations were similar to those shown in our main regression analysis (Table S8).

Proportion of variance in cognitive differences explained by childhood trauma

Finally, we analyzed the extent to which CT factors account for cognitive differences between cases and controls. On average, approximately 2 % of cognitive impairment in schizophrenia was related to cumulative CT (Table 5). The proportion of differences explained by the trauma subtypes was even smaller, although physical neglect (Ra2=0.018) and emotional abuse (Ra2=0.021) exhibited similar effect sizes for delayed recall. When cases and controls were matched 1:1 on demographics and exposure to CT (Table S9), we found that physical neglect explained more cognitive impairment than any other CT subtype (Table 6). That said, the average proportion of cognitive differences accounted for by physical neglect was only 0.5 %.

Discussion

In accordance with existing literature, we demonstrated that cognitive performance is impaired and reported childhood trauma is higher in individuals with schizophrenia compared to controls (McCutcheon et al., 2023; Mørkved et al., 2017; Rosenfield et al., 2022; Sheffield et al., 2018). The effect of CT on cognition did not differ between cases and controls, and schizophrenia participants with a history of CT exhibited greater cognitive impairments, but only in working memory and long-term verbal memory. These findings may reflect the loss of statistical power when reducing CT exposure to categories and indicate the need for studies to treat CT variables as continuous. When examining how CT and cognition are related in schizophrenia, we confirmed prior reports of negative CT-cognition associations (Dauvermann and Donohoe, 2019; Kilian et al., 2018; Mørkved et al., 2020; Shannon et al., 2009), but did not support our hypothesis for physical neglect specificity. Instead, we found that total trauma and four subtypes—emotional and physical neglect, and emotional and physical abuse—were associated with poor cognitive performance, with specificity between emotional abuse and long-term memory. We also showed that, across all controls in our sample, cumulative trauma accounted for the most variance in cognitive deficits.

Physical neglect and physical abuse were negatively related to all memory domains and verbal learning, respectively, in a simple linear fashion. These results corroborate existing work showing physical CT to be the most reliable association between early life adversity and cognition (Dauvermann and Donohoe, 2019) and indicate that the neural substrates of memory may be particularly sensitive to physical trauma. Conversely, we found novel and compelling evidence for nonlinear relationships with cognition for cumulative trauma and the emotional exposures. Since researchers have heretofore exclusively reported linear relationships between trauma and cognition in psychosis, these segmented effects were unexpected but may, in part, explain some of the inconsistencies in the literature that have been previously noted (Dauvermann and Donohoe, 2019).

Previous accounts show negative associations between total CT and cognitive function in schizophrenia (Vargas et al., 2019), although we are the first to detect nonlinear effects, specifically for general cognitive ability and immediate recall. These relationships may indicate a neuropsychological threshold where brain regions preferentially impacted by early life adversity, such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (Teicher et al., 2016), become overwhelmed by elevated exposure to abuse and neglect. This threshold effect is in congruence with the allostatic load model (McEwen and Stellar, 1993), which posits that the physiological impacts of stress compound, disrupt regulatory systems, and through repeated wear and tear on memory circuits, precipitate poor cognitive functioning (D’Amico et al., 2020; Juster et al., 2010; McEwen, 1998). In schizophrenia, the relationship between allostatic load—which is associated with childhood trauma (Piotrowski et al., 2020) and measured using cardiovascular, metabolic, inflammatory, and neuroendocrine markers—and cognitive deficits is significant and mediated by cortical thinning in the superior frontal gyrus (Zhou et al., 2021). A similar investigation in our sample may reveal appropriate allostatic functioning in individuals reporting minimal levels of total CT and dysregulated allostatic systems in those with greater CT exposure.

Working memory was significantly related to emotional neglect after a severity threshold at the upper limit of minimal exposure. While this finding is in discordance with prior reports of null and positive memory effects (Aas et al., 2012; Mørkved et al., 2020), it is consistent with others demonstrating negative associations with volumetric reductions in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in individuals with schizophrenia (Cancel et al., 2015). None have compared these structural differences among those reporting milder and greater amounts of emotional neglect but doing so may show commensurate nonlinear effects. Emotional abuse, in contrast, was negatively associated with delayed recall performance when a severe level was reported and, unlike the other relationships, remained significant even when controlling for co-occurring CT subtypes. Prior studies have failed to detect significant associations between emotional abuse and cognition in psychosis (Aas et al., 2012; Mørkved et al., 2020), thus we were surprised to find this robust relationship, which may support hippocampal and prefrontal plasticity in response to stress (Kolb et al., 2017; McEwen, 1999). This remains unclear, however, since the neurologic consequences of emotional abuse have not been established, even though other forms of abuse are related to altered functional connectivity of the amygdala and decreased grey matter in psychosis samples (Cancel et al., 2017; Sheffield et al., 2013). Future analyses ought to examine whether similar brain perturbations interact with these nonlinear CT-cognition associations. Also, given that we only investigated a single timepoint and cognition in psychosis changes throughout the lifespan (Harvey et al., 2022), subsequent work should include longitudinal data to determine whether segmented effects persist across time or are abolished.

Despite its influence on cognition in schizophrenia (Seow et al., 2016), comparable studies failed to control for PTSD comorbidity. Interestingly, CT-cognition associations were not significant in our limited comorbid PTSD group, which may be attributed to reduced statistical power, and when PTSD was removed from our covariate selection, relationships were commensurate with those in our main analysis, suggesting that comorbid PTSD does not have an outsized influence on associations between CT and cognition in schizophrenia. While PTSD may mask or confound these relationships, it is difficult to test this given our uneven groups, thus a large comorbid PTSD sample may be better equipped to address these issues. Moreover, no other study has directly quantified the degree to which childhood trauma accounts for differences in cognition between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls. The effect of cumulative CT on all cognitive domains, albeit small, supports evidence suggesting that early life adversity, independently of psychosis, perturbs hippocampal and prefrontal development through stress dysregulation, which in turn accentuates vulnerability for psychopathology (Murphy et al., 2022). Delayed recall deficits were specifically accounted for by physical neglect, which was also shown in our matched sample analysis and is in accord with existing evidence for specificity (Dauvermann and Donohoe, 2019; Kilian et al., 2018; Mørkved et al., 2020), and emotional abuse, which corroborates our segmented regression finding. Both effects may point to a unique sensitivity for the neurobiological substrates influencing long-term memory during childhood.

Cumulatively, our results (a) highlight the importance of controlling for co-occurring trauma influences and disaggregating CT measures to examine specificity, (b) reveal novel nonlinear relationships that may imply allostatic dysfunction and account for some of the equivocal findings in the literature, (c) indicate that a small proportion of the cognitive impairment in schizophrenia is related to early environmental factors that may be independent of, but increase risk for, psychosis, and (d) suggest the need for imaging and longitudinal analyses in a well-powered sample to clarify these variegated relationships. These findings also underscore the significance of supplementing psychopharmacologic interventions with cognitive remediation (Zoupa et al., 2022) and trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (Keen et al., 2017) to improve cognition in schizophrenia.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. For instance, CT was measured retrospectively using a self-report (CTQ) that relies on participants’ recall of events that occurred many years prior. This is especially concerning given the memory deficits observed in our clinical sample. Other methods of assessing CT, such as interviews (Janssen et al., 2004), show greater concordance with prospective measures of early life maltreatment (Baldwin et al., 2019). That said, retrospective reports have been demonstrated to be a reliable measure of CT in psychosis (Fisher et al., 2011). Additionally, the CTQ has robust psychometric characteristics (Bernstein et al., 2003) and enabled us to distinguish between specific adversity exposures instead of relying on broad or dimensional measures of CT. Another limitation is that cognitive assessment was restricted to a single instrument (SCIP), thus the study may lack the strength found in previous work that employed extensive test batteries (Mørkved et al., 2020). Though, the SCIP has been shown to adequately capture psychosis-related deficits (Rojo et al., 2010) and its briefness makes it an ideal substitute for more cognitively demanding, time-consuming workups.

Our analysis was also limited by not controlling for other variables known to impact cognition, such as socioeconomic status, duration of illness, or recent life stressors (e.g., hospitalizations) (de Winter et al., 2024; Hori et al., 2021; Wells et al., 2020). Covariate selection, however, did consist of parental education, which is also negatively associated with CT (Assari, 2020), and age, proxies for personal education without the influence of psychosis (Ventura et al., 2015) and illness duration, respectively. We also covaried for a comorbid diagnosis of PTSD to account for additional stress effects. Lastly, although lacking evidence for multicollinearity, our sensitivity models that controlled for co-occurring trauma subtypes, which few CT studies have performed (Ered and Ellman, 2019; Sheridan et al., 2017; Sloan et al., 2024), may be too complex, resulting in overfitting and suppression effects. Follow-up studies may explore dimensionality reduction and regularization techniques to help address these issues.

Conclusion

In sum, the relationship between early life trauma and cognitive functioning in schizophrenia is heterogeneous and may be best captured by cumulative measures of adversity. Our nonlinear findings indicate that the neurobiological substrates of memory may exhibit sensitivity to distinct levels of trauma exposure. Further analysis is required to clarify how CT contributes to brain perturbations associated with schizophrenia and interacts with the relationship between neuropathology and memory dysfunction over time.
