Sustained-release CGRP microspheres accelerate diabetic wound healing by synergistically promoting neurovascular regeneration through modulation of macrophage and endothelial cell functions
Authors: Ying Zhao, Lei Huang, Felix Sumampouw, Ziyue Ren, Xifeng An, Hengyu Du, Songtao Luo, Yuchen Zhang, Qiang Chang, Hongwei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic refractory wounds are a severe complication of diabetes, often synchronized with diabetic peripheral neuropathy. In this study, we demonstrated a significantly downregulated expression of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) in the skin tissues of both diabetic patients and diabetic mouse models. This observation implies the crucial role of CGRP in diabetic wound healing. Based on this discovery, we engineered glucose-responsive along with sustained-release antibacterial hydrogel microspheres (BA-HPCS@CGRP) for the controlled delivery of CGRP and conducted systematic evaluation of its therapeutic efficacy. In vitro findings demonstrated that microspheres not only directly enhanced the migration and tube formation capabilities of endothelial cells impaired by high glucose but also further facilitated the restoration of endothelial cell function by promoting the secretion of angiopoietin-like protein 4 (Angptl4) by macrophages after switching to M2 phenotype by CGRP. The results from diabetic mouse models showed that BA-HPCS@CGRP accelerated diabetic wound healing by modulating macrophage polarization towards to M2 phenotype and reduced inflammation, promoted neurovascular regeneration and restored the local CGRP expression. These findings suggest that sustained releasing of low concentration of CGRP provides novel therapeutic approaches for diabetic wounds via modulating macrophage. Moreover, BA-HPCS@CGRP achieves comprehensive sequential therapy through the synergistic modulation of the “neuro-immune-vascular” axis, which might open new perspective to chronic wounds and regenerative medicine.

Introduction

The global diabetes pandemic poses a crucial health challenge. As per the most recent data from the International Diabetes Federation, the global adult population with diabetes approximated 589 million in 2024 [1,2]. Among these patients, an estimated 15% - 25% develop diabetic non-healing wounds. Diabetic wounds are typified by persistent inflammation, defective angiogenesis, neurovascular dysfunction, and abnormal extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [3,4]. The hyperglycemic microenvironment instigates macrophage polarization imbalance (predominantly the M1 phenotype) [5], endothelial dysfunction [6], and impaired neural signaling [7], jointly establishing the “healing desert”. Therefore, targeting macrophage homeostasis, enhancing angiogenesis, and restoring neurovascular function are pivotal strategies for formulating effective therapeutic approaches in the management of diabetic wounds.

In recent years, diverse bioactive peptides and their intelligent delivery materials have demonstrated significant potential in both fundamental research and clinical translation, leveraging their unique capability for spatiotemporally precise modulation of the wound microenvironment [8,9]. This offers innovative solutions for skin wound healing, particularly for diabetic wounds. Research not only explores exogenous bioactive peptides derived from amphibians but also investigates the mechanisms and regulatory functions of endogenous mammalian peptides, including those from humans, within the wound healing cascade [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Notably, neuropeptides have received increasing attention due to their critical role in modulating inflammation, angiogenesis, and neural regeneration [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Upon injury, neuropeptides are released from nerves and then diffuse into the local tissues, after binding to functional receptors located on the surfaces of cutaneous cells, multiple cellular signaling cascades will be activated, which mediate their functions in both physiological and pathological scenarios [[18], [19], [20]]. Among them, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), a pleiotropic neuropeptide secreted from sensory nerve terminals, manifests multifunctional biological characteristics including vasodilation, promotion of angiogenesis, modulation of cutaneous antimicrobial adaptive immunity, and anti-inflammatory activities [[21], [22], [23]]. In cutaneous tissues, CGRP-positive nerve fibers densely innervate the dermal papillae, perivascular areas, and perifollicular regions [24]. This anatomical distribution strongly supports its regulatory function in wound healing. Genetic ablation of CGRP leads to impaired wound-induced angiogenesis and delayed healing [25], whereas topical administration of CGRP significantly enhances dermal and muscular regeneration in acute wounds [16]. These findings collectively position CGRP as a promising therapeutic candidate for diabetic wound treatment. However, despite its therapeutic potential, several critical translational barriers remain unresolved. CGRP suffers rapid proteolytic degradation within the wound microenvironment, and off-target effects resulting from nonspecific diffusion severely impede its therapeutic application and extensive usage. Moreover, the hyperglycemic microenvironment characteristic of diabetic wounds may further compromise peptide stability and bioactivity, thereby necessitating repeated administration and increasing the risk of systemic exposure. Although various CGRP delivery strategies have been explored [26,27], most primarily rely on direct application or hydrogel matrices to achieve local release, which provide limited protection against enzymatic degradation and lack responsiveness to the hyperglycemic microenvironment characteristic of diabetic wounds. Meanwhile, although such systems provide preliminary structural protection, drug release is predominantly governed by passive diffusion, which often lead to an initial burst release. Consequently, these systems fail to achieve microenvironment-adaptive drug release or sustained modulation of immune and vascular compartments. Therefore, there is an urgent need for an intelligent delivery platform capable of protecting CGRP from premature degradation, enhancing its local retention, enabling glucose-responsive controlled release, and simultaneously coordinating repair processes.

To address these challenges, a glucose-responsive composite hydrogel microspheres (BA-HPCS@CGRP) was fabricated using microfluidic technology. In contrast to other delivery systems that rely solely on diffusion-controlled release, our platform integrated structural protection, microenvironment responsiveness, and multi-regulation within a single construct. Specifically, these microspheres encapsulated CGRP within a composite matrix composed of GelMA and BA-HPCS. The GelMA-based microspheres form a three-dimensional (3D) network with tunable degradation behavior that protected CGRP from premature proteolytic degradation and ensured sustained release with the bulk degradation; simultaneously, their high surface-area-to-volume ratio and porous microarchitecture enhanced exudate management, nutrient diffusion, and cellular infiltration, thereby supporting uniform drug distribution within the wound microenvironment. Incorporation of hydroxypropyl chitosan (HPCS) provided intrinsic antimicrobial activity, an essential feature for infection-prone diabetic wounds. Upon phenylboronic acid grafting to form BA-HPCS, the material retained its antibacterial property while acquiring glucose-responsive behavior, allowing microenvironment-adaptive CGRP release under hyperglycemic conditions. By combining peptide protection with intelligent, glucose-triggered delivery, this platform was designed to address both the pharmacokinetic instability of CGRP and the complex neuro-immune-vascular dysregulation characteristic of diabetic wounds, thereby offering a multifunctional strategy for enhancing wound repair. Through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations, we demonstrated that BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres exhibited glucose-triggered release behavior and significantly enhanced endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation. Mechanistically, the system promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and stimulated the secretion of Angptl4, thereby synergistically enhancing endothelial cell function. Consequently, BA-HPCS@CGRP accelerated diabetic wound healing primarily by modulating immune responses and enhancing angiogenesis, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for diabetic wound treatment. Synthesis of Gel-MA

GelMA was synthesized through gelatin methacrylation following the established methods reported previously [28]. The appropriate amount of gelatin was added to the PBS solution. Stir the solution at a constant temperature of 50 °C until the gelatin was completely dissolved, thereby obtaining the 100 mg/mL solution. Subsequently, a suitable quantity of methacrylic anhydride was slowly introduced into the solution. Then, the mixture was continuously stirred at 50 °C overnight. On the following day, 5 times the volume of the PBS solution was added to halt the reaction. The resulting mixture was then transferred into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cut-off of 14 kDa and immersed in deionized water for dialysis over a period of 7 days, where the deionized water was changed 8 - 10 times daily to remove unreacted reagents and by-products. Finally, the ultimate product was lyophilized to obtain Gel-MA.

Synthesis of BA-HPCS

BA-HPCS was synthesized following a similar procedure described previously [29]. HPCS (0.5 g) was dissolved in 15 mL deionized water under magnetic stirring at room temperature (25 °C), assisted by brief sonication, until a homogeneous solution was obtained. Separately, BA (0.50 g, 3.01 mmol) and NHS (0.585 g, 5.1 mmol) were dissolved in 15 mL methanol and stirred at 25 °C in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, 70% of the total EDC·HCL (0.683 g, 3.57 mmol) was added and stirred for 15 min to generate the activated NHS ester. The activated solution was then slowly added dropwise to the HPCS solution over 20 min, and the reaction mixture was stirred at 25 °C in the dark for 36 h. After 6 h, the remaining 30% of EDC·HCL (0.292 g, 1.53 mmol) was added to improve the coupling efficiency. Upon completion of the reaction, the mixture was transferred into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 12 kDa and dialyzed against deionized water for 5 days, with the water replaced every 6-8 h. Finally, the solution was freeze-dried to obtain the slightly yellowish white foam. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis was used to confirm the successful synthesis of the BA-HPCS.

Preparation of BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres

Monodisperse BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres featuring uniform particle size were fabricated via microfluidic technology (Microfluidic syringe pump: Lead Fluid, Baoding, China; Model: TYD01-01. PDMS microfluidic chip: Zhongxin Qiheng, Nantong, China; Model: ZX-LD-100). Briefly, BA-HPCS (1 wt%) and GelMA (10 wt%) were dissolved in 2.5 mL of deionized water under magnetic stirring with brief sonication to obtain a homogeneous solution. The photoinitiator LAP (0.1 wt%) was subsequently added and gently mixed until fully dispersed. CGRP stock solution (500 nM) was then introduced to adjust the final concentration to 5 μM. The resulting aqueous phase was loaded into a 2.5 mL syringe and connected to a microfluidic device. The oil phase, consisting of paraffin oil containing 3 wt% Span 80, was loaded into a 20 mL syringe. The flow rate ratio of oil phase to aqueous phase was maintained at 5:1. As the droplets exited the microchannel, they were exposed to UV irradiation (365 nm, 30 s) to induce GelMA crosslinking, forming solidified hydrogel microspheres. The microspheres were collected, the supernatant oil discarded, and the microspheres were washed 3 times with acetone to remove residual oil, followed by 3 washes with deionized water to remove residual acetone and unreacted components. HPCS microspheres (1 wt% HPCS, 10 wt% GelMA) and BA-HPCS (1 wt% BA-HPCS, 10 wt% GelMA) microspheres were also fabricated using the same method.

In vitro release of CGRP and BA-HPCS

To characterize the glucose-responsive CGRP release behavior of BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres, we conducted in vitro release experiments under glucose-defined conditions following previously reported protocols for glucose-responsive delivery systems [30,31]. Specifically, glucose-free PBS was used to establish a baseline release condition, whereas 100 mg/dL glucose and 400 mg/dL glucose were selected to represent normoglycemic and hyperglycemic/diabetic levels, respectively. Briefly, the specific quantity of freeze-dried microspheres was separately placed in PBS, 100 mg/dL and 400 mg/dL glucose solutions. At the pre-determined time points, the supernatant was aspirated and served as the test solution. The test solution was centrifuged (4000 rpm, 15 min) and then added to ELISA kit (Mianmei Industrial Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China, cat# MM-71082R2) for sequential reaction and color development. The absorbance of each well was measured sequentially at the wavelength of 450 nm, and the actual concentration of the sample was calculated accordingly.

To detect the release of BA-HPCS from BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) labeled BA-HPCS was used to prepare BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres, the specific quantity of freeze-dried microspheres was separately placed in PBS, 100 mg/dL and 400 mg/dL glucose solutions, and the fluorescence intensity of the release supernatant was directly measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek Instruments, Inc., USA); the release concentration was calculated based on a standard curve generated from the same batch of labeled polymer.

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and fibroblast L929 cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). L929 cells were cultured in a high-sugar DMEM containing penicillin/streptomycin (1%) and FBS (10%) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2. Endothelial cells (HUVEC) were cultured in complete endothelial cell medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA, cat# 1001).

Extraction of primary macrophages from mice

After the mice were euthanized, they were immersed in 75% ethanol for 5 min for sterilization. All subsequent procedures were performed in a biosafety cabinet. Bilateral femurs and tibias were harvested, surrounding muscles and connective tissues were removed, and the bones were placed in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium until use. Bone marrow cells were flushed from the medullary cavity using a 1 mL syringe prefilled with medium by inserting the needle into the bone cavity and forcefully expelling the medium. The resulting cell suspension was collected into centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 10 min. After removing the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended, and 1 mL sterile water was added to lyse red blood cells by rapidly pipetting up and down 10 times. Serum-free RPMI 1640 medium was then added to terminate lysis. The suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer into a new centrifuge tube and centrifuged again at 1800 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and cells were resuspended in complete medium, seeded into 6-well plates, and supplemented with GM-CSF (40 ng/mL) for in vitro induction. A half-medium change was performed on day 2, and cells were cultured for 5-7 days prior to subsequent experiments.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

To assess the optimal concentration of CGRP within the microspheres, CGRP solutions with well-defined concentration gradients were formulated, spanning 1 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM, and 1 μM. HUVECs and L929 cells were independently seeded into 96-well microplates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, 1 mL CGRP solution of different concentrations were added, and the cells were incubated for 48 h. The cell culture medium was then discarded, and the cells were carefully washed 3 times with PBS. Thereafter, 10 μL of CCK-8 (Dojindo, Japan, cat# CK04) solution and 90 μL of DMEM were added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h in a dark environment, the cell viability was evaluated via the optical density (OD) at 450 nm, and the data were recorded simultaneously. The cell viability was calculated using the following formula:Cellviability%=ODTG−ODBlankODCG−ODBlank×100%where ODTG, ODCG, and ODBlank represent the absorbance values of the treatment group (TG, which consists of the culture medium, CCK-8 solution, and cells treated with different concentrations of CGRP solution), the control group (CG, comprising normal cells, culture medium, and CCK-8 solution), and the blank group (Blank, containing the culture medium and CCK-8 solution but excluding cells), respectively.

Cytocompatibility evaluation

To evaluate the biocompatibility and proliferation rate of endothelial cells and macrophagocytes, we employed green fluorescence cell membrane tracer Dio and live/dead staining reagents to stain the cells co-cultured with the microspheres. A co-culture system of cells and microspheres was established using transwell chambers (pore size: 0.4 μm), with the microspheres placed in the upper chamber and the cells in the lower chamber. For endothelial cells, fluorescence labeling was performed following the instructions of the Dio cell membrane fluorescent dye. Subsequently, fluorescent 5 × 103 cells were seeded into each well of a 24-well plate, and the co-culture was maintained in the dark for 6 Days. During this period, the culture medium was refreshed every 2 days, then observations and imaging were carried out using a Nikon inverted fluorescence microscope. Simultaneously, the Live/Dead Cell Double Stain Kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, cat# CA1630) was employed for staining. Specifically, endothelial cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and co-cultured with microspheres for 48 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed 2-3 times with PBS. Calcein-AM, propidium iodide (PI), and the buffer solution were mixed at a ratio of 1:3:1000 to prepare the working solution as the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, 250 μL of working solution was added to each well of the 24-well plate, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Following the incubation, fluorescence images were captured using a Nikon inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan, Modle: Eclipse Ti2-E). The staining procedure for macrophagocytes was analogous to that for endothelial cells.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial test adheres to the procedure previously employed [32]. Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis) were separately cultured in the corresponding peptone culture media and incubated overnight in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 °C. After centrifugation and washing, bacterial suspensions were resuspended in sterile PBS and adjusted to an initial concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Equal volumes of bacterial suspension were then co-incubated with hydrogel microspheres from each experimental group for 24 h. The BA-HPCS content in both BA-HPCS and BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres was 1% (w/v), corresponding to a precursor solution concentration of 10 mg/mL, and all microspheres used in the assay were prepared from this concentration. Following incubation, bacterial suspensions were collected and thoroughly resuspended. A 200-μL aliquot from each sample was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured to evaluate overall bacterial growth.Bacterialviability(%)=OD−sampleOD−control×100%where OD-control refers to the bacterial control group treated with CGRP solution only, which has no inherent antibacterial activity.

To assess cultivable bacteria, 10 μL of the diluted suspension was spread onto Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Finally, images of the bacterial growth on each agar culture plate were captured.

Cell scratching assay

Three parallel lines were delineated at the base of a 6-well plate using a marker pen. The plate was seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well to enable co-culture of the cells and microspheres for 4 to 5 days. Once the cells reached full confluence, the tip of a 200 μL yellow pipette was used to create a scratch perpendicular to the parallel lines at the center of each well. The well plate was then washed with PBS, and microscopic images were acquired at 0 h. Subsequently, the culture was continued using a medium containing 2% low serum and microspheres. Photographs were captured and documented at 36 h and 72 h post-scratch. Prior to photo acquisition, live cell staining could be carried out using Calcein-AM. Following the 30 - min incubation in the dark, images were collected with the ZEISS inverted fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany; Model: Axio Observer 7).

Tube formation assay

In 24-well plates, 5000 cells were placed in each well. After co-culturing the microspheres with the cells for 4 days, the cells in each group were digested, centrifuged and resuspended. Then, the cells were placed in 24-well plates that had been pre-treated with matrix gel at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells in each well. After 6 h, the tube formation was observed and photographed under a microscope.

Immunocytochemistry

Macrophages were seeded onto 24-well plate cell slides at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. Following cell adhesion, the cells were grouped and subjected to corresponding treatments. After 72 h of co-culture with microspheres, the culture medium was carefully aspirated, and the cells were rinsed with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were fixed at room temperature with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min. Afterward, the paraformaldehyde was removed, and the cells were washed multiple times with PBS. Next, the cell membrane was permeabilized with 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 diluted in PBS for 15 min. The cells were then washed 3 times with PBS and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. The primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C (CD206, 1:200; CD86, 1:200; Arg-1, 1:200). The primary antibodies were retrieved, and the cell slides were washed three times with PBS containing Tween 20 (PBST). The fluorescent secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor® 488, 1:400; Alexa Fluor® 594, 1:400) was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the cell slides were washed 3 times with PBST. The cell slides were then carefully removed and inverted onto a glass slide pre-dropped with the antifading mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Solarbio science & technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, cat# S2110). Fluorescence images were captured using a Nikon inverted microscope. The number of positive cells for the target proteins within the cells was counted using ImageJ software, and the proportion was calculated accordingly.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Macrophagocyte cells were categorized and processed sequentially following the protocols of RNA extraction, RNA quality assessment, mRNA enrichment, library construction, quality control, and machine sequencing. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by GenePlus Information Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). For the significance analysis of differences, the DESeq2 software was employed in this project. The thresholds for significant DEGs were set as padj (adjusted P value) < 0.05 and log2 absolute fold change (FC) values ≥ 2 (|log2FC| ≥2). The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were carried out using the GenePlus platform.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentration of Angptl4 was determined via the ELISA method (CUSABIO Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China, cat# CSB-EL001712MO). In strict accordance with the kit instructions, a series of steps were meticulously carried out, encompassing sample addition, incubation at room temperature, washing procedures, and color development. Specifically, within 5 min subsequent to the termination of the color development reaction, the OD value of each well was systematically measured at a wavelength of 450 nm by means of a microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Germany; Model: CLARIOstar).

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)

A transient knockdown was performed by transfecting siRNAs against Angptl4 (Anzhen Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) of in primary macrophages. Transfection was carried out with lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. The knockdown efficiency was assessed by quantifying Angptl4 mRNA expression levels via qRT-PCR.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity assay

The LDH activity assay was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, cat# C0016). Specifically, endothelial cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and underwent group treatments. Post-treatment, the cell culture plate was centrifuged (400 g, 5 min). Subsequently, 120 μL of the supernatant from each well was transferred to a new 96-well plate. Then, 60 μL of the pre-prepared LDH detection working solution was added to each well. After gentle agitation, the plate was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Finally, the absorbance of each well was measured at a wavelength of 490 nm.

Nitric oxide (NO) measurement

The concentration of NO in the endothelial cell supernatant was measured using a nitric oxide assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, cat# S0021S), strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the supernatant from grouped and treated endothelial cells was used as the test sample. A standard curve was prepared by diluting NaNO2 standard in complete culture medium to concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 μM. Then, 50 μL of each diluted standard or test sample was added into individual well of the 96-well plate. Subsequently, Griess reagent І and Griess reagent Ⅱ (both equilibrated to room temperature) were added to each well in sequence. After thorough mixing, the absorbance was measured at 540 nm. The NO concentration in the samples was finally calculated based on the standard curve.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from macrophages. Subsequently, the protein concentration was quantitatively determined using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit to complete the preparatory work for subsequent experiments. The proteins were separated via Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto an intact polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA, cat# IPVH00010 and cat# ISEQ00010). The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk and incubated with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10000) for 1 h at room temperature. Thereafter, all protein bands were washed 3 times and visualized by electrochemiluminescence using the enhanced chemiluminescence reagent solution. β-actin was employed as the housekeeping protein for target proteins. (CD206, 1:1000; CD86, 1:1000; Arg-1, 1:1000; iNOS, 1:1000; TNF-α, 1:1000; β-actin, 1:1000).

Clinical sample collection

Skin tissue samples of limbs were procured from 6 healthy adults and 6 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus who had the disease duration of 10-20 years without underlying cardiovascular diseases at department of burn and plastic surgery of Jinan University Affiliated Shunde Hospital (Foshan, China). The collection of clinical samples was carried out after obtaining the informed consent from all participants. Moreover, the experimental procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shunde Hospital Affiliated to Jinan University (Document No. JDSY-LL-2023089). All samples were fixed using 4% PFA and subsequently embedded into sections for immunohistochemical (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining.

Animal experiments

All animal studies were approved by Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Jinan University (Approval No. IACUC-20240910-04). Male C57BL/6 mice, at 5 weeks of age and weighing 19-20 g, were sourced from the Animal Experiment Center of Jinan University. The animal experiments were carried out at the Experimental Animal Center of Jinan University. These mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility maintained at 22 °C, with the 12-h light cycle per day, and were provided with ad libitum access to food and water. After a 3-day environmental acclimation period, the mice were continuously fed the high-fat feed for 4-5 weeks. Subsequently, following a 12-h fasting period, streptozotocin (STZ) was intraperitoneally injected at the dosage of 50 mg/kg for 5 consecutive days. 72 h later, the random blood glucose level of the mice was measured and continuously monitored for 2 weeks. A successful model was indicated when the random blood glucose of the mice remained consistently above 16.7 mmol/L. The mice with successful modeling were randomly allocated into 4 groups: Control group, BA-HPCS group, BA–HPCS@CGRP group, and Collagen group (positive control). Additionally, the mice in the Normal control (NC) group were C57BL/6 mice purchased during the same period and fed with normal diet. The mice were depilated, and 8-mm full-thickness total skin lesion wounds were created on their dorsal surfaces. For treatment, each wound received a single topical administration of approximately 65 mg of wet BA-HPCS or BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres, which were directly applied to completely cover the wound surface. In the Control and NC groups, wounds were treated with an equal volume of sterile 0.9% saline. In the Collagen group, wounds were covered with commercially available collagen dressing according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Wound healing progression was documented photographically on days 3, 7, 10, 14, and 18 after wound establishment. At each designated time point, wound tissues were harvested for subsequent experimental investigations.

The 10-week-old db/db mice (Leprdb/db) and their littermates (Lepr+/+) were procured from Shulaibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Subsequently, full-thickness skin samples were harvested from the dorsal regions of the mice. These samples were then fixed with 4% PFA, embedded, sectioned, and subjected to IHC and IF staining procedures.

Histological staining

The skin specimens were fixed with 4% PFA, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning at 4 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining were separately performed using the H&E staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, cat# G1120) and Masson’s trichrome stain kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, cat# G1340) as reagent instruction manual. Those pathological staining were scanned using the digital pathology imaging system (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH, Hungary). To label CGRP in the skin tissues, antibodies against human CGRP was used for IHC staining, then the IHC sections were scanned using the slide scanning imaging system (SQS-120P, TEKSQRAY, China). For immunofluorescence staining, the dewaxed sections underwent antigen repair using the modified citrate antigen repair solution and ready-to-use goat serum for 1 h. Subsequently, the primary antibody (1:200) was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The secondary antibodies consisted of Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse antibody (1:400) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (1:400). Finally, the sections were mounted with an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium containing DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using the Nikon or Zeiss inverted fluorescence microscope.

Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) multiple immunofluorescence staining

The dewaxed sections underwent antigen retrieval and were subsequently stained following the instructions of the TSA staining kit (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China, cat# G1259). Specifically, after antigen retrieval, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Then, they were blocked with ready-to-use goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the first primary antibody (1:1000). On the following day, the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200) was added and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. After washing 3 times with PBS solution, the IF 488-Tyramide working solution (1:500) was added, and the incubation was carried out in the dark for 10 min. Thereafter, the tissue sections were transferred to a box containing antigen retrieval solution and subjected to microwave heating for 15 min to remove the bound primary and secondary antibodies. Then the sections were blocked again with serum for 30 min. The second primary antibody (1:1000) was added to the sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200) was added and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. After washing the sections with PBS, the IF 594-Tyramide working solution (1:500) was added to the group, and the incubation was performed in the dark for 10 min. Finally, the slides were mounted with the anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium containing DAPI. The sections were stored at 4 °C in the dark, and images were observed and captured using the Nikon inverted microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate at least. The data from each experiment were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 Project software. For the comparison between two groups, two-tailed unpaired t-test was employed. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. The value of p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. The results were presented graphically with the aid of GraphPad Prism 10 and Origin 2024.

The expression level of CGRP in the skin of diabetic

The nervous system is a critical factor in tissue repair and regeneration. Studies have shown that peripheral nerves play a significant role in wound tissue regeneration, with CGRP derived from nociceptors serving as a key mediator [16]. While diabetic chronic wounds represent a persistent challenge in clinical research, the involvement of CGRP in diabetic wound pathogenesis remains largely unexplored. Early work by Martin et al. reported decreased CGRP expression in skin biopsies from type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) patients [33]. However, the expression profile of CGRP in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients has not been systematically investigated. To further elucidate CGRP expression in T2DM, we collected skin samples from patients diagnosed with T2DM for at least 10 years (DM, n = 6, aged 50-60 years, Patients exhibited inadequate glycemic control, Hemoglobin A1c > 6.5%) without underlying cardiovascular diseases or smoking history, and no evidence of spinal nerve root compression, along with age-matched healthy normal control (NC, n = 6, no family history of diabetes, no smoking history). First, IHC analysis revealed predominant perivascular deposition of CGRP, moreover, significantly reduced CGRP expression was observed in the skin of DM compared to NC (Fig. 2A and B). To further investigate the association with vascular system, IF staining demonstrated a marked decrease in cutaneous vascular density in DM compared to NC. Furthermore, abundant CGRP distribution surrounding vasculature, with quantification showing approximately 5.54-fold higher expression in NC versus DM (Fig. 2C and D). Simultaneously, we conducted the examination of skin samples obtained from db/db mice (Leprdb/db, a well - recognized murine model of type 2 diabetes) and their littermate control mice (Lepr+/+). Through comparative analysis, we found that, in comparison to Lepr+/+ mice, Leprdb/db mice exhibited significant decreases in both CGRP expression (1.61 - fold reduction, p < 0.01) and vascular density (Fig. 2E and F). Our findings demonstrated significantly reduced expression of CGRP in skin samples from both clinical diabetic patients and db/db mice, with a substantially greater reduction observed in clinical samples. This discrepancy is likely attributed to the convergence of three primary factors. Firstly, prolonged chronic hyperglycemia, coupled with complex comorbidities in diabetic patients, induces more severe cumulative neural damage. Secondly, the higher baseline CGRP levels in human skin - attributable to greater nerve innervation density than murine skin - amplifies the absolute magnitude of downregulation under hyperglycemic conditions. Finally, although db/db mice represent the excellent diabetic model, their relatively shorter disease duration, absence of human-specific comorbidities, and inherent species differences in neural regenerative capacity collectively yield neuropathy (as evidenced by CGRP reduction) that is generally less severe than that in long-term diabetic patients. Synthesis and characterization of BA-HPCS@CGRP

Building upon our aforementioned discoveries of a notable reduction of CGRP expression in diabetic skin compared to the normal specimen, we hypothesized the potential of exogenous additive of CGRP for diabetic wound repair. Given the distinctive pharmacodynamics of CGRP, where low doses are therapeutically effective while high doses demonstrate the “truncation effect” [[34], [35], [36]], and to surmount the challenges of rapid degradation, systemic dissemination, and potential off-target effects in vivo [37], we fabricated a glucose-responsive hydrogel microspheres for the sustained release of CGRP utilizing microfluidic technology. The borate ester bond, which is formed through the reversible combination of the phenylboronic acid group and glucose, endows the system with the ability to respond to glucose [38]. This not only exerts a local hypoglycemic effect without generating irritating by-products but also leads to the dissociation of the hydrogel and promotes drug release from the hydrogel matrix [39]. Considering that the high-glucose microenvironment of diabetic wounds is susceptible to causing infections, building upon our prior research regarding antibacterial hydrogels, HPCS was employed as the antibacterial matrix in this study. Initially, the phenylboric acid group was grafted onto HPCS to synthesize glucose-responsive BA-HPCS. The FTIR spectra revealed a diminished absorption peak at 1560 cm−1, corresponding to the N-H bending vibration of HPCS, indicating partial substitution of the amino groups. Characteristic absorption bands at 770 cm−1 (aromatic C-H out-of-plane bending) and 1550 cm−1 (benzene ring skeletal vibration) were observed, confirming the successful introduction of phenylboronic acid moieties onto HPCS polymer backbone (Fig. 3A). To determine the optimal initial feeding concentration of CGRP, we carefully considered two key factors during experimental design: the potential drug loss during microfluidic fabrication and subsequent washing processes, as well as the possible “truncation effect” caused by excessively high CGRP concentrations. Accordingly, BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres were prepared using initial CGRP concentrations of 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 μM, and their encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and loading capacity were systematically evaluated. The results showed that when the initial CGRP concentrations were 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 μM, the corresponding encapsulation efficiencies were 53.18 ± 3.89%, 74.56 ± 4.31%, 88.51 ± 2.09%, 77.12 ± 4.96%, and 72.29 ± 2.94%, respectively. Notably, the encapsulation efficiency exhibited a concentration-dependent trend, initially increasing and then decreasing, with a maximum value observed at 5 μM. In contrast, the loading capacity (μg/g, based on dry weight) increased progressively with rising initial concentration, reaching 19.6 ± 1.35, 82.51 ± 2.47, 172.49 ± 1.88, 205.53 ± 5.12, and 246.42 ± 4.79 μg/g, respectively. Although higher initial concentrations resulted in greater absolute drug loading, the encapsulation efficiency declined at elevated concentrations, indicating reduced utilization efficiency of the fed CGRP (Fig. S1A, Supplementary data). To enable direct visual observation of gel formation across different material compositions, we prepared 3 types of hydrogels: HPCS (1 wt% HPCS + 10 wt% Gel-MA), BA-HPCS (1 wt% BA-HPCS + 10 wt% Gel-MA), BA-HPCS@CGRP (1 wt% BA-HPCS + 10 wt% Gel-MA + 5 μM CGRP). The hydrogels were successfully prepared under UV light irradiation and appeared milky white after gelation (Fig. 3B and C). Subsequently, mineral oil containing 3% Span 80 as the oil phase and the liquid hydrogel solutions as the aqueous phase were injected into microfluidic device, respectively, the flow rates in two channels in the microfluidic chip were adjusted separately according to the shear force to prepare uniformly sized photo-crosslinkable hydrogel microspheres (Video 1–2, Supplementary data). Optical microscopy revealed that the BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres had the average diameter of 101.59 ± 0.33 μm (Fig. 3D and E). SEM imaging further demonstrated their loosely porous microstructure with the average pore diameter of 4.12 μm (Fig. 3F–H). To evaluate the glucose-responsive CGRP release profile, we quantified CGRP releasing in PBS and glucose solutions using the ELISA kit. The microspheres exhibited CGRP release in 400 mg/dL glucose solution is clearly higher than in 100 mg/dL glucose solution and PBS, confirming their glucose-triggered release capability while maintaining sustained CGRP release for up to half a month without initial burst effects (Fig. 3I). BA-HPCS also exhibited gentle responsive release under different glucose condition (Fig. S1B, Supplementary data). Meanwhile, through CCK-8 assays, we found that CGRP concentrations within 1 μM had no negative effect on cell viability (Fig. S1C, Supplementary data). Considering the encapsulation efficiency, sustained release profile, and fabrication-associated loss, the final concentration of CGRP in the microspheres was determined to be 5 μM. This ensures that the concentration of CGRP released from the microspheres during co-culture does not exceed 1 μM. Subsequently, to assess the biocompatibility of hydrogel microspheres, we co-cultured the microspheres with L929 cells in transwell chambers. Live/dead staining revealed that following 4 days of continuous co-culture, there was no statistically significant difference in cell viability between each microsphere group and the control group (Fig. 3J and K). Conventionally, CGRP has been acknowledged for its functions in vasodilation and pain modulation [37]. Nevertheless, recent accumulating evidence indicates that CGRP might facilitate wound healing through macrophages at the very beginning after injury [16]. Wound repair is a sophisticated physiological process encompassing inflammatory regulation, cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling. Among these phases, angiogenesis represents a crucial step, as it provides oxygen and nutrients for tissue regeneration. In recent years, the role of immune cells, especially macrophages, in angiogenesis has drawn substantial attention. However, the question of whether and how CGRP regulates the function of vascular endothelial cells via macrophages remains largely elusive. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the BA-HPCS@CGRP-treated group and the control group. To verify our hypothesis, we screened the DEGs and detected upregulated genes of Igf1, Angptl4, and Tnfsf14 (Fig. 5F). Subsequently, RT-PCR analysis confirmed that the mRNA level of Angptl4 in the BA-HPCS@CGRP group was 2.83-fold higher than that in the Control group, while the expressions of Igf1 and Tnfsf14 showed no statistical significance compared to the Control (Fig. 5G). This protein is involved in a diverse array of biological functions, encompassing the regulation of lipid metabolism, wound healing, the expansion of hematopoietic stem cells, and angiogenesis [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. Through the use of endothelial-specific Angptl4-knockout mice, Balkrishna et al. have demonstrated that Angptl4 is indispensable for maintaining the metabolic function of endothelial cells and plays a pivotal role in vascular permeability and angiogenesis [45]. Consequently, we conducted additional ELISA assays on the supernatant of macrophage cultures treated with microspheres. The findings demonstrated trace amounts of Angptl4 in both the BA-HPCS group and the control group, with no statistically significant disparity between them. Nevertheless, the Angptl4 level detected in the supernatant of the BA-HPCS@CGRP group was 6.23 times higher than that of the control group (Fig. 5H). This suggested that the BA-HPCS@CGRP facilitated the secretion of Angptl4 by macrophages, which was mediated by the sustained release of CGRP from the microspheres. Subsequently, to determine whether macrophage-derived Angptl4 impacts endothelial cell function, we utilized transient siRNA transfection to knock down Angptl4 expression in macrophages and then co-cultured these macrophages with endothelial cells. Following treatments of different groups, alterations in endothelial cell function were further investigated. The tube formation assay results showed that under high-glucose-induced injury, treatment with both macrophages and BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres improved the tube-forming capacity. However, when macrophages transfected with siAngptl4 were co-cultured with high glucose-injured endothelial cells, a significant inhibition of tube formation was observed (Fig. 5I). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is a cytoplasmic enzyme, and an increase in its release indicates cell membrane damage. Nitric oxide (NO), synthesized and released by endothelial cells, is a key vasodilator critical for maintaining normal vascular function. By measuring the levels of LDH and NO in the supernatant of endothelial cells, we found that even with BA-HPCS@CGRP treatment, the functional recovery of endothelial cells was significantly impaired when Angptl4 was knocked down in macrophages (Fig. 5J and K). BA-HPCS@CGRP significantly induce M2 macrophage polarization while effectively attenuating inflammatory cascade

Recent investigations have revealed that dysregulated macrophage responses are closely associated with impaired diabetic wound healing [46]. Specifically, under diabetic conditions, a notable increase in the number of monocytes differentiating into M1-type macrophages occurs within the circulation [47]. Consequently, macrophages are regarded as a highly promising therapeutic target for enhancing wound healing. As pivotal regulators of wound repair, macrophages polarized towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype can significantly mitigate the issue of delayed wound healing in diabetic patients [48]. Meanwhile, studies have found that compared to normal diabetic mice, CGRP-knockout diabetic mice exhibited significantly reduced macrophage infiltration in wounds, leading to decreased M2 polarization and impaired wound healing [49]. This suggests that CGRP may possess the ability to recruit macrophages. Therefore, to further investigate the effects of CGRP-sustained-release BA-HPCA@CGRP microspheres on macrophages during the inflammatory phase, we established the in vitro model using LPS-induced macrophages. Because macrophage subtype markers are key molecules for distinguishing their functional states, at present study, M1-type macrophages are characterized by the markers cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), while anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages are recognized by cluster of differentiation 206 (CD206) and arginase-1 (Arg-1). Thus, to elucidate the impact of BA-HPCS@CGRP on macrophage polarization, we systematically characterized the expression signatures of macrophage subtypes. Initially, macrophages were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS to induce M1 polarization, and with 20 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4) and 10 ng/mL interleukin-13 (IL-13) to drive polarization towards to the M2 phenotype. By means of immunofluorescence staining, the results showed that, compared to the LPS group, the LPS + BA-HPCS@CGRP group exhibited upregulated expression of CD206 and Arg-1 in macrophages, indicating that BA-HPCS@CGRP intervention promoted the transition of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype (Fig. 6A and B). Additionally, western blot analysis revealed that BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres reversed LPS-induced upregulation of M1 markers (CD86 and iNOS) while enhancing M2 markers (CD206 and Arg-1), confirming its role in shifting macrophages from M1 to M2 polarization (Fig. 6C and D). Furthermore, the excessive release of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) can also impede wound healing. By conducting RT-PCR analysis to measure the mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors, we have shown that BA-HPCS@CGRP effectively regulated the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory mediators under inflammatory circumstances (Fig. 6E). BA-HPCS@CGRP facilitate the healing of diabetic wounds

The wound healing efficacy of BA-HPCS@CGRP was evaluated in a diabetic skin wound model. The diabetic model was generated by 4-5 week high-fat diet feeding followed by multiple low-dose STZ injections (50 mg/kg, i.p.), as outlined in Fig. 7A. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the expression levels of CGRP and CD31 in the skin of diabetic mice were significantly lower than those in normal mice, a trend consistent with our observations in clinical diabetic patient skin samples and db/db mice (Fig. 7B). Subsequently, we used wild-type C57 mice as the normal control (NC) group, while successfully diabetic mice were randomly divided into 4 groups: Control group (0.9% saline, negative control), BA-HPCS group (treated with BA-HPCS microspheres), BA-HPCS@CGRP group (treated with BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres), and Collagen sponge group (treated with a commercially available wound dressing, positive control). The representative photographs of wound closure and schematic representation are shown in Fig. 7D and E. The results demonstrated that compared to the Control group, both the BA-HPCS and Collagen sponge groups moderately improved wound healing. However, among all groups, the BA-HPCS@CGRP group exhibited the best effect in promoting wound healing (Fig. 7C). For example, on day 7, the wound closure rate for BA-HPCS@CGRP group was 32.06 ± 4.32%, while for the Control, BA-HPCS, and Collagen sponge group were 22.76 ± 5.96%, 28.78 ± 6.17%, and 27.57 ± 5.04%, respectively. After treatment for 18 days, the wound healing rate of the BA-HPCS@CGRP group was up to 99.74 ± 1.58%, which was superior to that of the Control group (79.84 ± 3.14%), BA-HPCS group (90.71 ± 3.85%), and Collagen Sponge group (90.35 ± 2.79%). Skin samples were collected on days 3, 7, 14, 18, and 28 and subsequently underwent histological analysis. As depicted in Supplementary data Fig. S4, by day 14, the normal control (NC) group had achieved complete re-epithelialization. In contrast, diabetic mice still presented unhealed wounds at the same time point, thereby validating the successful establishment of a diabetic mouse model with delayed wound healing. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, as shown in Fig. 7F, indicated extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells and partial re-epithelialization across all groups during the inflammatory phase. Significantly, treatment with the collagen sponge led to strong adhesion between the absorbed wound exudate and granulation tissue. This, in turn, frequently caused disruption of the newly formed granulation tissue during dressing changes. Considering the intrinsically thin and fragile characteristics of wound granulation tissue, this commercial dressing seemed to be cost-effective and painful in its clinical applications. Along with time to day 18, compared to other treatment groups, the BA-HPCS@CGRP group exhibited the earliest formation of a completely regenerated epidermis, clearly visible mature hair follicles, and significantly more favorable wound healing outcomes (Purple line). The deposition and organization of collagen fibers are also indicative of the wound healing status. Consequently, Masson’s trichrome staining was utilized to assess the collagen production during wound repair. As depicted in Fig. 7G, throughout the entire healing process, the BA-HPCS@CGRP group displayed a greater degree of collagen deposition compared to the other three groups. During the observation period, all groups exhibited a biphasic pattern of collagen deposition. Specifically, there was a notable increase in collagen-positive staining prior to day 14. Subsequently, during the remodeling phase, a declining trend was observed, which was accompanied by condensation, regularization, and maturation of the collagen fibers, with these fibers gradually aligning parallel to the skin surface. By day 18, the BA-HPCS@CGRP group presented denser and more highly organized collagen deposition, closely resembling the normal dermis. Furthermore, we conducted continuous monitoring until day 28 after the surgery. H&E staining demonstrated no evident signs of foreign body reactions, including chronic inflammatory cell infiltration or granuloma formation. Masson’s staining further corroborated the presence of well-organized collagen deposition in the healed region, with no observation of abnormal fibrosis or excessive scar hyperplasia (Supplementary data Fig. S5A). Simultaneously, we conducted a quantitative assessment of the epidermal and dermal thickness of the skin in each group. The findings revealed that there were no statistically significant differences in either epidermal or dermal thickness among the groups (p > 0.05). This indicated that the application of BA-HPCS@CGRP facilitated normal tissue regeneration of the skin without inducing abnormal fibrosis or scar hyperplasia (Supplementary data Fig. S5B–C). Meanwhile, building upon our in vitro findings indicating that BA-HPCS@CGRP facilitated the secretion of Angptl4 by macrophages, thereby augmenting endothelial cell function, we utilized RT-PCR to analyze skin wound samples collected on day 10 (the proliferative phase). The results showed that the expression level of Angptl4 in the BA-HPCS@CGRP group was significantly higher than that in other groups. Specifically, it was 2.60 times greater than that in the control group (Fig. 7H). These results suggested that sustained-release CGRP could also promote the expression of Angptl4 in vivo during the process of wound healing.

Taken together, these findings demonstrated that the BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres with sustained CGRP release not only accelerated granulation tissue formation, re-epithelialization, collagen synthesis and remodeling, but also promoted the expression of Angptl4, which are crucial for effective wound healing.

We conducted the further assessment of the inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases of wound healing through immunofluorescence staining. Concerning the inflammatory phase, existing research has indicated that the hyperglycemic condition in diabetes sustains a protracted inflammatory microenvironment in wounds [50]. Thus, effectively promoting the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype has emerged as one of the crucial strategies for enhancing wound healing. Given our cytological experiments showing that CGRP could facilitate the polarization of M1 macrophages to the M2 phenotype under inflammatory circumstances, we utilized immunofluorescence staining to evaluate the impact of BA-HPCS@CGRP on macrophage polarization during the inflammatory phase of wound healing. The results indicated that compared with the Control group, the BA-HPCS@CGRP group was able to decrease the expression of CD86 by 3.23-fold and elevate the expression of CD206 by 1.51-fold (Fig. 8A and. 8C–D and Supplementary data Fig. S6). This finding suggested that BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres had the potential to facilitate the polarization of wound macrophages towards the M2 phenotype. Additionally, when compared with the BA-HPCS group, BA-HPCS@CGRP effectively augmented the expression of CD206 and diminished the expression of CD86. These results implied that the sustained release of CGRP played a pivotal role in promoting macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype. Impaired local neovascularization during the proliferative phase represents one of the pivotal factors contributing to delayed wound healing in diabetes [51]. Thus, enhancing angiogenesis in the wound is of utmost importance for the healing process. Consequently, we evaluated vascularization and vascular maturity via immunofluorescence co-staining of platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). As depicted in Fig. 8B and E (Supplementary data Figs. In comparison with the Control group, the BA-HPCS, BA-HPCS@CGRP, and Collagen sponge groups demonstrated a notable increase in mature vessels co-stained with CD31 and SMA. Among them, the BA-HPCS@CGRP group exhibited the most remarkable enhancement, with a 5.91-fold increase compared to the Control group. During the remodeling stage of wound healing, collagen deposition plays a crucial role in skin repair and significantly enhances the tensile strength of the newly formed tissue. Consequently, subcutaneous collagen deposition and remodeling within wounds serve as vital indicators of diabetic wound healing. Moreover, vimentin, acting as a marker for fibroblasts/myofibroblasts, facilitates wound closure and the remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM). In light of these findings, fluorescence staining was conducted on wound tissues during the remodeling phase. The results revealed that the expression of type I collagen (Col-1) in the BA-HPCS@CGRP group was approximately 2.99-fold higher than that in the Control group (Fig. 8B & F), and the expression of vimentin was 2.43-fold greater (Supplementary data Fig. S7B and S7E). Additionally, the expression of vimentin in the BA-HPCS group was 1.58-fold that of the Control group. These data suggested that BA-HPCS could, to some degree, enhance fibroblast activity in wound bed. And the incorporation of CGRP, further stimulated fibroblast activity, accelerated ECM remodeling, and thus promoted wound healing more effectively.

We discovered previously that CGRP as a neuropeptide reduced with a varying degree in all the diabetic skin, either type 2 diabetic patients, the classic type 2 diabetic model of db/db mice, or streptozotocin (STZ)-induced type 2 diabetic mice. Furthermore, we assessed the neural repair during the wound maturation phase with the application of sustained-release microspheres. Nerve growth factor (NGF) is an essential determinant for the survival and regeneration of sensory and sympathetic nerves [52]. During the remodeling phase, it expedites the reinnervation of damaged nerve fibers, thereby restoring sensory function in the wound area. Hence, we conducted a further assessment of wound healing by quantifying the expression levels of NGF, CGRP, and CD31. The results revealed that BA-HPCS@CGRP effectively promoted the increase in NGF expression, suggesting enhanced neural repair within this experimental group (Fig. 8B and G, and Supplementary data Fig. S8A). This enhancement may potentially mitigate the risk of chronic pain or sensory abnormalities. Simultaneously, the expression of CGRP in the BA-HPCS@CGRP group was also restored to a certain extent, and the positivity of CD31 was significantly augmented (Fig. 8B and H, and Supplementary data Fig. S8B). These results provided evidence that BA-HPCS@CGRP promoted nerve regeneration. The regenerated nerve terminals further modulated vascular function through the release of CGRP, thereby establishing a “neuro-vascular repair axis”.

In summary, the aforementioned results suggested that BA-HPCS@CGRP were capable of promoting the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype during the inflammatory phase. During the proliferative phase, they facilitated the formation of mature blood vessels. Moreover, in the remodeling phase, they accelerated the remodeling of ECM and the regeneration of nerve fibers. Consequently, these microspheres comprehensively expedited the healing process of diabetic wounds.

Discussion

The delayed healing of diabetic wounds can be primarily ascribed to macrophage dysfunction [53], neurovascular coupling impairment [54], oxidative stress [55] and persistent inflammation [56]. Diabetic neuropathy, the most prevalent comorbidity among diabetic wound patients, especially those with diabetic foot, is characterized by the insidious early progression [57]. Studies have shown that the progressive loss of peripheral sensory and autonomic nerves, along with reduced neuropeptide secretion, often precedes clinical symptoms such as paresthesia or pain [56]. This highlights the crucial significance of early neural intervention for wound healing. Notably, among the neuropeptides released from sensory nerve endings, CGRP has been recognized as a key regulator that expedites acute wound healing [16]. Moreover, investigations using CGRP-knockout diabetic mouse models have revealed significantly delayed wound repair, emphasizing the essential role of CGRP in tissue regeneration [26]. Our findings confirmed that the expression of CGRP was markedly decreased in the skin tissues of patients with type 2 diabetic wounds and db/db mice when compared to healthy controls. This demonstrated that downregulated CGRP was a critical pathological factor contributing to the impairment of diabetic wound healing.

The BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres synthesized via microfluidic technology were systematically characterized to demonstrate exceptional structural stability, biocompatibility, and suitability for wound healing applications. BA-HPCS@CGRP microsphere system was a homogeneous composite hydrogel microsphere constructed on a covalently crosslinked GelMA network, exhibiting interpenetrating network characteristics. In this system, GelMA formed a 3D covalent network framework upon photo-crosslinking. During the gelation process, BA-HPCS polymer chains were uniformly dispersed and physically interpenetrated within the GelMA network. CGRP was dissolved in the aqueous phase prior to network formation and became homogeneously encapsulated throughout the composite hydrogel matrix during crosslinking. Degradation studies confirmed their stability throughout the healing process, while controlled CGRP release over two weeks reduced the need for frequent dressing changes. Based on the concentration-dependent biological effects of CGRP and the hyperglycemic microenvironment characteristics of diabetic wounds, this system was designed with a glucose-responsive drug release mechanism: under normal or mildly elevated glucose conditions, it maintained a basal sustained-release rate to provide a consistent and stable therapeutic concentration; under pathological hyperglycemic conditions, the drug release rate was gently up-regulated through specific phenylboronic acid-glucose recognition, ensuring that the local concentration of CGRP remained precisely within the safe and effective therapeutic range. This achieved synergistic treatment through intelligent sensing and adaptive regulation. Furthermore, remarkable antibacterial properties were detected. All critical for tissue regeneration was consistently preserved. The hyperglycemic microenvironment impairs local vascular function at diabetic wound, which precipitates delayed healing and chronic inflammatory responses [58,59]. While, BA-HPCS@CGRP directly rejuvenated the functions of hyperglycemic-injured endothelial cell at the aspect of proliferation, migration, and tube formation capacity, meanwhile, promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and reducing inflammatory cytokine secretion. Furthermore, in macrophage-endothelial cell co-culture systems, our observations indicated that BA-HPCS@CGRP significantly augmented the migration and tube formation capabilities of endothelial cells when co-cultured with macrophages. Through gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of transcriptome sequencing data and differential gene screening, we identified the upregulated expression genes Igf1, Angptl4 and Tnfsf14. Subsequent validation by RT-PCR and ELISA confirmed the increased secretion of Angptl4 specifically from macrophage when treated with BA-HPCS@CGRP. Angptl4 belongs to the angiopoietin-like protein family and plays a crucial role in regulating lipid metabolism and angiogenesis across diverse tissues [60]. Our study demonstrated that BA-HPCS@CGRP not only directly promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and partially facilitated endothelial functional recovery, but also further enhanced its regulatory effect on endothelial cell function indirectly by inducing macrophages to secrete Angptl4. This finding suggested that the role of sustained-release CGRP in endothelial repair was not limited to direct effects but also involved a paracrine mechanism mediated by macrophages. To verify whether Angptl4 plays a critical role in this process, we subsequently used siRNA technology to knockdown Angptl4 expression in macrophages and evaluated its functional impact through a series of endothelial cell assays, including tube formation assay, as well as measurements of LDH and NO levels in the supernatant. The results showed that after Angptl4 expression was suppressed, the promotive effect of BA-HPCS@CGRP on endothelial cell functional recovery was significantly attenuated, indicating that Angptl4 was an essential mediator in CGRP-regulated endothelial repair. Previous research demonstrated that Angptl4 knockout mice displayed reduced neovascularization and decreased vascular permeability, underscoring the crucial role of Angptl4 in maintaining endothelial metabolic functions [45]. Zhan et al. found that Angptl4 contributed to endothelial cell proliferation by inducing autophagy [61]. These findings suggest that Angptl4 exerts a pivotal regulatory function in endothelial cell function. Notably, a study published by Chong et al. demonstrated that exogenous Angptl4 promoted angiogenesis in diabetic wounds by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway and upregulating the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in keratinocytes [62]. This finding, together with our results, highlights the pleiotropic functions of Angptl4 across different cell types and tissue microenvironments, further supporting its crucial role in tissue repair and vascular regeneration.

Wound healing represents a sophisticated and dynamic process that encompasses multiple distinct phases, including hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [63]. The remarkable progress in biomaterials has substantially enhanced the rate and effectiveness of wound repair [64]. The impaired healing of diabetic wounds is intricately linked to heightened inflammatory responses during the inflammatory stage [65]. This is evidenced by the persistent activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages, which leads to a notable elevation in the M1/M2 ratio. Such a phenomenon has been documented in both diabetic patients and animal models, ultimately causing a delay in the transition of wound healing from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase [66,67]. In recent years, investigations about CGRP in macrophages have witnessed a notable and substantial increase. In a mouse model of acute lung injury, CGRP acts on macrophages to suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reduce the production of mature IL-1β [68]. In a rat mandibular defect model, Kong et al. demonstrated that CGRP promotes the polarization of M1 macrophages toward the M2 phenotype by activating Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1). Moreover, CGRP is capable of mitigating ARDS-associated pathological damage, inflammation, and oxidative stress by inhibiting the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) pathway to modulate the balance of macrophage polarization [69]. However, studies on CGRP-mediated modulation of macrophage polarization in diabetic wounds remain scarce as of yet. Herein, we have demonstrated that BA-HPCS@CGRP promoted the transition of macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype during the inflammatory phase, thereby accelerating the progression of wound healing from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase. During the proliferative phase, our observations revealed that BA-HPCS@CGRP remarkably enhanced the co-staining of CD31 and α-SMA. This finding indicated its ability to facilitate the transformation of wound vasculature from primitive and unstable capillary networks into structured and functionally mature blood vessels. NGF, a core constituent of the neurotrophic factor family and a pivotal indicator of axonal regeneration, assumes a critical role in neural development, injury repair, and immune regulation. In diabetic skin, the expression of NGF is found to decline, suggesting that neural innervation defects contribute to the delay in wound healing [70,71]. In the remodeling phase, BA-HPCS@CGRP promoted neural and vascular regeneration synergistically by upregulating NGF, CD31, and CGRP, providing a dual-track repair mechanism for diabetic wounds. At the peripheral nerve terminals of nociceptors, locally released neuropeptide CGRP drives neurogenic inflammation and regulates tissue repair by binding to specific receptors on immune cells and vascular endothelial cells - primarily the heterodimer complex formed by receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) and calcitonin receptor-like receptor (CLR) - thereby triggering downstream signaling pathways. The core mechanism involves CGRP-RAMP1/CLR binding activating key signaling cascades, including cAMP/PKA. Studies demonstrate that CGRP activates the protein kinase A (PKA)/focal adhesion kinase (FAK) pathway in bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), enhancing osteogenic differentiation and osseointegration [72]. Within splenic germinal centers, CGRP+ nerve fibers augment B cell antibody secretion via the cyclic AMP (cAMP) signaling pathway, promoting humoral immune responses [73]. Warakorn Kulalert further elucidated that skin commensal microbiota colonization induces RAMP1 upregulation in CD8+ T cells, subsequently enabling CGRP-RAMP1/CLR signaling to suppress Th17 responses and modulate the homeostatic activation of microbiota-specific T lymphocytes [74]. Additionally, studies ablating Nav1.8+ nociceptors impair skin wound repair and muscle regeneration, confirmed this process depends on CGRP-RAMP1 signaling to polarize macrophages towards a reparative M2 phenotype-consistent which was also verified in our findings that BA-HPCS@CGRP promoted M2 macrophage polarization. Notably, tumor microenvironment research indicates tumor cells secrete NGF to induce proliferation of surrounding CGRP+ sensory neurons, subsequently exploiting CGRP-RAMP1/CLR signaling to activate either Rap1-mediated cytoprotective autophagy or calcium influx in tumor cells to sustain survival [75,76]. Concurrently, studies have revealed that nitric oxide production increases in endothelial cell when stimulated with CGRP, whereas it decreases in the presence of the CGRP antagonist during co-culture of endothelial cell with CGRP, indicating CGRP acts as the effector mediator in endothelial cell [77]. These findings collectively demonstrate neuro-immune or neuro-vascular regulatory axes mediated by CGRP across distinct tissue repair contexts. This study presented the novel discovery that BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres not only promoted M2 macrophage polarization but also further modulated endothelial cell function by regulating the secretion of Angptl4 from macrophages, suggesting the possible existence of a CGRP-mediated “neuro-immune-vascular” regulatory axis during cutaneous wound healing. In subsequent in-depth mechanistic and translational studies, we will further strengthen the evidence supporting the interplay among neural, immune, and vascular components within this axis by incorporating techniques such as axon guidance evaluation and neural-specific marker staining, thereby systematically refining this theoretical framework.

Hydrogel-based platform for controlled drug releasing strategy has also been explored in other diabetic tissue repair contexts [78]. Present study illustrated a novel microsphere platform for controlled CGRP delivery under certain hyperglycemia-stimulate. When compared with the clinical gold standard, the collagen sponge, the BA-HPCS@CGRP microspheres fabricated via microfluidic control not only exhibit the feasibility for large-scale production but also innovatively integrate triple synergistic treatment modalities. These include glucose-responsive release, dual-mode regulation of antibacterial and immune functions, and promotion of angiogenesis. This offers an innovative treatment paradigm for diabetic wounds that are prone to infection. Notably, the limitation of this study is that while the microsphere sustained-release system can achieve long-term sustained release of CGRP, it remains essential to further optimize the stability of CGRP and establish a real-time monitoring system. This is crucial to mitigate potential off-target risks.

In conclusion, BA-HPCS@CGRP exhibited remarkable therapeutic potential for diabetic wounds. It accomplished whole-course sequential treatment via its synergistic mechanism.

Conclusion

As the foremost origin, a notable reduction of CGRP expression was demonstrated in both clinical patients with type 2 diabetes and db/db mouse models. Considering the off-target effects of CGRP and the hyperglycemic characteristics of the diabetic microenvironment, this study devised a glucose-responsive microsphere system (BA-HPCS@CGRP) for the spatiotemporally regulated release of CGRP and discover its therapeutic potential in the context of diabetic wound healing. In vitro experiments have clearly demonstrated that BA-HPCS@CGRP exhibited outstanding biocompatibility and antibacterial characteristics. Simultaneously, it directly enhanced the function of endothelial cells. In macrophage-endothelial cell co-culture systems, these microspheres not only induced the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype but also significantly facilitated endothelial cell proliferation and migration by stimulating the secretion of macrophage-derived Angptl4. In diabetic mouse wound models, BA-HPCS@CGRP manifested stage-specific regulatory functions. It facilitated the polarization of M2 anti-inflammatory macrophages during the inflammatory phase, upregulated Angptl4 expression, and enhanced blood vessel formation and maturation in the proliferative phase. Furthermore, it improved organized collagen deposition and promoted neurovascular regeneration in the remodeling phase. This study offers novel strategies and perspectives for addressing the issue of delayed healing in diabetic wounds.
