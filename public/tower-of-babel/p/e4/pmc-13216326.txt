A novel L-shaped ortho-quinone analog and 2-Deoxy-D-Glucose a synergistic approach to inhibit hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation
Authors: Xiang Chen, Li Jiang, Qingmei Mo, Yiling Wei, Pan Wu, Weidong Pan, Heng Luo, Ming Zhuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract2-Dexy-D-glucose (2-DG) is a chemotherapy drug that blocks the glycolytic process, thereby exerting its metabolism inhibitor and tumor growth inhibitor function in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells. However, 2-DG requires prolonged high-dose treatment to achieve enough therapeutic efficacies, which may lead to persistent side effects such as elevated blood glucose levels, dizziness, and nausea. We have screened a novel L-shaped ortho-quinone analog named TC1, which exhibits excellent anti-tumor activity with broad-spectrum cytotoxicity against multiple tumor cell lines. In the meanwhile, it also shows a strong potential to inhibit metabolism in tumor cells, which makes it a good candidate for combined treatment with 2-DG. We used combined treatment of TC1 and 2-DG on HCC cell lines and analyzed cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and mitochondrial function. Additionally, we examined the effects of this combination therapy on nude mice bearing transplanted tumor. Furthermore, we explored the potential mechanisms using RNA sequencing and verified by RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence staining. In vitro experiments showed that the combination treatment of TC1 and 2-DG synergistically inhibited the proliferation of HCC cell lines including SMMC-7721 cells and Hepa1-6 cells, induced apoptosis, depleted ATP content in cells and increased ROS content in cells. In vivo experiments showed the combination treatment effectively inhibited the growth of SMMC-7721 cell transplanted tumors and prolonged the survival time of mice. Through RNA sequencing, we found that MAPK4 was down-regulated by the combination treatment. Through real-time quantitative PCR and immunofluorescence staining, we confirmed that both MAPK4 expression and AKT phosphorylation were significantly decreased, together with the increasing expression of BAX in the combination treatment group. Introduction

Chemotherapy drugs are widely used for cancer treatment. However, single drug treatment often has limited efficacy and drug resistance would frequently be seen after continuous treatment. Both of these factors will limit their long-term effectiveness. To reduce these effects, combination therapy strategies become more and more reported1. By simultaneously action on different targets or pathways in tumor cells, these strategies enhance treatment effectiveness while reduce risks of drug resistance. Meanwhile, the exploration of low-toxicity natural anti-tumor drugs has become a hotspot. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the synergistic combinations of conventional anti-tumor drugs with natural bioactive compounds exhibit significant chemotherapeutic properties while reducing the chance of drug resistance2. 2-DG is a glucose analog that targets the glycolytic pathway and may lead to depletion of energy in cancer cells. In addition, 2-DG exerts its anticancer effects through multiple mechanisms, including the induction of oxidative stress, disruption of protein N-glycosylation and interference with cellular thiol metabolism. These combined actions result in autophagy induction, inhibition of cell proliferation, and promotion of apoptosis in specific cancer cells3. As a promising anti-tumor agent, 2-DG is frequently employed as an adjuvant to conventional chemotherapy drugs. Extensive preclinical and clinical investigations have consistently shown that 2-DG exhibits favorable safety profile with minimal toxicity in both animal models and human subjects. This established safety record has facilitated its widespread application in combination with anti-tumor therapy research, spanning from preclinical studies to clinical trials3–5.

Drugs derived from natural products, such as paclitaxel and vincristine, have been widely used in the treatments of cancers6–8. By chemical derivatization, natural products could conserve their pharmacological profile while gain substantially improved pharmaceutical performances9. The combination of natural products with other anti-tumor drugs holds promise for enhancing drug efficacy while reducing side effects10. Furthermore, chemically modified natural products used in combination with other drugs may demonstrate greater potential for future applications11,12.

Salvia miltiorrhiza is a commonly used Chinese medicine13, and its main active ingredients are lipophilic compounds including tanshinone IIA(Tan-IIA)14,15. Tan-IIA has been reported to have anti-tumor activity against various tumor cells16. It can activate the AMPK pathway and inhibit the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway17. However, its low water solubility, short half-life, susceptibility to efflux pump clearance, and limited tumor-inhibiting effects severely restrict its application18. So far, Tan-IIA has not yet progressed to clinical trials. We have obtained a novel L-shaped ortho-quinone analog TC1, which is derived from Tan-IIA, from the Natural Products Research Center of Guizhou Province19,20. It exhibits broad-spectrum inhibitory activity against the proliferation of many cancer cell lines.

Based on the possible mechanism of TC1, we have screened a series of chemotherapy drugs in combination with TC1. The results demonstrated that the combination of 2-DG together with TC1 exhibited a significant synergistic therapeutic effect. To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the synergistic effect of 2-DG and TC1, this study systematically evaluated the antitumor effects of 2-DG combined with TC1 both in vivo and in vitro, with a focus on analyzing related signaling pathways. The findings are expected to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of the synergistic effect and provide experimental evidence for the development of drug combination strategies targeting hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

SMMC-7721 (human HCC cell line) and Hepa1-6 (mouse HCC cell line) were purchased from Procell Life Technology. Both cells were come with authenticated certifications by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling within the last year and only low passage cells were used for experiments. All cells were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial detection kit (Solarbio, CAT#CA1080), and only mycoplasma-free cells were used. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium for SMMC-7721 cells and DMEM medium for Hepa1-6 cells. In both medium, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (PS, Gibco, USA) were added. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. SMMC-7721 cell was selected for the in vivo study because it exhibited favorable synergistic effects in response to the combination treatment of TC1 and 2-DG in preliminary in vitro screening, and is a widely used and representative HCC line, was selected to establish a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model to ensure the reliability and comparability of the research results21.

Tan-IIA was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. 2-DG was purchased from MedChemExpress, USA. DMEM medium, RPMI-1640 medium, FBS, trypsin, crystal violet, and phosphate buffered saline (PBS) were all purchased from Solarbio.

TC1 and Tan-IIA were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 10mM. The stock solution was stored at–20 °C until use. For in vitro experiments, the stock solution was diluted with culture medium to the desired working concentrations, with the final DMSO concentration kept below 0.1% to minimize solvent toxicity. For in vivo administration, TC1 was freshly diluted in normal saline to achieve the required dosage.

2-DG was dissolved in ddH2O to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 0.5 M. The solution was filter-sterilized and stored at -20 °C. Working concentrations were freshly prepared by diluting the stock solution in culture medium or saline for in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Cell viability assay

CCK-8 cell proliferation Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was used to determine cell viability. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions. SMMC-7721 and Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 104 cells/100 µL per well) were seeded in a 96-well plate. Cells were pretreated with 2-DG for 24 h, and then TC1 was added for an additional 24 h treatment for all in vitro experiments. After treatment, CCK-8 reagent (4 µL/well) was added per well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Spark®, Tecan Group Ltd, Switzerland). The proliferation inhibition rate was calculated as described22. IC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA, USA). The Combination Index(CI) was calculated using CompuSyn software(CompuSyn, Inc., USA). When CI = 1, it indicates that the drugs have only additive effect but no synergistic effect; when CI < 1, it indicates synergistic effect; and when CI > 1, it indicates antagonism between the two drugs23.

EdU incorporation assay

Cell proliferation was determined by EdU incorporation assay using BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, red fluorescence). The cells were seeded on cell culture coverslips in a 24-well plate and treated the same way as listed in 2.2. After treatment, EdU was added at a final concentration of 10 µM and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, and washed twice with PBS. Then, 100 µL of Click reaction solution was added and cells were incubated in the dark for 30 min. After washing twice with PBS, the coverslips were mounted onto microscope slides. After imaging under a fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan), the results were analyzed using ImageJ software (Wayne Rasband, USA). Three sets of independent biological experiments were done and 40 cells were randomly selected fields for each experimental condition. The freehand tool was used to circle the cell nuclei, and the average fluorescence intensity was obtained using the Analysis tool.

For mice samples, EdU was administered at 50 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection 2 h before sacrifice. After sacrifice, part of the tumor tissue was dipped into mold filled with optimal cutting temperature compound (Tissue-Tek®, USA), snap frozen on dry ice and then stored at -80 °C. Tissue section was cut at 10 μm thickness and stained for EdU by the same protocol as cells.

Cell migration assay

SMMC-7721 cells were cultured in 24-well glass-bottom dishes until reaching confluence. A uniform linear wound was introduced into the monolayer using a sterile 100 µL plastic pipette tip. Following scratch formation, cellular debris was removed by gently washing the cells with PBS. Cell migration was monitored at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h post-scratching using an inverted microscope (Axio Observer 7, ZEISS, Germany). The migration distance was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software. Select the scratch area and measure the width of the scratch. In each field of view, at least 3 different positions were selected to measure the scratch width and calculate the average value.

Cell invasion assay

SMMC-7721 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of transwell chambers with matrix gel (Corning, USA) using 1640 medium without FBS at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL. 200 µL of cell suspension was added to each chamber and placed in a 24-well plate containing 600 µL of complete culture medium per well. After treatment, the cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet and photographed under a microscope (BA410E, Motic, China). Three random fields of view were taken for each sample. Counting was performed using ImageJ software.

Colony formation assay

SMMC-7721 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 800 cells per well and cultured for 3 days before treatment. After treatment, the media was changed every three days. After 14 days, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 15 min. After three times washing with PBS, images were captured under a microscope (BA410E), and the colony formation numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell apoptosis assay

Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD double-staining kit (Beyotime) was used to detect apoptosis cells. Briefly, 1 × 106 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. After treatment, the culture supernatant and trypsin-digested cells were collected into flow cytometry tubes. The cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS and resuspended in binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC (1:100) and 7-AAD (1:20) were added, followed by incubation in dark for 15 min. Finally, cells were analyzed on CytoFLEX V5-B3-R3 (Beckman). Data was analyzed using FlowJo software (Tristar, CA, USA). Apoptotic cells were gated by both Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD positive cells.

To further confirm the results from flow cytometry, we performed TUNEL staining on cells. The Beyotime one-step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (red fluorescence) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three random fields were imaged using a fluorescence microscope. The number of TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells and total cells were counted, and the apoptosis rate was then calculated.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential change

We used the enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (Beyotime) to detect the mitochondrial membrane potential change in cell. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, after drug treatment, the culture supernatant and trypsin-digested cells were collected into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Then the cells were centrifuged at 600 g for 3 min, resuspended in 500 µL of culture medium, mixed with 500 µL of JC-1 working solution, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Cells were centrifuged at 600 g and washed twice with PBS, then resuspended in JC-1 staining buffer and measured on microplate reader. The parameters used were green fluorescence (Ex488/Em530) and red fluorescence (Ex543/Em590). The experimental results are represented by the ratio of green to red fluorescence.

Determination of intracellular ATP content

Intraellular ATP levels were quantified using the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells in a 6-well plate were lysed with 200 µL of lysis buffer. The mixture was centrifuged at full speed to remove cell debris, and the supernatant was retained for ATP measurement. ATP was allowed to react with luciferase. The luminescence was measured using a microplate reader. By plotting the concentration of the standard solutions on the x-axis and the corresponding luminescence intensity values on the y-axis, a linear regression analysis was performed to derive the standard curve equation. The luminescence intensity values of the sample group were then substituted into the standard curve equation to calculate the concentration of ATP in the samples.

Determination of intracellular reactive oxygen species content

The cell-permeable fluorescent probe DCFH-DA from the Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Assay Kit (Beyotime) was used to detect intracellular levels of ROS in living cells. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, after treatment, cells were collected and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 20 min in a cell culture incubator. The mixture was shaken every 3–5 min to ensure thorough contact between the probe and the cells. The cells were washed three times with PBS and analyzed on a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX). The data obtained from flow cytometry was analyzed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence staining and image acquisition

In brief, 1 × 106 cells were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (Solarbio) for 30 min and washed three times with PBS. Primary antibodies, including mtTFA (1:1500; GeneTex, USA), MAPK4 (1:300; Cat. 26102-1-AP; Proteintech, China), and AKT (1:300; Cat.#23430; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) were applied to the coverslips and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies. Goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 (1:2000; Cat. A-11012) or Alexa Fluor 488 (1:2000; Cat. A-11008) (both from Invitrogen, USA) were used. The secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After three times washing with PBS, the slides were mounted with a DAPI-containing mount medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Finally, the cells were observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (IX71, Olympus).

Subcutaneous transplant tumor model in nude mice

Four-week-old male BALB/c-nu nude mice were purchased from Tengxin Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Chongqing, China). The involved procedure was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou University (ethical approval number: EAE-GZU-2023-E039). To construct transplant tumor models, SMMC-7721 cells were inoculated subcutaneously into the right abdomen of nude mice (1 × 107 cells per mouse). One week after inoculation, the length and width of tumor were measured daily. The treatment was initiated on day 8. The tumor volume (V, mm3) was calculated by the formula: V = 0.5×length×width2.

All the tumor bearing mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 10 for TanIIA + TC1 group and 5 for other groups) including the control group, the 2-DG (5 mg/kg) group, the Tan-IIA (23 mg/kg) group, the TC1 (23 mg/kg) group, the combined group of Tan-IIA (23 mg/kg) together with 2-DG (5 mg/kg) and the combined group of TC1 (23 mg/kg) together with 2-DG (5 mg/kg). Drugs were administered via intratumoral injection once every 3 days for a total of five doses throughout the experimental period. At the end of the experiment, a portion of the mice were euthanized and dissected at day 22. A total of 5 mice in TC1 and 2-DG combination treatment group were used to observe their survival time. When the tumor volume reached 1000 mm3, they were also euthanized by cervical dislocation and subsequently dissected.

Transcriptome analysis

To investigate the transcriptional changes after the combination treatment of TC1 and 2-DG, whole-transcriptome analysis was performed using high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) technology. 1 × 106 cells were inoculated in a 10 cm petridish and treated with drugs as described above. After treatment, the cells were digested and then centrifuged to discard the supernatant. The resulting cell pellet was promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen before transferred to dry ice for sample storage and submission. The RNA extraction and sequencing were conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and the library quality was detected by Qubit 2.0 quantitative and Agilent 2100. After being qualified, Illumina sequencing was performed24. In the expression analysis stage, after the normalization of gene expression data, p values were calculated by a statistical model, and FDR values were obtained after multiple test corrections to screen for significantly differentially expressed genes25. Differential gene analysis included Venn diagram display and multiple groups of experiments can be clustered. Finally, the background gene set are all annotated genes, covering up-regulated and down-regulated differential genes.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

To validate the RNA sequencing results, we detected the expression of related genes by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). After treatment, total RNA was extracted from SMMC-7721 cells by SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, China), and then 1 µg RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA by Evo M-MLV1 reverse transcription premix kit (Accurate Biology). PCR amplifications were performed on the Bio-Rad CFX Duet detection system in 20 µL reaction volumes containing cDNA, primers, and SYBR Green Pro Taq HS premix (Accurate Biology). Primers are listed in Table S1. The relative expression of mRNA was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method26.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (San Diego) was used for statistical analysis. The differences between groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA or t-test. Results are presented as mean values ± SEM. P values by Student’s t-test except as otherwise indicated. *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001. Data are representative of at least three independent experiments.

Results

The combination treatment of TC1 and 2-DG synergistically inhibits the proliferation of HCC cells

We analyzed the viability of SMMC-7721 cells after treatment with TC1 (Fig. 1A) and 2-DG (Fig. 1B). The IC50 of TC1 was 11.7 ± 0.72 µM after 24 h of treatment (Fig. 1C), while the IC50 of 2-DG was 21.4 ± 1.7 mM after 48 h of treatment (Fig. 1D). For combination treatment we used the combination of 10 µM of TC1 and 4 mM of 2-DG, our result shown this combination (Fig. 1E) significantly inhibited the proliferation of SMMC-7721 cells, with a more pronounced effect compared to monotherapy groups. The combination index of 0.71 indicates a synergistic effect in the combination group.

Additionally, we assessed EdU incorporation assay in SMMC-7721 and Hepa1-6 cells treated with TC1 and 2-DG to confirm the proliferation inhibiting effect. Compared to TC1 and 2-DG single-drug groups, the combination of 2-DG and TC1 (Fig. 1F) almost depleted nuclear DNA EdU incorporation in both cells, demonstrating that the cell proliferation was indeed significantly inhibited by the combination treatment.

The combination of TC1 and 2-DG significantly reduces the migration, invasion, and colony formation abilities of HCC cells

To evaluate the effects of TC1 and 2-DG on cell migration ability, we conducted a scratch assay. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the inhibitory effect of the TC1 and 2-DG combined treatment on cell migration ability was significantly stronger than that of the single-drug groups, indicating that the combination treatment had a markedly inhibitory effect on cell migration. To investigate the effects of TC1 and 2-DG on cell invasion ability, we conducted the transwell invasion assay. The combination treatment of TC1 and 2-DG significantly reduced the number of invaded cells compared to that of the single-drug groups (Fig. 2C, D), suggesting a synergistic inhibitory effect on cell invasion. We also investigated the effects of TC1 and 2-DG on cell colony formation. Compared to the control group, single drug treatment using TC1 or 2-DG alone had a weak impact on cell clonal formation. Notably, combined treatment significantly inhibited cell clonal formation, which is much stronger than the single drug group (Fig. 2E).

The combination of TC1 and 2-DG significantly promoted Apoptosis in HCC cells

We used the Annexin-V and 7-AAD double staining to detect apoptotic cells after treatment of the combination of TC1 and 2-DG on cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, 2-DG alone induced a low level of apoptosis in SMMC-7721 cells, whereas TC1 alone triggered more apoptosis. However, the combination of TC1 and 2-DG significantly enhanced the apoptosis rate. In Hepa1-6 cells we saw similar results. We have further conducted TUNEL staining to detect apoptotic cells. As shown in Fig. 3B, in the control group and the monotherapy group, there were almost no apoptotic cells being detected. However, in the group treated with TC1 in combination with 2-DG, there were a significant amount of apoptotic cells.

Effects of the combined treatment of TC1 and 2-DG on intracellular ATP and ROS levels

To evaluate the impact of combination treatment on HCC cells’ metabolism, we measured intracellular ATP content in SMMC-7721 cells after treatment. As shown in Fig. 4A, compared with the control group, treatment with 2-DG alone can significantly reduce the cellular energy metabolism level. However, after drug treatment, cellular energy metabolism is more strongly inhibited as the ATP is almost depleted. The depletion of ATP may lead to apoptosis and result in functional disturbances within the cell. Mitochondria are closely associated with ROS generation, serving as the primary source of ROS within cells. Abnormal oxidation of intermediate products in the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) is a key site of ROS production27. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ROS levels in both SMMC-7721 (Fig. 4B) and Hepa1-6 cells were significantly elevated after the combination treatment (Fig. 4C).

The combined treatment of TC1 and 2-DG significantly affects mitochondrial function in cells

We used the JC-1 fluorescent probe to detect the changes in mitochondrial membrane potential. Quantitative analysis showed that the green/red fluorescence intensity ratio in the treatment group was significantly higher than that of the single-drug groups. This result implies the potential impairment of mitochondrial function (Fig. 4D). To assess mitochondrial integrity, we performed mtTFA staining to examine mitochondrial distribution. As shown in Fig. 4E, 2-DG and TC1 treatment caused extensive mitochondrial accumulation, while in Hepa1-6 cells, it induced mitochondrial polarization (Fig. 4F). Compared to the control group, monotherapy with either TC1 or 2-DG alone had minimal impact on mitochondrial distribution in cells. After treatment with the combination of TC1 and 2-DG, a large amount of mitochondrial aggregation was induced. Both mitochondrial accumulation and mitochondrial polarization are important indicators of mitochondrial damage. Our results suggest that the combined treatment may have a significant impact on damaging mitochondria in HCC cells, which may affect the energy metabolism and survival status of the cells.

The growth of transplanted tumors was effectively inhibited by combined treatment of TC1 and 2-DG in vivo

We established a nude mice subcutaneous transplanted tumor model with SMMC-7721 cells. We defined the day of tumor implantation as day 0 for the experimental timeline, and treatment was initiated on day 8 while most of the tumor volume reached 100 mm³. The drug was administered via intratumoral injection, repeating every three days (Fig. 5A). Compared to the monotherapy group, the combination of 2-DG and TC1 significantly inhibited the growth of the transplanted tumor (Fig. 5B, C) with low impact on body weight (Fig. 5D)and extended survival significantly (Fig. 5E). After drug administration, the combination therapy group showed a gradual increase in body weight and slow tumor growth (Fig. 5F, G). To further investigate the inhibitory effect of the combination on tumor proliferation, we also performed EdU staining on tumor sections. The combination of 2-DG and TC1 significantly inhibited cell proliferation in the transplanted tumor (Fig. 5H).

MAPK4 expression was down-regulated by the combined treatment of TC1 and 2-DG

We used RNA-seq technology to investigate the transcriptional changes after treatment of TC1 and 2-DG. As shown in Fig. 6A, through RNA sequencing analysis, we identified a total of 107 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the TC1 and 2-DG combination group. Among these, 2 DEGs overlapped with the TC1 monotherapy group, 30 DEGs overlapped with the 2-DG monotherapy group, and 2 DEGs overlapped with the Tan-IIA monotherapy group. After excluding these overlapping genes, a total of 75 unique DEGs remained. Through literature review and functional annotation analysis using the Genecards (www.genecards.org) database, we identified 9 key genes from these DEGs that are closely associated with tumorigenesis and progression. 9 targets closely associated with tumors were further selected, with MAPK4 showing significant downregulation (Fig. 6B).

To verify the sequencing results, we used RT-qPCR to detect the mRNA expression levels of MAPK4 after drug treatment. The combination of 2-DG and TC1 significantly down-regulated the MAPK4 gene, which is consistent with the RNA sequencing data (Fig. 6C).

To further investigate the impact of combination treatment on MAPK4, the expression level of MAPK4 protein was detected by immunofluorescence staining. The fluorescence intensity of MAPK4 was significantly reduced in the group threatened by the combination of TC1 with 2-DG, indicating that the TC1 and 2-DG combination leads to a significant downregulation of MAPK4 (Fig. 6D, F).

AKT phosphorylation is a key marker of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway activation, and its levels directly reflect the state of pathway activation28. To evaluate MAPK4’s regulatory effect on downstream signaling, we measured the intracellular phosphorylation levels of AKT. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that compared to monotherapy, AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 6E, G)level was significantly reduced by the combination treatment of 2-DG and TC1.

BAX and Fos are the most important downstream genes of MAPK4 and are associated with tumor progression. We further detected the expression levels of these two genes by RT-qPCR; BAX showed a significant upregulation (Fig. 6H), while Fos was significantly downregulated (Fig. 6I). All these results confirm that the combination treatment downregulates MAPK4 signaling.

Discussion

Although chemotherapy drugs remain a cornerstone in the clinical management of HCC29, their therapeutic efficacy is often limited by suboptimal outcomes and significant adverse effects30,31. In recent years, the development of multimodal combination therapies has emerged as a pivotal strategy to enhance anti-tumor efficacy. The exploration of novel drug combinations holds considerable clinical promise, not only for potentiating anticancer effects but also for advancing therapeutic paradigms and improving patient prognosis and quality of life.

To minimize potential drug toxicity, this study initially estimated the IC50 of both 2-DG and TC1 through experimental determination. Subsequent combination therapy experiments were then conducted using drug concentrations carefully selected below these IC50 thresholds. In the current work, our in vitro results indicate that the combined treatment of 2-DG and TC1 significantly inhibits the proliferation of SMMC-7721 cells compared to the monotherapy groups, induces apoptosis, and markedly reduces the migratory and invasive capabilities as well as the colony formation of tumor cells. Metabolic reprogramming represents a hallmark of malignant cells. HCC cells often exhibit metabolic plasticity, enabling them to simultaneously utilize glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to meet their energy demands32.Although 2-DG could effectively inhibit glycolysis in tumor cells, this inhibition alone is insufficient to induce ATP depletion33. Through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, HCC cells could also get plenty of ATP and may compensate the loss from glycolysis. In our results, the combination of 2-DG and TC1 shows a much stronger effect in depleting ATP compared to 2-DG or TC1 alone. This effect hints that besides the inhibition of glycolysis, the function of mitochondria may also be inhibited by combination treatment. This hypothesis is further supported by our other results, including decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, elevated levels of intracellular ROS, and significant mitochondrial depolarization in cells treated with combination treatment. This dual inhibition deprives tumor cells the ability of both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, the two major energy supply pathways, thus results in the depletion of intracellular ATP and the death of tumor cells. This strategy holds the promise as a highly innovative and potentially effective anti-tumor strategy.

In the in vivo experiments, we observe that combination treatment showed significant tumor growth inhibition with only five times drug administration. Moreover, the combination treatment group exhibits a marked prolongation of survival in tumor-bearing mice. Given the fact that the treatment only lasts for 13 days, the survival data obtained thus far couldn’t fully reflect the maximum therapeutic potential of this combination therapy. In future studies, we will optimize the dosing regimen, extend the treatment duration and adjust the dosing frequency to further evaluate the sustained effects of this combination treatment strategy. Additionally, during the whole experiment, the body weights of the treated mice were steadily increased, indicating that the drug combination had a relatively minor impact on the overall condition of the mice. However, there are some limitations in the current study: First, the potential toxicological research is not comprehensive. Additionally, no studies have been conducted on the mitochondrial function in tumor tissue cells after drug administration. In future research, we will optimize the drug administration regimen, further investigate the effects of TC1 in combination with 2-DG on hematological parameters and the function of vital organs, and conduct histopathological examinations to more comprehensively assess the potential toxicity of the drugs. Through systematic in vivo experimental studies, we will be able to more comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of TC1 in combination with 2-DG against HCC.

Tan-IIA is a natural phenanthraquinone, but it suffers from poor water solubility and low bioavailability. To address these drawbacks and enhance its antitumor efficacy, Li Shengyou et al. successfully designed and synthesized a novel demethylated L-shaped molecule, TC1, through structural modification19. This compound features the removal of the methyl group at the C-3 position of the furan ring and the introduction of a nitrogen-containing side chain (diethylaminoethoxy) at the C-2 position, resulting in a unique L-shaped configuration. These modifications improve the solubility and bioavailability of the drug, as well as enhance its adaptability to the target. Due to its special configuration and the introduced side chain, TC1 may be more readily inserted into the mitochondrial membrane or bonded to the MAPK4 protein, thereby exhibiting more serious mitochondrial inhibition and MAPK4/AKT pathway inhibition activity. Our data validates the modification strategy of C-3 demethylation and C-2 nitrogen-containing side chain introduction, which helps improve target adaptability and provides a reference for subsequent structural optimization.

We analyzed the changes in gene expression in HCC cells treated with the drugs individually and in combination using transcriptome sequencing. MAPK4 was found to be significantly differentially expressed in the combination group, suggesting that it may play a key regulatory role. MAPK4 is an atypical mitogen-activated protein kinase involved in cell proliferation and differentiation34. Recent studies have shown that MAPK4 is aberrantly expressed in various types of cancer and is associated with poor prognosis in patients with HCC, potentially promoting tumor progression by regulating pathways such as PI3K/AKT35. However, therapeutic strategies targeting MAPK4 remain unclear, and its role in hepatocellular carcinoma as well as its therapeutic potential require further investigation36. Our findings revealed that the combined treatment with TC1 and 2-DG significantly inhibited the MAPK4/AKT signaling pathway, while simultaneously inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular energy depletion. Given the critical regulatory role of the AKT signaling pathway in modulating mitochondrial metabolism, maintaining mitochondrial membrane potential stability and suppressing apoptosis37, we hypothesize that the combined treatment, by downregulating the MAPK4/AKT signaling, particularly inhibits AKT activities, thus leads to the failure of its downstream protective mechanisms on mitochondria. This, in turn, disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis manifested as decreased membrane potential, increased ROS production, and impaired energy synthesis, ultimately inducing apoptosis or cell death. Indeed, our results reveal that the anti-tumor effect of TC1 in combination with 2-DG is closely related to the regulation of the MAPK4/AKT signaling pathway. RT-qPCR results show that the expression levels of MAPK4 and its downstream effecter molecule Fos are both significantly downregulated in the combined treatment group, while the expression of the pro-apoptotic gene BAX is upregulated. Immunofluorescence staining results further confirm that the combination treatment significantly reduces the expression level of MAPK4 protein and inhibits the phosphorylation of AKT. AKT phosphorylation holds significant importance in tumor biology, metabolic diseases, and drug research. Recent studies have identified a close functional connection between MAPK4 and AKT38. Our findings demonstrate that 2-DG, Tan-IIA, and TC1 may each effectively downregulate the expression of MAPK4. However, a particularly noteworthy observation is that when TC1 is combined with 2-DG, the inhibitory effect on MAPK4 is significantly more pronounced than the sum of the effects observed in the individual monotherapy groups. This suggests that 2-DG, in combination with TC1, may exert its influence on MAPK4 through a synergistic mechanism. Furthermore, the regulatory impact on downstream signaling molecules, such as AKT, is also substantially enhanced beyond what is achievable with monotherapy. Additionally, neither 2-DG nor TC1 alone could activate the expression of BAX, while the combination of 2-DG and TC1 could significantly upregulate BAX expression. These findings suggest that TC1 in combination with 2-DG may exert their anti-tumor effects by synergistically inhibiting the MAPK4/AKT signaling pathway. To further elucidate the role of MAPK4, certain points are needed to be addressed. The first point is that we have not yet conducted research to determine whether overexpression of MAPK4 could reduce cell apoptosis and decrease the expression of BAX. Another point is that the research on the expression regulation of MAPK4 is not fully clear at present. Therefore, we may employ the Chromatin Immunoprecipitation-chip (ChIP-on-chip) and conduct structural analysis of the promoter sequences upstream of MAPK4 to identify potential transcription factors. Future research can draw on the review by Chen et al. on the molecular regulatory network of tumors to further explore the interaction between the MAPK4/AKT pathway and the tumor immune microenvironment, aiming to develop more effective combination therapy strategies39.
