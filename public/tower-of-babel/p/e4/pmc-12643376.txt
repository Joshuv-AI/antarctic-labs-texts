Convergent Genetic Architecture in Concurrent Anorectal Malformations and Hypospadias: The ESRRA-STARD8 Axis as a Key Regulator of Urogenital Development
Authors: Chandramouli Goswami, Jyoti Sharma, Rahila Sardar, Dinesh Gupta, Sourabh Kumar, Vishesh Jain, Anjan Kumar Dhua, Devendra Kumar Yadav, Harpreet Singh, Prabudh Goel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Anorectal malformations (ARM) and hypospadias are common congenital anomalies that occasionally co-occur, suggesting shared developmental pathways. Despite their clinical significance, the genetic etiology of combined ARM-hypospadias remains poorly understood.Objective:The objective of this study was to identify genetic variants underlying concurrent ARM and hypospadias using whole exome sequencing and elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving their co-occurrence.Materials and Methods:Six boys with concurrent ARM and hypospadias underwent whole exome sequencing. Variants were filtered using stringent bioinformatic criteria (Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) <0.05, Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) >20) and analyzed for pathogenicity using multiple prediction tools. Gene enrichment analysis, protein–protein interaction networks, and tissue expression profiles were evaluated. All patients had normal 46, XY karyotypes.Results:Five patients harbored a recurrent heterozygous missense variant in ESRRA (c.1162C>T; p.Leu388Phe), whereas the sixth patient carried a Hemizygous STARD8 variant (c.2950C>T; p.Leu984Phe). ESRRA encodes an orphan nuclear receptor regulating steroidogenesis, whereas STARD8 modulates Rho GTPase signaling and cytoskeletal dynamics. Pathway analysis revealed ESRRA enrichment in steroid hormone biosynthesis and oestrogen signaling, whereas STARD8 was enriched in epithelial morphogenesis and cell adhesion pathways. String analysis confirmed ESRRA’s predicted transcriptional regulation of STARD8. Both genes showed peak expression during critical urogenital development windows (8-14 weeks).Conclusion:This study suggests a potential ESRRA–STARD8 regulatory pathway contributing to the pathogenesis of concurrent ARM and hypospadias. The convergence of hormonal dysregulation (via ESRRA) and structural defects (via STARD8) provides a unified molecular mechanism for these complex malformations. These findings have immediate implications for genetic screening, counseling, and potentially personalized surgical management of affected children.

INTRODUCTION

The development of the urogenital system is a complex, tightly regulated process involving the coordinated action of multiple genetic and hormonal pathways. Disruptions in these developmental programs can result in congenital anomalies affecting the anorectal region and external genitalia. Anorectal malformations (ARM) occur in approximately 1 in 5000 live births, whereas hypospadias affects 1 in 250 male newborns, making it one of the most common congenital anomalies.[1234] The co-occurrence of ARM and hypospadias, though rare, suggests shared developmental pathways between the urogenital sinus and hindgut derivatives.[5]

Despite advances in surgical management, the genetic etiology of combined ARM-hypospadias remains poorly understood. The available research has suggested the role of isolated hypospadias genetic variants in various signaling pathways, including Wnt, BMP, and androgen signaling; however, a unifying genetic mechanism has remained elusive.[6] The complexity of urogenital development, involving both hormonal regulation and tissue morphogenesis, suggests that multiple genetic factors likely contribute to these phenotypes.

This study represents the first whole exome sequencing analysis focused specifically on patients with co-existing ARM and hypospadias. This was designed to identify the genetic markers that might explain the concurrent development of both ARM and hypospadias, providing mechanistic insights into the pathogenesis of these complex malformations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional, observational study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric Surgery, All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi over 2 years as Part of an MCh thesis in pediatric surgery after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (AIIMS IEC/2023/PSurg/143).

Boys with concurrent ARM and hypospadias were included in this study. Observation parameters included detailed clinical examination including phenotypic characterisation, karyotyping, and pedigree analysis including documentation of family history. ARMs were classified using Krickenbeck classification system, which considers the type and location of the fistula and associated abnormality. Hypospadias subtypes were established anatomically depending on the site of the ectopic meatus: distal penile, midpenile, or posterior.

DNA extraction and quality control

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood collected in EDTA tubes using Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following manufacturer’s protocols. DNA quality was assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometry (260/280 ratio) and Qubit fluorometry for quantification.[7] Whole exome sequencing was performed on Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads, achieving a minimum coverage of 100x using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V8 Kit for library preparation. Quality control metrics included a minimum Q30 score >80%, coverage uniformity >90%, and target capture efficiency >85% [Figure 1].[8]

The raw reads were quality checked using FastQC v0.11.9,[9] adapter trimming performed with Trimmomatic v0.39[10] and aligned with the reference human genome (GRCh38/hg38) with the BWA MEM algorithm.[11] PCR duplicates were removed using the Picard tool[12] following base quality score recalibration using GATK v4.2.0, variant calling with GATK HaplotypeCaller following GATK best practices,[13] and annotation was carried out using ANNOVAR[14]

Data analysis and assessment of variants

Data were analyzed and filtered to exclude (i) false calls (only those SNVs with read depth ≥10× were preserved), (ii) benign SNVs (ACMG), (iii) intronic SNVs, (iv) synonymous SNVs, and (v) those with minor allele frequency ≥0.05 (prevalence >5%) population databases gnomAD.[15] SNVs common to the entire study cohort were prioritized and genotype–phenotype correlation was established.

In silico analysis was conducted SIFT2 (score <0.05 was considered deleterious),[16] PolyPhen-2 (score >0.85 was considered probably damaging)[17] and REVEL (score >0.5 was considered probably pathogenic).[18] A CADD score >20 was considered deleterious.

Short-listed SNVs were confirmed with validated through visual inspected with Integrated Genome Viewer[19] and Sanger sequencing with custom-designed primers.

Gene set enrichment and pathway analysis

Gene-level functional enrichment was performed using g: Profiler,[20] Enrichr,[21] and DAVID Bioinformatics Resources.[22] Enriched pathways were identified from curated databases such as KEGG,[23] Reactome,[24] and Gene Ontology (GO).[25] ESRRA and STARD8 were analyzed for enrichment in developmental, hormonal, and cytoskeletal pathways relevant to genital morphogenesis. Tissue-specific expression profiles were extracted from GTEx[26] and Human Protein Atlas[27] to validate spatial relevance to the urogenital tract.

Gene and protein interaction analysis

Gene and protein interaction networks were explored with the STRING v11.5 database[28] with medium to high confidence thresholds (interaction score >0.7). Additional curated interactions were cross-referenced from BioGRID[29] and GeneCards.[30]

RESULTS

Clinical details

The study cohort comprised of six participants. The mean age of participants at the time of enrolment was 6.83 ± 1.77 years. All six participants presented with high ARM with congenital pouch colon (CPC) Type IV (n = 1), rectovesical fistula (n = 2, including one participant with CPC), rectoprostatic urethral fistula (n = 2), and rectobulbar urethral fistula (n = 2). All patients presented with hypospadias concurrently which was posterior (n = 1), mid-penile (n = 4), and distal penile (n = 1). Other associated anomalies included crossed fused renal ectopia (n = 1), malrotated right kidney (n = 1), vesico-ureteric reflux (n = 2), sacral agenesis (n = 1), and cryptorchidism (n = 2 which was impalpable in 1 and bilateral in the other) [Figure 1].

All patients exhibited a normal 46, XY karyotype and none of the patient had reported a positive family history for similar or related conditions.

Variant details

The results of whole exome sequencing analysis are tabulated in Table 1. No single genetic marker common to the six participants in the study cohort was identified. A single, recurrent, heterozygous, missense variant in the ESRRA (11q13.1) gene (c.1162C>T; p.Leu388Phe) was present in five out of six participants. No variant pertaining to the ESRRA was identified in the sixth participant. However, a hemizygous missense variant (c.2950C>T: P.Leu984Phe) in the STARD8 (Xq13.1) gene was peculiar in the sixth patient. The STARD8 is transcriptionally regulated by ESRRA, suggesting a hierarchical interaction wherein STARD8 serves as a downstream effector of ESRRA-mediated steroidogenesis.

Silico pathogenicity assessment

The variant in ESRRA was located within the ligand-binding domain (exon 7) and predicted to be deleterious by multiple in silico tools SIFT (score: 0.001), PolyPhen-2 (score: 0.998), REVEL (score: 0.89), and had a high CADD score of 24.2 [Figure 2]. This single nucleotide variant (SNV) is highly conserved across species (PhyloP: 5.2) and was not reported in the East Asian subpopulation of the gnomAD database. Its global allele frequency was estimated at 4.5 × 10-5 with no homozygous individuals reported. The STARD8 variant is located within the StAR-related lipid transfer domain and was predicted to be of uncertain significance based on SIFT (score: 0.08), PolyPhen-2 (score: 0.75), and REVEL (score: 0.48), with a CADD score of 22.1. It is extremely rare, with a global allele frequency of 8.0 × 10-6 and was not observed in the hemizygous state in healthy males [Figure 2 and Table 2].

Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analysis

ESRRA is significantly associated with key developmental and hormonal pathways relevant to urogenital differentiation. Specifically, ESRRA was enriched in pathways involved in steroid hormone biosynthesis (Reactome: R-has-193144), the estrogen signaling pathway (KEGhashsa04915), and the regulation of mitochondrial metabolism and transcription (GO: 0006355, GO: 0005739), underscoring its role in coordinating metabolic and endocrine signaling during sexual development.

STARD8 showed enrichment in pathways related to Rho GTPase-mediated signaling and cytoskeletal dynamics (GO: 0051056, Reactohas R-HSA-194840), as well as epithelial morphogenesis and adherens junction assembly (GO: 0034330, GO: 0005912). Convergent pathways involving hormonal regulation (ESRRA) and structural organization (STARD8) drive urethral and genital development, and their dysregulation contributes to hypospadias.

Expression profiling and tissue specificity

Both ESRRA and STARD8 have moderate to high expression in foetal gonads, adrenal glands, urethra, and cloacal tissue, based on GTEx (TPM in testis: 45.2) and Human Protein Atlas datasets. ESRRA expression peaks at 8-14 weeks of gestation, coinciding with critical windows of genital tubercle and urethral fold development. STARD8 expression overlaps with urethral fold fusion (weeks 11–14) [Figure 3].

Gene and protein interaction analysis

STRING analysis shows a possible transcriptional regulatory relationship between ESRRA and STARD8, with ESRRA predicted to bind to the promoter region of STARD8, suggesting that STARD8 functions as a downstream effector within the ESRRA-regulated network. Further interaction mapping demonstrated that ESRRA forms a dense regulatory hub with key genes involved in steroidogenesis, including NR5A1, CYP19A1, and HSD17B3, all of which are critical for androgen and oestrogen biosynthesis during urogenital development [Figure 4]. The STARD8 was found to interact with core components of the RhoA signaling pathway RHOA, ROCK1, and PTPN13 constituting a cytoskeletal remodeling module essential for maintaining epithelial integrity and facilitating urethral seam fusion. These interaction patterns suggest a convergence of hormonal regulation and structural morphogenesis through a putative ESRRA–STARD8 pathway, offering mechanistic insight into the pathogenesis of hypospadias [Figure 5].

DISCUSSION

The study represents the first whole exome sequencing investigation specifically focused on patients with coexisting ARMs and hypospadias, revealing a novel genetic axis involving ESRRA and its downstream target STARD8. The identification of a recurrent heterozygous missense variant in ESRRA (p.Leu388Phe) in five of six patients, with the sixth patient harboring a hemizygous STARD8 variant (p. Leu984Phe), support possible evidence for a unified pathogenic mechanism underlying these complex urogenital anomalies.

ESRRA encodes a member of the orphan nuclear receptor family that functions as a master regulator of metabolic and hormonal pathways.[31] ESRRA plays a crucial role in steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, processes essential for proper masculinization of the external genitalia.[32] The p.Leu388Phe variant identified in the study cohort affects the ligand-binding domain of ESRRA, potentially altering the receptor’s transcriptional activity. This disruption is particularly significant given ESRRA’s dual role: It governs mitochondrial energy metabolism through coactivators like PGC-1α while concurrently modulating developmental transcriptional networks.[33]

The temporal expression pattern of ESRRA provides crucial insights into its pathogenic role. Peak expression occurs at 8–14 weeks of gestation, coinciding precisely with critical developmental events such as cloacal septation (7–9 weeks), genital tubercle development (8–12 weeks), urethral fold fusion (11–14 weeks), and external anal sphincter formation (9–13 weeks). This temporal overlap explains how disruption of ESRRA function can lead to both anorectal and urethral malformations, as both structures derive from the urogenital sinus and share common developmental pathways.

Furthermore, network analysis revealed ESRRA’s connections with key steroidogenic enzymes (CYP19A1, HSD) and transcription factors (NR5A1/SF-1), all of which have established roles in disorders of sex development. The enrichment of ESRRA in oestrogen signaling (KEGG: Hsa04915) and steroid biosynthesis pathways (Reactome: R-HSA-193144) aligns with the known importance of hormonal balance during urethral development.

STARD8, also known as DLC3, encodes a Rho GTPase-activating protein (RhoGAP) that regulates cytoskeletal remodeling, cell polarity, and epithelial junction integrity.[34] While regulated by ESRRA, STARD8 serves distinct functions critical for urogenital morphogenesis.

The enrichment of STARD8 in pathways related to Rho GTPase-mediated signaling (GO: 0051056), epithelial morphogenesis (GO: 0034330), and adherens junction assembly (GO: 0005912) provides mechanistic insight into the structural aspects of urethral malformations. Proper urethral development requires coordinated epithelial cell migration, adhesion, and tubularization—processes directly influenced by Rho signaling.

The p.Leu984Phe variant in STARD8, located within the StAR-related lipid transfer domain, likely disrupts the protein’s ability to modulate Rho GTPase activity. This impairment could affect epithelial sheet movements and cell–cell adhesion during urethral seam formation, contributing to the hypospadias phenotype. The involvement of STARD8 interactors (RHOA, ROCK1, and PTPN13) further supports this structural mechanism. Previous studies have also implicated STARD8 mutations in disorders of sex development, including 46, XY gonadal dysgenesis,[35] supporting our findings.

The putative ESRRA–STARD8 regulatory pathway

The current findings suggest a potential hierarchical relationship between ESRRA and STARD8, with ESRRA predicted to bind to the promoter region of STARD8. This suggests that STARD8 functions as a downstream effector within the ESRRA-regulated network. STRING analysis (confidence score >0.7) supports this direct transcriptional regulatory relationship, although experimental validation is needed. The genetic convergence observed in this study culminating in similar phenotypes due to disruption at either level supports a model with dual mechanisms. First, ESRRA variants disrupt steroid hormone pathways (hormonal dysregulation), affecting endocrine signals necessary for genital masculinization and urethral closure. Second, STARD8 variants (primary or secondary to ESRRA dysfunction) impair cytoskeletal dynamics and epithelial morphogenesis, resulting in structural defects. These dual mechanisms explain the complex phenotype observed and highlight why disruption at either level of the ESRRA-STARD8 axis can produce convergent clinical outcomes. The functional implications of the discovered mutations have not yet been confirmed in experimental models, even though in silico predictions, expression profiles, and pathway studies suggest the possible role of ESRRA and STARD8 in urogenital development. To prove causation, the suggested ESRRA-STARD8 connection needs to be investigated both in vitro and in vivo using techniques including promoter binding assays, CRISPR-based modeling, and transcriptome perturbation analysis. The gene–phenotype association should be regarded as biologically probable but not yet definitively confirmed until such investigations are conducted.

The phenotypic heterogeneity in our cohort, varying from posterior to distal penile hypospadias and the spectrum of high ARM subtypes, likely reflects the complex genetic architecture of these malformations. It was not possible to ascertain the de novo status or inheritance patterns of the discovered variations in this investigation due to the lack of parental samples for segregation analysis. The likelihood of X-linked inheritance is increased when a male patient’s STARD8 variation is hemizygous, especially when there is no family history. An autosomal dominant mechanism or recurring de novo mutation, on the other hand, may be the cause of the recurrent heterozygous ESRRA variation seen in five unrelated patients. To clarify the origin, inheritance, and penetrance of variants in these situations, future trio-based exome sequencing research will be essential. While ESRRA/STARD8 variants appear to be major contributing factors, the high prevalence of associated malformations (cryptorchidism, renal anomalies, and sacral agenesis) suggests potential modifier genes or environmental factors. The absence of positive family history, despite the genetic basis, indicates potential de novo variants or complex inheritance patterns. The heterozygous nature of the ESRRA variant suggests dominant-negative effects or haploinsufficiency, whereas the hemizygous STARD8 variant implies X-linked inheritance at this locus.[36]

The implication of the ESRRA-STARD8 axis has several practical implications for current clinical practice and future research. Genetic testing for children presenting with both ARM and hypospadias can provide valuable prognostic information and help estimate recurrence risks for subsequent pregnancies. Detection of these genetic variants enables more informed reproductive counseling, allowing families to make educated decisions regarding future pregnancies and consider options for prenatal diagnosis. Furthermore, establishing the association between specific genetic profiles and phenotypic severity or other associated malformations could guide individualized surgical strategies and optimal timing of interventions. Future research, elucidating the ESRRA-STARD8 regulatory network, may create opportunities for novel therapeutic strategies such as targeted therapy, hormonal or otherwise,[37] pharmacological modulation to restore the function of ESRRA,[38] or corrective gene therapy.[39]

The present investigation establishes several key methodological and analytical foundations for understanding the genetic basis of concurrent ARM and hypospadias through rigorous bioinformatic filtering, orthogonal validation through Sanger sequencing, and comprehensive pathway analysis. The study has demonstrated technically sound genetic variants within a biologically coherent regulatory network. The consistent involvement of the ESRRA-STARD8 pathway across the entire patient cohort, whether through direct ESRRA variants or downstream STARD8 disruption, strengthens the hypothesis of a unified pathogenic mechanism. However, while the convergent evidence from computational predictions, evolutionary conservation, and pathway enrichment analyses supports the pathogenicity of these variants, the definitive causal relationship between these specific genetic alterations and the observed developmental malformations awaits direct experimental validation through functional assays and mechanistic studies. Furthermore, the mechanisms underlying phenotypic variability and the role of potential modifier genes remain to be elucidated.

CONCLUSIONS

This study proposes the role of the ESRRA-STARD8 regulatory axis as a key determinant in anogenital development and implicates it in the pathogenesis of concurrent ARM and hypospadias. The convergence of hormonal regulation and structural morphogenesis pathways provides a comprehensive framework for understanding these complex malformations. The findings not only advance the molecular understanding of urogenital development but also offer potential clinical applications in genetic diagnosis and counseling for affected families.

The discovery of this novel genetic mechanism represents a significant step toward personalized medicine approaches in paediatric urology and colorectal surgery.
