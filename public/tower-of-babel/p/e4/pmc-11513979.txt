Goblet cell differentiation subgroups in colorectal cancer
Authors: Gulnar Abdullayeva, Haoyu Liu, Ta-Chun Liu, Alison Simmons, Marco Novelli, Irada Huseynova, Viorica L. Lastun, Walter Bodmer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe gut has three types of mature nondividing differentiated cells. One of these, goblet cells, lines the gut with mucus. These cells are replaced every 3 to 6 d by dividing stem cells. Colorectal cancers (CRC) arise when genetic changes in the stem cells give them a growth advantage over the unchanged stem cells. This changes the balance of differentiation toward cell growth. Using cancer-derived cell lines, we have defined five different categories of goblet cell presence in CRCs from nearly normal to completely absent. One category makes no mucus but only a protein that normally supports the mucus. These findings give further insight into the factors that drive the progression of cancers.

The poor prognosis of relatively undifferentiated adenocarcinomas, including colorectal cancers (CRCs), has long been recognized (1, 2). This strongly suggests that selection against differentiation and in favor of uncontrolled growth is one of the most powerful drivers of cancer progression. Strangely, this has hardly been recognized in overviews of the “Hallmarks of Cancer” such as the very widely quoted papers of Hanahan and Weinberg (3, 4).

An early example of the identification of a specific stable genetic change underlying the control of differentiation in CRCs came through the discovery that a subset of CRC-derived cell lines formed crypt-like lumens in three-dimensional matrigel culture (5, 6). This differentiation was shown to be controlled by the transcription factor CDX1 so that stable methylation of the promoter region of CDX1 switching off its expression was presumably a driving factor for selection against enterocyte differentiation in CRC (7–9). These studies had also shown the clear presence of goblet cells, identified by the presence of MUC2, the dominant mucin in the normal colon and rectum, in a subset of cell lines.

Goblet cells in CRC have been recognized by pathologists in FFPE slide sections based on their characteristic morphology and the presence of mucin. CRCs with goblet cells have thus been characterized as a distinct category of CRC called mucinous (10, 11).

There have been many studies on the molecular control of goblet cell differentiation, which, however, have mostly not focused on the relationship to mucinous cancers. The molecular studies have shown the importance of Notch controlled factors, including transcription factor HES1, which, when down-regulated leads to the expression of ATOH1 and SPDEF (12–15). Methylation of the promoter region of ATOH1 has been suggested as the primary control for switching off ATOH1 expression and so blocking goblet cell differentiation (12, 15, 16). Transcription factor CDX2, rather than CDX1, has also been suggested as critical for the control of the secretory lineage of goblet and enteroendocrine cells in the colon and rectum (13, 17). Further implicated transcription factors in the control of goblet cell differentiation include GATA6 and ETS2 (18–21).

Several proteins have been shown to be closely associated with MUC2, and so involved in the ultimate formation of mature mucus. Of these, the most interesting is the relatively small, secreted protein, TFF3, which was originally described as a factor helping the restitution of damaged colon (22, 23). It is now clear that in addition to this growth factor-like activity, TFF3 also binds to mucus by noncovalent attachment to a specific sugar helping the formation of mature mucus (24–26).

Our studies on CRC are based on a relatively large panel of about 80 CRC-derived cell lines nearly all fully characterized with respect to whole genome gene expression levels and key driver mutations (27–30). On the basis that mucinous may only be a partial description of goblet cell characteristics of CRC, we have undertaken a careful analysis of our complete cell line panel, mainly by immunofluorescence of MUC2 and TFF3 protein expression, coupled with transcription levels of MUC2, TFF3, and the most commonly associated transcription factors, in addition to certain other genes. Preliminary results clearly suggested a more complex characterization of CRCs based on MUC2 and TFF3 expression levels than simply mucinous versus not (31). MUC2 isolated cells from cell lines, if they were also TFF3 expressing, showed patterns of expression of other genes that have been associated with goblet cells (12–21). We assume, therefore, that it is highly probable that cells expressing both MUC2 and TFF3 are goblet cells, while accepting the possibility that there may be other subsets of goblet-like cells expressing different combinations of genes that do not exactly correspond to normal goblet cells. Our analysis of goblet cells is based only on the patterns of expression of MUC2 and TFF3. More detailed studies on the whole panel of cell lines have revealed five categories of CRCs with respect to MUC2 and TFF3 expression, including the unexpectedly large subset of MUC2− TFF3+ cancers, and shown that these categories can readily be identified in direct samples of patient tumors on FFPE slides.

The data show clear bimodality for both MUC2 and TFF3 with the norm being high expression. The relationship between MUC2 and TFF3 expression, given by the table shown in Fig. 1B, clearly indicates a strong inclusive association with hardly any MUC2+TFF3− cell lines and an unexpectedly high level of MUC2−TFF3+ lines. The similar analyses of the MUC2 and TFF3 expression levels for the TCGA data are shown in Fig. 1C. The blue hatched histogram is for the subset of normal tissues that are included in the TCGA data. The distributions reflect the relatively high proportion, perhaps as much as 30%, of normal cells included in the TCGA tumor samples. The relationship between MUC2 and TFF3 expression given in the table in Fig. 1D shows a similar highly significant inclusion association between MUC2 and TFF3 but with a large excess of MUC2+TFF3+ due to the presence of normal tissue. In each case, it is probable that the MUC2+TFF3− cases are false negative classifications for TFF3 because of the statistical difficulty in separating low level from negative expression. The TCGA data clearly show the presence of a relatively high proportion of MUC2−TFF3+ cancers (27.5%) just as in the cell lines, which, however, have a somewhat higher proportion of the MUC2− TFF3+ cell lines (39.2%). This would be expected because the cell lines, in general, are biased toward more aggressive cancers, which are less differentiated, and so have higher proportions of completely goblet cell–negative cancers.

Categorization of Cell Lines by IF (Immunofluorescence) Analysis of MUC2 and TFF3 Protein Expression.

The IF expression of MUC2, TFF3, and EpCAM (the most widely expressed protein in nearly all epithelial cells) in cell line LS180, using confocal microscopy at 100× magnification, is shown in Fig. 2A. The overlapping pattern of MUC2 and TFF3 expression, as is seen in all normal colonic epithelial cells, is clearly evident together with the fact that in this cell line, which is clearly positive for both MUC2 and TFF3 mRNA expression, only a subset of cells express both MUC2 and TFF3 at the protein level. Fig. 2B shows MUC2 and TFF3 expression in three cell lines whose overlapping MUC2 and TFF3 protein expression is found in a high proportion of all the cells in each of these cell lines. Once we proceeded with the characterization of the cell lines with respect to their MUC2 and TFF3 expression by IF it became clear that they fell into five groups defined by the expression profiles represented by the proportions of MUC2 and TFF3 expressing cells in a cell line, as shown in the table in Fig. 2C. Two groups, respectively, 1 and 5, had either between 16% and 100% cells MUC2 and TFF3 or no cells positive to either MUC2 or TFF3. Of the remaining groups, group 2 had between an average of 2.1% and 7.3% cells staining positive for both MUC2 and TFF3, while group 3 had only an average of between 0.5% and 1.4% staining for both MUC2 and TFF3. Group 4 had all cells, or very nearly 100% cells, staining positive for TFF3 and only 4/27 lines staining for very few MUC2 cells (0.4 to 2.9%). (see Fig. 3 for detailed numbers). The staining of 3 representative cell lines from each of the groups 2 to 5 is shown in Fig. 2. Nearly all the cells staining positive for MUC2 in groups 1 to 3 were also positive for TFF3. The white arrows indicate the occasional exceptions to this, mainly single cells expressing TFF3 and not MUC2. Single-cell mRNA analysis of the group 2 cell line LS180 showed the same phenomenon (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Analysis over time of clones derived from single cells of LS180 showed that cells expressing TFF3 appeared before MUC2 expression suggesting that these MUC2− TFF3+ single cells were incompletely differentiated goblet cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

The cell line expression levels of the 7 transcription factors most associated with goblet cell differentiation (ATOH1, SPDEF, CDX1, CDX2, GATA6, HES1, ETS2) were classified as high (yellow) or low (blue) using GMMchi analysis. The most unexpected of these five groups is group 4, in which essentially all the cells stain for TFF3, and at most a very few cells in some of the cell lines stain for MUC2. This is the category of cancers that was predicted by the mRNA level analysis.

The Transcription Factor mRNA Expression in the 5 groups of Cell Lines Defined by MUC2 and TFF3 Protein Expression Patterns.

Transcription levels for seven of the transcription factors most commonly associated with the control of goblet cell differentiation (12–21) were classified as high (+) versus absent or low (−) using GMMchi (32) analysis for each factor on each cell line. Fig. 3 shows these expression levels in all the cell lines grouped into their five categories by the MUC2 and TFF3 IF analysis. The color patterns of blue for negative and yellow for positive show very clearly the nearly complete absence of ATOH1 and SPDEF expression in the group 4 (MUC2−TFF3+) and group 5 (MUC-TFF3−) cell lines. This aligns with the published data that clearly indicates ATOH1 expression is most closely linked to the control of MUC2 expression and a close association between ATOH1 and SPDEF expression (16). The higher proportion of cell lines negative for CDX1, CDX2, and GATA6 in group 5 than in group 4 cell lines suggests a continuing residual function of these three transcription factors in the group 4 lines in spite of their lack of MUC2 expression. The considerable lack of expression of ETS2 in group 5 lines, and to a lesser extent group 4 lines, is quite striking, especially given that nearly all of the groups 1 to 3 MUC2− and TFF3-positive cell lines are positive for all 5 transcription factors other than ATOH1 and SPDEF. The overall data are consistent with a key role for ATOH1 expression in the control of MUC2 expression but do not explain the differences between these three groups, especially between group 1 with very high expression of MUC2 and TFF3 compared with groups 2 and 3.

To confirm the role of the ATOH1, SPDEF, and CDX1/CDX2 transcription factors in controlling MUC2 and TFF3 expression, we used siRNA technology to knock down their activities in a subset of cell lines. Fig. 4A shows the effect of ATOH1 and SPDEF knock down on the number of cells staining +ve for MUC2 and TFF3 for the group 2 line LS180. ATOH1 clearly has a stronger effect than SPDEF with some suggestion of an increased effect of double knock down. Similar results are shown for three other cell lines, RW7213 from group 1, with a substantially higher level of MUC2 and TFF3 expression, while SW1222 and LOVO are from group 3 with very much lower levels of MUC2 and TFF3 expression (Fig. 4B).

The effects of increases in ATOH1 and SPDEF expression following transfection by cDNA carrying plasmids were investigated in the group 2 cell line, LS180 (Fig. 4C) and for just ATOH1 in three completely negative cell lines with varying levels of expression of some of the transcription factors (Fig. 4D). As might be expected from the knockdown results, an increase of either ATOH1 or SPDEF resulted in an increase of both MUC2 and TFF3 expression in LS180. However, an increase of only ATOH1 expression in the group 5 cell lines had no effect, indicating that more than one factor knockout was needed to achieve complete absence of MUC2 and TFF3 expression, and so of any representation of goblet cells in such cancers. Perhaps ETS2 reduced expression could play a role in this.

Results of siRNA knockdown of transcription factors CDX1 and CDX2 together with ATOH1 in some cell lines are shown in Fig. 5. The results shown in Figs. 5 A and C indicate that, on its own, CDX2 has a larger effect on MUC2 and TFF3 expression than CDX1, as might be expected from published evidence for the role of CDX2 in the secretory lineage of differentiation in the colon and rectum (17). Combining knock down of CDX1 with CDX2 has little, if any, extra effect as might be expected from their closely similar functions at the molecular level. The CDX2 effect is, however, clearly less than ATOH1 on its own. The effect of CDX2 knock down on TFF3 expression alone in the group 4 cell line SNUC2B (Fig. 5C) is clear evidence that TFF3 expression on its own seems to be under the same control as MUC2. The effects of ATOH1, CDX1, and CDX2 knock down on mRNA expression of each, for cell line LS180, are shown in Fig. 5B. Knockdown of CDX2 has a major downward effect on the expression of ATOH1 and, though to a lesser extent, the same is true in reverse. Most striking, however, is the effect of CDX1 knockdown on the increase of ATOH1 and CDX2 mRNA expression.

Methylation Effects on MUC2 and TFF3 Expression Levels and on Goblet Cell–related Tanscription Factor Levels of expression.

As already mentioned, there is substantial prior evidence for the role of DNA methylation in the control of ATOH1 and MUC2 mRNA expression in CRCs (33). Since the general control of normal cellular differentiation is largely dependent on DNA methylation of CpG islands, it is to be expected that stable methylation, in other words, epigenetic changes in their true sense, could play a similar role to DNA-based mutations in the somatic evolutionary progression of cancers. As a preliminary approach to the study of the effects of methylation on MUC2, TFF3, and other expressions in our cell lines we investigated the effects of the methylation inhibitor 5-Aza-2′-deoxycytidine (5AZA-2’) on these expressions.

The effect of 5AΖΑ-2’ treatment on the number of cells testing positive by IF in the cell line LS180 is shown in Fig. 6A. Perhaps surprisingly, there was a considerable reduction in MUC2 and TFF3 expression. This is explained in Fig. 6B by the substantial increase in HES1 expression and consequent reduction in ATOH1 expression, which, through Notch activity, is well known to be a primary control of goblet cell differentiation. The effect of demethylation by 5AZA-2’ on MUC2 and TFF3 expression is clearly confirmed by Fig. 6C. This shows the gradual decrease in MUC2 and TFF3 expressing cells during treatment with 5AZA-2’ and its increase with recovery after removal of 5AZA-2’. The exact opposite effect is seen with the group 4 MUC-TFF3+ cell line COLO205 showing an increase in MUC2 expression with treatment and a decrease during recovery (Fig. 6D), but no effect on TFF3 expression suggesting that in this case HES1 is not involved. This suggests that the transition from groups 1, 2, or 3 to group 4 could simply be due to methylation of the MUC2 promoter region, which is consistent with the results of Okudaira et al. (33) that are based on methylation DNA sequence analysis of the MUC2 promoter region.

The DBZ (dibenzazepine) γ-secretase inhibitor has been widely used as a notch inhibitor that decreases the expression of HES1, and so increases ATOH1 expression and goblet cell differentiation (34). Treatment of the group 2 cell line LS180 with DBZ very significantly increased the expression of both MUC2 and TFF3 (Fig. 7A) and decreased the expression of HES1 resulting in the increased the expression of ATOH1 (Fig. 7B). This clearly suggests that the relatively low expression of ATOH1 in LS180 may account for its lower expression of both MUC2 and TFF3 than is found in the group 1 cell lines. Treatment of the group 4 cell line SKCO1 (Fig. 7C) or group 5 cell lines (Fig. 7C, DLD1 OXCO3 and COLO678) with DBZ had no effect on the expression of either MUC2 or TFF3 showing that the single change in ATOH1 expression is not alone enough to increase MUC2 and TFF3 expression in these other cell lines. The positive effect of demethylation of MUC2 by 5AΖΑ-2’ treatment on the expression of MUC2 in the group 4 MUC-TFF3+ cell line COLO205 (Fig. 6D) suggests that it is probably at least the methylation of the promoters of both MUC2 and ATOH1 that is required for the transition of any of groups 1, 2, or 3 to group 4.

Search for Other Factors Affecting MUC2 and TFF3 Expression: A Possible Role for TOX3, LGR5, and OFLM4.

A search for differences between the expression profiles of the 5 groups of cell lines, specifically between groups 1, 2, and 3 together versus 4, and 1, 2, 3, and 4 together versus group 5, revealed one possible candidate transcription factor, TOX3, and two other genes, OLFM4 and LGR5, as candidates for contributing to the control of differences between cell lines that had some features of goblet cell differentiation and those that did not. Volcano plots for groups 1, 2, and 3 versus 4 and groups 1, 2, 3, and 4 versus 5 are shown in Figs. 8 A and B. GMMchi analysis of the cell lines for genes OLFM4, TOX3, and LGR5 are shown in Figs. 8 C–E, respectively. The choice of candidate genes was based on a combination of a high level of difference and a small p-value, of interest because of their known function and because they are not known to be involved in the structural maintenance of mucus.

OLFM4 stood out in the difference between groups 1, 2, and 3 versus group 4, suggesting a role in the control of MUC2 expression. This is consistent with the recent report of a role for OLFM4 in colorectal differentiation rather than its direct control of stem cells (35). TOX3 (High Mobility Group Box Family Member 3) is involved in the control of alteration of chromatin structure and association with transcription factor complexes, also suggesting a role in the control of differentiation mediated by promoter methylation (36, 37).

The strong correlation between the completely goblet cell negative group 5 and low or absent expression of LGR5 (Fig. 8E) was completely unexpected. LGR5 is well known to be a key marker of stem cells in the normal colon (38). However, it is not expressed in transit-amplifying cells and certainly not in all CRCs or cell lines derived from them, suggesting its role is not directly connected with the control of growth through cell division. The expression of LGR5 in differentiating cell lines suggests a role in the control of differentiation rather than growth, as suggested by Su et al. (39). They showed that a subset of CRC cell lines that did not express LGR5, including HCT116, had methylation of the LGR5 promoter and did not differentiate, consistent with our classification of them in group 5. They then showed that transfection of HCT116 with LGR5-specific siRNA led to the re-expression of KRT20, a sign of differentiation (8). This suggests that the role of LGR5 in colonic stem cells is to prime them for differentiation as they move toward becoming transit-amplifying cells and, therefore, that its lack of expression may be a contributing factor to group 5 cell lines being completely goblet cell negative.

MUC2 expression in lumens from both cell lines LS180 and RW7213 showed goblet cell–like differentiation only from certain segments of the lumens (Fig. 9 A and B) and, as shown in the table in Fig. 9C, there was no significant association between lumen formation and goblet cell markers in the cell lines.

Blocking of MUC2 or TFF3 secretion was another possible basis for finding genetic or epigenetic changes that influence cancer progression. The cell lines were therefore screened for blockage by measuring the amount of MUC2 and TFF3 in culture supernatants by western blotting and use of the anti-MUC2 and TFF3 antibodies. Out of about 50 cell lines tested only 2 were clearly nonsecreting for MUC2 and these were from the lowest expressing group, 3. No clearly nonsecreting TFF3 cell lines were found among 29 tested (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Goblet Cell Subclass Validation in Primary CRC Sections.

To validate the classification into 5 groups of goblet cell features, represented by the 5 MUC2 and TFF3 expression level profiles found in the CRC-derived cell lines, tissue sections from primary CRCs were analyzed using the same IF analysis of MUC2 and TFF3 expression as used for the cell line analysis. Staining of an FFPE section from normal colon tissue by IF for MUC2 and TFF3, using CellBrite to delineate cellular margins, together with a parallel H&E stained section, is shown in Fig. 10A. This clearly demonstrates the expected complete overlap between MUC2 and TFF3 staining in the goblet cells along the normal crypts.

H&E and immunofluorescence images of healthy colon sections sourced from the VICTOR trial. H&E staining and immunofluorescence staining of MUC2 (PR5D5, 1:250), TFF3 (1:1000), DAPI (1:10000), and CellBrite (1:2000) in a healthy colon tissue section.

The same staining was then carried out on 21 FFPE sections from the “The Vioxx in Colorectal Therapy: Definition of Optimal Regime (VICTOR)” study (40). It was immediately apparent that the 5 different expression levels of MUC2 and TFF3 could be identified in the CRC sections, but that in many of the cancers, two or more of the expression level groups were present. This is as might be expected from the nature of the cancer evolutionary progression being based on stepwise selection for driver changes due to gene mutations or stable epigenetic expression changes in which a new arm of the evolutionary tree does not simply replace its precursor. Cell lines obtained from cancers are nearly always clonal, representing just one of what may be several different clones of this evolutionary progression. Thus, each pattern of MUC2 and TFF3 expression in cancer represents one evolutionary clone with respect to goblet cell features essentially corresponding to one of the 5 cell line groups. Examples of such patterns of staining are given in Fig. 11. These included areas in different single tumors with, for example, three groups, 1, 4, and 5 or 3, 4, and 5; with two groups 1 and 2 or 1 and 4; and with only 1 group, 4 or only 5. The grouping based on the MUC2 and TFF3 expression profiling as in Fig. 2C was clear-cut and based on exactly the same criteria used for the cell line classification given in Fig. 2C and Fig. 3. Normal areas on the slides were readily recognized by the staining pattern as illustrated in Fig. 10.

Relating these data to the standard pathology classification would suggest that group 1 cancers would be mucinous and most differentiated, while groups 4 and 5 not mucinous and group 5 least differentiated. However, the heterogeneity within the tumors, where, for example, a tumor classified as dominated by group 5 may still carry group 1, 2, or 3 features could easily be classified as still somewhat differentiated. In addition, given the lack of association between goblet cell differentiation and enterocyte differentiation, as measured in the cell lines by lumen formation, group 5 nonmucinous and nongoblet cell differentiated tumors may still have features of enterocyte differentiation. The fact that the H&E slides, on the basis of which the pathology assessment was made, were different from those used for the group classification and so possibly came from a different part of the tumor, also does not help.

The necessarily limited data on mutations in some of the known key drivers of CRC progression do not suggest any correlations with goblet cell differentiation (SI Appendix, Table S3).

With respect to tumor aggressiveness, the expectation would be that group 5 is most aggressive, group 4 moderately aggressive, and group 1 least so. The prediction for, say, group 1 and 5 tumors would be to progress from possibly having been nearly all group 1 to becoming all group 5, with a poorer prognosis. Similarly, group 4 and 5 tumors might also progress to all group 5. Each step from one group to another is presumably due to selection for a new mutation or epigenetic change leading to a less differentiated state, and finding out what that step might be is the question the molecular analysis of the different groups aimed to answer.

Discussion

The classification of human colorectal cancer into combinations of 5 groups of MUC2 and TFF3 expression levels, based on the extent of goblet cell differentiation using an analysis of CRC-derived cell lines, has clearly been validated by recognizing these groups in tissue sections from human primary CRCs. The most striking feature of this grouping is the existence of an approximately 30% subgroup of CRCs that express TFF3 but not MUC2, the defining mucin of colorectal tissue. TFF3 has been well recognized as a key factor for the restitution of damaged colon in addition to its role and contribution to the stabilization of mature mucus. Its role as a stimulation factor for growth suggests that blocking TFF3 function (41, 42) may be a useful contribution to the treatment of TFF3+ MUC2− CRCs. This alone would justify exploring whether the CRC grouping using IF staining for MUC2 and TFF3 could be identified by AI training of standard H&E labeled tissue sections (43).

It is now generally recognized that cancer is a somatic evolutionary process driven by stepwise selection for discrete genetic mutations or stable epigenetic changes and that it is these changes, or immediate consequences from the changes, that are markers for the detection of the progress of cancer and are potential druggable targets. Selection against differentiation, such as to goblet cells in the colorectum, is an obvious target for the evolutionary progression of cancer and so the steps leading to loss of goblet cell features, as described in our grouping, are clearly potential candidates for being driver genetic and epigenetic changes for CRC.

Based on extensive published data on goblet cell differentiation in the colorectum and on our analysis of differences in expression between the groups of cell lines, as well as knockout and knock in experiments, we suggest that the following genes are the most likely candidates for being drivers: MUC2, TFF3, ATOH1, SPDEF, CDX1, CDX2, GATA6, HES1, ETS2, OLFM4, TOX3, and LGR5. None of these genes have significant numbers of mutations in CRC any more than they have, for example, in breast cancers. Thus, if they are involved as drivers, it must be through stable methylation control of their expression, as has been suggested in CRC for ATOH1 and MUC2, and is known for CDX1 and CDX2. Five of these genes (TFF3, CDX2, HES1, OLFM4, and LGR5) have binding sites for the 5 transcription factors ATOH1, SPDEF, CDX2, GATA6, and HES1 perhaps suggesting some sort of complex between them. Since differentiation control in normal tissues must ultimately be through control of gene expression and not mutation, the lack of significant mutations in any of these genes is perhaps not surprising. While control of major changes in cellular gene expression can be through, for example, growth factor targeting of a cell surface receptor, such as EGF onto EGFR, leading to blocked intracellular phosphorylation of the EGFR, the ultimate normal effect, through the consequent triggering by EGFR phosphorylation, must be through some sort of stable transcriptional control at the DNA level. That is why so many of our suggested drivers, 8 out of 10, are transcription factors. The change from groups 1, 2, or 3 to 4 is suggested by gene expression analysis to be in part due to OLFM4, but as already mentioned, this also probably involves MUC2 and ATOH1 promoter methylation. For group 5, ATOH1, TOX3, and LGR5 are candidates. Of these, LGR5 was the most surprising, as it is largely associated with colorectal stem cells, but this may be primarily as a primer for subsequent differentiation. It would not be surprising if at least some group 5 cell lines, having a complete lack of any goblet cell features, had low or absent expression of all three genes, ATOH1, TOX3, and LGR5.

While the result from demethylation with 5AΖΑ-2’ on a group 4 cell line, namely increased expression of MUC2, was as expected, the opposite result with LS180 was also understandable. This disparity shows the difficulty of interpreting the 5AΖΑ-2’ treatment, which does not discriminate between genes or between positive and negative effects. Another complication is that significant differences may be in expression level thresholds, high or low, and not simply positive or negative. The only answer to taking this further is to do a careful analysis of DNA methylation by sequencing, for which we did not have adequate resources.

Another level of complexity is that transcription factors probably rarely, if ever, work on their own. It is the composition of a combination of factors that determines the specificity with respect to a particular differentiation pathway, such as to goblet cells. Any given transcription factor is likely to be involved in a wide range of different complexes that may have little relationship to each other apart from one or more factors in common. For this, ultimately, structural analysis of the complexes will be needed (44).

CMS (Consensus Molecular Subtypes) is a well-known and widely quoted scheme for classifying CRCs based on cluster analysis of gene expressions (45). There is some evidence that CMS3 may be the most connected classification with goblet cell markers, but not uniquely so (46). The problem with such overall classifications is that they do not match the fact that cancers are based on discrete evolutionary steps of selection. Each such step will be associated with a possibly quite large and variable group of changed expressions and, in any given cancer, there may be many such overlapping groups of clusters associated with different evolutionary pathways. It is likely to be very hard, if not impossible, from overall transcription clusters, like CMS, to disentangle the effects of key drivers. Certain mutations such as in KRAS or TP53 are more associated with a given CMS than others but it is not clear how that adds to the primary knowledge about these drivers, whether internal to the cancer, or external as in the effects of the tumor microenvironment. Of course, it may be that a downstream effect of a driver is a better target for prognosis, detection, or drug treatment development than the initial driver, but that would still be better served by starting from the driver rather than its entangled downstream expression effect.

A much larger study of primary tumor samples will be needed to assess the possibility of AI-based H&E recognition of the 5 goblet cell–related groups and of their prognostic differences. Appropriate clinical trials, including knockdown of TFF3 activity, will also be needed to assess the extent to which the goblet cell classification may influence precision-based treatment of colorectal cancer.

For future development, detailed methylation DNA sequence analysis of likely candidate genes, which may include DNA-coded RNA functional genes, will be essential to help identify the key somatic evolutionary steps involved in the determination of the five goblet cell MUC2 and TFF3 expressing differentiating groups and their possible further subdivisions. This will need to be accompanied by knocking in and out key candidate genes in cell lines to confirm their functional roles. Future work on identifying transcription factor complexes associated with different stages of differentiation and, eventually, their structure, will also be needed for the ultimate understanding of these differentiation processes and how they change in cancer progression.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Culture Conditions.

Human CRC cell lines were obtained from the Cancer and Immunogenetics Laboratory cryogenic collection at the Weatherall Institute of Molecular Medicine (Oxford, UK) and were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS.

Immunofluorescence.

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. They were then incubated with the antibodies as described in the SI Materials and Methods.

Histology of Human Tissue Sections.

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) human tissue sections were obtained from matched pairs of normal colon and tumours, as part of the study titled “Investigating molecular markers in samples collected from patients who took part in "QUASAR 2, VICTOR and SCOT trials” (40). They were deparaffinized and antigen unmasked using standard procedures and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X100. Staining for immunofluorescence against MUC2 and TFF3 was as already described.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining.

The Haematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit (Abcam, Ref. ab245880) was used to stain human FFPE sections following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunoblotting.

Samples were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer. For protein secretion, the medium was collected and centrifuged before washing the cells. Blotting against MUC2 and HSP90 was carried out using standard procedures with a 10% acrylamide gel and without heating.

Transient siRNA Transfections.

Experiments were carried out 72 h following standard siRNA-transfection. Knock down efficiency was generally at least 80%.

Transient Mammalian Cell Transfection.

Open reading frame (ORF) clones of ATOH1) and SPDEF were overexpressed in various CRC cell lines using standard transfection procedures.

qPCR.

Total cellular RNA was extracted from cells cultured for 72 h in 24-well plates. qPCR was conducted using TaqMan® at standard cycling conditions.

Lumen Formation Assay.

Lumen formation assays were performed based on a protocol published by Ashley et al. (9). Immunofluorescence staining was initiated by removing the medium and applying 100 μl of 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 min and then staining out as previously described.

Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) of fixed cells.

Cells were fixed and permeabilized by gradually adding ice-cold methanol to the cell suspension and then adding antibodies using standard procedures. Live/dead staining and data analysis were performed as described in Liu’s thesis (31).

Single-cell Clonal Selection Analysis.

Single-cell sorting was done using a combination of BD FACS Aria™ III and Fusion systems. Cells were fixed and analyzed using immunofluorescence at various time intervals.

Drug Treatments.

Dibenzazepine and 5-Aza-2′-deoxycytidine were dissolved in DMSO and kept at −20 °C.

Gene Expression Analysis.

Total RNA was isolated from the cells using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Gene expression analysis was conducted following Affymetrix instructions (47).

Computational and Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0. Immunofluorescence images were visualised using Fiji/ImageJ (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/) and QuPath (https://qupath.github.io/) software.

The microarray data obtained from 79 CRC cell lines were analysed using GMMchi (https://github.com/jeffliu6068/GMMchi; (32)). TCGA RNA-seq data was used to compare CRC patients and normal samples. The scRNA-seq normal colon data were provided by Professor Alison Simmons and analysed with our LS180-COLO678 single-cell RNA-seq data (48).

Further materials and methods details are in the SI Appendix.
