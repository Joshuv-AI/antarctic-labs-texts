Sustainable Valorization of Organic Waste Via α-Amylase-Mediated Biocatalysis: A Green Bioprocessing Strategy
Authors: Ashjan F. Khalel, Iman K. Abdel Gadir, Moayed A. Hakeem, Najwa Motaen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The increasing environmental impact of food waste, especially starch-rich residues, necessitates sustainable biodegradation methods to reduce pollution and resource wastage.Objectives: This study aimed to isolate α-amylase-producing bacteria from saline-rich organic soils in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, and assess their effectiveness in degrading food waste through enzymatic starch hydrolysis.Materials and Methods: Twenty bacterial isolates were screened on starch agar for amylolytic activity. The most productive strain, Bacillus subtilis A16, was identified using biochemical assays (Gram staining, catalase/oxidase tests) and 16S rRNA sequencing. Enzyme production was optimized at different pH levels (4–9) and temperatures (30–60 °C). Purification involved ammonium sulfate precipitation (80% saturation), dialysis, and chromatography (Sephadex G-100, DEAE-Sephadex A-50). The purified enzyme was applied to 10 kg of food waste (bread and fruits) to evaluate degradation efficiency.Results: B. subtilis A16 exhibited the highest α-amylase activity (1.467 U. mL-1) with optimal performance at pH 8 (0.588 U. mL-1) and 40 °C (1.386 U.mL-1). Purification resulted in a 58 kDa enzyme with a 3.7-fold increase in specific activity (5.448 U.mg-1). Food waste treated with AK16 showed a 50% volume reduction within 25 days, accompanied by rapid starch decomposition.Conclusions: The study highlights B. subtilis A16 as a highly effective and environmentally friendly biocatalyst for the degradation of starch-rich waste. Its excellent alkaline and thermal stability, along with its ability to efficiently reduce waste, demonstrate its potential for sustainable use in industrial and environmental settings. These results present a promising bio-based approach to managing organic waste, promoting a circular economy, and minimizing the environmental impact of waste disposal.

1. Background

Saudi Arabia is highly dependent on food imports to meet its nutritional needs due to limited domestic agricultural output. Despite this reliance, the country produces large amounts of food waste, which poses a serious challenge to food security ( 1 , 2 ) . These challenges show the urgent need for sustainable approaches to valorize organic waste, particularly green bioprocessing strategies to improve food security and environmental sustainability ( 3 ). Enzymes serve as indispensable biocatalysts in both natural and industrial processes, accelerating biochemical reactions with high specificity ad efficiency ( 4 , 5 ). Among these, α-amylase (EC 3.2.1.1) holds paramount industrial importance due to its ability to hydrolyze starch into glucose, maltose, and oligosaccharides, enabling applications in food processing, biofuels, and waste management ( 6 , 7 ). Globally, α-amylases account for approximately 30% of industrial enzyme production, underscoring their economic and ecological relevance ( 8 ).

While α-amylases are derived from plants, animals, and microorganisms, microbially derived enzymes dominate industrial applications due to their cost-effectiveness, scalability, and adaptability to extreme conditions ( 9 - 11 ). The genus Bacillus, particularly Bacillus subtilis, is known for its robust enzyme production capabilities, including thermostable and alkali-tolerant α-amylases ( 12 , 13 ). These enzymes are central importance for starch liquefaction, detergent formulation, and bioremediation, which meets the growing demand for sustainable alternatives to chemical catalysts ( 14 , 15 ). At the same time, food waste a byproduct of agricultural and domestic activities poses severe environmental challenges.

Millions of tons of starchy waste such as peelings, bread and fruit scraps are thrown away every year, exacerbating landfill pollution and greenhouse gas emissions ( 16 , 17 ). Traditional disposal methods are unsustainable, necessitating innovative strategies for waste valorization. Microbial α-amylases offer a promising solution by converting starch-rich waste into fermentable sugars, which can be further processed into biofuels or biodegradable materials ( 18 , 19 ). However, the efficiency of this process hinges on identifying amylases with high stability under industrial conditions. Saline-organic soils, such as those in coastal regions, represent underexplored reservoirs of extremophilic microorganisms capable of producing resilient enzymes ( 20 , 21 ). For example, bacteria isolated from high-salinity environments often exhibit increased Osmo tolerance and enzyme stability, properties that are advantageous for large-scale applications ( 11 , 22 ).

The aim of this study was to isolate and characterize α-amylase-producing bacteria from high-salinity, organic soils in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia to evaluate their efficacy in food waste degradation. We focused on the Bacillus species known for its enzymatic versatility, and hypothesized that strains from this unique ecosystem would produce α-amylases with superior activity under alkaline and moderate thermal conditions. The results contribute to develop environmentally friendly strategies for waste management while advancing the bioprospecting of microbial enzymes from extreme environments.

2. Objectives

The objective of the current study was to isolate α- amylase- producing bacteria from soil in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, optimize parameters influencing enzyme synthesis, and assess the enzyme’s potential for degrading food waste.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. Sample Collection and Bacterial Isolation

High-salinity soil samples enriched with organic matter were aseptically collected from Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, in March, 2024. Samples were homogenized and serially diluted in sterile saline (0.85% NaCl). Aliquots of dilutions ranging from 10-3–10-6 were spread onto the surface of starch agar plates (pH 7.4) containing (g. L-1 ): soluble starch (10.0), KH2PO4 (3.0), yeast extract (1.0), peptone (10.0), Na2HPO4(6.0), NaCl (0.5), CaCl2·2H2O (10 mM), and MgSO4·7H2O (100 mM). The plates were then incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Amylolytic activity was screened using Lugol’s iodine staining, with zones of clearance ≥2 mm indicating starch hydrolysis. Positive isolates were sub-cultured on nutrient agar for purity and stored at −80 °C in 20% glycerol.

3.2. Enzyme Production and Activity Assay

Selected isolates were inoculated into starch broth (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C, 250 rpm, for 72 h. Cell-free supernatants were obtained by centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C). α-Amylase activity was quantified using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method ( 23 ). In brief, 0.5 mL of enzyme extract was incubated with 0.5 mL of 1% soluble starch (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 6.5) at 60 °C for 20 min. Reactions were stopped with 1 mL DNS reagent, boiled for 5 min, and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm. One unit (U) of activity was defined as the amount of enzyme releasing 1 μmoL glucose/min under assay conditions. The isolate A16 exhibiting the highest enzyme activity was selected for further identification and application.

3.3. Biochemical and Molecular Identification

The putative amylolytic strain A16 was characterized morphologically (colony morphology, Gram staining) and biochemically (catalase, oxidase, motility tests). Genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method, and the 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR with universal primers 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′).The PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was performed using a typical reaction included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30-35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 Sec, annealing at 55 °C for 30 Sec, and extension at 72 °C for 1.5 min. A final extension at 72 °C for 7 min ensured complete amplification of the ~1500 bp product. A 50 µL PCR reaction mixture was prepared containing 1 µL of template DNA (1–100 ng), 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (0.2–1.0 µM), 5 µL of 10× PCR buffer, 1.5–2.5 µL of 50 mM MgCl2 (to obtain a final concentration of 1.5–2.5 mM), 1 µL of 10 mM dNTP mix (final concentration 200 µM of each dNTP), 0.25–0.5 µL of Taq DNA polymerase (1–2.5 units), and nuclease-free water added to make the final volume up to 50 µL. The amplified products (~1500 bp) were sequenced by Macrogen Inc., South Korea, and phylogenetic analysis was performed using BLASTn against the NCBI database.

3.4. Optimization of α-Amylase Production

The production of α-amylase is influenced by various physical and chemical factors. The impact of pH (4.0–9.0) and temperature (30–60 °C) on enzyme activity was investigated. Cultures were grown in starch broth under different conditions, and enzyme activity was measured as described. Growth kinetics were tracked using spectrophotometry at OD600 nm.

3.5. Enzyme Purification

The crude enzyme was precipitated with 80% saturation of ammonium sulfate, dialyzed against 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0 using a 12 kDa MWCO membrane for 24 h, and then purified through gel filtration chromatography using a Sephadex G-100 column (1.5 × 30 cm). The active fractions were collected and further purified by ion-exchange chromatography using DEAE-Sephadex A-50. The protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay with BSA as the standard. Purity and molecular weight were confirmed by SDS-PAGE on a 10% gel ( 24 ).

3.6. Food Waste Degradation Assay

Fresh food waste, including bread and fruits, was homogenized and placed in aerated containers measuring 24 × 25 cm. The inoculum was prepared by adjusting the cell suspension to an optical density of 0.5 at 600 nm (OD600), equivalent to approximately 3 × 108 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU. mL-1). For the waste degradation assay, 1 L of this standardized inoculum was applied per 10 kg of food waste. The waste was inoculated with Bacillus subtilis A16 culture at a rate of 1 L per 10 kg of waste and incubated at 37 °C. Degradation progress was monitored by measuring mass reduction, observing visual decomposition, and tracking microbial growth over a period of 25 days.

3.7. Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 16.0 (ANOVA, t-test; significance at *p* < 0.05). Results are presented as mean ± SD of triplicates.

Crude enzyme precipitated with 80% ammonium sulfate resulted in partial purification, with a specific activity of 5.448 U.mg-1. The most active fractions were found in numbers 31-41 (Fig. 5A). Subsequent purification steps using gel filtration (Sephadex G-100) and ion-exchange chromatography (DEAE-Sephadex A-50) led to the final pure purification. The highest amylase activity was observed in fractions 21-27 (Fig. 5B). SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed a single band at 58 kDa, consistent with α-amylases from Bacillus spp. (Fig. 6).

4.4. Food Waste Degradation by Bacillus subtilis A16

Inoculation of 10 kg of food waste with A16 resulted in visible decomposition within 3 days, including discoloration and softening of texture. By day 25, the mass reduction reached 48 ± 3%, with complete starch hydrolysis confirmed by iodine testing (Fig. 7). Control samples (uninoculated) showed less than 10% degradation under the same conditions.

5. Discussion

The enzymatic hydrolysis of starch by amylases, particularly α-amylases, has been extensively studied for its industrial and environmental applications, including the bioremediation of starch-rich food waste ( 15 , 25 ). This study focused on isolating and characterizing amylolytic bacteria from saline soils enriched with organic matter. The hypothesis was that such environments would harbor halotolerant micro-organisms with robust starch-degrading capabilities. This rationale stems is based on the ecological adaptability of bacteria in saline niches, where osmolytic strategies may enhance enzymatic stability under harsh conditions, a trait advantageous for industrial processes ( 21 , 26 ).

Twenty bacterial isolates were obtained using a serial dilution method ( 27 ) and screened on starch agar plates supplemented with iodine to assess amylase activity. The hydrolysis zone-to-colony diameter (HC) ratio was used to preliminarily evaluate amylase activity after 48 h of incubation at 37 °C. This qualitative method, validated by Thebti et al. ( 20 ), is based on the enzymatic breakdown of starch into reducing sugars, visible as clear zones around colonies. Bacillus subtilis A16 showed the highest HC ratio (>2.5) among the isolates, leading to its selection for quantitative analysis. Enzyme activity was quantified using a glucose standard curve, with one international unit (IU) defined as the amount of enzyme releasing 1 μmoL of reducing sugar per minute under standardized conditions.

Optimal α-amylase production by B. subtilis A16 was observed at pH 8.0 (0.588 U. mL-1) and 40 °C (1.386 U.mL-1). This alkaline preference is in line with the physicochemical stability of α-amylase, which typically shows improved substrate binding and catalytic efficiency under slightly basic conditions. The thermophilic activity of the enzyme is attributed to its structural robustness at high temperatures, a characteristic commonly found in Bacillus spp. due to their ability to form spores and adapt to changing environments. ( 17 , 28 ). Mechanistically, α-amylase breaks down α-1,4-glycosidic bonds in starch, producing oligosaccharides and maltose. The halotolerance of B. subtilis A16 is likely a result of osmoregulatory mechanisms, such as the accumulation of compatible solutes like proline and glycine betaine, which help maintain enzyme stability under saline conditions ( 16 , 29 ).

These findings are consistent with previous studies that have identified Bacillus spp. as the primary amylase producers in starch-rich waste. For example, Bacillus cereus isolated from kitchen waste has shown similar starch-degrading efficiency ( 16 , 30 ). Additionally, strains from spoiled food waste ( 17 ) and industrial effluents ( 31 ) further emphasize the ecological versatility of this genus. However, variations in optimal conditions, which stated by Mishra and Behera ( 32 ) who reported that peak activity at pH 7.0, indicate that different strains may have specific adaptations to their environments. The superior activity of B. subtilis A16 at pH 8.0 and 40 °C suggests its potential for bioremediation of alkaline, thermophilic waste streams, such as those generated by food processing industries.

While B. subtilis A16 shows promise, this study is limited by its narrow focus on a single strain and a lack of investigation into various environmental variables such as salinity gradients and alternative carbon sources. Molecular characterization techniques, like 16S rRNA sequencing, were not utilized, which hinders taxonomic precision. Furthermore, the long-term stability of enzymes under industrial conditions has not been confirmed. Future research should consider genetic engineering to enhance amylase production, optimize fermentation parameters on a large scale, and explore consortia-based strategies for processing complex waste materials.

6. Conclusion

Bacillus subtilis A16, isolated from saline, organically enriched soils in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, showed high amylase production under optimized conditions. Its main isoenzyme, Amyl, displayed excellent efficiency and stability in starch hydrolysis at acidic pH levels. This acid tolerance, combined with the strain’s halotolerant nature, makes it a promising candidate for degrading starch-rich food waste in industrial settings, especially in environments with varying pH or salinity. Further research should investigate the structural features of Amyl for acid stability and explore scalable applications to enhance sustainable bioremediation strategies.
