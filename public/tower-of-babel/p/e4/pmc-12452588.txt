Bioinformatics analysis of immune infiltration and cell cycle hub genes in atopic dermatitis
Authors: Xiaoyun Liu, Xizi Lu, Li Wang, Qian Du, Xiang Li, Yi Li, Xiaofeng Ding, Yongxian Lai, Xiaogang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease with complex pathogenesis. This study integrated bioinformatics and experimental validation to elucidate the molecular pathways and discover novel therapeutic targets for AD diagnostic and treatment.MethodsTranscriptomic datasets GSE182740 (training cohort, n = 75) and GSE5667 (validation cohort, n = 34) were analyzed. DEGs identified via GEO2R (|logFC|>2, p < 0.01) underwent KEGG/GO enrichment (Metascape; min overlap = 3, p < 0.01). STRING-constructed PPI networks (interaction score>0.4) visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.1) revealed top 5 hub genes by CytoHubba MCC scoring. Immune cell infiltration patterns were quantified using ssGSEA software, with in vitro validation conducted in TNF-α/IFN-γ-stimulated keratinocytes.ResultsAmong 979 significant DEGs, inflammation-related pathways (IL-17, TNF-α, PI3K-Akt, p < 0.05) were enriched. PPI analysis revealed five cell cycle-regulating hub genes (AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK), all upregulated in AD lesions. In vitro experiments confirmed the upregulation of these hub genes in AD. These genes correlated significantly with activated CD4 T cells, Th2 cells, Treg cells, and central memory CD8 T cells (p < 0.05). In vitro experiments confirmed the overexpression of five hub genes in keratinocytes induced by inflammatory factors.ConclusionThis study identified five hub genes driving AD by disrupting keratinocytes cell cycle regulation. These genes mediated keratinocytes-immune interactions, providing novel targets for AD therapy.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Bioinformatics analysis, Gene expression, Cell cycle, Immune cell

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease characterized by symptoms such as dry skin, pruritus, erythema, and skin barrier dysfunction [1]. It typically affects areas such as the face, neck, flexural surfaces, and distal limbs [2,3]. Approximately 15–20 % of children and 10 % of adults are affected by AD, with about 95 % of children experiencing early flare-ups before the age of 5 [4].

AD pathogenesis involves complex interactions between genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, and environmental factors [1]. Immune dysfunction is central to AD development [5], with T helper 2 cells (Th2) -derived cytokines, interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13, driving IgE production, eosinophil activation, and skin barrier disruption [[6], [7], [8]]. Besides Th2, dysregulation of Th17 cells, regulatory T (Treg) cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and keratinocytes further amplifies inflammation [9]. Although several immune pathways have been identified, there remains a lack of research on differential gene expression, immune cell infiltration, and their interactions during AD pathogenesis. Therefore, there is a pressing need for integrated analyses to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Bioinformatics approaches have emerged as powerful tools for investigating AD pathogenesis at the molecular level [10]. By integrating high-throughput gene expression data with immune infiltration analysis, we can construct gene regulatory networks and identify functionally significant AD-associated genes [11,12]. However, the reliability of conclusions depends on stringent dataset selection criteria, particularly for AD studies involving lesional heterogeneity and confounding factors. To ensure biological relevance and analytical rigor, this study implemented the following screening criteria for AD datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, sample size >50, inclusion of lesional/non-lesional/healthy skin specimens, and consistent detection platforms.

In this study, we utilized gene expression data from AD patients obtained from the GEO database. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, followed by enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) databases to uncover potential biological pathways associated with these genes. Through protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, we identified key hub genes, and further explored the correlation analysis between hub genes and immune cell in AD pathogenesis. Additionally, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was employed to quantify immune cell infiltration patterns and analyze their correlation with DEGs, with subsequent validation through in vitro experiments. This study aimed to use bioinformatics tools and immune cell analysis to explore the immunopathological mechanisms of AD. It also identified key differential genes and their interactions with immune cells, providing a new foundation for precision medicine in AD management.

Methods

Acquisition of AD datasets and identification of DEGs

Relevant datasets for AD were retrieved from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The dataset GSE182740 used as a training cohort contained 75 clinical samples, including 20 tissue samples (10 lesions and 10 non-lesions) and 6 healthy samples from 10 patients with AD. The validation cohort (dataset GSE5667) consisted of 34 clinical samples, 12 tissue samples (6 lesions and 6 non-lesions), and 5 healthy samples from 6 patients with AD were selected. Gene expression data from patients with AD and healthy individuals were analyzed using the GEO2R tool in the GEO database. Gene expression data from this dataset were homogenized and analyzed for intergroup sample correlations using the default setting options of the GEO2R tool. The screening criteria of p < 0.01 and |log fold change (FC) | > 2 were set to screen for DEGs between patients with AD and healthy individuals, and the results were presented using volcano plots.

KEGG and GO enrichment analysis

After identifying the DEGs in patients with AD and healthy individuals, we conducted KEGG and GO enrichment analyses using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/). Before beginning the analysis, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were imported into Metascape. The analysis parameters were set as follows: species restricted to Homo sapiens, minimum overlap of three, minimum enrichment factor of 1.5, and significance threshold of p < 0.01. GO enrichment analysis included categories of biological processes (BP), cellular composition (CC), and molecular function (MF). Subsequently, an online platform was used to generate figures and the results of the KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were presented using bar and bubble charts, respectively [13].

Constructing the PPI network of AD and identifying hub genes

PPI networks were generated using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to analyze the role of DEGs. The PPI network was generated with the limiting conditions of H. sapiens and a minimum required interaction score of 0.4. The analysis results were imported into the Cytoscape software (version 3.10.1) for visualization. The MCC scores of all DEGs were calculated using the CytoHubba plug-in, and genes with the top five scores were selected as hub genes for the PPI network [13].

Verification of hub genes

The expression profiles of the hub genes in the training and validation cohorts were visualized using box-and-line plots to clarify their roles in AD. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the accuracy of these hub genes in distinguishing AD patients from healthy individuals. The area under the curve (AUC) for each gene was calculated in both groups. Furthermore, one-way regression analyses were conducted to assess the hazard ratios (HRs), 95 % confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values for all hub genes. One-way regression analyses were performed using clinical data (AD score, Table S1) and gene expression profiling data from patients with AD and healthy individuals using the ‘survival’ package of R software (version 4.3.1).

Analysis of immune cell infiltration in AD

To investigate the potential role of immune cells in AD progression, we analyzed immune cell infiltration in the AD training cohort using the ‘ssGSEA’ method. Use the ‘GSVA’ package of R software for ssGSEA analysis and set the ‘kcdf’ parameter to ‘Gaussian’. This approach quantified the expression profiles of 28 distinct immune cell types in each sample, facilitating a comparative analysis of immune cell distribution across different groups. We used the ggplot2 package to generate a box plot to represent our findings visually. This plot illustrated the expression patterns of 28 immune cell types in patients with AD and healthy individuals. Additionally, to gain a deeper understanding of the interactions between the immune cell types in AD, we conducted relevant analyses of these immune cell types.

Correlation between immune cells and hub gene

To explore the relationship between hub genes and 28 types of immune cells, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient by combining the relative quantification results of immune cells in each sample cluster with the expression of hub genes and determined the significance of the correlation between hub genes and immune cells based on a significance level of p < 0.05 [13]. The correlation trend between the two was also clarified.

Cell culture and intervention

Human epidermal keratinocytes (HaCaT) that purchased from Thermo Fisher were used for in vitro experiments. After HaCaT recovery, cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA), cultured at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator. When HaCaT reached 80–90 % confluency, they were treated with 10 ng/mL recombinant TNF-α and 10 ng/mL recombinant IFN-γ for 6 h to establish inflammatory models.

RT-qPCR

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis of hub gene mRNA levels in HaCaT was performed using the StepOne RT-qPCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Biological repetition is 6. cDNA was synthesized from RNA using the Omniscript RT kit (Vazyme, China). Subsequently, RT-qPCR analysis was performed on the cDNA products. Using GAPDH as an internal reference, the relative mRNA expression levels of AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK were quantified via the 2-^^CT method. The primer sequences were listed in Table 1.

Results

Identification of DEGs for AD

Following the normalization of gene expression data from the training cohort (Fig. 1A), we conducted Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analyses (Fig. 1B–C) and found significant differences between patients with AD and healthy individuals. Using the screening criteria of p < 0.01 and | log FC | > 2, a total of 979 DEGs were identified (Table S2). The results were presented using volcano plots (Fig. 1D).

Identify DEGs in AD. (A) Boxplot of sample data after homogenization. (B) Plot of PCA results. Each point corresponds to one sample. (C) UMAP analysis plot. Each point corresponds to one sample. (D) Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes. Each point corresponds to one gene; blue points denote down-regulated genes, while red points indicate up-regulated genes. The dashed line on the x-axis corresponds to a |FC| of 1 and 2, while the dashed line on the y-axis represents significance thresholds of P = 0.05 and 0.01.

Pathway and ontology enrichment prediction using DEGs

After identifying the DEGs, we conducted KEGG and GO enrichment analyses using the Metascape database (Table S3–6). The bar graph in Fig. 2A showed the results of KEGG enrichment analysis, which revealed enrichment in 41 distinct pathways. Several pathways with particularly low p-values emerged, including the IL-17, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathways, and other signaling cascades. These findings suggested a crucial role of the inflammatory response in AD pathogenesis. Additionally, Fig. 2B presented a bubble plot summarizing the GO enrichment analysis. This visualization presented the top five terms with the lowest p-values for each category. In the BP category, we identified 492 terms encompassing diverse processes, such as the regulation of cytoskeleton organization, positive regulation of organelle organization, cell division, mitotic nuclear division, and cell cycle regulation. We identified 78 terms in the CC category, including centrosome, chromosomal region, transcription repressor complex, nuclear speck, and kinetochore. We identified 98 terms in the MF, including actin binding, actin filament binding, kinase activity, protein kinase activity, and phosphotransferase activity, with alcohol groups as acceptors. These findings suggested that these biological pathways and processes were involved in AD pathogenesis.

The classification of the pathway is shown on the right. (B) Bubble plot of GO enrichment analysis. Vertical coordinates are term names and horizontal coordinates are enrichment scores. The shape of the dots corresponds to different GO classifications, the size of the dots corresponds to the number of DEGs annotated to the term, and the color of the dots corresponds to the p-value.

Constructing PPI network for AD and identifying hub genes

After importing 979 DEGs into the STRING database, a PPI network with 876 nodes and 2901 edges was generated. The relevant information for the network included: the average node degree = 6.62, avg. Local clustering coefficient = 0.349, the expected number of edges = 2454, and PPI enrichment p-value <1.0e-16. The PPI network was visualized using the Cytoscape software (Fig. 3), which provides information on some of the targets with high degree values. The MCC score for each target was calculated using the CytoHubba plugin to further analyze the more central genes in this network. The top five scoring genes were AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK, which were the hub genes of this PPI network.

PPI network graph. Each point corresponds to a DEG. The larger the circle, the darker the color, and the larger the font size, the higher the degree value of the gene, indicating its higher importance in the PPI network.

AURKA, a member of the serine/threonine kinase family, regulates centrosome maturation and spindle formation during mitosis and induces cell entry into mitosis [14]. In inflammatory skin diseases, AURKA upregulation activates the AIM2 inflammasome and the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, enhancing the inflammatory response in keratinocytes [15]. AURKA exacerbates immune responses and inflammation by inhibiting autophagy activity in keratinocytes [15]. BUB1B and CCNB1 are involved in cell cycle regulation [16]. BUB1B encodes a kinase that is crucial for the function of the spindle checkpoint, which ensures accurate chromosome separation [17]. BUB1B is also linked to chromosomal instability through the p53 signaling pathway [18]. CCNB1 is a cyclin that may serve as a new marker for mild AD. It participates in the progression of AD by promoting the transition from G2 phase to mitosis [19,20]. MELK, a member of the AMPK protein family, is identified as a novel oncogene [21,22]. MELK regulates the fate of skin cells. Overexpression of MELK leads to apoptotic death of keratinocytes and necrosis of fibroblasts [23]. TTK is a key component of the SAC complex, involved in mitosis, and has been recognized as an effective therapeutic target in human cancers [24]. Most of the identified hub genes are involved in cell cycle regulation. They participate in the immune and pathological processes of AD by influencing the proliferation of keratinocytes and local inflammation.

Verifying the reliability of hub genes

The expression of the five hub genes in the training cohort was demonstrated using box-and-line plots, and all hub genes showed elevated expression in patients with AD (Fig. 4A). The ROC analysis of the training cohort showed that the AUC >0.9 for four hub genes and the AUC >0.7 for one hub gene, indicating that they could better distinguish AD patients from healthy individuals (Fig. 4B). Genes with an AUC >0.9 demonstrated exceptional diagnostic performance in distinguishing AD patients from healthy controls, highlighting their potential as early screening tools for clinical diagnosis. ROC analysis of the validation cohort revealed that three hub genes had an AUC >0.7, while two had an AUC >0.55. Although these values were lower, they allowed for some degree of differentiation between AD patients and health controls (Fig. 4C). These results highlighted the consistency of the hub across different sample cohorts and their diagnostic value across populations. In addition, combined with the AD scores of the patients, one-way regression analysis revealed that all five hub genes could significantly affect the AD scores of patients with AD (Fig. 5). These findings suggested that these hub genes may serve as potential biomarkers for AD, reflecting the severity of the disease.

Immune cell infiltration analysis of AD

To explore whether the 28 immune cell types participated in the AD pathogenesis, their expression in each sample was relatively quantified by single-sample immune cell infiltration analysis, which mapped the expression of immune cells to [0, 1] by linear transformation. The immune cell expression of each sample was analyzed statistically to determine whether there were significant differences between the groups. It was found that central memory CD4 T cells had the highest expression among all samples. Therefore, after a linear change, the relative expression in all samples was 1, and there was no difference between the groups. Between-group analysis showed that all cells, except myeloid-derived suppressor cells, were significantly different (p > 0.05) between AD patients and healthy individuals (Fig. 6A). Correlation analysis showed significant correlations between a variety of immune cells and positive correlations between most of the cells, except for activated B cells vs. Th2 cells, immature DCs, CD56 bright natural killer cells vs. Treg cells, activated B cells, and negative correlations between myeloid-derived suppressor cells, CD56 bright natural killer cells, and memory B cells (Fig. 6B). Analysis of immune cell infiltration revealed that AD progression was closely related to multiple immune cells.

Correlation analysis between immune cells and hub genes

We analyzed the correlation between hub gene expression and immune cells to investigate the impact of hub genes on immune cells (Fig. 7). The results indicated a significant positive correlation between activated CD4 T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and Th2 cells with all hub genes (p < 0.01). Additionally, a significant positive correlation exists between activated CD8 T cells and CCNB1, MELK, and TTK expression (p < 0.05). A significant negative correlation was found between central memory CD8 T cells and MELK (p < 0.05), mast cells and AURKA (p < 0.05), monocytes and AURKA, BUB1B, MELK, and TTK (p < 0.05), and Plasmacytoid dendritic cells and AURKA, MELK, and TTK (p < 0.05). In addition, Treg cells positively correlated with CCNB1 expression (p < 0.05). A strong association was found between the hub genes and activated CD4 T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and Th2 cells. These findings suggested that these hub genes interact with immune cells and participate in AD progression.

Expression of the hub gene in HaCaT

To further validate the role of hub genes in AD, we induced inflammatory HaCaT models using 10 ng/mL TNF-α and 10 ng/mL IFN-γ for in vitro experiments [25]. Stimulation of HaCaT with TNF-α and IFN-γ activated pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and induced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to immune cell infiltration and the development of inflammatory skin diseases or skin aging [26]. Untreated HaCaT served as the control group, while HaCaT cultured with TNF-α and IFN-γ were used as the model group. The mRNA expression levels of AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK mRNA were detected by RT-qPCR in the cells of both groups. The results showed that the expression of these hub genes was significantly upregulated (p < 0.01) in the skin inflammatory cell model (Fig. 8). These experimental results aligned with our bioinformatics analysis, confirming significant upregulation of these hub genes in AD lesions.

Discussion

This study integrated bioinformatics analysis to reveal the immune infiltration patterns and the potential roles of cell cycle hub genes in AD. It provided new insights into the immunopathological mechanisms of AD. We first identified five hub genes (AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK) associated with AD progression. Immune cell infiltration analysis showed significant associations between these genes and activated CD4 T cells, Th2 cells, Treg cells, and central memory CD8 T cells. In vitro experiments confirmed the expression trends of these hub genes in skin inflammation model cells. These results supported the idea that cell cycle regulation may contribute to AD by affecting immune cell function.

Although providing mechanistic insights, this study has the following limitations. First, the sample size of the dataset was small, which may not fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity of AD. Future studies should increase the sample size and use multi-center datasets for more reliable results. Second, confounding factors like age, gender, and disease severity were not controlled for in the analysis. Future research should address these factors or perform subgroup analyses to improve accuracy. Finally, the functions of the hub genes in AD were not experimentally validated. Future research should verify the roles of these genes in immune cell function using AD in vivo models, such as gene overexpression, silencing, and pathway inhibition experiments. This would enhance the reliability and clinical applicability of the results.

Enrichment analysis identified several inflammation-related signaling pathways, including IL-17, TNF-α, and PI3K-Akt, which are linked to AD progression. IL-17 is a key cytokine of Th17 cells [27]. Downregulation of the IL-17 signaling pathway reduces the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-17 and decreases mast cell aggregation, which could potentially treat AD [28,29]. TNF activation is associated with the activation of inflammatory cytokines in AD-like keratinocytess [30]. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is a classical cellular pathway and a key driver of immune-mediated skin diseases [31]. Dysregulation of the PI3K-Akt pathway is linked to several immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, including AD, alopecia, and psoriasis [32].

Through PPI network analysis, this study identified five hub genes (AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK) implicated in AD pathogenesis. Functionally, these genes take part in cell cycle regulation, with AURKA playing a pivotal role in cell division. Notably, while CTNNB1, EGFR, and TP53 were not classified as hub genes, their high network connectivity suggests multifaceted involvement in AD mechanisms. CTNNB1-mediated keratinocytes differentiation via Wnt/β-catenin signaling, EGFR-mediated restoration of Th1/Th2 balance suppressing mast cell proliferation, and TP53-mediated protection against oxidative keratinocytes damage [[33], [34], [35]].

The immune cell infiltration is related to the pathogenesis of AD [36]. In this study, we found that several immune cells showed elevated expression in AD patients, including activated CD4 T cells, Th2 cells, Treg cells, central memory CD8 T cells, mast cells, monocytes, and plasmacytoid DC. Th2 cells are crucial in AD. They promote IgE production, which binds to mast cells, causing the release of inflammatory cytokines and leading to eczematous skin damage [36]. CD4 T cells are one of the main immune cells in acute AD lesions, with a significant increase in the dermis [37]. Treg cells regulate immune responses by secreting inhibitory cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β, controlling Th2 cell activity [38]. Plasmacytoid DCs produce pro-inflammatory cytokines, activate Th1, Th2, and Treg cells, and express major histocompatibility complex-II and co-stimulatory molecules [39]. Notably, distinct from previous studies emphasizing Th2/Th17 dominance, the ssGSEA analysis revealed central memory CD8 T cells as a significant infiltrating population. This finding aligned with emerging evidence of CD8 T cell exhaustion in chronic AD [40], suggesting that cell cycle genes may function as immune checkpoint regulators.

The correlation analysis of hub genes and immune cell infiltration revealed a strong positive correlation between MELK and mast cell infiltration (r = 0.82, p < 0.01). Mast cells are key effector cells in the AD inflammatory response. Their degranulation requires precise cell cycle regulation. High infiltration of Th2 cells, Treg cells, CD4 T cells, and CD8 T cells was observed in high-expressing MELK breast cancer, but this needs further experimental validation in AD. Furthermore, in vitro experiments showed that inflammatory stimuli like TNF-α and IFN-γ significantly increased the expression of AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK. This result was consistent with the bioinformatics analysis and supported the potential involvement of these hub genes in AD through their regulation of immune responses and cell cycle in keratinocytess.

This study established a mechanistic foundation for the clinical translation of cell cycle inhibitors by elucidating the connection between mitotic regulation and immune microenvironment remodeling in AD. AURKA inhibitors effectively suppress AIM2 inflammasome activation [38]. Clinical analyses identified a positive correlation between CCNB1 and TNF-α levels in skin inflammatory patients [41]. Furthermore, TNF-α/IFN-γ induce hub gene expression in vitro, amplifying inflammation. Therefore, targeting these hub genes may disrupt the cell cycle-immune loop driving AD chronicity.

Conclusion

This study utilized bioinformatics and immune cell infiltration analyses to identify five cell cycle hub genes (AURKA, BUB1B, CCNB1, MELK, and TTK) that play crucial roles in the progression of AD. The study also revealed the close relationship between these genes and immune cells. These findings provided new insights into the immunopathology of AD and offered potential therapeutic targets for future targeted treatments.
