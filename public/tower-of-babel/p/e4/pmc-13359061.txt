Metastatic epidural spinal cord compression (MESCC) is a common and potentially devastating manifestation of advanced cancer.1–3 MRI plays a central role in diagnosing the degree of epidural disease, assessing spinal cord compression, and guiding treatment planning.1–3 In patients with spinal metastases, treatment decisions are commonly framed by neurological status, tumor biology and radiosensitivityn (oncological), mechanical stability, and systemic condition, as represented by the NOMS framework.4 Within this framework, mechanical instability itself is an important indication for surgical intervention, independent of tumor histology.4,5

Although epidural compression in metastatic spine disease is usually interpreted as direct tumor extension into the spinal canal, not all compressive epidural lesions are necessarily progressive tumor tissue. In the cervical spine, several nonneoplastic mass lesions associated with segmental instability, such as pannus or retro-odontoid pseudotumor, have been shown to regress after posterior stabilization alone.6–9 A particularly illustrative prior report described regression of a compressive ventral epidural pannus after posterior instrumented fusion without direct resection of the lesion,7 supporting the concept that some spinal canal lesions may be mechanically driven and reversible.

However, regression of a ventral epidural mass after stabilization alone in the setting of cervical spinal metastasis has rarely been described. We report a case of cervical metastatic disease with marked spinal instability in which a ventral epidural mass causing spinal cord compression regressed after posterior fixation prior to systemic therapy. This case suggests that, in selected patients with metastatic cervical lesions, an epidural mass may represent an instability-related reversible lesion rather than tumor progression alone.

Illustrative Case

An 82-year-old man with no significant medical history was brought to our emergency department after developing tetraparesis following a fall. In the weeks preceding admission, he had experienced progressive gait unsteadiness and had sustained repeated falls. On arrival, his level of consciousness was mildly impaired, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14 (E4, V4, M6). Neurological examination revealed diffuse weakness in all four extremities, with muscle strength below the wrists graded as 3/5 on manual muscle testing. He also complained of severe pain in all extremities. No bladder or bowel dysfunction was present. On general examination, marked neck pain was elicited during cervical motion.

Brain MRI showed no apparent intracranial abnormality, including traumatic lesions. Cervical MRI demonstrated deformity of the C7 vertebral body and a mass contiguous with the posterior aspect of the vertebral body, located ventral to the spinal cord, causing severe cord compression (Fig. 1A–F). The ventral epidural mass showed isointense signal relative to the spinal cord on T1- and T2-weighted images and was homogeneously enhanced with gadolinium. On axial contrast-enhanced images, an enhancing lesion extending into the right C7 pedicle was also identified, suggesting tumor involvement. CT revealed osteolytic destruction involving the C7 vertebral body and both pedicles (Fig. 1G and H). Plain radiographs were not informative because evaluation of the C7 level was obscured by a clavicular plate. Metastatic spinal tumor was suspected, and systemic evaluation was performed. Whole-body CT demonstrated marked enlargement of the prostate and a mass lesion in the right rib (Fig. 2A and B). The serum prostate-specific antigen level was greater than 10,000 ng/mL. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with metastatic prostate cancer involving the cervical spine with MESCC, and the neurological deterioration was considered to represent traumatic spinal cord injury superimposed on this lesion. The severity of the spinal cord injury was classified as Frankel grade C and American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale grade C. Assessment of the patient’s overall condition as a case of metastatic spinal tumor showed a revised Tokuhashi score of 13, a Katagiri score of 2, and a Spine Instability Neoplastic Score of 11. Given the presence of marked mechanical instability and symptomatic cord compression, palliative surgery was indicated. The expected prognosis and the palliative intent of surgery were discussed with the patient and family preoperatively. The goals of surgery were preservation and improvement of neurological function, pain relief, and mechanical stabilization rather than curative treatment. Although an anterior approach was considered, reconstruction at the C7 level is technically demanding because of the thoracic inlet. Given the patient’s advanced age, metastatic burden, and need for urgent decompression, a posterior-only strategy was considered the safest and most feasible means of achieving rapid decompression and stabilization. The patient underwent posterior decompression and fusion consisting of C4–6 laminectomy and posterior instrumented fixation from C4 to T2 (Fig. 3A). During surgery, granulation-like soft tissue was obtained from the right C7 pedicle for histopathological examination. Pathological analysis demonstrated metastatic prostate adenocarcinoma, confirming the diagnosis of spinal metastasis from prostate cancer (Fig. 3B). Early postoperative MRI was obtained to confirm adequate decompression of the spinal cord, and follow-up MRI at 1 month was performed to reassess the residual ventral epidural lesion in relation to neurological recovery. MRI obtained on postoperative day 1 showed persistence of the ventral epidural mass behind the C7 vertebral body, although adequate decompression of the spinal cord had been achieved (Fig. 4A and B). Remarkably, MRI obtained 1 month after surgery demonstrated complete spontaneous resolution of the ventral epidural mass despite the absence of prior systemic therapy (Fig. 4C and D). The patient’s neurological function gradually improved postoperatively to Frankel grade D, and his modified Rankin Scale score improved to 3. His neck pain also improved substantially. He was subsequently transferred to the urology service, where hormonal therapy was initiated. MRI obtained 3 months after surgery demonstrated no recurrence of the epidural mass (Fig. 4E and F). The deformity of the C7 vertebral body had also improved, accompanied by osteosclerotic remodeling consistent with mechanical stabilization after fixation (Fig. 4G and H). Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrates that a ventral epidural mass associated with cervical spinal metastasis can disappear after posterior decompression and stabilization, even before the initiation of systemic therapy. The initial surgical strategy was based on a principle similar to that of separation surgery, in that posterior decompression and stabilization were intended to restore a safe relationship between the spinal cord and the ventral epidural lesion prior to subsequent therapy.10 However, the unexpected spontaneous disappearance of the ventral epidural mass during follow-up raised a more fundamental question regarding the true nature of the lesion. Given the severe neck pain on motion, extensive osseous destruction involving the C7 vertebral body and both pedicles, and a Spine Instability Neoplastic Score of 11, marked mechanical instability likely played a central role in the pathophysiology. In other words, this case suggests that posterior decompression and stabilization may not only decompress the spinal cord indirectly but also modify a ventral epidural lesion when it contains a mechanically reversible component.

The most plausible explanation is that the ventral epidural lesion contained a mechanically reversible soft tissue component that regressed after elimination of abnormal motion by posterior fixation. This concept is analogous to previously reported instability-related lesions in the cervical spine. Brophy and Hoh described a compressive ventral epidural pannus that developed after two-level cervical disc arthroplasty and resolved after posterior instrumented fusion without direct resection of the ventral lesion, supporting the idea that some ventral epidural masses may be mechanically maintained and reversible.7 Retro-odontoid pseudotumors have likewise been shown to regress after posterior fixation.6,8,9 Although no closely identical case was identified, these analogous reports support the concept that some ventral epidural lesions may contain a mechanically reversible component. From this perspective, the present lesion may have represented not only metastatic epidural disease itself but also a reactive lesion maintained or aggravated by instability.

A pure tumor explanation appears insufficient. The lesion was contiguous with the C7 vertebral body, and pathological examination of tissue obtained from the C7 pedicle confirmed metastatic prostate adenocarcinoma. Thus, the overall disease process was unquestionably metastatic. Nevertheless, complete disappearance of the ventral epidural mass within 1 month before initiation of hormonal therapy is difficult to explain by tumor regression alone. A more reasonable interpretation is that the lesion was a mixed epidural mass, consisting of metastatic tumor background with a superimposed reactive and mechanically reversible component.

An epidural hematoma should also be considered because the patient developed tetraparesis after a fall. Both traumatic and spontaneous cervical epidural hematomas have been reported to undergo spontaneous resolution.11–13 However, this hypothesis does not adequately explain the preceding weeks of gait unsteadiness, repeated falls, and marked neck pain on motion, all of which suggest that the compressive lesion had likely been present before the final traumatic event. Therefore, although hemorrhagic change may have contributed to the acute neurological deterioration, epidural hematoma alone is unlikely to account for the entire clinical course.

Posterior migration of disc or fibrocartilaginous tissue is another possible explanation.14,15 In the present case, however, the lesion appeared contiguous with the vertebral body rather than the disc space, making a typical migrated disc fragment less likely. Nevertheless, displacement of disc- or fibrocartilage-like tissue in the setting of metastatic instability remains a conceivable mechanism.

A limitation of this case is that postoperative gadolinium-enhanced MRI was not obtained. Because the ventral epidural mass had already disappeared on T2-weighted images at 1 month after surgery, additional contrast-enhanced imaging was not performed. Therefore, the exact postoperative tissue composition of the ventral epidural lesion could not be definitively determined.

Although this single case should not be interpreted as evidence that ventral epidural lesions can generally be managed without direct resection, it does suggest that posterior decompression and stabilization may be sufficient to restore a safe relationship between the spinal cord and a ventral epidural lesion in selected patients with marked metastatic cervical instability. In this respect, the present case supports a principle similar to that of separation surgery. To our knowledge, no closely identical case has previously been reported.

Hormonal therapy is central to the systemic management of metastatic prostate cancer and is important for long-term control of metastatic lesions.16 However, in the present case, hormonal therapy was initiated only after the 1-month postoperative MRI study had already demonstrated complete disappearance of the ventral epidural mass. Therefore, the early regression of the ventral epidural lesion is unlikely to be attributable to hormonal therapy and was more likely related to posterior stabilization. In contrast, the subsequent improvement in vertebral body morphology and osteosclerotic remodeling may have been multifactorial, reflecting reduced abnormal motion after stabilization, osseous remodeling of metastatic bone, the osteoblastic nature of prostate cancer metastasis, and the later effect of hormonal therapy.17

Taken together, the present case is best understood as a mechanically unstable metastatic cervical lesion in which a ventral epidural mass contained a reversible component that regressed after posterior stabilization.

Lessons

Not all epidural compressive lesions associated with cervical spinal metastasis necessarily represent progressive tumor extension alone. In patients with marked instability, a portion of the compressive lesion may be mechanically reversible and may regress after stabilization. Recognition of this possibility may have implications for surgical strategy and for interpretation of pre- and postoperative imaging in metastatic cervical spine disease.
