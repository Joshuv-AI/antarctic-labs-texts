IL1B-Expressing Exhausted CD4+ T Cells Are Associated With Disease Aggressiveness in Glioblastoma
Authors: JINSHAN WANG, AO ZHU, JIAN WANG, BIN HU, KASHIF RAFIQ ZAHID, AILIANG ZENG, YIHENG TAN, JIA YANG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimGlioblastomas (GBMs) are aggressive primary brain tumors characterized by immune evasion and therapeutic resistance. Here, we aimed to decipher the transcriptional landscape of disease aggressiveness in GBM by employing a multi-layered transcriptomic approach.Materials and MethodsWe performed transcriptome-wide differential expression analysis using TCGA IDH-wildtype GBM data after stratifying the tumors based on epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signature scores. Functional and pathway enrichment analyses, STRING-based protein-protein interaction (PPI) network assisted with centrality and random-walk analyses, univariate and multivariate Cox survival analyses, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), single-cell RNA (scRNA) sequencing analysis, CIBERSORTx immune deconvolution, and T cell exhaustion profiling were performed. Key findings were validated in independent datasets.ResultsWe identified 2088 protein-coding genes associated with disease aggressiveness in GBM. Among 1070 upregulated genes, 432 were risk-associated and enriched for immune-inflammatory pathways. Network analysis identified IL1B and CD4 as top hub genes, both of which were independently prognostic for poor overall survival. scRNA-seq analysis attributed CD4 expression to tumor-infiltrating T cells, more than half of which also co-expressed IL1B. CIBERSORTx deconvolution suggested progressive accumulation and exhaustion of CD4⁺ T memory resting cells and Tregs along disease aggressiveness. CCL5 is preferentially expressed by tumor cells, whereas CCR1 and CCR5 by CD4⁺ T cells, supporting a CCL5-CCR1/CCR5-associated recruitment pattern. IL1B correlates with accumulation of exhausted CD4+ T memory cells and aligns with enrichment of disease aggressiveness and inflammatory signatures. IL1R1/NF-κB-related transcriptional targets downstream are coordinately upregulated in tumors having high EMT scores and high IL1B expression.ConclusionCD4⁺ T cells are recruited to GBM tumors preferentially via the CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 axis, where their exhaustion-related IL1B upregulation along disease progression aligns with mesenchymal reprogramming that hallmarks disease aggressiveness in IDH-wildtype GBM.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive and lethal primary brain tumor in adults, representing World Health Organization (WHO) grade IV gliomas and accounting for the majority of malignant primary brain tumor-related mortality worldwide (1). Despite incremental advances in understanding its molecular biology, the clinical outlook for GBM patients remains catastrophic, with a median overall survival of 14-16 months following the current standard of care comprising maximal safe surgical resection, concurrent temozolomide-based chemoradiotherapy, and adjuvant temozolomide, with a five-year survival rate persistently below 5% (2, 3). The consistent failure of novel therapeutic strategies, including anti-angiogenic agents, targeted therapies, and multiple generations of immune checkpoint inhibitors, to meaningfully improve survival in unselected GBM populations underscores the urgent need to better understand the fundamental mechanisms driving disease aggressiveness in GBM (4-6).

The 2021 WHO classification of central nervous system tumors formally integrated molecular parameters, most critically isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status, into glioma diagnosis, with IDH-wildtype as a key defining feature of GBM (7). Transcriptomic profiling has further stratified IDH-wildtype GBMs into molecular subtypes, including classical, mesenchymal, neural, and proneural, of which the mesenchymal subtype is most consistently associated with the poorest survival, greatest therapeutic resistance, and a deeply immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) (8, 9). The mesenchymal GBM phenotype is associated with the activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like transcriptional programs, which endow tumor cells with invasive, stem-like, therapy-resistant and immune-evasive properties (10-12). Despite the established clinical importance of mesenchymal reprogramming in GBM, the molecular modulators remain incompletely characterized.

The GBM TME is among the most immunosuppressive in human oncology, characterized by abundant tumor-associated macrophages, microglia, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and functionally impaired lymphocytes that collectively enable tumor immune evasion and contribute to the failure of immune checkpoint blockade (13, 14). Among tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells have historically received the most attention in GBM immunology; however, emerging evidence has revealed that CD4⁺ T cells are surprisingly abundant in the GBM TME and play complex roles in shaping both anti-tumor immunity and tumor-promoting inflammation (15, 16). CD4⁺ T cells encompass functionally heterogeneous subpopulations including T helper subsets, regulatory T cells (Tregs), T follicular helper cells, and memory T cells, each exerting distinct and sometimes opposing effects on tumor immunity and tumor cell behavior (17, 18). However, how these subtypes contribute to orchestrating disease aggressiveness in GBM is still not fully elucidated.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing, immune deconvolution of bulk transcriptomic data, and multi-omic integration have provided powerful new frameworks for dissecting the cellular and molecular crosstalk between tumor cells and the immune microenvironment at unprecedented resolution (19, 20). In the present study, we employed a multi-layered transcriptomic approach to decipher the transcriptional landscape of disease aggressiveness in GBM. We first stratified GBM tumors based on EMT scores, performed differential expression analysis and identified 2,088 protein-coding genes associated with tumor aggressiveness, including 1,070 upregulated genes, of which 432 were linked to poor survival and were enriched in immune-inflammatory pathways. Network-based prioritization highlighted IL1B and CD4 as central hub genes independently predictive of worse overall survival. Single-cell analysis revealed that CD4 expression predominantly originates from tumor-infiltrating T cells, more than half of which co-express IL1B, while immune deconvolution demonstrated progressive accumulation of exhausted CD4⁺ T memory resting cells and immunosuppressive Treg cells with increasing disease aggressiveness. Consistent with a potential recruitment-associated pattern, tumor-derived CCL5 and its receptors CCR1 and CCR5 on CD4⁺ T cells were associated with CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration into GBM tumors, whereas IL1B expression correlated with enrichment of inflammatory and aggressive tumor signatures, alongside increased exhausted CD4⁺ T cell infiltration. Furthermore, IL1R1/NF-κB-related transcriptional programs were coordinately elevated in tumors with high EMT and IL1B expression, supporting an association between inflammatory-immune signaling and disease aggressiveness in GBM.

Materials and Methods

TCGA-GBM cohort assembly and preprocessing. RNA sequencing data and matched clinical annotations for GBM were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) through the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Raw gene-level read counts generated by the STAR aligner (augmented STAR gene counts) were downloaded for all available GBM cases. Samples were filtered to retain only IDH-wildtype primary GBM tumors with available RNA-seq data, yielding a final cohort of n=316 tumors. Duplicate samples, recurrent tumors, and IDH-mutant cases were excluded. Gene expression data were normalized using variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) as implemented in the DESeq2 package (v1.36.0) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) in R (v4.5.2) (https://r-project.org/). Ensembl gene IDs were retained throughout preprocessing and mapped to HGNC gene symbols for downstream analyses using the biomaRt package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/biomaRt/). Clinical variables including age at diagnosis, gender, vital status, days to death, days to last follow-up, O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status, and GBM molecular subtype (classical, mesenchymal, neural, proneural) were extracted from the TCGA clinical data manifest.

EMT scoring and patient stratification. EMT scores were quantified for each tumor using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) as implemented in the GSVA package (v1.44.0) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/GSVA.html). The Hallmark_EMT gene set from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (https://gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) was used as the reference EMT signature. Samples were then ranked by EMT score and stratified into tertiles: the top tertile (n=104) was designated High EMT, the middle tertile (n=108) was designated Medium EMT, and the bottom tertile (n=104) was designated Low EMT. This stratification was used for all subsequent differential expression, survival, and immune analyses.

Differential gene expression analysis. Differential gene expression analysis between High EMT (n=104) and Low EMT (n=104) tumors was performed using DESeq2 (v1.36.0). DESeq2 modeling was performed on raw integer gene-level counts. Genes with fewer than 10 counts across all samples were excluded prior to analysis. The DESeq2 object was constructed using EMT group as the design variable (design = ~ EMT_group). Wald test statistics were used to compute log₂ fold changes (High EMT vs. Low EMT) and adjusted p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by adjusted p-value <0.05 and |log₂FC| >0. Only protein-coding genes were retained for downstream analysis, as annotated by Ensembl BioMart (GRCh38) (https://www.ensembl.org/info/data/biomart/index.html). Genes belonging to the canonical Hallmark_EMT gene set were excluded. The remaining protein-coding DEGs were classified as upregulated (log₂FC >0, padj <0.05) or downregulated (log₂FC <0, padj <0.05) genes. Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.4.0) (https://cran.r-project.org/package=ggplot2) in R, with gene categories color-coded as described. A heatmap of the top 25 up and downregulated genes was generated using the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.12.0) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/ComplexHeatmap.html), with Z-score normalization applied per gene across all samples.

Survival analysis. Overall survival analysis was performed using the survival (v3.4.0) (https://cran.r-project.org/package=survival) and survminer (v0.4.9) (https://cran.r-project.org/package=survminer) packages in R. For univariate analysis, expression of each gene was dichotomized at its optimal expression cutoff using the maxstat package (https://cran.r-project.org/package=maxstat), which identifies the cutpoint maximizing the log-rank test statistic. Univariate Cox proportional hazards models were fitted for each gene, and hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and log-rank p-values were computed. Genes with HR<1 and p<0.05 were classified as protective, and genes with HR>1 and p<0.05 were classified as poor-prognosis. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for IL1B and CD4 using optimal cutpoints identified by maxstat. Statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test. Forest plots were visualized using ggplot2. For multivariate Cox analysis, IL1B and CD4 were simultaneously tested as the primary covariates in a joint multivariate model adjusted for age at diagnosis (dichotomized at 60 years), gender, and MGMT promoter methylation status, in patients with complete clinical data (n=237).

Functional enrichment and pathway analyses. Gene Ontology (GO) (https://geneontology.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.0) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/clusterProfiler.html) in R with the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database (v3.15.0) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/data/annotation/html/org.Hs.eg.db.html). Enrichment analyses were conducted separately for the protective genes (downregulated in high EMT and associated with good survival) and risk-associated genes (upregulated in high EMT and associated with poor survival). Gene symbols were converted to Entrez IDs using the bitr function. GO enrichment was performed for Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function ontologies using enrichGO with a minimum gene set size of 10 and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (q-value cutoff 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment was performed using enrichKEGG with the same parameters. Results were visualized as bubble plots using the ggplot2 and enrichplot packages (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/enrichplot.html).

Protein-protein interaction network analysis. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) data for the 432 novel poor-prognosis EMT modulators were retrieved from the STRING database (v11.5, Homo sapiens, 9606) (https://string-db.org/) using a combined interaction score threshold of ≥0.4. The network was constructed and analyzed in R using the igraph package (v1.4.0) (https://cran.r-project.org/package=igraph). Network visualization was performed using igraph with node size and color scaled by hub score. Network centrality was quantified using the hub score (principal eigenvector centrality), computed via the hub_score function in igraph. Random Walk with Restart (RWR) analysis was performed with a restart probability of 0.7, using all 432 poor-prognosis genes as seed nodes, to identify genes with the highest network influence accounting for both direct and indirect connectivity. Genes were ranked by both hub score and RWR score independently, and top 20 were visualized in horizontal bar charts using ggplot2.

External proteomic and transcriptomic validation. Protein abundance and RNA expression data for GBM tumor (n=88) and normal brain (n=9-10) samples were obtained from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) through the cProSite (https://cprosite.ccr.cancer.gov/). IL1B and CD4 mRNA expression (log₂) and CD4 protein abundance (log₂ ratio) were compared between tumor and normal groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Results were visualized as boxplots. The GSE16011 dataset (21) comprising 159 GBM tumors and 8 normal brain samples was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) via the GEOquery package (v2.64.0) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/GEOquery.html) in R. EMT scores were computed for each GBM sample based on Hallmark_EMT (as mentioned previously). Samples were stratified into EMT tertiles (Low, Medium, High EMT; n=53 per group). CD4 and IL1B expression were compared between normal and tumor samples and across EMT groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. Single-cell RNA sequencing data from the Neftel et al. (22) IDH-wildtype GBM dataset (GEO accession: GSE131928) were processed and analyzed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) (https://satijalab.org/seurat/) in R. The original study profiled both Smart-seq2 and 10X single-cell datasets; for the present analysis, we used the processed adult Smart-seq2 subset with available cell-level annotations. The processed TPM expression matrix for adult IDH-wildtype GBM tumors was loaded and filtered to retain adult samples only (n=5,742 cells after exclusion of pediatric tumors). The retained cells were distributed across 20 harmonized patient/tumor IDs, 27 tumor/sample labels, and 92 plate/sample IDs, which were recovered from the original MGH cell barcodes. Cell type annotations provided in the original publication were used directly, classifying cells into six categories: GBM tumor cells (n=4,245), T cells (n=536), oligodendrocytes (n=509), macrophages (n=226), immune mixed (n=145), and unknown (n=81). For dimensionality reduction, normalized RNA expression was processed using Seurat’s standard workflow. Variable features were identified, data were scaled, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using 30 principal components with seed=42. UMAP visualization was generated from principal components 1-20 using the uwot implementation (https://cran.r-project.org/package=uwot), cosine distance metric, 30 nearest neighbors, two output dimensions, min.dist=0.3, spread=1, and seed=42. Available per-cell quality metrics in the retained object showed nFeature_RNA values ranging from 3,005 to 11,890 genes per cell, with a median of 5,589.5, and nCount_RNA values ranging from 4,976.76 to 24,183.83, with a median of 14,645.34. No additional post-download filtering based on nFeature_RNA or nCount_RNA was applied beyond retaining the adult annotated cells from the processed object. The percentage of cells expressing each gene was calculated per cell type using a detection threshold of log₂(TPM+1) >0, corresponding to the detection threshold used for single-cell feature and co-expression analyses. The percentage of cells expressing each gene was calculated within each major cell type. Co-expression analysis within Neftel-annotated T cells was performed by classifying each T cell into one of four mutually exclusive populations: CD4⁺IL1B⁺, CD4⁺IL1B⁻, CD4⁻IL1B⁺, and CD4⁻IL1B⁻. Chemokine ligand expression in GBM tumor cells and chemokine receptor expression in CD4⁺ versus CD4⁻ T cells were quantified as the percentage of cells expressing each gene above the detection threshold. All bar-plots were generated using ggplot2 with genes ranked by expression frequency in descending order.

Immune cell deconvolution. Immune cell composition of TCGA-GBM IDH-wildtype tumors (n=316) was estimated using CIBERSORTx with the LM22 leukocyte gene signature matrix (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/), which deconvolutes bulk RNA-seq data into 22 immune cell subsets. VST-normalized expression data were formatted and submitted to the CIBERSORTx web portal (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu) using the B-mode batch correction option. Samples with a CIBERSORTx deconvolution p<0.05 were retained for downstream analysis. Absolute immune cell scores for all 22 cell types were extracted and merged with EMT group and clinical annotations. CD4⁺ T cell subsets (CD4⁺ T naive, CD4⁺ T memory resting, CD4⁺ T memory activated, T follicular helper cells, and regulatory T cells/Tregs) were analyzed across EMT groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons (Bonferroni correction). Spearman correlations between CD4, IL1B, CCR1, and CCR5 mRNA expression and absolute immune cell scores were computed using cor.test. Samples were dichotomized into High/Low CD4 and High/Low IL1B groups at the median expression value. All correlation scatter plots were generated using ggplot2 with linear regression lines and 95% confidence intervals overlaid.

T cell exhaustion analysis. Expression levels of six established T cell exhaustion markers, PDCD1 (PD-1), HAVCR2 (TIM-3), LAG3, TIGIT, CTLA4, and ENTPD1 (CD39), were extracted from the VST-normalized TCGA-GBM expression matrix. Expression was compared across EMT groups (Low, Medium, High EMT) and CD4 expression groups (Low CD4, High CD4) using Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, respectively. Results were visualized as boxplots with individual data points overlaid, using ggplot2 and ggpubr (https://cran.r-project.org/package=ggpubr) packages. To evaluate exhaustion-associated features at single-cell resolution, the same six exhaustion markers were also analyzed within T-cell compartment from Neftel et al. (22). A single-cell exhaustion score was calculated for each annotated T cell as the mean expression of PDCD1, HAVCR2, LAG3, TIGIT, CTLA4, and ENTPD1. Exhaustion scores were compared between CD4⁺ and CD4⁻ annotated T cells, between CD4⁺IL1B⁺ cells and all remaining annotated T cells, and between CD4⁺IL1B⁺ and CD4⁻IL1B⁻ cells using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Results were visualized as violin plots with embedded boxplots using ggplot2 and ggpubr.

CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 recruitment axis analysis. To characterize the chemokine-mediated recruitment of CD4⁺ T cells into the GBM TME, CCL5, CCR1, and CCR5 mRNA expression were extracted from the VST-normalized TCGA-GBM matrix and compared across EMT groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons. Spearman correlations between CCR1 and CCR5 expression and CD4⁺ T cell subset scores estimated by CIBERSORTx were computed as described above. In the Neftel et al. (22) scRNA-seq dataset, the percentage of GBM tumor cells expressing each chemokine ligand and the percentage of CD4⁺ versus CD4⁻ T cells expressing each chemokine receptor were quantified using a detection threshold of log₂(TPM+1) >0.

Gene set enrichment analysis. Pre-ranked Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea package (v1.22.0) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/fgsea.html) in R. For IL1B-centered GSEA, all protein-coding genes were ranked by their Spearman correlation coefficient with IL1B expression across the TCGA-GBM IDH-wildtype cohort (n=316). The ranked gene list was used as input and GSEA was performed with 1,000 permutations. Normalized enrichment scores (NES) and adjusted p-values (Benjamini-Hochberg) were computed. Pathways with padj <0.05 were considered significant. Mountain plots for selected pathways were generated using ggplot2.

Downstream signaling analysis. IL1R1 mRNA expression was extracted from the VST-normalized TCGA-GBM matrix and compared across EMT groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons. A curated panel of 18 established NF-κB transcriptional target genes was assembled from published literature, spanning six functional categories: NF-κB core pathway components (NFKB1, RELA, NFKBIA, IKBKB), pro-inflammatory cytokines (CXCL8, CCL2, IL6, TNF, IL1A), EMT regulators (SNAI1, TWIST1, VIM, FN1, CDH1), invasion mediators (MMP9, MMP1), angiogenesis (VEGFA), and immune evasion (CD274/PD-L1). Mean expression per group was computed for three EMT groups (Low, Medium, High EMT) and two IL1B groups (Low IL1B, High IL1B). Z-score normalization was applied per gene across all five groups combined. The resulting heatmap was generated using ggplot2.

Statistical analysis. All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.5.2) with Bioconductor (v3.21) (https://bioconductor.org/). Differential expression was assessed using DESeq2 with the Wald test and the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for three-group comparisons and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two-group comparisons, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple pairwise comparisons where indicated. Spearman rank correlation was used for all correlation analyses due to the non-normal distribution of absolute immune cell scores. Survival analysis employed the log-rank test for Kaplan-Meier comparisons and Cox proportional hazards regression for univariate and multivariate analyses. All p-values are two-sided. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05 unless otherwise stated.

Results

Deciphering the transcriptional landscape of disease aggressiveness in GBM. To systematically decipher the transcriptional landscape of disease aggressiveness in GBM, we stratified IDH-wildtype TCGA-GBM tumors into High EMT, Medium EMT, and Low EMT groups based on enrichment of the hallmark_EMT gene set (23). Differential gene expression analysis between High and Low EMT tumors using DESeq2 identified 2,088 significantly differentially expressed protein-coding genes after excluding genes belonging to the canonical EMT signature used for stratification. Of these, 1,070 genes were significantly upregulated and 1,018 were significantly downregulated in High EMT tumors (Figure 1A). These findings indicated that High EMT GBM tumors are characterized by a shift from neural/proneural transcriptional programs toward mesenchymal gene expression (Figure 1B). To identify genes with independent prognostic significance, we performed univariate Cox proportional hazards survival analysis on both gene sets. Among the downregulated genes, 101 were significantly associated with better overall survival when highly expressed (HR <1), hereafter termed as protective genes (Figure 1C). Conversely, among the upregulated genes, 432 were significantly associated with worse overall survival (HR >1), hereafter termed as risk-associated genes (Figure 1D). Collectively, these results identify a set of novel protein-coding genes associated with GBM aggressiveness, encompassing both favorable (protective) and unfavorable (risk-associated) prognostic factors.

Divergent biological programs are enriched between protective and risk-associated players of disease aggressiveness in GBM. To characterize the biological processes underlying the two gene sets, we performed comprehensive GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. The protective genes were predominantly enriched for chromatin organization and epigenetic regulatory processes. GO Biological Process analysis revealed significant enrichment for nucleosome assembly, nucleosome organization, protein-DNA complex assembly, telomere organization, and epigenetic regulation of gene expression (Figure 2A). GO Cellular Component terms were dominated by nucleosome, centromeric chromatin, and chromosomal region annotations (Figure 2B), while GO Molecular Function analysis identified structural constituent of chromatin as the most significantly enriched term (Figure 2C). KEGG analysis confirmed enrichment for ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, transcriptional dysregulation in cancer, and related epigenetic pathways (Figure 2D). Collectively, these findings suggest that protective genes are associated with chromatin structural integrity and epigenetic homeostasis, programs that are lost as tumors transition toward a mesenchymal state. Conversely, the 432 risk-associated genes were strongly enriched for immune and inflammatory processes. GO Biological Process analysis identified leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, cell chemotaxis, leukocyte chemotaxis, leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, mononuclear cell migration, and regulation of inflammatory response as the top enriched terms (Figure 2E). GO Cellular Component terms reflected the secretory and extracellular localization of these proteins, with enrichment for the following: external side of the plasma membrane, tertiary granule, specific granule, and extracellular matrix components (Figure 2F). GO Molecular Function analysis revealed significant enrichment for immune receptor activity, cytokine activity, chemokine activity, cytokine receptor binding, and chemokine receptor binding (Figure 2G). KEGG pathway analysis identified cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, hematopoietic cell lineage, complement and coagulation cascades, and chemokine signaling pathway as top enriched pathways (Figure 2H). Taken together, these results indicate that the risk-associated genes are linked to TME remodeling, cytokine and chemokine signaling, and immune cell recruitment as GBM tumors transition toward an aggressive disease state.

IL1B and CD4 are hubs among risk-associated players of disease aggressiveness in GBM. Given the dominant immune-inflammatory nature of risk-associated players of disease aggressiveness in GBM, and growing interest in immunotherapy as a key treatment modality in cancer, we next aimed to focus on this subset of genes and sought to identify the most central and network-influential players within this gene set using PPI network analysis (Figure 3A). Hub score analysis identified IL1B as the primary network hub with the highest centrality score, whereas CD4 came up as the second-ranked hub (Figure 3B). Key network members surrounding these hubs included other immune and myeloid regulators, reflecting the immune-inflammatory architecture of the network (Figure 3A). Critically, RWR analysis also independently confirmed IL1B as the top-ranked gene and CD4 as the second-ranked gene in the network (Figure 3C). This concordance between hub score and RWR rankings robustly supported IL1B and CD4 as central and network-influential nodes among the risk-associated players of disease aggressiveness in GBM.

IL1B and CD4 are overexpressed and independently predict poor overall survival in GBM. To establish the clinical relevance of IL1B and CD4 in GBM, we first examined their expression in tumor versus normal brain tissue. Both IL1B and CD4 mRNA were significantly elevated in GBM tumor tissue compared to normal brain in the CPTAC Brain Cancer proteogenomic dataset (Figure 4A-B). In addition, CD4 protein was also significantly more abundant in GBM tumors compared to normal brain tissue providing orthogonal evidence at the protein level (Figure 4C), though IL1B protein expression data were not available. Notably, CD4 and IL1B mRNA were significantly elevated in GBM tumors compared to normal brain tissue in samples from an independent GEO dataset, GSE16011 (Figure 4D and E). Furthermore, within the GSE16011 GBM cohort, both CD4 and IL1B expression were significantly upregulated in tumors with high EMT scores (Figure 4F-G). These findings suggest that the upregulation of CD4 and IL1B in GBM is associated with disease aggressiveness in GBM. As univariate analysis established high IL1B and CD4 expression as significantly associated with poor overall survival in GBM (Figure 4H and I), we next aimed to determine whether these associations were independent of established clinical prognostic factors. Interestingly, both IL1B and CD4 expression remained independent predictors of poor overall survival after adjustment for age at diagnosis, gender, and MGMT promoter methylation status (Figure 4J). Together, these results support IL1B and CD4 as independent biomarkers of poor prognosis in IDH-wildtype GBM.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis identifies CD4⁺ T cells as a major IL1B expressing population in the GBM TME. The enrichment of immune-related mechanisms (Figure 2) and the emergence of the canonical T cell surface marker, CD4, as one of the central hubs of disease aggressiveness in GBM (Figure 3 and Figure 4) hinted towards the involvement of CD4-expressing T cells in the process. To resolve the cellular identity of CD4-expressing cells in the GBM TME, we interrogated single-cell RNA sequencing data of IDH-wildtype GBM. UMAP visualization identified that CD4 expression was highly enriched and concentrated within the T cell cluster, with minimal expression detected in other cell populations (Figure 5A and B), confirming the infiltration of CD4+ T cells within GBM. Notably, IL1B expression was strongly enriched within the T cell cluster as well (Figure 5C). Quantitative analysis of the percentage of cells expressing each of these hub genes per cell type confirmed that T cells showed markedly higher expression of both CD4 and IL1B compared to all other cell populations (Figure 5D). Co-expression analysis within tumor-infiltrating T cells revealed that the majority of these cells co-expressed both CD4 and IL1B simultaneously, followed by cells expressing CD4 alone, whereas only small minority populations expressed IL1B without CD4 (Figure 5E). Because IL1B is commonly associated with myeloid/inflammatory compartments, we further evaluated whether the CD4⁺IL1B⁺ signal was not an outlier driven by a single patient. Notably, CD4⁺IL1B⁺ cells were detected in all patient/tumor IDs containing annotated T cells (Figure 5F). The largest contributing patient, MGH105, accounted for 33.45% of the pooled CD4⁺IL1B⁺ annotated T-cell population (Figure 5G), indicating that the signal was not restricted to a single specimen, although its abundance varied across patients. Taken together, these findings support T cells as major IL1B-expressing population in the GBM TME.

CD4⁺ T memory and regulatory T cells accumulate in aggressive GBM and exhibit markers of T cell exhaustion. To characterize the CD4⁺ T cell infiltration landscape across disease aggressiveness in GBM, we performed immune cell deconvolution of the TCGA-GBM IDH-wildtype cohort using CIBERSORTx with the LM22 leukocyte gene signature matrix. Among the five CD4⁺ T cell subsets estimated by CIBERSORTx, CD4⁺ T memory resting cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) were significantly and progressively enriched with increasing EMT severity (Figure 6A). Correlation analysis confirmed that CD4 mRNA expression was most strongly correlated with infiltrating CD4⁺ T memory resting cells and Tregs (Figure 6B). Given that persistent antigen exposure and chronic inflammatory signaling within aggressive TMEs are known to drive progressive T cell dysfunction and exhaustion (24), we next assessed whether these accumulating antigen-experienced CD4⁺ T cell populations exhibit features of exhaustion. Notably, the accumulation of CD4⁺ T cells in High EMT tumors was accompanied by the progressive upregulation of T cell exhaustion markers (Figure 6C). Consistent with this, High CD4 tumors exhibited significantly elevated expression of T cell exhaustion markers compared to Low CD4 tumors (Figure 6D). To further confirm exhaustion-associated features at single-cell resolution, we calculated exhaustion scores within the T-cell compartment from single-cell data using the same six exhaustion markers. Notably, CD4⁺ T cells showed significantly higher exhaustion scores than CD4⁻ T cells. In addition, CD4⁺IL1B⁺ T cells showed higher exhaustion scores compared to all other annotated T cells combined, and a borderline increase in exhaustion scores compared with CD4⁻IL1B⁻ T cells (Figure 6E). Taken together, these data indicate that CD4⁺ T cells exhibit exhaustion-associated features in aggressive GBM tumors.

The CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 chemokine axis is associated with CD4⁺ T cells infiltration into the GBM TME. Having established that CD4⁺ T memory cells and Tregs accumulate in aggressive GBM tumors, we next sought to identify the molecular mechanism associated with their recruitment. Chemokine ligand expression profiling in GBM tumor cells from scRNA-seq data revealed that CCL5 was the most abundantly expressed chemokine in GBM tumor cells (Figure 7A). Interestingly, chemokine receptor profiling within the T cell compartment revealed that CCR1 and CCR5, the cognate receptors for CCL5, were preferentially expressed on CD4⁺ T cells (Figure 7B). This differential expression of CCR1 and CCR5 on CD4⁺ T cells is consistent with involvement of the CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 axis as the primary chemokine recruitment pathway directing CD4⁺ T cells into the GBM TME. To evaluate this association at the population level in bulk RNA-seq data, we examined CCL5, CCR1, and CCR5 mRNA expression across EMT groups in the TCGA-GBM IDH-wildtype cohort. All three genes were significantly and progressively upregulated with increasing EMT severity (Figure 7C). Furthermore, correlation analysis confirmed that both CCR1 and CCR5 mRNA expression were most strongly correlated with infiltrating CD4⁺ T memory cells, followed by Tregs (Figure 7D and E). Collectively, these data suggest that the CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 chemokine recruitment axis is coordinately upregulated in aggressive GBM tumors and is associated with the infiltration of CD4⁺ T cells.

IL1B expression in CD4⁺ T cells aligns with the mesenchymal program and inflammatory signaling in GBM. Having identified CD4⁺ T cells as major IL1B-expressing population in aggressive GBM, we next investigated transcriptional programs associated with IL1B expression in GBM tumor biology. Spearman correlation analysis in the TCGA-GBM IDH-wildtype cohort confirmed that IL1B mRNA expression was most strongly correlated with CD4⁺ T memory resting cell infiltration and Treg infiltration (Figure 8A). This is consistent with our single-cell finding that CD4⁺ T cells frequently co-express IL1B (Figure 5E) and supports the conclusion that exhausted CD4⁺ T memory cells are associated with IL1B expression within the immunosuppressive GBM microenvironment. IL1B signals through its cognate receptor IL1R1 on target cells. Critically, IL1R1 mRNA expression was significantly and progressively upregulated with increasing EMT severity in the TCGA cohort (Figure 8B). To determine the transcriptional programs associated with IL1B expression in GBM, we performed GSEA and found that High IL1B tumors showed significant enrichment of signatures related to disease aggressiveness (Figure 8C and D) and inflammation (Figure 8E and F). The strong enrichment for inflammatory programs and NF-κB signaling in High IL1B tumors led us to examine the expression of a curated panel of 18 established NF-κB transcriptional target genes spanning six functional categories: NF-κB core components, pro-inflammatory cytokines, EMT regulators, invasion mediators, angiogenesis and immune evasion, across tumor aggressiveness and IL1B expression groups in the TCGA cohort (Figure 8G). NF-κB target genes across all six functional categories were coordinately upregulated in High EMT tumors and independently in High IL1B tumors (Figure 8G), suggesting an association between NF-κB signaling, IL1B expression and CD4+ T cell exhaustion in GBM.

Discussion

GBM remains one of the most therapeutically intractable malignancies in human medicine, and its resistance to immune-mediated elimination represents one of the most formidable barriers to improving patient outcomes (2, 3). In the present study, we identified an immuno-inflammatory transcriptional pattern in which exhaustion of infiltrating CD4⁺ T-cell and associated IL1B expression are linked to tumor aggressiveness in GBM. Using a systematic multi-layered integration of bulk transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing and immune deconvolution, we demonstrate that IL1B-producing exhausted CD4⁺ T cells are a hallmark of the most aggressive, mesenchymal GBM phenotype. Notably, both IL1B and CD4 independently predicted poor overall survival in GBM, suggesting that CD4⁺ T cell infiltration and exhaustion are not merely bystanders of immune dysfunction, but are closely associated with disease aggressiveness and tumor progression in GBM.

Our transcriptome-wide identification of protein-coding players of disease aggressiveness in GBM revealed a striking functional dichotomy between the protective and poor-prognosis gene sets. The 101 protective genes were enriched predominantly for chromatin organization, nucleosome assembly, and epigenetic regulatory processes (Figure 1 and Figure 2), consistent with emerging evidence that loss of epigenetic homeostasis is a key contributor to mesenchymal reprogramming and stemness acquisition in GBM (25). The progressive silencing of chromatin regulatory programs in aggressive tumors is concordant with recent studies demonstrating that the mesenchymal GBM cell state is associated with broad epigenetic remodeling, including the loss of polycomb repressive complex activity and the gain of accessible chromatin (26, 27). On the other hand, the 432 poor-prognosis upregulated modulators were functionally dominated by immune cell recruitment, chemokine signaling, cytokine activity, and inflammatory response processes (Figure 1 and Figure 2), a finding that supports an association between immune-inflammatory crosstalk and EMT-associated aggressiveness in GBM. This is consistent with the growing recognition that the aggressive GBM phenotype is dynamically maintained through bidirectional communication with the immunosuppressive TME (28, 29).

Our network analysis of risk-associated genes identified IL1B and CD4 as key hubs, providing a systems-level rationale for the centrality of immune-related mechanisms in aggressive GBM (Figure 3). The independent prognostic significance of both IL1B and CD4 in multivariate Cox regression adjusted for established clinical covariates (Figure 4) also represents a clinically important finding. While both CD4 and IL1B have been implicated in poor prognosis and therapy resistance across multiple cancer types including GBM, colorectal, breast, and lung cancers (30-32), to the best of our knowledge, their independent prognostic significance in IDH-wildtype GBM specifically, adjusted for age, gender, and MGMT methylation status, has not been previously established. The independent prognostic value of CD4 is particularly noteworthy, as CD4 mRNA expression in bulk tumor reflects predominantly CD4⁺ T memory resting and Treg infiltration (as confirmed by our CIBERSORTx correlations), rather than CD4 expression on tumor cells or other non-T cell populations (Figure 5 and Figure 6). In addition, the finding that high CD4 predicts poor survival in GBM may appear counterintuitive given the general paradigm that T cell infiltration is associated with favorable prognosis in other tumors, a phenomenon underlying the success of immune checkpoint blockade (33, 34).

One of the most conceptually significant findings is the accumulation of exhausted CD4⁺ T cells in GBM, as exhaustion markers progressively upregulated with disease aggressiveness and significantly elevated in High CD4 tumors (Figure 6), indicating that the most mesenchymal GBM tumors harbor the most exhausted CD4⁺ T cell microenvironment. This observation is consistent with recent reports demonstrating that the GBM mesenchymal subtype is associated with the most severe T cell exhaustion transcriptional signatures across human cancers (35). Critically, our findings recontextualize this exhaustion not merely as evidence of immune failure but as a mechanistically consequential state in which exhausted CD4⁺ T cells retain IL1B production capacity (Figure 5). This is supported by the well-established hierarchy of functional loss during T cell exhaustion, in which cytolytic killing capacity and IL-2 production are among the earliest functions lost, while pro-inflammatory cytokine production can be retained to a certain extent (36). This retained cytokine production has recently been recognized as a mechanism through which exhausted tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes paradoxically promote tumor progression in contexts where their cytokines activate oncogenic signaling pathways in tumor cells (37). Our findings extend this paradigm specifically to the GBM context, demonstrating through single-cell co-expression analysis that more than 50% of tumor-infiltrating T cells simultaneously express CD4 and IL1B, and that IL1B expression in bulk RNA-seq correlates most strongly with CD4⁺ T memory resting cell infiltration (Figure 5 and Figure 8).

The preferential expression of CCR1 and CCR5 on CD4⁺ compared to CD4⁻ T cells, combined with the progressive upregulation of CCL5, CCR1, and CCR5 across EMT groups in the TCGA cohort, and the strong correlation of CCR1 and CCR5 with CD4⁺ T memory resting and Treg fractions (Figure 7), collectively provide convergent multi-platform evidence for the CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 axis is associated with CD4⁺ T cell infiltration with increasing disease aggressiveness. Notably, Tregs are co-recruited alongside CD4⁺ T memory cells via the CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 axis, which is consistent with established evidence that Tregs express CCR5 at high levels and are effectively recruited by CCL5 in tumor settings (38). Within the GBM TME, Tregs suppress CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cell activity through multiple mechanisms, including CTLA4-mediated co-stimulation blockade, IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, and IL-2 deprivation, thereby creating a permissive environment for tumor growth and EMT amplification (39, 40). The co-recruitment of Tregs and exhausted CD4⁺ T memory cells via a shared CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 mechanism represents a particularly potent immunosuppressive configuration in which Tregs suppress residual anti-tumor immune activity while exhaustion of CD4⁺ T memory cells is associated with IL1B expression (41), a functional division of pro-tumorigenic labor that we propose represents a defining feature of disease aggressiveness in GBM.

This study proposes several actionable therapeutic targets at multiple nodes. First, our findings suggest that the IL1B and CD4 expression levels, individually or in combination, may be added to the list of clinically useful biomarkers for stratifying GBM patients into high-risk subgroups who may benefit most from immune-targeted interventions (42). Given that both markers are independently prognostic in multivariate analysis, their incorporation into GBM risk stratification frameworks warrants prospective evaluation in future clinical trial designs. At the cytokine level, IL1R1 blockade using anakinra (recombinant IL1 receptor antagonist) or canakinumab (anti-IL1B monoclonal antibody) could interrupt IL1B paracrine signaling to GBM tumor cells and thereby suppress mesenchymal reprogramming (43, 44). Canakinumab has demonstrated anti-tumor activity in non-small cell lung cancer in the CANTOS trial (44) and is currently under investigation in multiple solid tumor contexts, yet it has not been evaluated in GBM. Anakinra has shown preliminary safety and biological activity in glioma patients in early-phase studies (43) and represents a promising candidate for combination with standard chemoradiotherapy in mesenchymal IDH-wildtype GBM, particularly given the progressive IL1R1 upregulation we observe with increasing disease aggressiveness (Figure 8).

Several important limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, the bulk RNA sequencing data from TCGA-GBM, while representing the largest available IDH-wildtype GBM cohort with comprehensive multi-omic profiling, cannot resolve the cellular source of gene expression signals at single-cell resolution. While we have used CIBERSORTx immune deconvolution and corroborated bulk findings with single-cell RNA sequencing data, the CIBERSORTx LM22 signature matrix was derived from peripheral blood immune cell signatures (45) and does not include exhausted T cell subsets as a distinct category, consequently classifying exhausted CD4⁺ T cells as CD4⁺ T memory resting cells, a known limitation of bulk deconvolution approaches in tumor settings. Future studies should employ tumor-specific immune deconvolution references or paired single-cell and bulk RNA-seq datasets to address this limitation. Second, the present study does not include functional experimental validation that would provide direct mechanistic proof of causality for individual nodes of the circuit. Establishing these causal relationships through in vitro co-culture systems, patient-derived GBM organoids, and in vivo mouse models represents an important priority for future work. Lastly, the scRNA-seq data (22) analyzed for this study provided valuable single-cell resolution for our co-expression and chemokine analyses, but it was generated from a relatively limited number of GBM patients and may not fully capture the cellular heterogeneity across the broader IDH-wildtype GBM population. Future validation in larger scRNA-seq cohorts would strengthen the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our multi-layered dissection of IDH-wildtype GBM distills the complexity of disease aggressiveness into an immuno-inflammatory transcriptional pattern centered on IL1B-expressing exhausted CD4⁺ T cells, which are a hallmark of the aggressive disease state. Enhanced infiltration of CD4⁺ T cell subset was associated with CCL5-CCR1/CCR5 chemokine axis and their progressive exhaustion with increasing disease severity. Notably, these exhausted CD4⁺ T cells showed IL1B co-expression, which was accompanied by increased IL1R1 expression and inflammatory signaling programs linked to mesenchymal transition, invasion, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. These findings support an association-based model in which tumor-associated immune recruitment, CD4⁺ T-cell exhaustion features, and IL1B-related inflammatory signaling are coordinated with the aggressive phenotype. Importantly, both IL1B and CD4 independently predict poor clinical outcomes in GBM. By identifying IL1B-expressing exhausted CD4⁺ T cells as correlates of disease progression rather than passive bystanders, our findings highlight a molecular link between immune dysfunction and tumor-intrinsic reprogramming and nominate this axis as a potentially actionable vulnerability for combinatorial immunomodulatory and anti-invasive strategies to mitigate GBM disease burden.
