Ionic liquid-functionalized magnetic polystyrene nanoparticles for efficient removal of methyl violet and acid red 88 from aqueous media
Authors: Taraneh Abbaspour, Sakineh Asghari, Ghasem Firouzzadeh Pasha, Mahmood Tajbakhsh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcerns about color pollution in aquatic environments have raised awareness of its environmental impact. Consequently, researchers are increasingly focused on removing colored pollutants due to their harmful effects on health and ecosystems. Metformin-modified magnetic polystyrene nanoparticles [MPS-DAB-MET].2Cl- were created as a novel solid catalyst supported by ionic liquids, and their structure was analyzed using various techniques, including TGA, FT-IR, XRD, VSM, SEM, EDS mapping, and CHN analyses. The adsorption capacity of the synthesized [MPS-DAB-MET].2Cl- was evaluated for its ability to remove Methyl Violet (MV) and acid red 88 (AR88). As a nano adsorbent, [MPS-DAB-MET].2Cl- effectively adsorbs both dyes under optimal conditions: a dye concentration of 20 mg/L, an adsorbent dosage of 20 mg, a pH of 7, and a temperature of 25 °C. The adsorption of MV and AR88 dyes was analyzed using the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models. The findings indicated a strong correlation with the Langmuir isotherm, achieving a remarkably high regression coefficient (R2 > 0.997) when compared to other models. Additionally, the maximum adsorption capacities were determined to be 434.78 mg/g for the dye MV and 476.19 mg/g for AR88. Kinetic analysis revealed that the process followed a pseudo-second-order model for both dyes, suggesting that physical adsorption was the primary mechanism affecting the adsorption rate. The thermodynamic study demonstrated that the adsorption processes were exothermic for MV and endothermic for AR88. The results showed that [MPS-DAB-MET].2Cl- is an affordable and eco-friendly nano adsorbent that effectively and efficiently removes cationic and anionic dyes from aqueous solutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20004-x.Keywords: Magnetic polystyrene, Methyl violet, Acid red 88, Metformin, Ionic liquids-supportedSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Today’s pollution poses a significant challenge to humanity; therefore, many countries around the world are considering solutions to protect the environment from ongoing destruction1,2. Pollution includes various forms such as air, water, soil, radioactive, sound, and thermal pollution, which pose a threat to the environment’s health. Therefore, it is necessary to eliminate pollution to safeguard human health, protect the ecosystem, and prevent many diseases. Various techniques such as ultrafiltration3, membrane filtration4, flocculation5, ion exchange6, oxidation7, and adsorption8 are being used for the purification and removal of organic pollutants. While these methods have certain limitations, such as significant energy requirements and intricate, time-consuming processes, the absorption techniques demonstrate encouraging outcomes and are widely utilized owing to their simple design and low associated risks.

In recent years, the surface absorption method has been one of the most common and effective methods for removing dyes and heavy metal ions from solutions, which has received worldwide attention due to its low cost, ease of implementation, low energy requirement, and high efficiency9,10. Nano-adsorbents have been of interest due to their unique properties, which include graphene-based materials11, metal oxides12, clay13, polymers14, activated carbon15, composite polymers/metal oxides16, and silicates17, to remove toxic pollutants.

Chemists are particularly interested in the development of highly active, reusable, and stabilized nano-adsorbents for environmentally friendly removal of pollutants. Compared with other adsorbents, polystyrene is a widely used polymer support in various fields such as material chemistry18, synthetic chemistry19, which is valued for its high mechanical strength20, harmlessness21, high surface area22, availability23, low cost24, easy separation25, and chemical inertness26.

Dyes play a crucial role in various industries like textiles, paper, plastics, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, and food, contributing to water pollution27,28. Organic dye compounds, which are derived from natural (plant-based) or synthetic sources, are classified into three types: anionic, cationic, and nonionic dyes. While Anionic dyes are further categorized as direct, acid, and reactive dyes, cationic dyes fall under the basic dyes category29,30. For instance, MV is a commonly used basic, cationic dye in the textile industry, particularly for dyeing cotton, acrylic, and silk. Despite various applications of MV, it is confirmed that it is toxic to organisms and harmful to the environment due to its high persistence in water. It can also easily irritate the human eyes and skin, and cause visual irritation and pollution31–33. Therefore, its removal from the wastewater before it enters the ecosystem is essential. On the other hand, AR88 is an anionic dispersed azo dye widely used in the textile, leather, paper, and wool industries due to its low cost, effective dyeing, and stable properties34–36. However, its toxicity, carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, and low biodegradability make it a significant pollutant in groundwater and surface water, posing health problems in ecological systems37. Therefore, there are numerous studies on the removal of anionic and cationic dyes from water using nano-absorbents due to the drawbacks and limitations of these dyes in industrial waste. Despite the advantages of the reported methods to reduce and eliminate dyes from the environment, they suffer from disadvantages such as the need for high energy, a long time, and low capacities. Therefore, to solve this problem, new systems for removing these dyes based on magnetic nano-absorbents were designed due to their very efficient properties, such as large surface area, high mechanical strength, suitable particle size distribution, and harmlessness for the environment. One of the most effective and easy methods for separating absorbents is the use of magnetic nano-absorbents. They are currently a valuable tool in biological and biomedical fields38–44. Their unique magnetic properties make them effective adsorbents for contaminants, which enable efficient two-phase separation and removal under external magnetic fields45–47.

Therefore, we aim to remove MV as a cationic dye and AR88 as the anionic dye from an aqueous medium using the magnetic supported ionic liquid on a polystyrene surface [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- as a sensitive nano-absorbent. The biguanide-like structure of the attached metformin (MET) on the magnetic polystyrene surface enhances catalyst homogeneity and catalytic activity, as well as its ability to absorb dyes from the aqueous medium.

Materials and methods

Chemical and reagent

For this study, all materials, including dyes, solvents, and precursors, were of high-grade purity and obtained from Merck and Sigma Aldrich, Germany. Deionized water (DI) was used to prepare the solution. All information regarding the analysis devices, materials, batch adsorption, and experimental data modeling is provided in the supporting file. Additionally, calibration curves for MV and AR88 dyes are presented in Fig. S1.

Preparation of adsorbent

Synthesis of nanomagnetic IL supported on the polystyrene ([MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl-)

A mixture of 1 g of chloromethylated polystyrene (PS-Cl) microspheres, 1.5 g of FeCl₃·6H₂O, and 0.6 g of FeCl₂·4H₂O was suspended in 12 mL of absolute ethanol. Continuous sonication at room temperature was applied until the mixture was uniformly mixed. The mixture was then heated under reflux conditions. After 1h, 10 mL of 25% NH₃·H₂O was quickly added to the reaction mixture, maintaining the same temperature, and allowed to stir for 1h. Once cooled to room temperature, the black precipitate was separated using a super magnet. It was subsequently washed multiple times with deionized water and ethanol to neutralize the solution. The resulting magnetic polystyrene microspheres (MPS) were dried in an oven at 60 °C to obtain as a black powder48.

To obtain DABCO-modified MPS [MPS-DAB].Cl-, a mixture of MPS (1 g) and 1,4-diazabicyclo[2.2.2]octane (DABCO) (2.24 g, 20 mmol) in 50 mL toluene was refluxed for two days. After cooling to room temperature, the [MPS-DAB].Cl- was separated using a super magnet and was repeatedly washed with DCM and EtOH. The resulting [MPS-DAB].Cl- was dried in an oven at 60 °C to obtain a brown powder49.

To modify the surface of [MPS-DAB].Cl- by chlorobutyl linker, a solution of 1,4-dichlorobutane (0.547 mL, 5 mmol) and [MPS-DAB].Cl- (1 g) in toluene (50 mL) was stirred under reflux conditions for 6 h. The resulting mixture was separated using a supermagnet and was washed with Et2O and ethanol. The brown powder chlorobutyl-modified [MPS-DAB-Cl]0.2Cl- was obtained after drying in the oven for one night50.

To the preparation of magnetic supported ionic liquid [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl-, 1 g of [MPS-DA-Cl]0.2Cl-, metformin (6 mmol), K2CO3 (12.0 mmol) in 20 mL DMF was refluxed for 24 h. The final nano-absorbent [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- as a brown powder was obtained after separation with an external magnet, washing with distilled water, DCM, and ethanol, and drying in an oven for one night (Fig. 1)51.

Result and discussion

Nano-adsorbent characterization

As can be seen in Fig. 2, the FT-IR spectra of the PS-Cl, MPS, [MPS.DAB]0.2Cl-, and metformin-functionalized magnetic polystyrene [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- microspheres are shown. The FT-IR spectrum of the PS-Cl showed main stretching vibration absorption peaks at 3024 cm-1 (=C–H) stretching vibration, 2927 cm-1 (–C–H), 1650–1450 cm-1 (C=C), 1262 cm-1 (H–C–Cl vibration), and 752 cm-1 (C–Cl).52 The observation of an additional peak at about 560 cm-1 in the FT-IR spectrum of the MPS related to the Fe–O stretching vibration confirms successful coating of the polystyrene surface with Fe3O4 NPs. In the FT-IR spectrum of the [MPS.DAB]0.2Cl-, disappearing the peaks at 1262 cm-1 and 752 cm-1 which ascribed into the C–Cl stretching vibration and as well as the observation of new peak at 1560 cm-1 (C-N stretching vibration) confirming the DABCO successfully attached onto the MPS surface and the C–Cl bond was replaced with C-N bond53. The FT-IR of the [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- showed a broad band around 3341 cm⁻1 related to the N–H vibrations, 2925 cm⁻1 ascribed to the C-H vibrations, 1620 cm⁻1 corresponded to the C=N vibrations, and 1477 cm⁻1 related to the C-N vibration, which exhibited ILs successfully supported onto the [MPS.DAB]0.2Cl- surface54.

Figure 3 shows the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the decomposition patterns of MPS and the [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- using a Mettler-Toledo 2-HT thermal analyzer. This analysis was conducted from 30 to 600 °C (10 °C/ min) to evaluate the composition and thermal stability of the catalyst. The TGA curves of all samples exhibit a 0.2% weight loss under less than 200 °C, resulting from the removal and evaporation of adsorbed water molecules and organic solvents. In all TGA graphs, a second weight loss occurs between 300 and 450 °C, which is attributed to the decomposition of organic components and the loss of weight from the polymer shell layer. The yield and stability of the samples decreased from MPS to [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- confirming the surface modification of magnetic polystyrene with ILs.55.

The pure polystyrene beads display a consistently smooth surface, which is crucial for their process ability in product applications. The SEM of MPS exhibited PS-Cl microsphere successfully coated with magnetic NPs. However, the integration of ILs onto the MPS surface resulted in surface roughness and increased particle size. Despite these changes, the ILs-modified PS retain an almost perfect spherical shape with a very smooth surface, indicating effective stabilization by the ionic liquids56.

According to the composite maps, the presence of expected elements, C, O, N, Cl, and Fe in elemental mapping analyses of [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- confirms the ILs-supported MPS was successfully synthesized57.

The XRD patterns of MPS, and [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- are the same, demonstrating the modification of MPS surface with ILs did not significantly change the catalyst surface59.

Batch adsorption studies

Study of [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- dosage effect

The amount of adsorbent plays a crucial role in the efficiency and capacity of pollutant adsorption. An inadequate amount of adsorbent can lead to incomplete removal of pollutants, whereas using too much may result in only marginal improvements and unnecessary expenses. Thus, optimizing the adsorbent dosage is vital for achieving an effective and economical adsorption process. As illustrated in Fig. 10, increasing the adsorbent dose from 10 to 20 mg enhances the percentage removal of both dyes (75% for MV and 72.5% for AR88), attributed to the rise in available active sites. However, higher amounts of adsorbent eventually reach a saturation point, indicating that all active sites on the adsorbent surface have been utilized. Additionally, further increases in adsorbent quantity do not significantly affect the adsorption of cationic dye, likely due to a reduction in the available dye concentration60.

Study of initial pH of the solution effect

pH is a crucial factor in determining the effectiveness of ionization during adsorption processes. The pH level of MV or AR88 solutions influences the ion exchange properties of adsorbents as well as their surface characteristics. In our study, we investigated how pH affects the adsorption of MV and AR88 by the adsorbent [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- within a pH range of 3 to 10. As can be seen in Fig. 11, for AR88 anionic dye, positively charged adsorbents exhibit better adsorption at acidic pH levels, while the percentage of adsorption significantly decreases at higher pH levels. In contrast, for cationic MV dye, negatively charged adsorbents demonstrate better adsorption at alkaline pH levels, with increased adsorption accompanying higher pH values. This phenomenon can be attributed to the polyamine structure of metformin, which enhances the electron density on the adsorbent surface, subsequently transferring a negative charge to the substrate surface61.

Effect of solution pH on the removal percentage of the adsorption MV and AR88. Condition: 20 mg of adsorbent, dye conc. 20 ppm, 120 min.

Study of temperature and contact time effect

The adsorption characteristics of the dye solution at different contact durations are illustrated in Fig. 12, which displays the findings at temperatures of 25 °C, 35 °C, and 45 °C. An increase in contact time significantly improves the interaction between the adsorbent and the adsorbate. During the initial phase of contact, the adsorbent possesses a sufficient number of available adsorption sites to bind the dyes effectively. However, as time elapses, a greater number of these sites become occupied. By the time the contact period reaches 60 min, the adsorption capacity attains a state of saturation, indicating that the quantity of dye adsorbed on the [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- is balanced by the quantity of dye being desorbed62.

Study of dye concentration and temperature effect

The adsorption data for the dye solution were obtained at concentrations of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 ppm for both dyes, measured at temperatures of 25 °C, 35 °C, and 45 °C. These experiments were conducted under optimal conditions, specifically at a pH of 7, utilizing 20 mg of adsorbent over 60 min. Investigating the impact of varying dye concentrations on the absorption rate at different temperatures provides significant insights. As illustrated in Fig. 13, the absorption rate of the dye exhibits an increase with rising temperatures; however, at elevated dye concentrations, the absorption rate diminishes due to the fixed quantity of adsorbent available63.

Effect of [MPS-DAB-MET].2Cl-concentration and temperature on the removal of MV and AR88. Condition: 20 mg of adsorbent, pH = 7, 60min.

To justify each functionalization step, we compared the adsorption capacities of Fe3O4, magnetic polystyrene (MPS), DABCO-modified MPS (MPS-DAB), and the final product [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl⁻ using MV and AR88 under identical conditions. Fe3O4 exhibited minimal adsorption due to its limited surface functionality, whereas MPS enhanced performance through increased surface area. MPS-DAB further enhanced adsorption, especially for AR88 because of basic nitrogen sites. The final adsorbent demonstrated the highest capacity, thanks to ionic liquid moieties that enhanced both ion-exchange and hydrophilic/hydrophobic interactions. These results confirm the effectiveness and importance of each modification step.

Adsorption isotherm models

Adsorption isotherms, specifically Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin models were utilized to evaluate adsorption equilibrium, which is essential for system design and process evaluation (Table 2). The R2 values for both Freundlich and Langmuir models show their suitability, as illustrated in Fig. 14. The higher regression coefficient for the Langmuir model suggests that the adsorption of MV (or AR 88) on [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- aligns more closely with the Langmuir model. The close alignment of the Langmuir isotherm with the experimental data for nano adsorbents concerning both dyes indicates that the adsorption of the dye occurs in a monolayer on the adsorbent particles. This also suggests a uniform distribution of active sites across the polymeric nano adsorbent. As presented in Table 2, the values of the Langmuir separation coefficient (RL) ranging from 0 to 1 indicate a favorable linear adsorption process. The Temkin model plot, shown in Fig. 14, is not suitable because it exhibits a lower regression coefficient compared to the other models.

Kinetic modelling

During adsorption, dye molecules transition from the aqueous solution to the adsorbent surface through physical or chemical interactions with [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- and either MV or AR88. As a result, the solution becomes clear and the adsorbent reaches a saturated state. Figure 15 and Table 3 show the results of the pseudo-first and second-order models. The R2 values for the first-order model are 0.873 and 0.939 for MV and AR88, respectively. However, the R2 values for the second-order coefficients are 0.990 and 0.981 for MV and AR88, respectively. Comparison of R2 values indicates that the data are not well-fit to the first-order model, and suggests that the pseudo-second-order kinetic model better describes the adsorption of both dyes. The equilibrium adsorption capacity from the second-order model closely aligns with the experimentally determined capacity for both dyes. This indicates that the pseudo-second-order kinetics model effectively describes the adsorption of dyes onto [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl-, as evidenced by high R2 values and minimal differences between experimental and predicted qe values. Thus, it can be concluded that the adsorption processes and their rate are primarily influenced by the chemical interactions between the dye molecules and [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- under the pseudo-second-order model. Thermodynamic study

To investigate the nature and mechanism of adsorption of AR88 and MV dyes removed by the [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- nano adsorbent, thermodynamic factors, including the change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG°, kJ/mol), enthalpy (ΔH°, kJ/mol), and entropy (ΔS°, J/mol K), were calculated from van ‘t Hoff equilibrium that illustrate in Fig. 16. As shown in Table 4, thermodynamic studies indicated a negative ΔG°, implying spontaneous adsorption. Additionally, a negative adsorption ΔH° suggests that the process is exothermic for MV and decreases the adsorption as the temperature increases. ΔH° values lower than − 20 kJ/mol indicate that physisorption interactions, such as Vander-Waals forces, are dominant64. In contrast, positive adsorption ΔH° demonstrates the process is endothermic for AR88 which improves adsorption as the temperature increases. The increased ΔS° observed for both samples suggests greater disorder at the solution-solid interface during the sorption process.

Reusability of nano adsorbent [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl-

Recovery and reusability of the magnetic absorbent [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- was investigated under optimized conditions. In this regard, the absorbent was first removed from the solution using a supermagnet and was washed with deionized water and ethanol to eliminate any adsorbed dye molecules. The separated [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- was then dried in an oven and treated with 50 mL HCl (0.1 M), making it ready for reuse. The [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- absorbent was employed for four successive adsorption cycles. The results show that a decrease in the dye adsorption percentage from 97 to 87% after 5 cycles, as exhibited in Fig. 17. The reduction in both absorption capacity and percentage is probably attributed to the obstruction of specific active site by dye molecules, which are difficult to desorb because of their interactions with the adsorbent’s surface. The SEM and XRD analyses of the reused [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- were performed to confirm the stability of the structure of the nano adsorbent after the five cycles. As can be seen in Fig. 18, the SEM and XRD results indicated that the sample retained its crystalline structure and morphology after multiple adsorption cycles, with no significant changes observed.

Mechanism

Based on experimental results and previous studies, it has been demonstrated that this mechanism primarily involves electrostatic attraction, π-π interaction, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and ion exchange81. The synthesized adsorbent with amine (NH2) and imine (C=NH–) groups related to the metformin-supported MPS can form strong interactions with MV and AR88 dyes through the hydrogen bond formation. Additionally, cationic dye MV and anionic dye AR88 exhibit strong adsorption through electrostatic interactions with ionic liquids supported on the MPS surface. Furthermore, π–π interactions occur between the aromatic moieties of dye molecule structures and the phenyl rings of polystyrene (Fig. 19).

Conclusion

This study prepared metformin and DABCO-modified magnetic polystyrene adsorbent ([MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl-) that demonstrates strong stability and effectiveness in removing MV and AR88 dyes from aqueous solutions under optimal conditions: 20 mg/L dye concentration, 20 mg [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- dosage, pH = 7, and 25 °C. The structure of synthesized [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- was characterized by techniques such as FT-IR, SEM, EDS mapping, TGA, XRD, BET and CHN. Experimental data indicate that the Langmuir and Freundlich models most accurately represent the equilibrium data for cationic (MV, R2 = 0.995) and anionic (AR88, R2 = 0.990) dyes. The maximum adsorption capacities reached 434.78 mg g−1 for MV and 476.19 mg g−1 for AR88, with an RL value ranging between 0 and 1, confirming a spontaneous and favorable process. Kinetic analysis showed that the process adhered to a pseudo-second-order model for both dyes, indicating that physical adsorption was the main mechanism influencing the adsorption rate. The thermodynamic study suggested that the adsorption processes were exothermic for MV (ΔH° = − 9.024) and endothermic for AR88 (ΔH° = 20.889), respectively. Furthermore, the [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- exhibited excellent reusability, maintaining performance over five consecutive desorption-adsorption cycles. The results showed that [MPS-DAB-MET]0.2Cl- is an affordable and eco-friendly nano-adsorbent for effectively and efficiently removing cationic and anionic dyes from aqueous solutions.
