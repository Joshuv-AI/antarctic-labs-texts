Non-Invasive respiratory support during elective intubation in preterm infants—a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Adrita Bose, Syamal Sardar, Somnath Pal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal intubation has a low success rate and can potentially be improved through apneic oxygenation. Previous studies have utilized nasal high-flow therapy during intubation, particularly in preterm infants. Notably, a significant proportion of neonates requiring intubation are already receiving non-invasive respiratory support modalities, including non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). The feasibility of maintaining non-invasive ventilation during the intubation process in newborns has not been thoroughly investigated, despite its potential physiological benefits.ObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of non-invasive ventilation through a soft nasal cannula during preterm infant intubation. The primary outcome was successful intubation on the first attempt without physiological instability, defined as a ≥ 20% decrease in arterial oxygen saturation or a heart rate < 100/min.MethodsThis unblinded parallel-group randomized controlled trial was conducted in a neonatal unit in eastern India between January and December 2024. Eligible preterm infants on NIPPV or CPAP requiring elective intubation were randomly assigned to one of two groups: the intervention group, which maintained pre-existing respiratory support via an Opti Flow junior nasal cannula during intubation, or the control group, which underwent the procedure without any respiratory assistance. The ventilatory settings remained unchanged in the intervention group, except that FiO2 could be increased to maintain SpO2.Results56 intubations were randomized into each group. First-attempt successful intubation without physiological instability was significantly lower in the control group (16/56 (28.57%) vs. 29/56 (51.79%), p = 0.021). Absolute risk reduction was 23.23% favoring intervention arm with the number needed to treat for one additional benefit was 5 [–]. The intervention demonstrated greater efficacy in infants weighing ≤ 1000 g and with a SpO2/FiO2 ratio < 1.5 at intubation. Furthermore, the intervention reduced desaturation rates (39.19% vs. 64.10%, p = 0.003), extended time to desaturation (29 vs. 20 s, p = 0.004), and shortened desaturation (7 vs. 9 s, p = 0.006) and bradycardia durations (6 vs. 8 s, p = 0.017).ConclusionContinuing pre-existing non-invasive respiratory support through a soft nasal cannula during intubation enhances first-attempt success rates without inducing physiological instability.Trial registration numberCTRI/2023/12/060931

Introduction

Non-invasive ventilation has become the standard of care in neonatology, particularly for preterm infants. Although it improves management in the delivery room and NICU, endotracheal intubation and invasive ventilation remain essential life-saving interventions. However, neonatal intubation remains challenging. The complexities increased with decreasing gestational age and infant weight. Several studies have reported varying intubation success rates. An international registry with data from developed countries has reported a successful intubation rate on the first attempt of 64% overall and 24% with inexperienced operators [1]. A similar study conducted in a low-and middle-income (LMIC) country reported success rates of 26%, 69%, and 67% for first-, second-, and third-year trainees, respectively [2]. This low intubation success rate might be attributed to a lack of opportunities, especially in sicker preterm infants [3]. In LMICs, where resources and training are constrained, this disparity is particularly significant. Strategies, such as simulation training programs, the use of video-laryngoscopy, and the application of apneic oxygenation, have shown potential for improving successful neonatal intubation [4, 5].

The patient is usually rendered apneic by muscle relaxants before intubation. Apneic oxygenation operates on the principle of delivering oxygen through diffusion across the alveolar-capillary interface in the absence of actual spontaneous respiratory effort by the patient. It prolongs apnea time and delays the onset of instability during intubation. It is primarily adapted from adult anesthesia, which is typically achieved through a low- or high-flow nasal cannula [6]. However, newborns that require intubation are usually on some form of non-invasive ventilation in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Therefore, using a nasal cannula for apneic oxygenation after removing pre-existing respiratory support in sick newborns may result in lung derecruitment. Studies have not yet investigated the option of continuing non-invasive ventilation during intubation in newborns, which appears to be more physiologically sound. Similar practices for maintaining non-invasive respiratory support during surfactant administration are already in place, such as less invasive surfactant administration (LISA) [7]. Therefore, we sought to explore the feasibility, safety, and effectiveness of non-invasive ventilation during intubation in preterm newborns.

Methods

This parallel-group, open-label, non-blinded randomized controlled trial was conducted in a tertiary-care neonatal unit in eastern India from January 2024 to December 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Institutional ethics committee approved the study. In our unit, we used either non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) as the primary respiratory support. Both NIPPV and CPAP are provided by ventilators (Drager Babylog VN 600 or Servo-n Getinge) with built-in software for non-invasive ventilation, including a facility for leak compensation [8, 9]. Bubble CPAP was discontinued in our unit in 2018. A heated humidified high-flow nasal cannula (HHHFNC) can be used as a post-extubation respiratory support along with NIPPV or CPAP but not as a primary respiratory support. We used either Drager or Fisher-Paykel interfaces with appropriately sized masks to deliver non-invasive ventilation to the baby.

All preterm infants (gestational age at birth less than 37 completed weeks) who were already on NIPPV or CPAP and required elective intubation during their NICU stay were eligible for enrollment. All the elective intubation procedures were performed orally by neonatology residents. Infants who underwent emergency intubation or intubated directly from room air or HHHFNC were excluded from the study. Intubation was considered an emergency if it was due to sudden unplanned clinical instability or resuscitation [10]. Additionally, infants who required intubation at the delivery point, those with major congenital anomalies, or those with a heart rate below 100/min before the intubation attempt were also excluded. The criteria for intubation were objectively defined and required the presence of one or more of the following: hypoxemia (SpO2/FiO2 < 1.5), hypercarbia (venous PaCO2 > 60 mmHg with pH < 7.2), recurrent or prolonged apnea, and shock despite being at maximum non-invasive ventilation setting [11]. The maximum CPAP setting allowed was 60% fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) and positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP)of 7 cm water, and the maximum NIPPV settings were FiO2 60%, PEEP 6 cm water, peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) 25 cm water, and respiratory rate (RR) of 50/min [12].

We approached parents of any preterm infant on NIPPV or CPAP for consent to be considered a prospective candidate for enrollment, regardless of whether they required intubation. If intubation was needed at any point during the NICU stay, parents were informed either in person or over telephone. Verbal consent over telephone was documented in the case record form. If they withdrew consent, they were excluded. Infants whose parents could not be contacted before the procedure were also excluded.

Block randomization was conducted using variable block sizes of 4, 6, and 8 (www.sealedenvelop.com) by a separate individual who was not involved in the study [13]. Randomization was stratified based on the gestational age at the time of intubation (≤ 30 and > 30 weeks). Infants from multiple pregnancies were individually randomized. Repeat intubation of the same infant was considered a separate episode. Allocation concealment was achieved using serially numbered opaque sealed envelopes (SNOSE) kept at the nursing station and only opened after randomization by the treating physician. All necessary equipment was assembled and checked prior to intubation. At least two individuals were required for the procedure. The use of preoxygenation (30% increase in FiO2 for 15 s) and premedication (fentanyl at 2 µg/kg, atropine at 20 µg/kg, and succinylcholine at 2 mg/kg) were left to the discretion of the treating physician [14, 15]. All infants were connected to a multipara monitor throughout the procedure (Philips IntelliVue MP30). The sensitivity and averaging time of the monitor were set to the maximum and 2 s, respectively.

If an infant was assigned to the intervention arm, the appropriately sized Fisher & Paykel Opti Flow (OPT) junior nasal cannula (see Fig. 1) was connected to the ventilator circuit by a Y-piece and adaptor after removing the nasal mask and interface immediately before the procedure [16]. The suggested sizes for Opti Flow cannulas were premature OPT for infants up to 32 weeks, OPT Neonatal for 32–36 weeks, and OPT infants for gestational age more than 36 weeks at the time of intubation. An Opti Flow cannula was then secured around the head, allowing the baby to continue receiving the existing respiratory support while intubation was attempted. The ventilator settings remained unchanged, except for FiO2, which could be increased from the pre-intubation level to maintain SpO2. The Opti Flow cannula was removed after successful completion of the intubation procedure. If any attempt failed or the infant was destabilized, the infant continued to receive ventilatory support through the Opti Flow cannula until the next attempt. Intubation was attempted without respiratory support in the control arm. In the event of infant destabilization, the original interface and nasal mask were reapplied until the infant’s condition improved. Prior to subsequent attempts in the control group, respiratory support was withdrawn during the procedure. The clinicians could not be blinded to the group allocation due to nature of the intervention. The entire session was video-recorded by a separate person not involved in the procedure for future reference.

The primary outcome of the study was successful intubation on the first attempt without any physiological instability. We chose to follow the same primary outcome as that of the SHINE trial, as it combines both procedural success and infant stability [17]. Physiological instability was defined as a decrease in peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) of more than 20% from the pre-intubation level (desaturation)and/or a heart rate less than 100/min (bradycardia), as described by Hodgson et al. [17].Secondary outcomes included median and lowest SpO2 levels, median and lowest heart rate, number and duration of desaturation episodes, and bradycardia during the procedure.Other outcomes included the number of intubation attempts required, successful first attempt irrespective of physiological stability, causes of failure of attempts, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) within 1 h of the procedure, death within 72 h and death before discharge. Time to desaturation, time to bradycardia and duration of total attempts required were also analyzed. We followed the same criteria as provided by Hodgson et al. for defining an intubation attempt, successful attempt and duration of an attempt [17]. Insertion of the laryngoscope blade beyond the infant’s lip, with the intent of placing an endotracheal tube in the trachea, was considered an intubation attempt. An attempt was considered successful if an endotracheal tube was correctly placed in the trachea, as confirmed by bilateral equal air entry and chest rise. Capnometry or a colorimetric device was not used to confirm the correct position of the ET tube as it was not available in our unit. The time in seconds between insertion and removal of the laryngoscope blade inside the infant’s mouth was considered the duration of that attempt, regardless of the success of the procedure. The total duration was calculated by summing the times required for all attempts for a particular infant.

We conducted a pilot study at the same unit with 12 infants from each group to determine the sample size required for this study. The success rate of the first intubation attempts without any physiological instability improved from 53 to 78% in the intervention group. Therefore, with a 5% type I error and 80% power, we would require 112 infants (56 in each group) for this trial.

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages), and continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (inter-quartile range [IQR]) depending on the normality of the data. Categorical variables were compared between the two groups using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the expected cell count. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test for normal and skewed distributions, respectively. Risk differences with a 95% confidence interval were calculated. Subgroup analysis was conducted according to gestational age, weight, SpO2/FiO2 ratio, preoxygenation, and premedication. The number needed to treat was calculated by taking the reciprocal of the risk difference between the two groups. An interim analysis was performed at 50% of the planned sample recruitment. Independent Data Safety and Monitoring Board reviewed the results of the interim analysis. Predefined trial stoppage criteria at the interim analysis were either evidence of efficacy of the intervention with a p-value of less than 0.025 or a highly significant difference between groups (p < 0.01) with respect to adverse events. All analyses were performed on an intention-to-treat basis. R software (version 4.3.3) was used for analysis, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

During the study period, 224 intubations were assessed for eligibility, of which 147 were included. Before the actual procedure, 20 parents could not be contacted, and 15 withdrew consent, leaving 112 available for randomization. 56 intubations were assigned to each arm. All randomized infants received the intended treatment and were included in the analysis (see Fig. 2).

Baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. The median gestational age and birth weight of the enrolled infants were 30 weeks (28-33weeks) and 1004 g (857–1264 g), respectively. The most common indication for intubation was hypoxemia (75.89%), followed by shock (40.17%), and apnea (33.93%). The median age at intubation was 2.5 days (inter-quartile range, 1–4 days). Nearly all infants (97.3%) were on NIPPV before intubation. Of the 112 intubations, 33 were repeat intubations (29.46%), and 64 of the intubation attempts were preceded by premedication (57.14%). The median FiO2 requirement in the intervention arm during intubation was 100% (66%-100%). The distribution of baseline clinical and respiratory parameters by group is shown in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively. The time to apply the nasal cannula in the intervention group ranged from 6 s to 11 s (median, 8 s). Successful intubation on the first attempt without SpO2 drop > 20% from baseline and/or Bradycardia was significantly lower in the control arm (16/56 (28.57%) vs. 29/56 (51.79%) in the control and intervention arms, respectively; p 0.021). The number needed to treat for one additional benefit was 5 [3–18]. Subgroup analysis based on gestational age at intubation, sex, antenatal steroid use, repeat intubation, preoxygenation, and premedication did not show any differences in the primary outcome. However, the intervention appeared to be more effective in infants weighing ≤ 1000 g and with an SpO2/FiO2 ratio of < 1.5 at the time of intubation (see Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of using non-invasive ventilation through a soft nasal cannula during intubation in preterm infants. This method resulted in a higher success rate on the first attempt, without causing desaturation and/or bradycardia. Additionally, it led to a lower rate of desaturation, longer time to desaturation, shorter duration of desaturation, and bradycardia. Ultimately, it improved survival until discharge.

The concept of oxygenation during intubation is not new. It is routinely used in adult anesthesia and the critical care unit [18, 19]. When muscle relaxants are administered as part of premedication, patients become apneic, during which a high fraction of oxygen is delivered to the pharynx. This oxygen then diffuses through its concentration gradient towards the alveoli [20]. The high concentration of oxygen in the alveoli facilitates diffusion across the blood-capillary barrier in the lungs [21]. Oxygen can be delivered through a variety of interfaces such as nasal cannulas, face masks, or high-flow nasal cannulas in adults [22]. This concept is popularly known as trans nasal humidified rapid-insufflation ventilatory exchange (THRIVE) [23]. It has gradually gained acceptance in pediatric intensive care [24]. However, this remains relatively unexplored in the neonatal practice. In 1966, Reid et al. reported successful use of diffusion respiration in a severely malformed baby with a difficult airway [25].Since then, its use in the neonatal population has remained sporadic. Recently, in a multicenter trial from Australia (SHINE trial), Hodgson et al. investigated the use of nasal high flow therapy during elective neonatal intubation. The study showed better chances of successful intubation on the first attempt without desaturation and/or bradycardia [17]. This practice also leads to stabilized cerebral oxygen saturation during intubation in preterm infants [26]. Other researchers are currently investigating the effect of high flow therapy (6 L/min) in preterm infants with 100% oxygen via nasal cannula during intubation (POINT trial) [27].

However, several aspects of apneic oxygenation during neonatal intubation remain unclear. Not all preterm infants are rendered apneic before intubation, particularly in the absence of experienced personnel. Thus, the intervention becomes “oxygenation” rather than “apneic oxygenation”. The choice of the interface delivering oxygen, flow rate, or FiO2 is also not agreed upon. Moreover, the application of this method in low-and middle-income countries (LMIC) has not been explored. The efficacy of apnoeic oxygenation can be enhanced by facilitating CO2 elimination from the alveoli, thereby establishing an increased pressure gradient between the proximal and distal airways for oxygen transfer [28]. In animal models, this can be achieved by increasing the flow rate or by delivering oxygen endobronchially [29]. However, in newborns, continuing positive pressure ventilation with oxygen during intubation may lead to the same effect. This is evidenced by a decreased PaCO2 in the intervention group (50.24 ± 10.56 mm Hg vs. 54.40 ± 9.78 mm Hg in the intervention and control arms respectively, p 0.016). Thus, continuing ventilation non-invasively compared to no support during intubation leads to a decreased incidence of physiological instability in the most fragile infants. Post hoc subgroup analysis revealed two important interactions between the intervention and infant characteristics. The tiniest (weight ≤ 1000 g) and sickest (SpO2/FiO2 < 1.5) infants tended to respond better to this strategy because they had limited respiratory reserve to tolerate any respiratory procedure. In accordance with the same principle, the continuation of NIPPV during intubation in preterm infants may prove more effective than high-flow oxygen, as the former may better sustain alveolar recruitment and reduce atelectasis during the procedure.

The strengths of this study lie in its robust methodology, timely participant recruitment, comprehensive analysis, and inclusion of a population representative of low-and middle-income countries (LMIC). The circumstances in LMICs can significantly differ from those in developed nations, characterized by a scarcity of experienced operators and limited availability of equipment. A structured protocol for intubation may be lacking in several units, leading to variations in practice. Many practitioners are reluctant to administer muscle relaxants as a premedication because of the fear of increased risk of complications. However, even in such situations, interventions appear to be beneficial.

This study had several limitations. Clinicians performing intubation and those analyzing the outcomes from video recordings were not blinded to the group allocation. Opti-flow junior cannulas were not originally designed to deliver non-invasive ventilation. The recommended maximum flow rate is 8 L/min. However, high-flow nasal cannulas can be effectively used with non-invasive ventilation to deliver inhaled Nitric-Oxide (NO) in infant models [30]. Almost half of the infants in this trial were not apneic. The exact positive pressure delivered to the respiratory system was not measured. We could not assess the effect of operator experience on the outcome, as all intubations were performed by neonatology fellows with adequate intubation experience. The effect of increased oxygen supplementation in the intervention arm was not assessed by measuring oxygen free radicals in blood or urine. In addition to mortality, other clinically important outcomes, such as retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) were not assessed.

Future studies should explore the strategy of continued non-invasive ventilation during intubation in adequately powered randomized controlled trials to identify potential differences in clinically significant and long-term outcomes, such as BPD or ROP.

Conclusion

Maintaining non-invasive ventilation during the intubation procedure for preterm infants is feasible in low- and middle-income country (LMIC) settings. This approach improved the success rate of intubation without complications in preterm infants, particularly in LMICs.
