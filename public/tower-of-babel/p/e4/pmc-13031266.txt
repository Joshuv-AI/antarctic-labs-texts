A novel lightweight hybrid CNN–ViT for maize leaf disease classification
Authors: Saber Mehdipour, Seyed Abolghasem Mirroshandel, Seyed Amirhossein Tabatabaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maize is a vital global crop, but its productivity is often threatened by plant diseases, highlighting the need for precise and timely diagnostic methods. Traditional manual inspection is inefficient and prone to errors, motivating the development of automated solutions. Recent advances in computer vision and deep learning have enabled effective automated plant disease diagnosis. While Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) have shown promise in plant disease classification, CNNs struggle to capture global contextual information, and ViTs require large datasets and high computational resources. Inspired by mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures, we propose a lightweight hybrid model that integrates CNN and ViT components, adaptively emphasizing local or global features based on input characteristics. Evaluated on a novel, real-world dataset of full maize plant images, our approach achieves 99.90% classification accuracy, significantly outperforming state-of-the-art baselines such as MobileViT, PiT, EdgeNeXt, and DeiT. These results demonstrate that lightweight hybrid architectures can deliver high-performance disease diagnosis suitable for practical agricultural deployment. The code is available at: https://www.github.com/sabermehdipour/MXiT.

Introduction

Agriculture plays a vital role in global food security and economic stability, and maize is among the most important staple crops due to its extensive cultivation and wide-ranging applications in food production, livestock feed, and biofuel industries1,2. However, maize production is highly vulnerable to various plant diseases that can significantly reduce yield and quality. Early and accurate disease detection is therefore essential for limiting disease spread, reducing unnecessary chemical treatments, and supporting sustainable crop management. Although expert inspection remains valuable, manual diagnosis is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to human error, which creates a strong need for automated, intelligent disease recognition systems3.

Recent advances in deep learning have significantly improved computer vision and enabled highly accurate plant disease classification4,5. (CNNs) have been widely adopted for image-based diagnosis due to their strong ability to extract local spatial features. However, despite their effectiveness, CNNs inherently struggle to capture long-range dependencies and broader spatial relationships within an image, which are often essential for distinguishing visually similar disease patterns. These limitations have motivated research into architectures capable of modeling more holistic representations6.

(ViTs)7 have recently emerged as a powerful alternative, offering improved global context modeling through self-attention mechanisms. While ViTs effectively capture long-range feature interactions, their practical deployment in agricultural settings remains challenging. ViT-based models typically require large-scale training datasets and substantial computational resources, limiting their suitability for real-world, resource-constrained environments.

Despite the complementary strengths of CNNs and ViT, achieving an effective balance between local feature extraction, global context modeling, and computational efficiency remains a significant challenge. Existing hybrid approaches often introduce substantial complexity or require large computational costs, making them difficult to deploy in practical agricultural environments8. Furthermore, most plant disease recognition models are trained on datasets that include single-leaf images collected under controlled conditions, leaving a notable gap in methods designed for full-plant images captured in diverse and unpredictable real-world field settings9,10.

These limitations underscore the need for architectures that can combine local and global reasoning while remaining lightweight enough for deployment on resource-constrained devices11. Hybrid CNN–ViT models provide a promising direction by leveraging CNNs for fine-grained local features and Transformer modules for high-level spatial interactions across the entire image. A key challenge, however, is enabling such models to adaptively manage computational capacity based on the structure of each input, avoiding unnecessary overhead while retaining strong representational flexibility.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures offer an appealing solution by activating only a subset of specialized components for each sample, thereby improving efficiency without sacrificing performance. Sparse MoE variants, such as V-MoE, have demonstrated that selectively routing inputs through expert layers can enhance scalability in vision models while reducing inference cost12. More recent studies show that even lightweight MoE configurations with only a few experts can provide meaningful gains when equipped with effective routing strategies13. These findings motivate a minimal, adaptive hybrid design in which a CNN-based expert and a ViT-based expert are combined via a gating mechanism to efficiently capture both local and global dependencies.

Building on these insights, we propose a lightweight, heterogeneous two-expert architecture that integrates a CNN-based expert and a ViT-based expert through a computationally efficient gating mechanism. This adaptive design allows the model to selectively leverage local and global feature extraction on a per-sample basis, providing both interpretability and efficiency. Despite its minimal configuration, the model achieves 99.90% accuracy on full-plant, real-world maize disease images and outperforms competitive baselines–including MobileViT, PiT, EdgeNeXt, and DeiT–while using fewer parameters.

Related works

Deep learning algorithms have demonstrated remarkable success across various domains and have achieved significant advancements in the accurate diagnosis of plant diseases. These methods constitute a valuable tool, particularly for the timely identification of critical diseases that threaten major crops such as maize. In recent years, there has been a notable increase in studies focusing on plant leaf classification and disease detection using CNN and ViT architectures. These approaches have proven especially effective, driving the development of more robust diagnostic methods. ViTs, as a more recent advancement, have shown promising results in image classification tasks, particularly when applied to large datasets. Both CNNs and ViTs can be leveraged to analyze agricultural images and accurately classify disease types, thereby facilitating early detection and reducing the risk of crop losses. Numerous studies have explored the application of these two architectures for plant image classification. This section provides a summary of some of these studies.

DFCANet15, a novel lightweight t CNN, was proposed for identifying maize leaf diseases in real-world environments with complex backgrounds and varied lesion areas. The authors noted that traditional CNN models often suffer from low accuracy and a large number of parameters, making them unsuitable for deployment on mobile or edge devices. DFCANet addresses these issues through two main components: a Double Fusion block with Coordinate Attention (DFCA) and a Down-Sampling (DS) module. The DFCA block employs a double feature fusion approach to effectively combine shallow and deep features, while the Coordinate Attention module focuses on diseased areas and suppresses background noise. Experimental results demonstrate that DFCANet achieves an average recognition accuracy of 98.47%, outperforming several classic CNN models, including VGG1616, ResNet5017, and MobileNet-V218.

An improved Swin Transformer (SwinT)19 was proposed for cucumber leaf disease recognition. The enhancements to the SwinT backbone include step-wise small patch embedding for more effective feature extraction and a Coordinate Attention block to enhance features without increasing the number of parameters. The authors also developed a SwinT-based, attention-guided GAN (STA-GAN) to generate high-quality, realistic diseased images by applying disease spots only to the leaf region, thereby preserving complex backgrounds. This approach advances prior methods, which either required larger training datasets or failed to isolate the leaf region effectively. The effectiveness of the method was demonstrated on a practical cucumber leaf disease dataset. When trained with the STA-GAN-augmented dataset, the improved SwinT model achieved a recognition accuracy of 98.97%, outperforming the original SwinT20, EfficientNet-B521, and ResNet-10117. Moreover, the improved SwinT required only a minimal increase in parameters compared to the original model. These results indicate that the proposed methods are robust and capable of addressing common challenges, such as limited data and complex backgrounds, in practical agricultural applications.

A recent study22 introduced an advanced ViT model for the timely and accurate identification of diseases in maize leaves. The proposed method adapts the Multi-axis ViT (MaxViT) model23 to create a lightweight architecture. To further enhance performance, the authors replaced the conventional convolutional structure in the MaxViT architecture’s stem with a Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) block and employed a Global Response Normalization (GRN)-based MLP from the ConvNeXtV2 architecture. The method achieved a remarkable accuracy of 99.24%.

In a separate study24, the authors proposed a deep learning framework, MaizeNet, to address the challenges of maize leaf disease detection in real-world field images, which often contain complex backgrounds, varying lighting, and distortions. The paper highlighted limitations in existing methods, noting that many models were trained on datasets with clear, static backgrounds and struggled with images captured in natural, uncontrolled settings. To overcome these issues, the MaizeNet model was built on an improved Faster R-CNN25 network using ResNet-5017 as its backbone. The authors also incorporated spatial and channel attention mechanisms into the network to extract more robust, fine-grained features from noisy backgrounds. The results showed that the model achieved an average accuracy of 97.89% and a mean average precision (mAP) of 0.94.

In another study26, researchers proposed a novel approach for identifying maize leaf diseases in complex, noisy field environments. Unlike many existing studies that rely on images with simple backgrounds, this work addressed the challenges of real-world settings containing visual disturbances. The authors introduced a model based on the Transformer architecture and a self-attention mechanism, differing from traditional CNNs. This model represented local image regions and used the attention mechanism to calculate correlations between these regions, effectively emphasizing disease lesions while suppressing background noise. The results demonstrated that the model outperformed five mainstream CNNs while maintaining a significantly lower computational cost.

An improved MobileNetV3 deep learning model, called CD-MobileNetV327, was proposed for the accurate identification of maize leaf diseases. The authors aimed to address the limitations of existing deep learning models, such as large parameter sizes and long running times, which make them unsuitable for deployment on mobile or edge devices. The CD-MobileNetV3 model introduces several key innovations: it replaces the cross-entropy loss function with a bias loss function to focus learning on unique features; it uses an efficient channel attention (ECA) module instead of SE module to reduce parameters; and it incorporates dilated convolutions to expand the receptive field. Experimental results demonstrated that the model achieved an accuracy of 98.23%, a precision of 0.9826, and an F1 score of 0.9826, outperforming classic deep learning models.

Another work proposed ConvViT28, a hybrid model combining CNN and ViT to address the challenge of kiwifruit disease identification in complex natural environments, where background noise often reduces model robustness. The model leverages CNN to capture global image features, while ViT focuses on disease-relevant local features, enhanced by an overlapping patch embedding strategy to preserve local continuity. Three scalable ConvViT variants (Tiny, Small, Base) were designed to balance accuracy and efficiency across devices with different computational resources. Experiments demonstrated superior accuracy (up to 98.78%) and generalization compared to traditional models, while reducing parameters and FLOPs by more than 10%. These results suggest that ConvViT is a robust and efficient backbone for plant disease recognition tasks in real-world scenarios.

An independent work29 proposed a hybrid model called TLMViT to address the limitations of traditional CNNs, which can struggle with extracting global features, and ViTs, which require large datasets and are computationally expensive. The TLMViT model consists of four stages: data acquisition, image augmentation, two-phase feature extraction, and classification. The feature extraction process is a key contribution: a pre-trained model first extracts initial features and reduces image dimensionality, followed by a ViT that extracts deep features from the reduced dimensions. The results show that the model achieved high validation accuracies of 98.81% on the PlantVillage dataset and 99.86% on the Wheat dataset.

A related study30 proposed an improved Transformer-based method for the precise and fast identification of multiple classes of strawberry diseases, specifically addressing the challenge of complex backgrounds. To overcome the limitations of existing datasets, the authors constructed a new multi-class strawberry disease dataset containing 5,369 images across 12 common disease types. The method employs a multi-head self-attention mechanism to capture long-range feature dependencies and introduces a Spatial Convolutional Self-attention-based Transformer (SCSA-Transformer) to reduce network parameters and improve efficiency. Various data augmentation strategies, including image fusion, were applied to increase sample diversity and address data imbalance. The method was validated on the constructed strawberry disease dataset, achieving a recognition accuracy of 99.10%, which the authors reported as outperforming other methods. Experimental results further demonstrated that the model improves recognition efficiency due to its reduced parameter count.

Other researchers31 introduced a new deep learning model called MDSCIRNet for classifying potato leaf diseases. The model is built on two core components: depthwise separable convolution (DSC) and a multi-head attention mechanism. The use of DSC allows the model to perform classification with fewer parameters and lower computational cost compared to traditional CNNs, addressing the issue of high processing power requirements. By combining the strengths of Transformer and CNN architectures, MDSCIRNet aims to increase the model’s learning capacity while minimizing information loss. The authors also applied image enhancement techniques, such as Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), to improve dataset quality. The model achieved a high accuracy of 99.24%. The study further proposed and tested hybrid methods that combine MDSCIRNet with classic machine learning algorithms, such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest (RF), with the highest accuracy, 99.33%, achieved by the hybrid method combining MDSCIRNet and SVM.

Another related study32 addressed the challenges of limited labeled datasets and the need for computationally efficient models in plant disease diagnosis. To tackle this, the authors proposed a novel data augmentation model called LeafyGAN. This model comprises two main components: a pix2pix GAN for accurate leaf segmentation from the background and a CycleGAN to apply synthetic disease patterns to the segmented leaf regions. This approach improves upon prior methods, which struggled with precise boundary detection and sometimes applied disease patterns to non-leaf areas. LeafyGAN is designed to produce high-quality, realistic images to enhance the training data for minority disease classes. To validate the effectiveness of their data augmentation method, the authors trained a lightweight MobileViT model, chosen for its optimal balance of high performance and low computational requirements. The study’s findings demonstrate that this approach effectively identifies plant diseases, making it a viable and practical solution for deployment on low-resource devices in real-world agricultural settings.

This literature review highlights the substantial impact of deep learning approaches, particularly ViTs, on plant leaf disease detection. These methods have consistently outperformed traditional CNNs while demonstrating notable flexibility and applicability in agricultural contexts. Although numerous studies have proposed innovative architectures and techniques to achieve high accuracy in disease identification, several challenges remain, including the effective utilization of real-world datasets and the development of lightweight models suitable for deployment on devices with limited computational resources.

Datasets description

This section outlines the datasets used in this study, emphasizing their characteristics and relevance. We first describe a publicly available, real-world maize dataset (available upon request), followed by the PlantVillage dataset, an open-source resource widely used in related research.

PlantScanner dataset

In this study, we utilized a novel dataset, which is part of the PlantScanner14 dataset (publicly available upon request), consisting of top-down images of maize plants that resemble drone-captured views commonly used in precision agriculture. Each image contains multiple maize leaves and is annotated as either infected or healthy based on the visible presence of disease symptoms. The infections represented in this dataset are caused by the biotrophic fungus Ustilago maydis in maize leaves. While prior studies have often relied on publicly available datasets such as PlantVillage33, these resources consist of single-leaf images captured under controlled conditions with plain backgrounds and uniform lighting. Although such datasets are effective for initial training, they do not adequately capture the complexity of real-world field conditions. In contrast, the PlantScanner dataset provides a more realistic setting by incorporating entire plants with overlapping leaves and natural variability in plant morphology, presenting a more practical challenge for developing robust disease detection models.

The original dataset used in this study consists of 80 videos captured from a top-down perspective, showing pots containing maize plants. Of these, 40 videos depict healthy plants and 40 depict infected plants. Each video is approximately 20 seconds long, covering a complete imaging cycle of the plant. To generate a high-quality image dataset, a custom Python script utilizing OpenCV34 was employed to extract frames at regular intervals. Frames were sampled every 0.25 seconds and evaluated for sharpness using the variance of the Laplacian method, a standard approach for blur detection. Only frames with a Laplacian variance above a threshold of 10 were retained, ensuring clarity and consistency. The selected frames were saved in high-quality JPEG format. A manual review was then conducted to remove any remaining low-quality frames, resulting in a clean and reliable dataset. Ultimately, the dataset contains 2,544 images of healthy maize plants and 2,351 images of infected maize plants. Table 1 provides the details of the dataset.

Unlike existing public datasets, in which each image typically depicts a single, isolated leaf and thus fails to capture the unique imaging conditions (e.g., drone altitude, top-down angle, leaf overlap), this dataset reflects real-world conditions by capturing the entire plant canopy within each frame. Consequently, each image provides a holistic representation of the plant rather than focusing on individual leaves. A notable challenge associated with this dataset arises from the subtle visual distinctions between healthy and infected samples. Specifically, if any leaf within a plant exhibits signs of infection, the entire image is labeled as infected; conversely, if no infection is observed in any leaf, the image is labeled as healthy. Methodology

This section describes the methodology used in our work. It covers the overall model structure, provides formal definitions, and details the Single-Head Self-Attention module along with the modifications applied to it. Together, these components form the foundation of our proposed model.

Model architecture

The proposed model, MXiT, is a hybrid CNN–ViT architecture that integrates lightweight Single-Head Self-Attention, depthwise convolutions, and a dynamic expert-selection mechanism within a multi-stage framework. Previous studies, such as LeViT35 and EfficientViT36, organize their architectures into sequential stages. In contrast, MXiT replaces the notion of stages with levels, providing a more flexible structural organization. The architecture comprises three levels: the first two levels each contain a single stage, whereas the third level consists of two parallel stages.

This four-stage model is designed to progressively refine and capture image representations by leveraging the complementary strengths of CNN and Transformer architectures. The input image is first processed through a patch embedding module, which divides it into smaller patches. These patches are then fed into the CNN module to extract local spatial features. In the second stage, the CNN and Transformer modules are combined to capture both local and global dependencies. At the third level, the architecture branches into two parallel processing paths: one CNN-based and the other Transformer-based. Finally, the outputs of these two branches are fused using a lightweight gating mechanism, which dynamically assigns importance weights to the CNN and Transformer outputs based on the input. The main motivation behind this design is the lack of inductive biases in ViTs. While ViTs have demonstrated strong performance in capturing long-range dependencies and global contextual information through self-attention7, they inherently lack certain priors that are naturally embedded in CNNs. This absence of built-in structure can result in suboptimal performance, particularly in domains such as agriculture, where datasets are relatively small and visual patterns can vary significantly across scales9. CNNs are designed with inductive biases such as locality, translation equivariance, and hierarchical feature extraction, which make them highly effective for learning low-level and mid-level visual features. These properties allow CNNs to efficiently model spatially local patterns, including edges, textures, and simple shapes, which serve as fundamental building blocks for visual understanding9. In contrast, ViTs, owing to their patch-based representation and reliance on the self-attention mechanism, excel at modeling long-range interactions and capturing complex global dependencies across the entire image. However, the absence of built-in inductive biases makes them data-hungry and less efficient when trained from scratch on limited datasets. By combining CNNs with ViTs in a hybrid model, it becomes possible to leverage the strengths of both approaches. CNNs contribute robust local feature extraction and structural priors, whereas ViTs enhance the model’s capacity to integrate global context and capture high-level semantic relationships. This complementary nature enables the hybrid architecture to achieve improved generalization, efficiency, and representational power compared with using either CNNs or ViTs in isolation.

The model begins by applying a series of convolutional layers in the patch embedding module to downsample the input image and extract low-level features. Unlike the standard ViT model7, we employ an overlapping patch embedding process, in which the input image is passed through a sequence of three convolutional blocks. Each block consists of a 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3 convolution with stride 2 and padding 1 (Fig. 4), followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation, except for the final layer. This design ensures that patches are extracted with overlap, which better preserves local texture and edge information compared with non-overlapping methods. The spatial resolution gradually decreases while the channel depth increases. These feature maps are subsequently passed through a sequence of hierarchical stages (Stage1 to Stage4), each of which is designed for a specific type of processing and has its own depth.

Stage1 is CNN-based and operates on patch embeddings to capture local spatial features, such as edges, textures, and fine details. It includes a Depthwise Convolution (DWConv) layer (Fig. 5) to aggregate local features or apply conditional positional embeddings, as well as a Feed-Forward Network (FFN) to facilitate channel-wise interactions. This design is effective because CNNs excel at learning localized patterns with translation invariance, making them well-suited for early-stage feature extraction, where fine-grained details are most critical. Subsequently, a downsampling operation reduces the spatial dimensions, allowing the network to focus on higher-level abstractions while keeping computational costs manageable.

Stage2 combines CNN and Transformer modules to capture both local and global dependencies. The CNN components continue refining localized feature maps, ensuring that important spatial hierarchies are preserved, while the Transformer components model long-range relationships across the image. The addition of a Single-Head Self-Attention (SHSA) layer enables the model to capture global context. This hybrid design is effective because CNNs alone may overlook global information, whereas Transformer alone may lack the inductive biases needed to capture local structures. By integrating both approaches, the model efficiently leverages CNN for robust low- and mid-level feature extraction and Transformer for capturing holistic image semantics. As the stages progress, the representations become increasingly abstract, allowing the network to handle complex tasks, such as classification, detection, and segmentation, with greater accuracy and robustness.

In the third level, the architecture splits into two parallel processing stages. Stage3 is CNN-based and identical to Stage1, whereas Stage4 is Transformer-based (containing only SHSA + FFN modules without convolutional components). By processing the same intermediate representation in parallel, the model ensures that both local and global perspectives are learned simultaneously, avoiding a bias toward either fine details or global semantics alone. To incorporate the inductive biases that ViTs inherently lack, we employ this hybrid architecture to combine CNN-based and Transformer-based components. Although both stages receive the same input feature maps, they process the information through fundamentally different mechanisms. By integrating these two paradigms within a unified framework, the model benefits from the complementary strengths of both architectures, preserving the locality-aware inductive biases of CNNs while harnessing the global reasoning capabilities of Transformers. This synergy allows the system to adaptively focus on the most informative features for each input, resulting in more accurate and robust disease classification.

Each stage operates with residual connections and integrates FFN layers to enhance representational capacity. Spatial downsampling between stages is performed using a combination of 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}1 convolutions, depthwise separable convolutions, and SE blocks37 for efficient spatial compression and channel-wise recalibration. As the network progresses deeper, it transitions from fine-grained spatial processing to more abstract, semantically rich feature representations. Crucially, two parallel expert pathways (Stage3 and Stage4) are deployed toward the end of the network to specialize in different types of information processing. Stage3 focuses on spatially dense, texture-sensitive representations, whereas Stage4 emphasizes global dependencies and structural relationships across the image. Unlike standard ViTs, all stages operate directly on 2D feature maps rather than flattened tokens. This preserves spatial locality and simplifies integration with convolution-based components.

To dynamically fuse the outputs from these two expert branches, a Gating Network is introduced. This module acts as a lightweight decision-making system that predicts the relative importance of each expert pathway for a given input. The Gating Network receives the shared input to Stage3 and Stage4 (i.e., the output from Stage2) and performs Global Average Pooling (GAP) followed by a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP), which then produces a 2-dimensional softmax output representing normalized weights. These weights determine how much each expert’s output contributes to the final representation before classification. Following the gating decision, the outputs of Stage3 and Stage4 are pooled globally and converted into feature vectors. The Gating Network’s softmax weights are then applied to these vectors, producing a weighted sum that constitutes the final representation. This fusion strategy enables the network to adapt its behavior on a per-sample basis, emphasizing either local convolutional features or globally aware attention features depending on the input’s characteristics. The fused representation is subsequently passed through a linear classifier for final prediction. The final classification head is implemented as a BatchNorm-Linear (BNLinear) layer, which normalizes the input features before applying the linear classifier. We use SHSA in the stages that contain the Transformer module. Experiments

In this section, we present the results of our experiments. We begin by describing the implementation details and evaluation criteria. We then compare the performance of our model with that of the baselines to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach.

Training setup

All experiments were conducted on a machine running Ubuntu 24.04, equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6148 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 40 processor and an RTX 4090 GPU. Baselines

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed model, we compare its performance against four representative architectures:DeiT (Data-efficient Image Transformer)40PiT (Pooling-based Vision Transformer)41EdgeNeXt42MovileViT43These models serve as strong baselines due to their varying architectural strategies and demonstrated performance across standard vision benchmarks. DeiT40 is a version of the ViT that doesn’t require large-scale datasets or extensive computational resources. It offers a balance between accuracy and efficiency, making it a widely adopted baseline in Transformer-based image classification. PiT41 improves upon standard ViT by introducing pooling layers that progressively reduce spatial dimensions between Transformer stages. This hierarchical structure allows the model to learn multi-scale features efficiently, similar to CNNs. By combining self-attention with spatial downsampling, PiT achieves better accuracy and computational efficiency for image recognition tasks. EdgeNeXt42 is a lightweight vision model that combines convolutional and Transformer-based components to balance accuracy and efficiency for edge devices. It uses depthwise separable convolutions, efficient attention mechanisms, and residual connections to reduce computational cost while maintaining strong feature representation. MobileViT43 is a lightweight hybrid architecture that integrates convolutional layers with Transformers to balance local feature extraction and global context modeling. Unlike conventional lightweight CNNs, which are limited to local receptive fields, MobileViT enables each pixel to interact with all others through Transformer-based global processing. This design achieves state-of-the-art accuracy for mobile vision tasks with low latency and a small parameter budget.

In addition to the models included in our experiments (DeiT, EdgeNeXt, MobileViT, and PiT), several alternative classification architectures could also be considered. Traditional convolutional networks such as ResNet17, EfficientNet21, and MobileNet44 remain strong baselines for image classification, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Likewise, Transformer-based models including ViT7, SwinT20, and ConvNeXt45 provide competitive performance on various vision benchmarks. While these architectures represent valid alternatives, we focused our comparison on models that are specifically lightweight, mobile-friendly, or hybrid, as they align more directly with the design goals of our proposed model. Future work could expand the benchmark to include a broader set of CNN and Transformer architectures.

Implementations of the baseline models were obtained from the timm library46 and trained from scratch, without the use of pretrained weights. To enable a robust comparison, we examined two variants of each model that differ in their parameters and characteristics. Each of these models represents a different point in the trade-off between accuracy, model complexity, and computational cost. By including them in our evaluation, we aim to demonstrate the performance of our proposed model across diverse paradigms, ranging from resource-efficient designs to deep, high-performing architectures.

Experiments on PlantScanner dataset

Conclusion

This study introduced MXiT, a novel lightweight hybrid architecture that leverages the complementary strengths of CNNs and ViTs for accurate maize disease classification. The proposed model achieved 99.99% accuracy on a challenging dataset while requiring substantially fewer parameters and exhibiting lower computational complexity than state-of-the-art methods. Ablation studies demonstrated the substantial contributions of data augmentation and the modified Single-Head Self-Attention mechanism to overall performance. The model exhibits strong potential for scalable, cost-effective field deployment, enabling real-time decision support for farmers and other agricultural practitioners. Although the current study focuses on disease classification, future work will extend the model to assess disease severity, further enhancing its practical utility in precision agriculture.
