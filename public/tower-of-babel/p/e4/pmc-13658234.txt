Meta-awareness, mind-wandering, and the control of automatic external and internal orientations of attention
Authors: Isaac R. Christian, Samuel A. Nastase, Lauren K. Kim, Michael S. A. Graziano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ‘default’ mode of cognition involves simulating internal experiences. However, in some contexts, external focus can become captivating enough to act as the default mode. To examine how these orientations relate, we record fMRI activity in thirty-nine participants as they view naturalistic video clips, each one repeated four times in sequence. When instructed to direct attention externally on the video, most mind-wandering occurs on the last repetition and during less engaging clips. When instructed to direct attention internally on breathing, most mind-wandering occurs on the first repetition and for more engaging clips. Inter-subject correlation, within-subject correlation, and general linear models find similar fronto-parietal networks engaged during transitions from automatic to controlled attention across both conditions, indicating greater overlap in internal and external processing than previously assumed. We conclude that whether the automatic draw of attention is internal or external, and whether its disruptors are internal or external, is context-dependent.

Introduction

We are captured by the objects of our attention. Such objects can be external (e.g., a cute cat) or internal (e.g., a fond memory). Attention research has almost exclusively focused on characterizing the former, the dynamics of external attention. However, attention is often, if not more frequently, directed away from the external environment towards internal thoughts, memories, or plans1,2. This “internal bias” of attention is a result of the stable and predictable environment humans commonly find themselves in. Because we often engage in habitual routines (e.g., the same commute to work each day), it is more valuable to think about previous experiences and to plan for upcoming scenarios than it is to pay attention to predictable external events. As a result, the landscape of human thought is automatically tuned towards internal thoughts and periodically the strength of this internal pull is so strong that we may miss important cues in the environment, such as a sign on the highway. But is this internal orientation truly intrinsic, or does it depend on the richness of the external environment? In the present study, we ask whether the automatic orientation of attention is necessarily internal, or whether sufficiently captivating external stimuli can reverse this bias, and what the neural consequences of such a reversal look like.

Decades of neuroimaging have found one network to be associated with internal thought, named the default mode network (DMN)3,4. Called “default” because the mind tends to automatically drift inward—toward the simulation of past, future, and hypothetical experiences—in the absence of an explicit task goal. In contrast, this network classically deactivates, while others come online, when the focus of attention turns towards external events and stimuli5. The network most often associated with this kind of externally directed, sensory attention is the dorsal attention network (DAN). The DAN enables intentional deployment of attention to features in the visual field6, controls visual working memory7, and exhibits a trend opposite to that of the DMN, deactivating for tasks requiring internal attention8. Another network, the fronto-parietal control network (FPCN), dynamically calls upon networks specializing in internal or external processes depending on the task demands9–11. While it is now well known that these networks are not exclusively involved in internally or externally oriented cognition12,13, it is considered established in the literature that there is a distinction between external and internal attention14,15 and that there is a neural basis for an automatic, internally oriented mode of cognition16.

This view of an automatic internal orientation, however, hides some complexity. It could be argued that an automatic attentional orientation applies equally well to internal and to external modes, depending on circumstances. The brain naturally moves to one state or the other depending on which environment is more interesting. For example, when external stimuli have more attentional pull, then external attention becomes automatic, and it takes extra cognitive effort to pull attention internally. Contexts in which external content can be just as captivating as internal thought are increasingly prevalent in the modern world: recent evidence shows that 25% of adults and 45% of US teens engage with social media “almost constantly”17 and often face challenges disengaging from these sources18. Many have ventured that such uncontrolled engagement warrants classification as a mental health disorder, as uncontrolled use has been shown to produce detrimental effects on personal, social, and professional life19,20 as well as on cognition21. Thus, internal and external sources can provoke similar uncontrolled modes of attention.

Mind-wandering is usually studied in tasks where people try to attend externally but wander to internally oriented cognition (i.e., stimulus-independent thought)22. In contrast, the study of meditation measures the ability of individuals to maintain attention on a single internal task while resisting the attraction of free, unconstrained internal thought. In the meditation literature, therefore, mind-wandering is usually considered a transition from on-task internal content to off-task internal content23. Neither perspective typically considers the case when attention is supposed to be maintained on an internal task (e.g., thinking about a homework problem) but is distracted by external events (e.g., a siren) and control is required to resist the external distraction. Thus, “mind-wandering” as a term could just as fruitfully be applied to internally or externally oriented cognition. In some cases, the mind involuntarily drifts from desired external to distracting internal cognition, as is traditionally studied in mind-wandering. But in other cases, people may try to attend internally and become distracted by external events; a process that may be adaptive insofar as it prevents personally relevant external information from being missed entirely16. The mind-wandering processes may be quite similar in both cases, or involve the evolution of attention states according to similar principles.

Controlling one’s own attention to prevent mind-wandering involves at least two cognitive functions: first, becoming aware of the current state of attention, and second, directing attention based on that assessment24. The first function is often referred to as meta-awareness, or the explicit knowledge of the contents of thought25. It has also sometimes been conceptualized as a model of one’s own attention26. Meta-awareness is believed to reduce the frequency of mind-wandering27. Meta-awareness naturally occurs intermittently in dynamic thought, but can be primed to occur more frequently if the task requires it. Sometimes people are asked to continuously monitor their attentional state for lapses, a method often denoted as “self caught.”28 Another method is to give people intermittent reminders to use meta-awareness to check their attentional state29.

The present study addressed three questions. First, can we find behavioral evidence for automatic, or prepotent, attentional states that shift depending on circumstance? Using behavioral data, we examined the frequency of mind-wandering when the task was to focus externally, and compared this to mind-wandering when the task was to focus internally. Second, can we corroborate these automatic states using neural measures? To address this, we examined whether the networks supporting the shift from automatic to controlled attention over time were shared across internal and external attention conditions or specific to each. Third, we examined the neural correlates of meta-awareness and attentional reorienting, the moments surrounding each button press when participants caught themselves mind-wandering and redirected attention back to the task.

We presented participants with 2-min video clips. In one condition, the external attention condition, we asked participants to attend to the video and to press the button when they noticed that their attention had wandered off the video (i.e., to self-report mind-wandering events; Fig. 1A). In a second condition, the internal attention condition, we asked them to ignore the video, attend to their own breathing, and press the button when they noticed their attention wandering off their breathing (Fig. 1B). In both attention conditions, subjects watched four repetitions of the video clip, back to back. When participants attended externally, mind-wandering increased as videos became more familiar across repetitions; when attending internally, mind-wandering was greatest on the first viewing, when the video was novel, and declined thereafter. Across conditions, more mind-wandering events were reported in the internal condition, pointing to an automatic orientation of external attention that can be evoked given the right context. This pattern was corroborated neurally: shared attention to the external stimulus, measured via inter-subject correlation, decreased across repetitions in both conditions and remained consistently higher in the external condition. Contrary to our expectations, networks canonically associated with external and internal attention each strengthened their functional connectivity in both conditions, indicating that the internal-external distinction in network organization is less exact than previously assumed. This overlap extended to moments of meta-awareness and reorienting surrounding each button press, where fronto-parietal networks were similarly engaged regardless of attentional direction. Automatic attentional states and the control mechanisms that regulate them may be more context-dependent and domain-general than canonical accounts propose.

Results

Behavioral results

Button presses indicated moments when participants believed their attention had wandered from the task at hand. More button presses suggested greater difficulty in attending to the target task; less button presses suggested less difficulty. Figure 2A shows the mean results averaged across all subjects, for all trials involving the high-salience videos (Sherlock, Shrek, and The Office). The graph shows the number of button presses across four repetitions of the video for the external attention task and the internal attention task. A 2 × 4 analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed a significant main effect of external versus internal attention and a significant interaction (for external versus internal attention, F = 83.1, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.69; for the interaction, F = 5.91, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.14). A main effect indicating an overall change in button presses across the four repetitions was absent, due to the opposing patterns in which button presses decreased across the four repetitions for the internal attention condition and increased across the four repetitions for the external condition (F = 1.07, p > 0.05, partial η² = 0.03). In Fig. 2A, in the external attention task (blue line), when subjects were asked to attend to the video for the first time, they performed with few mind-wandering events (mean of 1.44 button presses), suggesting that they had little trouble keeping their focus on the video. In each successive repeat of the video, more mind-wandering events occurred, until, on the final repetition, subjects pressed an average of 3.02 times. This increasing trend was statistically significant (regression analysis on external attention data, t = 3.46, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.84). As the video became more familiar, the subjects had more difficulty staying on-task and, as a result, engaged in more mind-wandering.

In contrast, the orange line shows the results for the internal attention task, when subjects were asked to attend to their own breath. On the first viewing of the video, subjects had difficulty keeping their attention away from the video and maintaining attention on their breath, registering an average of 7.26 mind-wandering events. By the fourth presentation of the video, subjects' minds wandered less, pressing the button an average of 4.98 times. However, the change in attention was not gradual over the four repetitions. Instead, the shape of the curve suggests that on the first viewing, there was an initial draw to attend to the engaging video, distracting subjects from their task and producing more mind-wandering events, whereas on subsequent repetitions of the same video, subjects were able to resist the distraction to a similar extent. Button presses during the first exposure to the video were significantly greater than presses during the second, third, and fourth repetitions, but button presses were not significantly different among the second, third, and fourth repetition (t-test between first and second repetition, t = 2.49, df = 112, p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.42; t-test between first and third repetition, t = 2.35, df = 112, p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.40; t-test between first and fourth repetition, t = 2.34, df = 112, p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.38).

The data in Fig. 2A also suggest that the internal attention task was overall more difficult to hold focus on compared to the external attention task. Across all four repetitions of the video, the number of button presses, and thus mind-wandering events, was higher in the internal task than in the external task. Even on the fourth presentation of the video, when one might think the video had become so boring that subjects would find it difficult to keep their attention on it, and easy to keep their attention on their own breath, the internal condition still had significantly more button presses than the external condition (t = 2.97, df = 112, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.72). This finding does not mean that internal attention is always harder to engage than external attention. Instead, it suggests that in the context of this task, when subjects watched high salience videos, external attention on the video was easier than internal attention on their own breath. In effect, in this context, external attention behaved as the automatic orientation of attention.

Figure 2B shows the mean results for the low salience video clips (instructional videos on origami, baking a cake, and brushing teeth). In some respects, the pattern is similar to that from the high salience videos, including a significant main effect in which button presses were overall greater for the internal versus the external attention condition (2 × 4 ANOVA, F = 17.53, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.107), suggesting that it was more challenging to remain on task in the internal condition; a significant interaction between repetition and attention condition (F = 2.64, p < 0.05, partial η² = 0.030); and no significant main effect of repetition (F = 0.59, p > 0.05, partial η² = 0.014). In the external attention condition (blue line), button presses were low on the first presentation of the video and increased with subsequent repetitions, with a significant increasing trend (regression analysis on external attention data, t = 3.04, df = 226, p < 0.01, R² = 0.04). In the internal condition (orange line), though button presses were higher on first presentation and lower on subsequent repetitions, the trend was not significant (t = − 0.87, df = 226, p > 0.05, R² = 0.003). Subjects seemed relatively uniformly able to resist the distraction of the video through all repetitions; we did not observe a difference in button presses between the first and subsequent repetitions (t- test between first and second repetition, t = 0.75, df = 114, p > 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.13; t-test between first and third repetition, t = 0.76, df = 114, p > 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.13; t-test between first and fourth repetition, t = 0.90, df = 114, p > 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.17).

In a direct comparison between the high salience videos (Fig. 2A) and the low salience videos (Fig. 2B), some differences were observed. High salience videos appeared to be overall easier for subjects to attend to than low salience videos. For example, in the external attention condition, when subjects were asked to attend to the video, they registered fewer mind-wandering events for the high salience videos than for the low salience videos (t-test between high and low salience, in the external attention condition, t = 3.96, df = 454, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.68). In the internal attention condition, the high salience videos were more distracting, making it harder for subjects to attend to their own breathing, as shown by the greater number of mind-wandering events for the high salience videos than for the low salience videos (t-test between high and low salience, in the internal attention condition, t = 2.06, df = 454, p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.55).

Following the experiment, we asked participants to rate their level of engagement with each video on a scale of 1–10. In total, we obtained 114 ratings for low salience videos (data available for 38 subjects × 3 videos) and 114 ratings for high salience videos. We found that self-reported levels of engagement were higher for the high salience videos (mean rating = 7.76) as compared to the low salience videos (mean rating = 4.05). The difference was significant (df = 226, t = 13.14, p < 0.001, η² = 0.43). This result validates our separation of video stimuli into the high salience and low salience categories.

Neural results: ISC

ISC was used to test whether shared neural responses to the video, a signature of externally directed attention, diminished across repetitions, and whether this diminution differed between conditions and salience levels. ISC is well-suited to this purpose because it measures the degree to which participants’ brain activity is temporally synchronized to a common external timecourse: when participants attend to the same stimulus events at the same moments, their neural responses correlate; when attention drifts idiosyncratically, that correlation decreases.

Three predictions guided the ISC analysis. First, ISC should be higher in the external than the internal attention condition, since participants in the external condition were explicitly tasked with attending to the video. Second, ISC should decrease across repetitions in both conditions, as the stimulus becomes more familiar and external attention wanes. Third, high salience videos should produce higher ISC than low salience videos, consistent with the hypothesis that high salience videos draw more external attention.

Figure 3A, B display unthresholded ISC values, that is, the average ISC across all voxels, all subjects, and all videos within each salience category, for each repetition and condition. The first two predictions were clearly confirmed. ISC was consistently higher in the external than the internal condition across all repetitions and both salience categories, suggesting that participants successfully reduced their processing of the video when tasked with attending to their own breath. ISC also decreased with each repetition in both conditions, reflecting diminishing shared neural responses as the stimulus became more familiar. A mixed linear effects model confirmed both trends: ISC decreased significantly across repetitions regardless of condition or salience (main effect: z = − 17.69, p < 0.001, ΔR²m = 0.058), and was significantly higher in the external than the internal condition regardless of repetition or salience (main effect: z = − 14.48, p < 0.001, ΔR²m = 0.088). Mean ISC also decreased at a quicker rate in the external condition than in the internal condition (two-way interaction between repetition and condition: z = 4.17, p < 0.001, ΔR²m = 0.002).

The third prediction, that high salience videos would produce higher ISC than low salience videos, was not confirmed in the unthresholded brain-wide analysis (z = − 0.50, p > 0.05, ΔR²m < 0.001). However, globally averaging across all voxels may obscure salience-related differences that are anatomically specific. Neural results: connectivity analysis

Whereas ISC was designed to detect the degree of shared external attention over time, the connectivity analysis was designed to track how internal attention was coordinated over time. In this analysis, we calculated connectivity matrices by correlating the time course of each brain region to the time courses of all other regions, using a 200-region parcellation of the cortex (see “Methods”), and averaged these region-level connections within each of the 34 cortical networks to produce a network-level connectivity matrix. We then subtracted the connectivity values of the first repetition from the connectivity values of the fourth repetition.

The pattern of connectivity increases was similar across conditions. In both, the most prominent increases were between components of the FPCN and the DAN (e.g., L DorsAttnA and L ContC in the external condition; L DorsAttnA and R ContC in the internal condition), consistent with increased top-down attentional control over time. Notably, in both conditions, connectivity also increased between the FPCN and networks associated with internal attention, including the temporal-parietal junction (e.g., R ContC and R TempPar). This pattern was present regardless of whether participants were attending externally or internally, suggesting that sustained attentional control—whether directed outward or inward—recruits a common set of fronto-parietal interactions over time.

Neural results: general linear model

Whereas ISC and functional connectivity captured sustained, slow-changing dynamics of attention over the course of repeated viewing, the GLM analyses were designed to identify the transient neural responses associated with two discrete cognitive events: meta-awareness, the moment when participants recognized that their attention had lapsed, and attentional reorienting, the moment when participants redirected attention back to the task. We constructed four GLMs in total, two targeting the 3-s period before each button press (capturing meta-awareness) and two targeting the 3-s period after each button press (capturing attentional reorienting), with separate models for the internal and external attention conditions.

The networks engaged in the 3 s preceding button presses were largely consistent across conditions (Fig. 6A, B; see Supplement 1 for cluster-level details). The most robustly activated regions included the FPCN, DMN, and salience network, consistent with prior work implicating these networks in the detection of attentional lapses30,31. However, some condition-specific activations were observed and are worth noting. Orbitofrontal cortex and paracingulate cortex were more active in the external condition, while inferior temporal cortex, lateral occipital cortex, and superior parietal lobule were more active in the internal condition. These differences are consistent with the possibility that the functional roles of overlapping networks differ subtly by condition. During the external task, orbitofrontal and paracingulate regions, often associated with the DMN, may support the detection of internally generated distraction. During the internal task, superior parietal and occipital regions, more closely associated with the DAN and sensory processing, may support the detection of externally driven distraction. The condition-specific activations thus point toward a shared meta-awareness architecture that is nonetheless sensitive to the content being monitored.

The second set of GLMs examined networks involved in reorienting attention during the 3-s period immediately following button presses, with one GLM for the internal attention task and one for the external attention task. Discussion

Our study asked whether the automatic orientation of human attention is intrinsically internal, called an internal bias of attention, or whether a similar automatic state can exist for external attention. We evoked automatic external and internal attention states and compared the strength of each using self-reported mind-wandering events, whole-brain neural synchrony, and functional connectivity. We also investigated the cortical networks involved by analyzing moments of meta-awareness, when attention was directed at the current state of attention, and moments when attention was reoriented towards internal or external tasks.

We found that in both external and internal attention conditions, the behavioral evidence of attention on the videos (based on reported mind-wandering events) and the neural evidence of attention on the videos (based on synchrony to the external stimulus) were highest on the first repetition relative to the last repetition, suggesting that the external stimulus was most easily represented and hardest to ignore when it was novel. We suggest that this attention to the novel, external stimulus represented an automatic, externally oriented state. In this interpretation, automatic, externally oriented states exist, provided the right context. Our findings are in some ways comparable to the literature on external-internal multitasking paradigms in which information maintained in working memory conflicts with or facilitates an external task32,33. Those previous studies measured transient conflict between internal and external attention with low-dimensional stimuli; we dynamically assessed internal-external intrusions over an extended period of time using more complex, ecologically valid stimuli.

We predicted that the strongest internal pull of attention would exist on the fourth repetition of the video, producing a natural tendency to disengage from the external environment. This hypothesis was confirmed. According to the neural results, the external stimulus was least represented on the fourth repetition for both internal and external conditions, suggesting the least external attention. However, the results did include an interesting complexity. On the fourth repetition, there was still more mind-wandering when the task was to pay attention internally than when the task was to pay attention to the video. We had expected that four repetitions of attending to the same video would be more challenging than four repetitions of attending internally. Yet even after participants viewed the video clip four times, it was easier to pay attention to the video than to ignore it and pay attention to one’s own breathing. Despite previous literature advocating for a prepotent internally oriented bias of attention1, this finding shows a context in which an internal focus of attention is harder to maintain than an external one. These findings do not preclude that an internal bias exists in the broader context of day-to-day activities, but rather that there are some contexts, such as when engaged with videos (and perhaps also social media), when an external stimulus is exceptionally captivating, more so than internal simulation.

The term “mind-wandering” has recently been criticized for its ambiguity, with some calling attention to the diverse range of definitions that vary by experimenter and task design34,35. Most commonly, mind-wandering has been defined as a shift in the contents of thought away from an ongoing task in the external environment to internally self-generated thoughts and feelings22. However, others have suggested that the definition of mind-wandering must involve a reference to intentionality15,36, as it can be influenced by volitional processes, such as motivation37 and can be intentional if the external task is not cognitively demanding38.

The present study integrates both ideas, incorporating dominant external and internal attention states and their relationship to voluntary and involuntary cognition. We operationalize mind-wandering as involuntary attention on items that are off task, regardless of the internal-external distinction. Our findings are consistent with the view that mind-wandering can occur bidirectionally, from the internal to the external environment as well as from the external to the internal environment39. Our findings are also consistent with a previous suggestion that researchers should work to characterize the dynamics of mind-wandering rather than simply trying to define it40.

The relationship between mind-wandering and meta-awareness is primarily explored in mindfulness and meditation research. Meta-awareness is associated with increased control41,42, decreased mind-wandering events43–46, and increased ability to deflect negative internal thought47–49. These studies have generally tested meta-awareness towards internal or external contents, without comparing the two. Studies that directly compare them focus on unimodal meditation objects that do not dynamically change50,51. Other studies find meta-awareness, and metacognition more broadly, activate midline and orbital prefrontal structures46,52, but also bilaterally in the insula30,31. We found some activations outside of these canonical regions, including areas in the DMN and control networks. These regions may play a more pronounced role in meta-awareness than previously thought. Alternatively, these activations may have been due to other cognitive (e.g., mind-wandering) and sensory-motor control processes occurring before the button press.

Cortical networks are often characterized along an internal-external axis53,54. The DAN is associated with activation during goal-directed external attention, and deactivation during internally oriented attention8,55. In contrast, the DMN deactivates when external attention is engaged and activates when internal attention is required5,56. Similarly, positive correlation between the FPCN and DAN is a signature of goal-directed external attention, and positive correlation between the FPCN and the DMN is a hallmark of internal attention5,9–11.

However, in the present study, the results did not support a simple internal-external distinction. When we analyzed moments of attention reorientation, attention networks were similarly engaged during shifts of attention toward external and toward internal content. When we analyzed attention control over time, networks traditionally associated with external sensory processing, including the DAN, remained significantly correlated with the FPCN in the internal attention condition, often more so than in the external condition. Similarly, the R TempPar, often associated with internal attention, was still significantly connected to the FPCN when the focus of attention was directed externally. This pattern indicates that networks traditionally associated with external, sensory processing or with internally oriented cognition may serve functional roles beyond the internal-external dichotomy, potentially reflecting more general processes, such as sustained effort57,58. Alternatively, the increased coordination of these attention networks over time may reflect sustained or ramping of meta-cognitive monitoring or control59.

There are a number of limitations to note. We manipulated salience in the external condition by displaying videos that participants judged to be more or less exciting. In contrast, internal salience was held constant: participants were instructed to attend to their breath across all four repetitions, rather than to alternate among various internally focused tasks. Future work should aim to manipulate the internal task as well. For instance, instructing participants to focus on an autobiographical memory or simulating a future scenario—processes that naturally occur during unconstrained internal mentation60,61—may elicit more engagement with the internal environment compared to the external one. Enhancing internal salience in this way could weaken the dominance of the automatic externally oriented state evoked in our paradigm and help delineate the contexts in which internal and external modes of cognition compete15.

Furthermore, mind-wandering during external attention is not always driven by spontaneous internal thought, nor is mind-wandering during internal attention necessarily caused by external distractions. When the task involves focusing externally, off-task episodes may arise not only from internal cognition (e.g., memories or thoughts) but also from competing external distractors in the environment (e.g., scanner noise). Conversely, when attention is directed inward, mind-wandering may stem from spontaneous internal mentation as well as from external sensory inputs. We did not have participants distinguish between mind-wandering sources as others have done51,62. Nor did we distinguish between mind-wandering that was triggered by the video content itself (i.e., stimulus-dependent thoughts) versus mind-wandering unrelated to the stimulus63. In the context of film viewing in particular, a substantial proportion of off-task thought has been found to be stimulus-triggered64, suggesting that episodes in which participants mentally revisited earlier moments of the video clip may have gone untracked in our paradigm.

It is also worth noting that our behavioral measure captures only a specific variety of mind-wandering. We use the term “mind-wandering” throughout to refer to instances of off-task thought that were accompanied by meta-awareness. Mind-wandering frequently occurs outside of meta-awareness, and our button-press measure cannot capture those episodes27. Our measure is therefore not a ground truth index of mind-wandering in its entirety, but rather of a particular type: meta-aware mind-wandering. Following prior work using comparable self-caught designs31, we retain the term with the understanding that it refers to this more specific variety.

We do not claim that our experiment, relying on participants’ own introspection and judgement, is the most accurate way to capture the phenomenon of mind-wandering. Our goal in employing the self-caught method, rather than alternative paradigms with stronger validation65, was to elicit sustained meta-awareness and attentional control without interrupting the external stimulus. To this end, the self-caught method was suitable for the present design. However, other methods of measuring and manipulating mind-wandering may be useful in future designs.

The present study also lacks direct behavioral verification that participants complied with the task instructions. Future work would benefit from incorporating more direct manipulation checks. For example, a recognition memory test administered after each video could provide behavioral evidence that participants encoded the external stimulus. It is also worth noting that participants were not screened for prior familiarity with the videos. Although our counterbalancing procedure would attenuate any systematic bias introduced by differential prior exposure, pre-experimental familiarity remains a potential confound, as prior exposure may have influenced engagement ratings or accelerated habituation across repetitions.

In conclusion, the present study asked whether the automatic orientation of human attention is intrinsically internal or whether automatic external orientations can emerge given the right context. Using behavioral measures, whole-brain neural synchrony, and functional connectivity, we found evidence for both. We suggest that automatic attentional states arise from the interaction between the individual and their environment, and that understanding mind-wandering requires accounting for the pull of external stimuli alongside the pull of internal thought.

Methods

Subjects

Forty healthy human volunteers (aged 18–21, normal or corrected to normal vision, 37 right-handed, 17 female) were recruited from the community and from a subject pool sponsored by Princeton University. All subjects provided consent and received course credit for participation or monetary compensation. One subject was excluded due to a scanner malfunction. All procedures were approved by the Princeton Institutional Review Board. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Experimental setup

Subjects lay supine on the MRI bed. Visual stimuli and video content were displayed using PsychoPy266 and projected onto a screen 80 cm from the eyes through an angled mirror mounted on top of the head coil using a digital light processing projector (Hyperion MRI Digital Projection System, Psychology Software Tools, Sharpsburg, PA, USA) with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels at 60 Hz. Audio for each video clip was delivered with MRI-compatible Sensimetrics in-ear headphones. All responses were recorded using a button box held in the subject’s right hand and operated with the index finger.

Video stimuli

Six video stimuli were used. Three originated from widely popular mainstream media sources, including a clip from BBC’s TV drama series Sherlock (2 m 19 s), Dreamwork’s animated comedy Shrek (2 m 7 s), and NBC’s sitcom The Office (2 m 25 s). In Shrek, the ~2 min clip consisted of a personal discussion between two characters about one character’s feelings of prejudice; in Sherlock, the scene comprised an initial synopsis of a crime scene; in The Office, three characters interacted comically in a skit. These videos were selected on the basis of their popularity and engaging story lines encompassing vulnerability, drama, and comedy, respectively. Our hope was that these videos would be highly engaging to participants, and for this reason, we categorized them as “high salience.” In contrast, the second set of three videos were instructional clips selected from YouTube: how to make an origami heart (1 m 55 s), how to bake a cake (2 m 21 s), and how to brush your teeth (2 m 9 s). Our hope was that instructional videos on simple tasks would elicit less engagement compared to video content professionally designed to captivate audiences. We therefore categorized them as “low salience.” To measure the efficacy of high versus low salience categorization, at the end of the experiment, participants were asked to rate how engaging each video clip was on a scale from 1 to 10.

Task design

The task design is shown in Fig. 1. All subjects participated in two attention conditions. In the external attention condition, a video clip was presented on screen. Participants were instructed to pay attention to the video and press the button if they noticed that their minds had wandered from the video content. Mind-wandering was explicitly defined as content in the mind that was unrelated to the content of the video, such as thinking about potential dinner plans or memories about the previous weekend. Participants repeated this task four times in a row, sequentially viewing the same video clip and indicating any lapses of attention to the video with button presses. Once they finished four repetitions, participants were given an optional 30 s of rest and an opportunity to communicate with the experimenter. In the second condition, the internal attention condition, participants viewed another video clip (different from the video seen during the external attention condition), and this time were tasked with ignoring the video content and instead focusing on the rhythmic sensation of their breathing, silently reciting “breathe in, breathe out” while keeping their eyes open and on screen. Once again, subjects were instructed to press the button if they noticed that their mind had wandered from their breathing, for example, if their mind had wandered to the content of the video. The internal attention task was completed four times, with the same video clip presented each time. Thus, one complete run of the external attention condition consisted of focusing on a video clip for four repetitions, and one complete run of the internal condition consisted of focusing on breath and ignoring an external video clip for four repetitions.

Participants completed three runs of the external attention condition and three runs of the internal attention condition. For each run, a unique video clip was used. All participants viewed the same six videos, but the attention condition (external, internal) and order of video presentation were randomized for each subject. The six video clips were pseudo-randomly sorted into two groups, with two high salience videos and one low salience video assigned to group A, and one high salience and two low salience videos assigned to group B. Half of the participants completed the external condition using the three video clips in group A and the internal condition using the three video clips in group B. The other half of participants did the opposite, completing the internal condition using the three video clips in group A and completing the external condition using the three video clips in group B. Within each set of three videos, the order of video clips was randomly varied between subjects. Finally, half of the participants began the experiment performing three runs of the external attention condition followed by three runs of the internal condition, and the other half began with the internal attention condition followed by the external condition.

fMRI data acquisition

Functional imaging data were collected using a 3 T MAGNETOM Skyra scanner (Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany), equipped with a 64-channel head/neck coil. Gradient-echo T2*-weighted echo-planar images (EPI) with blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) contrast were used as an index of brain activity67. Functional image volumes were composed of 40 near-axial slices with no interslice gap and an in-plane acceleration factor of 2 using generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisition (GRAPPA), with slice thickness = 3.0 mm, field of view (FOV) = 200 mm, 80 × 80 matrix, 2.5 mm × 2.5 mm in-plane resolution, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 70°, and bandwidth of 1690 Hz/Px. A full functional volume was collected every 1.5 s (TR = 1500 ms).

Before functional runs, matching spin-echo EPI pairs with reversed phase-encode blips produced pairs of images with distortions going in opposite directions for blip-up/blip-down susceptibility distortion correction. An additional high-resolution structural image was collected for each participant, with a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence, voxel size = 1 mm isotropic, FOV = 256 mm, matrix size = 256 × 256, 176 slices, TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.96 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1000 ms, flip angle = 9°, iPAT GRAPPA = 2, bandwidth = 240 Hz/Px, anterior-posterior phase encoding, and ascending acquisition.

fMRI preprocessing

Data were preprocessed using FMRIPREP version 23.0.0. T1-weighted volumes were corrected for intensity nonuniformity using N4BiasFieldCorrection v2.1.068 and skull-stripped using the OASIS template in antsBrainExtraction.sh v2.1.0. Spatial normalization through nonlinear registration to the ICBM 152 Nonlinear Asymmetrical template version 2009c (https://nist.mni.mcgill.ca/icbm-152-nonlinear-atlases-2009/) was completed using the antsRegistration tool69. Brain tissue segmentation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white-matter (WM) and gray-matter (GM) using Fast70 was performed on extracted T1w images.

Functional data were slice time corrected using 3dTshift from AFNI v16.2.0771 and motion corrected using mcflirt (FSL v5.0.9)72. Flirt (FSL) performed boundary-based registration with six degrees of freedom to co-register the corresponding T1w images to functional data73. Motion correcting transformations, BOLD-to-T1w transformation, and T1w-to-template Montreal Neurological Institute warp were concatenated and applied in a single step using antsApplyTransforms (ANTs v2.1.0) using Lanczos interpolation. All functional images were high-passed (0.007) using Nilearn’s signal cleaning function (https://nilearn.github.io/stable/modules/generated/nilearn.signal.clean.html). For further description of fMRIprep’s preprocessing pipeline, see: https://fmriprep.readthedocs.io/en/latest/workflows.html. Before each video repetition, a 7.5 s countdown was displayed on screen. This data was trimmed so that only TRs consisting of the video presentation were used for analysis. Three buffer TRs were appended to the end of each repetition to account for hemodynamic lag.

fMRI analysis: inter-subject correlation

In conditions when participants attend to the external video stimulus, the stimulus should influence their brain activity in a manner that is temporally correlated among the participants. In contrast, in conditions when participants are paying little or no attention to the video stimulus, the stimulus should evoke less temporally correlated brain activity among subjects. Instead, cognitive processes unique to each subject will prevail and result in uncorrelated activity. Inter-subject correlation (ISC) was used to identify voxels that processed external information similarly across all subjects74,75. To derive this measure, we used Brainiak’s Leave One Subject Out ISC function76. To calculate ISC, the activation pattern of all voxels during stimulus presentation was extracted for one subject and correlated to the average timeseries across all other subjects for each respective voxel. This procedure was rotated for each subject, iteratively calculating the correlation of one subject’s activation patterns to the group’s activation patterns. This analysis produced an ISC value for every held-out subject at every voxel.

We derived two complementary measures from these ISC maps: A group-level (thresholded) ISC map, which identified voxels whose activity was significantly correlated across subjects, and an unthresholded ISC measure, which provided an overall, descriptive estimate of how similar subjects’ neural responses were on average, without statistical testing. To identify voxels showing significant cross-subject correlations, we used a nonparametric bootstrapping method (https://brainiak.org/docs/brainiak.html). In each bootstrap iteration, ISC values from all held-out subjects were resampled with replacement and averaged, generating a distribution around the true mean ISC value for each voxel. After 10,000 iterations, voxel-wise hypothesis tests were conducted to identify ISC values significantly greater than zero. The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using whole-brain FDR correction (p < 0.05). This analysis was performed for all repetitions in both the internal and external attention conditions, and results were averaged by salience type. To compute the unthresholded ISC measure, we averaged each subject’s held-out ISC map across all voxels to obtain a single value reflecting how strongly that participant’s brain activity aligned with the group overall. These subject-level ISC values were then averaged across participants, yielding a global measure of shared attention for each condition and repetition, which were again averaged by salience type.

fMRI analysis: within-subject connectivity analysis

Within-subject functional connectivity is a way to measure correlations between the BOLD time courses of two different brain regions within the same individual. We used the Schaefer 200-parcel atlas (https://nilearn.github.io/dev/modules/description/schaefer_2018.html) to divide the cortex into regions, and averaged region-level connectivity values within each of the 34 cortical networks defined by the atlas to produce a 34 × 34 network-level connectivity matrix of Fisher-z transformed values. In principle, for each subject, a separate matrix could be computed for each of the 4 repetitions, for each of the 6 videos, resulting in 24 matrices.

To understand how connectivity may have changed as subjects experienced the same videos in repetition, we directly compared the first and last repetitions in the following way. For each subject, we averaged together the three videos from the internal condition and the three videos from the external condition; then we averaged across subjects, averaging together all matrices for the internal attention condition and all matrices for the external attention condition. Finally, the aggregate matrices from the first repetition were subtracted from the aggregate matrices from the fourth repetition. This subtraction yielded two group-level difference matrices. One showed how, in the internal attention condition, connectivity changed from the first to the last repetition; the other showed the same, but for the external attention condition.

To assess statistical significance, we used a permutation-based approach. Prior to group-level averaging, we applied sign-flipping across subject-level matrices: a random vector of 1s and −1s (equal in length to the number of subject-video matrices) was multiplied element-wise across matrices, flipping the sign of correlations for a subset of subjects. We then averaged these permuted matrices across subjects and videos to generate one null matrix per iteration. Repeating this process 10,000 times produced a null distribution of correlation matrices. We performed two-sample t-tests between each cell of the original group-level matrix and its corresponding distribution from the 10,000 permuted matrices. Resulting p-values were false-discovery-rate (FDR) corrected across all unique network pairs.

fMRI analysis: GLM analysis

To assess the activity associated with meta-awareness and the focus of attention, we constructed two event-related GLMs for the period prior to and after button presses. We hypothesized that the period immediately prior to the button press (0–3 s) would roughly capture episodes when meta-awareness was engaged and that the 0–3 s period following the button press would capture when participants redirected attention internally or externally following a mind-wandering event. We selected these 3 s windows on the basis of previous work31. To set up both GLMs, we concatenated each set of four repetitions into one functional run. This produced 6 functional runs per subject, one for each video presented. The periods of interest (3 s before or after the button press) were convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function77 and inserted into a design matrix (one for each button press event) along with nuisance regressors. Nuisance regressors were defined as the first 5 principal components for CSF and WM masks, as well as 3 translation and 3 rotation time series. Before fitting the model, functional time series were spatially smoothed using a 4 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

First-level regression was performed using NiLearn’s FirstLevelModel function. The result was 12 beta maps for each subject, 6 for the period prior to the button press in each of the six videos, and 6 for the period following the button press in each of the six videos. We then averaged the beta maps of the three videos belonging to the internal attention condition and separately averaged the three videos belonging to the external condition. Thus, for each subject, there were two whole-brain beta maps for the meta-awareness period right before the button press (one for the external attention condition, one for the internal attention condition), and two beta maps for the period when the focus of attention shifted after the button press (again, one for the external and one for the internal attention condition).

A second-level model was fit using Nilearn’s SecondLevelModel, with the first-level contrasts of interest (external, internal, external minus internal) entered as input. To assess statistical significance while controlling for multiple comparisons, we applied a nonparametric permutation-based two-sided t-test (nilearn.glm.second_level.non_parametric_inference). This approach performs 10,000 random sign-flip permutations on whole-brain beta maps to generate a null distribution of voxelwise t-statistics under the null hypothesis of no effect. A voxelwise cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.001 was used. The resulting z-statistic map reflects the distribution of group-level effects corrected using permutation testing.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical tests were two-tailed with α = 0.05. Behavioral data were analyzed using mixed-design ANOVAs, independent-samples t-tests, and linear regression; ISC data were analyzed using nonparametric bootstrapping and linear mixed-effects models; connectivity data were analyzed using sign-flip permutation tests; and GLM results were assessed using nonparametric permutation-based inference. Multiple comparisons were controlled using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction where applicable (ISC voxel-wise tests; connectivity network-pair tests). Effect sizes are reported throughout as Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons, partial η² for ANOVA terms, ΔR²m for linear mixed-effects model fixed effects, and R² for regression analyses. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size; a post-hoc power analysis is reported in the Supplementary Materials. Code and anonymized data are publicly available at https://github.com/isaacrc/metaawareness-and-default-states.
