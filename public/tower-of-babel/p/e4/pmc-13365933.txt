Women’s Health
Authors: Elizabeth Dalgarno, Joanne Elliott, Tina Purnat, Elena Petelos, Gethin Hopkin, Greg Williams, Jedidah Mould, Abin Thomas, Amy Wilcock, Laura Swain, Arpana Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen and children’s health sits within a wider social justice framework where structural inequalities shape these outcomes across differing global contexts. Health is gendered, and we do not reflect this enough in research, service, product design and delivery. In the current polarised political environments, women’s health advocates are concerned that erosion of human rights, health services, and programmes is not just a line we need to defend from divestment. We are going into a time where fundamental values around health and changing the dynamics of structural determinants of health, is paramount. Structural violence, political conflict and systemic restrictions can produce profound barriers to women when accessing care, the undermining of women’s autonomy and the reinforcement of health inequities.Across diverse settings, this gender inequality also persists via more subtle but equally harmful mechanisms. This includes interpersonal biases that reflect structural discrimination including misogyny and racism. In addition, deprivation can constrain women’s ability to engage in behaviours that promote health improvement across the life-course.Gaps in maternal and postnatal care, including inadequate guidance policies and frameworks, reveal how women’s needs are still being deprioritised within healthcare systems. Midwives and allied professionals are also constrained in their duties yet play critical roles in health improvement.Reproductive health inequalities are further shaped by misinformation and financial barriers, which limit equitable access to required services. Collectively, these studies highlight that knowledge alone cannot translate into empowerment for women and children without the structural support needed to facilitate this.Community-based interventions, such as those focused on peer support and inclusive health programmes, indicate the potential for transforming these systems into woman-centred care that is culturally responsive.These health inequities require dedicated approaches to tackle these social injustices, which means shifting away from top-down biomedical models towards community-led, strengths-based, place-based and rights-based approaches where these voices are centred. Only by addressing the political, economic and cultural determinants of health can we truly restore agency.
