Cross-species transferability and genetic diversity analysis in Linum species using microsatellite markers
Authors: Vijaykumar Kailasrao Raut, Umakanta Ngangkham, Aneeta Yadav, Vikender Kaur, Pavan Kumar Malav, Ankit Saroha, Gyanendra Pratap Singh, Dhammaprakash Pandhari Wankhede, Mamta Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Linum comprises over 230 species, with L. usitatissimum valued for linen and oil, yet crop wild relatives (CWRs) remain underutilized due to limited availability and poor molecular characterization. In this study, 96 accessions representing 16 wild and one cultivated species were evaluated using 49 SSR markers to assess genetic diversity and cross-species transferability (CST). A total of 473 alleles were detected, with allelic richness ranging from 1 to 22 alleles per locus and polymorphic information content (PIC) values reaching up to 0.91, underscoring the discriminatory power of the markers. High genetic diversity parameters, including Shannon’s index (up to 1.296), revealed substantial allelic variation in species such as L. bienne and L. lewisii. Population structure analysis indicated moderate differentiation (global Fst = 0.186) and the presence of admixed individuals, reflecting complex evolutionary histories shaped by multiple gene flow events and introgression. Cluster analysis proved effective for uncovering pedigree relationships among genotypes and offered advantages over PCoA, while CST rates ranged from 45.45 to 100% across species. The high CST observed within sections (Linum, Dasylinum) highlights conserved genomic regions, whereas variable amplification in Linastrum points to divergent evolutionary trajectories. Together, these findings demonstrate that SSR markers not only capture allelic richness and population structure but also illuminate evolutionary processes such as speciation, introgression, and lineage divergence. The study establishes a valuable foundation for integrating Linum CWRs into breeding programs, thereby broadening the genetic base in cultivated linseed. These findings can enhance the scope and effectiveness of future breeding initiatives, particularly where such markers are lacking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42744-0.Keywords: Linum, Simple sequence repeat (SSR) marker, Cross-species transferability (CST), Evolution, Genetic diversity, Population structure, Cross-amplificationSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Linseed (Linum usitatissimum (L. us) L., X = 15, 2n = 30) is a member of the Linaceae family. It stands as the second most important oilseed rabi crop in India after rapeseed mustard in terms of production and cultivation1. Genetic diversity and trait variability are fundamental to breeding efforts; therefore, effective management of genetic resources is of paramount importance. Crop wild relatives (CWRs) of cultivated species (spp.) can serve as valuable sources of agro morphological and economically important traits for crop improvement. Global germplasm collections housed in genebanks contain a total of 46,513 Linum accessions (acc.); however, the wild progenitor Linum bienne (L. bn) is significantly underrepresented, with only 279 acc. documented in genebanks worldwide2. Indian Council of Agricultural Research-National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (ICAR-NBPGR), New Delhi, India is actively engaged in collection, conservation, evaluation, scientific studies, and documentation to identify germplasm with valuable trait variation. Utilization of linseed CWRs in the pre-breeding activities and breeding programmes remains significantly limited due to their low availability in genebanks and, the lack of comprehensive molecular characterization. The genus Linum has more than 230 spp3. In addition to cultivated L. usitassimum (L. us), five wild spp. as, L. strictrum (L. st), L. bienne (L. bn), L. grandiflorum (L. gr), L. mysorense (L. my) and L. perenne (L. pn) have been reported in India4. Potential usage of some CWRs of Linum include fiber quality and core hardness (L .bn), bud fly resistance, lignan and neolignans synthesis (L. gr), nutrition and oil quality L. lewisii (L. lw), drought and rust tolerance L. marginale (L. mr). Being a self-pollinating annual crop, the genetic base of linseed is narrow. Selective breeding of linseed for two different products, fiber, and seeds has led to the development of two distinct cultivar groups; fiber flax and linseed, which are grown in different parts of the world as different crops. The main purpose of the fiber is the production of high-quality linen whereas; linseed oil originating from the seeds has many industrial applications in paints, linoleum, carpets, and ink. In recent years it has become more difficult to breed new fiber flax cultivars with better fiber quality, increased fiber yield and the required resistances, due to the repeated use of modern cultivars as crossing parents. Also, experts dealing with testing plant cultivars for DUS (Distinctness, Uniformity and Stability) criteria or Plant Breeder’s right previously noticed that morphological variation among new cultivars had significantly decreased5. Dutch flax breeders also experienced increasing difficulties in developing new cultivars with improved fiber content, fiber quality and other important traits over the existing cultivars.

The introduction of genes from CWRs into cultivated crops has been widely recognized as a critical strategy for enhancing traits of major importance to global food security6,7. Limited diversity available in the cultivated Linum gene pool for various agro-morphological traits including fiber strength and quality, demands enhancement of genetic base by expanding the genetic diversity. In most cases, the domestic gene pools lack the genetic diversity to enable plant breeders to select for the required trait. Linum CWRs can serve largely for this purpose.

Pale flax (L. bn), a wild progenitor of the cultivated linseed possesses huge potential to serve as an important reservoir of agronomically valuable traits, in particular for resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses8. The importance of utilization of wild Linum germplasm has been realized in the past also. Investigation of the genetic variation in the gene pool of fiber flax and linseed and the resultant low amount of genetic variation prompted to investigate the genetic diversity among wild spp. related to L. us9.

Among various molecular markers, microsatellite markers or SSR (Simple Sequence Repeat) markers are the most popular and versatile molecular marker system that has been widely used because of their valuable features such as abundance, co-dominance inheritance, high polymorphism, reproducibility, and ease of assay by PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) amplification. All these features make them very suitable for genetic diversity studies in plants10,11. Microsatellites arise in regions consisting of short numbers of tandem repeats of DNA sequences in the genome, among closely related spp. and varieties and, reveal extreme polymorphism. The number of repeats is a characteristic of an allelic deviation at a certain locus. However, the genomic resources and knowledge of SSR markers are scarce in distantly related CWRs of Linum12. In the absence of specific markers, cross-amplification of microsatellites across wild spp. can potentially fill the gap and pave the way for their molecular characterization.

Considering the huge potential of linseed CWRs, the present study was performed to evaluate the molecular diversity of a total of 96 acc. (representing 16 wild and one cultivated spp.) of Linum germplasm using SSR markers. A greater understanding of molecular diversity in the genus Linum may help in enhancing the use of these CWRs in various other studies to discern the basis of trait variation in Linum. This information may also be useful in upgrading the breeding potential of the parental materials conventionally used under breeding programmes for enhancing the realized heterosis for various economic traits. The objective of this study was to generate information on the genetic diversity of the different CWRs of Linum spp. assessed through SSR markers. Further, the cross-species transferability (CST) of these markers from cultivated Linum was checked in 16 wild spp. The polymorphic SSR markers identified in the present study can be directly used in other related spp. for genetic and evolutionary studies especially in the spp. where they are not available.

Materials & methods

Plant materials

Plant material used in the present study at ICAR-NBPGR comprised acc. of 16 wild spp. along with the cultivated Linum spp. comprising 89 exotic and 7 indigenous acc. with diverse geographical distributions (Table S1 & Fig. 1). These acc. were used to study CST and molecular diversity of SSR markers (Table S2). The set of 96 acc. comprised of Linum biene (L. bn) (15 acc.), Linum perenne (L. pn) (12 acc.), Linum usitatissimum (L. us) (02 acc.), Linum lewisii (L. lw) (32 acc.), Linum marginale (L. mr) (02 acc.), Linum grandiflorum (L. gr) (03 acc.), Linum austriacum (L. au) (11 acc.), Linum pallescens (L. pa) (01 acc.) and Linum altaicum (L. al) (01 acc.) belonging to section Linum13–16, Linum hirsutum (L. hm) (06 acc.) belonging to section Dasylinum13–16 and Linum tenuifolium (L. tn) (02 acc.), Linum hudsonioides (L. hd) (01 acc.), Linum strictum (L. st) (02 acc.), Linum aristatum (L. ar) (03 acc.), Linum corymbiferum (L. co) (01 acc.) Linum maritimum (L. ma) (01 acc.) and Linum sulcatum (L. su) (01 acc.) belonging to section Linastrum13–16 (Table S3). While certain Linum species included in the study had relatively fewer accessions (Table S1) due to constraints in accession availability, they were retained in the analysis to ensure broad taxonomic and geographic coverage. While this approach strengthens the comparative framework at the interspecific level, it does introduce limitations for interpreting species level diversity. Sampling constraints may limit the capture of genetic variation, and diversity estimates for underrepresented species are best regarded as a foundation for future insights into their genetic diversity. As accession availability is uneven across germplasm collections, the present study was conducted with the available sampling set, acknowledging this as an inherent challenge in germplasm-based research. The Linum germplasm accession used in the study have either been imported or collected/material transferred within India as per national and international guidelines and regulations (Table S1). The import was facilitated by ICAR-National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (ISO 9001:2015 Certified Institute), a nodal organization in India for import of germplasm for food and agriculture. The name of the institutions from where the import was effected in India and country name from where it was originally procured at the source organizations, along with the PI number have been mentioned in the Table S1. From the total wild germplasm, five germplasm accessions (L. perenne- 4 acc. and L. grandiflorum- 1 acc.) were collected/material transferred within India as per the national guidelines. Two cultivated species accessions (released variety) namely T-397 (IC0526166) and TL-99 (IC632379) were procured from National Genebank, India. The wild L. grandiflorum, IC633096 (identifier ID-VKNT-19/1-representing Vikender Kaur & Nalini Tiwari) was collected and the voucher sample was deposited in the National Genebank, located at ICAR-NBPGR, New Delhi, India-110012 from source institute (Chandra Shekhar Ajad University of Agriculture &Technology, Kanpur, India) (https://nbpgr.org.in/nbpgr2023/germplasm-exchange/) by one of our coauthors Dr. Vikender Kaur. The source of collection was mentioned from the original institution from Kanpur, Uttar Pradesh (26.49135216, 80.3067286).

DNA isolation

The DNA samples of different Linum spp. were collected by growing the seeds of various germplasm acc. under laboratory conditions. The seeds were placed in plastic petri plates (90 mm diameter). 15 to 25 seeds of each acc. were placed in the petri plates with germination paper after sterilization with 0.1% bavistin treatment to avoid any contamination due to microbial growth. The seeds were allowed to germinate under dark conditions for two to seven days followed by their exposure under light conditions (10 to 15 days) until the seedlings gained sufficient biomass for DNA isolation. To get enhanced germination and seedling growth the seeds of wild species were provided with a Hoagland’s solution17 supplemented with 0.01% gibberellic acid (GA). Around 3-week-old seedling was used for DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted following the Cetyl Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide (CTAB) method with few modifications as described by Doyle and Doyle18.The quality and quantity of the isolated DNA was checked by 1.0% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis and ScanDrop2 spectrophotometer (Analytik Jena).

PCR amplification

PCR amplifications were performed in a 20µL reaction containing the following reagents: 0.25 µM of each forward and reverse primer, 1 × PCR buffer supplemented with 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.25 mM dNTPs, 1 U Taq DNA polymerase, 40 ng genomic DNA and nuclease free sterile water. PCR was performed in thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) with the following programme. Initial denaturation 94ºC for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94ºC for 30 s., primer annealing at 48–60 °C (variable annealing temperature according to melting temperature of primers) for 30 s. and primer extension at 72 °C for 1.5 min, with the final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplification products were visualized by electrophoresis on 3.0% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide. The length of each fragment (bp) in the amplified product was determined with the scale of the reference DNA ladder. The gels were visualized and photographed in the gel documentation system (G-Box Syngene).

Statistical analysis

Allele sizes were evaluated using 50 bp and 100 bp DNA ladders (G-Biosciences Ltd.). SSR allele sizes were scored using AlphaView SA (version 3.4.0.0) software, with manual curation. The genotypic data of 49 SSR markers were used for the estimation of genetic diversity parameters such as number of different alleles per locus (Na), number of effective alleles (Ne), Shannon’s Information Index (I), observed heterozygosity (HObs), expected heterozygosity (HExp) and, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed by plotting eigenvector values in a scatter graph taking the first principal component and the second principal component as the axes using GenAlEx 6.503 software.19 A hierarchical analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) among populations was conducted using GenAlEx 6.503 software.19 Measures of genetic differentiation between populations were interpreted20,21 as Fst values equal to 0 when subpopulations are identical in allele frequencies and 1 when they have different alleles. Populations with little divergence have Fst values < 0.05; moderately differentiated populations, 0.05–0.15; greatly differentiated populations, 0.15–0.25; and very greatly differentiated populations, > 0.2520,21. Polymorphic information content (PIC) was calculated using Cervus 3.0.7 (Field Genetics Ltd., London, UK), and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA 11 software22.

Population structure analysis

The population structure of 96 Linum genotypes was analyzed using genotypic data from 49 SSR markers with STRUCTURE software (version 2.3.4)23. To estimate Bayesian distributions, the program was run for K = 1 to K = 10 with five independent replications per K using the admixture model, a burn-in period of 200,000, and 200,000 MCMC iterations. The optimum K values were estimated using the StructureSelector program24, based on the peak value of ΔK25. STRUCTURE generates a Q-matrix (QST) listing the estimated membership coefficients for each genotype in each subgroup at every value of K. Each individual was assigned to a K subgroup if more than 70% of its genome fraction value corresponded to that subgroup.

Evaluation of cross-species transferability (CST) of SSR markers in Linum spp.

To study CST of SSR primers, bands were scored across 16 crop wild relatives (CWRs) of Linum spp. with reference to L. usitatissimum, and recorded as present ( +) or absent ( −). The transferability of SSR primers in Linum CWRs was assessed across taxonomic sections, using cultivated L. usitatissimum as the reference.

Results

Genetic diversity statistics of Linum spp.

Polymorphism for the SSR loci

A set of one cultivated and 16 CWRs of Linum, comprising 96 germplasm accessions from diverse geographical regions (Table S1), were screened with 49 SSR markers (Bickel et al., 201126; Table S2) for diversity analysis. A total of 473 alleles were generated across 96 acc. belonging to 17 different spp. with a mean of 9.65 ± 0.61 alleles per locus (K), ranging from 1 to 22 (Table S2). The allele size of the 49 SSR markers ranged from 50 to 400 bp with the exception of primers 12–10, 13–14, 27-SR1551, and 47–188103, which amplified band sizes greater than 400 bp. SSR primer 47–188103 produced the highest number of alleles (22). The value of expected heterozygosity (HExp) ranged from 0.00 (Primer 23–108) to 0.42 ± 0.08 (Primer 49–668637) with a mean value of 0.20 ± 0.06. Observed heterozygosity (HObs) ranged from 0 (primers 10, 16, 17, 18, 19, 21, 23, 28, 29, 30, 31, 33, 36, 37, 44, 45) to 0.47 ± 0.11 (primer 49–668637), with a mean value of 0.09 ± 0.04. The PIC value of the markers varied from 0.00 (Primer no. 23–108) to 0.91 (Primer no. 47–188103) with an average value of 0.748 (Table S2). Of the 49 SSR markers, 48 loci (97.95%) were highly informative, with PIC values ≥ 0.50, indicating strong capability to differentiate genotypes and assess genetic diversity. Shannon’s Information Index (I) values ranged from 0 (primer 23–108) to 0.80 ± 0.18 (primer 49–668637), with an average of 0.37 per locus. Negative F values for primers 15–31, 24–128, and 49–668637 suggest an excess of heterozygosity at these loci.

Population genetic diversity

Linum CWRs (Table S3) were assessed for genetic diversity using SSR markers. The mean number of alleles (Na) amplified over SSR loci ranged from 0.347 (L. al) to 5.163 (L. bn) (Table S4). The total number of alleles detected in each spp. ranged from 17 (L. al) to 253 (L. bn) indicating the extent of genetic variation detected across multiple loci in diverse Linum spp. used in this study. The spp. with Na > 2 comprised L. bn (5.2), L. pn (2.551), L. lw (5.061) and L. au (2.980) with a total number of alleles 253, 125, 241, and, 146, respectively, indicating allelic diversity. The private alleles (Np) were detected in 14 spp. except in L. tn, L. al and L. ma. For the remaining spp., the number of private alleles (Np) ranged from 0.020 (L. st, L. us, L. gr and L. co) to 1.163 (L. bn) (Table S4). All the acc. of L. bn confirmed the presence of private alleles with a minimum of 4 SSR (EC1073064) loci and a maximum of 14 SSR loci (EC1073068) (Table S5), revealing the potential of this species to contribute to the genetic enhancement of the cultivated Linum gene pool. Across population, the heterozygosity percentage (Ho) ranged from 0 (L. co and L. al) to 0.216 (L. au). The graphical representation of heterozygosity and allelic pattern in 17 Linum spp. is depicted in Fig. 2. The mean PIC value for the applied markers for individual spp. was 0.593 for L. bn, 0.581 (L. lw), 0.390 (L. au), 0.353 (L. pn), 0.249 (L. hm) and 0.215 (L. ar). For rest of the spp. PIC values ranged from 0 (L. co, L. al) to 0.175 (L. tn, L. hd, L. st, L. us, L. mr, L. gr, L. pa, L. ma and L. su). Out of 49 SSR markers used for study, 41 were polymorphic (83.67%) in L. bn, 40 were polymorphic (81.63%) in L. lw, 25 were polymorphic (51.02%) in L. au and 21 were polymorphic (42.86%) in L. pn (Table S4). For rest of the spp., the polymorphism ranged from 0 (L. hd, L. us, L. pa, L. co, L. al, L. ma and L. sul) to 22.45% (L. hm and L. ar). Shannon’s Information Index (I) values for independent populations varied from 0 (L. al and L. co) to 1.296 (L. bn). Authors emphasize here that for species represented by only limited/one accession (e.g., L. corymbiferum, L. altaicum and L. maritimum), diversity values appeared as zero due to undersampling rather than true genetic uniformity. These values are retained for broad coverage but remain indicative only, highlighting the need for expanded accession sets in future studies. The negative and zero F values of L. hd, L. ma, L. pa and L. su (-1) and L. co, L. al (0) indicated high level of heterozygosity in these sub-populations. Consequently, we performed an Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) to quantify the level of genetic differentiation between the predefined groups (Table S6). The main contribution to the genetic variance in our germplasm originated from the difference among individuals belonging to the overall population (68%). Differences among subpopulations and between samples within each subpopulation accounted for 19% and 13%, respectively. All results showed a high level of significance (P < 0.001). Genetic differentiation coefficient (or Global F statistics = Fst) results indicate the presence of highly differentiated populations (Fst = 0.186), which points towards the occurrence of population structure. To better reveal the relationships between the populations, the gene flow (Nm) and genetic differentiation coefficient (Fst) were estimated for all pairs of populations. Fst between populations ranged from Fst = 0.204 (L. bn—L. lw) (Table S7) with substantial gene flow (Nm = 0.977) (Table S8) to Fst = 1 (L. co—L. al) with strictly no genetic exchange (Nm = 0). When compared to cultivated L. us, the least differentiation was observed with L. bn and L. lw (Fst = 0.445 and 0.496), with medium genetic exchange estimates (Nm = 0.299 and 0.254).

The graphical representation of heterozygosity and allelic pattern in 17 Linum spp.

Genetic relatedness

Hierarchical analysis (HCA)

Relationships among analyzed accessions were visualized using a consensus phenogram constructed from pairwise distances with the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) clustering method. The genetic distance between the population pairs ranged from 0.056 (L. ma—L. co) to 0.461 (L. lw—L.au) with a mean value of 0.23 (Table S9a). In reference to cultivated Linum (L. us), maximum genetic identity was observed with L. bn (0.379), followed by L. pn (0.295) and L. lw (0.253) (Table S9b). The cluster diagram grouped the 96 wild Linum genotypes into seven major clusters (CI–CVII), which were further subdivided into distinct sub-clusters (Fig. 3). The acc. grouped under a predefined spp. mostly clustered together either forming an individual cluster (CIII, CIV and CVI) or sub-clusters of an individual cluster (CI, CII, CV and CVII) each were sub-grouped into three to four sub-clusters. For instance, Cluster CI was subdivided into three sub-clusters: CI-a (10 acc. of L. bn), CI-b (2 acc. of L. us), and CI-c (L. bn, 5 acc.; L. mr, 2 acc.). CII-a comprised all 12 acc. of L. pn; CII-b contained 2 acc. of L. tn; and CII-c included all 6 acc. of L. hm. Clusters CIII, CIV, and CVI contained 6, 12, and 8 acc. of L. lw, respectively. Single acc. of L. su and L. ma were grouped in CIV and CVI respectively along with L. lw. Cluster CV was subdivided into four sub-clusters: CV-a (3 acc. of L. ar), CV-b (3 acc. of L. gr), and CV-c (2 acc. of L. st together with single acc. of L. pa and L. hd). Custer CVII possessed all 11 acc. of L. au, 6 acc. of L. lw, only single aac. of L. al and L. co.

Hierarchical clustering of 96 wild Linum genotypes, yielding 7 distinct clusters (CI-CVII) with sub-clusters, effectively distinguishing between species, and revealing patterns of genetic diversity.

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA)

The PCoA based on the pairwise genetic distance matrix among all the 96 Linum acc. showed that the lines were distributed in all four quadrangles, showing a high degree of genetic variability (Fig. S1). A scatter plot generated from the PCoA analysis showed that the first three components accounted for 7.9%, 5.8% and 4.2% of the genetic variation resulting in a comparatively limited total genetic variation of 17.96% (Table S10).

Population structure analysis across Linum spp.

Understanding population structure is essential before undertaking more sophisticated analyses. As described by Wright (1965)27, Fst values of 0.05–0.15 indicate moderate differentiation, 0.15–0.25 indicate high differentiation, and values > 0.25 indicate very high differentiation. Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA), of the 96 acc. had shown evidence of population differentiation with a high Fst value (0.186) and the existence of population structure (Table S6). The 96 individuals of flax wild germplasm were further evaluated for group structures using the STRUCTURE program. Bayesian structure analysis revealed a notable peak plateau for the ad hoc measure ΔK at K = 2 and the second highest peak at K = 5 (Fig. 4a). This finding suggests that the entire population can be effectively categorized into two to five distinct groups. Population structures of 96 genotypes based on 49 SSR markers are shown in Fig. 4b. Different colors within groups indicate the proportion of shared ancestry with other groups, reflecting admixture. The population structure group of 96 genotypes based on inferred ancestry values was also estimated (Table S11). The percentage of individuals in each population belonging to each group was estimated based on Q-matrix (QST) for each value of K and the cut-off probability for assigning to a group was assumed to be 70%. Seventy-five of the 96 genotypes showed membership coefficients > 70% of their genome fraction value and were therefore assigned to specific groups. This shows the suitability of the population for tracing the phylogenetic history. However, only 21 genotypes were classified as admixture since they showed estimated membership coefficients of less than 70% which pose some challenges due to their admixed lineages to trace the unbiased evolutionary history.

Population structure analysis across Linum spp. (a) Bayesian structure analysis identifies two primary population clusters (K = 2) and five subpopulations (K = 5) within Linum spp; (b) Population structure analysis of Linum spp. reveals genetic differentiation and admixture patterns.

Evaluation of cross-species transferability (CST) in Linum spp.

Of the 49 SSR primers, 33 (67%) could be successfully amplified in L. us. Out of these 33 markers, eight markers (24.24%) were successfully cross-amplified in all 17 spp., and two primers (6.06%; primers 32 and 37) produced strong amplification in at least eight spp. (Table S12). All the studied spp. in the current study belonged to three taxonomic Sects. 13–16. The CST was evaluated in reference to the cultivated Linum to various sections viz. Linum, Dasylinum and Linastrum. CST showed varied transferability among sections, ranging from 77.49% in Linastrum to 96.97% in Dasylinum (Fig. 5a). The percentage of transferability ranged from 45.45% (spp. L. al, section—Linum) to 100% (spp. L. bn, L. pn and L. lw, section—Linum) (Fig. 5b). Both maximum and minimum CST were observed in this section i.e., Linum among all the sections. The average cross-transferability was 89.015% within the botanical section Linum. In section Dasylinum, only a single spp. L. hm was available with 96.97% CST. In section Linastrum CST ranged from 66.67% (L. co) to 90.91% (L. ar) with an average of 77.49% (Table S13).

Discussion

Genetic diversity statistics of Linum spp.

Polymorphism for the SSR loci

Molecular markers are effective for assessing genetic variation in plant populations by analyzing numerous genomic loci28. Various marker systems, including AFLP, RAPD, ISSR, IRAP, SSR, and SNPs, have been used in Linum to study genetic diversity8,29. A study of 708 cultivated Linum acc. and 10 wild spp. using polymorphic IRAP markers found the highest genetic diversity in wild Linum spp. compared to landraces, breeding lines, and cultivars30. In the current study comprising 96 acc. of Linum (Table S1), the total number of alleles (473) and mean number of alleles (9.65 ± 0.61) per locus ranged from 1 to 22 (Table S2) with 49 SSR primers, which were substantially higher than Soto-Cerda et al.,4 who reported 52 total alleles ranging from 2 to 4, and an average of 2.26 alleles per locus using EST-SSR markers in eleven diverse Linum spp. EST-SSRs have earlier been reported to show lower allelic variation than G-SSRs31. Indeed, Roose-Amsaleg et al.,32 and Deng et al.,33 reported an average of 3.32 and 3.45 alleles per locus, respectively in L. us using G-SSRs. However, the numbers of spp. used in these cases were lower than current study. Out of 49 SSR markers, 24 (48.97%) showed allele numbers more than 10. Using a similar set of primers developed by Bickel et al.,26 in seven diverse wild Linum spp., the number of alleles ranged from 2 to 8, with a mean of 3.32.We speculate that the higher allele numbers observed in our study may result from analyzing a greater number of diverse spp. compared to previous studies. A higher number of alleles at a locus indicates greater genetic variability and enhances the utility of markers34. When selecting genetic markers for research purposes, certain characteristics are preferred to ensure informative results such as markers with a greater number of alleles tend to be more informative and possess greater discriminatory power35. These markers may facilitate a better assessment of genetic diversity and structure, which is crucial for managing genetic resources. Additionally, they can also be used for marker-trait association. From a conservation perspective, identifying marker locus with higher allele numbers can help develop strategies to preserve genetic diversity, highlighting loci essential for adaptability and resilience to environmental changes36. The higher proportion of markers with high PIC values (Table S2) indicates their usefulness for studying the genetic diversity of wild Linum spp. The PIC measures the informativeness of genetic markers in distinguishing genotypes, considering both allele numbers and frequencies37. A high PIC value > 0.5 typically indicates considerable genetic diversity within a population. Previous studies reported PIC values ranging from 0.08 to 0.55, with averages around 0.384, 0.3538 and 0.4726. Our study, however, showed a higher average PIC of 0.748, indicating substantial genetic variability critical for adaptability. Interestingly, despite this high PIC, we found a low Shannon’s Diversity Index (I). This implies that although spp. diversity is present, uneven abundance among spp. leads to a less balanced community structure39.

Population genetic diversity

CWRs of Linum may be investigated for their potential as trait donors (Table S3) in Linum improvement programmes as they may possess diverse alleles of genes of interest. In the current study, high allelic diversity for SSR loci was observed in various Linum CWRs. The wild progenitor (L. bn) exhibited high allelic abundance, with a mean number of alleles (Na = 5.163) and a total of 253 alleles, indicating significant genetic variation (Table S4). This allelic richness is crucial for conservation efforts and understanding population bottlenecks. Molecular marker-assisted methods demonstrated even higher allelic richness40,41 and, in simulated collections of CWRs, effectively conserving localized alleles at risk of loss42. Private alleles (Np) indicate potential evolutionary significance43. The wild progenitor (L. bn) has the highest number of Np (1.163) among studied populations (Table S4) ranging from 4–14 (Table S5), highlighting its conservation value for creating fertile hybrids with desirable traits in Linum breeding programs. The low number of private alleles in other populations may result from bottlenecks, genetic drift, isolation, or small sample sizes. Therefore, it is important to develop comprehensive conservation strategies to maintain allelic diversity in these populations. Population decline can be estimated by assessing excessive heterozygosity44. The level of heterozygosity was less in all studied spp. (Table S4) indicating that these spp. have maintained genetic integrity due to limited outbreeding in the past. The allelic pattern of the studied Linum CWRs showed highly appreciable diversity for population variability (Fig. 2). The SSR loci with high polymorphic information content (PIC ≥ 0.50%) for L. bn and L. lw demonstrate strong discriminatory power among genotypes of each spp. This suggests that these markers can effectively reveal genetic diversity within populations. Earlier studies reported a PIC of 0.46 for Linum varieties and spp26. Comparatively low Shannon diversity index (I) across spp. (Table S2) than within spp. (Table S4) was realized under the current study, likely attributed to the uneven distribution or relative abundance of spp. in communities comprising population. The Shannon diversity index is a quantitative measure of diversity that considers both spp. richness and evenness45. A higher Shannon diversity index value indicates a greater number of spp. and a more even distribution of individuals among that spp46. The relative abundances of spp. in communities can affect the diversity indices of such communities as it is a measure of how common a spp. is compared to other spp. in a given population. The reason is that diversity not only takes into consideration the spp. richness (the number of spp.) of a community but also the evenness in the distribution of the individuals that make up a community among the various spp. present47,48. While our analysis revealed low levels of diversity across Linum species (Table S4), we acknowledge that unequal sampling sizes- particularly species represented by only a single accession such as L. corymbiferum and L. altaicum- limit species-specific diversity estimates. In these cases, the absence of observed variation should be interpreted as a consequence of under-sampling rather than true genetic uniformity. Unequal sample sizes may bias diversity estimates, so the interpretations emphasize relative interspecific patterns rather than absolute measures. The low diversity observed in comparatively better-represented taxa however indicates that many Linum species maintain genetic integrity, while also highlighting the need for expanded sampling to validate species-specific diversity. Thus, our results provide a valuable preliminary framework for Linum diversity, while underscoring the importance of more balanced sampling strategies in future work.

The negative and zero F values of certain spp. (L. hd, L. ma, L. pa, L. su, L. co and L. al) may be attributed to sampling bias, owing to the limited number of representative acc. of these wild relatives available for study. In the studies by Wu et. al.44, negative inbreeding coefficients were interpreted an excess of heterozygotes due to outbreeding, with a possible risk of outbreeding depression. A high Fst (fixation index) value indicates that the two populations have diverged genetically, suggesting a low level of gene flow (Nm) and potentially a history of isolation. The results of the AMOVA (Table S6) analysis (Global F statistics = 0.186; P = 0.001) also provided evidence to support a population structure. The pairwise Fst values (Table S7) showed that there was a moderate to high level of genetic differentiation between each pair of populations. When considering the cultivated spp. (Table S8), L. us was least differentiated (low Fst) with L. bn (Fst = 0.445) and L. lw (Fst = 0.496) with comparatively higher gene flow (Nm) than other spp. The degree of differentiation based on Fst has been defined by earlier researchers49–51. Wright (1965)27 reported that the average number of migrants exchanged between local populations (Nm < 1) indicates limited gene flow among subpopulations. For the rest of the spp., cultivated Linum was found highly differentiated with limited exchange of immigrants. Though, L. lw, seems less likely to outcross with cultivated Linum52, the moderate gene flow between L. us and L. lw (Nm = 0.254) might be due to the violation of the assumption that migration rates among the populations sampled are not subject to a large variance or transfer of genes (introgression in ancestral history). This may result in moderately large Nm values when sampling includes clusters of populations widely separated from other clusters completely isolated from genetic exchange53. Li et. al.,54 worked on the hypothesis that high genetic diversity could be detected within populations and significant genetic differentiation could exist among populations due to the restricted natural distribution of the spp. and low to moderate degree of gene flow between populations. Gene flow could offset genetic drift and prevent differentiation when Nm > 155. In our case, the Nm values were < 1 for each pair of spp. may be a major reason for genetic differentiation, in addition to geographic isolation or possible bottleneck events.

Genetic relatedness

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA)

Studying population structure across multiple spp. helps understand spp. interactions. Discerning the evolutionary processes and conservation strategies by revealing patterns of genetic diversity and gene flow, are crucial for predicting future population trends and managing biodiversity56. UPGMA-based cluster analysis, STRUCTURE analysis and PCoA were used to thoroughly investigate the genetic structure of the population. The genetic identity between population pairs in our study was < 0.5 (mean = 0.23) (Table S9a) indicating that these are distantly related and have diverged further in evolutionary history. However, among all the studied CWRs of Linum, the cultivated spp. L. us showed maximum genetic identity and minimum genetic distance with L. bn (Table S9b), supporting the fact that this spp. is the wild progenitor of modern cultivated Linum. The results of the UPGMA-based cluster analysis showed that the 17 spp. of Linum were clustered into seven groups (Fig. 3). A group (CI) comprising cultivated Linum and its wild progenitor discriminated from the rest of the other groups comprising the rest of the spp. However, PCoA showed scattered individuals across all quadrants (Fig. S1), indicating that principal coordinates could not fully capture the data pattern, reflecting complex and non-linear relationships among diverse Linum CWRs. PCoA, being a linear method, might not be able to capture them accurately57. Cluster analysis, on the other hand, directly seeks to group individuals based on their similarity or dissimilarity58. It can be more effective in revealing hidden structures in the data that are not captured by the first few principal coordinates and are mostly integrated together to understand the data patterns59. The SSR markers used in the current study discriminated the individuals belonging to a specific spp. quite efficiently either into a single cluster or sub-clusters and thus can be used to develop spp. specific marker system for complex phylogenetic studies (Fig. 3). However, a few spp. with single representative accession, were found clustering with other spp. All spp. exhibited specific clustering, except for L. lw, which appeared in distinct groupings across four different sub-clusters. Although L. lw acc. did not form unique groups, this may be due to its widespread use in restoration and landscaping in the western USA, where its adaptability and striking blue flowers allow it to thrive in diverse habitats60. Due to its extensive overlapping distributions, it can thrive in a diverse range of habitats, plant communities, and ecosystems, leading to varying degrees of local adaptation. This may have caused the spp. to cluster into distinct groups regardless of spatial distribution. Delving deeper into these clusters may provide valuable insights into how subtle spatial patterns influence spp. grouping in significant ways. Understanding these relationships is essential for appreciating and conserving biodiversity. Pang et. al.,61 have compellingly shown how utilizing quantitatively derived clusters of spatially associated spp. can revolutionize our understanding of ecological patterns. Their research reveals that these clusters offer a powerful, spatially explicit framework to better understand complex spatial patterns among spp. distributions and the disparate effects of global changes on biodiversity.

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA)

According to Cliff criterion (Franco and Hidalgo)62, the coordinates whose accumulated values account for ≥ 70% of the total variance, are considered acceptable. But in the current study, the variance captured is very low (Table S10). No specific patterns of grouping were discerned through PCoA (Fig. S1). The low variance explained by the principal coordinates analysis despite the distribution of germplasm lines in all four quadrangles, indicates that the first two axes are not sufficient to explain overall structure or spp. composition. Another speculated reason for capturing low variance might be due to a comparatively smaller number of spp. representatives in a few samples that may also limit the ability of PCoA to capture meaningful patterns and explain a larger proportion of the variance. Using multiple spp. in a PCoA analysis can lead to a lower proportion of genetic variance being captured by the first two principal coordinates because the overall genetic diversity is diluted, and the spp.specific patterns become less pronounced. The first two principal coordinates are typically used to visualize the main patterns of genetic variation. With multiple spp., the within-spp. patterns might be overshadowed by the between-spp. patterns, leading to the first two coordinates capturing less of the overall genetic variation. The inclusion of multiple spp. increases the dimensionality of the data, meaning there are more potential axes of variation to consider. This makes it harder for the first two coordinates to capture all the relevant information. Pocovi and Alberto63 reported disagreed grouping obtained through PCoA and cluster analysis along with relatively low captured genetic variance. Intraspecific variation gives insights into selective pressures on traits within populations but trait correlations across spp. often apply poorly within spp.64, indicating intraspecific variation is more than a subset of interspecific variation65. According to the findings of Mohammadi and Prasanna21, cluster analysis emerged as a superior method for uncovering pedigree relationships among genotypes compared to PCoA. This advantage is particularly evident when the first two or three principal components account for less than 25% of the total variation, highlighting the importance of using cluster analysis in genetic studies for more accurate insights.

Population structure analysis across Linum spp.

Hierarchical trees constructed had divided the 96 acc. into major seven clusters, each further sub-divided into sub-clusters (Fig. 3). In estimating the number of ancestral populations, we observed the primary ΔK peak at K = 2, consistent with the bias reported by Janes et al.,66. To ensure this, cross‑validation of STRUCTURE outputs with hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was done, which supported grouping at K = 5. The concordance between STRUCTURE and HCA provided confidence that K = 5 represents the most biologically meaningful subdivision of Linum accessions, despite the ΔK bias toward K = 2. The existence of sub-populations were in agreement with the results of the population structure analysis that suggested the existence of five distinct groups (Fig. 4a). Differences in population structure confirmed the genetic diversity due to spp. level genetic differentiation along with the presence of admixed individuals (Fig. 4b). Among all the populations 75 individuals (78.13%) belonged to a specific group and only a small fraction 21 individuals (21.88%) had admixed lineage (Table S11). It might indicate complex population history either attributed to multiple waves of gene flow or introgression to exploit new niches under selection pressure, often requiring novel phenotypes leading to specific adaptation during the course of shifts in evolutionary space. This further highlights the challenges involved in tracking the population history of admixed lineages. To avoid erroneous and biased inferences, comprehensive sampling and validation of potential source populations can be critical67. Classical phylogenetic trees are known to be unsuitable for the representation of histories of admixed populations68–70. The results of this study provide useful information to Linum breeders for the selection of lines that might be suitable for intraspecific adaptations. The admixed lineage often leads novel phenotypes to exploit new niches or develop conservation strategies due to the intentional or accidental invasion of genomes71. The emergence of nascent genomes holds incredible potential for evolutionary success, as they equip organisms with the remarkable ability to adapt to shifting environmental conditions. However, this potential is threatened by admixture, especially in spp. teetering on the brink of extinction71. Yet, there is a silver lining: admixed individuals, with their diverse ancestral backgrounds, offer an invaluable opportunity to unravel the complex genetic “mosaic” that reflects various ancestral segments. This insight can greatly enhance our understanding of past population interactions and demographic histories. In advanced studies, we can model these admixed individuals to illustrate the proportion of their genomes derived from multiple source populations, ultimately crafting concise visual representations that reveal the intricate population structures within a sample72. In fact, the models based on PCA analysis and the admixture proportion inference approaches are two of the most used approaches to describe population structure in a sample23,73.

Evaluation of cross-species transferability (CST) and evolutionary insights in Linum spp.

Among the sections, the average CST was 89.02% in the section Linum, while it was notably higher in the section Dasylinum at 96.97% (Fig. 5a). There are two likely reasons for the relatively lower CST in the Linum section compared to Dasylinum. First, there is only one representative spp. in the Dasylinum section. Secondly, the spp. L. al, which belongs to the Linum section, exhibited the lowest CST. This suggests that further taxonomic investigation may be necessary to reorient this spp. with a reasonably satisfactory number of representative samples. In section Linum: L. us and L. bn are cross-compatible and form fertile hybrids. For other spp. of the section Linum, we observed 100% CST between L. pn and L. lw (Fig. 5b). The spp. L. lw is a close relative of L. pn74,75 and is a distant relative of cultivated Linum. The high level of CST (> 96–100%) of SSR markers (Table S12 & Table S13) in Linum broadly reflects genetic relatedness, but with important exceptions that highlight the complexity of genome evolution. As expected, the species taxonomically related to L. us within section Linum (L. bn, L. pn, L. lw and, L. mr) exhibited nearly complete CST and placed themselves either in the same cluster or proximate clusters in phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3). The other spp. of the section Linum: L. mr clustered with L. bn group along with cultivated Linum in the current study and showed 96.97% CST (Table S13). L. mr is morphologically quite similar to the members of L. pn group but can be distinguished based on its linear stigmas and ciliate sepal margins. Members of the L. pn group have capitate stigmas and entire sepals with smooth margins. The high amplification success which is consistent with their phylogenetic proximity indicates strong conservation of SSR flanking regions. Such conservation is typical of recently diverged taxa, where primer binding sites remain stable across lineages. As noted by López-Román et al.,76, SSR flanking regions remained conserved across closely related Vicia species, supporting high CST and indicated their origin likely from coding regions of the genome. At this point, it should be noted that publication on Linum SSR markers (Bickel et al.,)26 does not specify the origin of these sequences from genic or non-genic region.The high SSR transferability is in conjunction with the studies of McDill77 for the suitability of the molecular markers in implications of establishing the phylogeny of Linum and their systematics. Although Linum (L. us and L. bn) (Chromosome number, 2n = 30) and L. gr (2n = 16) are from the same section differ in ploidy, they are substantially similar as observed by C/DAPI banding patterns of chromosomes and the arrangement of ribosomal genes78 which make a basis to place them in same taxonomic section. Similarly, the marker-specific information has earlier identified several potentially informative insertions or deletions in the members of the L. pn group of section Linum (L. au, L. lw, L. pa and L. pn) that share a common 4 bp insertion in ITS 1 region77. However, L. gr and other taxonomically close spp. namely L. au, L. pa, and L. al clustered distantly phylogenetically in this study. Interestingly, these phylogenetically distant members of section Linum (L. gr, L. au and L. pa) showed high CST (> 80–97%). This indicates a probability that SSR loci targeted in this study are not uniformly evolving but instead retain conserved flanking sequences across divergent lineages. The persistence of these conserved regions may reflect functional constraints on genic SSRs, where mutations in flanking regions could disrupt gene function, or selective retention of ancestral motifs that remain stable across evolutionary time. Thus, even distantly related species can exhibit high CST when SSR loci are embedded or located near conserved flanking regions79 making them less tolerant to mutations80.

Although the SSR markers used in this study were originally developed for L. us, amplification success in cultivated varieties (T397 and TL99) was lower than in several wild taxa. This apparent paradox can be explained by the narrow genetic base of cultivated flax, where domestication bottlenecks and selective breeding must have reduced allelic diversity and introduced variation in primer binding sites, leading to null alleles or allelic dropout. Domestication exerts two major impacts on plant diversity. Firstly, it manifests as alterations in traits selected for human use, collectively referred to as the ‘domestication syndrome’81, which are reflected in distinct selection signatures at specific genomic loci82,83. In contrast, many wild relatives retain ancestral flanking regions that are more conserved, allowing successful primer binding and amplification. Thus, the lower amplification in cultivated flax may be interpreted as lineage‑specific divergence and domestication‑driven loss of diversity, while the higher success in wild relatives highlights hidden conservation and evolutionary stability of certain SSR loci. Additionally, lower amplification may be attributed to genotypic variation within L. us. The markers were originally developed using Canadian cultivars (Bison and Stormont Cirrus)26, whereas the present study employed indigenous Indian cultivars (T-397 and TL99). Previous reports have shown that cultivars differ not only in allelic composition but also in genome size by several megabases, as observed between CDC Bethune84 and T-39785. This highlights that the utility of these 49 markers extends to wild species, while their polymorphism in cultivated flax remains limited, consistent with the findings of Bickel et al., (2011)26. These findings underscore the importance of wild germplasm as a reservoir of genetic diversity and provide an evolutionary perspective on SSR transferability patterns. In fact, using the similar set of markers in 12 flax varieties the SSRs were found less polymorphic than the CWRs26.

Only 45.45% CST in L.al of section Linum indicates partial conservation of these loci in L.al Although L. us and L. al are classified within the same section, our SSR analysis revealed limited cross-transferability and placed the species in the distant cluster. This highlights that taxonomic proximity does not necessarily predict microsatellite conservation and phylogenetic proximity, as SSR loci are subject to lineage-specific mutations and structural variation. Reduced CST of SSRs often results from lineage-specific divergence in SSR flanking regions86. The lowest to highest CST values as recorded in the same section Linum, demonstrated that amplification rates differed greatly in the assessed spp. The SSR transferability depends on the genetic distance between individual specimens87. However, since specific genomic resources are not available for the different spp. of Linum, the markers showing amplification across spp. may serve as great genomic tools to generate further information on genomic organization. Such attempts to investigate CST have been done in various other crops such as genus Vicia12,88, Cowpea89 and Piper spp.90, etc.

The spp. belonging to the section Dasylinum- L. hm showed 96.97% cross-spp. amplification that supports the number of transferable genomic SSRs to this spp. and reflects similar functional sequences shared with cultivated Linum that may induce chromosome pairing during artificial hybridization due to orthology. Orthologous genes have originated by speciation; they tend to retain the function of the original gene in the spp. of origin and as a result, they tend to be conserved91,92. Further, all the acc. of L. hm were grouped along with L. pn (section Linum) in CII-c of CII, which again shows some level of shared ancestry between these spp.

Within section Linastrum, two potentially informative indels (4 bp and 16 bp) in the ITS region are shared among L. tn, L. st, L. ma and L. su, forming what has been described as the yellow-flower clade. Interestingly, the clustering patterns of these species in the present study did not fully align with their defined sectional classification, despite exhibiting moderate to high SSR transferability. This discrepancy suggests that while transferable SSR markers capture conserved regions across taxa, they may not encompass the full spectrum of genomic divergence shaped by accumulated mutations, structural variation, and reproductive isolation. Consequently, each species maintains a unique genetic landscape that is not entirely resolved by SSR-based analyses. Nevertheless, the high CST observed within Linastrum underscores the practical utility of these markers, as they reveal conserved sequences shared with L. us and provide a valuable resource for comparative genomics, diversity assessment, and marker development across distantly related taxa.

The success of interspecific SSR marker amplification relies on the evolutionary relationships between spp. and the location of SSRs in the genome. Orthologous regions are more likely to amplify across spp., so analyzing gene sequence similarity would shed more light on orthology of these genomic regions harboring SSR loci93,94. Given that orthologs originated by speciation, further integration of data from chromosomal mapping92,94 is highly recommended. Orthologous genes are the genes in different spp. that evolved from a common ancestor through speciation.

In short, the CST patterns observed across Linum species revealed evolutionary signals of both conservation and divergence. Close relatives of L. us exhibited nearly complete CST, reflecting strong conservation of SSR flanking regions among recently diverged taxa. Interestingly, phylogenetically distant species such as L. gr and L. au also showed high CST, suggesting selective retention of ancestral motifs or functional constraints on genic SSRs. In contrast, L. al demonstrated low CST despite sectional proximity, highlighting lineage-specific divergence in SSR loci. Species from other sections generally showed moderate CST, nearly consistent with greater evolutionary distance, though exceptions such as L. hm indicated deep conservation across sections. Together, these patterns underscore that SSR transferability is governed by locus-specific conservation rather than taxonomy alone, revealing both hidden conservation across divergent lineages and unique divergence within proximate groups.

The high conservation rate of SSR markers in this study may help identify important regulatory regions despite evolutionary divergence and geographic isolation. Advances in sequencing technologies have also expedited the generation of SSR markers95,96. This approach is time, energy, and resource efficient when developing SSR markers in related spp. Cross-spp. amplification has been utilized in several crops to facilitate genetic and evolutionary investigations11. Earlier, attempts have been made to study the transferability of molecular markers to distinguish between fiber vs. oilseed flax97 and to study the intraspecific relationship among Linum spp. based on marker transferrability4,26,30. However, more such studies need to be performed involving diverse spp. representatives to accomplish end-use specific breeding programme and, to meet diversified applications of Linum CWRs. The polymorphic SSR markers found in this study are suitable for direct application in other spp. for diversity analysis and genetic and evolutionary relationship studies, particularly in cases where these markers are unavailable.

Summary and conclusions

In present time, CWRs are being looked upon as important donor genetic resources for enhancing the narrow genetic base of cultivated crop plants. Wild genetic resources or CWRs are the reservoir of unique and beneficial genes in respective gene pools. Current study identified SSR markers that revealed the allelic richness in a set of Linum CWRs. When selecting genetic markers for research purposes, certain characteristics are preferred to ensure informative results such as markers with a greater number of alleles tend to be more informative and possess greater discriminatory power. The CWRs including its wild progenitor (L. bn) exhibiting high allelic abundance indicated the existence of significant genetic variation for further studies such as conservation efforts and understanding population bottlenecks. The SSR loci identified with high polymorphic information content for L. bn, L. lw and other CWRs, demonstrated strong discriminatory power among genotypes of each spp. and their utility for diversity analysis. The gene flow (Nm) observed in this study supports the evidence of their speciation over the course of evolution. The SSR markers in this study effectively identified individuals of a specific spp., enabling the development of a spp.-specific marker system for complex phylogenetic studies. Cluster analysis emerged as a superior method for uncovering pedigree relationships among genotypes compared to PCoA.

Differences in population structure confirmed genetic diversity due to spp.-level differentiation and the presence of admixed individuals. This suggests a complex population history, possibly involving multiple gene flow events or introgression. Such factors allow populations to adapt to new niches under selection pressure, highlighting the difficulties in tracing the history of admixed lineages. The high SSR transferability among and within taxonomic sections implies the suitability of studied molecular markers in implications of establishing the phylogeny of Linum and their systematics. While the underrepresentation of certain species posed a limitation in fully capturing the breadth of genetic diversity, this gap highlights valuable opportunities for future sampling and serves to emphasize the importance of continued germplasm collection and inclusion. CST patterns in Linum reveal both conservation and divergence: close relatives showed strong flanking region stability, some distant taxa pointed to retain ancestral motifs, while L. al illustrated lineage-specific divergence. Moderate CST in Linastrum reflected gradual divergence with shared motifs, underscoring complex evolutionary trajectories across the genus. These insights open opportunities for future work using genome-wide markers and their validation to unravel hidden conservation, clarify lineage-specific divergence, and expand marker utility for breeding, diversity assessment, and evolutionary inference. Addressing this in future work will further validate and extend the applicability of our findings across the genus. In conclusion, this study strongly advocates for the inclusion of diverse CWRs to complement targeted breeding programs meeting specific needs and harnessing the full potential of Linum CWRs. The polymorphic SSR markers identified in this research are not only relevant but also highly transferable to other spp., thus greatly facilitating diversity analysis, advancing genetic and evolutionary studies. Their availability is particularly crucial in Linum spp. where such markers are lacking. Embracing these findings can significantly enhance the scope and effectiveness of future breeding initiatives involving CWR.
