Significant pain reduction in refractory post-mastectomy pain syndrome with closed-loop spinal cord stimulation: illustrative cases
Authors: Brooke Elberson, Lauren Banko, Jonathan Bauer, Erika Petersen, Gregory Lawson Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDBreast cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and is often treated with surgery. Post-mastectomy pain syndrome (PMPS) can have disabling consequences. The incidence of PMPS ranges from 20% to 68%. Undertreated PMPS results in 10% of opioid-naive patients continuing to use opioids 90 day after surgery. Management has been centered around medications, psychological support, and peripheral nerve blocks. Case reports and series have been published describing dorsal root ganglion stimulation and spinal cord stimulation (SCS), but overall literature is lacking for this complex problem and no studies used closed-loop SCS.OBSERVATIONSTwo patients experienced ≥ 80% relief of pain with SCS for refractory post-mastectomy pain. In both cases, percutaneous cylindrical leads were positioned over the cervicothoracic junction and utilized an evoked compound action potential–controlled closed-loop pulse generator (Evoke).LESSONSClosed-loop SCS is an effective option for refractory PMPS and should be considered when conservative options fail. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25218

The exact etiology of persistent post-mastectomy pain has not been established. It has a high incidence of 20%–68% and can be disabling, with undertreatment common and 10% of previously opioid-naive patients remaining on opioids at least 90 days after surgery.1–3 Several independent risk factors for post-mastectomy pain syndrome (PMPS) have been identified, including young age, psychological history, and chronic pain history.4 PMPS often affects the arm, shoulder, or chest wall underlying the breast. PMPS is described as a burning or shooting pain, which supports it having a neuropathic origin. Neuropathic pain is known to be particularly difficult to treat. Younger age has been shown to be a predictive factor for a higher incidence of PMPS.5 Extended surgery, axillary lymph node dissection, and reconstructive procedures have been hypothesized as contributors to chronic pain. Injury to the intercostobrachial nerve is another possible cause of PMPS; however, protection of this nerve did not decrease rates.6 Depression and anxiety are also associated with PMPS. Current pain regimens seem to be insufficient in many cases and include medications, intercostal nerve blocks and radiofrequency ablations, and epidural therapy. Should these treatments fail, neuromodulation should be considered as a next step.7–9 A recent retrospective review of tonic spinal cord stimulation (SCS) described a 50%–60% reduction in pain for patients with chronic neuropathic pain.10 The EVOKE randomized clinical trial supported the superiority of evoked compound action potential (ECAP)–controlled closed-loop SCS (CL-SCS) over open loop, allowing titration of a neural dosing regimen toward accomplishing maximal analgesic effect.11 Levy et al. found that a dose regimen of 2.8 µV and dose ratio of 1.4 was effective.12 The Evoke SCS (Saluda Medical) uses burst stimulation parameters, which are associated with less paresthesia.13,14 In this report, we present the cases of 2 patients with refractory post-mastectomy pain who significantly benefited from CL-SCS, which has not been discussed in the literature prior to this series. We implanted the stimulation devices in both patients in the cervicothoracic region, as this is known to cover arm and chest pain.15,16 There is scarce literature on SCS for PMPS; a retrospective study using open-loop SCS in 61 patients described a decrease in visual analog scale scores by ≥ 4 points,15 supporting SCS as an effective modality for this type of pain syndrome, as well as suggesting room for improvement.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 72-year-old female patient underwent a right mastectomy with reconstruction for ductal carcinoma in situ. She first presented to our chronic pain clinic 4 years later. At that time, she reported 4 years of pain in her right chest wall. She described the pain as sharp, stabbing, and radiating. It was exacerbated by movement or touching, was worse during the day, and was present most of the time. She reported the intensity as a numeric rating scale (NRS) score of 8. During this time, she partially controlled the pain with gabapentin and L-carnitine. She underwent a right serratus plane block followed by right T4–7 intercostal nerve blocks. She reported 0% relief for both interventions. Two years later, she underwent trial SCS implantation with leads over C4–7 and T2–T5 (12-contact arrays), reporting 80% relief at the end of this 1-week trial using CL-SCS. We then placed percutaneous epidural spinal cord stimulator electrodes (12-contact array) at T1–4 on the right and C6–T2 at midline (Fig. 1A). The active lead contacts were 17+ 18− 20+ 21+, which corresponds to the lower right lead. The Saluda Evoke implantable pulse generator (IPG) was implanted in the same operation with ECAPs captured and closed-loop programming completed (Fig. 1B). The patient’s SCS was programmed for a neural dose regimen with a dose ratio of 1.3 as shown in the activation plot. On postoperative day 12, she reported a decrease in pain, with an NRS score of 5. At her 3-month follow-up, she reported continued significant improvement in her pain. Overall, her pain flares were much less frequent, and she felt that the SCS increased her quality of life. At 2 years postimplantation, she continued to report an average of 80% pain relief overall. FIG. 1.Case 1. A: Cervicothoracic radiograph with lead positions. Case 2

A 51-year-old female patient underwent a right-sided lumpectomy with lymph node dissection for breast cancer with adjuvant radiotherapy. She experienced immediate numbness of the entirety of the right arm as well as paresthesia and pain. After 2 years she, sought relief for this persistent pain and sensory disturbance. At the initial neurosurgical evaluation, she continued to experience numbness in the posterior upper arm, occasionally into the lateral forearm, with paroxysms of pain through the arm, constant dull pain in the arm and breast, and painful paresthesia over the right nipple with erythema over the right breast. Her pain was never less than an NRS score of 4, which frequently reached a score of 7. Her pain was disabling, affecting her work on a computer and her leisure time, and was only partially controlled with marijuana and pregabalin before sleep. She was unable to wear certain clothes, carry anything with her right arm, or be touched in the right arm due to severe provocation of pain. Her rehabilitation for lymphedema was limited as well. She was evaluated for interventional pain procedures prior to evaluation by the neurosurgerical team but was told there were no reasonable procedures to attempt for her symptoms and that she should consider neuromodulation. Three years after symptom onset, she underwent placement of a percutaneous trial SCS using CL-SCS (two 12-contact arrays) spanning C2–T1 on the left of midline and C4–T4 on the right. These leads were removed after 1 week, at which time she reported 95% pain relief. This was followed by permanent implantation of two percutaneous 12-contact arrays, both spanning C5–T3 (Fig. 2A) and a Saluda Evoke IPG. The active lead contacts were 22+ 23− 24+, which corresponds to the lower right lead. The patient’s SCS was programmed for a neural dose regimen with a dose ratio of 1.12 as shown in the activation plot (Fig. 2B). At her most recent follow-up 6 months postimplantation, she continued to report > 90% pain relief. FIG. 2.Case 2. A: Cervicothoracic radiograph with lead positions. Discussion

Observations

Neuromodulation is fast becoming standard of care for refractory neuropathic pain syndromes and persistent spinal pain syndromes, among other pathologies. Within SCS, both open loop where the pulse generator delivers constant therapy from range of selectable programs or closed loop where the generator makes real-time adjustments in delivered therapy are utilized. Patient positioning, movement, breathing, and heart rate may change the efficacy of open-loop SCS to either supra- or subtherapeutic levels as the contact of the electrodes with the dura and the position of the spinal cord changes. These fluctuations in therapy affect patient adherence negatively, which has a cumulative effect over time. As we know that SCS has both long- and short-term neuromodulatory effects, erratic usage worsens long-term outcomes.12 CL-SCS can adjust for these continuous changes in position using ECAPs to deliver more consistent therapy, termed “dose optimization.”12

Dose ratio is the current at the median ECAP amplitude divided by the ECAP threshold, which is the minimum neural recruitment level of dorsal column A-beta fibers at which paresthesia can be felt.12,17 The activation curve is generated by plotting the voltage versus the delivered current at specific points: the ECAP threshold, paresthesia threshold, minimum comfortable paresthesia level, maximum comfortable paresthesia level, and maximum tolerable level Figs. 1 and 2). This measurement creates a normalized value for comparison across patients. Investigation into optimal dose ratio values has shown that a range from 1.2 to 1.6 (which corresponds to 20%–60% above the ECAP threshold) provides optimal pain relief in most patients.12,17 The patient in case 2 had significant pain relief at a dose ratio of 1.31. However, some, such as the patient in case 1, experienced paresthesia at a lower dose ratio and found a value of 1.12 preferential for pain relief without detectable sensation change. CL-SCS allows this type of patient-specific adjustment in stimulation without significant concern for fluctuation in delivered dose due to change in position of the spinal cord or in responsiveness of the dorsal column fibers, which leads to better outcomes.11,12,14,17,18 Finally, the activation plots were quite different between our 2 patients (Figs. 1 and 2), which further supports closed-loop versus open-loop SCS.18 Previously published data supporedt greater long-term efficacy and consistency with CL-SCS with ECAP-directed dose-optimization compared with open-loop technology.11,12

To date, there are limited studies on the treatment of PMPS with SCS,8,9 and further research is needed. We present the cases of 2 patients with ≥ 80% relief of pain, one durable over 2 years and the other reaching > 90% at the 6-month follow-up, which matches recently published large-cohort data for other pain conditions.12 Interestingly, the patient in case 1 had no pain relief from common interventional pain procedures but experienced significant relief with SCS, suggesting a more complicated pathology than simple peripheral nerve etiology. Both patients had rapid and durable relief from pain using CL-SCS, reaching their steady-state NRS scores relatively quickly after implantation. CL-SCS allowed for 688 million automatic adjustments in stimulation over 6 months for one of the patients and 2.8 billion over 36 months for the other, with less than one manual adjustment by the patients per day. These cases support further investigation regarding indications for CL-SCS for refractory pain syndromes in novel regions of the body. Finally, as discussed above, there is growing evidence that CL-SCS not only produces better efficacy when active but results in better adherence to therapy.

We reached better outcomes (> 80% durable pain relief for both patients compared with an average of 50% reported by Li et al.15) without a multiple-month lag period in improvement, and our outcomes are similar to larger closed-loop studies.18,19 Significant data exist supporting CL-SCS over open-loop SCS, specifically for adherence to therapy, time spent with device on, reliability of pain relief without troubling fluctuations with over- or understimulation, and overall pain relief outcomes.11,12,17,18 Finally, our patients had pain over multiple body areas, rendering methods such as dorsal root ganglion stimulation7 less preferable options.

Lessons

This report described 2 patients with post-mastectomy pain who benefited from dorsal column stimulator placement utilizing a closed-loop system measuring ECAPs. To our knowledge, this is the first case report of treating post-mastectomy pain with CL-SCS. CL-SCS appears to be able to provide superior therapeutic results compared with conventional techniques.
