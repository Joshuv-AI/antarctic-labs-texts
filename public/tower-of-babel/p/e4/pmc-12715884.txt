The utility of C‐reactive protein in neonatal early‐onset sepsis screening: A territory‐wide cohort analysis
Authors: Xuelian Wang, Hoi Ying Sharon Lau, Hiu Ying Peggy Chan, Po Gee Genevieve Fung, Hugh Simon Lam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImportanceC‐reactive protein (CRP) is a commonly used biomarker for sepsis.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate CRP's diagnostic performance to detect neonatal early‐onset sepsis (EOS), while accounting for both gestational and postnatal age.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective study of neonates born from January 2006 to December 2017 in public hospitals in Hong Kong. Neonates who had CRP tested within 72 h of birth, prior to antibiotics initiation, were included. CRP values were analyzed by gestational age and time after birth. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and likelihood ratios (LRs).ResultsAmong 100 327 tested neonates, 448 developed culture‐confirmed EOS, and 34 had meningitis. In uninfected neonates, CRP concentrations rose naturally after 8 h of life, peaking at 24–32 h, with higher values in term neonates at 24–48 h (median 6.2 mg/L, interquartile range 2.9–14.3). Diagnostic performance was better in preterm neonates (e.g., the area under the ROC curve after 4 h of life: 0.88 in <34 weeks, 0.83–0.92 in 34–36 weeks, and 0.73–0.77 in term neonates). Sensitivity within the first 4 h was low across all groups. LRs increased with higher CRP values in preterm neonates. A CRP value of >12.0 mg/L was associated with increased risk of meningitis.InterpretationCRP is not recommended for EOS screening in term neonates, but it may be considered for assessing the risk of EOS in preterm neonates. Interpretation must account for gestational and postnatal age.

INTRODUCTION

Neonatal early‐onset sepsis (EOS), which refers to sepsis in the first 3 days of life, is a potentially life‐threatening disease. Despite the low incidence (0.02%–0.4%), clinical suspicion of EOS remains one of the most common indications for empirical antibiotics during the first few days of life, with a reported prescription rate of up to 15%–20%. 1 , 2 , 3 This is mainly due to the subtle and nonspecific clinical features, the devastating consequences, the long turnaround time of blood culture, and a lack of ideal diagnostic biomarkers optimized for EOS.

C‐reactive protein (CRP) is one of the most commonly used biomarkers for EOS, which has often been incorporated into algorithms for sepsis screening or criteria for sepsis diagnosis. 4 , 5 , 6 Serial CRP measurements have a high negative predictive value for neonatal sepsis. 7 However, cut‐off values of CRP in the first few days have not been optimized in large‐scale studies. 5 During the first 1 or 2 days, the concentration of CRP physiologically rises among healthy neonates and gradually decreases back to baseline within days. The kinetic patterns differ between term and preterm neonates regarding the peak values and the time taken to reach the peak. 8 , 9 In addition, a variety of perinatal non‐infectious conditions, e.g., vaginal delivery, maternal pregnancy‐induced hypertension, prolonged labor, or maternal antenatal use of steroids, could lead to elevated CRP concentrations. 9 , 10 , 11 Therefore, it may be more appropriate to set dynamic cut‐off values for CRP in different periods after birth and stratified by gestational age. 12 Previous studies on the diagnostic performance of CRP for EOS often involved small sample sizes, especially the number of confirmed sepsis cases, which have shown large variations in the sensitivity (22%–100%) and specificity (0%–100%). 13 , 14 It is therefore important to use population‐based data to clarify how the dynamic behavior of CRP impacts its diagnostic performance for neonatal EOS, in order to better interpret CRP test results, reduce unnecessary antibiotic use, and improve EOS risk stratification.

This study aimed to use population‐based data to investigate the diagnostic performance of CRP for EOS in neonates at risk of sepsis and to suggest the cut‐off values optimized by gestational and postnatal age.

METHODS

Ethical approval

This was a multicenter cohort study. Data of eligible subjects from January 1, 2006, to December 31, 2017, were extracted from the electronic health records network of all public hospitals in Hong Kong, managed by the Hospital Authority Data Collaboration Laboratory. The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki to protect the participants’ rights. Ethical approval was obtained from the Joint The Chinese University of Hong Kong‐New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (No. 2020.026), and a waiver of informed consent was granted in accordance with local ethical guidelines for secondary use of deidentified health data.

Participants

All live births in Hong Kong public hospitals during this period were considered for inclusion. The public hospitals serve 60%–70% of the population, with approximately 40 000 live births per year. Neonates were included if they had at least one CRP test performed within the first 72 h after birth and before the initiation of antibiotic therapy. Neonates were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: 1) uncertain gestational age; 2) no CRP test performed within the first 3 days of life; or 3) CRP measured only after starting antibiotic treatment. CRP testing was performed based on clinical judgment in the presence of risk factors or concerns for early‐onset infection, with no standardized time points across hospitals. Blood samples were collected via peripheral veins, heel pricks, or arterial/venous catheters according to standard procedures, and tested by chemiluminescent immunoassay. The CRP results were analyzed only if the blood samples were collected before the initiation of antibiotic treatment. If a neonate underwent repeated CRP tests prior to antibiotic initiation, all such results were included. The following information was extracted: sex, year of birth, gestational age, mode of delivery, birth weight, Apgar scores at 1st and 5th minutes, diagnoses, CRP, bacterial culture results, medications, clinical notes, and maternal information (i.e., age, diagnoses, use of antenatal steroids and antibiotics, and bacterial culture results).

EOS was confirmed if pathogenic bacteria or fungi were isolated from a normally sterile site (blood or cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]) sampled during the first 72 h of life. Early‐onset meningitis (EOM) was confirmed if pathogens were isolated from the CSF of the EOS cases. The following conditions were treated as contamination: antibiotics prescribed for <5 days in live neonates; single sample yielding coagulase‐negative Staphylococci, Micrococcus, Bacillus, Corynebacterium, or Diphtheroids. Antenatal steroids, referred to dexamethasone or betamethasone administered to the mothers during pregnancy for fetal lung maturation. Intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) was defined as intravenous antibiotic treatment provided to the mother within a 48‐h period prior to birth, intended to treat the fetus. Prolonged rupture of membranes (PROM) refers to rupture of membranes ≥18 h.

Statistical analysis

Power calculation was not performed as the study was based on all eligible neonates with CRP testing over the study period. Statistical analysis was performed using R 3.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data were summarized by mean (standard deviation), median (interquartile range [IQR]), or number (percentage), as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the t‐test if normally distributed or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test (Mann–Whitney U test) for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. P‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Gestational age was divided into three groups: <34 weeks, 34–36 weeks, and ≥37 weeks, based on established differences in EOS risk stratification and the characteristics of CRP dynamics.9 Postnatal age at the time of CRP sampling was categorized into the following time intervals for analysis: <1h, 1–<4h, 4–<8h, 8–<12h, 12–<24h, 24–<48h, and 48–<72h after birth. The diagnostic performance of CRP was evaluated by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). The optimal cut‐off values from the ROC curves were determined using the Youden Index. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and likelihood ratios (LRs) were determined and compared using different cut‐off values. A multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to assess factors associated with CRP concentrations in EOS cases. The following factors were considered for analysis: antenatal steroid exposure, IAP, maternal age and comorbidities, gestational age, birth weight, time of CRP sampling, neonatal death, causative organisms, sex, Apgar scores at 1st and 5th minutes, PROM, clinical chorioamnionitis, time of antibiotic initiation, and diagnosis of fetal distress. Factors were included in multivariate analysis if the difference between groups was at a probability of P < 0.1 in univariate analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF), with values >4 suggesting further investigation and values >10 indicating the need for correction of multicollinearity.

A total of 123 335 CRP testing results were eligible. A total of 2089 samples were obtained soon after birth, and 69 997 within 12 h after birth. Of these, 558 tests were performed in neonates who developed EOS. The number of CRP tests for the neonates in subgroups of < 34 weeks of gestation, 34–36 weeks of gestation, and ≥ 37 weeks of gestation are presented in Table S1. Totally 20 362 neonates (20.3%) had repeated CRP tests performed across different postnatal time intervals, and 2646 neonates (2.6%) had repeated CRP tests within the same time interval. All eligible tests were analyzed individually. The median CRP levels are presented in Table S4. In uninfected neonates, the CRP levels increased after 8 h of life, reaching peak values during 24–<48h of life. Compared to preterm babies, term babies exhibited a more pronounced rise in CRP levels during the first 8–<48 h of life, with a median value of 6.2 (IQR 2.9–14.3) mg/L at 24–<48 h after birth. In cases with EOS, the median CRP levels were higher in groups with lower gestational ages.

The individual CRP values are plotted in Figure 2. In neonates without confirmed EOS, CRP levels exhibited two peaks: one at birth and another around 24–32 h after birth. Among neonates born at <34 weeks’ gestation who developed EOS, many showed highly elevated CRP levels within 4 h after birth. In contrast, none of the term neonates with EOS had CRP levels exceeding 10.0 mg/L (range: 0–9.3) in the first 8 h. That is, elevated CRP values (≥10.0 mg/L) within the first 8 h after birth in term babies were attributable to conditions other than culture‐confirmed sepsis.

The AUROC values of CRP for predicting EOS, stratified by gestational and postnatal age, are presented in Table 2 and Figure 3A–C. Different time intervals were analyzed for neonates born at <34 weeks of gestation. In this group, CRP demonstrated high predictive performance (AUROC: 0.88) after 4 h of life. In contrast, among late preterm and term neonates, the diagnostic performance of CRP was poor within the first 4 h of life, improved slightly after 4 h of life, and improved further after 12 h of life. The LRs at different cut‐off points with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are presented in Table S5. For neonates born at <34 weeks of gestation, a cut‐off value of 3.4 mg/L was recommended at birth, 4 mg/L at 1–<4 h, and 8 mg/L after 4 h, with corresponding LRs of 5.1, 11.9, and 7.5, respectively. Although sensitivity remained low during the first 4 h of life, rising CRP values were associated with a marked increase in the probability of EOS, as reflected by increasing LRs. Among late preterm neonates, CRP sensitivity within the first 12 h of life was very low when high specificity was desired; however, LRs increased significantly when CRP exceeded 4.0 mg/L (LR = 8.0 within 4 h; LR = 13.3 at 4–12 h). A cut‐off value of 10.0 mg/L was recommended beyond 12 h after birth. In terms, neonates, sensitivity, specificity, and LRs remained low across all time intervals and cut‐off values.

A total of 34 neonates with EOM underwent CRP testing prior to antibiotic treatment (48 episodes); 26 of these neonates were born term. Most CRP tests were conducted between 12 and 72 h after birth (34 neonates and 40 episodes). Compared to EOS cases without concurrent meningitis, those with EOM had significantly higher median CRP levels (36.0 mg/L [IQR: 7.9–89.3] vs. 3.4 mg/L [IQR: 1.3–15.0]; P < 0.001). The ROC curve for EOM is presented in Figure 3D. The AUROC was 0.862 (95% CI 0.797–0.926). At a cut‐off value of 12.0 mg/L, sensitivity was 76.5%, specificity 91.0%, PPV 0.3%, NPV 100.0%, and LR was 8.5. Using a cut‐off of 10.0 mg/L yielded the same sensitivity but lower specificity (89.3%), while a cut‐off of 15.0 mg/L resulted in reduced sensitivity (70.6%) and slightly higher specificity (93.0%).

Among neonates with culture‐confirmed EOS, multivariable linear regression analysis identified several factors independently associated with CRP concentrations. Factors associated with higher CRP levels included later timing of CRP sampling (β = 0.78, 95% CI 0.67–0.87, P < 0.01), antenatal steroid exposure (β = 9.01, 95% CI 1.08–16.94, P = 0.03), maternal age >40 years (β = 11.86, 95% CI 1.25–22.47, P = 0.03), concurrent meningitis (β = 34.48, 95% CI 25.88–43.07, P < 0.01), and maternal emergency diseases (β = 15.61, 95% CI 2.51–28.71, P = 0.02). Factors associated with reduced CRP levels included neonatal death (β = −11.45, 95% CI −20.09 to −2.81, P < 0.01) and infection with non‐GBS organisms (e.g., for E. coli: β = −7.74, 95% CI −13.29 to −2.20, P < 0.01) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CRP for EOS screening based on a large population‐based dataset. Our findings highlighted the fluctuations of CRP levels in a ‘real‐world’ setting, influenced by various non‐infectious conditions. The results suggest that although CRP has limited diagnostic utility, elevated levels may still indicate a higher risk of EOS in preterm neonates and are associated with increased risk of meningitis.

The dynamic changes in CRP concentrations have been explored in many studies. 15 , 16 Our findings further demonstrate that the CRP levels increase over the first 2 days of life in non‐infected neonates, especially in term neonates. In addition, from a population perspective, elevated CRP values were frequently observed immediately after birth. This phenomenon is supported by a follow‐up study by Ainbender et al., which reported that neonates with severe conditions—such as shock, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, and Rh incompatibility—may exhibit peak CRP values on the first day of life. 15 In our study, the upper limit of the IQR for CRP concentration at 24–<48 h in term neonates was 14.3 mg/L. Some reached peak values as high as 50.0 mg/L. Given the low sensitivity, specificity, and LR of CRP for detecting EOS in term neonates, we do not recommend its use for EOS screening in this population.

Compared to term neonates, we found that CRP exhibited less pronounced physiological dynamics in non‐infected preterm neonates but responded more rapidly in those with EOS. 11 , 17 CRP is regarded as a late marker of sepsis, rising 12 to 24 h after antigen exposure. However, in neonates born at <34 weeks’ gestation, the sensitivity of CRP increased significantly after 4 h of birth. This earlier CRP elevation in preterm neonates with EOS may reflect an inflammatory process that was more likely to have begun in utero and to share an association with preterm birth. 18 These may explain why CRP demonstrated better diagnostic performance for EOS in preterm neonates compared to term neonates within the first 72 h of life. A recent study by Naramura et al. 19 involving a small cohort of preterm neonates reported similar diagnostic performance of high‐sensitivity CRP to that observed in our population born at <34 weeks. Another study by Yoon et al. 20 indicated that using an umbilical cord CRP cut‐off of 0.2 mg/L yielded a sensitivity of 82%, specificity of 74%, PPV of 11%, and NPV of 99% for proven EOS in preterm neonates. In contrast, we did not observe comparable diagnostic performance immediately after birth in our preterm cohort. This discrepancy may be attributable to our larger sample size and a substantially lower incidence of EOS (0.5%) in preterm neonates in our study population compared to theirs (4%). Nevertheless, a high CRP concentration at birth significantly increases the probability of EOS in preterm neonates. Therefore, our results support the practice of CRP testing after birth in this group of neonates.

Based on our results, we recommend a CRP cutoff of 8.0 mg/L after 4 h of birth for neonates <34 weeks’ gestation, and 10.0 mg/L after 12 h for late preterm neonates. Although moderate LRs were observed at lower CRP thresholds (e.g., 4–<8 mg/L) in preterm neonates during early life, the overall diagnostic performance remained only moderate; therefore, CRP results should be interpreted in conjunction with other clinical parameters when assessing for the risk of EOS. A recent study involving late preterm and term neonates proposed a CRP cut‐off of 16 mg/L within 36 h after initiating antibiotic therapy to rule out EOS. 7 In contrast, our results showed that a cut‐off of 10.0 mg/L after 12 h in late preterm neonates yielded a high AUROC and values between 10.0 and 15.0 mg/L were associated with high positive LRs (12.3–19.7) for EOS; therefore, we believe that a lower cut‐off value in this subgroup may be safer.

The indication to perform a lumbar puncture in cases of suspected EOS can be nuanced. Approximately 30% of neonates with EOM may have negative blood cultures. 21 The NICE guideline recommends a lumbar puncture when CRP levels exceed 10 mg/L. 22 Our findings suggested a higher cut‐off of 12.0 mg/L for predicting EOM, based on its diagnostic performance at this threshold. Adopting a 10.0 mg/L cut‐off in our cohort could have led to more neonates undergoing unnecessary procedures. Nevertheless, CRP could be incorporated as one component of the initial sepsis evaluation, alongside other risk factors and clinical features. An elevated CRP level—particularly above 12.0 mg/L—may still warrant a lumbar puncture to rule out EOM, especially when supported by additional clinical indicators. Further studies are needed to refine the role of CRP in the diagnostic workup of EOM.

Our study benefited from a large sample size, including a substantial number of culture‐confirmed EOS cases. CRP testing was used for screening EOS in the postnatal ward and as part of sepsis evaluation in most neonatal settings in Hong Kong. To avoid confounding by treatment effects, we only included CRP measurements taken before antibiotic initiation. However, our study has several limitations. First, there was no standardized protocol for sepsis evaluation across hospitals during the study period. The observed incidence of EOS was higher in our cohort than in the general birth population, reflecting the selective inclusion of neonates who underwent CRP testing due to clinical concerns. This enrichment is appropriate given our study aim—to evaluate CRP performance in real‐world clinical contexts where testing is prompted by suspicion of sepsis. Additionally, although CRP is generally evenly distributed in circulation, minor pre‐analytical variations related to sampling site (capillary, venous, or arterial) or technique may exist. Nevertheless, such differences are typically small and unlikely to affect clinical interpretation. Second, detailed clinical information—such as specific signs of sepsis—was limited for individual cases. We were unable to identify culture‐negative EOS cases, which means some infected neonates may have been misclassified as non‐infected. However, since such cases likely represent a very small fraction of the total cohort, their impact on the overall results is probably very small. Due to the retrospective, big‐data nature of this study, it was not feasible to reliably account for culture‐negative EOS. Future prospective studies incorporating molecular diagnostics (e.g., polymerase chain reaction) may improve the detection of culture‐negative sepsis. Third, serial CRP measurements at different postnatal ages were not available for all neonates. However, the CRP trends observed in non‐infected neonates in our cohort were consistent with longitudinal patterns previously reported in the literature. 10 , 23 Finally, a small number of neonates had repeated CRP tests before starting antibiotics. Although repeated sampling within the same time interval was uncommon, it may have introduced minor bias. Despite these limitations, our population‐based analysis provides a robust evaluation of CRP's diagnostic performance at the time of clinical suspicion for EOS. We intentionally retained potential factors that influence CRP levels to better reflect real‐world clinical conditions, thereby improving the generalizability and practical applicability of our findings. We believe these results may help clinicians utilize CRP—a simple, cost‐effective, and widely available test—more effectively in the evaluation of neonatal sepsis for select groups of patients.

Although CRP is not an ideal biomarker for EOS screening, it can provide valuable information regarding EOS risk in preterm neonates and aid in the decision to further evaluate for EOM. Until more sensitive biomarkers with high diagnostic accuracy in the early stages of EOS become available, CRP may still serve as a useful tool for risk assessment in a select portion of the newborn population.
