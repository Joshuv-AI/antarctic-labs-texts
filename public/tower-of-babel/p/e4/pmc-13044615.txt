Crystal Growth of Cu2O Consisting of Sequential mm Size Grains by Thermal Oxidation of Cu
Authors: Konstantinos Koutsokostas, Matthew Zervos, Ioannis G. Paschos, Matin Ashurov, Pavlos G. Savvidis, Maria Karatzia, Sophia C. Hayes, Nikoletta Florini, Philomela Komninou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cu 2O has been grown via the thermal oxidation of 2 mm × 20 mm, 45 μm thick Cu under Ar: O2 at 1020 °C, followed by annealing at 1100 °C and controlled cooldown at −5 °C/min under Ar. We obtain Cu 2O consisting of millimeter-size sequential grains that have a cubic crystal structure and exhibit minimal bending and bowing, which allows the removal of the Kirkendall voids that form in the middle of the bulk due to the bifacial oxidation, by postgrowth polishing. We describe in detail this growth and processing strategy, which employs a considerably lower thermal budget compared to optical float zone growth, and show in contradistinction that the thermal oxidation of Cu wires using the same growth conditions leads to the formation of Cu 2O containing a significantly larger density of Kirkendall voids compared to the planar case that cannot be removed by annealing or polishing. We discuss the impact of these voids existing in feed and seed rods employed for the growth of Cu 2O by the optical float zone method and suggest that the growth strategy described here is an important alternative toward the realization of high crystal quality Cu 2O.

Introduction

Cuprous oxide (Cu 2O) is a p-type metal-oxide semiconductor that has a direct energy bandgap of 2.1 eV and a dark ruby-red color. It has a cubic crystal structure belonging to the (Pn 3− m) crystallographic space group with a lattice constant of 4.2696 Å and a melting point of 1232 °C. Similarly, cupric oxide (CuO) is also a p-type metal-oxide semiconductor but has an indirect energy bandgap of 1.2 eV with a monoclinic crystal structure and lattice constants of a = 4.68 Å, b = 3.42 Å, and c = 5.13 Å. It is black and has a slightly higher melting point of 1326 °C.

Cu 2O has been investigated extensively in the past and is an archetype for the study of excitons that were observed for the first time in this novel p-type metal oxide semiconductor by Gross et al. in 1956. It has also been suggested to be suitable as a solar cell absorber due to its high absorption coefficient of α ∼ 105 in the visible, but so far, device efficiencies have been limited to less than 10%. − In addition, Cu 2O has been shown to have very good photocatalytic properties for water splitting , but also for CO2 reduction. Consequently, Cu 2O is still an active topic of ongoing investigation that attracted even more attention after the observation of giant Rydberg excitons with principal quantum numbers up to n = 25 by Kazimierczuk et al. as well as exciton-polaritons in a SiO2/Ta2O5/Cu2O/Ta2O5/SiO2 Fabry-Pérot cavity by Orfanakis et al. More recently, Paul et al. used a top-down approach to show how exciton confinement can be realized by focused-ion-beam etching of bulk Cu 2O to form pillars with diameters of 1 to 5 μm without noticeable degradation of the excitonic properties. However, it is important to note here that these giant Rydberg excitons and polaritons have only been observed in naturally occurring crystals of Cu 2O, so further efforts into the growth of high crystal quality Cu 2O are required, which is important from a fundamental and also a technological point of view.

In the past, Cu 2O has been deposited on different substrates by plasma-assisted molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), pulsed layer deposition (PLD), electrodeposition (ELD), and aerosol-assisted chemical vapor deposition (AACVD) at low and intermediate temperatures. Only Steinhauer et al. observed Rydberg excitons in Cu 2O on a substrate. In this case, Cu 2O was obtained by thermal oxidation of 700 nm Cu deposited on Si. The largest Rydberg excitons observed in Cu 2O grown in a laboratory had a principal quantum number of n = 10, as shown by Lynch et al., who prepared Cu 2O feed and seed rods via the thermal oxidation of 5 mm diameter Cu rods at 1100 °C for 40 h in air that were subsequently used to grow single crystal Cu 2O by the optical float zone method. A flat wafer of Cu 2O was cut from this ingot that was polished but contained voids near the center, and copper vacancies were observed in the photoluminescence (PL). Before we elaborate further, it is important to mention that no one has previously considered the properties of the feed and seed rods used for the growth of Cu 2O by the optical float zone method. It is necessary, then, to consider in detail their structural and optical properties, which are important in improving the crystal quality further, but also to try to reduce the thermal budget required to obtain single-crystal Cu 2O for investigating the physics of Rydberg excitons and polaritons. The Cu 2O obtained at such high temperatures is not suitable for the fabrication of cost-effective, competitive solar cells, not to mention the difficulties of handling and integrating such a brittle crystal into a device.

Recently, we showed that Cu 2O may be obtained in a controlled way via the thermal oxidation of 10 mm × 30 mm, 100 μm thick free-standing Cu at 1020 °C under Ar: O2 after annealing the Cu under Ar: H2. However, the Cu 2O obtained by the bifacial oxidation of the 100 μm thick Cu contained Kirkendall voids in the middle of the crystal, which merged at many points forming extended voids. A sparse distribution of smaller Kirkendall voids formed in the case of Cu 2O obtained by thermal oxidation of 30 μm thick Cu. These voids cannot be eliminated solely by high-temperature annealing above 1100 °C, as it leads to a deterioration in crystal quality due to the out-diffusion of vacancies and voids, which in turn leads to the generation of pinholes at the surface. In addition, the Cu 2O obtained by the thermal oxidation of Cu in a horizontal configuration is bent and distorted due to a loss of tensile strength and differences in the thermal expansion of Cu and Cu 2O at elevated temperatures. − This makes it very difficult to polish the Cu 2O uniformly to eliminate the Kirkendall voids and also isolate a single crystal grain by scribing, considering that Cu 2O is a very brittle crystal.

Here, we show that it is possible to obtain Cu 2O with minimal bowing by reducing the mass of the Cu, i.e., by trimming a 45 μm thick Cu foil into a 2 mm × 20 mm tape and supporting it on 100 μm thick, 5 mm × 10 mm Cu pads with bent, pointed corners to prevent reaction with quartz. This geometry promotes grain growth along the longitudinal direction resulting into sequential mm-size grains that have two parallel straight edges and two grain boundaries that extend over the transverse direction after annealing at 1100 °C and by employing a controlled cool down rate of −5 °C/min under Ar at 10–2 mbar We show that the Kirkendall voids that form in the bulk may be removed by polishing due to the minimal bowing and that it possible to isolate a single crystal grain, by scribing along the transverse direction, having a size that is similar to that of natural crystals employed for the observation of Rydberg excitons. The Cu 2O obtained in this way uses a considerably lower thermal budget at temperatures between 1000 and 1100 °C for 12 h., as opposed to 48 h., which is required for the preparation of Cu 2O feed and seed rods for optical float zone growth. In contrast to the above, we show that the thermal oxidation of Cu rods and wires under identical growth conditions leads to the formation of Cu 2O containing Kirkendall voids that cannot be removed by annealing or polishing. We discuss the adverse effect of these voids that occur in the feed and seed rods employed for the growth of Cu 2O by the optical float zone method and suggest that the growth and processing strategy outlined here is a significant alternative toward achieving high crystal quality Cu 2O in a shorter time and at lower cost.

Methods

Cu (99.99%) foils with a thickness of ∼45 μm were cut and trimmed into rectangular pieces with a length of 20 mm and a width of 1–3 mm. The Cu was trimmed in such a way that the ridges due to rolling ran parallel to the long side. To support the 2 mm × 20 mm Cu, we trimmed 100 μm-thick, 10 mm × 5 mm Cu support pads with bent, pointed corners. The distance between the Cu pads was ∼10 mm to prevent Cu from bending due to loss of tensile strength at high temperatures. In addition, a two-dimensional mesh of 100 μm diameter Cu wires (99.99%) was cut into 10 mm × 20 mm and supported in the same way by using 100 μm thick, 10 mm × 5 mm Cu support pads.

All Cu was cleaned in acetone, isopropanol, and dried with N2, after which it was annealed under a flow of Ar and H2 in a 1” hot wall, chemical vapor deposition reactor capable of reaching 1200 °C, that was fed by a manifold consisting of four mass flow controllers connected to Ar, NH3, O2, and H2. The reactor was initially purged with Ar and H2 at room temperature for 10 min to remove traces of air, after which the temperature was ramped up to 1000 °C at 30 °C/min. Upon reaching 1000 °C, the same flow of Ar and H2 was maintained for 180 min to reduce all oxides and promote grain growth. Finally, a cool-down process was carried out under the same flow of Ar and H2 all the way down to room temperature. A constant flow of 100 mL/min of Ar and 100 mL/min of H2 at 10 mbar was maintained throughout the entire process. After annealing, the Cu was removed and stored under vacuum in a desiccator with silica gel to prevent its oxidation in air.

The thermal oxidation of Cu into Cu 2O was carried out under Ar and O2 in a different 1” hot wall reactor capable of reaching 1100 °C, which was fed by a manifold also consisting of four mass flow controllers connected to Ar, NH3, O2, and H2. This was initially purged at room temperature for 10 min using a flow of 100 mL/min Ar and 100 mL/min H2 to remove air, after which the temperature was ramped up to 900 °C at 30 °C/min by maintaining the same flow of Ar and H2. At 900 °C, the ramp rate was reduced to 10 °C/min until 1020 °C to prevent an overshoot. Cu has a melting point of 1085 °C. At 1020 °C, the same flow of Ar and H2 was maintained for a further 30 min, after which the flow of H2 was interrupted, and a flow of 100 mL/min Ar was maintained for 15 min to purge the H2 and admit O2. Subsequently, the Cu foil was oxidized for 30 min under a flow of 100 mL/min Ar and 10 mL/min O2, after which the temperature was ramped up to 1100 °C at 5 °C/min under 100 mL/min Ar in order to anneal the Cu 2O, which has a melting point of 1235 °C. The Cu 2O was annealed between 1 and 3 h. Finally, the Cu 2O was allowed to cool down at −5 °C/min under a flow of 100 Ar at 10–2 mbar all the way down to room temperature. The Cu 2O was removed at room temperature and inspected under an optical transmission microscope, after which the supports were broken off.

The surface morphology of the Cu and Cu 2O was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the crystal structure and its composition by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Miniflex 6G X-ray diffractometer in the Bragg–Brentano θ–2θ geometry with a Cu-Ka source having a wavelength of 1.54 Å.

The structural properties and composition of Cu 2O were also investigated by Raman and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Raman spectra were acquired using a customized Raman microscope (Raman Alpha Source RLS4032, Nano Bio-Analytics) equipped with a 532 nm single-longitudinal-mode, continuous-wave laser (Integrated Optics and Changchun New Industries Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd.) operating at a power of 4 mW. A ×20 microscope objective was used to focus the excitation laser onto the sample surface. The scattered light was collected via an optical fiber and directed to a Czerny–Turner spectrometer (Spectra Pro SP2300i, Princeton Instruments) with a 0.30 m focal length and a 1200 grooves/mm diffraction grating. The signal was detected using a liquid nitrogen–cooled CCD detector (Spec10:100B/LN2, 1340 × 100 pixels, back-illuminated, Princeton Instruments).

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out using a Nexsa G2 Surface Analysis System from Thermo-Scientific under high vacuum, i.e., 4.7 × 10–9 mbar at room temperature. The X-ray gun had an energy of 1486.6 eV, and two circular spot sizes with diameters of 200 and 400 μm were used.

TEM and HRTEM were used to resolve the crystal structure in the vicinity of the Kirkendall voids using a JEOL JEM-F200 Cold Field Emission Gun (CFEG) TEM/STEM microscope operated at 200 kV with a point-to-point resolution of 0.19 nm. The bulk Cu 2O crystal was sandwiched between two Si pieces, forming a 4 mm × 3 mm structure, which was then cut into 1 mm × 3 mm slices using a diamond wheel saw for TEM and HRTEM. These slices were mechanically ground and polished using a MultiPrep system until a thickness of 30–40 μm was achieved. Final thinning was carried out using a Gatan Precision Ar Ion Polishing System (PIPS), after mounting the sample on a TEM support ring, which also enabled its direct transfer from the PIPS to the TEM holder. In the case of the Cu 2O wires, a section was prepared using a Focused Ion Beam (FIB; Helios 5 UX, Thermo Fisher) for HRTEM characterization. The specimen was milled and thinned using a 30 kV Ga+ beam with currents ranging from 0.44 nA to 41 pA. A final low-energy cleaning step was performed at 5 kV/21 pA and 2 kV/17 pA to minimize surface damage, resulting in a final specimen thickness of 50–100 nm.

Finally, the properties of Cu 2O were investigated by photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. In this case, a polished sapphire (Al2O3) wafer was utilized as a holder for the brittle Cu 2O crystal that was placed inside a closed-cycle cryostat at 5 K. We obtained PL emission from the Cu 2O by using 1.5 mW of continuous wave diode laser excitation at 3.1 eV with a 5 μm spot. The PL emission was collected by a 0.42 NA objective lens and guided onto the slit of a Princeton Instruments spectrometer 500, while a long-pass filter was utilized in the collection path to filter the excitation laser beam.

Results and Discussion

A schematic diagram of the 2 mm × 20 mm Cu supported by the 100 μm thick Cu pads is shown in Figure a, and a typical SEM image of the as-received (AR) Cu is shown in Figure b. The surface is not flat, and ridges run along the rolling direction. The AR Cu exhibited clear, well-resolved peaks in the XRD corresponding to the (111), (200), and (220) crystallographic orientations of the face-centered cubic (fcc) crystal structure of Cu, as shown in Figure c. To improve the crystal quality and phase purity of the AR Cu, it was annealed under Ar: H2 at 1000 °C for 3 h. to promote grain growth and reduce all native oxides. The total time taken to anneal Cu under Ar: H2 was ∼6 h. SEM images of the Cu after annealing are shown in Figure d, from which one may observe that it consists of grains as large as 250 × 250 μm2. The ridges have almost been eliminated due to the sublimation of Cu, which occurs at 1000 °C and 10 mbar; Cu has a melting point of 1085 °C. A higher-magnification SEM image is shown in Figure e, where one may observe step bunching in specific grains, while other grains are relatively flat. According to Deng et al., this surface texture and step bunching occur via the release of thermal stress and are closely related to the packing density and atomic configuration of Cu. They found that Cu (111) exhibits the lowest surface roughness, since it is the highest packed crystal facet. In contrast, (100) and (110) Cu crystal facets form nanoscale periodic steps due to surface energy minimization. The Cu exhibited clear peaks in the XRD after annealing, as shown in Figure f, corresponding to the (111), (200), and (220) crystallographic planes of fcc Cu. We do not detect any peaks belonging to CuO or Cu 2O. It has been shown that a large flow of H2 is very effective in reducing CuO and Cu 2O into metallic Cu. CuO is reduced to Cu 2O by H2 at approximately 60 °C, while complete reduction from Cu 2O to metallic Cu occurs at 480 °C. The improvement in the crystal quality of the Cu occurs due to the fact that H2 penetrates the Cu lattice, which in turn promotes the rearrangement of metal atoms during annealing and its conversion into a single crystal. It has been shown by Jin et al. that polycrystalline Cu, Ni, and Co foils cannot be converted into single crystals during annealing at high temperatures under Ar without H2.

One of the main challenges with the growth of Cu 2O via the thermal oxidation of Cu is that the latter must be maintained as flat as possible during preannealing under Ar: H2 and oxidation under Ar: O2, as bending will give rise to a compressive and tensile strain distribution and the formation of crystallographic imperfections. Consequently, care was taken during handling the 45 μm thick, 2 mm × 20 mm Cu to prevent bending or distortion.

A schematic diagram describing the bifacial oxidation of Cu under Ar: O2 at 1020 °C is shown in Figure a.

(a) Schematic diagram of the bifacial thermal oxidation of metallic Cu; the dotted lines represent vacancies and voids that move along the oxidation front to the middle of the bulk where they form a Kirkendall void layer (KVL) (b) image of Cu2O under white light illumination (c) optical transmission microscopy image of Cu2O under white light where two grains extend along the width of the Cu2O (d) SEM image of Cu2O also showing a mm-sized grain extending across the width of a 1.5 mm × 20 mm Cu2O (e) higher magnification SEM image of a Cu2O grain exhibiting steps with an average height of ∼11 nm and spacing of ∼170 nm as shown by the AFM image inset (f) crystal structure of Cu2O.

The Cu reacts with O, leading to the formation of Cu 2O. Cu atoms move upward and O downward. The oxidation of Cu into Cu 2O proceeds in the vertical and lateral directions, but it should be noted that the oxidation rate of the (100) is larger than that of the (110) and (111) facets of Cu in this order. This can be understood qualitatively by considering that the (111) facet is the most densely packed crystallographic facet of Cu. The oxidation rate is then dependent on the crystal orientation of a grain of Cu, and the strain due to the lattice mismatch between Cu and Cu 2O leads to the formation of crystallographic imperfections, such as vacancies at the oxidation fronts, i.e., the interface between Cu and Cu 2O. The vacancies move along with the oxidation fronts and form voids, but the oxidation occurs from the front and back surfaces, so when the voids and vacancies meet in the middle, this leads to the formation of a layer with a high density of crystallographic imperfections, which we call the Kirkendall void layer (KVL), as shown in Figure b.

A typical image of ruby-red Cu 2O obtained in this way is shown in Figure b. The Cu 2O exhibits minimal bending and bowing along the longitudinal direction compared to their larger 10 mm × 30 mm Cu 2O counterparts. This is attributed to the reduced mass of the 2 mm × 20 mm Cu, which is supported on the 100 μm thick Cu pads. An optical transmission microscopy image of Cu 2O is shown in Figure c. For completeness, a typical SEM image of a single crystal grain that extends over the width of a 1.5 mm × 20 mm Cu 2O crystal is shown in Figure d. It is important to note that Cu 2O retains a memory of the surface texture of the annealed Cu. An SEM image of a Cu 2O grain with surface texture and steps is shown in Figure e; the spacing between these steps is ∼171 nm as determined by AFM, shown as an inset, where the average variation in height is estimated to be ∼10 nm. The average grain size of Cu 2O was estimated to be 800 μm2. We obtained sequential, millimeter-size Cu 2O grains as shown in Figure c,d in a reproducible way by annealing the Cu 2O under Ar at 1100 °C up to 3 h. Grain enlargement occurs due to the increased grain boundary mobility at high temperature. Extending further, the annealing time had no significant effect on the grain size, while increasing the temperature above 1100 °C led to the formation of pinholes on the surface and a deterioration in crystal quality. The time taken to obtain the Cu 2O by thermal oxidation of Cu, including annealing, was ∼6 h.

The Cu 2O exhibited clear peaks in the XRD as shown in Figure a, corresponding to the (111) and (222) crystallographic orientations of the cubic crystal structure of Cu 2O shown in Figure f. We do not detect any Cu or CuO in the XRD and find that the Cu 2O is strained by −0.40% but more relaxed in comparison to the Cu 2O that was not annealed at 1100 °C under Ar, which exhibited a −0.75% compressive strain. This structural relaxation is important since the energy band gap of Cu 2O changes strongly with strain, which in turn will influence the optical properties. We suggest that Cu 2O undergoes strain relaxation during annealing by grain boundary diffusion, leading to the growth of (111) and (222) Cu 2O grains, considering that the surface energy of (111) grains is lower than that of (110). Even though we obtained sequential millimeter-sized grains of Cu 2O, neighboring grains may have a different crystallographic orientation, so there is no strict continuity of the crystal along the longitudinal direction. In addition, Cu 2O contains crystallographic imperfections in the middle of the bulk. A typical SEM image of the Cu 2O section is shown in Figure b. The grain-boundary grooving, which occurs during annealing of Cu under Ar:H2 at 1000 °C, can also be observed on the section of the Cu 2O, , indicating the preservation of the surface texture of Cu. One may also observe the existence of isolated Kirkendall voids due to the bifacial oxidation of the free-standing Cu even after annealing at 1100 °C for 1 h. The average void size is 3 ± 1 μm, and the void middle layer density was estimated to be 400 ± 252 voids/mm2. We have also observed voids in sections, which occur at the grain boundaries, confirming that the latter act as sinks for copper vacancies and act as regions of void formation.

Now, the formation of Kirkendall voids is inherent to the thermal oxidation of Cu, and there is no way to prevent this from happening altogether. However, we have shown clearly in the past that the size and density of the voids are strongly dependent on the thickness of the Cu, i.e., we obtain a sparse distribution of smaller voids in the case of thin Cu foils due to the shorter time it takes to complete the oxidation. One could also reduce the size and density of the voids by using a slower oxidation rate, although this would have an impact on the thermal budget, which is already very high.

Before elaborating further, it is instructive to consider the structural properties of the Cu 2O in the vicinity of the Kirkendall voids by TEM and HRTEM. Figure a shows a TEM image of a small particle in the vicinity of the void, while Figure b,c present HRTEM images of that particle at two different points. A polycrystalline structure of alternating (200) and (111) Cu 2O crystallographic orientations is shown in Figure c. An amorphous region containing nanoscale-sized crystals of Cu 2O in a different region of the void is shown in Figure d. Lattice fringes corresponding to the (200) planes of Cu 2O are clearly resolved in these nanocrystals with an interplanar spacing of 0.21 nm. It should be noted that we did not observe any grain boundaries or voids in the bulk Cu 2O residing above and below the KVL detected by TEM and HRTEM.

Interestingly, we did not find any CuO in the vicinity of the Kirkendall voids, but traces of CuO may exist in the Cu 2O bulk. In contrast, we observe the existence of CuO nanoscale crystals on the surface of the Cu 2O, as shown by the TEM images in Figure e,f, which was confirmed by Raman and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy as described below. However, before considering this in detail, we ought to mention that annealing the Cu 2O at 1100 °C did not eliminate the Kirkendall voids, which we had to remove by polishing. This was possible since the Cu 2O exhibits minimal bending and bowing, which is, in turn, significant as the Cu 2O is very brittle. A typical SEM image of the polished single-crystal Cu 2O on section is shown in Figure c, from which it is evident that the Kirkendall voids have been removed successfully.

The Raman spectra from ten different points on the surface of a 3 mm × 20 mm Cu 2O crystal at room temperature are shown in Figure a,b, corresponding to the back and top sides of the Cu 2O, respectively. One may observe peaks belonging to Cu 2O as well as CuO. The peaks at 550 and 660 cm–1 correspond to vibrational modes of Cu 2O (T 2g and T 1u (TO/LO), respectively), while those at 600 and 1100 cm–1 (B 2g and multiphonon transition second-order band 2B g, respectively) correspond to vibrational modes of CuO. − The peak at 800 cm–1 may be related to SiO2 contamination from the quartz. We find an upshift of the vibrational modes of CuO and a concomitant downshift in those of Cu 2O between the front and back side, respectively, confirming the existence of strain. The Raman spectra acquired from different points were consistent in vibrational frequencies, indicating uniformity, although notable variations in the intensity occurred due to the strain that can enhance or suppress the intensity of certain Raman peaks. ,

We have tried to remove the surface layer of CuO in HCl and HNO3, but this led to a significant loss of transparency due to an increase in surface roughness. We also tried to reduce the CuO on top of the Cu 2O by postgrowth annealing at 500 °C and 10–1 mbar under 50 mL/min Ar: 50 mL/min H2. We showed previously that Cu 2O was deposited by reactive sputtering of Cu under Ar: O2 on m- and r- Al2O3 sapphire, which did not exhibit good crystallinity and contained CuO, which was eliminated by annealing under 50 mL/min Ar: 50 mL/min H2 at 500 °C and 10–1 mbar for 60 min. However, annealing the Cu 2O crystal obtained here under the same conditions resulted in a loss of transparency. We find that the best way to remove CuO on top of Cu 2O without compromising the optical transparency is again by polishing. Nevertheless, it must be emphasized that the surface layer of CuO on top of the Cu 2O acts as a very stable natural passivation layer, which prevents the oxidation of the underlying Cu 2O upon prolonged exposure to ambient O2 and H2O. Consequently, the surface layer of CuO has to be removed by polishing just before the integration of the Cu 2O into a cavity considering that CuO is a semiconductor with a smaller, indirect energy band gap that will act as a sink for the photogenerated electron–hole pairs and excitons where they will recombine in a nonradiative fashion via mid gap states which exist at the CuO/Cu 2O interface due to the large lattice mismatch.

So far, we have shown the thermal oxidation of 45 μm thick, 2 mm × 20 mm Cu under Ar: O2 at 1020 °C, and annealing at 1100 °C under Ar leads to the formation of Cu 2O consisting of sequential mm-size single crystal grains which can be polished to remove the Kirkendall voids as well as the surface layer of CuO. The growth and processing strategy described here uses a considerably lower thermal budget compared to the optical float zone method, which de facto takes longer in preparing feed and seed rods with diameters of 5 mm. However, no one has previously investigated the properties of the Cu 2O obtained by thermal oxidation of Cu rods and wires at temperatures above 1000 °C, which is important for improving the crystal growth of Cu 2O by the optical float zone method.

The Kirkendall voids in the Cu 2O obtained by thermal oxidation of Cu wires at 1020 °C are difficult, if not impossible, to remove by annealing or polishing due to the geometry. Planar surfaces lend themselves to controlled polishing, not curved ones. This imposes a limitation in obtaining high crystal quality, uniform single crystals of Cu 2O by the optical float zone method, as the voids in the feed and seed rods are incorporated in the final crystals. We suggest that the crystal quality of the Cu 2O feed and seed rods for optical float zone growth has to be accounted for, but the crystal growth of Cu 2O by thermal oxidation of Cu in the planar geometry is an important, versatile alternative in obtaining high crystal quality crystals free of voids for exploring the physics of Rydberg excitons and polaritons in this novel metal oxide semiconductor. In addition, the growth of Cu 2O by thermal oxidation of Cu in the planar geometry is attractive due to the lower thermal energy budget and shorter times that it requires. Evidently, other important issues need to be addressed carefully, such as crystal purity, contamination, etc., but we have nonetheless identified some of the most important bottlenecks and how to overcome them.

Conclusions

Cu 2O has been obtained via thermal oxidation of 2 mm × 20 mm, 45 μm thick Cu supported by 100 μm thick Cu pads under Ar: O2 at 1020 °C followed by annealing at 1100 °C and controlled cooldown at −5 °C under Ar. We obtain Cu 2O consisting of millimeter-size sequential grains that have a cubic crystal structure and exhibit minimal bending and bowing, which allows the removal of the Kirkendall voids that form in the middle of the bulk due to the bifacial oxidation by polishing. In contrast, the thermal oxidation of Cu rods under the same conditions leads to the formation of Cu 2O that contains a significantly higher density of Kirkendall voids compared to the planar case, which cannot be removed by annealing or polishing due to their geometry. These crystallographic imperfections are inevitably included in the Cu 2O obtained by the optical float zone method and limit the crystal quality. The growth and processing strategy described here is therefore an important alternative toward the realization of high crystal quality Cu 2O, which also uses a lower thermal budget compared to optical float zone growth.
