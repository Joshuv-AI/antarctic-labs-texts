Construction of a prediction model for hepatic encephalopathy in acute-on-chronic liver failure patients
Authors: Shenglong Lin, Xiangmei Wang, Zixin Xu, Lu Li, Rui Kang, Songtao Li, Xiaoyong Wu, Yueyong Zhu, Haibing Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a serious complication of acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) that requires early detection and intervention to positively impact patient prognosis. This study aimed to develop a reliable model to predict HE in ACLF patients during hospitalization.MethodsRetrospectively recruiting 255 hepatitis B-related ACLF patients, including 67 who developed HE during hospitalization, the study analysed clinical data and biochemical indices collected during the first week of admission. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was used to identify characteristic predictors for hospitalization HE events, and a logistic regression model was subsequently developed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and bootstrap resampling were used to evaluate the model’s discrimination, consistency, and accuracy, and a nomogram was created to visualize the model. An external validation cohort of 236 liver failure patients collected from the same medical centre between 2007 and 2010 was used to validate the model.ResultsThe study found that blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), international normalized ratio (INR), serum ammonia, and infection complications during hospitalization were risk factors for HE in ACLF patients. The new model predicted the development of HE in ACLF patients with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 85.2%, which was superior to other models. The best threshold for the new model was 0.28, resulting in a specificity of 81.4% and a sensitivity of 80.6%. In the validation group, the new model showed similar results, with an AUROC of 79% and a specificity of 83.6% and a sensitivity of 56.6%.ConclusionThis study developed and validated a new prediction model for HE in ACLF patients offering a useful tool for early identification of patients with a high risk of HE in clinical settings. However, to ascertain the model’s general effectiveness, future prospective multicentre studies are warranted.

Introduction

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a complex disease of the central nervous system that arises from metabolic disturbances and is one of the primary causes of death among patients with liver disease [1]. Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is a life-threatening disease characterized by a sudden deterioration in liver function, leading to organ failure and a high risk of short-term mortality. When ACLF is complicated with HE, prompt attention is necessary, given that the condition can be life-threatening, regardless ACLF severity [2].

HE is a common complication in ACLF, with a reported prevalence ranging from 17.2% to 57.8% [3–5]. Furthermore, HE is linked to a substantial increase in mortality risk in ACLF. A pooled odds ratio from 14 studies revealed a 5.62-fold increase in mortality risk in patients with ACLF and HE [6]. Out of the 1348 patients included in the study, 460 (34%) had HE, with a one-year mortality rate of 43%. The short-term mortality rate was significantly higher in patients with more advanced grades of HE [4]. Effective treatment can reverse HE; thus, preventive measures and early detection in high-risk patients are essential in enhancing the survival rate and improving the patients’ quality of life.

HE is challenging to predict due to its multifactorial and complex nature. Several prediction models for HE have been extensively studied, which are based on the patients’ behaviour, clinical signs, and symptoms, such as asterixis, hyperreflexia and clonus. These models consist of the scoring systems, West Haven criteria and HE scoring algorithm (HESA), which grade the severity of HE based on a patient’s consciousness and behaviour. However, these tools are subjective and may be affected by reproducibility issues across operators [7, 8]. Additional models are based on clinical indicators from the patient, such as ammonia level, model of end-stage liver disease (MELD) and Japan hepatic encephalopathy prediction (JHEP), along with its revised version, rJHEP. These models offer an objective, quantitative predictive risk for HE development [9, 10]. Generally, HE can be classified into covert and overt types. Since minimal HE has mild symptoms and is mainly diagnosed through psychometric tests, it presents as a heterogeneous entity. Thus, distinguishing minimal HE from normal remains controversial. Furthermore, regulatory agencies, such as the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) have transitioned from defining HE events as grade I to requiring at least grade 2 or higher for multicentre clinical trials [1]. Our study focused on overt HE events in ACLF patients. Most of the previous risk prediction models for HE in liver disease focused on patients with hepatic cirrhosis or post-operation of the transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) [11–14]. Few studies have examined risk prediction for ACLF complicated by HE. Therefore, our study aims at creating a predictive model to identify overt HE in ACLF patients.

Materials and Methods

Patients and data collection

The study enrolled 255 participants diagnosed with hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related ACLF between January 2015 and December 2018 at Fujian Medical University Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital in Fujian Province, China. The diagnostic criteria for ACLF were defined by the Chinese Medical Association (CMA) as the acute or sub-acute deterioration of preexisting chronic liver disease, extreme fatigue with severe digestive symptoms, progressively worsening jaundice within a short period [serum total bilirubin (TBIL) ≥ 10 mg/dL (171 μmol/L) or daily elevation ≥ 1 mg/dL (17.1 μmol/L)] and an obvious haemorrhagic tendency with prothrombin time activity (PTA) ≤ 40% (or INR ≥ 1.50) [15]. The inclusion criteria were age above 16 years old, chronic HBV infection > 6 months, and an anticipated hospital stay of ≥ 1 week. The exclusion criteria were the presence of HE on admission, liver failure caused by other aetiologies such as hepatitis C virus, hepatitis E virus, autoimmune hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury and alcoholic hepatitis, patients with other significant medical conditions that could interfere with the study outcomes or increase the risk of complications such as severe cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, neurological disorders or malignancy and pregnant women. Another 263 liver failure patients admitted to the same medical centre between January 2007 and December 2010 were collected as a validation cohort. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for these patients were the same, but different aetiologies were still included, in addition to HBV. Both the training and validation groups of patients received standard medical treatment and artificial liver therapy after admission. The medical records of all participants were retrospectively screened. The demographic characteristics, complete blood count, biochemical indicators and coagulation results of each patient were collected within the first week of admission. Overt HE and infection during hospitalization were also recorded. The time window for the occurrence of infection events was defined as the period prior to the onset of hepatic encephalopathy. The diagnostic criteria for overt HE used in this study were those exceeding the operative criteria proposed for the diagnosis of grade 1 HE by the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) clinical guideline [7].

The study was conducted following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital in Fujian Province, China (approval number: 2020_036_01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the use of their medical records.

Statistical methods

The statistical analysis was conducted using the R 4.2.2 software (http://www.r-project.org). The multiple imputation method, with the MICE package, was used to handle missing data [16]. For continuous variables, the T-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test was selected based on the distribution of the data, while the Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. Highly skewed variables were transformed to be normalized before fitting the prediction model. Risk factors associated with HE were identified using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO). The one standard error (1se) of the lambda for LASSO regression was used to select the best subset of predictors, achieving a balance between model complexity and fit [17]. The logistic regression model was constructed using the variables selected by LASSO, calculating the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Cook’s distance was used to examine the influence of each data point, and values of less than 0.1 were considered acceptable. The multicollinearity of each variable was measured using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The performance of the new model was evaluated for discrimination and calibration. Discrimination assessed the model’s ability to distinguish high-risk individuals from low-risk individuals, which was measured using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Calibration evaluated the accuracy of absolute risk estimation using the Brier score and calibration curve. Both internal (Bootstrap method) and external validation were performed to validate the performance of the model. Finally, a nomogram was developed to facilitate the practical use of the prediction model. Statistically significance was set at P value < 0.05.

Results

Patient’s characteristics

Table 1 lists the baseline characteristics of the training cohort. Of the 255 patients with HBV-related ACLF, 57 were female and 198 were male. During hospitalization, 67 patients (26%) developed HE. Patients with HE experienced a shorter hospital stay compared to those without HE, with median durations of 25 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 13–35) and 30 days (IQR: 15–46.25), respectively (p = 0.049). The median time to HE onset was 7 days (IQR: 3–17.5). None of the patients underwent liver transplantation prior to the development of HE. Patients who developed HE exhibited higher levels of TBIL, globulin, BUN, and serum ammonia, alongside lower level of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), compared to those without HE. Additionally, these patients had prolonged prothrombin time (PT), decreased PTA, and increased INR. Patients who developed HE also had more infection events than those without HE (94% vs. 74%; p < 0.05). Identification of predictive factors for HE

LASSO regression was performed to identify the risk factors associated with HE development in ACLF patients. The best model based on lambda.1se (0.07) selected the following predictive factors: BUN, AFP, INR, serum ammonia and the presence of an infection complication.

Construction of predictive model

The logistic regression model was constructed with BNN, AFP, INR, serum ammonia, and whether an infection occurred (Table 2). The formula of the model was λ = −5.49 + 0.76 × ln(BNN) − 0.39 × ln(AFP) + 0.55 × INR + 0.03 × Ammonia + 1.69 × Infection (yes = 1, no = 0). The probability of the HE used this new model could be calculated as p = 100/(1 + eλ). The OR and 95%CI of the risk factors were BUN: 2.15 (1.17-4.06), AFP: 0.68 (0.53-0.85), INR: 1.73 (1.16-2.60), serum ammonia: 1.03 (1.02-1.04), and superimposed infection: 5.41 (1.72-22.30).

Assessment and validation of the model

The Cook’s distances were calculated to examine the influence of each observation for the model. No outlier was detected, as none of Cook’s distance exceeded 0.1 (See Figures S1 in the Supplementary Material). The VIF values of BUN, AFP, INR, serum ammonia and infection events were 1.23, 1.12, 1.17, 1.08 and 1.04, respectively. All of the VIF values of the risk factors used in the model were less than 10, indicating that there was no collinearity relationship between these variables. The model’s AUROC was 85.2%, and the optimal threshold for the model was 0.28. At this threshold, the model’s specificity was 81.4%, and the sensitivity was 80.6%. The AUROC values of previously reported models, such as serum ammonia, MELD score, CLIF-C AD and rJHEP score, were also calculated, which were 72.8%, 63.4%, 59.7% and 58.0%, respectively. Compared to those models, the new model had better discrimination, as illustrated in the Figure 1.

ROC cure of the new model. As shown in the figure, comparing the AUROC of each model, the new model (AUROC = 85.2%) performed better in predicting HE compared to ammonia (AUROC = 72.8%), MELD (AUROC = 63.4%), rJHEP (AUROC = 58.0%), and CLIF-C AD (AUROC = 59.7%).

The calibration curve demonstrated that the predicted probability was highly consistent with the actual probability. The Brier score was 0.13 (Figure 2, left panel). After 100 repetitions of bootstrap resampling, the C-index after bias correction, calibration intercept, and calibration slope were 0.84, −0.04 and 0.93, respectively. The bootstrap procedure provided a bias-corrected estimate of the calibration curve, with a mean absolute error of 0.031. The new model successfully passed the bootstrap internal validation, as shown in the Figure 2 (right panel).

The external validation cohort includes 236 ALCF patients, in which 135 (57%) were HBV infection-related ACLF, and 53 (22%) developed HE during hospitalization. The ROC curves showed that the new model still had better discrimination in the external validation cohort, when compared to serum ammonia, MELD, CLIF-C AD and rJHEP score. The AUROC values of those models were 79.0% (New model), 66.3% (serum ammonia), 58.6% (MELD), 64.8%(CLIF-C AD), and 64.0% (rJHEP), respectively (Figure 3(A)). At the threshold of 0.28, the model’s specificity was 83.6%, but the sensitivity declined to 56.6%. The subgroup analysis of patients with ACLF related to HBV infection in this cohort produced similar results, as shown in the Figure 3(B).

Nomogram is user-friendly, intuitive and can be easily incorporated into clinical practice, making it a valuable tool for evidence-based decision-making, which has been shown to be more accurate than traditional scoring systems or algorithms. Thus, a nomogram was built to facilitate the clinical usage of our new prediction model. The nomogram allows clinicians to input the risk factors, such as BUN, AFP, INR, serum ammonia and infection complication, to calculate a personalized probability for HE development (Figure 4).

Nomogram of the new model for predicting HE. Discussion

This retrospective study developed a prediction model that accurately identified patients with high predisposition to HE during hospitalization for HBV related ACLF. The study comprised 255 patients with HBV-related ACLF, of whom 26% developed HE. Patients who developed HE experienced reduced hospital stays due to their deteriorated and aggravated conditions, with a majority leaving against medical advice in the current cohort. By using LASSO regression, this study identified BUN, AFP, INR, serum ammonia and infection complications as factors contributing to the risk of HE in ACLF patients. The new logistic regression model established had a higher accuracy rate in predicting HE compared to other existing models, such as serum ammonia alone, the MELD score, the CLIF-C AD, and the rJHEP score. The new model can serve as a helpful tool for early detection of patients with high HE predisposition in clinical settings.

Serum ammonia levels and inflammation are key factors in the pathogenesis of HE, a condition characterized by elevated levels of neurotoxic ammonia in the brain, principally produced in the gut through protein digestion, amino acid deamination and bacterial urease activity. While a healthy liver manages ammonia concentrations in the body, its dysfunction can disrupt the inter-organ metabolism of ammonia. Ammonia plays a vital role in biochemical reactions across several organs and is transported through plasma membranes via multiple mechanisms. Elevated ammonia levels may trigger cellular swelling, oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic disruption across different organs, such as the liver, muscles and immune system [1, 20–22]. While there is no direct correlation between the level of hyperammonaemia and the severity of HE, individual patient sensitivities to the same ammonia levels and the potential unreliability of ammonia measurements may account for this lack of correlation. The absence of elevated ammonia levels is incompatible with an HE diagnosis [23]. Our logistic regression analysis indicated a positive association between elevated serum ammonia levels and the risk of developing HE. In the training dataset, the AUROC of serum ammonia outperformed the MELD score, the CLIF-C AD scores and the rJHEP score but underperformed the new model (as illustrated in the Figure 1). In addition to gut bacterial translocation and inflammation in the liver, superimposed infections exacerbate systemic inflammation, contributing to blood–brain barrier dysfunction and neuroinflammation, resulting in HE symptoms. Bacterial infections tend to trigger and worsen the severity and prognosis of HE [24]. Previous clinical evidence has demonstrated the link between bacterial infections and the severity of HE. Merli et al. confirmed the presence of cognitive impairment (overt or subclinical) in 42% of cirrhotics without infection, in 79% with infection and in 90% with sepsis [25]. Additional research by Hung et al. further highlighted the impact of infections on the mortality rate of cirrhotic patients with HE [26]. Their study showed that pneumonia and sepsis without specific focus were particularly detrimental to these patients. Moreover, another study showed a clear correlation between systemic inflammation and advanced HE, ultimately leading to an increased risk of mortality [27]. Our study consistently found that infection events posed the highest risk for HE (OR = 5.41) among all other risk factors.

AFP is a frequently used serum biomarker for detecting hepatocellular carcinoma in clinical practice. Elevated levels of AFP indicate liver cell growth and regeneration. An increase in AFP level after liver injury is associated with hepatic regeneration; however, its significance in chronic liver disease has yet to be established [5]. A study of patients with acetaminophen-induced liver injury observed that AFP levels of 20 ng/mL or higher were linked with a favourable survival [28]. In another study investigating acute liver failure, higher AFP ratios between day 1 and day 3, rather than absolute AFP levels, were associated with improved outcomes [29]. Our study had consistent results, demonstrating that elevated level of AFP was associated with a reduced risk of HE development in ACLF patients (OR = 0.68).

Our study showed that patients with ACLF and HE had elevated levels of BUN and INR, in line with previous findings. A recent study reported that BUN was a biomarker of risk for HE severity [13]. The BUN serves as an indicator of acute kidney injury (AKI) in acute decompensated cirrhosis, which accounts for 20-50% of cases. High BUN levels could indicate impaired kidney function contributing to the development of HE [30, 31]. Moreover, nitrogen metabolism has a significant role in the pathogenesis of HE in liver diseases. As a byproduct of nitrogen metabolism, BUN may contribute to the development of HE. Further studies are necessary to confirm this underlying mechanism [32]. Additionally, an elevated INR value can also indicate liver damage, which is a known risk factor for the development of HE. In patients with advanced liver cirrhosis, an elevated INR was associated with the severity of HE [33].

The identification of high-risk patients can delay the progression of HE and improve the prognosis of patients with end-stage liver diseases. Effective treatments can reverse the effects of HE. Therefore, reliable tools are required for clinical practice. The Fulminant Hepatitis Study Group of Japan developed a prediction model for HE called JHEP, which included four indicators: TBIL, PT, age and aetiology. Japan implemented a referral system based on the results of the JHEP model. Patients with a probability of HE development exceeding 20% and 50% according to the JHEP model were directed to a liver centre and provided with intensive care, including artificial liver support. The introduction of JHEP greatly reduced the prevalence of HE in patients with severe liver injury. The revised JHEP (rJHEP) model, which did not consider aetiology, had a better predictive ability for HE development than the original JHEP model [10, 34]. Additionally, scoring systems for severe hepatopathy, including MELD and CLIF-C AD, can be utilized to assess the risk of HE development. Although there are other prediction models for HE that employ the latest imaging techniques and machine-learning methods, they are complex and require further research before clinical application [14, 35]. On the other hand, our model surpassed the performance of MELD, CLIF-C AD and rJHEP in accuracy in predicting HE development. Importantly, the new model employs easily obtainable clinical indicators, has a simple nomogram and is more clinically acceptable.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, due to the retrospective medical data and small sample size, we could not conduct more detailed analyses of (1) the presence of time-dependent risk predictors for the development of HE; (2) the sequential organ failure assessment models such as CLIF-ACLF and COSSH-ACLF [36]; and (3) the nutrition status of patients, such as sarcopenia. Accordingly, future studies with comprehensive data are required to enhance our model. Secondly, the training and validation cohorts in this study were from the same medical centre, which may limit the model’s generalization. Hence, future prospective validation studies should be conducted in multiple centres. Thirdly, the diagnostic criteria for ACLF in this study were based on the CMA definition, diverging from the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) and the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) criteria. Therefore, further evaluation is necessary to determine the applicability of our model for ACLF patients in line with the EASL and APASL criteria.

Conclusion

In the present study, we have established a prediction model for HE development in ACLF patients. The model consists of five predictors: BUN, AFP, INR, serum ammonia, and infection complications, which are easily obtained in clinical settings. Our simplified nomogram can aid medical practitioners in identifying ACLF patients who are at a high risk of developing HE at an early stage and initiate empirical treatment to prevent or delay HE development. Future prospective studies with comprehensive data in multiple centres will be necessary to validate the effectiveness of this new model.
