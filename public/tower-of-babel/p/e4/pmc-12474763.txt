Relationship between Weight Bias Internalization, Diabetes Stigma, and Perceptions of Healthcare Interactions among People with Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Tracy J. Sims, Richa Kapoor, Chanadda Chinthammit, Erik Spaepen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWeight and diabetes stigma among healthcare professionals (HCPs) may negatively impact treatment decisions, patient outcomes, and physician–patient interactions. We assessed the relationship between weight stigma, diabetes stigma, perceptions of healthcare quality, and avoidance of healthcare among adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D).MethodsThis observational, online survey-based study included 857 US adults with T2D. The survey included perceptions of patient-centered care with questions from the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS) survey, perceptions of provider communication with questions from the Diabetes Attitudes, Wishes, and Needs (DAWN) study, de novo questions assessing participants’ interactions with HCPs, perceived weight stigma and discrimination, and healthcare quality/avoidance delay questions. Mean scores were reported for patient-reported outcome measures: Modified Weight Bias Internalization Scale, Weight Self-Stigma Questionnaire, and Type 2 Diabetes Stigma Assessment Scale. Additional analyses were based on CAHPS, DAWN, and healthcare quality/avoidance responses.ResultsHigh degrees of weight bias internalization (WBI) and diabetes stigma were observed among participants dissatisfied with their HCP’s overall involvement in their care and those who perceived judgment from the HCP because of their weight. Participants with high degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma were more likely to avoid seeking care, felt uncomfortable with body examinations, and rarely underwent regular health checkups. Those who had suboptimal interactions with their HCPs reported greater stigma.ConclusionsIncreasing awareness among HCPs regarding weight and diabetes stigma and promoting compassionate communication in healthcare interactions may help diminish these forms of stigma, thereby potentially improving health outcomes for people with T2D.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01786-9.

Introduction

The global prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) and obesity has far-reaching public health implications. In 2021, the prevalence of diabetes among adults was estimated to be 10.5% worldwide and 14.7% in the USA, with T2D accounting for more than 95% of all diabetes diagnoses [1–3]. Furthermore, nearly 42% of adults in the USA in 2020 and approximately 16% of adults worldwide in 2022 had obesity (defined as a body mass index [BMI] ≥ 30 kg/m2) [4, 5]. These conditions often co-occur and are associated with an increased risk of micro- and macrovascular complications, often contributing to mortality [6, 7].

Diabetes stigma, expressed as negative stereotypes, judgment, and prejudice against people with diabetes, is a common psychosocial stressor that can worsen health and lead to feelings of shame, blame, and exclusion among patients with T2D [8]. They may also encounter weight stigma, the social devaluation of people due to excess body weight, leading to negative weight-based stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination [9]. Weight stigma is associated with impaired quality of life, poor mental health outcomes, and an elevated risk of obesity or T2D, physiological stress, eating disorders, depression, and anxiety [10–13]. In addition, weight-related self-stigma, referred to as weight bias internalization (WBI), is associated with psychological distress, adverse health outcomes, avoidance of health-seeking behaviors such as physical activity and routine checkups, obesogenic factors, body image dissatisfaction, and clinical attrition [10, 13–17].

Research has demonstrated the presence of weight bias among healthcare providers (HCPs), including physicians and dietitians, leading to concerns of suboptimal diagnosis, patient care, disease management, and poor health outcomes [15, 18]. Implicit bias among HCPs can impact patient care and HCP-patient interpersonal relationships [19]. In addition, perceived bias in the healthcare setting may hinder equitable access to care and prompt patients to expect differential treatment [16]. Recent evidence suggests that 40–60% of people with T2D experience weight and diabetes stigma during interactions with HCPs (hereafter referred to as healthcare interactions), including blame and judgment, internalized weight stigma, and diabetes-specific distress [11, 20]. However, to date, limited research has empirically studied the extent to which these forms of stigma affect healthcare experiences for people with T2D.

Given the presence of weight and diabetes stigma among HCPs and the implications for patients’ health and psychosocial outcomes, it is imperative to better understand these forms of stigma in the context of patients’ experiences with their healthcare teams and their perceptions of the quality of healthcare they receive. Individuals with weight and diabetes stigmas may tend to limit their interactions with HCPs, and improved awareness about these forms of stigma may help in reducing the adverse health effects associated with these stigmas. The current study sought to explore the relationship between these stigmas and perceptions of healthcare quality, avoidance of healthcare, patient-centered care, and patient–provider collaboration among adults with T2D.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This cross-sectional, observational, self-administered survey was conducted among US adults with T2D between 10 April 2023 and 20 April 2023. Participants were recruited through the AmeriSpeak® panel of the National Opinion Research Center, an external electronic data capture organization that specializes in web surveys (Supplementary Methods). The survey included English-speaking US residents ≥ 18 years of age (except in Alabama and Nebraska, where eligible adults were ≥ 19 years of age) with a self-reported diagnosis of T2D. The exclusion criteria were participation in a T2D or chronic weight management trial, pregnancy at the time of the study, and end-stage renal disease during the study period. The study methods and results are reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies [21].

Survey Description

The study was conducted using a web-based survey. Potential participants received an email or telephone invitation to the survey, with a link to the screening questions to verify their eligibility. Eligible participants provided electronic consent before proceeding to the survey questions. The survey assessed participants’ perceptions of healthcare interactions and healthcare quality and included the following (see Supplementary Table 1 for details): (1) perceptions of patient-centered care with questions from the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS) survey [22, 23]; (2) perceptions of provider communication with questions from the Diabetes Attitudes, Wishes, and Needs (DAWN) study [24]; (3) de novo questions assessing participants’ interactions with their healthcare provider and regarding perceived weight stigma and discrimination in general; and (4) healthcare quality/avoidance delay questions [25–29].

The following patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures assessed the participants’ diabetes stigma and WBI: (1) Modified Weight Bias Internalization Scale (WBIS-M), for which higher scores indicate a greater degree of WBI [30]; (2) Weight Self-Stigma Questionnaire (WSSQ) and its subscales—WSSQ Self-devaluation, and WSSQ Fear of Enacted Stigma—for which higher scores indicate worse self-stigma [31]; and (3) Type 2 Diabetes Stigma Assessment Scale (DSAS-2) and its subscales—DSAS-2 Treated Differently, DSAS-2 Blame and Judgment, and DSAS-2 Self-stigma, for which item responses are summed within each subscale and higher scores indicate greater diabetes stigma [32].

Survey responses were anonymized using unique participant identifiers, and any participant-identifying information was removed from the data.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Software, version 9.4. Categorical variables were summarized using numbers and percentages (with the percentage excluding the number with missing data in the denominator), while continuous variables were summarized using numbers, means, and standard deviations (SD).

Descriptive data included participants’ self-reported sociodemographic and clinical characteristics such as sex, age, weight, height, ethnicity, race, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels. Mean scores and SDs for each PRO measure and the proportion of participants for each response to the de novo questions were reported. Additional analyses assessed the relationships between WBI, diabetes stigma, and the participants’ perceptions of interactions with their HCP, healthcare quality, and healthcare avoidance, by performing subgroup analyses on the basis of the participants’ responses to the CAHPS, DAWN, and healthcare quality/avoidance questions. The significance level was set at a two-sided α of 0.05; p-values were derived using nonparametric tests for continuous data: the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for comparisons between two arms) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (for comparisons between more than two arms). In addition, the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test was used to compare categorical data with more than two arms. No data imputation was performed for missing data. Data from participants who completed only a portion of the survey were included in the analyses where possible.

Compliance

The Western-Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol and informed consent forms (tracking no.: 20231182; date: 15 March 2023). The study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and consistent with the guidelines for good pharmacoepidemiology practices. Informed consent was obtained from each participant at study entry before the study procedures were conducted. This article does not contain any studies with animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Descriptive Analyses

A total of 27,590 AmeriSpeak panel members were invited to take the survey, of whom 6785 individuals completed the eligibility screening questions. Of the 875 individuals who screened eligible, 857 respondents completed the electronic survey (Supplementary Fig. 1). The median time taken to complete the survey was 20 min. Participants’ baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 2 (mean [SD] age: 57.4 [15.7] years; male participants: 61.6%; female participants: 38.4%; mean [SD] HbA1c: 7.0% [1.5%]; mean [SD] BMI: 33.4 [8.4] kg/m2; median duration of diabetes: 9.0 years).

Most participants (96.3%) reported visiting their HCP during the 12 months before the study (Supplementary Table 2). Overall, 11.3%, 12.9%, and 12.5% of participants felt that their HCP only sometimes or never explained things in a way that was easy to understand, listened carefully to them, or showed respect for what they had to say, respectively. About 17% of participants felt their HCP sometimes/never knew the important information about their medical history, and 12.5% felt that their HCPs included them in decisions about their care less than/a lot less than they wanted (Table 1). Nearly 17% of participants felt that their HCP sometimes or never spent enough time with them. Over 12% of participants disagreed with the DAWN statements: “My doctor spends enough time with me,” and “I feel that I am fully involved in treatment decisions” (Table 1). Overall, 10.6% of participants felt that it was difficult to talk to their primary doctor (Table 1). Of the total sample, nearly half of the participants had previously experienced stigma in general (48.4%) or had been teased because of their weight (44.3%), while over a quarter reported that they had been treated unfairly (29.9%) or had been discriminated against (25.6%) because of their weight (Table 1). Over 39% of participants sometimes, usually, or always felt judged by an HCP because of their weight, 31.7% avoided visiting their HCP, and 26.8% delayed or avoided getting the care they thought they needed (Table 1). Nearly one-fourth of participants (22.4%) somewhat/strongly agreed with the statement, “I avoid seeing my primary or other healthcare provider because I feel uncomfortable when my body is being examined.” Overall, 16.0% of participants felt that their HCP sometimes or never listened carefully to them, 14.5% felt that their HCP never or only sometimes showed respect for what they had to say, and 12.1% rated the quality of healthcare they received in the past 12 months as fair or poor.

Additional Analyses

Stigma and Healthcare Interactions

Participants who felt that their HCP never seemed to know the important information about their medical history (Table 2) or the values and beliefs important to them (Supplementary Table 3) reported higher mean scores across all PRO measures (including WBIS-M, WSSQ, and DSAS-2 total and all subscales except DSAS-2-Treated Differently) compared with those who felt that their HCP knew about their medical history or their values and beliefs (p < 0.0001 for all). Similarly, participants who felt that their HCP did not involve them in their care as much as they wanted (Table 2) or who felt that their HCP never spent enough time with them (Supplementary Table 3) reported higher mean scores across all PRO measures (p < 0.0001 for all), indicating higher degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma relative to participants who felt otherwise. Approximately two-thirds of participants who felt that their doctor never knew important information about their medical history (63.2%) or about their values and beliefs (67.3%) had previously experienced stigma (Table 2; Supplementary Table 3). Among participants who felt a lot less involved in care-related decisions or felt that their doctor never spent enough time with them, 68.8% and 75.0%, respectively, had previously experienced stigma.

Participants who felt that their doctor did not spend enough time with them had higher mean WSSQ and DSAS-2 Total and subscale scores (indicating high degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma) relative to those who felt that their doctor spent enough time with them (p < 0.05 for all; Table 3). Those who felt somewhat involved in their treatment-related decisions had higher degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma (higher WBIS-M, WSSQ-Total, and WSSQ-Self-devaluation scores) relative to other participants (p < 0.0001 for all). Participants who found it difficult to talk to their primary doctor had higher scores across all PRO measures compared with those who found it easier to talk to their doctor (p < 0.0001 for all), except on the WSSQ-Self-devaluation subscale. Similar results were observed for participants’ perceptions of communication between the people involved in their treatment and their perceived relationship with the people they see about their disease (Supplementary Table 4). Finally, approximately 59% of participants who felt that their doctor did not spend enough time with them had previously experienced stigma (Table 3). In addition, 61.3% of participants who did not feel fully involved in treatment-related decisions and 72.5% of those who found it difficult to talk to their main doctor had previously experienced stigma. Stigma and Healthcare Quality and Avoidance

Participants who avoided visiting their HCP even when they needed to (Supplementary Table 5), those who avoided visiting their HCP because they felt uncomfortable about their body being examined (Table 4), and those who rarely underwent regular health checkups (Supplementary Table 5) had higher mean scores across all PRO measures (except the DSAS-2-Treated Differently subscale) relative to participants who felt otherwise (p < 0.0001 for all). Likewise, participants who delayed or did not get the care they thought they needed had higher scores across all PRO measures than those who did not delay care (p < 0.0001 for all; Supplementary Table 5). Participants who felt that their HCPs never listened to them had high WSSQ-Total, WSSQ Self-devaluation, DSAS-2 Total, and DSAS-2 Treated Differently scores (p < 0.0001 for all), while those who felt that their HCPs listened to them only sometimes had high WBIS-M, WSSQ Fear of Enacted Stigma, DSAS-2- Blame and Judgment, and DSAS-2 Self-stigma scores (p < 0.0001 for all; Supplementary Table 5). Participants who felt that their HCP sometimes respected what they had to say reported higher mean scores across all PRO measures (p < 0.0001 for all) than those who felt otherwise (Supplementary Table 5). Those who always felt judged by their HCP because of their weight reported higher mean scores across all PRO measures relative to those who did not feel judged (p < 0.0001), except DSAS-2 Self-stigma (Supplementary Table 5). Participants who rated the quality of healthcare that they received in the past 12 months as only fair reported higher mean scores across all PRO measures compared with those who were generally satisfied with the quality of healthcare (p < 0.0001), except the DSAS-2-Treated Differently subscale. Notably, 90.9% of participants who always felt judged by their HCP because of their weight (Supplementary Table 5) and 75.0% of those who rated their healthcare quality as fair (Table 4) had previously experienced stigma. Among participants who avoided visiting their HCP, 62.7% had previously experienced stigma. Over 65% of those who felt uncomfortable about their body being examined or had delayed or avoided getting the care they thought they needed had previously experienced stigma. The majority of participants who felt that their HCP never listened carefully to them (84.6%) or only sometimes showed respect for what they had to say (71.4%) had previously experienced stigma.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional, survey-based study of adults with T2D, we observed high degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma among participants who felt dissatisfied with their HCP’s overall involvement in their care. Similarly, participants who perceived that they had ineffective interactions with their HCPs reported greater weight and diabetes stigma than other participants. Compared with participants reporting lower levels of stigma, those who reported high degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma or had experienced stigma previously were more likely to avoid or delay seeking care when they needed it, felt uncomfortable with their body being examined, and rarely underwent regular health checkups. Individuals who perceived judgment from the HCP because of their weight were also likely to have high degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma and to have previously experienced stigma owing to their weight.

Our findings align with prior research examining links between weight stigma and poorer quality healthcare experiences and avoidance of healthcare reported by adults with obesity [33, 34]. While little research has examined these relationships among people with T2D, recent qualitative evidence found that women with T2D or prediabetes and high body weight reported weight stigma in their interactions with HCPs (including experiencing embarrassment, internalized feelings of failure, and feeling blamed for their weight and health conditions) and, as a result, perceived a decreased quality of care [35]. Our study supplements this literature, showing that both weight stigma and diabetes stigma are associated with poorer healthcare experiences and utilization among adults with T2D.

Productive clinical engagement is a key factor in effective chronic disease care. Our findings suggest, however, that for many patients with two highly prevalent chronic diseases, interactions with their HCP may be suboptimal, possibly reinforcing existent WBI and stigma and increasing the risk of T2D- and obesity-related health and social sequelae. Only about 50% of people with diabetes achieve standard-of-care HbA1c outcomes and only about one in five people achieve cumulative standard-of-care HbA1c, blood pressure, and cholesterol measures [36]. There is value in supporting and elevating HCPs’ appreciation of the negative influence of WBI and stigmatization on health behaviors and outcomes and prioritizing training that aids in better awareness and management of WBI and stigma in the clinical setting. This could also prompt a need to address weight bias and stigma among HCPs.

Previous research has acknowledged gaps in addressing stigma among HCPs, emphasizing the under-utilization of stigma-reduction strategies and inadequate sensitization of HCPs [37]. In the obesity field, researchers have increasingly studied strategies to reduce weight stigma among HCPs, with evidence pointing to the need for multiple stigma-reduction interventions [33]. Some studies also suggest that reframing conversations between physicians and patients on obesity causes and the role of factors outside of personal responsibility in weight management to increase patients’ awareness of obesogenic factors may help reduce WBI and self-stigma [38]. However, to our knowledge, no research has tested interventions to reduce diabetes stigma among HCPs, and our findings provide evidence in support of increasing calls for efforts to broadly tackle both weight and diabetes stigma, specifically in the medical community [8, 39].

The study results concur with previous studies illustrating the association between healthcare avoidance and weight or diabetes stigma. For instance, among adults with T2D in the USA, WBI and diabetes self-stigma were associated with ineffective interactions with HCPs, specifically among those who perceived weight-related judgment from a doctor [20]. In addition, a multicountry study among enrollees in a weight management program found that WBI was associated with avoidance of healthcare, perceptions of judgment from doctors due to excess weight, fewer routine checkups, inadequate listening or respect from HCPs, and lower healthcare quality [15].

Our study findings add additional insights about experienced and internalized stigma in the context of healthcare experiences among people with T2D and indicate that additional empirical attention is warranted to determine the nature, mechanisms, and impact of both weight and diabetes stigma on diabetes care, and well-tested means to reduce each in the clinical setting. Further, our findings emphasize the need for optimal and equitable healthcare that takes patients’ sensitivities into consideration and that can provide adequate support to patients experiencing WBI and diabetes stigma, as well as minimize the adverse health outcomes that may arise because of these forms of stigma.

Several study limitations should be noted. The survey collected self-reported data on participants’ experiences with weight and diabetes stigma as well as their interactions with HCPs, and the possibility of recall bias cannot be ruled out. However, several measures were employed to ensure data integrity and robustness of survey responses. Certain results may be underreported, as participants could skip survey questions or withdraw from the survey at any time. The association of WBI and diabetes stigma with important social determinants of health (such as gender, socioeconomic status, and race/ethnicity) was not assessed in the current analysis and was outside the scope of the current study. The study did not include multivariable analyses owing to the high correlation of the study variables, as indicated by a Cronbach alpha value of around 0.80 when analyzing the stigma scales. Using them as independent fixed effects in multivariable models would lead to multicollinearity and unstable results. Further studies investigating the impact of stigma and healthcare interactions in people with T2D on their health and patient care outcomes in the context of these sociodemographic factors are warranted.

Conclusions

Previous research indicates that the presence of weight and diabetes stigma in the healthcare setting may undercut diagnosis, treatment, and positive health outcomes among people with T2D. In the current analysis, people with T2D who experienced suboptimal interactions with HCPs reported greater degrees of WBI and diabetes stigma and were likely to have previously experienced stigma. In addition, people who previously experienced stigma were more likely to avoid or delay seeking care and underwent regular health checkups less frequently. Educating HCPs about both weight and diabetes stigma and enhancing provider–patient communication to promote engaging language and compassionate, individualized care may help diminish WBI and diabetes stigma, thereby potentially improving health outcomes for people living with T2D. Further research is needed to develop specific interventions for HCPs to address the potential diabetes stigma. Alternative non-weight-focused outcome measures may encourage individuals to interact better with HCPs, aiding in better management of T2D- and obesity-related health complications.
