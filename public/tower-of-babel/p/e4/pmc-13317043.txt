Do new labels solve old problems? Conceptual, ethical, and clinical challenges of constructing restrictive eating behaviours as “self-harm”
Authors: James Downs, Anna Carnegie, Mollie Twitchell, Bella Reed, David Morris, David Viljoen, Samuel Chawner, Agnes Ayton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe concept of “Restrictive Intake Self-Harm” (“RISH”) has recently been introduced to conceptualise divergent and/or complex presentations of restrictive eating behaviours as a form of self-harm, rather than symptoms of an eating disorder. Whilst this may represent a well-intentioned response to poorly met clinical needs, it also raises significant conceptual, ethical, and clinical concerns. This paper, written by authors with clinical, academic, and lived experience, situates “RISH” within a broader history of premature classificatory expansion in eating disorders, whereby new terminology has been introduced with insufficient empirical support and a lack of substantive coproduction with people with lived experience. Our analysis highlights the unintended consequences of this approach and how conflating restriction with self-injurious behaviour risks conceptual ambiguity, diagnostic misclassification, and the diversion of patients away from specialist services and evidence-based treatments that may benefit them. We advocate an empirical, data-driven approach to understanding the heterogeneous nature of restrictive eating and its diverse biological, psychological, social, and cultural drivers, across diagnoses. In clinical practice, greater skill in differential diagnosis and collaborative, individualised formulation is needed, alongside reform of service design to facilitate more integrated care. Vitally, terminology and care pathways for disordered eating need to be meaningfully coproduced with the people who need them, recognising the plurality and legitimacy of experiential knowledge as central to creating more inclusive and attuned care.

Introduction and aims

The creation of new classificatory terms in psychiatry often reflects the intersection of clinical aspiration with systemic constraint. Within the field of eating disorders, successive and contested labels such as “Severe and Enduring Eating Disorder” (“SEED”) and “terminal anorexia” illustrate attempts to address perceived limitations in existing diagnostic frameworks [1–3]. While each has been introduced with the intention of providing greater conceptual clarity and therapeutic guidance, they have also generated a range of concerns regarding their ethical, epistemological, and practical consequences when used in addition to—or in the place of—existing diagnoses [4–13].

“Restrictive Intake Self-Harm” (“RISH”) is the most recent addition to this landscape. A recent paper by Fenton et al. (2025) develops a framework and guidance for clinical management based on a single composite case study and an unpublished clinician survey [14]. The authors define “RISH” as a pattern of restrictive intake or eating behaviour understood as a means of self-harm, functioning “to regulate emotion, communicate distress and elicit medicalised care for an unmet need” (Fenton et al., 2025, p.1) [14]. The terminology has also appeared in NHS England-labelled documentation that describes itself as representing “emerging national practice consensus” (Ellison et al., 2025, p.22) [15], and in a decision-making tool promoted for use in primary care settings [16].

The rapid movement of “RISH” from speculative literature into practice-facing documentation warrants careful scrutiny. Classification in psychiatry is not a neutral, descriptive act: once embedded in policy and service infrastructures, new terms begin to organise clinical reasoning, referral pathways, and notions of legitimacy and identity [17–20]. Yet, unlike the extensive literature that already exists examining the relationships between self-injury and eating disorder behaviours [21–25], the “RISH” framework has not emerged from a programme of systematic empirical investigation or sustained theoretical development. Instead, its current evidential basis rests largely on anecdotal clinical observation, leaving fundamental questions unresolved regarding its conceptual status and suitability to be used as though it were a stable clinical construct.

For example, Ellison et al. describe “RISH” as a formulation-based conceptualisation rather than a diagnosis, and note that it may occur in isolation or alongside other diagnoses [15], yet it remains unclear how the construct is intended to relate to existing diagnostic categories in clinical and organisational terms [27, 28]. It is uncertain whether “RISH” is being used as a transdiagnostic formulation, a descriptive label, a service-orienting framework, or a putatively distinct construct. This ambiguity may have important consequences for referral pathways, service ownership, commissioning responsibility, and access to established evidence-based eating disorder treatments. Moreover, whilst the development of “RISH” has involved people with lived experience, their representativeness, number, and role have been poorly specified [14–16]. This raises epistemic concerns about who is positioned as an authoritative knower in the construction of new clinical categories, and whose interpretations of restrictive intake and self-harm are marginalised or excluded [13, 29, 30].

This paper offers a critical conceptual analysis of “RISH”, informed by empirical literature as well as clinical, ethical, and lived experience perspectives. Our aim is not only to examine the current evidence base for the construct, but also to analyse the conceptual assumptions, normative implications, and possible clinical consequences that may follow from its use. Our authorship represents a collaboration of people with lived experience, caregivers and supporters, clinicians, and researchers, reflecting the plurality of perspectives we argue should underpin the future development of eating disorder classification and care [13, 31]. We situate our analysis within broader debates on psychiatric naming and exclusion, by which we mean the ways in which diagnostic labels and classificatory language shape recognition, legitimacy, and access to care [32]. At the same time, we recognise that the development of “RISH” may reflect a genuine attempt to respond to clinical complexity, distress, and significant unmet need among people whose presentations are not always well served by existing approaches to eating disorder treatment. Our critique is therefore directed not at the intention to improve care, but at the conceptual, evidential, and practical risks of introducing a poorly specified construct into clinical discourse and service pathways.

The paper begins by situating “RISH” within the broader landscape of eating disorder classification, then draws on empirical literature and conceptual analysis to examine its conceptual ambiguity, possible clinical risks, ethical concerns, and epistemic implications, before concluding with recommendations for coproducing more credible ways of understanding and responding to those experiencing restrictive intake and self-harm.

RISH in the landscape of eating disorders

Restrictive eating behaviours are well recognised within several established eating disorder diagnoses, including anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), and avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) [26, 27]. Each is defined in major diagnostic manuals through specific behavioural and cognitive criteria, with the inclusion of new diagnostic entities typically following an extensive process of research, peer review, and international consensus-building to meet accepted standards of reliability, validity, and clinical utility [33–35]. This process ensures that diagnostic expansion and refinement proceed cautiously, building on cumulative evidence and multidisciplinary consensus rather than individual interpretation. This is not to suggest that existing classificatory systems are neutral or unproblematic, nor that the broader history of diagnostic expansion in eating disorders has been uniformly beneficial—questions beyond the specific focus of this paper. Insofar as “RISH” is being introduced within existing clinical and service systems, it becomes necessary to ask whether it satisfies the standards those systems ordinarily claim to require.

“RISH” unsettles these established nosological boundaries by reinterpreting restrictive intake predominantly as an intentional act of harm on the body rather than as a manifestation of eating disorder psychopathology [14–16]. To date, there is no empirical evidence supporting the existence of such a discrete population, nor any longitudinal or neurobiological findings suggesting distinct mechanisms. Instead, contemporary research situates restrictive intake within the heterogeneity of established eating disorder and disordered-eating presentations, including where these co-occur with self-harm. For example, a recent systematic review of adolescents and young adults demonstrated that restrictive eating and non-suicidal self-injury occur in diverse and overlapping patterns, with restriction embedded within broader disordered-eating profiles rather than delineating a distinct subgroup [21]. Likewise, a large qualitative evidence synthesis showed substantial functional overlap between eating disorder behaviours and self-harm, locating restriction within a wide and heterogeneous eating disorder landscape rather than supporting a novel self-harm category [22]. Claudino & Hay (2024) likewise argue for broadening existing diagnostic descriptions before proliferating new disorders [36]. Taken together, these findings suggest that “RISH” may be understood not as a data-driven development, but as a speculative reinterpretation introduced ahead of supporting research findings.

Such departures from evidence-based nosology are not unprecedented in the field of eating disorders. For example, the “SEED” construct was designed with the benevolent intention of acknowledging and supporting the needs of those with longstanding illnesses [1, 2]. However, the label was developed without systematic evaluation of its validity and prognostic value, and, to date, no agreed definitions of either the “severe” or “enduring” dimensions have been identified [37]. As such, “SEED” has been described as a “pseudo-diagnostic” label [38, 39], used as justification for creating separate care pathways which often deprioritise behaviour change (such as weight restoration) within the context of poorly-resourced and overstretched services. [11, 12, 40, 41]

Similarly, “SEED” was also formulated without meaningful involvement of people with lived experience, despite the direct implications for their sense of personal identity, treatment access, and experiences of care [5, 6, 11–13]. Whilst some patients find the label validating [42], many have reported feeling unhelpfully categorised rather than recognised [5]. Concerns about “SEED”’s association with prognostic pessimism and reduced access to treatment have also been shared by caregivers and clinicians in published literature [5, 40, 43]. The absence of coproduction in its creation has been identified as an example of epistemic injustice, i.e. the systematic exclusion or devaluation of lived experience as a credible form of knowledge. [6, 13, 29, 30]

Similar concerns have been raised in relation to “terminal anorexia,” a hypothecated diagnostic label proposed on the basis of only three case studies and arbitrary criteria [44], provoking international debate about the categorisation of despair, treatment withdrawal, and the justification of assisted dying where legal [7–11, 38]. Relatedly, the category of “AN-Plus” has been used to denote anorexia nervosa with additional complexity, often encompassing co-occurring conditions and "treatment resistance" [45]. While intended to capture clinical heterogeneity, AN-Plus has been similarly criticised for its potential to function as a way of legitimising the impossibility of effective treatment provision rather than offering constructive guidance for care [6].

Together, these examples illustrate how poorly-validated classificatory expansion may shape recognition, identity, and access to care. In the sections that follow, we develop a conceptual and ethical analysis of the “RISH” framework specifically and the implications it may have for clinical practice.

Part 1: Conceptual and ethical challenges

Conceptual ambiguity

“RISH” brings together phenomena that have traditionally been distinguished within clinical and research contexts: eating disorder behaviours and non-suicidal self-injury. While empirical overlaps between eating disorders and self-harm are well recognised [21–26], these phenomena are neither synonymous nor interchangeable. Diagnostic precision matters here not as an abstract classificatory exercise, but because how behaviours are conceptualised shapes research agendas, clinical formulations, and service responses. When restrictive eating is reclassified as a form of self-harm, fundamental questions arise for which the “RISH” framework offers little clarity: how should clinicians distinguish between anorexia nervosa, ARFID, and “RISH”? Does “RISH” apply only in the absence of other eating disorder cognitions and behaviours, or does it reframe anorexia itself? Left unresolved, such questions risk introducing conceptual ambiguity that complicates, rather than illuminates, the phenomena under consideration. To describe restrictive intake as a form of self-harm “distinct from AN” (Ellison et al., 2025, p.1) is to ascribe intention, agency, and motivation to only one of the many reasons for dietary restriction (which may or may not align with the subjective understanding of those who experience such behaviours). For example, in three case studies described in Viljoen et al. (2024), a range of reasons are identified as contributing to the maintenance of dietary restriction for patients Lorna, Lyn, and Mollie [46]. These include restriction as a “method of self-destruction” (Lorna), to manage weight (Lorna, Lyn, Mollie), to regain control (Lorna, Lyn, Mollie), to cope with life (Lyn, Mollie), to purify herself from past trauma (Lorna), to deal with voices (Lorna), to regulate emotions (Lorna, Lyn), to manage interpersonal difficulties (Lyn), and to gain a sense of achievement (Lorna, Mollie, Lynn) [46]. These case studies highlight how restrictive eating can be driven and maintained by a range of factors across biopsychosocial domains, which may co-occur, overlap, and be difficult to differentiate from one another. They also reflect how existing eating disorder diagnoses and treatments are capable of encompassing and effectively responding to heterogenous presentations of restrictive eating, including those described within the “RISH” framework as involving, "self-harm, thoughts of dying by diet restriction and/or using diet restriction to manage difficult emotions and/or seeking care and connection and communicating psychological distress” (Ellison et al., 2025, p.3) [15]. By collapsing these diverse experiences into a self-harm framework, “RISH” risks erasing nuance by imposing a singular interpretive lens on phenomena (such as emotional dysregulation and difficulties communicating distress) that are well-established features of diagnosable eating disorders [47, 48].

Crucially, this framing risks misattributing psychological states that may arise as consequences of starvation as evidence of primary motivation or intent. A substantial literature documents the psychological, cognitive, and behavioural effects of starvation, including affective instability, interpersonal sensitivity, and changes in self-concept [49], which may increase vulnerability to self-harm and personality-based formulations independent of premorbid psychopathology. Relatedly, empirical studies demonstrate that non-suicidal self-injury and suicidal ideation frequently occur within established eating disorder diagnoses, with markers of nutritional compromise predicting elevated risk, rather than constituting a distinct behavioural syndrome [50]. These findings call into question the need for a separate classificatory category such as “RISH”, and challenge the reframing of starvation-mediated psychological sequelae as evidence of an alternative underlying psychopathology.

The predominantly psychological and relational interpretation of restrictive eating offered by “RISH” therefore introduces a conceptual imbalance that sits uneasily with the established norms of medical practice. Both the biopsychosocial model and evidence-based eating disorder treatment frameworks emphasise the inseparability of biological, psychological, and social determinants, and caution against privileging any single explanatory domain [51–56]. By centring restrictive eating primarily as an expression of unmet relational needs or emotional distress, the “RISH” framework risks reproducing concerns long articulated in the field: namely, that eating disorders are often conceptualised and treated in dualistic ways that do not sufficiently account for the interplay of biological vulnerability, illness chronicity, sociocultural context, and structural determinants of care. [12, 38, 40, 57–59]

Clinical displacement and enhanced risk

Positioning “RISH” outside of existing diagnostic frameworks for eating disorders also carries ethical risks, since it is within these established categories that treatment approaches are most fully developed. Current treatment guidelines already emphasise the importance of providing multidisciplinary eating disorder care that is tailored to the individual needs of patients [48–50], amongst which multiple, often overlapping drivers of restrictive eating are well-evidenced, including self-harm (as noted). This is not to deny the limitations of existing treatments in terms of their efficacy [60–63], availability/accessibility [64–66], and responsiveness to the needs of diverse and underrepresented populations [67–71]. Rather, our concern is that a poorly specified construct such as “RISH” may further displace people from specialist assessment, risk monitoring, and multidisciplinary care in a context where existing provision is already limited and uneven.

One particularly worrying dimension is the use of “positive risk-taking”. This is positioned as a central management principle within the “RISH” framework, which describes ways to reduce the responsiveness of services where clinicians deem that responses to patients' requests for help may reinforce their psychological dependency on providers and thus inadvertently worsen symptoms over time [15]. This approach directly contradicts the content of established methods for assessing and mitigating risk [52]. It may be especially dangerous given the well-established excess mortality associated with eating disorders characterised by restrictive intake [72, 73]. In a large-scale systematic review and meta-analysis of over 300,000 individuals with eating disorders, Semchishen et al. (2026) report an almost five-fold increase in all-cause mortality compared with the general population, with the highest risk observed in anorexia nervosa, driven by both natural and non-natural causes including markedly elevated suicide-related mortality [74]. Importantly, mortality risk increased with lower body mass index and the presence of psychiatric and medical comorbidities—salient factors in restrictive eating presentations that are less likely to be adequately monitored if diverted away from specialist eating disorder services.

Beyond mortality outcomes, restrictive eating is associated with a range of serious and potentially fatal physical complications that require specialist expertise to identify and manage [73]. For example, population-based cohort studies by Solmi et al. (2024) demonstrate that eating disorder–related electrolyte abnormalities are independently associated with adverse outcomes and death, even after adjustment for psychiatric comorbidity [61], highlighting the limits of non-specialist mental health care in managing medical risk.

Notable amongst the cases described by Viljoen et al. [46] is the presence of unrecognised neurodevelopmental differences—a well-documented phenomenon amongst those with eating disorders [75], many of whom experience incongruent explanations, misdiagnosis, and ongoing unmet clinical need [41, 43, 76, 77]. Similar processes of diagnostic overshadowing and misrecognition have been seen in case studies where gastrointestinal conditions such as gastroparesis (delayed stomach emptying) have been involved in the development and maintenance of restrictive dietary intake, where misdiagnosis and inaccurate explanations have contributed to significant adverse outcomes including death [58, 78]. Taken as illustrative examples, these cases suggest that those living with high levels of risk may also be disproportionately vulnerable to misclassification with “RISH”, resulting in exclusion, inappropriate care pathways, and harm [67–71].

Stigma and epistemic injustice

The framing of restrictive intake as self-harm also demands attention to stigma and cultural narratives. Self-harm has historically been laden with stigma, often pathologised as a manipulative or intractable behaviour [79–82]. Eating disorders, too, are frequently subject to cultural myths of vanity, choice, and resistance [13, 83, 84]. The convergence of these stigmas risks creating a doubly burdensome label: individuals with “RISH” may be viewed as both “resistant” eating disorder patients and “manipulative” self-harmers. As mentioned prior, such narratives have material consequences, shaping clinical attitudes, influencing funding priorities, and impacting the willingness of services to engage with certain patient groups [11–13].

These concerns are compounded by the absence of transparent methodological detail regarding the involvement of people with lived experience in the development of the “RISH” framework. While existing documents note that lived experience input was sought, there is limited clarity regarding who was involved, the diversity of experiences and perspectives represented, the role participants played, and how their contributions shaped the conceptualisation, language, and proposed clinical responses [15]. This lack of transparency is particularly striking given the broader movement within mental health research and policy towards more rigorous and explicit reporting of lived experience involvement, including attention to diversity, power-sharing, and the epistemic status accorded to experiential knowledge [30, 85, 86]. Increasingly, such involvement is understood not as optional consultation, but as an ethical and methodological obligation that can generate more relevant, acceptable, and effective interventions and care pathways [30, 31, 87].

Whilst these advances are important, the promise of coproduction is not automatic [30, 88]. Without clear standards, reflexivity, and accountability, involvement risks becoming symbolic rather than substantive [30, 88–91]. At its most problematic, poorly specified or tokenistic coproduction can reproduce the very epistemic injustices it purports to address: instrumentalising lived experience, extracting legitimacy without sharing authority, reinforcing dominant interpretive frameworks, and marginalising dissenting or uncomfortable accounts [13, 30, 92–94]. In such circumstances, the credibility and interpretive resources of groups already excluded from high-quality, accessible, and inclusive care may be further undermined rather than strengthened. In the case of “RISH,” the absence of detail regarding the nature, scope, and impact of lived experience involvement makes it impossible to assess whether coproduction functioned as a genuine corrective to epistemic imbalance or as a procedural gesture without substantive influence.

Obscuring systemic failures in care

The emergence of “RISH” cannot be considered without an appreciation of the wider structural pressures on eating disorder services. Chronic underfunding [40, 95], insufficient staff training [96, 97], and regional inequities [98] are pervasive features of what has been described as the “resource-starved ecosystem” in which eating disorder care is delivered [12]. In the UK context, the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman’s 2017 report, “Ignoring the Alarms: How NHS Eating Disorder Services Are Failing Patients” [99], documented persistent failures in recognition, coordination, and timely intervention. The majority of these problems remain largely unaddressed many years later [40, 100, 101].

As critics of labels such as “SEED” have previously argued, classificatory expansion can function as a displacement activity, redirecting attention away from structural deficiencies and towards ostensibly novel patient categories [5, 11, 12, 38]. When services are unable to meet demand, new diagnostic framings may offer a linguistic solution to a practical problem, allowing systems to manage risk, ration care, or justify exclusion without explicitly acknowledging the limits of provision [5, 13]. This dynamic has been linked to forms of moral injury among clinicians working within constrained systems, who are required to reconcile professional values with organisational realities that prevent the delivery of adequate care [11, 102]. In this context, the development of “RISH” may be read not only as a clinical hypothesis but as a response to systemic pressure, in which institutional incapacity is subtly reframed as a feature of the patient.

Such reframing risks individualising and pathologising the downstream consequences of unmet need. Many of the psychosocial features emphasised within the “RISH” framework (including emotional dysregulation and difficulties articulating distress and communicating need) can arise as consequences of prolonged illness, repeated service exclusion, and iatrogenic harm within eating disorder care [39, 41, 43, 58, 100, 103]. Rather than signalling a distinct form of psychopathology, these presentations may reflect adaptive responses to environments in which patients have learned, through experience, that verbal, measured expressions of distress can be minimised, misinterpreted, and generally insufficient to secure support. By locating these affective and relational phenomena primarily within the individual, the systemic conditions and forms of harm that produce and sustain them may be overlooked.

Finally, the ambiguity surrounding the status and function of “RISH” within clinician-facing publications and documentation carrying the NHS logo further compounds these risks. The materials in which the term appears are variably described as “practice considerations for the management of RISH”, and “decision-making tools,” [14–16] yet they do not meet the standards typically associated with formal clinical guidance, including transparent methodology, systematic evidence appraisal, or clear governance arrangements [104–106]. This lack of clarity raises important questions about how such tools are intended to be used, by whom, and with what degree of authority. When speculative constructs are embedded within decision-support mechanisms without robust evidential grounding, they risk acquiring de facto regulatory power, shaping clinical decisions in ways that are neither empirically justified nor ethically robust.

This concern is amplified by emerging anecdotal reports that individuals labelled with “RISH” are being discharged from specialist eating disorder services, denied further treatment, or redirected to care pathways lacking an established evidence base. While systematic data on such outcomes are not yet available, the potential for clinical displacement is ethically significant given the risks associated with restrictive eating outlined above. Where a new category operates to legitimise withdrawal of care, particularly for individuals already at risk of marginalisation within services, it reproduces a familiar pattern seen with earlier constructs such as “SEED” and “terminal anorexia,” in which diagnostic pessimism becomes entangled with resource limitation [11]. This outcome sits in direct tension with the stated purpose of the “RISH” framework itself, which is explicitly presented as a means of improving recognition and “support[ing] treatment access” rather than constraining or curtailing it (Ellison et al., 2025, p.1). [15]

Part 2: Recommendations for research, policy, and practice

The challenges we have outlined concerning “RISH” do not arise from a failure to recognise unmet need, but from a misidentification of where that need is located and how it should be addressed. There is little dispute regarding the substantial body of evidence demonstrating how current service provision often struggles to respond adequately to people who restrict dietary intake in the context of complex psychological, relational, and social distress.

The central difficulty, however, lies in the implicit assumption that improving recognition or access to care depends upon the introduction of a new diagnostic or classificatory category. In the absence of systematic empirical investigation, validation, and transparent coproduction, terminological expansion risks mischaracterising service-level and structural problems as diagnostic ones. Addressing unmet need is therefore unlikely to be achieved through reclassification alone, and instead requires sustained attention to the organisational, evidential, and epistemic conditions that shape service provision and clinical decision-making.

Progress therefore requires action across the following domains:

An empirical approach to nosology and trustworthy clinical guidelines

Any diagnostic construct proposed to inform clinical reasoning, service pathways, or treatment decisions should be subject to the same standards of empirical scrutiny required for inclusion in major classificatory systems such as the ICD-11 or DSM-5 [27, 28]. For “RISH,” this would entail the coproduction of clearly specified operational criteria, formal assessment of reliability and construct validity, and evidence of distinction from established eating disorders and relevant differential diagnoses, informed by data-driven and dimensional approaches to restrictive eating and self-injurious behaviour [107–110].

In parallel, clarity is required regarding the status and intended function of documents that reference or operationalise “RISH.” Where materials are positioned as informing clinical decision-making, they should meet established standards for trustworthy clinical guidance. Methodological frameworks for guideline development emphasise the need for transparent scope definition, explicit clinical questions, systematic and up-to-date evidence synthesis, clear articulation of recommendation strength, and appropriate management of conflicts of interest [111–113]. In “How to recognize a trustworthy clinical practice guideline”, Lima et al. (2023) highlight the importance of including patient-important outcomes, considering proportionality between evidential certainty and recommendation strength, and paying explicit attention to applicability across settings and populations [114].

Adopting these standards prospectively would help ensure that any future guidance related to restrictive eating and self-harm is empirically grounded, methodologically transparent, and proportionate to the strength of the available evidence, whilst reducing the risk that provisional frameworks acquire unintended normative authority in clinical decision-making.

Strengthening clinical practice

Improving diagnostic accuracy depends on clinicians being able to recognise the many and varied pathways into restrictive eating. Training should therefore equip healthcare professionals with skills in differential diagnosis, diagnostic overshadowing, and the impact of co-occurring physical, psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions in creating individualised, collaborative formulations that take account for the diverse ways in which restriction manifests and may be treated across biopsychosocial domains.

There is growing evidence that integrated, autism-informed, and multidisciplinary care models can improve outcomes when implemented with appropriate intensity and continuity. For example, The PEACE Pathway, developed by Tchanturia and colleagues, demonstrates that adapting treatment environments, communication styles, and care planning for neurodivergent individuals enhances engagement, reduces distress, and increases the likelihood of treatment completion [77, 114, 115].

Similarly, integrated multidisciplinary models of treatment, such as Integrative Cognitive Behavioural Treatment for Eating Disorders (I-CBTE), show that coordinated, formulation-driven approaches produce better physical and psychological outcomes, even for those with longstanding and severe illnesses [46, 54, 56]. This demonstrates how services that are adequately resourced, equipped with specialist training, and organised around shared, individualised formulations can effectively treat multiple presentations of restriction ‘in-house’, thereby significantly reducing the risks of clinical displacement and subsequent harm.

Because restrictive intake and self-harm occur across a wide range of medical and psychiatric contexts, clinicians must undertake careful differential diagnosis while also recognising that these presentations are frequently overlapping, comorbid, and fluid over time. Trauma histories, neurodevelopmental differences, chronic illnesses, and other co-occurring conditions are common within eating disorder populations and should not be taken to imply that an eating disorder is therefore absent, secondary, or outside the remit of specialist assessment and treatment. Rather than sorting individuals into neat and mutually exclusive categories, complexity-oriented assessment requires clinicians to hold multiple possibilities in view simultaneously and to develop collaborative formulations that can account for interacting biological, psychological, and social drivers over time. Addressing systemic gaps in care

A credible response to these challenges requires investment in the infrastructure of care, with progress being achieved not by the proliferation of new categories but by demonstrable improvements in the accessibility, consistency, and quality of eating disorder care. Commissioning frameworks must be resourced to provide treatment continuity across settings, reduce health inequalities for marginalised groups, and address unacceptable levels of preventable harm that exist across eating disorder diagnoses, including mortality [31, 43, 73, 99, 101]. The integration of specialist eating disorder training within medical and mental health education can be achieved with structural changes to curricula and adequate funding [96, 97].

Within existing clinical services, time and resource needs to be allocated to embedding reflective practice and clinical supervision as central components of ethical caregiving. This can help providers identify cognitive biases, areas where they may feel “stuck” [116, 117], and tensions between their aspirations for treatment provision and the structural conditions that may need to change in order to fulfil them. [11, 100]

Sustained support for eating disorder research is also essential, particularly longitudinal and inclusive studies capable of capturing the heterogeneity of illness trajectories and treatment experiences across diagnostic and demographic groups [69, 118, 119]. Eating disorder research has been characterised as overly niche, limiting knowledge exchange, meaning greater collaboration across disciplines is required [120–122].

Embedding meaningful coproduction

Future frameworks must be developed through transparent processes of coproduction. This requires clear reporting of how lived experience involvement was undertaken, including who was involved, the diversity and representativeness of contributors, the stages at which they contributed, the methods used, the degree of influence contributors held, and how their input shaped the resulting framework or recommendations. Such expectations are consistent with guidance for reporting the involvement of patients and the public (such as the GRIPP2 reporting checklist) [123] and with more recent mental health-specific guidance on reporting lived experience engagement [124, 125].

Coproduction must also be substantive and equitable, which means moving beyond consultation to treat lived experience as expertise in its own right [30, 90, 91]. Structured, supported methodologies for achieving this include deliberative workshops, participatory research programmes, and jointly developing clinical resources and components of care based in dialogue between a diverse range of patients, carers, and professionals [30]. This may include welcoming perspectives that challenge established clinical models [126, 127], such as feminist and sociocultural critiques of pathologisation, as well as perspectives that support strengths-based, recovery-oriented, or non-pathologising responses in relevant contexts. The value of coproduction here lies not in resolving all tensions [13], but in ensuring that new terminology and care pathways are developed with sufficient shared scrutiny to remain grounded in evidence while staying open to the plurality of meanings that restrictive eating and distress may hold for individuals who experience them.

The eating disorder field has already seen examples of how coproduction can support tangible improvements in treatment engagement, continuity, and outcomes, for example in projects focussing on transitions in eating disorder care and interventions to support parents and caregivers [128, 129]. Building on these precedents, the field should invest in creating durable infrastructure for coproduction that will enable shared authority over the development of terminology, the piloting and refinement of care pathways, and the evaluation of outcomes, with people with lived experience occupying leadership roles throughout these processes.

Normative ethical principles, risks, and required safeguards in the context of “RISH”

Non maleficence

The obligation to avoid causing harm and to prevent foreseeable risks arising from clinical decisions.

“RISH” increases the risk of harm by encouraging misclassification of restrictive intake as a new construct rather than investigating medical, psychiatric, or eating disorder causes.

This may lead to inadequate monitoring of physical risk, missed diagnoses, inappropriate diversion to general mental health services, and unsafe assumptions about capacity and intent.

Require a thorough, comprehensive assessment that considers differential diagnosis and physical risk before applying any interpretive framework.

Ensure that refusal of food or rapid weight loss triggers risk-based monitoring.

Avoid reassigning patients to non-specialist teams when malnutrition or diagnostic uncertainty are present.

Implement regular clinical review to prevent drift away from evidence-based care.

Beneficence

The obligation to act in the person’s best interests by providing interventions that are effective, evidence-based, and proportionate to need.

“RISH” may lead clinicians to pursue interventions focused on generic self-harm management rather than evidence-based eating disorder care.

This undermines best interest practice when malnutrition, starvation-driven cognitive change, or post-viral dysmotility are present.

Ensure that eating disorder pathways remain the primary route for any presentation involving sustained restriction.

Provide integrated, multidisciplinary care that aligns with the individual’s formulation.

Review outcomes regularly to ensure care is producing benefit.

Autonomy and capacity

Respect for the person’s agency, decision-making ability, and right to participate meaningfully in treatment planning.

Mislabeling restrictive intake as “RISH” may distort assessment of capacity and intention, implying deliberate self-injury when the person may lack the cognitive, emotional, or nutritional stability needed for autonomous decision-making.

This may also reduce access to collaborative formulation and shared decision-making when pathways assume risk management rather than understanding.

Positive risk-taking is described as a central management principle within “RISH”, which lacks empirical evidence and contradicts established methods for assessing and mitigating risk.

Require formal capacity assessments when there is doubt.

Use collaborative, formulation-based approaches as routine practice.

Avoid attributing singular motives to restriction.

Support the involvement of carers and supporters where appropriate.

Ensure that service pathways do not override autonomy by prematurely assigning a self-harm framework.

In restrictive eating disorders, autonomy must be calibrated against the well-established cognitive, affective, and mortality risks.

Justice

Fair and equitable access to appropriate, safe, and effective care, irrespective of diagnosis, demographic factors, or complexity.

Creating a new category outside established eating disorder diagnoses risks unequal access to specialist care, especially for marginalised groups who already experience barriers.

“RISH” may therefore function as a rationing tool when services are stretched, reinforcing geographical and service level inequities.

Ensure equal access to specialist eating disorder assessment for all forms of restriction.

Commission integrated services that can manage complexity without exclusion.

Audit service outcomes to identify patterns of displacement or inequitable pathways.

Implement explicit safeguards to prevent the use of novel terminology as a gatekeeping mechanism.

Epistemic justice

The fair valuing and inclusion of lived experience knowledge.

This is undermined by denying credibility (testimonial injustice) or by imposing interpretive frameworks that do not reflect the person’s experience (hermeneutic injustice).

“RISH” embeds a clinician-defined narrative of restriction as self-harm, regardless of whether the individual experiences it that way. It is introduced without meaningful coproduction.

This risks erasing the plurality of reasons for restriction, especially among neurodivergent individuals, people with trauma histories, and those with complex medical presentations. It may silence or pathologise individuals whose own accounts differ from the imposed framework.

Embed coproduction at all stages of terminology development, pathway design, and policy guidance.

Prioritise lived experience within clinical assessment, not as an adjunct but as core information.

Provide structured opportunities for people to articulate the meanings, functions, and contexts of their restrictive eating.

Avoid interpretive closure within services and support clinicians to work with uncertainty.

Fidelity and trustworthiness

Maintaining trust by providing care that is consistent, transparent, and grounded in recognised clinical standards.

Use transparent communication about uncertainty and diagnosis.

Provide honest discussion of the likely benefits, limitations, and possible harms of treatment options, so that patients and carers can participate meaningfully in informed and collaborative decision-making.

Adhere to recognised guidelines for assessment and risk management.

Maintain continuity of care and avoid abrupt changes in pathway based on speculative terminology.

Conclusion

The proposal of “Restrictive Intake Self-Harm” exemplifies a recurring pattern in the eating disorder field: the creation of new categories to address clinical difficulties that, in practice, risk reinforcing exclusion and narrowing care. By reframing restriction as self-harm, “RISH” conflates distinct phenomena, overlooks systemic contexts, and imposes unvalidated interpretive frameworks that may not be clinically helpful for those most affected. Introduced with insufficiently rigorous coproduction, it perpetuates epistemic injustice and highlights broader cultural tendencies to prioritise patient-level labelling over structural reform.

To avoid repeating cycles of substitutional naming, progress must be grounded in empirical evidence, clinical skill, and shared authority. Investment should therefore focus on strengthening nosological research, improving skills in differential diagnosis and individualised formulation, and ensuring comprehensive training for clinicians across settings. Systemic barriers such as under-resourcing and fragmented treatment provision must be addressed directly, rather than displaced through new terminology. Crucially, language, diagnostic guidance, and care pathways should be meaningfully coproduced with patients and caregivers, embedding plurality and ethical accountability throughout. By centring evidence, collaboration, and justice, the field can move beyond speculative constructs and focus more intently on the practical imperative of delivering more consistent and effective care.
