ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesKey terms describing the activity status and clinical outcomes of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) lack standardized definitions. To address the need for uniform definitions, we sought to develop formal consensus-based terminology to define key aspects pertinent to the management of CIDP.MethodsIn April 2025, the GBS|CIDP Foundation International convened a Task Force of 17 international CIDP experts, 2 guest experts from related specialties, and 3 patient representatives. Task Force panels iteratively reviewed, discussed, and voted on proposed definitions for No Evidence of Disease Activity, Relapse, Response, Refractory, Remission, and Residual Symptoms. Relevant literature was reviewed to inform each definition. A modified Delphi approach was used to achieve consensus, defined as a median rating ≥7 on a 9-point scale with ≥80% agreement. Voting included 17 content experts and 3 patient representatives. Guest experts provided nonvoting input.ResultsComposite metrics incorporating disability, strength impairment, and patient perception were determined to provide the most sensitive and specific assessment of clinical change in CIDP. Using commonly used clinical outcome measures, including minimally clinically important differences where available, unique definitions were developed for each clinical term. Definitions for No Evidence of Disease Activity, Relapse, Response (minimal, partial, and optimal), Refractory, Remission, and Residual Symptoms were iteratively refined to integrate patient-reported experiences, standardized disability scores, and objective measures of strength impairment, ensuring that each term captured a multidimensional view of disease status.DiscussionThe development of consensus-based definitions for key clinical terms in CIDP addresses a longstanding gap in standardizing the assessment of disease activity and treatment outcomes. By combining patient-reported experiences with objective disability and strength measures, the Task Force created comprehensive definitions reflecting the patient experience. The iterative Delphi process ensured broad expert agreement while allowing patient perspectives to inform the terminology. These standardized definitions may improve consistent evaluations in clinical practice, facilitate communication among healthcare providers, and support more robust design and interpretation of clinical trials. Furthermore, the incorporation of composite metrics sensitive to meaningful clinical changes may enhance the ability to detect treatment effects and disease progression, ultimately promoting more precise and patient-centered management of CIDP.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is a group of immune-mediated peripheral nerve disorders with a prevalence of 1.0–8.9 persons per 100,000.1 The syndrome is clinically heterogeneous, with 60%–80% of patients manifesting the “typical” clinical features of progressive or relapsing, relatively symmetrical, proximal and distal, motor and sensory findings evolving over at least 8 weeks.2 The remaining 20%–40% of patients present with regional or modality-restricted manifestations, corresponding to the recognized distal, multifocal, focal, motor, and sensory CIDP variants.3 The precise immunobiological basis for typical CIDP or the variants is unknown, and the syndrome is without a reliable diagnostic biomarker.3

Evidence-based treatments for CIDP include corticosteroids, immunoglobulins, plasma exchange, and FcRn antagonist therapies.3,4 Just as there is no diagnostic biomarker for CIDP, there are no validated biomarkers to measure disease activity or treatment response. In clinical practice, therapeutic benefit is often assumed based on patient-reported improvement, which may not be supported by objective neurologic findings. It has been observed that up to 85% of patients misdiagnosed and treated as CIDP report subjective improvement with immunotherapy, but only rarely in the misdiagnosed patient is there objective improvement on validated measures.5 When objective measures of disability have been applied in randomized controlled trials, 37%–69% of patients do not relapse when randomized to placebo,6,7 suggesting that subjective experiences may be misleading and drive over utilization of immunotherapy in clinical practice. These observations highlight the critical need for well-defined clinical metrics to assess treatment effectiveness and infer immunologic disease activity.8

The key terms used to describe the activity state of CIDP have not been uniformly defined. What it means to “respond” or “relapse” has been left to the discretion of individual clinicians or by boundaries embedded within individual clinical trials. Similarly, “refractory to treatment,” “remission,” and “residual deficits” are also without uniform definitions. Each definition hinges, to some extent, on the likelihood that the disease is immunologically active or not. Residual deficits pose a particular challenge in assessing disease activity. Neurologic impairments presumed to be irreversible occur in 66%–88% of patients with CIDP9,10; yet, the extent to which these deficits are truly permanent remains uncertain.

The objective of this Task Force was to consider the evidence that provides insight into the disease activity status of CIDP9 and to use that evidence to develop consensus definitions for key terms routinely used during clinical care and clinical trials. These definitions aim to establish a unified terminology for patients, clinicians, and clinical trialists to enhance the management and evaluation of CIDP.

Workshop Methodology

The consensus-building workshop was held from April 25 to 27, 2025, in Boston, MA. Participants included a Task Force of 17 international CIDP experts (2 participated remotely) and 3 representatives from the patient community. Two invited guest experts represented the myasthenia gravis (MG) and multiple sclerosis (MS) research communities. The panel was chosen by GBS|CIDP Foundation International leadership to represent the breadth of knowledge and a wide variety of opinions from CIDP experts internationally.

Before the consensus workshop, a patient workshop was held in Conshohocken, PA, with 16 attendees from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Italy. Attendees were selected by GBS|CIDP Foundation patient liaisons based upon their experience with the condition, diversity of perspectives, and ability to provide meaningful input for the workshop. Patients shared their experiences through structured activities and discussed how the proposed terms applied to their journey. Qualitative data were summarized and presented to the Task Force.

The Task Force used a modified Delphi approach with structured voting to reach consensus on each term. The methodology was informed by similar consensus efforts in MG11 and MS.12,13 A formal systematic review of the literature was not performed. The Task Force steering committee (J.A.A., F.E., L.Q., C.S.) reviewed and summarized literature cited in international CIDP guidelines, supplemented by other literature from clinical trials and cohort studies published after completion of the European Academy of Neurology (EAN) and Peripheral Nerve Society (PNS) 2021 CIDP guideline.3 For each definition, an iterative process was conducted until the voters reached consensus. Steering committee members formulated initial guidance definitions based on literature review and presented the definition and relevant literature to the Task Force. After discussion, an updated definition was developed and voted upon. Definitions that did not reach consensus were revised and subjected to further discussion and voting. Participants rated agreement using a 9-point scale similar to the MG practice recommendations case study (Figure 1); agreement was defined as a median score ≥7 and consensus as ≥80% of ratings ≥7. Voting members included all content experts (17) and patient representatives (3). The 2 invited guest experts did not vote.

Developing CIDP Definitions: Key Evidence Considered

Evidence-Based Measures to Monitor Clinical Status in CIDP

The EAN/PNS CIDP Guideline highlights measurement domains considered important to assess disease status in CIDP.3,14 Disability metrics with the strongest level of data in CIDP are the Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause and Treatment (INCAT) disability scale15 and the Inflammatory Rasch-built Overall Disability Scale (I-RODS) disability scale.16 Impairment metrics with the strongest level of data are the Medical Research Council (MRC) 60 point sum score (MRC-SS)17 and hand held grip strength dynamometry.18 Minimum clinically important differences (MCIDs) for both disability and impairment measures have been established and are routinely applied in clinical trials, often with practical adjustments to enhance feasibility (Table 1).6,15-22 The MCID for worsening and improvement has subsequently been included in the EAN/PNS guideline3 as a target to be considered when using each metric in clinical practice.

Studies comparing the relative sensitivity and specificity of each metric to detect change provide a mixed picture. INCAT and MRC-SS, for example, are insensitive to change in patients' with mild disease.23 In a study comparing grip strength to INCAT, both metrics detected treatment response at 6 weeks, but only grip strength was sensitive enough to capture change at 2 and 3 weeks.24 In newly diagnosed patients initiating treatment and in relapsing patients off therapy, I-RODS is more sensitive than INCAT as an assessment metric.19 However, enhanced sensitivity frequently comes at the cost of specificity, a limitation that is further exacerbated when relying on a single metric to define an outcome.

In a post hoc analysis of a randomized IV immunoglobulin (IVIg) withdrawal trial, it was observed that lower MCID thresholds enhanced sensitivity to detect deterioration, while higher thresholds favored specificity.8,25 The methodology of this trial which randomized patients on IVIG to continued IVIG or placebo does not match the real-world situation, and hence, the determined sensitivity/specificity profiles require cautious interpretation when extrapolating to routine clinical practice. However, an important observation was that detection of deterioration was most effective when outcomes were evaluated in combination. The best results were observed when an I-RODS 4-point change was combined with either grip strength (8 or 14 kPa change) or MRC-SS (2 points).25 Similar results supporting the value of combined outcomes assessment were observed in a retrospective longitudinal study of 85 patients with CIDP.26

Taken together, these studies indicate that current MCIDs may have suboptimal sensitivity and specificity for detecting clinical deterioration or improvement when applied in isolation. These properties limit the usefulness of the metrics on an individual patient level. In a clinically and biologically heterogeneous disease such as CIDP, this finding is not surprising. Combining concordant metrics from multiple domains may enhance the balance between sensitivity and specificity.

Incorporating the Patient Voice and Feedback From the CIDP Patient and Care Partner Workshop

Although the pitfalls of relying solely on patient perception should not be overlooked,5 if a patient's perception of their disease status does not change, then the meaning of change on a disability or impairment metric is questionable. Indeed, when determining MCID, anchoring a patient's impression of change to an outcome is one method to determine if a change in a metric is clinically meaningful.

The Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) is a self-reported measure that can provide structure to the subjective assessment of a patient's overall impression of improvement or decline.27 Participants rate how they feel compared with a prior visit or evaluation before a treatment change was made. Ratings are provided on a 7-point scale from +3 (very much improved) to −3 (very much worsened). Although not specifically studied in CIDP, ratings of “much” (±2) or “very much” (±3) change reflect patient-perceived meaningful change, whereas “minimal” or “no change” are of less certain clinical importance.28 Considering that MCIDs were designed to reflect group-level changes, incorporating PGIC into the routine assessment of clinical status may clarify the interpretation of MCID in individual patients.29

Patient workshops held before the Task Force assembly highlighted several key priorities for defining CIDP-related outcomes. Patients emphasized the need for definitions of treatment response and relapse that better reflect lived experience. They advocated for a therapy-specific definition of refractoriness, emphasizing that “refractory” should describe a specific treatment failure, rather than implying that refractoriness is a patient feature that influences response to any treatment. In addition, patients called for clearer distinctions between remission and residual symptoms. Based on this input, the Task Force recognized the importance of incorporating PGIC into CIDP outcome and disease activity definitions.

Lessons Learned From Other Disease States

Within the oncology community the term “No Evidence of Disease” or NED is used to describe the absence of detectable cancer after treatment. Although NED does not confirm that the disease is entirely cured (cancer cells may be present but undetectable), it does indicate that testing shows no signs of cancer. The components that comprise NED vary depending on the specific cancer but are typically a combination of symptoms, imaging, biomarkers, and sometimes patient-reported outcomes.30,31 Although not obviating the need for ongoing surveillance, achieving NED is a significant milestone and a helpful prognostic indicator. NED is also recognized as a clinically meaningful outcome in oncology trials, especially when evaluating complete response, long-term remission, and potential cure.

The MS community has also recognized the importance of outcome measures that comprehensively capture therapeutic success and hence has developed a version of NED to meet the needs of patients with MS. The impetus for this concept in MS has been driven, in part, by the rapid emergence of new and more effective disease-modifying therapies. “No Evidence of Disease Activity” (NEDA) applies a framework analogous to NED but incorporates specific components chosen as surrogate markers of neuroinflammation and/or neurodegeneration.32 In the most widely used NEDA score (NEDA-3), 3 components must be satisfied to be confident that no disease activity is present: no clinical relapses, no sustained disability progression (defined by the Expanded Disability Status Scale score), and no activity on magnetic resonance imaging during a time period of usually 3–12 months.33 NEDA-3 is now used in clinical practice and trials as a standardized approach to evaluate treatment efficacy, monitor relapse, guide therapy decisions, predict outcomes, and support clear communication with patients.34

As with MS, the MG community has witnessed a profound growth in proven effective immunotherapies and expansion of clinical trials. Recognizing the ambiguity with which clinicians, patients and clinical trialists define treatment success, a Task Force of the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America developed consensus definitions designed to target postintervention status, including remission (on and off immunotherapy), minimal manifestations, improvement, unchanged, worsening, and exacerbation.35 These definitions have evolved to explicitly incorporate treatment goals, emphasizing attainment of minimal manifestation status or better.11 They are regarded as essential in clinical practice and trials, as they provide a unified terminology to define a clinically meaningful threshold of disease control.

Using Adjacent Disease States to Develop CIDP Definitions

In contrast to oncology and MS, CIDP currently lacks reliable imaging or biomarker tools capable of accurately detecting disease activity. Unlike MG, CIDP is more often progressive and associated with irreversible neurologic impairments. The Task Force considered the NEDA framework in MS to be a useful model for CIDP, adapted to use combined metrics of impairment and disability most appropriate to assess clinical status in CIDP. Although advances in CIDP biomarkers have increased their use in clinical trials, they currently provide limited guidance for individualized management and are not widely accessible to most patients. Consequently, the Task Force opted not to include them in the proposed CIDP definitions, while simultaneously acknowledging that the NEDA structure allows for future incorporation of biomarkers as they become available.

Goals of Treatment: Freedom of Disease Activity

Treatment goals in CIDP include (1) improve CIDP-related symptoms, (2) maximize functional abilities, and (3) optimize quality of life. These goals apply both in the short term, after initiation of immunotherapy when clinical improvement is prioritized, and in the long term when prevention of disability accumulation becomes the primary objective. Two important concepts are considered when defining these goals. First, while immunotherapies may be neuroprotective by shielding peripheral nerves from immune-mediated injury, they are not directly neuro-reparative. Second, given the frequent occurrence of irreversible nerve injury and the lack of a measure to quantify irreversible damage, an individual patient's capacity for recovery is inherently uncertain, especially in the short term. With these considerations in mind, the goal of CIDP treatment shifts from an exclusive focus on improvement in the induction phase or prevention of worsening in the maintenance phase to durable suppression of immunologic disease activity. Since biomarkers informing on the inflammatory activity status in CIDP are lacking, surrogates of inflammatory activity become essential when assessing the freedom of disease activity goal. Treatment response definitions need to consider the continuum of the treatment response, where treatment responses may be completely or partially effective, and must also use periodic reassessment of clinical status with validated scores to ensure that freedom of disease activity is sustained over time. The freedom of disease activity goal may be achieved with the use of immunotherapy and may be sustained with or without ongoing immunotherapy (about 30% of patients with CIDP maintain their best possible clinical status without chronic immunotherapy).9 In either case, if freedom of disease activity is reached and maintained, symptoms, functional status, and quality of life may improve toward the maximum level attainable for a given individual.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This consensus analysis did not involve human participants, experimental interventions, or patient data; therefore, no approvals, registrations, or consents were required.

The concepts of NEDA and EDA are proposed to directly address the primary treatment goal: arresting immune-mediated disease activity in CIDP. Although the overarching goal is freedom from disease activity, it is recognized that no biomarker currently exists that can reliably make this determination; therefore, disease activity status is assessed using composite changes in established measures of impairment and disability. The Task Force recommends that these classifications be qualified by treatment status (for example, “NEDA-2 on treatment” or “NEDA-2 off treatment”). As NEDA-2 provides the highest level of confidence that disease activity has been arrested, the recommended NEDA status to be targeted in clinical trials is NEDA-2. The Task Force cautions interpreting changes (for the better or worse) in disability and/or impairment when no patient-reported change is present. Such findings should be assessed on a case-by-case basis using the clinician's best judgment to determine if the disability/impairment changes are clinically meaningful. A minimum change of 1 point on the PGIC was permitted to establish patient perception of worsening.28 Although a 1-point change by itself may have uncertain clinical significance, the Task Force elected to maximize sensitivity for detecting clinical change by considering any patient-perceived change as meaningful provided it was supported by a change in disability or impairment.

The Task Force recommends the use of “minimal response” as evidence of an early partial response to a specific treatment, but cautions against interpreting “minimal response,” as defined by these parameters, as a uniformly satisfactory outcome. While this definition satisfies 1 treatment goal of improvement in CIDP-related symptoms, it does not necessarily achieve the goal of maximizing functional capacity. Accordingly, the Task Force encourages the pursuit of higher MCID targets (partial response) in clinical practice and during clinical trials (Table 3). Because it may not be feasible to achieve these higher levels of improvement within 12 weeks, a treatment period of up to 6 months after treatment initiation or modification may be needed.

The Task Force recognizes the challenges inherent in determining an “optimal response.” What is considered “optimal” varies substantially between individuals and cannot be known with certainty in a given patient. Accordingly, determination of an “optimal response” should be made on a case-by-case basis, incorporating clinical assessment of impairments and disabilities that are likely to be permanent and irreversible despite immunotherapy. Furthermore, the Task Force acknowledges that “optimal response” is not mutually exclusive with “response” regardless of which MCID threshold was used to establish the response. In some circumstances, an “optimal response” may be appropriately defined by minimal improvement, whereas in other patients, a greater capacity for recovery may be realized. The concept of “optimal response” is intended to unify the overarching goals of treatment: to mitigate disease activity and thereby enable the maximum improvement attainable for an individual patient.

The Task Force recommends a treatment-centered approach to “refractory,” such that “refractory” is connected to a specific therapy. Caution is advised against a broader interpretation of refractory, as refractory to one therapy should not preclude intervention with a different therapy provided the diagnosis of CIDP is reconfirmed and the EDA definition is met. The Task Force defers the definition of adequate treatment to those previously published in CIDP guidelines or clinical trials.36 Treatment duration of 3 months is typically sufficient to assess response, although in rare cases, clinical benefit may take longer to emerge. If no improvement is observed by 3–6 months, benefit thereafter is unlikely and it would be appropriate to consider the patient “refractory” in most circumstances.

The Task Force emphasizes that “remission” is an estimation of immunologic activity, rather than neurologic impairment.

Discussion

A diverse coalition comprising of CIDP content experts, patient representatives with lived experience, and external specialists was convened to develop standardized terminology for CIDP. Consensus on the key definitions “No Evidence of Disease Activity,” “Relapse,” “Response,” “Refractory,” “Remission,” and “Residual symptoms” was achieved through a structured modified Delphi process. These definitions build on existing guidelines3 and classification schemes,9 informed by a comprehensive literature review and supplemented by expert input.

The definitions are intended to guide disease monitoring during clinical management and within clinical trials (Table 4, Figure 2) and may provide a standardized terminology to support clear and consistent dialogue between patients and clinicians. As a living document, these recommendations will require updates in response to evolving evidence and continued input from patient, clinical, and research communities to ensure their relevance and applicability.

To capture CIDP-related disease activity, we rely on changes in outcomes with the strongest evidence for clinically meaningful change: patient perspective, disability, and strength. Several important limitations warrant mention.37 First, although data supporting the use of these parameters to detect change in CIDP are well established, it must be recognized that no evidence of disease activity is not the same as no disease activity, as subtle active inflammation may be undetectable by these metrics. Second, while composite scoring improves sensitivity and specificity to detect change, it remains inherently limited by the shortcomings of the individual measures from which it is derived. Third, some of the outcomes may provide overlapping information. I-RODS and PGIC, for example, are both patient-reported measures that gauge treatment response. However, because PGIC represents a global judgment of change, whereas I-RODS quantifies specific functional abilities, when used in combination the scores confirm that a perceived improvement (PGIC) aligns with measurable functional change (I-RODS), strengthening confidence that a patient-meaningful change has occurred. Fourth, the extent to which NEDA status will be maintained in the stable CIDP patient over time is uncertain. In a prospective longitudinal MS study, for example, 92% of patients lost NEDA status by year 7, most commonly due to clinical rather than MRI criteria.38 In CIDP, loss of NEDA status can similarly be anticipated over time, with an uncertain correlation between disease activity and confounding factors such as comorbid conditions or age-related changes. Fifth, the time points proposed to establish NEDA (6 months), relapse (12 weeks), response (12 weeks), optimal response (6 months), and remission (12 months) are incompletely supported by the available evidence. These recommendations were derived through expert consensus voting informed by review of the existing literature.6,7,23,24,39-41 Observation periods may evolve as clinical trial and observational data become available.42 Finally, we acknowledge that the impairment metrics are strength-focused and may not adequately capture meaningful change in all patients with CIDP, particularly those with sensory-predominant variants. The use of composite metrics incorporating patient-reported outcomes and disability may help mitigate this limitation.

Assessment tools such as electrophysiologic studies and imaging have, in some settings, demonstrated correlations with clinical outcomes and treatment response.43,44 However, their role in monitoring disease activity in individual patients is insufficiently defined.39 Given the uncertain sensitivity and specificity of these modalities for detecting disease activity, along with practical limitations related to access and cost, the Task Force elected not to include them as NEDA components. There is a critical need for serum or plasma biomarkers to quantify nerve integrity, immunopathologic activity, or treatment response in CIDP; however, no such assay currently enables reliable individual-level disease activity assessment.

The adoption of “no evidence of disease activity” as a therapeutic objective in CIDP marks a shift in treatment strategy, emphasizing mitigation of disease activity as the primary goal. The proposed definitions reflect considerations of the available evidence surrounding each term, together with consensus agreement where data is lacking. The Task Force encourages the adoption of this terminology into the shared vocabulary of patients, clinicians, and clinical trialists, promoting consistency across clinical care and clinical trials. The Task Force recognizes the limitations of these definitions and emphasizes the importance of their evolution in response to emerging clinical and scientific evidence. In addition, the Task Force acknowledges the need to broaden these definitions to encompass clinical scenarios not currently addressed, including the distinction between CIDP-related progression and progression of symptoms, signs, or functional capacity attributable to other causes.
