Clonal Hematopoiesis Risk Score (CHRS) Evaluation as a Prognostic Marker for Death in Cardio-Oncology Cohort
Authors: Jaiveer Singh, Rachel Jaber Chehayeb, Yunju Im, Irvin Yi, Talal El Zarif, Ana Ferrigno Guajardo, Jennifer VanOudenhove, Stephanie Halene, Alok Jha, Jennifer M. Kwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Central Illustration

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is the presence of somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem cells with a variant allele frequency at or > 2% in the absence of an overt blood cancer. Mutations can occur in genes like epigenetic regulators DNA Methyltransferase 3 Alpha (DNMT3a) and Tet methylcytosine dioxygenase 2 (TET2) that provide selective fitness advantages to aging hematopoietic stem cells, giving rise to clonally expanded stem cell populations.1 The prevalence of CHIP increases with age, with an estimated prevalence of 20% in adults over the age of 70.1 It also commonly occurs in the context of selective pressures like cytotoxic therapies among cancer patients.1 CHIP has been found to be associated with an increased risk of hematological cancer, cardiovascular disease, and mortality.2

What is the clinical question being addressed? Does the CHIP risk score, which has been prognostic for the outcome of death in the general UK Biobank cohort, also have prognostic value in a cardio-oncology cohort? What is the main finding? Our study is the first to show that it may not have the same prognostic value, and that a dedicated risk score for cancer patients is needed.

What is the clinical question being addressed?

Does the CHIP risk score, which has been prognostic for the outcome of death in the general UK Biobank cohort, also have prognostic value in a cardio-oncology cohort?

What is the main finding?

Our study is the first to show that it may not have the same prognostic value, and that a dedicated risk score for cancer patients is needed.

The Clonal Hematopoiesis Risk Score (CHRS) is a tool developed from the UK Biobank (UKB) to stratify patients with high, intermediate, and low risk for developing myeloproliferative neoplasms and was shown to have prognostic value for risk of mortality and adverse cardiovascular events.3 A score was developed using factors including the number of mutations, the maximum variant allele frequency, mean corpuscular volume, red cell distribution width, and age—with cutoffs for low, intermediate, and high being ≤9.5, 10 to 12, and ≥12.5, respectively. However, given that patients with solid tumors make up <20% of the UKB cohort, it is unclear if this tool can provide prognostic insights in a cancer population. This study aimed to evaluate whether the CHRS could be used as a prognostic marker for death in cancer patients.

In a retrospective study approved by the Yale Institutional Review Board, we enrolled 236 patients undergoing treatment for solid tumors, including those with prior heart disease or heart failure, between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023, who were referred to cardio-oncology or seen by the cardiology service. Patients with active blood borne cancer were excluded. We evaluated for CHIP status as well as starting point for time of death by blood draw at the time of enrollment. Methodology to determine CHIP has been previously described.4 Chart review was performed to evaluate patient demographics, comorbidities, oncologic history, cardiac medications, and outcomes. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and multivariate Cox regression models stratified by CHIP and CHRS were generated.

Our median follow-up time for survival was 1.78 years (IQR: 1.18-2.43). Among the 236 patients, 90 (38%) had CHIP. DNMT3A was the most common mutation (40%), followed by TET2 (12%). The average age of the CHIP cohort was 71 (IQR: 62-77). The most common cancer types included in the study were breast 99 (41.9%), lung 37 (15.7%), and genitourinary (GU) cancers 34 (14.4%). Patients with GU cancers had a higher prevalence of CHIP (22.2% vs 9.6%; P = 0.012) whereas breast cancer patients had a lower prevalence of CHIP (32.2% vs 47.8%; P = 0.021). There was no difference in cause of death between CHIP and no CHIP. When stratified by CHRS, 9 (10%) had a high-risk CHRS, while 35 (38.9%) had an intermediate CHRS, both of which have been associated with increased mortality and adverse cardiovascular events. The remaining 46 (51.1%) had low-risk scores. Those in the high-risk group were significantly older (high: 79 [IQR: 70-79], intermediate: 72 [67-76], low: 66 [57-75]; P = 0.047). The intermediate-risk group had a significantly shorter time to death (2.0 months [IQR: 1.0-3.0]) compared to the high- (7.0 months [IQR: 4.5-9.5]) and low-risk CHIP score patients (8.0 months [IQR: 5.0-16.0]), potentially reflective of several factors including higher prevalence of metastasis in the intermediate-risk group (56%) and closer medical attention provided to the high-risk group given their older age. There were no significant differences between the CHRS groups in comorbidities, cancer therapies (outside of anthracyclines; P = 0.014), cardiac medications, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, and outcomes (including cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, arrhythmias, metastasis, diastolic dysfunction, and death). Additionally, while the Kaplan-Meier curves showed a significant mortality difference between patients with and without CHIP (P = 0.00031), there were no significant survival differences among the CHRS groups (P = 0.62). From the Cox regression, CHIP was also found to be an independent risk factor for death in this population, doubling the risk (HR: 1.979; 95% CI: 1.05-3.73; P = 0.035), as seen in Figure 1. Of the CHIP patients in the cohort, 32 were deceased, with a median time to death of 5 months (IQR: 1-12). We previously showed that CHIP is an independent risk factor for cardiomyopathy and immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) myocarditis.4,5 Our current study shows that although CHIP is an independent risk factor for death in cancer patients, the CHRS was not able to further risk stratify for death in those with CHIP. Cancer patients with intermediate-risk CHRS had the shortest time to death but CHRS was not an independent risk factor for predicting mortality in cancer patients (Figure 1 [bottom panel]). ICI use was associated with increased risk of death in this population, likely secondary to having developed cardiotoxicity and/or cancer severity.

Our study has several limitations including heterogeneity of the cohort (cancer site, stage, treatment, and performance status). Most of our patients were referred to cardiology, so the baseline incidence of cardiac comorbidities in our patient population is higher than in a more general oncology cohort. Additionally, this is a single-center cohort, and larger multicenter cohorts would allow for further validation. A cohort size of larger than 90 CHIP patients would be helpful to better assess the high-risk CHRS group, which was just 9 patients in our study.

Altogether, although the CHIP risk score has been prognostic for the outcome of death in the UKB cohort, it may not have the same prognostic value in a cancer population, despite overall trends in having a shorter time to death in the intermediate-risk group compared to high- and low-risk CHIP risk scores in this population. Of note, although cardiovascular disease is the top killer in CHIP patients in a general population, cancer was still the top cause of death in patients with solid malignancy. Larger studies to re-examine these findings are warranted, especially given the smaller size of the high-risk CHRS group in our cohort, and a dedicated risk score for cancer patients is needed.
