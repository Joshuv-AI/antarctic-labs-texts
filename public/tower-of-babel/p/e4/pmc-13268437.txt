Predictors of clinical outcomes and muscle recovery after total hip replacement surgery: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Dong Hyun Ye, Yoon Tae Jung, Jong-Yoon Chang, Seung Hun Lee, Jung-Min Koh, Pil Whan Yoon, Won Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identifying predictors for functional recovery following total hip replacement (THR) is essential for optimizing postoperative care. We aimed to identify preoperative factors affecting muscle recovery and functional outcomes 1-year after THR. This prospective observational study enrolled 103 patients scheduled for unilateral THR. Demographic data, body composition (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry), and physical functions (grip strength, knee extension strength, and gait speed) were assessed preoperatively. Patients were reevaluated 1-year after surgery using the same parameters and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs: modified Harris hip score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities osteoarthritis index, EuroQol-5D). Of the initial cohort, 57 patients completed the 1-year follow-up (dropout rate: 44.7%). Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors. Significant improvements in muscle strength, mass, and PROMs were observed 1-year postoperatively. Multivariable analysis revealed that leg mass, preoperative grip strength, and appendicular skeletal mass, adjusted by body mass index, were significant independent predictors of postoperative PROMs. However, indices adjusted by height squared did not demonstrate consistent associations. Preoperative gait speed was also strongly correlated with functional outcomes. Patients undergoing THR experienced significant improvement in their muscle strength, mass, and functional recovery 1-year after the surgery. Skeletal muscle indices adjusted for body mass index or weight, rather than height2, were more consistently associated with surgical outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of the “load-to-capacity” ratio in weight-bearing joints.

1. Introduction

Hip osteoarthritis (OA) is a common joint disorder that causes pain, stiffness, and reduced mobility. It occurs when the cartilage that cushions the hip joint wears off over time with age, leading to direct bone-on-bone contact and thereby causing damage to the joints. Individuals with hip OA suffer from worsening functional levels, which eventually leads to the deterioration of their daily activities and quality of life.[1–4] Various factors can cause hip OA, but it proceeds faster in people with already damaged joint cartilage. For example, hip dysplasia is a well-established risk factor for developing hip OA and a well-known reason for total hip replacement (THR).[5–7] Avascular necrosis (AVN) occurs when the blood supply to the femoral head is disrupted. When AVN progresses, the ball part of the hip joint collapses and loses its shape, eventually leading to severe hip arthritis, a common reason for surgery.[8]

THR is an effective treatment option for alleviating hip pain and improving the functional status of the patient. However, patients undergoing THR experience a significant loss of muscle mass and strength in the affected limb perioperatively.[9,10] Although the occurrence of muscle atrophy is known in patients undergoing THR, the extent of muscle recovery and functional improvement is not well-known. A previous study reported changes in the muscle cross-sectional area and radiological density through computed tomography (CT).[10] Nevertheless, longitudinal changes in muscle mass, strength and functional capacity following THR remain a subject of ongoing debate. Judd et al reported difficulty in recovering the muscle strength and walking ability of the affected leg as much as that of the unaffected leg 12 months after surgery.[2] Insufficient recovery of muscle strength and functions following THR can provoke activities of daily living problems, especially for older patients. From this perspective, identifying high-risk THR patients is essential and evaluating prognostic factors before THR requires individualized treatment plans for better functional recovery.

Postoperative muscle recovery is affected by various factors such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), surgical technique and physical activity.[11] Previous studies have suggested preoperative physical function as the most influential factor for surgical outcomes.[12–14] Sekita et al presented preoperative muscle strength, age, weight and physical activity as predictive factors for lower extremity strength 12 months after THR.[12] On the other hand, Slaven et al proposed preoperative BMI, sex, age and walking speed as predictors for the functional outcomes 6 months after THR.[14] There is a growing trend to utilize preoperative physical function as a predictor for postoperative outcomes; however, the relative importance and impact of different factors therein remain uncertain. In this study, we quantitatively measured the changes in muscle mass, strength and quality of life evaluated before and 1 year after THR. Through this study, we aimed to identify the predictive factors that affect postoperative muscle mass, strength and functional recovery after THR.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

This was a prospective observational study enrolling 57 patients who underwent THR for hip OA or osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) from May 2017 to March 2020 at the Asan Medical Center. The patients’ demographic data were collected, which included information on their age, sex, weight, height, BMI, and etiology of hip pathology. All operations were conducted by a highly experienced surgeon. Surgery was performed in the lateral decubitus position through a posterior approach. The postoperative rehabilitation was the same for all patients; the patients were allowed flatfoot partial weight-bearing with a walker or crutches on postoperative day 2 and allowed full weight-bearing, as tolerable, from 6 weeks postoperatively. All participants underwent preoperative evaluation and returned to the hospital 1 year after surgery for a follow-up evaluation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their enrollment, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (2017-0553).

2.2. Body composition analysis

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) was performed for all participants, after which a follow-up assessment was scheduled to monitor changes in body composition 1 year after surgery. We measured the muscle and fat percentages of all extremities and defined appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) as the sum of muscle mass in both arms and legs. The parameters were defined using a Lunar Prodigy DEXA unit (GE Lunar) and Encore Software version 11.0 (GE Healthcare). The inspection was performed using the standard protocol specified by the manufacturer.

2.3. Grip and knee extension strength

Before taking the measurements, techniques for properly using the instruments were demonstrated to the patients. Maximum isometric hand grip strength was measured using a hand-held dynamometer (Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.). While standing with the elbow extended and the wrist in a neutral position, the participants were instructed to grip the device twice with maximal effort using their dominant hand. In addition, we measured the knee extension strength of the affected leg. The maximal knee extension strength was measured with an isometric dynamometer (TKK5710e, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.). While sitting on a strength-measuring table (TKK5715, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.), the knee extension strength was assessed at 90° of knee flexion with the hip at 90° of flexion. All participants performed maximal voluntary isometric contractions twice, and strong verbal encouragement was provided for each trial.

2.4. Gait speed and chair stand test

The gait speed was calculated using the 4-m walk test, performed as follows: a 6-m walkway was set up, with the 1st 1 m marked as acceleration and deceleration zones; the participant was instructed to walk at the usual pace to the end of the walkway; (3) the time taken by the participant to walk the central 4 m was measured with a walking speedometer (Gaitanalyzer ONE, Dynamicphysiology). In addition, the chair stand test was performed as follows: the participant was asked to sit in a chair with their arms crossed over the chest; on the command “go,” the participant had to stand up and then sit down 5 times, as quickly as possible, with the arms remaining crossed over the chest; the time taken to complete the task was recorded with a stopwatch.[15] Both tests were performed twice, and the average time was recorded.

2.5. Patients-reported outcome measures

In our study, the modified Harris hip score (MHHS), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), and EuroQol-5 dimensions (EQ-5D) were used as functional outcome measurement tools 1 year after the surgery. WOMAC is a widely used evaluation tool for hip and knee OA. It is a self-administered questionnaire consisting of 24 items divided into 3 subscales (i.e., pain, stiffness, and physical function).[16–18] The WOMAC questionnaire was scored on a Likert scale from 0 to 4, where 0 represents no difficulty or no pain and 4 represents extreme difficulty or extreme pain. The scores from each dimension were added together to create a total WOMAC score, which ranges from 0 to 96. Higher scores on the WOMAC indicate more severe symptoms and greater physical disability. MHHS is a disease-specific questionnaire used to evaluate the functional status and clinical outcomes of patients who underwent THR.[16,19] The MHHS assessed the hip function based on the following 9 different domains: pain, gait, range of motion, hip joint stability, activities of daily living, need for assistive devices, absence of deformity, limb length discrepancy, and the presence of any other joint disease. Each domain was scored on a scale of 0 to 6, with a total possible score of 100. The EQ-5D questionnaire was used to assess the quality of life perioperatively. It is a standardized health-related quality of life questionnaire that measures the following 5 dimensions of health: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has 3 levels, indicating the absence of a problem, the presence of some problems, or extreme problems. These results were combined to obtain a health index score, where a value of 1 indicated perfect health and negative values indicated severe problems across all 5 dimensions.[20–25] In addition to the EQ-5D index, the EQ-5D questionnaire included a visual analogue scale (VAS), which measures an individual’s overall self-rated health status. The EQ-5D VAS is a 20-cm vertical scale ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health state) to 100 (best imaginable health state).

2.6. Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics are presented as the means ± standard deviation. The mean difference of variables evaluated before and 1 year after the surgery was analyzed by a paired t test. The normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. We applied a sex-adjusted univariate linear regression model to identify the correlation among PROMs, muscle strength and mass indices. To identify independent predictors of postoperative outcomes, we performed a multivariable linear regression analysis. The model included potential confounders such as age, sex, and diagnosis (OA vs AVN), and preoperative PROMs. Additionally, to account for the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. An false discovery rate -adjusted P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the difference in the major surgical outcomes according to sex, the Mann–Whitney U test was performed. All statistical calculations were conducted using SPSS (version 25 IBM Co.), and P < .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

3. Results

Of the 138 eligible patients with hip OA or ONFH, 103 patients provided their consent and underwent baseline assessments before unilateral THR. Finally, 57 patients completed the planned assessments at 1-year follow-up and 46 dropped out of the study (Fig. 1). The mean age of the patients was 58.9 ± 9.9 years. Of the total patients, 22 were men (38.6%) and 35 were women (61.4%). The reason for surgery was hip OA in 35 patients (61.4%) and ONFH in 22 patients (38.6%), as shown in Table 1. Also, there were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the 57 participants who completed the 1-year follow-up and the 46 participants who did not (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1).

Table 2 presents the changes in demographics, parameters, and PROMs over time. The strength assessment showed a statistically significant improvement in knee extension strength for both the affected side (22.97 ± 10.88 kg to 29.25 ± 11.47 kg) and the unaffected side (30.11 ± 12.51 kg to 32.12 ± 13.19 kg) following the surgery. However, no significant alteration was observed in grip strength. Both the leg muscle mass and ASM indicated a statistically significant increase, but there was no significant change in both arms’ mass or fat percentage. Functional assessment revealed a statistically significant improvement in gait speed, but there was no significant change in the chair stand test. The improvements observed in PROMs were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful, exceeding the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for THR patients. The mean improvement in the MHHS score was 28.15 points, which substantially exceeds the previously reported MCID of 10.8 points for patients undergoing hip arthroplasty.[26] Similarly, the reduction in WOMAC scores (25.37 points) surpassed the conservative MCID estimate of 9 to 12 points in the literature.[27,28] Regarding quality of life, the gains in both the EQ-5D Index (0.20) and EQ-5D VAS (17.49) were well above the MCID thresholds of 0.17 and 7.81, respectively.[29]

In the postoperative skeletal muscle indices, the muscle masses of both the legs’ masses, knee extension strength, ASM and grip strength, after adjusting for weight and BMI, indicated significant correlations with postoperative WOMAC (Table 4). On the other hand, only a few postoperative indices, such as the muscle mass of both the legs’ mass after adjusting for weight, BMI, and height2, grip strength by BMI, and ASM by BMI, showed significant correlations with postoperative MHHS.

In terms of functional assessment, preoperative gait speed had a statistically significant correlation with PROMs before and after surgery, while postoperative gait speed displayed a correlation with postoperative PROMs. On the other hand, the preoperative performance of the chair stand test demonstrated a significant correlation with preoperative PROMs, but not with PROMs 1-year after surgery. Moreover, the postoperative performance of the chair stand test showed no correlation with PROMs 1-year after surgery.

In summary, in the sex-adjusted univariate analysis, skeletal muscle indices adjusted by weight and BMI showed significant correlations with postoperative PROMs, whereas those adjusted by height squared (HT2) did not show consistent significant associations. Preoperative gait speed was strongly correlated with both MHHS and WOMAC scores after surgery.

Following the univariate analysis, to identify independent predictors, we performed a multivariable linear regression analysis adjusting for age, sex, diagnosis, and preoperative scores. Table 5 presents the results of the multivariable linear regression analysis adjusting for age, sex, diagnosis, and PROMs. Even after adjustment, the preoperative both-leg mass adjusted by BMI remained a significant independent predictor for postoperative MHHS (β = 0.379, 95% CI: 0.02–0.74, P = .04) and WOMAC (β = −0.368, 95% CI: −0.70 to −0.04, P = .03). Similarly, Grip strength and ASM adjusted by BMI were significantly associated with postoperative outcomes, confirming that muscle quality relative to body constitution is a crucial prognostic factor.

Table 6 presents the results of the analysis of the differences in PROMs based on sex. The results indicated that, both before and 1 year after surgery, MHHS, WOMAC, and EQ-5D scores were significantly better for males than for females. However, there were no significant differences in the changes before and after surgery for these 3 variables. Further analysis of the specific domains suggested no sex-related difference in the pain domain of MHHS and WOMAC before surgery. However, men showed significantly better pain outcomes than women, postoperatively. In terms of the function domain, men showed better results both before and after surgery, albeit there was no difference in the stiffness domain.

4. Discussion

THR surgery helped improve pain symptoms that patients mainly complain of, albeit the long-term changes in muscle strength and mass of the affected leg remain controversial.[10,30] Muscle loss and deterioration in the quality of life of patients receiving THR are the primary concern for healthcare providers. To the best of our knowledge, this is the 1st study to comprehensively describe quantitative changes in muscle mass, strength, and functional outcomes in Asian patients after THR. The primary finding of this study is that skeletal muscle indices adjusted by weight or BMI, rather than height2, are independent predictors of functional recovery. These results suggest that muscle power relative to body mass is a more critical determinant of postoperative outcomes than absolute muscle mass alone.

4.1. Conceptualization of muscle indices: the “load-to-capacity” ratio

A key conceptual implication of our study lies in the adjustment strategies for muscle indices. While sarcopenia is traditionally assessed using height-adjusted indices (HT2), this approach may underestimate muscle deficits in overweight populations – a condition known as “sarcopenic obesity.”[31,32] Our cohort, with a mean BMI of 25.77 kg/m2, represents an overweight population by Asian criteria.[33] Notably, in our overweight cohort, height-adjusted indices failed to demonstrate consistent correlations with clinical outcomes. However, our multivariate analysis confirmed that indices adjusted for weight or BMI were robust independent predictors. This highlights the clinical importance of the “load-to-capacity ratio” – the ability of muscles to support the body’s mass – in weight-bearing joint arthroplasty. This finding aligns with the biomechanical understanding that body weight is closely related to hip OA progression due to excessive joint loading.[34,35] Therefore, in the context of THR, weight- or BMI-adjusted indices offer a more clinically relevant assessment of functional sufficiency.

4.2. Conceptualization of measurements

Regarding the conceptualization of measurements, we evaluated knee extension strength as an indicator of hip function. Although hip strength is the direct effector, accurate measurement is often compromised by pain inhibition. Previous investigations have reported that quadriceps weakness persists even several months after THR, and it adversely affects mobility and increases the risk of falls, even with improvements in pain and functional performance.[36–38] Consequently, we interpreted knee extension strength as a functional proxy reflecting disuse atrophy of the affected limb, which is closely linked to gait stability and fall risk. In addition, grip strength was measured to determine generalized muscle weakness, which supports previous reports on systemic muscle deficits in hip OA.[30,39,40] Although the mechanism of muscle weakness in hip OA patients remains unclear, it is believed to be caused by complex factors such as reduced physical activity, pain, and dysfunction.[30,41]

4.3. Comparison with literature and functional outcomes

Mobility and functional markers, such as gait speed and chair stand test, are well-established predictors of recovery, morbidity, and mortality following hip surgery.[42,43] Consistent with previous literature, our study found that preoperative gait speed was strongly correlated with both pre and postoperative PROMs. This reaffirms gait speed as a simple and powerful prognostic indicator of functional recovery. Conversely, the chair stand test showed no significant correlations in our multivariate model. This discrepancy might be attributed to the “ceiling effect” in our relatively younger (mean age 58.9 years) and functionally capable cohort compared to typical Western THR populations.[44] While patients showed significant improvements in muscle strength and mass at 1-year follow-up, the affected leg did not fully recover to the level of the unaffected leg. This persistent asymmetry suggests the need for targeted, long-term unilateral strengthening even after pain relief is achieved.

4.4. Sex-based differences

Hip OA has been reported to have a greater prevalence of clinical pain and physical difficulty in women than in men.[45–47] Timothy et al reported no sex-related differences in the THR outcomes, and recent systematic reviews have reported differences in various areas, including pain, functional outcomes, and kinematics from the acute postoperative period.[48–50] Our data revealed consistent sex-related differences, with men showing more favorable absolute scores in PROMs both pre and postoperatively (Table 6). These discrepancies can be attributed to biological differences in baseline muscle mass and societal factors, such as women potentially seeking surgical intervention at a more advanced disease stage (evidenced by significantly worse baseline WOMAC scores). Nevertheless, it is crucial to note that the magnitude of functional recovery (Δ) was comparable between sexes. This implies that while women start at a lower functional baseline, they derive equal benefit from THR surgery.

5. Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the study population was relatively younger (58.9 ± 9.9 years) with better functional status than typical geriatric cohorts, potentially masking age-related declines. Third, although a systematic rehabilitation program was prescribed, quantitative data regarding patient adherence, frequency, and intensity of the rehabilitation exercises were not strictly monitored. Finally, the follow-up period was limited to 1-year; future prospective studies with longer follow-up are warranted to assess the long-term sustainability of muscle recovery.

6. Conclusion

Patients undergoing THR experience significant improvement in muscle strength, mass, and functional recovery. Skeletal muscle indices adjusted for body mass (BMI or weight), rather than height2, were more consistently associated with surgical outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of considering the load-bearing capacity of muscles in the management of hip arthroplasty patients.
