Perceptions of intimate partner violence among men and women in southern India: a qualitative study
Authors: William T Story, Avita R Johnson, K G Jayachitra, Dara L Jefferson, Sylvia A Ayieko, Nancy A Gnanaselvam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore the perceptions of intimate partner violence (IPV) and its potential consequences among adults (both married and unmarried) and adolescents in peri-urban India.DesignA qualitative study design using thematic analysis was applied to 7 focus group discussions (FGDs) (n=56) and 33 in-depth interviews (IDIs) among adults (aged 19–48 years) and adolescents (aged 15–18 years). Interview guides for the FGDs and IDIs explored the perceptions of gender-based violence with a focus on IPV, risk factors and circumstances that justify IPV, triggers and consequences of IPV and prevention of IPV.SettingThis study was conducted in two peri-urban villages, namely Mugalur and Handenahalli, in Sarjapura Hobli, Anekal Taluk, Bangalore Urban District.ResultsThis study uncovered the types of IPV, the drivers of IPV and the responses to IPV at the interpersonal and community levels. The types of IPV that participants described were emotional, verbal, physical and sexual violence within and outside of marriage. The drivers of IPV included women’s lack of support, poverty, alcoholism and individual attitudes, such as perceived disobedience and infidelity. Violence was widely justified and normalised in communities as well. Women’s individual responses to violence included silence to avoid escalating the situation, whereas men stated their willingness to stand up against IPV at a community level.ConclusionsMisogynistic attitudes in the community continue to prevail, and perceived stigma related to IPV is common, despite some men expressing motivation to confront IPV. Going forward, interventions must directly address the forms of hegemonic masculinity that normalise or legitimise violence, particularly among adolescents.

Introduction

Despite legislative efforts to address violence in the home, intimate partner violence (IPV) continues to be a major challenge in India. Nearly one-third (32%–33%) of ever-married women between the ages of 18 and 49 years reported that they have experienced physical, sexual or emotional spousal violence.13 Gender-based violence, including IPV, has serious consequences for women’s health, including their mental and physical well-being.4 Among ever-married women who have experienced spousal abuse, one-fourth (25%) have sustained injury, such as cuts and bruises, broken bones or broken teeth.1 5 In addition to physical injuries, there are other negative health outcomes linked to IPV, such as unwanted pregnancies, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and suicidality.6 7 The impacts of IPV begin as early as birth, with maternal IPV being consistently linked to a broad range of negative maternal and child health outcomes.3 Because of the social and cultural norms in India regarding the roles of men and women and the long history of patriarchy and unequal power dynamics in the home,8 children are exposed to violence as a typical behaviour within the home, which may perpetuate IPV during the adolescent period.9

Violence against women has also been linked to income inequality; a crisis in male identity can be triggered when men are unable to attain social expectations of manhood due to poverty. Men may use violence to resolve this identity crisis, perceiving that by harming women, they can express power that they otherwise lack.10 This type of violence may be higher in areas where income inequality is growing, such as the peri-urban areas of large Indian cities.11 Prior studies on IPV in India have focused on geographical areas, such as urban and rural4 6 or slum populations.3 5 However, there are unique and understudied circumstances in areas that are shaped by the urbanisation of former rural areas on the urban fringe (ie, peri-urban areas). Peri-urban areas of India are an important area to study because they are experiencing high levels of income inequality and undergoing rapid social change. Over half (55%) of India’s population currently lives in or around large cities, which includes peri-urban areas.12 Since India’s urban and peri-urban population is projected to grow rapidly in the next 25 years, it is an ideal moment to examine IPV in the context of the rapid changes and growing income inequalities that characterise India’s peri-urban settings. There is a paucity of qualitative studies that explore the types of IPV in India from the perspective of youth and adults. In one systematic review, it was found that only 1% of studies had evaluated the IPV experiences of young adolescents, while the majority (88%) of studies focused on women aged 15–50 years.6 Exploring youth perspectives may further clarify the causes and consequences of IPV. With the differential impact of IPV on both adults and youth, it is important to understand their unique perspectives. Further, this study takes a multilevel approach by exploring the types of IPV, the drivers of IPV and the responses to IPV at the interpersonal and community levels. According to the conceptual framework in figure 1, the different types of IPV (ie, emotional, physical and sexual) are influenced by the drivers of IPV, which can include individual susceptibility to IPV, interpersonal triggers to IPV and societal justification of IPV. Consequently, when IPV occurs, the responses can be both positive and negative at the individual and community levels.

The goal of this study is to describe the different types of IPV as perceived by adults and youth in a large, peri-urban population of India and to examine the multilevel drivers and responses to IPV among these individuals.

Methods

Study setting

St John’s Medical College is in Bangalore, and the rural service area of the department of community health is located 30 km away from the college. Two health subcentre areas in the rural service area, Mugalur and Handenahalli, were selected for the study, with a total population of around 10 000. The study area is peri-urban with adequate access to transportation and healthcare. This study was conducted from September 2019 to January 2020, before the COVID-19 pandemic and related restrictions were in place.

Study population

This study was conducted among adolescents (aged 15–18 years) and adults (aged 19–48 years) in Mugalur and Handenahalli. We chose both married and unmarried adults for the study to provide a diverse range of opinions about IPV within and outside of marriage. Middle and late adolescents were chosen because their understanding of the research topic, which is sensitive in nature, is better in this age group. We chose both adults and adolescents in this study because we were particularly interested in the formation of gender norms related to IPV during adolescence, and we wanted to capture age-related variations in perceptions of and experiences with IPV.

Study methods

This is a qualitative study using a thematic analysis approach involving focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews (IDIs). These two data collection methods were selected because FGDs allowed respondents to comment and build on one another’s responses, whereas IDIs enabled respondents to disclose attitudes and behaviours that they might not reveal in a group. Although the mode of asking the questions was different, the contents of the FGDs and IDIs were the same. Purposive sampling was used in this study by choosing participants who could provide sufficient information to answer the research questions. Participants who were sampled for the study came from self-help groups, farmers/co-operative groups, religious meetings (men/women) and schools/sports teams (boys/girls). A sampling grid was developed for the study to ensure participants from different demographic groups were represented: age groups (adolescents/adults), marital status (married/unmarried), gender (male/female) and residence (Mugalur/Handenahalli villages) (table 1).

We recruited and trained four experienced qualitative interviewers to conduct the FGDs and IDIs in the local language (Kannada). FGDs took place in a central location, such as a community centre, and IDIs took place in a private area in the participant’s home. To ensure that the participants felt comfortable discussing sensitive topics, FGDs were conducted separately for adolescent girls, adolescent boys, married men, married women, unmarried men and unmarried women. In total, 56 individuals participated in 7 FGDs and 33 in the IDIs, and all participants were provided with refreshments. Data collection was completed after the achievement of saturation.

Study tools

The interview guides for the FGDs and IDIs were developed for different groups (ie, adolescent boys, adolescent girls, men and women) with a focus on gender norms and gender-based violence. The questions for this paper mainly focus on the types of IPV, the drivers of IPV (ie, who is at risk for IPV, circumstances that justify IPV and triggers of IPV) and responses to IPV (ie, consequences of IPV and IPV prevention) (table 2). The interview guides used for adolescents and adults are presented in onlinesupplemental tables 14. The research team—led by WTS and coordinated by ARJ and NAG—developed the interview guides based on their experience of working with the communities. The guides were face-validated by KGJ, a social worker working in the study communities. The questions were selected based on the study objectives, and the guides were pilot-tested among one married woman, one married man, one adolescent boy and one adolescent girl who lived in the communities of interest. The participants’ feedback, as well as the feedback from the interviewers (during their training), was incorporated into the final interview guides.

Data analysis

All FGDs and IDIs were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and translated into English for analysis using NVivo V.11. The research team thematically analysed the English transcripts using a deductive coding process.13 The University of Iowa principal investigator (WTS) developed a thematic qualitative codebook based on the semi-structured FGD and IDI guides. The codebook was subsequently reviewed and revised by the larger research team. The qualitative data analysis team comprised a graduate student from the University of Iowa (DLJ) and a social worker from St John’s Medical College (JKG). The analysis team (WTS, DLJ and JKG) conducted qualitative intercoder reliability checks, establishing coding consensus prior to conducting independent coding of all FGD and IDI transcripts using NVivo V.11. After the completion of thematic coding, the analysis team reviewed coded excerpts and developed code summaries, which is a second layer of qualitative analysis that identifies main and deviant narratives within each thematic code.

To enhance credibility, dependability and confirmability of the data, we conducted member checking after data analysis with selected research participants who reviewed the findings and indicated that they agreed with the themes generated. An audit trail was maintained by WTS and NAG, containing the study design, topic guides and methods (ie, the justification for methods, sample size, data saturation, complete audio recordings and transcripts (Kannada and English)). In addition, memos were maintained by NAG and shared with other team members since she was working closely with the community and observed the nuanced issues in the community, such as caste hierarchies, endogamy due to arranged marriage and the normalisation of violence in the community. Finally, peer debriefing was conducted every 2 weeks during the data collection and analysis phases of the study via online meetings. Triangulation of the data was conducted by comparing the themes across a diverse range of participants.

Ethical approval

Since the topic of IPV is sensitive in nature, all participants were informed that if they became triggered and distressed due to participation in this research, they would be referred to St John’s Medical College’s weekly family counselling clinic or the community mental health clinic, and the research interaction would be discontinued. St John’s has an excellent rapport with the community due to their ongoing long-term health services in the area and has developed safety planning prior to this study in collaboration with women’s self-help groups. All interviewers were trained in gender sensitisation and gender-based violence to address their own biases. To maintain confidentiality, we ensured that no other family members were present throughout the interviews. If any family members’ arrival or interference during the interview was considered a threat, the family member was informed that we had come from the health centre for a routine health interview and follow-up. We then asked the participant a few health-related questions and stopped the interview, ensuring follow-up on a later date. The consent process was completed in privacy, and after explanation and signing, the form was collected from the participant and kept safely with the study team. All participants were informed about the St John’s Family Counselling Clinic, which could be used by the participant and their family members as needed. Any participant who expressed distress during the interviews was approached with empathy, and the interview was stopped as per the participants’ wishes.

Authors reflexivity

WTS is a white male academic faculty member from the University of Iowa. DLJ is a Native American female graduate student from the University of Iowa. SAA is a black female doctoral scholar from the University of Iowa. The non-Indian researchers were not a part of the data collection and had no interaction with the community. NAG and ARJ are married female faculty members from India. JKG is a female social worker from India. Due to their employment status and position as healthcare providers, the Indian researchers hold power and privilege in the community, as compared with the research participants. Interviews and discussions among male participants were conducted by male research assistants. The three female researchers were present during the data collection processes among female participants. The data collection team was trained to conduct research among children and adolescents, and they ensured freedom of expression during interviews by informing the children that they would ensure confidentiality. Children were also assured that the study team would not reveal any of the shared information to their parents or teachers. During the data analysis process, interpretive disagreements arose due to the different social and ethnic backgrounds of the researchers on the team. During the intercoder reliability process, a discussion about interpretations took place, and the interpretations of investigators based in India were elevated above the interpretations of the non-Indian investigators. The multicultural team of investigators added to a more holistic understanding of the data.

Patient and public involvement

Results

A total of 56 individuals participated in the study. Responses were collected from both married (64%) and unmarried (36%) individuals who participated in FGDs and IDIs. There were slightly more females (54%) than males. The study included both adolescents and adults with an average age of 23 years. Most participants had completed some level of high school education (11 years on average). More than half of the participants (57%) were from an ‘Other Backward Class’ caste, a term for socially and educationally disadvantaged communities in India, and 27% belonged to the Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe categories. There were slightly fewer participants from Handenahalli (45%) compared with Mugalur. Participants had lived in the region for 0–48 years, with an average of almost 21 years. The average family income was about 14 640 Indian Rupees (table 3).

Findings from the interviews with both married and unmarried adults and youth revealed three interrelated themes associated with IPV: (1) types of IPV, (2) drivers of IPV and (3) individual and community responses to IPV (figure 1).

Types of IPV

Various types of violence were mentioned by respondents, ranging from psychological violence to physical violence. Sexual violence was also included as a type of violence. Control and coercion were included in the topic guide, and participants did not discuss this type of violence. Emotional and verbal abuse was described as women being scolded by their husbands and others, others speaking badly about them or in a negative manner and cursing. Participants also discussed physical violence against partners, which included fighting and beating. Other forms of extreme physical assault reported were acid attacks against women, kidnapping and murder.

Emotional and verbal violence

Psychological aggression—such as scolding from their husbands, cursing or using derogatory words to belittle partners—was mentioned by women as a common act of violence. Married men said that this type of violence is quite common in the community. Some participants alluded to emotional abuse, such as using blackmail, as a means of dominance against women.

“…[men] will raise their voices towards women since they cannot dominate. [They] quarrel or fight and beat their wives if she does not obey him or argues with him. They talk emotionally or blackmail…” (IDI with an unmarried woman from Mugalur).

Other forms of psychological violence reported involved men who inflicted dominance or emotional pain on their partners, including threatening or punishing their children.

Physical violence

Most participants from the IDIs and FGDs described physical abuse, including fighting, beating and torture. One unmarried man stated that sometimes husbands would beat their wives at home. A few respondents also reported hearing about more extreme forms of violence, such as acid attacks against women, kidnapping and murder, as described below.

“When women don’t listen, then men will put acid on them. They also get angry, and all the violent activities will be done by them.” (IDI with an unmarried girl from Mugalur).

“In my neighbor’s house, men come home drunk and beat their wives.” (FGD with unmarried women from Handenahalli).

Another participant reported violence experienced by girls and women in schools and in the home.

“There will be [teasing] in school. [Also], a husband might beat his wife at home sometimes. …Yes, I have seen physical abuse of women in our village.” (IDI with an unmarried man from Mugalur).

Sexual violence

A few participants narrated occurrences of non-consensual sexual intercourse within marriage (ie, marital rape), with some reporting that women were forced to have sex by their partners, even when they did not want to. One female participant felt that married men were obligated to have sex with their wives, describing sexual intercourse as a duty that needed to be fulfilled.

“There will be changes in men’s hormones. Men who [get] drunk always… force their wives [to have sex].” (IDI with an unmarried woman from Mugalur).

“If we talk about sex, they don’t even ask our permission. Even though we are not well, they need sex. …[Sex] happens with force; I can assure you that every woman will face this problem. That is the only reason for a male to get married.” (FGD with married women from Mugalur).

Some study participants stated that rape occurred in the community, including among those in trusted relationships. While rape was sometimes triggered by sexual attraction, other participants believed that rape could be due to retaliation.

“If a boy likes a girl, he might rape her, and sometimes they do it for their pleasure.” (IDI with an unmarried girl from Mugalur).

“When girls insult them, then boys will take revenge. Also, [rape] happens when boys are attracted [to girls] by seeing her features.” (IDI with an unmarried girl from Handenahalli).

Although participants described incidents of sexual violence, statements about the absence of sexual violence revealed unique perspectives regarding sexual assault. Some stated that they had not heard of any cases of sexual violence in their village. Most male respondents acknowledged that while sexual violence is common in other places, in their own village, rape does not occur.

Drivers of violence

Study participants shared their perceptions of IPV as they discussed determinants that influenced the drivers of IPV, including susceptibility to IPV, triggers of violence and justification of IPV among partners.

Susceptibility to violence

Study participants shared their perspectives on situations or determinants that made women susceptible to violence. The most salient subthemes that emerged included lack of support, socioeconomic status and personal characteristics/individual behaviours.

Respondents stated that women who did not have supportive networks, including their families or other male figures, were more susceptible to IPV. This excerpt indicates that women who did not have male relatives as allies were likely to experience violence.

“Women who do not have parents or relatives to support them, those kinds of women [have] faced violence. [When] a woman goes to work [alone]…and some men notice her, [if] she does not have support from her family, she might face violence. Such women will be kidnapped or raped.” (FGD with unmarried boys from Mugalur).

“Women who are alone and who don’t have any support from men. Those women get into problems like this.” (FGD with unmarried women from Mugalur).

Various socioeconomic factors (eg, education, income and class) influenced the likelihood of experiencing IPV. For example, a married man from Handenahalli and an unmarried boy from the same village stated that the most important factor was education. Lack of education and lack of social networks were revealed as factors that increased women’s dependency on their spouses, which could contribute to IPV.

“If girls are educated, they will know how to handle [this] situation; they will know how to talk with others too.” (IDI with a married man from Handenahalli).

“Women who are not educated, or if they are not strong enough, or if they are dependent on others, or if they don’t have any support in their lives.” (IDI with an unmarried woman from Handenahalli).

Participants also mentioned low income as a determinant of IPV, as poor women or those with lower social status are more likely to experience IPV compared with women who are wealthy.

“The girls who are very poor, they will get into these problems.” (IDI with a married woman, Handenahalli).

“It will happen to low caste people; it also depends on [how they] dress.” (IDI with unmarried girl, Handenahalli).

Certain personal characteristics also put women at risk for IPV. Some participants argued that women who were amiable and submissive were generally protected from IPV compared with those who were considered to have fewer desirable characteristics. Participants asserted that confidence and good behaviours decreased susceptibility to IPV.

“If girls have good nature, they can manage even if they come back home by 1 am. Also, if girls have bad nature, then they might face such issues.” (IDI with married man, Handenahalli).

Conversely, unmarried youth reported that attractive women were likely to experience sexual harassment regardless of their educational level. Participants believed that women with attractive physical features were susceptible to sexual violence, and women with undesirable demeanours were more prone to physical violence.

“If the girls look good, then the problem will start. …When women look attractive, men start thinking wrongly.” (FGD with unmarried men, Handenahalli).

Triggers of violence

Participants reported various situations and behaviours that provoked IPV, ranging from work-related stress, feelings of dishonour in the household and alcohol consumption. Participants described some behaviours that provoked IPV perpetrators to act violently.

Men often brought work-related stress home and transferred it to their families. When men struggle at work, they may lack patience, ignore their wives and display anger towards their families, leading to violence.

“If the man has a [heavy] workload and stress, he will be irritated. If the wife says something and the husband forgets, the wife reminds him, which further irritates the man. Then, they raise their hand on their wives.” (IDI with a married man from Mugalur).

Some of the respondents felt that men always wanted their wives or women in their homes to listen to and follow them. Men disliked women as decision-makers; they raised their voices or got angry and beat women who were domineering.

“Men [believe] that women should listen to them, but if women do not listen, then they will get angry and start troubling them. In general, all men get angry very quickly.” (IDI with a married woman from Handenahalli).

Both female and male respondents believed that alcohol consumption triggered some men to behave violently and lose control in the household or the community. Participants reported that women who confronted their husbands about alcohol consumption were likely to experience violence since drunk men got angry. When questioned about how individuals got themselves into violent situations, study participants mentioned that both men and women who consumed alcohol were likely to experience violence. Specifically, men who consume alcohol are said to commit acts of violence, such as harassment and rape.

“Men drink and come home. When women questioned [them] about [drinking], they raised their hands on the women.” (FGD with unmarried girl from Mugalur).

“Having drinks is the main reason for these things. Married women will get into these [violent situations]. I am not sure how it happens in the city, but here it mostly happens because they have drinking habits.” (IDI with an unmarried boy from Handenahalli).

Justification of violence

A subtheme that emerged from the IDIs and FGDs was the perception that violence was justified in certain circumstances. Some participants believed that men were justified in beating their wives if the women did not complete their duties, acted irresponsibly or had an affair.

Violence was justified when women failed to perform their responsibilities (eg, childcare, taking care of the family, financial management and food preparation) and made their husbands angry. When women made mistakes or the husband felt that the woman was irresponsible, many respondents agreed that beating one’s wife was permitted.

“A woman has some responsibility in her life. But if she does not complete [the tasks]…then men can beat [her]. But beating is not the solution; they should make her understand.” (IDI with an unmarried woman from Handenahalli).

“If something happens at home, like if a woman is irresponsible and the kids do something like opening the gas cylinder, [then] the man feels it is her mistake and raises his hand against her.” (IDI with an unmarried man from Mugalur).

While participants believed that violence was justified in some cases, they also felt that there were better ways of resolving conflict. Both male and female participants felt that fights or arguments in a relationship could be resolved but that extramarital affairs resulted in physical violence against women. Some respondents felt that men were justified in ‘raising their hands’ against women whenever there was a third person involved in the relationship.

“If a woman has an illegal affair, then the man will beat his wife.” (FGD with married women from Mugalur).

Responses to violence

Participants conveyed different perspectives about how victims were expected to respond to IPV. Responses towards IPV were either addressed at the individual level or required intervention at the community or societal level.

Individual responses to violence

Some respondents felt it was better to be silent during violent situations to prevent escalation. Participants stated that violence could be ameliorated when women became quiet during fights, suggesting that if both parties spoke during a fight, there would be more violence. A few women mentioned that when they are blamed or beaten for something they did not do, they still need to keep calm to help de-escalate the conflict. Being quiet was also viewed as showing respect towards the husband and a way to protect the sanctity of marriage. For this reason, women often choose to remain silent since reacting excessively can have severe consequences. One participant was very clear that as soon as violence starts, couples should immediately address it and not allow it to grow.

“…[wives] should be silent when the husband is drunk and not argue…and everything will be normal.” (FGD with married men from Handenahalli).

“Things must be managed when they start; it should be cut-off while they are small.” (IDI with an unmarried man from Mugalur).

Participants encouraged women who experienced IPV to inform their parents, village elders or the community about the violence and seek help. A few participants recommended talking with a counsellor or therapist, whereas others suggested the establishment of rules and regulations by the government to prevent IPV. Women also mentioned that they should lead the way by starting conversations about violence so that younger women will start feeling safe.

“She can also complain to the police. There is also Mahila Mandal [a women’s group], which is a helpline for women. She can complain over there.” (IDI with a married woman from Mugalur).

Community responses to violence

Many of the men stated their willingness to stand up against IPV in their communities and support women who were victims of IPV, whether they were from their village or from other places. Respondents also mentioned that men who struggle with alcohol use disorders could be taken to a rehabilitation centre or receive counselling.

“Yes, we raise [our] voice for them whether it is in our village or outside the village, we unite to address the issue.” (IDI with a married man from Mugalur).

Women said that discussions about IPV outside the home are discouraged because husbands will get angry if they find out that she told others about the IPV she experienced.

“We don’t talk about [violence in the] house because we should not reveal our house secrets.” (IDI with a married woman from Mugalur).

“[Women] will not complain. If they share [about IPV], then everyone will speak bad about them, and if men come to know that she has shared with others, they will come home drunk and beat them again.” (FGD with unmarried women in Handenahalli).

Non-disclosure of IPV was considered a virtue, and such women are respected for protecting their communities by not gossiping about their families. Participants also mentioned that when women share about IPV, they are seen as troublemakers or mentally ill, and other women will start to gossip about them.

“Some people ignore these women, and some people advise them. But most of the women start gossiping with others about them.” (IDI with an unmarried woman from Mugalur).

Discussion

The present study explored the perceptions of youth and adults concerning different types of IPV and the potential consequences of and responses to IPV among a large, peri-urban population in southern India. The findings uncovered the presence of various forms of IPV in this setting, including physical violence, emotional and verbal abuse and sexual violence. There were several drivers of IPV that contributed to its various forms. Women were more susceptible to violence if they lacked support systems and were of a lower socioeconomic status. Additionally, work-related stress, alcohol consumption and perceived disobedience were identified as significant triggers for all forms of violence. In some cases, both men and women justified IPV due to neglected household responsibilities and infidelity. For those who experienced or witnessed IPV, there were mixed reactions about how to respond at the individual and community levels, with some stating that violence should be kept confidential as a family issue, while others encouraged community support for IPV victims. There were a few gendered cultural norms that emerged from the study, where married male participants did not perceive any occurrences of sexual violence in their communities and were vocal about uniting against IPV. On the other hand, women were often blamed for triggering IPV and felt the need to remain silent to prevent further IPV or to avoid gossip in their communities.

The findings corroborated evidence from other studies in India that showed that violence against women is still prevalent,4 14 highlighting the determinants that put women at risk for IPV. While there is research suggesting that men also experience IPV,15 this was not evident from the IDIs and FGDs in this population.

Similar to previous research, participants in this study reported various forms of physical violence, in which the perpetrators intentionally attempted to inflict physical pain or injury on their partners by beating, fighting or burning them.1618 Scholars describe psychological violence as abuse expressed by manipulation or domineering behaviours, verbal assault, ridicule, degradation or humiliation.19 20 Findings from the IDIs and FGDs uncovered other extreme forms of emotional abuse, where partners used blackmail, including talking about harming themselves or their children. A few studies have shown an association between suicidality and IPV,21 22 suggesting the need for enhanced social support for individuals experiencing IPV.

Sexual harassment as a continuum of sexual behaviours ranging from non-verbal sexual abuse (stalking or making sexual gestures) to physical sexual violence (unwanted sexual touch)23 was reported in this study. Other studies have reported the presence of sexual violence in India and the risk factors associated with sexual violence, such as early marriage, low socioeconomic status and having more than one child.14 The nuances of sexual violence, especially marital rape, can present complexities24 where the marital union is perceived as an institution permitting sex regardless of whether it is considered consensual by both husband and wife.

While the participants reported various forms of IPV, coercion or controlling behaviour as a form of IPV25 was not explicitly mentioned. However, physical violence reported for not completing household chores or the fear that women would experience more violence if they reported IPV, suggests that some husbands exhibit domineering behaviours.

As reported in other studies, findings indicated that women with lower socioeconomic status (including lack of education, poverty and lower caste) were at risk of IPV26 27 due to fewer resources, limited information about their rights and lack of empowerment.28 In peri-urban areas of India, there are significant disparities in employment opportunities, partly due to the transient nature of work and the labour force transitioning from agricultural activities to more industrial and business-related activities.29 These job-related disparities increase work-related stress among men and make it more difficult for women to find gainful employment, both of which increase the risk for IPV. Conversely, some studies have found that higher educational attainment among women, often leading to improved employment opportunities and increased income, can contribute to interpersonal tensions and feelings of jealousy within marital relationships, thereby intensifying the risk of IPV.30 31

The socioeconomic status of women is also a significant determinant of how husbands behave towards their wives,25 as poorer women or those with lower social status are more likely to experience IPV compared with women who are empowered.32 While early marriage, a lower education level and poverty have been identified as risk factors associated with sexual IPV in India,14 32 the present study identified additional determinants that contribute to women’s susceptibility to IPV. For example, some men (and boys) stated that women who ‘look attractive’, regardless of their socioeconomic status, were more susceptible to IPV. Future studies should consider interventions to address norms that victimise individuals who are vulnerable to IPV.

Numerous studies have discussed the various types of IPV and the associated determinants and risk factors.16 17 However, few studies have described the sociocultural factors related to IPV and how they differ by marital status and gender. The narratives about IPV from women in the current study describe the belief that IPV can be justified in marriage because women do not always fulfil their household duties, or they ‘did something wrong’. Further, married women perceive the need to maintain the family unit or keep ‘family secrets’, which demonstrates the social stigma associated with IPV. Partner loyalty, even in the face of violence, suggests a challenge for public health practitioners in disrupting power dynamics and gender norms to reduce IPV and improve the health and well-being of women.33 The findings from this study highlight the importance of community dialogue, conflict resolution and education about acts of violence that could harm women, children and other household members.

In a systematic review, Feder and colleagues31 identified divergent perspectives regarding the disclosure of IPV beyond the familial context. Specifically, women who did not interpret their partner’s behaviour as abusive often reacted negatively to external discussions about IPV, perceiving them as intrusive or offensive. Conversely, women who recognised the presence of IPV within their relationships demonstrated a greater openness to conversations about IPV and were more inclined to seek assistance. In the present study, although married women emphasised the importance of confidentiality concerning IPV within the family, the specific nature or severity of the violence experienced was not delineated.

Many respondents connected violence to alcohol use, household power dynamics and oppression. The findings from the present study are consistent with the results reported in other studies on the relationship between substance abuse and IPV in India.8 28 Research across the globe indicates an association between alcohol use and increased aggression and domestic violence.34 35 Alcohol consumption has been linked to physical, emotional and sexual violence, with the violence occurring when the perpetrators were under the influence. In the current study, participants reported that women were likely to experience sexual violence, such as rape, when men were intoxicated. Therefore, policies that protect individuals from non-consensual sexual activity, including in the context of marriage, should be reaffirmed and upheld by all community members.

This study has implications for public health practice in India. Violence prevention is a critical aspect of public health,36 as it can impact the physical and emotional well-being of individuals in a community, especially women. Further research is needed to prevent cyclical violence and the stigma associated with IPV, which leads to victims being blamed and further abused for speaking out. Women should not just be empowered to speak out, but there is a need to address the social norms that allow IPV to persist, as well as a need for access to support systems where women can receive the help they need. While legal action may help mitigate violence in the short term, societal and political commitment is crucial to preventing future IPV and supporting IPV victims in the long term. As reported by the participants, violence prevention needs to occur at multiple levels (individual, family, community and institutional/political) to address the problem through intersectoral action.37 For example, it is critical to strengthen primary healthcare (PHC) and the National Mental Health Programme in India by committing to three evidence-based interventions: (1) community-based screening of common mental disorders, (2) psychological first aid by community health workers and (3) implementing the LIVES framework (Listen, Inquire, Validate, Enhance safety and Support) at the PHC level with referrals for further care.38 39

There were several strengths and limitations to this study. Regarding strengths, the study included a heterogeneous sample of participants (male and female; adolescents and adults; married and unmarried individuals) from different villages in a peri-urban population in southern India. The in-depth, thematic approach of this study provided a more nuanced, unique and diverse perspective on IPV in this community.

A limitation of the study was the effect of social desirability bias, whereby participants may not have wanted to discuss IPV issues in their communities. Some people who have experienced or witnessed violence may avoid talking about any occurrences of IPV due to fear of retaliation, while perpetrators of violence may also be cautious in their responses. To minimise such biases, study participants were assured that the information they shared during the IDIs was confidential, and FGDs were moderated to ensure that the study participants felt safe. The current study did not specifically focus on coercive and controlling behaviour in marriage, many of which are normalised and culturally accepted. Therefore, future studies should explore these types of behaviours in marriage. Although the findings may not be generalisable to other parts of India and Southeast Asia, results from this study highlight the frequency of occurrences of IPV and the need to mitigate violence within homes and in the community.

Conclusions

IPV remains prevalent in communities in peri-urban India, and the determinants of IPV include lower socioeconomic status, the lack of support systems, work-related stress, alcohol consumption, perceived disobedience and infidelity. Community support for victims of IPV was challenging to provide, considering the misogynistic attitudes among men, the perceived stigma related to IPV among women and the lack of awareness of the mental and physical health consequences of violence. Health system recommendations include developing survivor-centric, community-based interventions for screening, psychological first aid and referral for cases of IPV.
