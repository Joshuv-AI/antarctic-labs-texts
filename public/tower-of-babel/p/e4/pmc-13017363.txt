Suppression of haptoglobin and loss of striatal neurons in mice chronically exposed to chlorpyrifos-contaminated drinking water
Authors: Emma Fikse, Faith Anderson, Soyeon Cho, Jessica Landry, Elisa Carloni, Karl Biggs, Kathleen Paul, Thomas Daley, T Y Chang, Arminja Kettenbach, Matthew C Havrda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to agricultural chemicals is a risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD). Chlorpyrifos (CPF) is an organophosphate insecticide widely used in agricultural and occupational settings. Epidemiological studies have associated CPF exposure with developmental impairments and an increased risk of AD and PD. Experimental characterization of the impact of chronic, systemic CPF exposure is essential for understanding how organophosphates actually influence disease risk. Multiple studies have assessed the effects of gestational exposure to CPF in preclinical models. To model exposure faced by adults, we administered CPF-contaminated drinking water to mice from 6 to 22 mo of age. This chronic exposure led to systemic effects, including reduced levels of the acute-phase protein haptoglobin (HTP) in both plasma and liver. Notably, the combination of aging and CPF exposure resulted in astrogliosis in the hippocampus and striatum, as well as neuronal loss in the striatum, primarily due to the loss of GAD65/67-immunoreactive interneurons. Having identified CPF-driven suppression of HPT in peripheral tissues, we examined HPT expression in brain tissues. We readily detected HPT expression in brain microglia. We then cultivated primary microglia and found that CPF exposure decreased HPT secretion in vitro. These findings indicate systemic and neurotoxic effects resulting from adult exposure to CPF-contaminated water.

Age-related neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD), pose significant health challenges as the global aging population increases (Dorsey et al. 2007). Most neurodegenerative diagnoses lack a known genetic cause, with as few as 10% to 15% of cases associated with hereditary mutations. It is widely accepted that complex gene–environment interactions influence the onset and progression of neurodegenerative disorders (Cannon and Greenamyre 2013; Eid et al. 2019). Epidemiological studies have established a link between pesticide exposure and an elevated risk of developing neurodegenerative diseases (Hatcher et al. 2008; Costello et al. 2009; Amor et al. 2010; Tanner et al. 2011). Although a growing body of evidence associates pesticide exposure with neurodegeneration, there are gaps in our understanding of how chronic systemic pesticide exposure impacts the central nervous system (CNS).

The organophosphate (OP) pesticide chlorpyrifos (CPF) is one of the most prevalent and readily available OPs in the United States, persisting in the environment for extended periods of time (Mackay et al. 2014). CPF is primarily thought to function as an acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitor; however, other neurotoxic mechanisms have been identified (Rohlman et al. 2011; Anderson et al. 2021b). The use of CPF in agriculture leads to inadvertent contamination of air, soil, surface water (such as rivers and lakes), and groundwater (Poet et al. 2014; Tudi et al. 2022; Wołejko et al. 2022). CPF has a half-life ranging from 2 to 120 d, and its degradation yields several metabolites, including chlorpyrifos oxon (CPO) and 3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCPy) (Solomon et al. 2014). CPO is considered more toxic as it retains the well-established AChE inhibition properties of the CPF (Solomon et al. 2014); however, TCPy is more persistent in the environment and in humans, where TCPy can be detected in urine, indicating CPF exposure (Wołejko et al. 2022). OP pesticide residues constitute over 50% of those detected in the environment (Wołejko et al. 2022). Broadly, there are 4 groups of people most at risk for CPF exposure: (i) workers preparing and applying CPF, (ii) farmers and field workers in agricultural settings where CPF is widely used, (iii) agricultural community members who may be exposed to CPF or its metabolites in the nearby environment, and (iv) consumers whose food was grown under conditions of pesticide use (Tudi et al. 2022; Wołejko et al. 2022). Although a ban by the Environmental Protection Agency limited CPF use from August 2021 to February 2023, a subsequent court decision overturned this ban, and CPF tolerances have been reinstated. The continued use of CPF, together with an aging US population exposed to a lifetime of environmental toxicants, prompts efforts to gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of CPF toxicity.

Acute exposure to CPF can result in cholinergic syndrome, also known as cholinergic toxicity or cholinergic crisis, which manifests with symptoms including cramps, increased salivation, lacrimation, muscular weakness, paralysis, muscular fasciculation, diarrhea, and blurry vision. Such symptoms are attributed to AchE inhibition and, given adequate time, can be managed or reversed in a clinical setting (Tudi et al. 2022). New evidence suggests that chronic low-dose CPF exposure may have neurological effects (Naughton and Terry 2018; Tudi et al. 2022; Wołejko et al. 2022). CPF can cross the blood–brain barrier (Parran et al. 2005; Williamson Leah et al. 2006; Li and Ehrich 2013; Kopjar et al. 2018), and epidemiological studies associate chronic subtoxic CPF exposure with various neurological disorders. Rauh et al. (2006, 2011, 2012) consistently reported adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in children prenatally exposed to CPF. In their 2006 study, children exposed to CPF prenatally had significantly higher odds of developmental delays at age 3 yrs, with an odds ratio of 2.37 for mental delay and 4.52 for psychomotor delay. The 2011 follow-up study with 7-yr-old children showed that every 1-SD increase in CPF exposure was associated with a 1.4% decline in IQ and a 2.8% decline in working memory. A subsequent 2012 neuroimaging study from the same research group identified structural brain abnormalities linked to prenatal CPF exposure. More recently, Khan et al. (2024) extended these findings, reporting a positive association between household OP exposure and internalizing behavior in children (β = 2.06, P = 0.05) (Rauh et al. 2006, 2011, 2012; Khan et al. 2024).

Beyond developmental outcomes, several studies have linked CPF exposure to neurodegenerative diseases such as PD and AD (Dhillon et al. 2008; Gatto et al. 2009; Hayden et al. 2010; Manthripragada et al. 2010). Dhillon et al. (2008) observed a significantly increased risk of PD with CPF use (OR = 2.0; 95% CI = 1.02 to 3.8) (Dhillon et al. 2008), whereas Gatto et al. (2009) reported an odds ratio of 1.87 (95% CI = 1.05 to 3.31) in individuals exposed via well water (Gatto et al. 2009). Hayden et al. (2010) further noted a higher risk of AD with OP exposure (HR = 1.53; 95% CI = 1.05 to 2.23) (Hayden et al. 2010). Collectively, these human studies provide a strong foundation for preclinical evaluation of CPF’s impact on the CNS. However, as with many observational studies, they are limited by potential confounders, exposure misclassification, and the challenge of establishing causality.

Mechanistic studies in rodents suggest that early-life CPF exposure leads to the loss of nigral neurons later in life (Zhang et al. 2015), whereas exposure at occupational levels contributes to microglial dysfunction and exacerbates AD-related symptoms (Voorhees et al. 2019). Conversely, some experimental findings indicate that AChE inhibition may actually be neuroprotective (Ye et al. 2010; Zhang and Greenberg 2012), and AChE inhibitors are often prescribed as cognition-enhancing medications for AD treatment (Sharma 2019). Such discrepancies underscore the need for ongoing characterization of the effects of systemic CPF exposure.

Although there is a limited body of work on the mechanisms of CPF toxicity, evidence suggests that cellular mechanisms extend beyond AchE inhibition (Naughton and Terry 2018). Recent work from our laboratory indicates that CPF exposure stresses neuronal proteostasis machinery, ultimately inducing intrinsic apoptosis (Anderson et al. 2021b). Additional CPF exposure studies indicate mechanisms of toxicity affecting mitochondrial function and viability (Middlemore-Risher et al. 2011), axonal transport (Gearhart et al. 2007; Terry et al. 2007; Hernandez et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2017), oxidative stress (Kaur et al. 2007), and neuroinflammation (Mense et al. 2006; Banks and Lein 2012; Jang et al. 2015; Varma et al. 2024). All of which can contribute to and exacerbate neurodegenerative pathology (Anderson et al. 2023). Notably, many previous mechanistic studies utilize in vitro models (Gearhart et al. 2007; Kaur et al. 2007; Middlemore-Risher et al. 2011; Gao et al. 2017; Anderson et al. 2021b) or mimic acute (Anderson et al. 2021b), neonatal (Levin et al. 2002), or developmental exposure (Varma et al. 2024). Chronic exposure paradigms that incorporate aging as a variable are necessary to understand how continuous CPF exposure affects the CNS and to elucidate the mechanistic changes that confer a risk of neurodegeneration.

In this study, we exposed C57BL/6 mice to CPF in their drinking water for 16 mo to model adult community and occupational exposure, allowing us to examine the effects of long-term CPF exposure during aging. CPF exposure had a minimal impact on overall health and behavior, but led to a systemic suppression of haptoglobin (HPT), an acute-phase protein that neutralizes oxidative damage (MacKellar and Vigerust 2016). We observed altered glial morphology and number in striatal and hippocampal regions, coinciding with the loss of striatal neurons. Our lifelong exposure study reveals systemic and neurotoxic changes related to chronic CPF intoxication and aging, identifies a specific neuronal subtype vulnerable to CPF exposure, and emphasizes suppression of HPT as an indicator of OP exposure.

Materials and methods

In vivo exposure study design

Mouse experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by Dartmouth’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). 48 C57BL/6J (WT) mice (Stock No. 000664, Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, Maine) were included in the study, with 32 assigned to the behavioral cohort and 16 allocated for baseline and intermediary timepoints for tissue collection. Mice were multi-housed in cages of 3 to 4. CPF (45395) stock solutions were prepared in EtOH and diluted to working solutions containing 1e−5 v/v EtOH. Mice were aged to 6 mo and then continuously maintained on either 5 mg/kg/d CPF (45395) supplemented water (Zhang et al. 2015; Lan et al. 2017) (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts) or vehicle throughout the remainder of their lifespan. The 5 mg/kg/d dose is comparable to or lower than doses used in other rodent CPF exposure studies, which have led to biological effects such as dopaminergic loss (Zhang et al. 2015), changes in hypothalamic neuropeptides (Tait et al. 2009), impairments in axonal transport (Hernandez et al. 2015), and microglia dysfunction associated with cognitive deficits and neurodegeneration (Voorhees et al. 2019). The delivery method was selected to mimic passive oral administration, reminiscent of human exposure to contaminated well water. Light-impermeant bottles were used for CPF-containing water, as CPF is a light-sensitive compound. Water consumption per cage was measured at each replenishment, and dosages were adjusted to maintain the target dose of 5 mg/kg/d. Body weight was recorded monthly.

CPF and TCPy measurements in drinking water and mouse tissues

Measurement of CPF and related metabolites in drinking water, mouse tissue, and plasma was conducted by the Agricultural Chemistry Laboratory, a joint facility between Louisiana State University and Louisiana Department of Agriculture and Forestry. In the pesticide residue section of the laboratory, analyses were performed using ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution accurate mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRAMS) on a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer. All equipment, glassware, and storage are exclusively dedicated to the use of the pesticide residue section to ensure accurate trace-level analysis. This laboratory was selected due to its extensive experience and routine handling of similar sample types. The lab conducts semiannual proficiency testing, administered by the State of Wisconsin under a US EPA grant, which assesses competency in detecting pesticides in various matrices, including CPF, CPO, and TCPy.

Behavioral analysis

Behavioral assessments were conducted on a cohort that consisted of 32 mice, 16 per treatment (8 males and 8 females). Plasma was harvested from these mice at the 6-mo baseline and 22-mo time points via tail nick. Behavioral analysis was evaluated longitudinally over 18 mo, with baseline measurements made immediately before the exposure period (6 mo) and repeated measures made at 4-mo intervals. Open field studies were conducted as previously described (Martinez et al. 2017; von Herrmann et al. 2020) using a computer-assisted photobeam detection system (TruScan, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, Massachusetts). Animal activity was recorded in 30-min sessions. Outcome measures included total distance traveled and time spent in margins. Barnes maze assessment was performed as described (Attar et al. 2013). Briefly, standard Barnes mazes (Pitts 2018) with a total of 20 holes (2 in diameter) equally spaced around the perimeter, 1 in from the edge. Fluorescent lights were located ∼4 to 5 ft directly above both mazes. Identical visual cues were mounted around the room in the same locations relative to the mazes. After each mouse was removed from the maze, the entire maze was disinfected with 1:16 diluted Clidox reagent to remove any residual odor cues. The Barnes Maze paradigm consisted of 3 phases carried out over a 6-d time course. Phase 1: Habituation (1 d)—The mice were placed in a 1000 mL beaker for 30 s in the maze’s center. Then, the mice were slowly (10 to 15 s) guided to the escape hole by moving the beaker. The mice were given 3 min to independently enter the escape hole. If, after 3 min, they did not enter on their own, they were nudged into the hole with the beaker. They were allowed to stay in the escape hole for 1 min before being returned to their home cage. Phase 2: Training (2 d)—Day 1 Training: The mice were placed in an opaque cylinder in the maze’s center for 15 s, the next day. After the holding period, the cylinder was removed, and the mice were allowed to explore the maze for 2 min. If they found the escape hole, they were allowed to remain there for 1 min before being placed back in their holding cage. If they did not find the escape hole, they were gently guided to it using the glass beaker and allowed to enter the hole independently. If they did not enter the hole within 3 min, they were nudged into it with the beaker. If they still did not enter after being nudged for 1 min, they were manually placed in the hole and allowed to remain there before being returned to their holding cage. This process was repeated twice for 3 training sessions on the first day’s 3 training sessions. A period of 10 to 15 min was allowed between each training session. Day 2 Training: The process above was repeated twice during the second day of training, resulting in 5 training sessions across the 2 training days. Phase 3: Probe (1 d)—2 d after the last training day, the escape hole was removed, and the mice were placed in an opaque cylinder in the maze’s center for 15 s. After the holding period, the cylinder was removed, and the mice were allowed to explore the maze for 2 min. After 2 min, they were returned to their holding cage. The 5 training sessions for each mouse were hand-scored for latency to the first hole (s), total number of hole nose pokes, and latency to enter the escape hole (s). All probe day sessions were recorded using a Basler acA1300-60gm GigE camera (Basler Inc., Exton, PA) and analyzed using EthoVision XT software (Noldus, Leesburg, VA). Outcome measures included total distance traveled and latency to the goal/escape hole.

Immunohistochemistry and cell quantification

Following live-animal studies, mice were terminally anesthetized and serially perfused with ice-cold PBS and formalin. Brains and other organs were harvested and postfixed in formalin for endpoint histologic analysis. Plasma was harvested via cardiac stick, and brains and other organs were harvested, sectioned, and either flash-frozen or formalin-fixed. RNA was collected from brains and livers using the Trizol (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts) extraction protocol and the Qiagen RNeasy (74104, Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland) protocol.

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed as previously described (Martinez et al. 2017; von Herrmann et al. 2020). Briefly, formalin-fixed tissues were paraffin-embedded, and 5 μm histological sections were prepared from the brain regions described. Immunohistochemical studies were conducted using protocols optimized by the Dartmouth Research Pathology Shared Resource using an automated staining system (Leica Bond Max, Buffalo Grove, Illinois). Sections were stained with either H&E or one of the following antibodies: Ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA1) (NB100-1028, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, Colorado), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (2021-05, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California), neuronal nuclei (NeuN) (ab177487, Abcam, Cambridge Biomedical Campus, Cambridge, United Kingdom), and Glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 and 67 (GAD65 and GAD67) (ab183999, Abcam, Cambridge Biomedical Campus, Cambridge, United Kingdom). Cell counting was performed using the computer-assisted Optical Fractionator Method (Stereo Investigator, Microbrightfield, Williston, Vermont).

RNAScope and immunohistochemistry

Adjacent sections to those analyzed as described above were prepared for dual immunohistochemical and mRNA analysis using RNAScope technology. The RNAscope probes, additional RNAScope reagents, and protocol were provided by Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACD) (Newark, California). Paraffin-embedded sections were baked at 60 °C for 1 h and deparaffinized for target retrieval. Sections were then incubated with RNAScope probes for 2 h at 40 °C, washed, and incubated with AMP1 for 30 min, AMP2 for 30 min, and AMP3 for 15 min at 40 °C. Depending on the RNAScope probe channel, sections were incubated with C1, C2, or C3 for 15 min at 40 °C, washed, and then incubated with TSA Vivid Fluorophore 520 (323271) for 30 min at 40 °C. Sections were washed and incubated in the provided HRP blocking solution for 15 min at 40 °C.

To combine RNAScope with immunohistochemistry, sections were blocked and stained with preconjugated antibodies for Iba1 (Alexa Fluor 555 Conjugate, 36618), GFAP (Alexa Fluor 647 Conjugate, 15913), and NeuN (Alexa Fluor 647 Conjugate, 62994) following the immunofluorescent protocol described above. Sections were stained with DAPI and coverslipped with Prolong Gold antifade mounting media (ThermoFisher, P10144). Confocal images were processed for visualization in Fiji (ImageJ).

Establishment and exposure of primary cultures

Primary mixed glial cultures were established as previously described (Anderson et al. 2021a). Briefly, brains from neonatal mice (P0 to P3) were dissociated using 0.25% trypsin without EDTA (Gibco, 15050057) and repeated trituration using a pipette. Brain homogenate was plated on poly-L-lysine (Millipore Sigma, P4707-50ML) coated T75 culture flasks (2 brains/flask) in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; GIBCO). The following day, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS and then cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and 10% L929-conditioned media (Biggs et al. 2025). After 7 to 10 d, loosely attached microglia were shaken off the astrocyte layer by incubation on an orbital shaker for 2 h at 200 RPM (Biggs et al. 2025).

Primary BMDM cultures were established as previously described (Weischenfeldt and Porse 2008; Lee et al. 2013). Briefly, femurs from 3- to 4-mo-old WT mice were isolated, and the bone marrow was collected using a solution of DMEM + F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Cells were cultured following repeated pipetting, 2 washes with DMEM + F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S, and a final wash with plating media DMEM + F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and 30% L929 conditioned media as a source of growth factors.

For experiments, primary cultures were seeded in appropriately sized well plates and allowed to adhere overnight in DMEM/10% FBS/1% P/S (for glia) or DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and 30% L929 (for BMDM). The following day, CPF (45395, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts), TCPy (33972, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts), and Rotenone (557368, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts) exposure were conducted in serum-free media as previously reported (Zhang et al. 2017), consisting of 4-h treatments of 10 µM CPF and TCPy, and 50 µM Rotenone. Conditioned media were collected and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris, then frozen at −80 °C until analysis via ELISA. RNA was collected using Qiagen RNeasy (74104, Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland) protocols.

Molecular assays

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was measured using an enzymatic assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri) in mouse plasma and conditioned tissue culture media to determine cell viability and cytotoxicity. Mouse plasma was diluted 1:50 in cell culture media, whereas the conditioned media from glial cultures remained undiluted.

HPT levels were measured using a colorimetric immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (ab272472, Abcam, Cambridge Biomedical Campus, Cambridge, United Kingdom) in (i) crude plasma collected from mice exposed to CPF-contaminated drinking water and (ii) conditioned media from glial and BMDM exposed cultures. Dilutions were made according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cholesterol levels, including high-density lipoproteins (HDL) and low-density lipoproteins (LDL), were measured in the plasma and liver tissues of 22-mo-old CPF-exposed mice using a colorimetric assay (ab65390, Abcam, Cambridge Biomedical Campus, Cambridge, United Kingdom). Dilutions were made according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma APOA1 levels were measured using an ELISA (ab238260, Abcam, Cambridge Biomedical Campus, Cambridge, United Kingdom). Dilutions were made according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Total RNA from tissues collected from baseline, CPF-exposed, and vehicle-exposed mice were reverse-transcribed with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California). HPT expression was measured by real-time PCR analysis using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California) with SYBR Green reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California). All primers were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, Iowa).

Statistical analysis

Experiments were conducted with 3 or more biological replicates in technical triplicate, and all data were expressed as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Shapiro–Wilk tests for normality were performed before statistical analysis. For survival analyses, Kaplan–Meier curves were generated and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. For multiple comparisons and longitudinal behavioral data, 2-way repeated measures ANOVAs and mixed-effects analyses were performed, with Šidák’s correction applied for post hoc tests to control family-wise error whereas maintaining statistical power. When normality was violated in at least 1 group, nonparametric Mann–Whitney tests were conducted, and results are summarized alongside parametric outcomes in Table S1. For behavioral analyses, parametric and nonparametric analyses provided similar results; therefore, ANOVA results were used for data presentation in figures (Table S1). Paired data were analyzed using 1-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons and, in 1 case, a paired t-test. RNA expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, which showed stable expression across all liver tissue samples in our dataset and was therefore used as the reference gene. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with marginal significance noted for values between 0.05 and 0.10. All statistical tests were conducted with GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, Colorado).

Results

Persistent plasma TCPy in mice exposed to CPF-contaminated drinking water

Exposure to CPF during gestational and neonatal development results in neurological deficits (Levin et al. 2002; Rauh et al. 2006, 2011, 2012; Varma et al. 2024); however, its potential toxicity in aging adults is less understood. To explore the relationship between aging and CPF exposure, we subjected 6-mo-old C57BL/6 (WT) mice (n = 32) to chronic CPF exposure in drinking water (5 mg/kg/d) or vehicle until 22 mo of age (Fig. 1A). Throughout the exposure period, we conducted motor and cognitive behavioral assessments every 4 mo in a subset of animals (n = 16 per group). Tissues and plasma were obtained at interim timepoints and the final endpoint for longitudinal evaluation of neurological changes and CPF bioaccumulation (Fig. 1A).

Longitudinal chlorpyrifos (CPF) exposure paradigm showing that CPF-treated water was consumed more readily than vehicle-treated water, which did not affect survival or body weight and resulted in the detection of 3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCPy) in plasma. Behavioral testing and blood/tissue collections took place every 4 mo until the 18-mo mark. At 22 mo, the remaining mice were sacrificed, and tissues were collected. B) CPF degrades in aqueous solution and in vivo, resulting in 2 primary metabolites, Chlorpyrifos oxon (CPO) and TCPy. C) CPF rapidly degraded in drinking water, where CPF, CPO, and TCPy were detected at similar levels within 72 h. D) In mice maintained on CPF-supplemented water, TCPy was detectable in the plasma. Survival, body weight, and water consumption of mice maintained on vehicle- and CPF-supplemented water were analyzed over the course of the 22-mo study. E to G) CPF administration did not affect survival, as assessed by Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank (Mantel–Cox) tests (overall: P = 0.7664; by sex: P = 0.8066; by sex × treatment: P = 0.3659). Water consumption (mLs) was collected every week and averaged across cages. J) Mice exposed to CPF-supplemented water drank more than mice on vehicle-supplemented water, ANOVA P < 0.0001.

CPF breaks down in both water and living organisms into 2 primary metabolites: CPO and TCPy (Fig. 1B) (Solomon et al. 2014). To confirm CPF dosing in the contaminated drinking water paradigm, we first evaluated CPF, CPO, and TCPy levels in the CPF-supplemented water provided to the treatment group at various time points (0, 48, 72, and 96 h). As expected, CPF rapidly degrades in water (Solomon et al. 2014), decreasing from a high level of 10 parts per million (ppm) at 0 h to levels comparable to those of TCPy and CPO at 96 h. TCPy and CPO levels remained low and stable throughout the 96-h measurement period (Fig. 1C). Based on these data, we refreshed water every 48 h throughout the study (Fig. 1C). We also assessed the concentrations of CPF, CPO, and TCPy in the plasma of our mice at 3 time points during the longitudinal study (6 mo baseline, 14 , and 22 mo). We consistently detected the more stable TCPy metabolite in the plasma of mice receiving CPF-supplemented water (Fig. 1D). We chose to quantify TCPy directly rather than perform AChE activity assays, which have already been extensively reported in rodent models (Chakraborti et al. 1993; Chanda and Pope 1996), because TCPy represents the clinically relevant biomarker (Wołejko et al. 2022). This strategy enhances translational relevance by aligning our measurements with the same biomarker applied in human populations. The plasma TCPy concentrations measured in our study were modestly higher than the 95th percentile reported for the US general population (0.0099 ppm) (Barr et al. 2005) and lower than the range observed in occupationally exposed populations (13.4 to 8,052 ppm) (Farahat et al. 2011; Singleton et al. 2015). These values indicate that the exposure paradigm used here exceeds background levels but remains within the anticipated range of human occupational exposure.

Chronic exposure to CPF in drinking water did not affect survival or body weight (Fig. 1E to I). The only factor influencing body weight in our study was sex, with males weighing more than females (Fig. 1I). During the initial 300 d of the study, deaths occurred only in the CPF-treated group (Fig. 1E). In total, 8 mice (5 females, 3 males) in the CPF group died prior to study completion, 4 of which were euthanized after the 300-d mark due to poor health. In the vehicle group, 5 mice (3 females, 2 males) were euthanized after the 300-d mark due to poor health. Although lethal toxicity was observed only in CPF-exposed animals, the incidence was low and did not reach statistical significance in this cohort. Notably, mice receiving CPF-supplemented water consumed significantly more water than those in the vehicle group (Fig. 1J).

Exposure to CPF-supplemented water decreases circulating and liver HPT in mice

Crude plasma was collected at the 6-mo baseline and the 22-mo endpoint, during which various proteins and lipids were examined to evaluate the toxic effects of CPF (Fig. 2A to D). LDH levels in circulation serve as a general indicator of cell death resulting from disease or injury (Wroblewski and Gregory 1961). No significant differences in plasma LDH levels were observed in mice exposed to CPF (Fig. 2A). We measured a series of plasma proteins potentially related to CPF exposure and cell stress (Bertaggia et al. 2014; Chaubey et al. 2016; MacKellar and Vigerust 2016; Hofmaenner et al. 2022; Marques-da-Silva and Lagoa 2023) in plasma samples from 6-mo baseline, 22-mo CPF-treated, and 22-mo vehicle-treated mice. HPT is an acute-phase plasma glycoprotein that binds to free hemoglobin in instances of erythrocyte distress, thereby facilitating its removal from circulation through scavenger receptors expressed by phagocytic immune cells (Andersen et al. 2012; MacKellar and Vigerust 2016). The resulting complex prevents excess extracellular hemoglobin from causing oxidative damage and inflammation associated with hemolysis (Boretti et al. 2009; Bertaggia et al. 2014; MacKellar and Vigerust 2016). HPT is primarily produced in hepatocytes and macrophages, which are central to systemic detoxification, metabolic regulation, and immune defense (MacKellar and Vigerust 2016). These properties make HPT a compelling candidate for toxicant exposure, as reductions may reflect impaired hemoglobin transport, early hepatic stress that limits detoxification capacity, and dysregulated immune responses. In our study, HPT levels were found to be significantly lower in the CPF-exposed mice relative to baseline, a reduction not observed in vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2B).

A panel of plasma protein and lipid levels indicates that CPF administration leads to changes in circulating and liver Haptoglobin (HPT). B) Plasma HPT levels decreased in mice administered CPF at the 22-mo end-point compared with mice at the 6-mo baseline, ANOVA P = 0.0068, multiple comparison P = 0.0055 (baseline vs CPF). Age and CPF administration did not affect circulating C) cholesterol levels, ANOVA P = 0.7573, or D) levels of Apolipoprotein A-1, ANOVA P = 0.9449. E to G) Liver H&E staining from 6-mo baseline, 22-mo vehicle, and 22-mo CPF-exposed mice. No differences were visually detected. H) RNA isolated from liver tissues indicates a marginal downregulation of HPT transcription in the livers of 22-mo CPF-exposed mice, ANOVA P = 0.0754, multiple comparison P = 0.0644 (vehicle vs CPF). The dotted line represents HPT at 6-mo baseline.

Having noted a change in HPT, we measured levels of various lipids and apolipoproteins, all of which are also involved in reserve liver transport. Cholesterol, lipoproteins such as HDL, and apolipoproteins, including APOA1, possess immunomodulatory properties. They also act as scavengers by binding, neutralizing, and assisting toxins in their transportation to the liver for processing or removal (Hofmaenner et al. 2022). Furthermore, free unesterified cholesterol contributes to the composition of lipid membranes and is essential for the intracellular transport of toxins, as toxins can utilize cholesterol-rich lipid rafts to infiltrate cells (Eum et al. 2009; Clark et al. 2016; Cacciottolo et al. 2020; Marques-da-Silva and Lagoa 2023). There were no changes in circulating cholesterol (total, free/unesterified, HDL, and LDL/VLDL) or APOA1 (Fig. 2C and D), indicating that the decrease in circulating HPT is not due to global deficits in toxin transport to the liver.

Hepatocytes and macrophages are major sources of circulating HPT (MacKellar and Vigerust 2016). Histological analysis, utilizing hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fischer et al. 2008), indicated the anticipated changes in liver histology related to age but did not reveal significant changes resulting from CPF exposure (Fig. 2E to G). Nevertheless, a transcriptional analysis of HPT in mRNA prepared from liver tissues indicates that exposure to CPF modestly but significantly reduced HPT expression in mice treated with CPF compared with vehicle-treated animals (Fig. 2H). Collectively, our findings suggest widespread changes in HPT expression and protein levels resulting from lifelong CPF intoxication.

CPF exposure improves aspects of spatial memory in mice

Drawing from epidemiological and experimental evidence that exposure to CPF and other environmental toxins increases the risk of PD and AD (Dhillon et al. 2008; Gatto et al. 2009; Hayden et al. 2010; Manthripragada et al. 2010; Zhang et al. 2015; Voorhees et al. 2019), we evaluated motor, anxiety-like, and cognitive functions in mice chronically exposed to CPF-supplemented water, which are behaviors commonly affected in neurodegenerative disorders and disease models (Klein et al. 1999; Head and Isom 2010; Mazzoni et al. 2012; Attar et al. 2013; Levenson et al. 2014). Beginning at the 6-mo baseline, we assessed the mice in both the CPF and vehicle groups every 4 mo until they reached 18 mo of age, using open field (Fig. 3A to C) and Barnes maze (Fig. 3D to F) paradigms. We identified an age-related decline in motor function in both groups, confirming findings from previous studies on aging in mice (Forster et al. 1996; Noda et al. 2020). However, CPF exposure did not affect ambulatory activity, as indicated by the total distance traveled throughout the study (Fig. 3B). To assess anxiety-like behaviors, we evaluated the time mice spent in the margins of the open field arena. Mice typically explore the perimeter of an arena, a behavior known as thigmotaxis, where an increase signifies anxiolytic symptomology (Simon et al. 1994). Our findings indicated that CPF exposure did not alter anxiety-like behaviors (Fig. 3C).

We employed the Barnes maze paradigm to assess learning and memory, using training and testing phases to assess spatial learning and memory, respectively. Considering CPF’s AchE inhibition properties, we analyzed the total distance traveled during testing compared with individuals’ 6-mo baseline performance, whereas also measuring the latency to the correct or target hole. Mice exposed to CPF covered greater distances during the task (Fig. 3E) but located the target hole more quickly (Fig. 3F) than vehicle-treated animals. Because we did not detect changes in spontaneous movement (Fig. 3B), we interpreted this finding to indicate enhancement in spatial memory in CPF-exposed mice detectable as an increase in goal-directed activity.

Region-specific neuronal loss and astrogliosis in mice chronically exposed to CPF-contaminated water

The hippocampus and striatum play a crucial role in spatial learning and memory (Bird and Burgess 2008; Du et al. 2023) and are both susceptible to aging and age-related neurodegeneration (Przedborski et al. 2003; Pandya and Patani 2021). H&E staining of hippocampal areas CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG), along with the striatum from 6-mo baseline, 22-mo CPF, and 22-mo vehicle mice, did not reveal major alterations in cellular morphology or tissue architecture (Fig. S1). We next conducted immunohistochemical analysis using markers for neurons (NeuN), microglia (Iba1), and astrocytes (GFAP). CPF exposure did not lead to neuronal loss (Fig. 4A to D) or microgliosis (Fig. 4E to H) in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the hippocampus. However, we did note an age and CPF-related effect on the total number of astrocytes in those regions, indicative of astrogliosis (Fig. 4I to L). These findings suggest that astrocytes may be particularly sensitive to CPF exposure within hippocampal regions.

More than 95% of striatal neurons are inhibitory GABAergic medium spiny neurons (MSNs) (Graveland and Difiglia 1985; Burke et al. 2017). These neurons integrate signals and regulate motor and cognitive functions through inhibitory projections and collateral connections (Burke et al. 2017). We conducted immunohistochemical analyses using antibodies targeting GAD65/67, enzymes involved in GABA synthesis, and NeuN (neurons) to determine if the neuronal loss we identified (Fig. 5A and B) resulted from the loss of MSNs (Fig. 6A). CPF exposure resulted in an age-related loss of striatal GAD65/67/NeuN dual-immunoreactive MSNs (Fig. 6B). Interestingly, although there was a statistically significant decline in NeuN immunoreactive striatal neurons (Fig. 5A and B) of CPF-exposed mice at 22 mo compared with the 6-mo baseline and the 22-mo vehicle group, the loss of GAD65/67/NeuN dual immunoreactive MSNs was only observed between the 6-mo baseline and the 22-mo CPF-exposed group (Fig. 6B). The absence of a difference between the 22-mo vehicle and 22-mo CPF-exposed groups could suggest further loss of other neuronal subtypes. These findings indicate that whereas CPF exposure is toxic to MSNs in the striatum over time, additional neuronal subtypes may also be susceptible to age-related or CPF-induced neurodegeneration.

HPT is expressed in microglia and modulated by environmental toxicants in macrophage-like cells

We next determined if CPF also modulated HPT in brain tissues. Notably, recent studies have identified noncanonical functions for HPT during cerebral ischemia, a type of brain injury caused by restricted blood flow, where HPT administration modulates microglia to facilitate tissue repair (Morimoto et al. 2022). To determine the cellular localization of HPT expression in the brains of our mice, we employed RNAScope in situ hybridization in conjunction with immunohistochemistry. In the mouse brain tissue, we found clear evidence that HPT mRNA colocalizes with Iba1-positive microglia but did not identify any evidence of HPT co-localization with GFAP-positive astrocytes (Fig. 7A and B).

We investigated HPT release in the context of environmental toxicants, including CPF, in vitro, utilizing primary cultures of microglia and astrocytes. These cells were treated with CPF, TCPy, or the well-characterized metabolic toxin rotenone (Sherer et al. 2003) for 4 h, after which HPT levels were assessed in the conditioned media. In microglial cultures, HPT release significantly decreased after CPF exposure (Fig. 7C), whereas astrocyte cultures exhibited no notable changes in HPT release with toxicant exposure (Fig. 7D). Similarly, exposure of microglia to rotenone results in reduced HPT release; thus, the decrease in HPT release in microglia may indicate a broader glial response to neurotoxic stress, rather than a specific reaction to OP exposure.

Microglia function similarly to macrophages in several ways, yet they represent a distinct population within the CNS (Colonna and Butovsky 2017). We expanded our characterization of CPF on HPT regulation to include primary macrophages after observing that plasma HPT levels are reduced in 22-mo-old mice exposed to CPF (Fig. 2). We conducted in vitro studies with primary bone marrow-derived macrophages to further examine the effect of CPF on HPT regulation. Although there were no statistically significant differences in HPT concentration among treatment groups (Fig. 7E), CPF exposure was associated with a slight reduction in HPT levels compared with untreated controls (Fig. 7F). Overall, these findings suggest that microglia both express HPT and are uniquely responsive to environmental toxicants in regulating HPT expression, unlike astrocytes and peripheral macrophages, which exhibit little or inconsistent reactions when subjected to identical exposure.

Discussion

This research study models the effects of chronic exposure to CPF-contaminated drinking water during adulthood. CPF-exposed mice showed no significant changes in body weight or survival. CPF exposure resulted in a reduction in circulating and hepatic HPT levels, without affecting other readily transported lipids and proteins, such as cholesterol and apolipoproteins. CPF exposure had no impact on spontaneous or anxiety-related activity; however, it enhanced aspects of spatial memory performance in the Barnes maze. Immunohistological analysis revealed striatal neuronal loss and astrogliosis, particularly evident in the loss of the GABAergic medium spiny neurons. We confirmed that HPT is expressed in microglia and observed reduced HPT release in primary microglia exposed to CPF and other environmental toxicants. These findings suggest that whereas CPF may enhance certain cognitive functions, potentially due to its AchE-inhibitory activity, it also induces neuroinflammatory changes and neuronal loss in specific brain regions, indicating a complex interaction between CPF exposure, aging, and neurodegeneration.

Epidemiological data on developmental CPF exposure have been well documented to cause lasting behavioral deficits, including impaired cognition, altered motor functions, and increased vulnerability to neurodevelopmental disorders (Rauh et al. 2006, 2011, 2012; Khan et al. 2024). At 6 mo, mice are fully mature (Flurkey et al. 2007), which eliminates developmental exposure variables. Our study is among the first to evaluate adult CPF exposure and aging, building on previous literature that has modeled gestational (Levin et al. 2002; Zhang et al. 2015; Lan et al. 2017) and early-life exposure. Our study reveals new and more nuanced effects, in which CPF exposure leads to neuroinflammatory changes and selective neuronal loss in GABAergic MSNs, paradoxically improving certain aspects of spatial memory performance. CPF’s AchE-inhibitory properties (Solomon et al. 2014) could explain the improvement in memory performance paradigms, given that such treatments are used to enhance memory in AD patients (Sharma 2019). Thus, whereas AchE inhibition may be behaviorally advantageous in the short term, chronic increases in synaptic acetylcholine, potentially causing cholinergic toxicity via nicotinic and/or muscarinic receptors (Naughton and Terry 2018), could lead to region-specific neuronal loss and glial changes in aging organisms (Haam and Yakel 2017). This divergence from developmental exposure outcomes suggests age-dependent differences in CPF effects on glia and selective neurotoxicity. Moreover, it helps explain why short-term anti-cholinesterase treatments provide cognitive benefits to AD patients that do not appear to include actual neuroprotection (Passeri et al. 2022).

One explanation for such neurotoxic differences, that may or may not be attributable to CPF’s AchE-inhibitory mechanisms, is the independent toxic effects of CPF’s metabolites. Throughout our study, we were able to readily detect the TCPy metabolite in mouse plasma, which serves as a stable metabolite of CPF and is used as a biomarker of human exposure (Wołejko et al. 2022). Although historically viewed as a less harmful metabolite (Solomon et al. 2014), our study suggests that TCPy exposure in microglia leads to a similar reduction in HPT levels, potentially indicating that TCPy itself may exert toxic effects and contribute to chronic neurological alterations. Importantly, interspecies differences in CPF metabolism are important considerations for the determination of translational relevance (<estimating-the-maximum-safe-starting-dose-in-initial-clinical-trials-for-therapeutics-in-adult-healthy-volunteers.Pdf>). Mice and humans differ in rates of bioactivation and detoxification, which can influence dosage, plasma concentrations, and enzymatic activity (Foxenberg et al. 2007; Carr et al. 2015; Abdou et al. 2020). Taken together, these findings emphasize the need to reassess the toxicological significance of TCPy, particularly in the context of chronic low-dose exposure in aging populations.

The observed loss of striatal medium spiny neurons (MSNs) in mice chronically exposed to CPF is of particular significance, given the role of these inhibitory GABAergic neurons in regulating motor control, reward signaling, and cognitive processes (Chuhma et al. 2011). MSNs comprise over 95% of striatal neurons and are critically involved in the corticostriatal circuitry (Graveland et al. 1985; Chuhma et al. 2011; Burke et al. 2017), which is disrupted in neurodegenerative disorders such as PD (Deutch 2006; Taverna et al. 2008; Tozzi et al. 2011) and Huntington’s disease (Graveland et al. 1985; Ehrlich 2012). Our findings of MSN-specific vulnerability align with the understanding that striatal dysfunction contributes to the motor and cognitive symptoms associated with these conditions (Paulsen 2011; Bates et al. 2015; Moustafa et al. 2016; Aarsland et al. 2021). Although CPF exposure did not affect spontaneous activity or induce overt microgliosis in the striatum, the presence of astrogliosis and neuronal loss suggests underlying circuit-level changes that may precede or occur independently of behavioral decline.

Going forward, it will be important to deeply characterize behaviors in this exposure paradigm whereas also expanding the histopathological analysis to identify changes in neural subpopulations throughout regions of the brain differentially impacted across motor, memory, and mood disorders. The discrepancy between the total loss of NeuN-positive neurons and the loss of GAD65/67-positive MSNs suggests that additional non-GABAergic neuronal subtypes in the striatum are susceptible to CPF-induced age-related degeneration. Cholinergic interneurons comprise another 1% to 2% of striatal neurons and modulate striatal output and plasticity (Lim et al. 2014). Given CPF’s potent AchE properties (Solomon et al. 2014), this neuronal population may be particularly vulnerable. Although not intrinsic to striatal neurons, dopaminergic projections from both the direct and indirect pathways are crucial for MSN activation and the overall function of the striatum (Surmeier et al. 2011; Menegas et al. 2015). The convergence of literature on the role of pesticides in dopamine loss in PD further implicates this pathway as potentially susceptible to CPF toxicity (Hatcher et al. 2008; Tanner et al. 2011; Cannon and Greenamyre 2013). Collectively, MSNs, cholinergic interneurons, and dopaminergic projections may be key contributors to CPF’s neurotoxic effects on the striatum. Although our study provides evidence of MSN striatal loss, follow-up studies using additional subtype-specific markers would help clarify the vulnerability of neuronal populations and their contributions to overall neuronal loss in the striatum.

The results of this study provide a framework to evaluate HPT as a biological indicator of chronic exposure to CPF. Previous studies have indicated that microglial HPT may play a crucial role in modulating inflammatory resolution (Morimoto et al. 2022). In CPF-exposed mice, plasma levels of HPT were significantly reduced at 22 mo of age, despite no changes in other systemic markers. These reductions were mirrored at the transcriptional level in liver tissue, and brain tissue analysis revealed that microglia express HPT mRNA. Although we cannot rule out HPT production by peripheral myeloid cells, our in vitro exposure experiments confirmed that CPF and TCPy suppress HPT release more avidly in microglia compared with cultured macrophages derived from the bone marrow. This cell type specificity supports the role of microglial HPT as a potential indicator of CPF-induced neurotoxic stress, making a case for HPT as a biomarker relevant to both the peripheral and CNSs. Given the ease of detecting HPT in the clinic (MacKellar and Vigerust 2016), as well as its responsiveness to CPF exposure, HPT may have previously unrecognized potential for monitoring CPF exposure, related neurotoxicity, and systemic stress. However, further studies are required to validate HPT as a robust and reliable biomarker of CPF exposure across experimental models and in human populations.

In conclusion, our study highlights the complex and age-dependent effects of chronic CPF exposure in adult mice. The persistence of TCPy in patients and models, along with our in vitro observation of direct toxicity, suggests continued evaluation of this prevalent CPF metabolite. Findings that CPF modulates HPT in multiple tissues and experimental models suggest its promise as a practical means of identifying exposure toward preventing CPF-related toxicity. Taken together, our findings provide novel mechanistic insights into CPF-induced neurodegeneration and lay the groundwork for further investigations into CPF toxicity in aging populations, as well as the development of biomarkers for improved risk assessment and monitoring.
