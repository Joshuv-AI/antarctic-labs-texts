A Real‐World Pilot of the Collaborative Care Model for Adults With Cognitive Impairment and Neuropsychiatric Symptoms in Primary Care
Authors: Daniel Y Johnson, Erin M Staab, Peter Hahn, Carly Lusk, Erin K Zahradnik, Neda Laiteerapong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Collaborative Care Model, which integrates primary care, behavioral health, and structured care management, is a promising approach to improving outcomes for patients with cognitive impairment and neuropsychiatric symptoms. This study evaluated a pilot of Cognitive Impairment Collaborative Care (CICC) at a large urban academic medical center.MethodRetrospective chart review of adults enrolled in the first year of the program (November 1, 2021 to October 31, 2022). CICC included assessments, biweekly telehealth visits, care management, psychiatric consultation, and therapy. The primary outcome was clinical effectiveness, defined as the change in Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) scores. Secondary outcomes were adoption (number of clinicians referring), reach (patient enrollment), and implementation (number of encounters and psychiatric case reviews).ResultsTwenty‐two patients enrolled in CICC; all had depression or anxiety. About 5% of primary care clinicians referred an average of 1.6 patients (SD 1.7) in the first year. On average, patients were enrolled for 144.9 days (SD 79.7), had 2.3 encounters per month (SD 1.0), were case reviewed 8.6 times (SD 5.3), and 50% graduated in the first year. Patients exhibited significant reductions in depression (PHQ‐9: mean change −4.7, SD 5.0, p = 0.003) and anxiety scores (GAD‐7: mean change −4.3, SD 5.5, p = 0.01).ConclusionsCICC achieved modest adoption and reach with demonstrated effectiveness in reducing depression and anxiety among patients with cognitive impairment.Relevance to Clinical PracticeAmid a shortage of geriatricians and geriatric psychiatrists in the U.S., collaborative care is a possible therapeutic option for the growing geriatric population.

A third of adults over 65 years old in the United States have cognitive impairment or dementia (1, 2), and 21%–40% of people with dementia have comorbid behavioral and neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as depression and/or anxiety (3, 4, 5, 6). Without treatment, depression and anxiety in patients with cognitive impairment have been associated with greater cognitive decline (7, 8, 9, 10, 11). However, there is a national shortage of geriatricians and geriatric psychiatrists to care for these patients (12).

One treatment option that has shown promise in addressing behavioral and psychological symptoms among older adults with cognitive impairment is the Collaborative Care Model (CoCM) (13, 14, 15, 16). CoCM has four key components: a multi‐professional approach to patient care involving primary care providers, psychiatrists, and social workers; a structured management plan informed by routine symptom measurement; scheduled patient follow‐ups; and enhanced inter‐professional communication (17). A retrospective cohort study of a CoCM program for patients with dementia and depression integrated into primary care clinics and home health visits across Indiana demonstrated a reduction in depressive symptoms (15). Another prospective cohort study of a CoCM program for patients with anxiety, depression, and mild cognitive impairment in primary care clinics in Ontario, Canada showed improvements in depressive and anxiety symptoms compared to standard care (16). Additional real‐world studies are needed as these programs have not been widely adopted and implementation can be challenging (18, 19).

This study evaluates the implementation and outcomes of CoCM for adults with mild cognitive impairment and neuropsychiatric symptoms at a large urban academic medical center in Chicago, Illinois. We hypothesized that CoCM would be effective at reducing depression, anxiety, and other neuropsychiatric symptoms among older adults with cognitive impairment.

METHODS

Program Setting and Description

The CoCM was implemented in an academic general internal medicine clinic (35 faculty and 100 resident physicians; ∼28,000 patients) for adult patients with depression and/or anxiety and cognitive impairment (Cognitive Impairment Collaborative Care [CICC]) in November 2021. During the initial 4 months, the program was advertised to a limited number of clinicians while procedures were refined, and then the program was advertised more widely to clinicians. To be eligible, patients had to be over 50 years old; have mild cognitive impairment, diagnosed dementia, or a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score ≤25; and have depression, anxiety, stress, or other neuropsychiatric problems. The presence of neuropsychiatric symptoms was first assessed by the referring provider, then confirmed during the first encounter with the social worker using a Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) score cutoff of 10 and/or a General Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) score cutoff of 10. Patients were ineligible for CICC if they were already seeing a psychiatrist or in need of long‐term psychiatric care for disorders such as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder, as determined by their primary care provider or the social worker.

After a patient was referred to CICC, the social worker completed an initial assessment with the patient, consisting of a biopsychosocial assessment, the PHQ‐9 (20, 21), the GAD‐7 (22, 23) and/or the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) (24), depending on their symptoms. For patients who required collateral information on depressive symptoms, the social worker completed the Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia (CSDD) with the patient and caregiver (25). One patient was unable to answer these questions on their own, so their assessment relied entirely on their caregiver. For patients with behavioral disturbances related to dementia, caregivers completed the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) to measure neuropsychiatric symptoms (26). After the initial assessment, the social worker was expected to have telehealth visits at least every 2 weeks. Follow‐up visits consisted of psychoeducation, brief therapeutic interventions, and monthly PHQ‐9, GAD‐7, PSS, CSDD, and/or NPI assessments, depending on which were clinically warranted. The social worker consulted with the psychiatrist after the initial assessment and if the patient's PHQ‐9, GAD‐7, or NPI scores were not improving. Additionally, the social worker could refer the patient to community behavioral health clinicians for long‐term therapy, if needed for severe symptoms. The social worker and psychiatrist met remotely to review cases every week. Patients graduated from CICC after demonstrating a 50% reduction in PHQ‐9, GAD‐7, NPI, or CSDD scores for at least 1 month. This retrospective chart review used electronic health records and patient‐level data collected by the social worker. The study population included all patients enrolled in CICC from November 1, 2021 through October 31, 2022. Two reviewers independently extracted demographic and patient‐level outcome variables. Any discrepancies were discussed until resolution.

The outcomes aligned with the RE‐AIM framework, which is used to evaluate the implementation of new programs across five dimensions: reach (R), effectiveness (E), adoption (A), implementation (I), and maintenance (M) (27). Effectiveness was measured by the change in depression (PHQ‐9, CSDD), anxiety (GAD‐7, PSS), and/or neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPI). The primary outcomes were the change in PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7 scores. Reach was assessed by the total number of patients enrolled in the program. Adoption was evaluated by determining the percentage of clinicians within the clinic who referred to CICC and the mean number of referrals per clinician. Implementation was analyzed by calculating the mean number of days patients were enrolled in the program, patients' mean number of encounters from the initial encounter to graduation, patients' mean number of assessments per month, utilization of therapy, and referrals to external therapy. Patients were expected to graduate within 90 days, have two social work encounters per month, and one assessment per month. Case reviews were expected to be completed after the initial visit and when patients were not clinically improving. Implementation of psychiatric case reviews was evaluated by recording the number of case reviews per patient, the number of patients discussed per case review, and the number of medication recommendations discussed at case review. Finally, implementation of graduation procedures was evaluated by the number of patients who graduated from CICC in the first year.

Initial and final scores were compared using paired t‐tests. Patients enrolled in CICC were stratified by clinically significant scores of depression (PHQ‐9 ≥ 10), anxiety (GAD‐7 ≥ 10), or other neuropsychiatric symptoms at baseline, and their graduation status at the end of the study period. All statistical testing was two‐sided with an α level of 0.05. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). This project was deemed to be quality improvement, and an institutional quality improvement determination was obtained.

RESULTS

A total of 22 adult patients with cognitive impairment and neuropsychiatric symptoms enrolled in CICC between November 1, 2021 and October 31, 2022 (Supplemental Table S1). A majority (72.7%) of patients were 65 years old or older, and over three‐quarters (77.3%) were female. At baseline, the mean MoCA score was 19.6 (SD 6.1) among the 15 patients with assessments. Not all patients had a baseline MoCA score because they were often completed by referring providers or because a diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment was already documented. The mean baseline PHQ‐9 score was 11.8 (SD 6.4) and 15 (68.2%) patients had a PHQ‐9 score ≥10. The mean baseline GAD‐7 score was 10.8 (SD 5.6) and 14 (63.6%) patients had a baseline GAD‐7 score ≥10. Furthermore, 9 (40.9%) patients had both a baseline PHQ‐9 score ≥10 and a baseline GAD‐7 score ≥10, indicating at least moderate depressive and anxiety symptoms.

Among all patients, there were significant decreases in PHQ‐9 (mean −4.7, SD 5.0, p = 0.003) and GAD‐7 scores (mean −4.3, SD 5.5, p = 0.01) (Supplemental Table S2). There were decreases in PSS (mean −2.6, SD 3.2, p = 0.08), CSDD (mean −3.3, SD 8.2, p = 0.48), and NPI scores (mean −6.8, SD 9.4, p = 0.18) among all patients, but these changes were not significant. In the first year, 11 (50.0%) patients graduated from the program because of a 50% reduction in PHQ‐9, GAD‐7, or NPI scores. Those who did not graduate were still enrolled, lost to follow‐up, declined to continue participating, or referred to external providers (Supplemental Figure S1).

In subgroup analyses, there were decreases in PHQ‐9 (mean −8.1, SD 3.5, p < 0.001) and GAD‐7 scores (mean −6.4, SD 6.2, p = 0.02) among graduates (n = 11), but no significant decreases in PSS, CSDD, or NPI scores (Table 1). Among patients with depression (n = 14), there were decreases in PHQ‐9 (mean −5.1, SD 4.7, p = 0.01) and GAD‐7 scores (mean −5.2, SD 6.2, p = 0.03). Among patients with anxiety (n = 15), there were also decreases in PHQ‐9 (mean −8.1, SD 3.5, p < 0.001) and GAD‐7 scores (mean −6.4, SD 6.2, p = 0.02). Among patients with other neuropsychiatric symptoms (n = 6), there were no significant changes in PHQ‐9, GAD‐7, CSDD, or NPI scores (Supplemental Table S3).

As a measure of the program's reach, 22 patients enrolled out of 22 referred. Only 15.0% of attending physicians and 1.7% of resident physicians referred patients with cognitive impairment to CICC. On average, there were 1.6 (SD 1.7) referrals to CICC by these clinicians (Supplemental Table S2). Patients were enrolled in CICC for an average of 144.9 days (SD 79.7) (compared to the expected 90 days) and had 2.3 encounters per month (SD 1.0) (similar to the expected 2 encounters per month). On average, PHQ‐9 assessments were conducted 0.9 times per month (SD 0.7) and GAD‐7 assessments were conducted 0.9 times per month (SD 0.7). The social worker provided brief therapy to 18 (78.3%) patients, and 6 (26.1%) patients were referred to external behavioral health clinicians to address a higher level of need or alternative forms of therapy. Case reviews with the social worker and psychiatrist were conducted for all 22 patients (as expected), and medication recommendations were made in 14.7% of all case reviews.

DISCUSSION

Our findings showed that participation in CICC was associated with a reduction in symptoms of depression and anxiety among adults with cognitive impairment according to PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7 scores. Within the first year, half of enrolled patients exhibited a 50% reduction in PHQ‐9 or GAD‐7 scores and graduated from the program. Patients exhibited improvements in neuropsychiatric symptoms according to the NPI and CSDD, but these changes did not reach statistical significance, likely because fewer patients were evaluated using these assessments. CICC was implemented according to the original program design with consistent patient encounters and assessments.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first real‐world evaluations of CoCM for patients with comorbid cognitive impairment as implemented in regular clinical practice. A previous clinical trial of CoCM in primary care led to a greater reduction in depressive symptoms among patients with cognitive impairment compared to those receiving usual care (28). However, clinical trials may not reflect real‐world populations or implementations, particularly for patients with cognitive impairment, so real‐world evidence is needed to complement these findings (29, 30). Our study fills this gap by demonstrating the effectiveness of a real‐world implementation of CoCM at a large, urban academic medical center for depression. In addition, our study demonstrates the effectiveness of CoCM for anxiety, an outcome not evaluated in many comparable studies (15, 16, 28). The percentage of patients who graduated was slightly lower than that of other CoCM programs, likely due to the setting and smaller scale of the program (15). In addition, the medical and social complexities of our patient population—such as comorbid cognitive impairment and neuropsychiatric symptoms, in addition to limited technological proficiency and health literacy—may have limited retention and graduation from the program. Nevertheless, the feasibility of addressing neuropsychiatric problems among patients with cognitive impairment within a CoCM holds promise for improving the overall care and outcomes for these patients.

In terms of reach and adoption, a small number of patients were enrolled in our CoCM program and a limited number of clinicians referred to CoCM during its first year. Reach and adoption were likely low because the program was not widely advertised initially, and the clinic is staffed by many clinicians who have limited clinic time due to teaching and learning responsibilities, so new interventions tend to take longer than usual to take hold. Potential strategies to increase CoCM uptake include more education for clinicians on how to describe the program and engage patients, clinician champions to promote the program, regular interaction between clinicians and care managers, and accessibility of care managers to help with patient engagement (e.g., warm handoffs) (31, 32).

Implementation of CICC closely adhered to the CoCM framework. Multidisciplinary case reviews involving the social worker and psychiatrist were regularly conducted for all patients. Patients had regular follow‐up with the social worker, which is key to the success of CoCM (19), and half of the patients graduated from the program within the first year. While prior CoCM programs have noted implementation challenges such as limited institutional support, competing demands among providers, and resistance in patient engagement (18, 19), CICC benefited from a clear protocol, dedicated time for providers, integrated evaluation, and sufficient institutional support. In turn, participants in CICC demonstrated improvements in symptoms of depression and anxiety despite the medical and social complexities of the patient population.

There are several limitations. First, there was a small sample from a single healthcare institution, which limits generalizability. Second, we were unable to determine the proportion of patients referred to CICC out of the total number of potentially eligible patients. Third, the institution offered other services to patients with cognitive impairment and/or neuropsychiatric symptoms, but evaluation of these programs was outside the scope of the study. Fourth, patients were not randomly assigned to CICC and there was no control group, making it challenging to establish causality.

CONCLUSION

The CoCM was effective in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety among patients with cognitive impairment in the first year of this pilot program. The modest adoption of the program among clinicians highlights the need for increased awareness and promotion. The comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach of the program suggests it can address the complex needs of patients with cognitive impairment and neuropsychiatric problems. As the geriatric population in the U.S. expands amid a shortage of geriatricians and geriatric psychiatrists (4, 12), CoCM may help treat depression and anxiety among patients with cognitive impairment.
