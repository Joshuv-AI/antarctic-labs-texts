Correlation Between Aortic Valve Area Measured by Cardiac Computed Tomography and Transthoracic Echocardiography in Patients with Aortic Stenosis
Authors: Shehroz Sultan, Neeraj Joshi, Ali Haider Awan, Shabana kauser, Hafsa Malik Aulakh, Bilal Ghafoor, Muhammad Ammar Ajmal, Abida Perveen, Jahanzeb Malik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate assessment of aortic valve area (AVA) is essential for determining the severity of aortic stenosis (AS) and guiding clinical management. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the standard first-line imaging modality, while cardiac computed tomography (CT) provides direct anatomical planimetry and may offer complementary information in selected patients.ObjectiveTo evaluate the correlation and agreement between CT-derived and TTE-derived AVA measurements and to examine their association with clinical outcomes in patients with AS.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included 1857 consecutive adults with moderate to severe AS who underwent both TTE and cardiac CT at the Rawalpindi Institute of Cardiology between January 2023 and July 2025. Correlation between modalities was assessed using Pearson’s coefficient, while agreement was evaluated with Bland–Altman analysis. Associations between AVA thresholds (≤1.0 cm2 vs > 1.0 cm2) and adverse clinical outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsThe mean AVA measured by TTE was 0.84 ± 0.19 cm2 and by CT was 0.87 ± 0.21 cm2. A strong correlation was observed between the two modalities (r =0.82, p < 0.001), with acceptable agreement on Bland–Altman analysis. Patients with AVA ≤1.0 cm2 had significantly higher rates of adverse clinical outcomes compared with those with larger valve areas.ConclusionCT-derived AVA demonstrates strong agreement with TTE and provides complementary information for severity assessment and risk stratification in patients with aortic stenosis. Integrating CT with conventional echocardiography may improve clinical evaluation, particularly in cases with discordant or borderline findings.Keywords: Aortic stenosis, Aortic valve area, Cardiac computed tomography, Transthoracic echocardiography, And diagnostic accuracy

Introduction

Aortic stenosis (AS) is one of the most prevalent valvular heart diseases, particularly in the aging population, and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality if left untreated.1 The accurate assessment of aortic valve area (AVA) is essential in determining the severity of AS and guiding timely intervention. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remains the cornerstone of initial diagnostic evaluation due to its wide availability, non-invasiveness, and cost-effectiveness.2 The continuity equation, applied via Doppler-based velocity measurements, is commonly used in TTE to estimate AVA; however, its accuracy can be compromised by poor acoustic windows, incorrect left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) measurements, or suboptimal flow alignment.3

Cardiac computed tomography (CT), specifically multidetector CT, has emerged as a valuable adjunctive imaging modality for the anatomical assessment of the aortic valve. CT provides high spatial resolution and allows for direct planimetric measurement of AVA during mid-systole, overcoming some limitations inherent to echocardiography.4 Moreover, CT-derived AVA has been increasingly utilized in cases of low-flow, low-gradient AS, where echocardiographic parameters may be discordant or equivocal.5 Despite its growing clinical utility, CT is associated with radiation exposure and contrast administration, which limits its use in certain populations, necessitating a balanced evaluation of its correlation with established methods such as TTE.

Understanding the correlation between AVA measurements obtained by cardiac CT and TTE is critical in validating CT as a reliable alternative or complement to echocardiography in selected clinical scenarios. Previous studies have reported moderate to strong correlations between the two modalities, but discrepancies can occur due to methodological and physiological differences.6,7 This study aims to assess the degree of correlation between AVA measured by cardiac CT and TTE in patients with aortic stenosis, to better inform clinical decision-making and guide the integration of imaging modalities in the diagnostic workup of AS.

Methods

2.1. Study design and ethical approval

This study was conducted as a single-center retrospective cohort observational study at the Rawalpindi Institute of Cardiology, Pakistan. Patients were evaluated during routine clinical care and the collected data were subsequently analyzed retrospectively with follow-up outcomes available up to 12 months. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board under study ID CPSP/Sec/2025/447. All procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives.

2.2. Study population

A total of 1857 consecutive patients with a confirmed diagnosis of aortic stenosis were included between January 2023 and July 2025. Eligible patients were adults aged 18 years or older who had moderate to severe aortic stenosis identified on transthoracic echocardiography and subsequently underwent cardiac computed tomography as part of routine diagnostic or pre-interventional assessment. Patients were excluded if they had prior surgical or transcatheter aortic valve replacement, significant concomitant aortic regurgitation, active infective endocarditis, contraindication to iodinated contrast, severe renal dysfunction preventing CT imaging, or inadequate image quality on either modality.

2.3. Reference standard and outcomes

The reference standard for the assessment of aortic stenosis severity was based on an integrated evaluation by a multidisciplinary heart team comprising experienced cardiologists, cardiac imaging specialists, and cardiac surgeons. Severity classification was determined according to current guideline-recommended criteria, incorporating aortic valve area (AVA ≤1.0 cm2), transvalvular gradients, flow status, and clinical presentation, including symptom burden and functional status. All available imaging data, including transthoracic echocardiography and cardiac computed tomography, were reviewed in conjunction with clinical findings to reach a consensus decision.

For prognostic analyses, the primary endpoint was a composite of hospitalization for heart failure, aortic valve intervention (surgical aortic valve replacement or transcatheter aortic valve implantation), or cardiovascular mortality during the follow-up period.

2.4. Transthoracic echocardiography

All participants underwent comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography performed by a team of six experienced cardiologists using standard ultrasound systems. To minimize inter-rater variability, all cardiologists followed standardized acquisition and measurement protocols based on American Society of Echocardiography guidelines,8 and a subset of studies was independently reviewed for quality assurance and concordance analysis. The aortic valve area was calculated using the continuity equation derived from left ventricular outflow tract diameter, pulsed-wave Doppler velocity-time integral, and continuous-wave Doppler measurements of aortic jet velocity. All measurements followed the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography.

2.5. Cardiac computed tomography

Cardiac CT examinations were performed with a 256-slice multidetector scanner using retrospective electrocardiographic gating. Mid-systolic image reconstruction allowed direct planimetric measurement of the aortic valve area by tracing the minimal orifice on multiplanar reformatted images aligned perpendicular to the valve plane. Image analysis was conducted by two experienced cardiac radiologists who were blinded to echocardiographic measurements. The average radiation exposure during CT acquisition ranged from approximately 6 to 9 mSv, and the typical iodinated contrast volume used was 70 to 90 mL, depending on patient characteristics and scan protocol.

2.6. Data collection and blinding

Demographic variables, cardiovascular risk factors, comorbid conditions, and imaging findings were extracted from institutional clinical records. Measurements obtained from CT and echocardiography were recorded independently by separate investigators who were blinded to the results of the other modality in order to minimize measurement bias.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The relationship between CT-derived and echocardiography-derived aortic valve area measurements was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis, and agreement between modalities was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to examine the ability of imaging-derived valve area measurements to discriminate patients at risk for adverse outcomes.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of severe aortic stenosis and clinical events. Variables included demographic factors, major comorbidities, left ventricular ejection fraction, functional status, and aortic valve area measurements from imaging. Selection of variables was based on clinical relevance and previously published literature. To avoid multicollinearity, CT-derived and echocardiography-derived valve area measurements were evaluated in separate regression models. Valve intervention during follow-up was considered a clinical outcome rather than an adjustment variable. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, and a two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

A total of 1857 patients with diagnosed aortic stenosis were included in the analysis. The mean age was 68.5 ± 10.2 years, with 60.3% males (n = 1120) and 39.7% females (n = 737). Hypertension was present in 74.9%, diabetes mellitus in 43.7%, and coronary artery disease in 37.6% of patients. The mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 52.3 ± 9.6%. The average aortic valve area measured by transthoracic echocardiography was 0.84 ± 0.19 cm2, whereas cardiac computed tomography showed a slightly higher mean value of was 0.87 ± 0.21 cm2. Correlation and agreement between imaging modalities

A strong positive correlation was observed between CT-derived and echocardiography-derived aortic valve area measurements (r = 0.82, 95% CI 0.76–0.85, p < 0.001), indicating substantial agreement between the two modalities (Fig. 1). Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated a mean bias of approximately +0.05 cm2, with 95% limits of agreement ranging from −0.19 to +0.29 cm2, suggesting acceptable consistency between techniques (Supplementary Figure 1).

4.1. Reclassification of aortic stenosis severity

CT-based assessment led to reclassification of disease severity in a subset of patients compared with echocardiography. Specifically, 132 patients (7.1%, 95% CI 6.0–8.3) initially classified as moderate AS on echocardiography were reclassified as severe using CT measurements, whereas 94 patients (5.1%, 95% CI 4.2–6.2) initially labeled severe were reclassified as moderate. Overall diagnostic concordance between modalities for severe AS was 87.5% (95% CI 86.0–88.9), while 12.5% (95% CI 11.1–14.0) of cases were discordant (Table 2).

4.2. Diagnostic and prognostic performance

Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated good discrimination for adverse clinical outcomes with both imaging techniques. CT-derived aortic valve area yielded an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI 0.84–0.88, p < 0.001), while echocardiography demonstrated an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI 0.76–0.81, p < 0.001). Although CT showed numerically higher discriminatory ability, overlapping confidence intervals indicate that the modalities provide complementary prognostic information rather than definitive superiority (Supplementary Figure 2).

4.3. Subgroup analysis

Across hemodynamic subtypes of aortic stenosis, CT consistently measured slightly larger valve areas than echocardiography. In patients with normal-flow AS, AVA measured 0.92 ± 0.15 cm2 by TTE versus 0.96 ± 0.17 cm2 by CT, with a mean difference of 0.04 cm2 (p < 0.001). Among those with low-flow, low-gradient AS, AVA was 0.78 ± 0.13 cm2 versus 0.83 ± 0.16 cm2, corresponding to a difference of 0.05 cm2 (p < 0.001). Similar findings were observed in paradoxical low-flow AS (0.81 ± 0.14 vs 0.86 ± 0.15 cm2; difference 0.05, p < 0.001) and high-gradient AS (0.80 ± 0.20 vs 0.85 ± 0.22 cm2; difference 0.05, p < 0.001). 4.4. Clinical outcomes

Patients with smaller aortic valve areas experienced significantly higher rates of adverse outcomes during follow-up. Among individuals with AVA ≤ 1.0 cm2, adverse events occurred in 37.8% (95% CI 35.2–40.4) of those classified by CT compared with 15.2% (95% CI 13.4–17.1) among those with larger valve areas (p < 0.001). Similarly, 35.6% (95% CI 33.1–38.1) of patients with severe AS defined by echocardiography experienced events compared with 17.1% (95% CI 15.3–19.0) in those without severe stenosis (p < 0.001). 4.5. Multivariable regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several independent predictors of severe aortic stenosis. After adjustment for clinical variables, AVA ≤ 1.0 cm2 measured by CT remained the strongest predictor (OR 4.15, 95% CI 3.55–4.84, p < 0.001). Echocardiography-derived AVA ≤1.0 cm2 was also independently associated with severe disease (OR 3.72, 95% CI 3.19–4.34, p < 0.001). Additional significant predictors included age ≥ 75 years (OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.39–2.03, p < 0.001), LVEF < 50% (OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.60–2.36, p < 0.001), female sex (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.17–1.69, p = 0.002), and NYHA class III–IV symptoms (OR 2.12, 95% CI 1.76–2.55, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This large-scale observational study evaluated the correlation between aortic valve area (AVA) measured by cardiac computed tomography (CT) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) in 1857 patients with aortic stenosis. Our findings demonstrate a strong correlation between the two modalities (r = 0.82), consistent with previous studies showing good agreement in AVA quantification using CT and echocardiography.9,10 However, CT tended to measure slightly larger AVA values compared with TTE, which may be explained by the planimetric measurement used in CT that avoids geometric assumptions required for the continuity equation applied in echocardiography.11

In the present analysis, CT demonstrated slightly higher diagnostic performance with greater sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve compared with TTE. Previous investigations by Clavel et al. and Pawade et al. have similarly highlighted that CT provides more direct anatomical visualization and is less influenced by flow-related hemodynamic variations.12,13 This advantage may be particularly relevant in patients with low-flow or discordant AS, where CT can help clarify disease severity. Our subgroup findings also support the potential complementary role of CT in different AS phenotypes.

From a prognostic standpoint, both imaging modalities showed that patients with AVA ≤1.0 cm2 experienced worse clinical outcomes. CT-derived AVA demonstrated a numerically stronger association with adverse events compared with TTE-derived AVA, suggesting that CT may provide additional prognostic information in selected patients.14 Importantly, CT measurements were associated with reclassification of AS severity in a subset of patients compared with echocardiography. However, these observations should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects on management decisions, as treatment strategies were determined by treating physicians in routine clinical practice and were not protocolized or adjudicated.

Our regression analysis also indicated that CT-derived AVA remained a strong independent predictor of severe disease after adjustment for clinical and echocardiographic parameters. These findings suggest that CT may contribute incremental value in risk stratification and evaluation of patients with discordant or borderline echocardiographic findings.15,16 Rather than replacing echocardiography, CT appears to serve as a complementary imaging modality that may enhance diagnostic confidence in selected cases.

Several limitations should be considered. This was an observational study, which may introduce selection bias, and clinical outcomes were obtained through retrospective review of medical records. In addition, management decisions were made by treating clinicians who had access to imaging findings, and no formal adjudication process was used. Therefore, the observed associations between CT findings and management patterns cannot be interpreted as causal effects. Furthermore, radiation exposure and resource availability remain practical considerations for broader implementation of CT in routine assessment of aortic stenosis. Future prospective studies are needed to determine how CT-guided evaluation may influence long-term clinical outcomes. Further, The interpretation of prognostic analyses should be approached with caution, as the composite outcome included aortic valve intervention, which may be influenced by imaging findings and clinical decision-making, thereby introducing potential treatment and ascertainment bias.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a strong correlation between aortic valve area (AVA) measurements obtained by cardiac computed tomography (CT) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) in patients with aortic stenosis, with CT showing slightly higher diagnostic accuracy and prognostic utility. Both modalities reliably identified patients at increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes, but CT-derived AVA was a stronger predictor of event rates and provided better discrimination across AS subtypes, particularly in low-flow and discordant cases. The findings underscore the value of CT as a complementary tool to echocardiography in the comprehensive evaluation and risk stratification of aortic stenosis. Integrating CT assessment into routine clinical pathways may enhance diagnostic precision and inform more effective treatment planning, especially in patients with ambiguous or borderline echocardiographic findings. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these results and assess the impact of CT-guided management on long-term outcomes.
