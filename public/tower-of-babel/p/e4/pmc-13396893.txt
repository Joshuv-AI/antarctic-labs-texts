Knowledge, attitudes, and applied practices regarding “Do Not Attempt Resuscitation” among healthcare workers in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia: a multicenter cross-sectional questionnaire-based survey
Authors: Hana Felemban, Farid Merzoug, Bassam Alshammari, Aiman Alshammari, Bandar Alruwaili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDo Not Attempt Resuscitation (DNAR) decisions are ethically and clinically complex, shaped by prognostic uncertainty, communication barriers, and sociocultural factors. In Saudi Arabia, Fatwa No. 12086 and Ministry of Health national policy permit withholding cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) without family consent when three qualified physicians deem resuscitation futile. Despite this established framework, inconsistent DNAR implementation persists across Saudi clinical settings.ObjectiveTo assess the knowledge, attitudes, and applied practices regarding DNAR among multidisciplinary healthcare workers (HCWs) in the Aljouf region of northern Saudi Arabia and to identify barriers to its consistent implementation.MethodsIn this multicenter cross-sectional survey, 461 licensed HCWs, including physicians, nurses, and allied health professionals, from 10 hospitals and 7 primary healthcare centers across the Aljouf region completed a structured, bilingual, self-administered online questionnaire between September 20 and November 15, 2025. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons, and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of high knowledge scores. Exposures and outcomes were defined a priori.ResultsOverall, 96% of participants achieved satisfactory knowledge scores (≥60%), with a median score of 9 out of 10 (IQR: 8–9). However, two clinically critical gaps were identified: only 52.9% correctly recognized that family consent is not legally required for DNAR in Saudi Arabia, and only 51.2% identified the correct management of cardiac arrest following confirmed brain death. Physicians scored significantly higher than nurses, technicians, and pharmacists (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.0003). In multivariable logistic regression, prior direct involvement in a DNAR decision was the strongest independent predictor of high knowledge (OR 2.39; 95% CI 1.11–5.16; p = 0.03), while nurses were approximately 50% less likely than physicians to achieve a high knowledge score (OR 0.50; 95% CI 0.29–0.83; p = 0.008). Although 78.3% considered DNAR ethically necessary and 82.2% agreed it protects patients from unnecessary suffering, 78.5% found family discussions challenging and 83.1% acknowledged strong cultural influences on DNAR decisions. Only 55.1% knew where to locate their institutional DNAR protocol.ConclusionThis study suggests that, although most HCWs appear to possess adequate theoretical knowledge of DNAR policy, two clinically critical gaps were identified: widespread misconception that family consent is legally required and poor understanding of cardiac arrest management following confirmed brain death. Acknowledging the limitations of convenience sampling and potential self-selection bias, these findings point toward the value of targeted multidisciplinary education, structured communication training, and improved institutional protocol accessibility as potentially important strategies to bridge the gap between policy and practice.

Introduction

Do Not Attempt Resuscitation (DNAR) orders represent ethically, clinically, and emotionally complex decisions in contemporary medical practice.1 These decisions are shaped by prognostic uncertainty, communication challenges, and sociocultural factors. International evidence demonstrates that DNAR is frequently misunderstood, with healthcare professionals and families equating it with withdrawal of all care rather than limitation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) alone, contributing to variability in end-of-life care and ethical conflict.2, 3 Consequently, patients with terminal or irreversible conditions may receive non-beneficial interventions that prolong suffering rather than promote comfort.

Globally, inadequate training and inconsistent understanding of DNAR policies among HCWs remain prevalent.1, 2 Physicians often delay DNAR discussions because of fear of litigation or concern about damaging therapeutic relationships with families.4, 5 Nurses, who are closely involved in end-of-life care, report limited involvement in DNAR decision-making and moral distress when non-beneficial interventions are continued despite a poor prognosis.5, 6, 7 These factors contribute to inconsistent DNAR implementation across healthcare systems and specialties.2, 11, 12

In Saudi Arabia, DNAR decisions are uniquely shaped by religious, legal, and institutional frameworks. Islamic bioethics permits withholding life-sustaining treatment when further intervention is deemed futile, a position formally supported by Fatwa No. 12086, issued in 1989 by the Presidency of Islamic Research and Ifta, which permits withholding CPR if three qualified physicians deem resuscitation futile, without requiring family consent.13, 14 The Saudi Ministry of Health has established a national DNAR policy, and the Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions mandates that healthcare facilities implement DNAR protocols and provide staff training.15, 24 End-of-life care is also highlighted as a national priority under Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030.10

Despite this established regulatory framework, DNAR implementation in Saudi clinical practice remains inconsistent. Studies conducted in Riyadh, Jeddah, the Eastern Province, and Aseer have reported persistent confusion regarding DNAR indications, legal authority, and documentation requirements among physicians and nurses.16, 17, 18 Most existing Saudi studies are limited by single-center designs, small sample sizes, or a narrow professional focus. No study has systematically evaluated DNAR knowledge, attitudes, and practices among multidisciplinary HCWs in the Aljouf region of northern Saudi Arabia. Based on the authors' direct clinical experience in this region, DNAR implementation in Aljouf appears to face distinctive challenges related to geographic remoteness, limited specialist availability, and reduced access to structured end-of-life training compared with larger urban centers. These factors underscore the need for a dedicated regional assessment. This study therefore aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and applied practices regarding DNAR among HCWs in Aljouf and to identify barriers to its consistent implementation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter cross-sectional survey was conducted among licensed healthcare workers (HCWs) in the Aljouf region of northern Saudi Arabia between September 20, 2025, and November 15, 2025. The Aljouf region encompasses four main governorates—Sakaka, Qurayyat, Dumat Al-Jandal, and Tabarjal—served by a network of governmental hospitals and primary healthcare centers. Participating hospital facilities included Prince Mutaib bin Abdulaziz Hospital (Sakaka), King Abdulaziz Specialist Hospital (Sakaka), Mental Health Hospital (Sakaka), Women and Maternity Hospital (Sakaka), Dumat Al-Jandal General Hospital, Qurayyat General Hospital (Qurayyat), Issawiya Hospital (Qurayyat), Abu Ajram General Hospital (Abu Ajram), Miqwa General Hospital (Miqwa), and Tabarjal General Hospital (Tabarjal). Seven affiliated primary healthcare centers were also included: Nabk Abu Qasr Healthcare Center, Qara (1) Healthcare Center, Fayad Tabarjal Healthcare Center, Khaw’a Healthcare Center, Al-Mukhatat Healthcare Center, Al-Shalhob Medical Center, and King Fahd Primary Health Care Center (Sakaka). These institutions were selected because DNAR decisions are routinely encountered across their inpatient departments and intensive care units (ICUs). No private facilities were deliberately targeted; the negligible proportion of private-sector respondents (1.1%) resulted from incidental inclusion via shared professional networks and was retained for descriptive purposes only.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Eligible participants included physicians, nurses, pharmacists, respiratory therapists, and allied health professionals who held an active Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS) license and were practicing in the Aljouf region during the data collection period. A multidisciplinary sample was deliberately sought for two reasons. First, DNAR implementation is a team-based process in which nurses, respiratory therapists, and pharmacists play direct roles in carrying out or withholding interventions at the bedside. Second, understanding knowledge and attitudes across all disciplines is necessary to inform targeted, profession-specific educational strategies. Although legal authority to issue a DNAR order rests exclusively with qualified physicians under Fatwa No. 12086, consistent and safe implementation requires that the entire clinical team understands the scope and implications of the order.

Individuals were excluded if they: (i) did not hold an active SCFHS license; (ii) practiced exclusively outside the Aljouf region; (iii) submitted an incomplete questionnaire (defined as omitting more than 20% of items); or (iv) held a non-clinical administrative or engineering role with no direct patient contact. A small number of respondents in non-clinical roles were inadvertently reached through shared departmental communication channels; these individuals were excluded from all analyses.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Abdulaziz Specialist Hospital, Sakaka, Saudi Arabia (IRB No. H-13-S-161/104; approval date: September 14, 2025). Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained before participants could access the questionnaire. Anonymity and confidentiality were ensured throughout the study, and no identifiable personal information was collected.

Recruitment strategy

Convenience sampling was used to recruit HCWs across all participating facilities. The survey was not restricted to specific clinical departments; rather, all licensed HCWs across all inpatient and outpatient departments within each participating hospital and primary healthcare center were eligible to participate. Department heads and nursing supervisors at each facility were contacted directly by the research team and asked to distribute the survey link to all eligible staff within their respective units via official hospital email systems and verified professional WhatsApp groups. This two-step, coordinator-facilitated distribution approach was adopted as a pragmatic strategy to achieve hospital-wide reach across clinical departments and work shifts in the absence of a centralized HCW registry. It is acknowledged, however, that this approach may have introduced selection bias by limiting distribution to staff within established departmental communication networks, and that response rates varied across facilities. To minimize duplicate responses, the survey platform was configured to permit only one submission per unique institutional email address. Data were collected over eight weeks, from September 20, 2025, to November 15, 2025. Two reminder messages were sent at two-week intervals. The response rate was calculated as the number of completed, eligible questionnaires divided by the total number of invitations delivered.

Although convenience sampling limits the generalizability of findings and may introduce self-selection bias, it was adopted as a pragmatic approach given the absence of a centralized HCW registry across the participating facilities. This limitation is discussed further in the Limitations section.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered online questionnaire developed in both Arabic and English. The questionnaire was constructed based on the Saudi Ministry of Health DNAR policy, the Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions accreditation standards, and previously published DNAR-related instruments.15, 20 The questionnaire underwent forward and backward translation to ensure linguistic accuracy. Content validity and cultural appropriateness were assessed through pilot testing with 20 HCWs from various disciplines in the Aljouf region prior to full distribution; minor modifications were made based on their feedback. The knowledge section demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79). The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material. The questionnaire comprised four sections: (1) demographic and professional characteristics; (2) DNAR knowledge, including the definition of DNAR, the legal and ethical framework under Fatwa No. 12086, physician authorization requirements, documentation standards, ongoing care obligations under a DNAR order, valid indications, management of cardiac arrest following confirmed brain death, mandatory pain management, family disagreement procedures, and order review frequency; (3) attitudes toward DNAR, assessed using a five-point Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree); and (4) applied practice, including prior involvement in DNAR decisions, receipt of formal DNAR training, awareness of the institutional DNAR protocol, and frequency of DNAR encounters in clinical practice.

Definition of DNAR in the Saudi legal context

In Saudi Arabia, a Do Not Attempt Resuscitation (DNAR) order is a physician-issued clinical directive specifying that cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)—comprising chest compressions, defibrillation, and advanced airway management—will not be initiated in the event of cardiac or respiratory arrest. A DNAR order does not authorize the withdrawal of other active medical treatments, including analgesics, antibiotics, intravenous fluids, or ventilatory support, unless these are independently discontinued under a separate clinical decision. This distinction is consistent with Fatwa No. 12086, issued in 1989 (1409H) by the Presidency of Islamic Research and Ifta, and is operationalized in the Saudi Ministry of Health national DNAR policy. The fatwa permits withholding CPR when three qualified physicians—at least one of whom must hold consultant rank—collectively determine that resuscitation is medically futile, without requiring family consent. DNAR orders must be reviewed regularly in accordance with institutional policy and may be rescinded if the clinical situation changes.

Scoring of knowledge items

Knowledge items were scored dichotomously, with one point awarded for each correct response and zero points for incorrect or “do not know” answers. Total knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 10. A score of ≥60% (≥6 out of 10) was considered indicative of satisfactory knowledge, a threshold commonly used in knowledge–attitude–practice (KAP) studies. For logistic regression analyses, a high knowledge score was defined as ≥75% (≥8 out of 10) to provide a more discriminating threshold and reduce potential ceiling effects. Attitude and applied practice items were analyzed descriptively.

Outcome and exposure variables

The primary outcome was the DNAR knowledge score, defined as the total number of correct responses across the 10 knowledge items. The primary exposure variables—selected a priori based on the study objectives and prior literature—were professional category (physician, nurse, technician, pharmacist, respiratory therapist), highest academic qualification, frequency of DNAR encounters in clinical practice, and prior direct involvement in a DNAR decision. Receipt of formal DNAR training within the preceding two years was included as a secondary exposure variable.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata version 15 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (reported to one decimal place), and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in knowledge scores across professional categories were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test; statistically significant results were followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests. To identify independent predictors of high DNAR knowledge (score ≥75%), a multivariable binary logistic regression model was constructed. Candidate variables were selected a priori and included professional category, highest academic qualification, frequency of DNAR encounters, prior direct involvement in a DNAR decision, and receipt of formal DNAR training. Collinearity among candidate predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF); no VIF exceeded 3.0, indicating acceptable collinearity. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given the a priori specification of regression model covariates, no additional correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the multivariable model. Attitude and applied practice data were analyzed descriptively; inferential analyses for these domains were limited to associations directly addressing the study objectives.

A further 15 incomplete submissions (omitting more than 20% of items) were excluded, yielding a final analytical sample of 431 participants for knowledge score comparisons and multivariable logistic regression. DNAR knowledge

Overall, participants demonstrated high levels of DNAR knowledge, with a median score of 9 (IQR: 8–9). Most participants (96%) achieved a satisfactory score (≥60%), and 76.5% achieved a high knowledge score (≥75%). Eight of the ten knowledge items were answered correctly by more than 80% of respondents. Participants showed strong understanding of the definition of DNAR (93.3%), the requirement for three qualified physicians to authorize the order (94.6%), mandatory pain management (95.9%), valid indications (93.5%), and the appropriate response to family disagreement (82.0%). Notable knowledge gaps were observed in two specific domains. Only 52.9% correctly identified that family consent is not legally required for DNAR in Saudi Arabia, and 51.2% correctly identified the appropriate action following cardiac arrest in a patient with confirmed brain death (withholding CPR and documenting death). Attitudes toward DNAR

Most participants expressed strong ethical support for DNAR: 78.3% agreed or strongly agreed that DNAR is an important part of ethical medical care, and 82.2% agreed that it protects patients from unnecessary suffering. However, 78.5% found discussing DNAR with families challenging, and 83.1% acknowledged that cultural factors strongly influence DNAR decisions in Saudi Arabia. A strong preference for hierarchical decision-making was also evident, with 88.3% agreeing that DNAR decisions should be made only by senior physicians. Applied practice and protocol awareness

Regarding applied practice, 68.5% of participants (n = 316) reported having been involved in a DNAR decision at some point during their clinical career. However, only 30.8% had received formal DNAR training within the preceding two years, and only 55.1% knew where to locate their hospital’s official DNAR protocol. DNAR encounters were infrequent for the majority of participants: 73.1% reported encountering DNAR situations rarely or never, and only 26.9% encountered them monthly or weekly, Table 1.

Discussion

This multicenter cross-sectional survey assessed DNAR-related knowledge, attitudes, and applied practices among multidisciplinary HCWs in the Aljouf region of Saudi Arabia. Although most participants demonstrated high overall theoretical knowledge, two clinically significant gaps were identified: fewer than half correctly identified the legal status of family consent under Saudi policy, and only half correctly identified the appropriate response to cardiac arrest following confirmed brain death. These findings are discussed below in the context of existing literature.

Gap between policy and perception

The most clinically consequential knowledge gap identified in this study was the widespread misconception regarding family consent. Although Fatwa No. 12086 and the Saudi Ministry of Health DNAR policy explicitly state that family consent is not legally required when three qualified physicians determine that resuscitation is futile, only 52.9% of participants correctly identified this. This finding is consistent with prior Saudi studies reporting persistent confusion about the legal authority for DNAR decisions among physicians and nurses.15, 16, 18 The practical implication is significant: if HCWs incorrectly believe that family approval is mandatory, they may delay or forgo issuing appropriate DNAR orders, thereby exposing patients to non-beneficial interventions that prolong suffering.2, 3

A similarly important gap was identified regarding the management of cardiac arrest following confirmed brain death, with only 51.2% selecting the correct response. This reflects a broader pattern of uncertainty at the intersection of DNAR and brain death protocols, an area that requires dedicated attention in institutional training programs. The remaining eight knowledge items were answered correctly by more than 80% of respondents, indicating that general familiarity with the policy framework is adequate but that specific legal and procedural applications remain poorly understood.

Sociocultural barriers and ethical support

A notable paradox emerged between ethical endorsement and practical application. Although 78.3% of participants agreed that DNAR is an important component of ethical medical care, 78.5% reported that discussing DNAR with families was challenging, and 83.1% acknowledged that cultural factors strongly influence these decisions in Saudi Arabia. This suggests that the principal barrier to effective DNAR implementation in this region is not ethical opposition but communication challenges and cultural pressures, a pattern consistent with findings from other Middle Eastern and Islamic healthcare contexts.8, 21 Despite the regulatory framework provided by the Ministry of Health and Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions, HCWs in Aljouf appear hesitant to initiate DNAR discussions because of perceived legal vulnerability15, 16 and anticipated family resistance.18

The strong preference for hierarchical decision-making—with 88.3% agreeing that DNAR decisions should be made only by senior physicians—further reflects the influence of cultural and institutional norms on end-of-life care in this setting.9, 10 While this preference is understandable within the Saudi healthcare culture, it may inadvertently contribute to moral distress among junior physicians and nursing staff when they encounter DNAR situations without senior guidance.5, 6

Professional disparities and training effectiveness

Physicians demonstrated significantly higher DNAR knowledge scores than nurses, technicians, and pharmacists (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.0003), and nurses were approximately 50% less likely than physicians to achieve a high knowledge score in the multivariable model (OR 0.50; 95% CI 0.29–0.83). These findings are consistent with prior literature documenting profession-based knowledge disparities in DNAR and end-of-life care domains.4, 7, 15 Given that nurses are frequently the first responders in cardiac arrest situations and play a central role in executing or withholding DNAR orders at the bedside, this knowledge gap has direct implications for patient safety.

Formal DNAR training within the preceding two years was not independently associated with higher knowledge scores in the overall sample (OR 1.27; 95% CI 0.70–2.31; p = 0.43). This finding suggests that current training programs may not be sufficiently practical or profession-tailored to translate into measurable knowledge gains across all disciplines. Similar educational interventions aimed at improving ethical decision-making have demonstrated the value of structured coaching and reflective practice in end-of-life care settings.19 In contrast, direct participation in a DNAR decision was the strongest independent predictor of high knowledge (OR 2.39; 95% CI 1.11–5.16; p = 0.03), indicating that experiential learning currently outweighs formal didactic education in this context. This is consistent with evidence from comparable settings demonstrating the primacy of clinical experience over classroom-based instruction in DNAR-related competence.22

These findings align with the recently published European Resuscitation Council (ERC) Guidelines 2025 on Ethics in Resuscitation, which recommend establishing ethical reasoning as a core competency in resuscitation training and advocate for simulation-based ethics education that equips healthcare professionals with skills to navigate DNACPR decisions, engage in advance care planning, and manage ethically complex end-of-life scenarios.23 The underperformance of nurses and the limited impact of formal training observed in the present study underscore the urgency of translating such international recommendations into locally adapted, profession-specific curricula in Saudi regional hospitals. Notably, the ERC 2025 guidelines highlight that equitable access to ethics education reduces clinical uncertainty and builds provider confidence, a principle directly applicable to the knowledge gaps and protocol inaccessibility identified in this study.

Institutional protocol awareness

Despite 68.5% of participants reporting prior involvement in a DNAR decision, only 55.1% knew where to locate their hospital’s official DNAR protocol. This dissociation between clinical experience and institutional protocol awareness suggests that many HCWs navigate DNAR situations based on precedent or senior guidance rather than formal policy. Combined with the fact that 73.1% of participants encountered DNAR situations rarely or never, this creates a high-risk environment for inconsistent and potentially inappropriate application of end-of-life care.10, 17

Future research should employ prospective observational designs and qualitative methods to assess real-world DNAR decision-making and the cultural and hierarchical pressures that shape end-of-life care in the Saudi clinical context.

Recommendations for practice

Based on these findings, the following targeted interventions are recommended:•Targeted legal education: All clinical staff, particularly nurses, should receive dedicated training on the legal framework governing DNAR in Saudi Arabia, specifically clarifying that family consent is not required under Fatwa No. 12086 and the Saudi Ministry of Health policy.•Communication skills training: Structured programs should equip HCWs with skills to conduct culturally sensitive DNAR discussions with families, addressing the barriers of anticipated conflict and perceived legal vulnerability reported in this study.8, 9•Improved protocol accessibility: Institutions should ensure that DNAR protocols are not only established but also prominently accessible to all clinical staff, regardless of their frequency of encounters.•Simulation-based training: Given that direct clinical involvement was the strongest predictor of knowledge, simulation exercises incorporating realistic DNAR scenarios—including brain death management—may bridge the gap between formal education and experiential learning.17

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, convenience sampling may introduce self-selection bias, as HCWs with greater interest in or familiarity with DNAR may have been more likely to participate, potentially overestimating true knowledge levels in the broader workforce.

Second, as a self-reported survey, responses are subject to social desirability bias. Participants may have selected answers perceived as professionally or institutionally appropriate rather than reflecting their actual knowledge or clinical practice. Some respondents may also have been reluctant to express disagreement with DNAR policy due to perceived hierarchical or cultural pressures.

Third, the high proportion of participants achieving satisfactory knowledge scores (96%) suggests a ceiling effect. Although this may reflect genuine familiarity with the national DNAR policy framework, certain questionnaire items may not sufficiently discriminate between varying levels of knowledge and practical competence.

Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although direct clinical involvement was independently associated with higher knowledge scores, it remains unclear whether clinical experience improves knowledge or whether HCWs with stronger foundational knowledge are more likely to be involved in DNAR decisions. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify this relationship. Despite these limitations, this study provides the first systematic, multicenter assessment of DNAR knowledge, attitudes, and practices among multidisciplinary HCWs in the Aljouf region, contributing important regional data to the broader Saudi and international literature.

Conclusion

This multicenter cross-sectional survey suggests that, although most HCWs in the Aljouf region appear to possess adequate theoretical knowledge of DNAR policy, two clinically critical gaps were identified: widespread misconceptions that family consent is legally required and poor understanding of the appropriate management of cardiac arrest following confirmed brain death. Acknowledging the inherent limitations of convenience sampling and the potential for self-selection bias, these findings point toward the value of targeted multidisciplinary education emphasizing the legal framework under Fatwa No. 12086, structured communication training for culturally sensitive family discussions, and improved institutional accessibility of DNAR protocols as potentially important strategies to bridge the gap between policy and practice.
