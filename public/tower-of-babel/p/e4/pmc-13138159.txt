Prolonged Intracranial Control With Stereotactic Radiosurgery Plus Anlotinib for Multiple Brain Metastases From Alveolar Soft Part Sarcoma: A Case Report
Authors: Shoaib Bashir, Minting Ye, Mingyao Lai, Linbo Cai
Published: 2026

Introduction

Alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) is a rare condition, accounting for 0.2% to 0.9% of all soft tissue sarcomas (STS), and is characterized by t(X;17) (p11;25) translocation, resulting in the diagnostically useful ASPSCR1-transcription factor E3 fusion protein. ASPS typically occurs in young adults, most commonly affecting the lower limbs in adults and the head and neck in children. ASPS is indolent but highly metastatic. Due to inherent resistance to conventional anthracycline-based chemotherapy, surgical resection is the primary treatment with neoadjuvant or adjuvant radiation therapy (RT), as recommended by National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. The prognosis for localized disease is generally favorable; however, outcomes are poor for metastatic disease.1,2 New effective treatment strategies for advanced-stage ASPS are needed.

ASPS is a tumor rich in blood vessels, easily leading to hematogenous metastasis. Up to 40% of patients are diagnosed with metastatic disease,3 and most have lung, bone, intracranial, and liver metastases at initial diagnosis.4 The role of systemic therapy for metastatic ASPS is not well understood. As reported, the ASPSCR1-transcription factor E3 fusion protein could promote tumor angiogenesis and cell proliferation through the mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET) signaling pathway5,6; multi-receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as sunitinib, pazopanib, and cediranib, are used to treat ASPS. Pazopanib was approved in 2012 by the Food and Drug Administration to treat STS. However, only approximately 35% of ASPS patients achieved clinical benefit from pazopanib, and treatment-limiting toxicities such as hypertension and diarrhea occurred in 35% of patients, necessitating dosage reductions.3 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) may be a strategic option for various metastatic diseases. In ASPS, the median progression-free survival (PFS) of patients on ICIs was only 6 to 6.9 months.7,8 However, the research was hampered by the scarcity of ASPS. A recent phase 3 trial reported the anticancer activity of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGF) TKIs (VEGF-TKIs) against ASPS. Anlotinib, a multitarget TKI, is also a representative of the VEGF-TKI and can suppress cancer growth via inhibition of the MET signaling pathway rather than VEGFs.5 Anlotinib showed a longer PFS and lower adverse effects (AEs) compared with pazopanib. The median PFS for anlotinib in ASPS patients was approximately 23.6 months, with AEs occurring in 6.3% of patients.3 ICIs combined with anlotinib treating STS demonstrated better clinical effectiveness, with the PFS reaching 11.74 months, versus 6.81 months for ICIs alone.8 In June 2019, anlotinib was approved in China for treating advanced ASPS.9

Stereotactic RT (SRT)/stereotactic surgery (SRS) represents an established treatment option for treating brain metastasis. ASPS has one of the highest rates of brain metastasis among sarcomas. However, published data on radiation therapy for ASPS brain metastases remain limited, with most patients undergoing RT following surgical resection.10, 11, 12, 13 Nevertheless, more studies are required to determine the role of RT and the dosage for treating brain metastasis from ASPS. Here, we present a case of multiple synchronously diagnosed brain metastases from ASPS, successfully controlled with combined SRS and anlotinib.

Case Description

History, physical examination, and investigations

A 29-year-old male with a 6-month history of a slow-growing lump in the medial side of his left thigh presented with paroxysmal dry cough, headache, and left eye proptosis; his Karnofsky Performance Status score was 80. On physical examination, the mass in the left thigh was found to be hard, nonmovable, and painless, measuring 3 × 3 cm. Fundoscopic examination revealed papilledema. Whole-body positron emission tomography-computed tomography detected increased fluorodeoxyglucose-18 (FDG) uptake within the primary lesion in the left medial femur (considering the malignant tumor from muscle or mesenchymal tissue origin) and metastatic lesions in the lungs, adrenal glands, intracranial, and left eye muscles. Brain magnetic resonance imaging with contrast revealed multiple cranial lesions with significant perilesional edema; a whole spinal magnetic resonance imaging with contrast was done to exclude spinal metastases. Neurologic examination was unremarkable except for papilledema noted on fundoscopic examination. A needle biopsy of the tumor in the left thigh confirmed the diagnosis of ASPS (Fig. 1). Staging was performed using the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system (8th ed, 2017), and the cancer was identified to be at stage IV at initial diagnosis.

Management

As part of palliative management, following multidisciplinary evaluation, systemic therapy with anlotinib 12 mg was initiated on July 29, 2020, administered in cycles of 2 weeks on and 1 week off. Concurrent SRS was initiated on July 30, 2020, and delivered to 3 dominant metastatic intracranial lesions over 2 days. Tumors 1 (volume 6.333cm3), 2 (volume 9.737cm3), and 3 (volume 21.21cm3) were located in the right parietal lobe, right cerebellar hemisphere, and left temporo-occipital lobe, respectively. Tumors 1 and 3 were targeted by single-fraction SRS (16 Gy), whereas tumor 2 was targeted with fractionated SRS (24 Gy in 2 fractions), due to its proximity to critical structures. Given the presence of multiple nondominant brain lesions, whole-brain RT (WBRT) was administered in September 2020 (36 Gy/18f), with a boost to the left frontal lobe lesion (54 Gy/18f) and left periorbital lesion (24 Gy/6f). In July 2021, RT (60 Gy/20f) against the primary tumor in the left thigh was administered. In September 2021, irradiation to a dominant metastatic lesion in the lung (40 Gy/8f) was also delivered. From June 2022 to July 2023, the patient received toripalimab in combination with anlotinib. Toripalimab is an anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitor that blocks the interaction between PD-1 and its ligands, thereby enabling T-cell activation against tumor cells. In June 2023, the patient received RT to the bilateral adrenal gland metastatic lesions (clinical target volume: 40 Gy/20f and GTV: 60 Gy/20f).

Treatment response

The patient was regularly followed up with imaging, and the changes in the volume of SRS-targeted metastatic brain tumors over time are demonstrated in Fig. 2. At 2 years post-SRS, treatment response assessment showed that the primary lesion and lung and brain metastases were stable; bilateral adrenal gland metastases were evaluated as progressive. Additionally, new metastatic sites were noted in the pancreas, bilateral renal parenchyma, and heart. Toripalimab 160 mg, once every 2 weeks, was added to anlotinib, and the patient received 14 cycles of toripalimab in total.

The patient died on October 26, 2023, due to uncontrolled systemic disease progression. However, the cranial metastatic lesions remained stable with continuous size reduction, resulting in an intracranial PFS of nearly 39 months.

Discussion

Here, we present the case report of synchronously diagnosed brain, lung, adrenal gland, and left eye muscle metastases from ASPS. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of ASPS. The progression of intracranial metastatic lesions was successfully controlled with combined SRS and anlotinib. Following SRS, the patient's neurologic symptoms were gradually relieved, and brain imaging showed evidence of a reduction in the size of SRS-irradiated lesions (Fig. 2). Meanwhile, the patient continued on anlotinib systemic therapy. However, due to the progression of systemic metastases, toripalimab, an anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitor, was added to anlotinib.

We conducted a comprehensive literature search on PubMed and found only 2 publications regarding SRS for intracranial lesions from ASPS, where patients underwent SRS without any prior intervention to cranial metastases, as in the present case. One is a case report, and the other is a case series comprising 3 cases.14,15 Both studies demonstrated adequate local control of SRS-targeted intracranial lesions, which is consistent with our findings. Lim et al15 reported that ASPS brain metastases treated with SRS doses of ≥25 Gy achieved better local control than those receiving doses of <25 Gy. In our case, 3 dominant intracranial lesions were targeted with doses of <25 Gy either with fractionated or nonfractionated SRS and achieved adequate local control, suggesting that optimal dosing strategies require further investigation. Currently, there are limited studies on the use of RT for ASPS brain metastases. Our treatment approach was guided by NCCN and American Society for Radiation Oncology guidelines for brain metastases and our institutional experience, with dose selection accounting for indolent disease biology, tumor volume, proximity to critical structures, and the plan for subsequent WBRT.

However, there are notable differences between our case and that of Lim et al.15 First, whereas Lim et al15 used the Gamma Knife for SRS, we employed the Novalis Tx system (BrainLAB AG; Varian Medical Systems) for SRS. Second, the metastatic lesions in Lim et al's15 report were smaller (ranging from 0.1 to 4.0 cm³) compared to our case (ranging from 6.333 to 21.21 cm³). Third, all lesions in Lim et al's15 study were supratentorial, whereas our patient had 2 supratentorial and 1 infratentorial lesion. Nevertheless, all studies, including ours, support using SRS for multiple brain lesions from ASPS as an alternative, especially when lesions are surgically inaccessible or when surgical benefits are limited due to multiplicity. Additionally, previous case reports used pazopanib in combination with SRS; our case represents the first report demonstrating the efficacy of anlotinib combined with SRS.

An important consideration in this case is the differential response observed across various metastatic sites treated with different radiation dose-fractionation schemes. To better understand these outcomes, we calculated the biologically effective dose (BED) for each treatment site. All BED values were calculated using an α/β of 4 Gy, appropriate for soft tissue sarcoma.16 The brain metastases received single-fraction SRS (BED4 80 Gy) and hypofractionated SRS (BED4 96 Gy), followed by WBRT (BED4 54 Gy), resulting in cumulative BED4 values of 134 Gy and 150 Gy, respectively, and achieving excellent local control with progressive shrinkage. The primary lesion (BED4 105 Gy) and lung metastasis (BED4 90 Gy) were treated with moderately hypofractionated intensity modulated RT and remained stable. In contrast, the bilateral adrenal metastases progressed despite receiving conventionally fractionated intensity modulated RT with a BED4 of 105 Gy, possibly reflecting biological factors including tumor heterogeneity, drug penetration limitations, or delayed treatment timing.

Regarding systemic therapy, a notable observation in our case is that no new intracranial lesions developed during the 39-month follow-up period, including nearly 3 years on anlotinib monotherapy and more than 6 months after the addition of toripalimab following systemic progression. This suggests that anlotinib, and potentially the combination with toripalimab, may have contributed to preventing new central nervous system metastases in ASPS, though the exact mechanism remains unclear. This observation warrants further investigation.

Our case suggests that combined SRS and anlotinib therapy may help control intracranial metastasis from ASPS, with an intracranial PFS of 39 months. SRS emerges as a viable alternative for brain metastatic ASPS in select cases, particularly when lesions are unresectable or surgical benefits are marginal. Furthermore, SRS presents a reasonable option for patients who decline surgical resection. Moreover, dose escalation using stereotactic techniques may be considered for ASPS whenever feasible, given the indolent disease biology and long survival of these patients. However, treatment planning must carefully respect normal tissue constraints, as long-term survivors are at risk for late radiation toxicity. Further studies are needed to develop optimal dosing strategies of SRS for ASPS brain metastases and to clarify the role of systemic therapy in both treating existing lesions and preventing new central nervous system disease.
