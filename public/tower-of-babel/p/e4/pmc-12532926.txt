Integrated nomogram for predicting adolescent depression: psychological, familial, and social risk factors
Authors: Juan Zhao, Ying Li, Yangjie Chen, Xiaomei Liu, Ahmad Naqib Shuid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent depression is a rising public health issue, particularly in China, due to increased social and changing family dynamics. This study aimed to develop a nomogram model that integrates individual traits, family background, and social support factors to improve the early detection and intervention of adolescent depression.MethodsThis study involved 943 adolescents (232 cases and 711 controls). Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used to select key predictive variables, and a multivariate logistic regression model was constructed. The model’s performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA) to assess its discriminatory power, calibration, and clinical utility.ResultsThe nomogram demonstrated high discriminatory capacity with areas under the curve of 0.988 for the training set and 0.987 for the validation set. Independent risk factors included self-harm behavior, poor familial relationships, and reduced sleep quality. The calibration curves showed strong concordance between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, as validated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = 0.671). DCA revealed that the nomogram offered superior net benefits over traditional screening methods, particularly in identifying high-risk individuals when the risk threshold exceeded 20%.DiscussionThis study developed a nomogram incorporating key risk factors, with sleep quality and self-harm behavior emerging as strong predictors of depression. Strained family dynamics also contribute to depression risk. Background

Adolescent depression has emerged as a significant public health issue globally, and its prevalence has markedly increased over the past few decades. Recent societal pressures, shifts in family structures, and increasing academic burdens have exacerbated mental health challenges among adolescents. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), between 12% and 17% of adolescents worldwide experience depression, with the detection rate among Chinese adolescents being even higher. The National Mental Health Report of China (2019–2020) indicated that the detection rate of depression among Chinese adolescents has reached a staggering 24.6% [1],reflecting the proportion of adolescents identified as being at risk for depression through screening.Furthermore, the incidence of depression is particularly elevated in high-risk groups, such as those facing intense exam-related pressures, those from single-parent households, and families experiencing financial difficulties [2, 3].This condition not only severely impacts the mental health of adolescents but also has far-reaching negative effects on their social functioning, academic performance, emotional development, and overall quality of life. In severe cases, it can lead to self-harm and suicidal behaviors [4, 5].

The early identification and diagnosis of adolescent depression present numerous challenges because of the complex interplay of various factors. Adolescents experience rapid physical and psychological development, which often results in diverse and concealed symptoms of depression. These symptoms can easily be mistaken for typical emotional fluctuations or temporary reactions to academic pressure. Research indicates that adolescents with introverted personalities, heightened emotional sensitivity, and low self-esteem are more susceptible to depressive symptoms. Additionally, strained parent-child relationships, insufficient family support, and economic hardships are significant contributors to depression [6]. School-related factors, peer pressure, and interpersonal relationships further complicate the identification of depression. Consequently, the complexity of these multidimensional factors makes early detection of high-risk adolescents particularly challenging, often resulting in diagnoses and treatment only after the condition has deteriorated.

To address these challenges, there is an urgent need to develop a multidimensional predictive model for adolescent depression. Current screening tools, which are primarily based on questionnaire surveys, are limited in their effectiveness due to their inability to comprehensively integrate individual, family, and social factors. In recent years, multifactorial analysis techniques, combined with tools such as nomograms, have been increasingly employed to increase the accuracy and sensitivity of predictions [6]. For example Byeon utilized a Bayesian nomogram model to predict the risk of adolescent depression in South Korea, identifying key predictive factors such as somatic symptoms, aggression, social withdrawal, and communication time with parents [7]. Similarly, Huang et al. developed a depression nomogram model for medical students, highlighting family functioning, social support, and life satisfaction as important predictors [8]. These advancements underscore the potential of integrating diverse factors into predictive models to improve early identification and intervention strategies for adolescent depression.

This research builds upon existing studies to establish a theoretical foundation for developing a predictive nomogram model for adolescent depression in China that integrates individual traits, family background, and social support factors. This study aims to investigate how these multidimensional factors interact within China’s unique sociocultural context, affecting the onset and progression of depression among adolescents. The nomogram model, a predictive tool grounded in multifactorial analysis, is extensively utilized in the medical field because of its capacity to incorporate multiple clinical variables and deliver personalized risk assessments. By visually representing the combined influence of various variables on outcomes, the nomogram is particularly effective for risk prediction in clinical practice. Previous research, such as the study by J. Höltge et al., has illustrated the utility of nomograms in integrating multiple factors to predict the risk of hypertension in children and adolescents, highlighting their broad applicability and accuracy in public health contexts [9]. In this study, data were collected from adolescent depression patients and healthy controls in a Chinese province to construct a multifactorial logistic regression-based nomogram prediction model. This model serves as a personalized risk assessment tool for clinicians and mental health professionals, facilitating the early identification of high-risk individuals. By evaluating the model’s discriminatory power, calibration, and clinical utility, this study provides valuable theoretical insights and practical guidance for the early identification and prevention of adolescent depression.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was conducted between June 2022 and June 2023, and involved adolescents aged 13–18 years from a tertiary hospital and local schools in Shanxi Province, China. The participants were selected through convenience sampling. The case group included adolescents with depression from a tertiary care hospital’s psychiatric department, whereas the control group included students from local schools. This study aimed to develop a predictive model for adolescent depression, that integrates individual characteristics, family background, and social support factors.

The sample size calculation for this study was meticulously conducted following the four-step method outlined in the BMJ guidelines (PMID: 32188600), with the primary aim of ensuring the stability of the prediction model and preventing overfitting [10]. In alignment with the literature suggesting the use of 5–10 key independent variables in model development, this study used 10 variables, resulting in a maximum calculated sample size of 849 participants. The target sample size was adjusted to 943 to account for a potential 10% dropout rate. Given that the detection rate of depression among Chinese adolescents was estimated to be 24.6%, the sample was divided into a case group (232) and a control group (711).

The case group consisted of adolescents diagnosed with depressive disorder on the basis of the ICD-10 criteria. The control group included healthy adolescents with no psychiatric history. The inclusion criteria was normal cognitive ability and communication skills, whereas the exclusion criterion ruled out depressive disorders caused by organic diseases or prior psychiatric illness.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (approval number: 2021k-149).

Study tools

To capture the multidimensional influences of adolescent depression, this study identified 20 high-frequency keywords from the literature, categorized into three themes: individual characteristics, family and social environments, and coping mechanisms. Each theme was assessed via standardized tools.

Demographic data

The collected data included age, gender, religious beliefs, family background (e.g., number of siblings, family economic status, family history of depression), educational level, and residential area. Adversity-related factors such as trauma history, school bullying, and domestic violence were also recorded.

Individual characteristics

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC): A 25-item scale assessing psychological resilience (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.961)[11, 12].

Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ): A 20-item scale evaluating positive and negative coping styles (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.872)[13, 14].

Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS): This scale is used to assess depressive symptoms, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.854[15, 16].

Family and social environment

Family Environment Scale-Chinese Version (FES-CV): This scale measures family dynamics across 10 dimensions (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.911)[17, 18].

Family Adaptability and Cohesion Evaluation Scales (FAD): This scale is used to evaluate family functioning (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.944)[19].

Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS): This scale measures social support (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.795)[20, 21].

Physiological state and coping mechanisms

Self-Harm Questionnaire for Adolescents (SHQAt): This scale assesses the frequency and severity of self-harm (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.955)[22].

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI): This instrument measures sleep quality (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.892)[23].

Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI-20): This instrument is used to evaluate fatigue levels (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.879)[24].

Data analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted via IBM SPSS version 25.0 and R version 4.1.2. The specific steps for the data analysis are outlined below:

Descriptive statistics: For continuous variables that were not normally distributed, the data are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges [M (Q1, Q3)], and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to assess differences between the training and validation groups. Categorical variables are displayed as counts and percentages [n (%)], and differences between the groups were evaluated using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests.

LASSO regression for variable selection: Key independent variables were selected using the LASSO regression method, with the optimal regularization parameter (λ) determined through 10-fold cross-validation. This method employs a regularization mechanism to identify the most predictive variables while excluding those of lesser importance.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis: The variables identified by LASSO were subsequently incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model. A bidirectional stepwise approach was employed to select the final set of predictive variables. These selected variables were then utilized to construct a nomogram model for predicting the outcome of interest [25].The nomogram serves as a graphical representation of the regression model, converting the calculated coefficients into a visual tool that enables clinicians to estimate an individual’s risk based on various input factors.

Model evaluation: Model evaluation involves several techniques. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the model’s discriminatory ability, with the area under the curve (AUC) indicating discrimination strength. Calibration was examined through a calibration curve, which was used to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, whereas the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to evaluate the model’s goodness-of-fit. Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve analysis were performed to determine the model’s clinical utility by evaluating the net benefit across various risk thresholds.

All the statistical tests were two-sided, with the significance level set at α = 0.05.

Results

Study participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 982 adolescents were initially recruited for the study. After applying the exclusion criteria, a final sample of 936 participants (229 cases and 707 controls) was retained for analysis. These participants were randomly divided into a training set (n = 654) and a validation set (n = 282) using a 7:3 ratio (As shown in Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics and outcome data between the training and validation sets were well-balanced, except for the controlling dimension of the Family Environment Scale (as shown in Table 1).

LASSO regression feature selection

A total of 68 variables were included as predictors, with depression occurrence as the outcome variable. LASSO regression was performed with 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal regularization parameter λ, and the value of Lambda 0.1 se was determined to be 0.033 (as shown in Figs. 2A and 2B). A total of 12 variables were ultimately selected: education level, history of school bullying, trauma experience, history of domestic violence, parent relationships, family history of depression, number of close friends, age, self-harm behavior, sleep onset time, sleep efficiency, and daytime dysfunction.

Nomogram construction and prediction analysis

By utilizing the significant variables from the multivariate logistic regression analysis, a nomogram prediction model was developed. This model incorporated the following eight variables: education level, parent relationships, family history of depression, number of close friends, age, self-harm behavior, sleep onset time(PSQIB), and sleep efficiency(PSQID) (as shown in Fig. 2C). The cumulative risk scores generated from these variables were employed to estimate the probability of depression onset in adolescents. For example, for a 15-year-old adolescent(age score: 17)with a high school education level (score 64), experiencing frequent parental conflict (score 20), with a family history of depression (score 38), having a substantial number of close friends (score 0), engaging in self-harm behavior (score 23), taking 15 min or less to fall asleep (PSQIB, score 0), and having sleep efficiency between 75% and 84% (PSQID, score 9), the corresponding scores for each variable are 17,64, 20, 38, 0, 23, 0, and 9, respectively. The total cumulative score of 171 indicates an estimated depression risk of 60% (as shown in Fig. 3) A detailed breakdown of the scoring system for each predictor is provided in the Appendix, where Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 list the assigned values and corresponding scores for each variable.

Example of the nomogram for predicting the risk of adolescent depression.The nomogram assigns points to each predictor proportional to its regression coefficient; total points are then mapped to predicted probability

In the training set, the area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curve was 0.988 (95% CI: 0.982–0.995), indicating excellent discriminative ability with a specificity of 0.923 and a sensitivity of 0.981, as shown in Fig. 4A. The optimal cutoff value was determined to be 0.158. The calibration plot (as shown in Fig. 4C) revealed a good model fit, which was supported by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 5.792, P = 0.671) and a Brier score of 0.035. Internal validation through 1000 iterations confirmed a high degree of consistency between the predicted risks and actual outcomes.

For the validation set, the ROC curve demonstrated an AUC of 0.987 (95% CI: 0.974-1.000), as depicted in Fig. 4B. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated a good fit with χ² = 7.931 (P = 0.440), and the Brier score was 0.034. Decision curve analysis and clinical impact curves

The decision curve analysis (DCA) curves revealed that the nomogram prediction model consistently provided greater net benefits across various risk thresholds. In these curves, the horizontal line signifies the net benefit of no intervention, which is zero, whereas the gray diagonal line represents the net benefit of intervening in all cases. Notably, the red line, which denotes the net benefit of the nomogram model, consistently outperforms both no intervention and universal intervention for all cases at risk thresholds up to 0.8 (as shown in Figs. 5A and 5B). Furthermore, the clinical impact curves illustrated in Figs. 5C and 5D provide additional insights. Here, the red solid line indicates the number of individuals classified as high-risk at each threshold, whereas the blue dashed line reflects the actual number of high-risk individuals. Importantly, when the risk threshold exceeds 20%, the model’s ability to identify high-risk individuals aligns closely with the actual incidence of depression, underscoring its clinical predictive accuracy.

Sensitivity analysis

To further examine the robustness of the model, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which all SES variables were forcibly entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. The SES-forced model demonstrated comparable discrimination and calibration to the stepwise-selected model(Supplementary Table S3; Supplementary Figure S1), indicating that the exclusion of SES variables did not materially compromise model validity.

Discussion

This study successfully developed a nomogram model for predicting adolescent depression by integrating multidimensional risk factors, including individual characteristics, family background, and social support. The model, constructed via multivariate logistic regression analysis, highlighted the significant roles of self-harm behavior, poor family relationships, and poor sleep quality in the onset of depression. It exhibited excellent discriminatory power and calibration, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.988 for the training set and 0.987 for the validation set. Decision curve analysis (DCA) further confirmed the model’s clinical utility across various risk thresholds, particularly when the risk threshold exceeded 20%, where it provided significant net benefits for the early identification of adolescent depression. Although the model demonstrated unusually high discrimination, supplementary validation analyses (Supplementary Figure S2; Supplementary Tables S4–S5) indicated that overfitting was unlikely. Nevertheless, external multicenter validation remains necessary to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

Compared with existing single-dimensional screening tools, this study offers a more comprehensive assessment by integrating individual, family, and social factors specific to the Chinese adolescent population. This multidimensional approach enhances the accuracy of early depression detection. Additionally, the visual nature of the nomogram increases its practical utility in clinical settings, as it facilitates early intervention by enabling clinicians to identify high-risk individuals more effectively.

Self-harm behavior as a key predictor of depression

In this study, self-harm behavior was identified as an independent predictor of adolescent depression, particularly among those who experienced difficulties in emotional regulation and stress management. Adolescents, often grappling with academic pressures, family issues, and social challenges, may resort to self-harm as a maladaptive coping mechanism. This behavior, dispite providing temporary emotional relief, ultimately exacerbates social isolation and weakens connections with family and peers, further impairing emotional regulation.

Adolescents with poor emotional regulation often resort to self-harm for temporary relief, but this behavior worsens social isolation and impairs relationships with family and peers, further weakening emotional regulation. Research consistently shows a strong link between poor emotional regulation and increased self-harm in adolescents. For example, Silverstein and Auerbach reported that adolescents with poor emotional regulation are more prone to self-harm, a behavior strongly associated with depression severity [26]. Similarly, a study by Fan et al. revealed a significant between inadequate emotional regulation and increased self-harm among Chinese adolescents, particularly those under academic pressure [27].

Self-harm significantly impacts adolescents’ mental health, social functioning, and academic performance. This often leads to a decline in self-esteem and social withdrawal, resulting in further isolation and a lack of emotional support. This isolation exacerbates emotional distress and contributes to academic challenges, such as difficulty concentrating and decreased motivation, which in turn heighten psychological pressure. Liu et al. identified self-harm as a strong predictor of future emotional disorders, including major depression and suicidal behavior [28]. Given these consequences, early intervention is essential. Schools and families must provide emotional support and counseling to help adolescents develop healthy coping strategies. Clinicians should monitor for early signs of self-harm to prevent the progression to more severe depressive states.

Sleep and depression risk

This study highlights that sleep onset time and sleep efficiency are independent predictors of adolescent depression, underscoring the crucial role of sleep quality in mental health. High-quality sleep, characterized by quick sleep onset (within 15 min) and efficient sleep, facilitates emotional regulation and reduces anxiety, thereby lowering the risk of depression. In contrast, poor sleep quality—particularly delayed sleep onset or low sleep efficiency—can lead to chronic stress, heightening emotional fluctuations and exacerbating depressive symptoms. These findings are consistent with the literature; for example, Liu et al. reported that prolonged sleep onset significantly increased the incidence of depression in adolescents [29]. Similarly, Malheiros et al. reported a direct correlation between poor subjective sleep quality and the exacerbation of depressive symptoms, especially among adolescents experiencing high stress levels [30].

The issue of sleep efficiency identified in our study aligns with Scott et al., who observed a significant increase in depression among adolescents experiencing sleep deprivation on school days [31]. These findings emphasize the need for improved sleep management and support for adolescents, particularly in high-pressure academic settings, to mitigate depression risk. Clinical interventions should prioritize enhancing adolescents’ sleep patterns by establishing healthy sleep routines, reducing late-night study sessions, and ensuring adequate sleep. Additionally, interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) may effectively improve sleep quality and reduce depression risk [32].

Family background and social support

This study underscores the significant influence of family dynamics, the presence of close friendships, and a family history of depression on the risk of adolescent depression. In particular, family relationships, especially parent‒child interactions, are pivotal in shaping adolescent mental health. Within the Chinese cultural context, parental conflict and emotional detachment can profoundly impact adolescents, often resulting in feelings of loneliness and helplessness. Such prolonged emotional stress can impair adolescents’ resilience in facing life challenges, thereby increasing the risk of depression. Research indicates that symptoms of adolescent depression markedly increase in the presence of parental conflict, discord, or emotional detachment [33]. Furthermore, poor family relationships can hinder adolescents’ emotional regulation, particularly when parents fail to provide adequate emotional support or when family conflict is prevalent [34]. Parental discord not only disrupts the family environment but also indirectly impedes adolescents’ ability to adapt to school and social settings, exacerbating depressive tendencies. Additionally, a family history of depression serves as an independent risk factor for the onset of depression. It not only contributes to genetic susceptibility but also affects the family atmosphere and emotional support, complicating adolescents’ ability to manage life pressures [35]. Therefore, strengthening emotional support and communication within the family is crucial [36, 37]. Enhancing parental relationships and fostering emotional support for children can effectively alleviate adolescents’ psychological burdens and mitigate the risk of depression.

Clinical significance and limitations

This study represents a notable advancement in predicting adolescent depression risk, but several limitations should be acknowledged. The data were sourced primarily from hospitals and schools in Shanxi Province, potentially restricting the generalizability of the findings. To validate the model’s applicability across broader demographics, future research should expand the sample pool to include more diverse populations. Additionally, the study’s reliance on self-report questionnaires introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, which may have influenced the results. To mitigate this, incorporating objective psychological assessment tools, such as biomarkers or behavioral assessments, could enhance the model’s accuracy and reliability. Specifically, integrating biomarkers such as cortisol levels or electroencephalogram data would further optimize the model, increasing its applicability in complex clinical contexts. Another limitation is the absence of long-term follow-up, which restricts our ability to assess the temporal dynamics of the identified risk factors or establish causal relationships. This limitation is particularly relevant for variables such as self-harm and sleep quality, which may serve not only as predictors but also as consequences of depression. Thus, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal inferences. To address this gap, future studies should incorporate longitudinal follow-up to better understand how these risk factors evolve over time and their potential impact on the onset and progression of depression in adolescents.

Furthermore, while several important factors, such as socioeconomic status and history of violence, were considered in the study, they were excluded from the final model due to LASSO regression variable selection. This exclusion may limit the model’s comprehensiveness.To address this issue, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which SES variables were forced into the model regardless of their statistical significance. The SES-forced model performed comparably to the stepwise-selected model in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility (Supplementary Figure S2; Supplementary Table S5), suggesting that the exclusion of SES variables did not materially compromise model validity. Future research should incorporate these factors into predictive modeling and conduct external, multicenter validation in more diverse populations to better establish their contribution and enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study developed a nomogram that integrates individual, family, and social risk factors to predict adolescent depression. The model demonstrated strong predictive performance, evidenced by high AUC values, and shows significant clinical utility for early intervention. As data sources expand and biomarkers are integrated, the model’s accuracy and applicability will improve, thereby enhancing adolescent mental health management. This tool holds great potential to support early intervention and prevent depression, ultimately improving adolescents’ long-term health and quality of life.
