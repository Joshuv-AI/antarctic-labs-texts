Current clinical practice of radiotherapy for the non-operative treatment of patients with unresectable locally recurrent rectal cancer: results of a national survey of the dutch society for radiotherapy and oncology
Authors: F. E. C. Vande Kerckhove, A. E. Verrijssen, R. H. N. Tijssen, J. C. M. Theuws, D. C. Rijkaart, J. S. Cnossen, S. G. van Ravensteijn, I. E. G. van Hellemond, J. Nederend, J. G. Bloemen, J. W. A. Burger, H. M. U. Peulen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeOver half of patients with locally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC) are ineligible for curative-intent multimodality treatment, typically including neoadjuvant treatment followed by surgery, due to unresectable disease, distant metastases, and/or frailty. Non-operative management for unresectable LRRC may include (chemo)(re-)irradiation, but evidence remains scarce. This study explores the current clinical practice of radiotherapy for LRRC in the Netherlands within this setting.Materials and methodsA web-based survey was designed and distributed to members of the Dutch Society of Radiation Oncology between December 2024 and February 2025. The survey comprised 28 items, addressing clinical indications, target delineation, treatment planning, dose and fractionation, radiotherapy techniques and knowledge gaps in current clinical practice.Results23 radiation oncologists from 18 out of 21 Dutch institutes completed the survey. While agreement existed regarding indications for radiotherapy in the non-operative management for LRRC, marked variability was observed in dose and fractionation schedules and target volume delineation. There seemed to be no consensus on response evaluation or follow-up schedules. Reported clinical challenges included patient heterogeneity, absence of evidence-based guidelines, limited knowledge on radiation-induced toxicity, difficulties estimating prognosis and management of therapy-resistant symptoms.ConclusionsThis national survey highlights the clinical heterogeneity and lack of consensus regarding radiotherapy for unresectable LRRC. Variations in dose and fractionation schedules, target volume delineation and follow-up underscore the need for prospective studies. Future research initiatives should prioritize optimal dose and fractionation schedules and the role of dose escalation, while integrating quality assurance programs and patient-reported outcomes to guide development of evidence-based recommendations for this complex patient group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13014-026-02890-1.

Introduction

In the Netherlands, approximately 3000 patients are diagnosed with rectal cancer each year [1]. Following curative treatment for primary rectal cancer, local recurrence occurs in approximately 6–12% of patients [2–6], corresponding to an estimated 180–360 cases annually. Current management of locally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC) remains complicated by heterogeneity in clinical presentation, prior treatment and tumour biology. Encouragingly, however, prospective randomized controlled trials for LRRC patients have been initiated. In an effort to standardise the multimodal management of patients with resectable LRRC, the PelvEx II trial has been initiated, a randomized phase III trial investigating the role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in addition to standard neoadjuvant chemo(re)irradiation prior to surgery [7]. This is supported by the development of the Dutch national guidelines for LRRC [8]. Consequently, for neoadjuvant radiotherapy, consensus-based delineation guidelines have been established, incorporating quality assurance programs and guidelines on treatment planning and dose (50 in 25 fractions of 2 Gy for radiotherapy-naïve patients and 30 Gy in 15 fractions of 2 Gy for reirradiation, both with concomitant capecitabine) [9, 10].

Nevertheless, approximately 40–60% of patients are not eligible for curative-intent multimodality treatment, which typically consists of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and/or chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery, due to locally unresectable disease, extensive distant metastases, poor clinical condition, and/or frailty [11–14]. In this setting, individualized treatment strategies constitute a central component of care. Non-operative treatment of patients with unresectable LRRC broadly comprises two clinical scenarios. In patients with unresectable LRRC without extensive distant metastases, or with oligometastases, upfront definitive chemo(re)irradiation can provide clinical benefit with acceptable toxicity, aiming to improve local and symptom control [15–18]. Notably, if inadequately treated, the local recurrence may lead to substantial, often debilitating symptom burden with profound impairment of quality of life without necessarily shortening survival. Conversely, in patients with unresectable LRRC with extensive distant metastases, palliative systemic therapy remains the standard of care (with possible (additional) use of target therapy), while palliative (chemo)(re-)irradiation can be considered to provide symptom control [8, 15, 19–21]. Overall, for both groups, evidence regarding the role of radiotherapy in the non-operative management of unresectable LRRC remains scarce, with heterogeneous cohorts and limited insight into current practice patterns or treatment strategies.

This study reports the results of a national web-based survey assessing current clinical practice of radiotherapy for unresectable LRRC in the Netherlands. The survey aimed to investigate current indications, radiotherapy techniques, and treatment regimens. Secondarily, by identifying variation in practice, the survey further sought to inform research priorities, support quality improvement, and guide the development of future evidence-based recommendations.

Materials and methods

A web-based survey was developed in December 2024 to assess current clinical practice of radiotherapy for the non-operative management of patients with unresectable LRRC in the Netherlands. The online questionnaire (Supplementary File 1) consisted of 28 questions for radiation oncologists with experience in LRRC management. The questionnaire was compliant with the CHERRIES guidelines for reporting results of internet e-surveys [22]. The survey was first piloted internally, revised for clarity and subsequently implemented using Qualtrics software (XM®; Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA).

The questionnaire was distributed to all 21 radiotherapy centres in the Netherlands via the Dutch Society of Radiotherapy and Oncology (NVRO) from December 2024 to February 2025. Invitations were sent by email and participation was voluntary. No incentives were offered. The study did not require ethical approval, as only professional opinions and experiences were collected. No patient-level data or personal information were obtained. Multiple respondents per centre were permitted. Responses were captured and stored automatically on the secure Qualtrics platform, with safeguards against unauthorized access. Only fully completed surveys were included in the analysis. Descriptive statistics were performed for all variables using SPSS Statistics 29.0 software (IBM, Endicott, NY, USA). Data visualizations were generated with Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Results

In total, 23 expert radiation oncologists from 18 out of 21 (86%) radiotherapy centres in the Netherlands completed the survey. Participating radiotherapy centres were dispersed across the country, including five independent institutes, six academic centres, six departments in general hospitals and one comprehensive cancer centre. LRRC patients undergoing radiotherapy were regularly discussed and treated in accordance to a multidisciplinary team in 96% of the centres. The majority of centres (13/18, 72%) discussed approximately between 10 and 36 LRRC patients per year who were not suitable for operative treatment, the remainder between 2 and 8 patients per year.

Treatment modalities for LRRC ineligible for operative treatment

Survey respondents were asked which available treatment modalities are used most commonly for patients with LRRC ineligible for operative treatment in their respective centres, giving them opportunity to select several options. The most frequently reported treatment modalities are radiotherapy (83%) and chemoradiotherapy (61%), followed by systemic therapy (39%), best supportive care (26%) and (palliative) surgery (17%). 13/23 (57%) respondents reported that no local radiotherapy protocol or guideline is available for LRRC patients undergoing definitive or palliative radiotherapy. Of those reporting to have an available protocol or guideline, 3/10 mentioned that the palliative radiotherapy guideline does not distinguish between palliative primary rectal cancer or LRRC treatment.

Indications for radiotherapy predominantly consist of providing symptomatic relief (100%) and improving overall quality of life (83%) (Fig. 1). Other indications include (prevention of) locoregional complaints (44%), prolonging life expectancy (9%) and haemostasis (9%).

Radiotherapy planning

Treatment planning

In 18/23 (78%) of respondents a non-contrast treatment planning CT is acquired. The remaining prefer a treatment planning CT with contrast (2/23, 8%), MRI (2/23, 8%) and one respondent reported to always use a treatment planning MRI. For target volume delineation, 17/23 (74%) of radiation oncologists employ registration of other diagnostic scans, predominantly MRI scans (17/17, 100%). In selected cases, a CT scan (4/17, 24%) or PET/CT scan (5/17, 29%) is also used. 20/23 (87%) of respondents implement a full bladder filling protocol. Reported reasons for not adhering to consistent bladder filling are cases with a low anatomical tumour location or low radiotherapy dosage. In 46% of respondents daily cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is applied and in two centres (8%) patients are treated with daily online adaptive radiotherapy.

Radiotherapy techniques, dose prescription and fractionations

All respondents reported to use volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT). In select cases, intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) (4/23, 17%) is used. For radiotherapy-naïve LRRC patients undergoing radiotherapeutic treatment, variation in dose was observed ranging from 5 × 5 Gy (48%), 25 × 2 Gy (30%), 5 × 4 Gy (13%), 10 × 3 Gy (4%) and 30 × 2 Gy (4%) (Fig. 2). Determinants for dose are based on clinical condition (96%), tumour size (44%) and patient preference (48%). Other commonly reported considerations are the extent of metastatic disease. In only 4/23 (17%) were dose considerations supported by a local protocol.

Similarly for previously irradiated LRRC patients, variation in dose was also observed, ranging from 15 × 2 Gy (48%), 5 × 4 Gy (48%) and 10 × 3 Gy (4%) (Fig. 3). Factors influencing radiotherapy dose are clinical condition (83%), tumour size (39%) and patient preference (48%). Another commonly reported consideration is extent of distant metastasis. 17/23 (74%) of respondents take into consideration the interval between prior radiotherapy, with cut-offs ranging between 3 and 12 months. Respondents were asked which radiotherapy fractionation schedules were used at their centre (multiple responses allowed). Hypofractionated (65%) and normofractionated (57%) radiotherapy is most commonly reported, while hyperfractionation is not reported by any respondents. In two centres, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) is used selectively depending on indication, tumour volume and prognosis. In terms of delineation, clinical target volume (CTV) margins range between 0.5 and 1.0 cm, with 1.0 cm being used most commonly (78%), followed by 0.5 (17%) or 0.8 (4%). A majority 14/23 (61%) of respondents reported to adapt CTV margins based on critical anatomic structures such as vasculature or bowels. Planning target volume (PTV) margins range from 0.5 to 1.0 cm, with 1.0 cm being used most commonly (65%), followed by 0.8 cm (13%), 0.5 (9%) or 0.7 cm (4%). Four respondents clarified that they used anisotropic margins from 0.7 to 1.5 cm. 15/23 (65%) of respondents employ a high α/β ratio of 10 Gy for tumour, with the remainder ranging between 3 and 5 Gy. 3/23 (13%) do not use an α/β ratio.

Follow-up

13/23 (57%) of respondents reported to have no standardized follow-up scheme in their respective centre. As such, frequency and scheduling of physical outpatient-clinic consultations or telephone consultations are often determined based on presence of symptoms and patient preference. In 5/23 (22%) respondents where a local standard follow-up scheme is available, patients were typically followed for 2 to 3 months post-radiotherapy. Further follow-up is completed by the referring specialist (i.e., medical oncologist or surgical oncologist). When asked which factors played a key role in determining the timing and frequency of follow-up (with 0 as not important and 5 as very important), the goal of therapy (e.g., locoregional control or quality of life) was ranked as most important (mean 4.1; SD 0.8), followed by type and severity of toxicity (mean 3.5; SD 1.4) and lastly expected prognosis (mean 2.8; SD 1.6) (Fig. 4).

Challenges

Radiation oncologists reported several key challenges encountered in clinical practice when treating patients with unresectable LRRC eligible for definitive or palliative (chemo)(re-)irradiation. A central issue is the heterogeneity of the patient population and the lack of available standardized treatment protocols. As such, radiation oncologists reported to adapt an individualized and personalised treatment plan according to clinical presentation, goal of therapy, clinical condition and patient’s preferences. Along with this variability, there were several concerns regarding radiation-induced toxicity and tolerance of organs of interest (OOI). Another challenge noted was the role of radiotherapy in managing therapy-resistant or relapsing symptoms, with uncertainty regarding expected symptom duration and options for repeat treatment strategies. Lastly, the difficulty in estimating prognosis was also reported to complicate patient-tailored treatment. The role of reirradiation and dose escalation were raised as areas of interest requiring further exploration.

Variability between respondents of the same centre

In three of the participating centres, multiple radiation oncologists completed the questionnaire allowing for comparison of responses. Notably, significant discrepancies were observed, particularly in the reported availability and use of protocols, adherence to full bladder filling protocol, radiation dosages for both radiotherapy-naïve and reirradiation settings, CTV-margins, alpha-beta ratios and approaches for response evaluation and follow-up. These findings underscore the considerable variability and lack of consensus also within the same radiotherapy centres.

Discussion

Over the last decade, multimodality treatment of LRRC has become increasingly standardized through multidisciplinary collaboration, prospective trials, and national guidelines. Nonetheless, non-operative treatment strategies for patients with unresectable LRRC remain highly variable. While current guidelines recommend definitive and palliative (chemo)(re-)irradiation to achieve local control and reduce symptomatic burden [8, 15, 19, 20], evidence in this setting remains scarce and largely derived from heterogeneous, and often outdated studies that predate contemporary advances in radiotherapy techniques. This national survey, therefore, provides timely insight into contemporary practice across radiation oncologists in the Netherlands, to investigate current indications, radiotherapy techniques, and treatment regimens, and to identify heterogeneity in clinical practice to further inform research priorities and evidence-based guideline consensus.

Overall, the survey demonstrates considerable variation across radiotherapy centres and radiation oncologists, reflecting challenges posed by a heterogeneous patient population and absence of evidence-based recommendations. Clinical decision making is frequently decentralized and often shaped by patient-specific considerations and institutional experience. Among participants, symptom control, improvement of quality of life and (prevention of) locoregional complaints emerged as predominant indications for radiotherapy in this setting. This aligns with existing literature, that reports overall reported palliation rates are 57–100% with a median duration of palliation of approximately 6–9 months for patients undergoing palliative radiotherapy [8, 21, 23, 24]. In patients undergoing definitive (chemo)(re-)irradiation, Valentini et al. (40.8 Gy in 1.2 Gy twice daily fractions) has reported response rates of 44.1% with a symptomatic response rate of 83.3% [17].

For radiotherapy-naïve patients, most respondents reported the use of volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), with short-course (5 × 5 Gy) or long-course (25 × 2 Gy with concomitant capecitabine) schedules. In previously irradiated patients, 15 × 2 Gy with concomitant capecitabine or 5 × 4 Gy regimens predominated, with heterogeneity in the use of hypofractionated and normofractionated radiotherapy. These findings are broadly consistent with a recent Dutch retrospective study, reporting short-course (5 × 3–5 Gy) and long-course (25 × 2 Gy or 28 × 1.8 Gy) schedules, and reirradiation schemes (15 × 2 Gy) in the non-operative setting [21]. In this study, use of hyperfractionated regimens was not reported as it is currently not standard of care in the Netherlands. Nonetheless, evidence from (pre)clinical studies suggests that hyperfractionation may result in decreased late adverse effects [17, 25]. The biological rationale for hyperfractionation leverages the differential sensitivity of tumour and late-reacting normal tissues with lower α/β ratios, resulting in a potentially more favourable therapeutic ratio. In reirradiation settings, this approach may be particularly beneficial for dose-escalation. Lastly, this survey demonstrated that currently SBRT was used selectively in two centres based on indication, tumour volume and prognosis. This indication aligns with the 2021 Delphi consensus, which supported the use of pelvic SBRT reirradiation following multidisciplinary review [26]. Overall, current national variability in treatment schedules is likely attributable to the adherence on local protocols, adapted from palliative primary rectal cancer regimens and reported reirradiation strategies for LRRC.

The clinical variability identified in this survey reflects the inherent challenges in the non-operative management of LRRC. Heterogeneity in disease presentation, prior treatment and tumour biology complicate therapeutic decision-making and prognostication. In daily clinical practice, clinicians therefore reported tailoring regimens to individual patient characteristics, balancing expected benefits of therapy with risks of radiation-induced toxicity and patient preferences. Concerns regarding OOI tolerance, cumulative radiation dose, and the limited evidence further contribute to diverging practice patterns. In the reirradiation setting, dose escalation was highlighted as an area of interest, aligning with literature reporting on improved locoregional control [17, 18, 27]. In the current study, a reirradiation regimen of 5 × 4 Gy was reported by 48% of respondents. While a cautious radiotherapy dose is understandable in this patient group, particularly in the context of reirradiation to mitigate toxicity, there is an inherent risk of under-treatment which must be carefully balanced against the need to achieve optimal tumour control. Overall, the absence of standardization complicates benchmarking of treatment outcomes and radiation-induced toxicity reporting.

The main strength of this study is its national scope and focus on a clinically challenging yet under-researched patient group. To our knowledge, this is one of the first study exploring practice patterns in radiotherapy for the non-operative management of irresectable LRRC. Limitations include the modest sample size, variable response rates between centres, and the self-reported nature of survey data, which may not fully capture decision-making complexity or actual practice. Nonetheless, the respondents represent broad national coverage (participation of 18 out of 21 (86%) Dutch radiotherapy centres), and given that non-operative treatment of LRRC is an area not routinely managed by all clinicians, the sample size of respondents is appropriate and provides a representative reflection of current practice. Secondly, the survey design, prioritizing brevity to encourage participation, limited the ability to fully explore other relevant aspects of care, such as integration with systemic therapy and detailed assessment of radiation-induced toxicity considerations and online set-up verification. Lastly, responses to questions on clinical challenges were limited, which may reflect the open-ended format of these items.

The current heterogeneity in national clinical practice, combined with the lack of high-quality data and formal guidelines, underscores the need for prospective registration initiatives and the development of consensus-based recommendations from dedicated collaborative groups (i.e., Reirradiation Collaborative Group). The considerable variation in treatment strategies observed in this survey likely reflects the limited evidence base and lack of dedicated guidance for patients with unresectable LRRC. Although individualized treatment decisions remain essential in this heterogeneous patient population, the findings suggest that there may be opportunities to further harmonize clinical practice. For example, variation was observed in the use of radiotherapy dose-fractionation schedules, concurrent chemotherapy, and treatment selection in symptomatic patients. Development of consensus recommendations and prospective studies may help to reduce practice variation and support more evidence-informed decision-making in this setting.

Research priorities include defining optimal dose and fractionation schedules. Ongoing prospective studies include the phase II ReRAD II trial (NCT04695782), evaluating dose-escalated pencil beam proton therapy for reirradiation in LRRC, and the observational RETRY study, assessing multimodality strategies in LRRC, including conventional chemoradiotherapy (≥ 40 Gy), proton and photon therapy, SBRT, or carbon ion radiotherapy, with consolidation chemotherapy for high-risk, borderline resectable, or unresectable disease. In the Netherlands, the STEP-UP feasibility study (Overview of Medical-Scientific Research in the Netherlands (OMON) Registry: NL-011150 and International Clinical Trial Registry Platform (ICTRP): NL-OMON61161) is in development to evaluate dose-escalated radiotherapy for patients with unresectable LRRC. Integration of patient-reported outcomes and health-related quality-of-life measures will be essential to align treatment strategies with patient priorities in the non-operative setting. Ultimately, results from prospective multicentre studies, including phase I–III trials where feasible, will further guide evidence-based recommendations and optimization of non-operative management for this complex patient population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this national survey reveals substantial heterogeneity in current clinical practice of radiotherapy for unresectable LRRC in the Netherlands. These findings reflect both the complexity of this patient population and the absence of high-quality evidence or consensus guidelines. Addressing these gaps through prospective research, guideline development, and systematic outcome registration is essential to achieve more uniform, evidence-based, and patient-centred care.
