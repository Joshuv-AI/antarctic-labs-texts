Influence of recurrence risk perception on depressive mood in first-time stroke patients and the mediating effect of coping styles and perceived discrimination
Authors: Haoliang Wang, Dewei Zhu, Zhixin Yan, Jun Zhao, Ruirui Zhu, Yuying Sun, Ping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the associations and mediating roles of coping styles and perceived discrimination in the relationship between recurrence risk perception and depression among first-ever stroke patients. Convenience sampling was used to select 382 first-ever stroke patients admitted to our hospital between June 2023 and June 2025. General information questionnaires, the Recurrence Risk Perception Scale, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ), and Perceived Discrimination Scale (PDS) were used for assessment. A chain mediation model was constructed and tested. A total of 382 questionnaires were distributed, and 375 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding an effective response rate of 98.17%. The total scores for recurrence risk perception, depression, coping style, and perceived discrimination among the 375 first-ever stroke patients were 35.65 ± 7.37, 5.86 ± 1.39, 43.36 ± 5.12, and 20.77 ± 3.67, respectively. Recurrence risk perception was significantly positively correlated with depression, avoidance, resignation, and perceived discrimination, and significantly negatively correlated with confrontation (P < 0.05). Patients with stronger perceived discrimination had a higher recurrence risk perception, more severe depression, and were more inclined to adopt negative coping styles. The specific indirect effect through coping style was 0.230, accounting for 17.54% of the total effect; the specific indirect effect through perceived discrimination was 0.214, accounting for 16.32% of the total effect; and the chain mediating effect through coping style and perceived discrimination was 0.044, accounting for 3.36% of the total effect. Coping style and perceived discrimination were associated with recurrent risk perception and depression in patients with first-ever stroke. Healthcare professionals should address patients’ risk perception of recurrence, focus on guiding them toward adopting positive coping styles, and mitigate the negative psychological impact of perceived discrimination.Keywords: First-ever stroke, Recurrence risk perception, Depression, Coping style, Perceived discriminationSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Stroke, a highly harmful cerebrovascular disease, is one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. The situation is particularly severe in China, where a new stroke occurs every 12 s and one patient dies from stroke every 21 s. Its mortality rate has surpassed that of malignant tumors and cardiovascular diseases, ranking first1. While experiencing severe physical impairment, patients with first-ever stroke also endure substantial psychological pressure, with depression being extremely common and serious. Relevant studies indicate that approximately one-third of stroke patients develop post-stroke depression2. Depression not only exacerbates psychological suffering, reduces quality of life, and increases mortality but also significantly adds to the economic burden on families and society. Among the numerous factors associated with depression in patients with first-ever stroke, risk perception of recurrence has gradually gained attention. Patients who strongly perceive the risk of recurrence often experience persistent anxiety and fear. Long-term accumulation of negative emotions can easily be associated with depression3. Concurrently, coping style and perceived discrimination, as important mediating factors, also played key roles. Coping style refers to the cognitive and behavioral strategies that individuals adopt when facing stressful events. Positive coping styles help patients better adjust their psychological state and alleviate negative emotions, whereas negative coping styles may be associated with greater psychological distress4. Perceived discrimination refers to patients’ experiences of unfair treatment or prejudice from others, which can further weaken their psychological resilience and worsen their depressive symptoms5. This study aimed to thoroughly investigate the associations and pathways linking recurrence risk perception to depression in first-ever stroke patients, clarifying how recurrence risk perception is associated with depressive symptoms and the mediating roles played by coping styles and perceived discrimination in this process. By revealing these complex relationships, we hope to gain a more comprehensive understanding of factors related to depression in first-ever stroke patients and provide a solid theoretical basis for developing more targeted and effective intervention measures.

Methods

Participants

Convenience sampling was used to select first-ever stroke patients admitted to our hospital between June 2023 and June 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for first-ever stroke according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Cerebral Hemorrhage (2019)6, (2) being conscious and able to cooperate in completing the questionnaire survey, (3) stable vital signs, and (4) complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) comorbidity with other severe organic diseases such as malignant tumors or severe liver or kidney dysfunction; (2) presence of cognitive impairment or a history of mental illness, rendering patients unable to accurately understand the questionnaire content; (3) unstable condition or being in the acute rescue phase; and (4) comorbidity with diseases such as heart failure or respiratory failure. The sample size was calculated using the rough estimation method proposed by Zheng et al.7, in which the sample size was 10 times the number of variables. A sample size of 200 was required with 20 variables in this study. Considering an attrition rate of 20%, the final sample size was 382. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University, and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Measures and data collection

General information questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect information, including patient name, age, marital status, education level, history of underlying diseases, and family history.

Recurrence risk perception scale

The Recurrence Risk Assessment Scale compiled by Lin et al.8 was used. This scale includes perceptions of recurrence severity, behavioral risk factors for recurrence, and disease risk factors for recurrence, totaling 17 items. It uses a 3-point scoring method with total scores ranging from 17 to 51. Higher scores indicated a higher level of recurrence risk perception. The internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) of this scale was 0.89.

Depression scale

The Chinese version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), translated by Xu et al.9, was used. This scale consists of nine items and uses a 4-point scoring method, with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. A score of ≥ 5 indicates the presence of depression, and higher scores indicate more severe depression. The internal consistency reliability coefficient for this scale was 0.91.

Coping style questionnaire

The Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ)10 was used for the assessment. This questionnaire included three dimensions: confrontation, avoidance, and resignation, with a total of 20 items. It uses a 4-point scoring method, with scores for each dimension ranging from a minimum determined by the number of items to a maximum. Based on reviewer feedback regarding potential issues by aggregating opposing dimensions into a single “total score,” subsequent analyses focused on the three separate dimensions rather than the total MCMQ score. The internal consistency reliability coefficient of the questionnaire was 0.78.

Perceived discrimination scale

The Perceived Discrimination Scale (PDS)11 was used. This scale includes individual- and group-perceived discrimination. It uses a 5-point scoring method in which patients judge the frequency of these events occurring to them. Higher scores indicate a higher level of perceived discrimination. The internal consistency reliability coefficient for this scale was 0.90. While this scale was originally developed for left-behind children, its items assess general experiences of unfair treatment and prejudice (e.g., feeling looked down upon or treated unfairly), which are applicable to adult populations experiencing stigma related to their health conditions. We confirmed face validity for stroke patients, and minor wording adjustments were made during the pilot phase to ensure contextual relevance (e.g., specifying “due to your health condition after stroke”).

Quality control

After obtaining approval from the Hospital Ethics Committee and informed consent from patients and their families, data were collected by uniformly trained researchers. The researchers distributed the questionnaires to patients 1–2 days before discharge and explained the completion methods and precautions in detail to ensure that patients understood the content. For patients with lower education levels or poor eyesight, researchers could complete the forms on their behalf based on verbal responses, but were required to repeat and confirm the answers with the patient. Questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site to ensure response rate and data validity. Upon collection, researchers conducted a preliminary review of the questionnaires. Specific exclusion criteria were applied during this review: questionnaires were excluded if (1) missing values exceeded 20% of the total items; (2) response patterns indicated non-serious completion (e.g., straight-lining or alternating extreme responses across all items); (3) logical inconsistencies were present (e.g., age reported as 20 years with a stroke history exceeding 10 years); or (4) responses were biologically implausible based on known clinical ranges. If omissions or errors were identified, the patients were promptly asked to supplement or correct them. A total of 382 questionnaires were distributed, and 375 valid questionnaires were recovered, resulting in an effective response rate of 98.17%. After data collection, data from valid questionnaires were entered into an Excel spreadsheet and preliminarily cleaned by checking for completeness and consistency and removing erroneous data according to predefined criteria.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Count data are described using frequencies and percentages. PROCESS macro (version 3.4) was used to test the mediation effects. AMOS 25.0 was used to construct the structural equation model, and the bootstrap method was employed to verify mediation effects. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. A subgroup analysis was conducted to compare the mediating effects of the two dimensions of perceived discrimination (“Individual PD” and Group PD”) in the model.

Mediation effect analysis

A model was constructed with recurrence risk perception as the independent variable, depression as the dependent variable, and the three coping style dimensions (confrontation, avoidance, and resignation), as well as perceived discrimination (total score), as mediating variables. Given the conceptual opposition between confrontation and avoidance/resignation, and based on correlation patterns, we constructed a model in which recurrence risk perception predicted the three coping dimensions, which in turn predicted perceived discrimination. Finally, all variables predicted depression. Specific paths were determined based on modification indices and theoretical coherence. The structural model was fitted using the maximum likelihood method. The fit indices were as follows: χ2/df = 2.112, GFI = 0.957, NFI = 0.978, IFI = 0.988, and CFI = 0.988. All values were within the acceptable range, indicating a good model fit. The mediation effect test using the bootstrap method showed that the 95% confidence intervals for the key paths did not include 0, indicating significant mediation effects. For clarity, we present a final simplified model figure (Fig. 1) focusing on the significant structural paths, with standardized coefficients (β) and significance levels marked (e.g., * for P < 0.05). The total indirect effect was 0.488, the total effect was 1.311, and it accounted for 37.22% of the total effect. Specifically, the indirect effect through coping style was 0.230, accounting for 17.54% of the total effect; the indirect effect through perceived discrimination was 0.214, accounting for 16.32% of the total effect; and the chain mediating effect through coping style and perceived discrimination was 0.044, accounting for 3.36% of the total effect. Subgroup analysis of perceived discrimination dimensions

We constructed two separate mediation models: one using “Individual Perceived Discrimination” as the mediator and another using “Group Perceived Discrimination.” The indirect effect of “Individual PD” was β = 0.195 (Boot SE = 0.045, 95% CI: 0.110–0.288), accounting for 14.87% of the total effect. The indirect effect through “Group PD” was β = 0.226 (Boot SE = 0.049, 95% CI: 0.133–0.328), accounting for 17.24% of the total effect. While both dimensions showed significant mediating roles, the effect size for “Group PD” (societal stigma) was slightly larger than that for “Individual PD” (personal slights). This finding suggests that perceptions of broader societal prejudice may have a marginally stronger association with depression.

Discussion

Based on structural equation modeling and bootstrap sampling testing, this study investigated the associations and mediating pathways involving coping style and perceived discrimination in the relationship between recurrence risk perception and depression in first-ever stroke patients. The results not only confirmed the significant direct association between recurrence risk perception and depression, but more importantly, revealed a chain pathway from cognitive appraisal to behavioral coping, then to socio-psychological perception, and finally affecting emotional state. The three distinct dimensions of coping style (confrontation, avoidance, and resignation) and perceived discrimination played key mediating roles, with a total indirect effect accounting for 37.22% of the total effect. While this highlights the relevance of psychosocial factors, it is crucial to acknowledge that the direct effect remained substantial (62.78%), highlighting that a significant portion of the variance in depression is associated with factors not captured in this model, such as neurobiological vulnerabilities (e.g., lesion location, neurotransmitter dysregulation, inflammatory processes) and other unmeasured psychosocial or environmental determinants12,13. These findings provide a theoretical basis for considering the psychosocial factors in comprehensive intervention plans.

The correlation analysis in this study showed a significant positive association between recurrence risk perception and the depression total score (r = 0.816, P < 0.05; direct effect β = 0.823). This finding aligns with conceptual models that emphasize the role of illness-related fear in negative emotions14. This strong association suggests that catastrophic appraisals about disease recurrence constitute a persistent psychological stressor that potentially depletes emotional regulation resources and correlates with core depressive symptoms15. Therefore, recurrence risk perception can be regarded as an important cognitive factor associated with poststroke depression.

Mediation effect analysis revealed that coping style dimensions played a significant mediating role. Specifically, higher recurrence risk perception was associated with lower use of confrontation and higher use of avoidance and resignation coping strategies. These negative coping patterns, while potentially offering short-term relief, may hinder adaptive disease management in the long term, correlating with deepened helplessness and depression16,17. Conversely, confrontation coping, involving active problem-solving and information seeking, is a known protective factor for resilience18. Our findings suggest that clinical interventions should aim to guide patients toward more adaptive coping patterns.

This study also showed that perceived discrimination was another significant pathway linking recurrence risk perception and depression (effect = 0.214). Experiences of stigma or unfair treatment, whether perceived in personal interactions (Individual PD) or as part of societal attitudes (Group PD), can reinforce negative self-evaluations and be associated with feelings of worthlessness and shame19. The subgroup analysis indicated that “Group PD” might have a slightly stronger association, suggesting that interventions addressing broader societal attitudes and public awareness about stroke disability could be particularly valuable. Psychological rehabilitation must extend beyond individuals in order to address social reintegration.

This study is the first to verify the existence of the chain mediation pathway “recurrence risk perception → coping style → perceived discrimination → depression” in first-ever stroke patients (effect = 0.044, 3.36% of the total effect). Although the effect size of this specific chain is relatively small, it elucidates a theoretically meaningful sequential process: Internal cognitive appraisal influences behavioral coping, which in turn shapes social experiences and perceptions, culminating in emotional outcomes. This finding highlights the interconnectedness between these factors. However, the modest proportion of variance explained by this chain (3.36%) suggests that, while it is a valid pathway, its standalone clinical significance for informing complex interventions may be limited. It likely operates in conjunction with other more potent direct and indirect pathways. The chain mechanism illustrates how these factors can be sequentially interrelated.

The identified pathway by which coping styles predict perceived discrimination can be strengthened theoretically. Drawing on social identity theory and attributional processes, we posit that adopting a resigned or avoidant coping style may lead patients to withdraw from social interactions, reduce opportunities for positive social feedback, and potentially confirm negative self-views. This behavioral withdrawal might be misinterpreted by others as a disinterest or inability, inadvertently eliciting less supportive or more patronizing responses, which are then perceived as discrimination. Furthermore, an internal attribution style associated with resignation (e.g., “It is my fault I cannot recover”) may increase sensitivity to ambiguous social cues, interpreting them as discriminatory. Thus, coping behavior can shape both objective social opportunities and subjective interpretations of social encounters, thereby influencing the levels of perceived discrimination.

The results of this study should also be contextualized within the rapidly evolving field of AI-driven intelligent diagnosis and management of depression. Recent research has leveraged sophisticated computational methods to analyze multimodal data for depression recognition, moving beyond traditional self-report methodologies toward objective, scalable assessment tools. A comprehensive 2025 review by Eryılmaz Baran et al.20 highlighted that AI approaches—including machine learning (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM, SVM) and deep learning models (e.g., CNN, LSTM, GRU)—can identify early signs of depression by analyzing speech patterns, behaviors, EEG signals, and text. Their study reported that LightGBM achieved 96% accuracy for anxiety and depression prediction, while LSTM and GRU models demonstrated exceptional performance in sequential data analysis, with GRU reaching 93% accuracy in text-based suicide and depression detection. Multimodal fusion represents a particularly promising frontier. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Wang et al.21 demonstrated that AI-assisted multi-modal methods (integrating EEG, eye movement, video, audio, and gait analysis) achieved a pooled AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.92–0.96), significantly outperforming uni-modal approaches (pooled AUC: 0.84–0.92). Subgroup analysis indicated that deep learning models showed superior performance, with an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97)21. The integration of large language models (LLMs) into mental health assessment represents another paradigm shift. Fine-tuned transformer models such as RoBERTa have demonstrated accuracy comparable to human experts in classifying symptomatic sentences from psychotherapy transcripts, achieving 74% accuracy in detecting anxiety and depression-related language22. This suggests that LLMs could support scalable mental status evaluation by analyzing patient narratives. Koutsoumpas et al.23 further demonstrated that LLMs outperform traditional methods on noisy and small datasets, particularly when training examples vary significantly in text length and genre. However, they also noted that psycholinguistic features and encoder-based models (e.g., BERT) can achieve comparable performance when trained on clinically confirmed depression texts, highlighting their potential in targeted clinical applications. Perhaps the most cutting-edge development is the emergence of closed-loop systems that integrate real-time depression recognition with intelligent intervention. Shen and Zhu24 proposed “EmoSavior,” a novel framework that combines multimodal physiological signals (EEG, ECG, GSR) with LLMs to enable accurate depression recognition and generate personalized, adaptive interventions. This framework employs LLMs for high-fidelity signal reconstruction, cross-modal attention mechanisms for deep fusion of heterogeneous data, and dynamic generation of intervention strategies based on real-time mental state assessment. By establishing a full-process closed-loop system from multimodal data perception to adaptive psychological regulation, such approaches represent a significant advance toward personalized, just-in-time mental health care.

This study has several limitations. First, cross-sectional design is a major limitation. Although it reveals associations and plausible mediating pathways, it cannot establish causality or temporal precedence. The observed relationships were correlational. For example, it is equally plausible that pre-existing or concurrent depressive symptoms lead to more pessimistic risk perceptions, influence the choice of coping styles, and increase sensitivity to perceived discrimination. Future longitudinal studies with multiple time points are essential to disentangle these temporal dynamics and to provide stronger evidence for the proposed directions of influence. Second, this study primarily focused on psychosocial variables. Future studies should incorporate neurobiological and broader social environmental factors to build a more comprehensive model. Third, the use of PDS, although adapted, warrants further validation in adult stroke populations. Fourth, the generalizability may be limited by the single-center convenience sampling method.

In conclusion, coping style dimensions and perceived discrimination are associated with recurrence of risk perception and depression in patients with first-ever strokes through multiple pathways, including a chain mediation effect. Healthcare professionals can consider these psychosocial factors in comprehensive care strategies.
