Would patients over 65 years of age accept vaccination while hospitalised? An assessment of eligibility and acceptability
Authors: Victoria Deacon, Kristen Overton, Parthsinh Solanki, Ethan MacMinn, Anthea L. Katelaris, Jeffrey J. Post
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaccination is effective at preventing severe illness related to influenza, pneumococcal disease and coronavirus disease 2019 COVID‐19. Vaccine coverage among older Australians is incomplete, and hospitals currently administer very few vaccine doses.AimsThis study investigated vaccination coverage for influenza, pneumococcal disease and COVID‐19 among inpatients aged 65 years and over and patient‐reported likelihood of accepting these vaccinations during hospitalisation.MethodsA patient survey and audit of the Australian Immunisation Register were performed on 2 days in 2023 at Prince of Wales Hospital in Sydney, Australia. All inpatients aged 65 years and over on the identified days (n = 506) were included in the audit. A total of 447 patients were offered surveys, with 311 (69.6%) responses. The likelihood of accepting vaccinations was self‐reported on a 5‐point scale. Audit and survey data were not linked.ResultsSurvey‐reported vaccination coverage was 39.2% for COVID‐19 in the last 6 months, 68.2% for influenza in 2023 and 32.1% for pneumococcal vaccination ever. The proportion of patients ‘likely’ or ‘very likely’ to accept inpatient vaccination was 58.2% for COVID‐19, 62.7% for influenza and 61.1% for pneumococcus. The median likelihood score was higher among respondents who reported to be vaccinated than unvaccinated (4 vs. 3; P < 0.0001). Audit vaccination coverage was 21.1% for COVID‐19 in the preceding 6 months, 60.5% for 2023 influenza vaccine and 20.6% for the recommended pneumococcal vaccine.ConclusionsThe study demonstrated incomplete vaccination coverage for COVID‐19, influenza and pneumococcal disease among older inpatients and patient interest in an inpatient vaccination programme.

Introduction

The Australian National Immunisation Program (NIP) provides government‐funded vaccinations for influenza, pneumococcal disease and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19). 1 However, national vaccine coverage data demonstrate suboptimal vaccination coverage among older Australians. 2 Hospitalisation may be a suitable healthcare contact to provide catch‐up vaccinations and may serve to reduce hospitalisation, morbidity and length of stay related to vaccine‐preventable conditions.

Influenza carries the leading burden among vaccine‐preventable diseases in Australia, particularly among adults aged over 65 years. 3 From 1 January to 19 October 2025, there were 412 888 influenza notifications in Australia. 4 Over this period 3215 patients were admitted to sentinel hospitals in Australia due to influenza; 7.5% patients were admitted to intensive care. 4 Influenza vaccination is the primary means of preventing severe influenza infection 5 and is associated with a 54% reduction in hospital admissions among patients aged ≥65 years. 6 Although funding for influenza vaccination is provided under the NIP for adults aged ≥65 years, 1 national influenza vaccine coverage in 2023 was only 64.3%. 2

Pneumococcal disease, caused by the bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae, has the second leading burden of vaccine‐preventable disease in Australia. 3 Invasive disease predominantly affects adults over 65 years and children under 5 years. 7 A single dose of 13‐valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (13vPCV) is recommended and funded at 70 years of age. 1 However, 13vPCV coverage for Australians aged ≥70 years was only 34.6% in 2023. 2

COVID‐19, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, continues to cause a considerable burden of disease. Patients with COVID‐19 have higher in‐hospital mortality and longer intensive care unit (ICU) stays compared to patients who do not have COVID‐19. 8 COVID‐19 booster vaccination has demonstrated efficacy at reducing hospitalisation and death, and protection is highest within 3 months of vaccination and declines with time. 9 , 10 A retrospective cohort study demonstrated that COVID‐19 booster has 82% effectiveness at protecting against hospitalisation, compared with 70% effectiveness of a primary course. 11

Australian guidelines at the time of writing recommend that immunocompetent adults aged 65–74 years receive a COVID‐19 booster every 12 months and that adults aged ≥75 years receive 6 monthly boosters due to the risk of severe illness with increasing age. 1 At commencement of the current study in March 2023, a booster dose was recommended for adults aged ≥65 years at 6 months after previous COVID‐19 vaccination or infection. 12 In December 2023, national data indicated only 23.2% adults aged ≥75 years and 15.0% adults aged 65–74 years had received a booster in the preceding 6 months. 13 In December 2024, 25.6% adults aged 65–74 years received a COVID‐19 vaccine in the preceding 12 months, and 14.2% adults aged ≥75 years received a COVID‐19 vaccine in the preceding 6 months. 2

These data indicate suboptimal vaccination coverage for influenza, pneumococcal disease and COVID‐19 among older Australians. Barriers to accessing vaccinations may include poor health education or knowledge, mobility or transportation limitations, lack of engagement with primary healthcare and chronic medical conditions affecting access to health services. 14 , 15 Offering vaccinations to eligible hospital inpatients may provide a mechanism to overcome these barriers and is not currently being widely utilised. In 2024, the majority of NIP‐funded vaccines received by adults aged over 65 years were administered in general practice or pharmacy settings, whereas a trivial proportion were administered in hospitals. 2

The current study had two aims. One aim was to investigate vaccination coverage against influenza, pneumococcus and COVID‐19 among inpatients aged ≥65 years. This age group was selected due to its increased vulnerability to severe illness and based on NIP eligibility criteria. The other aim was to assess patient likelihood of accepting age‐appropriate vaccination if offered during hospitalisation. This study will inform the suitability of a hospital‐based vaccination programme for eligible inpatients. It is anticipated that such a programme may overcome barriers to improving vaccination coverage and, consequently, reduce hospitalisation.

Methods

The study was conducted via survey and vaccination audit of inpatients aged ≥65 years at Prince of Wales Hospital, Sydney, on 2 days in September and November 2023. These dates were chosen based on staff availability, with a 2‐month gap to minimise patient overlap. All admitted patients were identified via the electronic medical record (EMR) on the selected dates. Patients aged under 65 years were excluded. Patients in intensive care, recovery, psychiatry and dementia wards were excluded from the survey but included in the audit. Patients admitted on both days were included on the first day only. South Eastern Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee provided ethics approval (2023/ETH01441).

Survey

Survey questions included age group, whether the participant had experienced COVID‐19 illness in the last 6 months and whether they had received COVID‐19 booster in the last 6 months, influenza vaccine in the current year and (if over 70 years) pneumococcal vaccination ever. Patients indicated on a 5‐point scale their likelihood of accepting each vaccination in hospital. A score of 1 was ‘very unlikely’, while 5 was ‘very likely’ (Appendix S1).

Surveys were distributed and collected by researchers on the study days. Patients completed the survey by written or verbal means according to their preference. Patients were advised that completion of the survey was optional and had no impact on their hospital care. No identifying information was collected in the survey.

Vaccination audit

Data extracted from the Australian Immunisation Register (AIR) included age, gender and vaccination status for COVID‐19 primary vaccination, COVID‐19 booster in the last 6 months, COVID‐19 booster in 2023, influenza vaccination in 2023 and pneumococcal vaccination at any time. AIR data were accessed via the EMR. The public health unit provided a direct AIR report for a small number of patients for whom the AIR was not visible on the EMR. Data from the audit and survey were not linked as the surveys were anonymous.

Analyses

The proportion of patients vaccinated was calculated for each vaccine, overall and by age group. The proportion of patients who indicated that they were likely or very likely to accept vaccination in hospital was calculated for each vaccine, overall and by age group. Chi‐squared tests 16 were performed to compare data by age groups. A median ‘likelihood score’ and interquartile range (IQR) from the 5‐point likelihood scale were also calculated for each vaccine, overall and by age. Correlation between likelihood score and self‐reported vaccination status for each illness was performed using a Mann–Whitney analysis. 16

The proportion of patients who were vaccinated based on the AIR audit was calculated overall, by age group and by gender, for each vaccine. Chi‐squared tests were performed to compare vaccination coverage by age group and gender. Significance level for all analyses was P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 946 inpatients were identified on the two study days, of whom 543 patients were aged ≥65 years. After accounting for 37 patients admitted on both days, 506 unique patients were included in the audit. There were 59 patients excluded from the survey based on their ward location. Survey response was 69.6% (311/447) (Fig. 1).

Among survey respondents, 43 participants (13.8%) were aged 65–69 years, 109 participants (35.1%) were 70–79 years and 149 participants (47.9%) were ≥80 years. Age was not indicated by 10 (3.2%) participants. Among audit subjects, mean age was 80.0 years with a standard deviation of 8.4 years, and 48.2% participants were female.

Among respondents, 39.2% reported receiving a COVID‐19 booster within the last 6 months; 46.6% received a COVID‐19 booster and/or experienced COVID‐19 infection in the last 6 months. Reported vaccination for influenza and pneumococcus was 68.2% and 32.1% respectively. There were no significant differences in vaccination coverage by age.

Discussion

This study investigated vaccination coverage among inpatients aged over 65 years. It identified a group of incompletely vaccinated older adults who were willing to receive opportunistic vaccination during hospitalisation.

The study found that the influenza vaccination coverage among hospital inpatients aged ≥65 years was approximately 60%. This is below the nationally published 2023 influenza vaccination coverage. 2 Although this study population had a high COVID‐19 primary vaccination coverage, the booster vaccination coverage was low, with approximately 39% patients aged ≥65 years having had a booster in 2023 and 21% having had a booster in the preceding 6 months. This coverage is only slightly higher than national data for adherence to COVID‐19 vaccination guidelines among adults ≥65 years. 2 With respect to pneumococcal vaccination, the proportion of audited patients who had received 13vPCV was lower (20.6%) than the nationally reported coverage of 34.6% in 2023. 2 Pneumococcal disease had the lowest survey‐reported vaccination coverage and the highest proportion of survey respondents stating that they were unsure if they were vaccinated. This suggests poor awareness and understanding about pneumococcal disease and prevention.

When considering why some vaccinations had lower coverage among inpatients than the general community, patients frequently admitted to hospital may experience additional barriers to accessing vaccinations. Australians aged ≥45 years with ‘high health needs’ have been determined to be 3.3 times more likely to not see a general practitioner when required, relative to patients with ‘low health needs’. 17 Reported barriers include difficulty obtaining medical appointments, 17 as well as lack of mobility, transport or language, financial barriers and geographical remoteness. 15

Self‐reported vaccination coverage in this study is slightly higher than AIR‐recorded coverage. This is consistent with existing literature, which demonstrates modestly higher vaccination coverage when measured by self‐report methods than by review of medical records. 18 , 19 The reasons for this discrepancy may include gaps in AIR data recording and over‐estimation by participants due to memory errors and/or a social desirability bias. 20

Overall, this study highlighted that hospital inpatients aged over 65 years are a population with suboptimal vaccination coverage, particularly for COVID‐19 and pneumococcal disease. This study also found that approximately 60% of inpatients aged over 65 years would be likely or very likely to accept inpatient vaccination. Even among the unvaccinated cohort, the median likelihood score of 3 for each condition suggests that half of the unvaccinated cohort would be likely to accept vaccination. These findings provide support for the introduction of a hospital‐based vaccination programme with a goal to increase vaccination coverage for older Australians.

A previous Australian study of eligibility for influenza vaccination among general medicine inpatients found that 81.8% of unvaccinated patients had no medical contraindications to inpatient vaccination, although vaccination was not routinely provided in hospital. 21 A review of the effectiveness of hospital‐based interventions to improve influenza vaccination found that standing order protocols (SOPs) were most effective. 14 Effectiveness was further improved when SOPs were combined with staff education. 14 An intervention involving a clinical decision support system which prompts clinicians to prescribe influenza vaccines for eligible patients was also associated with improved vaccination coverage in a paediatric hospital. 22

The widespread uptake of inpatient vaccination programmes is likely to require demonstration of cost effectiveness. A cost‐analysis of a proposed influenza vaccination programme for inpatients at a Melbourne hospital found the programme to be cost effective due to the expected reduction in hospital admissions. 23 These calculations assumed an uptake of at least 67%. 23 Further investigation into the funding model of an inpatient vaccination programme is indicated.

A limitation of the current study is that it did not explore reasons for patients' willingness or unwillingness to receive inpatient vaccination, such as being up to date with vaccinations, preferring community‐based providers or vaccine hesitancy. Likelihood scores demonstrated that vaccinated participants were more likely to accept inpatient vaccination than unvaccinated individuals. With logistical barriers to vaccination being removed through offering inpatient vaccination, it is likely that vaccine hesitancy, at least in part, contributed to that trend.

A model to describe vaccine hesitancy proposed three determinants: confidence, complacency and convenience. 24 Factors that contribute to confidence and/or complacency, such as knowledge, trust in healthcare providers and communication, 24 could be targeted in the inpatient setting. A study investigating the behavioural and social drivers of influenza vaccination intention found that healthcare worker recommendation was an important contributor to intention. 25 However, only 47% of patients reported receiving a recommendation to receive influenza vaccination in the previous 2 years, and only 19% reported receiving a reminder in the last 12 months. 25 An inpatient admission could provide a setting to facilitate this interaction.

The current audit was dependent on the accuracy of the AIR. Although the AIR was expanded to include adults in 2016, mandatory reporting of NIP vaccinations, including pneumococcal vaccination, was not introduced until 2021. 26 It is therefore possible that some older adults may have received pneumococcal vaccination prior to this legislation and, hence, had their vaccination omitted from the AIR. Additionally, a small number of patients did not have accessible AIR records. Finally, the study did not incorporate eligibility criteria for vaccination other than age. Future investigations could assess vaccination coverage among other at‐risk populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates patients aged over 65 years in an Australian metropolitan hospital have suboptimal vaccination coverage for influenza, COVID‐19 and pneumococcal disease. It also shows patient acceptance of a potential hospital‐based vaccination programme for inpatients in this age group. Such a programme could benefit the health of the population and the health system by reducing hospitalisation, morbidity and mortality related to vaccine‐preventable infections.
