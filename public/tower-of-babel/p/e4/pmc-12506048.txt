Modified Mediterranean diet effects on Parkinson’s disease (MED-PARK): a single-centre randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Elenamaria Pirovano, Franca Marino, Elena Rossi, Andrea Gennari, Emanuela Rasini, Margherita Uslenghi, Inês Figueira, Licia Iacoviello, Marco Ferrari, Marco Cosentino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOnly symptomatic treatments are available for patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most common chronic neurodegenerative disease worldwide, and it is therefore imperative to identify disease-modifying interventions that can alter the course of the disease. Epidemiological studies in PD patients suggest that a Mediterranean diet is associated with better motor and non-motor symptoms, slower progression and reduced mortality. Few interventional studies, however, investigated the relationship between diet and PD severity and progression. This study aims to determine whether a Mediterranean nutritional intervention can benefit motor and non-motor symptoms experienced by PD patients. As a secondary aim, the effects of a modified Mediterranean diet on the immune system, metabolomics and microbiome will also be assessed.Methods and analysisThis is an interventional, non-pharmacological, superiority, randomised, controlled, single-centre, masked study with two parallel groups to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a modified Mediterranean diet on motor and non-motor patient-reported symptoms. PD patients meeting inclusion criteria will be enrolled (44 participants, aged between 40 years and 85 years), block-randomised and split into two parallel arms to either maintain their usual diet (control) or follow a modified Mediterranean diet for 6 months (intervention).Patient-reported symptomatology is the primary outcome, measured through the Movement Disorders Society Unified PD Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) I+II score. Secondary outcomes include the immunophenotype of circulating cells of the adaptive immune system, the nasal and faecal microbiome composition, faecal and urinary metabolites and the measurement of inflammatory and metabolic markers. Disease severity (MDS-UPDRS III), non-motor symptomatology (Non-Motor Symptoms Scale), participant’s well-being (36-Item Short Form Health Survey), gastrointestinal symptomatology (Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale and the Patient Assessment of Constipation Quality of Life) and intensity of dopaminergic replacement therapy (levodopa equivalents) will also be assessed. Evaluations will be conducted before the start and at the end of the intervention.Ethics and disseminationThe Ethical Committee ‘Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 5’ first approved this study on 17 September 2024 Prot. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a slowly progressing, chronic neurodegenerative disease characterised by motor and non-motor symptoms, resulting in dramatically poor quality of life and a rising economic burden for patients as well as for their caregivers.1 Disease onset is preceded by a long prodromal phase with features such as anosmia, constipation and sleep disturbances.2 Globally, PD is the second most frequent chronic neurodegenerative disease1 and the fastest-growing of these disorders.3,4 The Global Burden of Disease reports a worldwide prevalence of 6.1 million, of which 52.5% were men.4 Since 1990, prevalence has increased globally by 21.6%,1 leading to increased economic and social costs and overburdening of healthcare systems.5 6

The disease’s pathogenesis is characterised by the accumulation of α-synuclein in Lewy bodies in the midbrain and by neuroinflammation due to microglial activation. This triad drives a slow and progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the pars compacta of the substantia nigra.7,10 Immune alterations and inflammation in PD pathogenesis and progression have recently emerged as crucial in the risk and progression of PD, starting years before clinical manifestations.11 Immune alterations affect adaptive, innate and humoral immunity. 12 13Inflammation and reactive microglial cells have long been described in postmortem PD brains.9 Patients with prodromal and overt disease have a decreased lymphocyte count,14 15 likely due to a decrease in CD4+T lymphocytes. Moreover, within CD4+, a reduction in T helper (Th) 2, Th17 and T regulatory lymphocytes and a subsequent Th1 bias has been reported.16 This Th1 bias is thought to contribute to neuronal damage. In agreement with evidence of neuroinflammation and immune dysregulation, T lymphocyte infiltrates have been found in PD-affected brain areas that seem to initiate and propagate neuronal death.17 18 Aberrant forms of α-synuclein can stimulate a specific T-cell adaptive response in some PD patients,19 which seems mediated mainly by IL-4 or IFNγ-producing CD4+T cells.19 Therefore, the immune system seems a promising target for novel disease-modifying therapeutic approaches. 20Several studies also suggest relevant alterations in PD patients’ gut and nasal microbiota.21 The nasal microbiota might play a fundamental role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases and specifically of PD.22 Nasal microbes can travel through various pathways, for example, the olfactory and trigeminal nerves and the bloodstream, to the brain, where they can exacerbate or trigger inflammation or neuronal damage. 23Moreover, gut dysbiosis might be a crucial step in the aetiology of PD24 and might partly explain the observed immunophenotype alterations.25 PD patients have an alteration of gut bacteria producing short-chain fatty acids.24 A meta-analysis reported an increase in the genera Lactobacillus, Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium, along with a decrease in bacteria from the Lachnospiraceae family and the Faecalibacterium genus.26 Interestingly, the family Lactobacillaceae, repeatedly reported as increased,27 28 is known to be an important immunomodulator.29 According to an intriguing hypothesis, pathological aggregation of α-synuclein may originate in the enteric nervous system25 30 since α-synuclein aggregates have been found both in the substantia nigra and the enteric nervous system of PD and inflammatory bowel disease patients.30 31 Inflammatory bowel disease seems to increase the risk of PD, while vagotomy30 and possibly appendectomy seem to diminish the risk25 through an interruption of the gut-brain crosstalk. This suggests that dysbiosis is an early event in PD pathogenesis and might be a potential disease-modifying target. Although gut and nasal microbiota offer great therapeutic and diagnostic potential, the association and causality remain unconfirmed.

Importantly, PD patients currently rely just on dopaminergic replacement therapy, which is only symptomatic32 and no disease-modifying treatments exist so far.33 The burden of motor and especially non-motor symptoms greatly compromises patients’ and caregivers’ quality of life.34 35 Patients and caregivers experience an increasing burden as the disease progresses and the symptomatology worsens. Moreover, as the disease progresses, pharmacological adverse effects such as levodopa-induced dyskinesia might appear.36 The many unmet needs of patients and caregivers lead to significant costs for them and society.37 Therefore, identifying strategies to improve quality of life, enhance patient involvement (empowerment) and slow disease progression is of the utmost importance.

The risk of PD can be modulated by certain food groups. Notably, dairy is known to increase the risk38 while coffee and Solanaceae (eg, pepper and tomato), which contain nicotine, are known to decrease the risk.39,41 Moreover, food groups common in the Western diet, including butter and margarine, red meat, cheese, fast food, carbonated and sweet beverages, pastries and sweets, appear to be associated with more severe PD symptoms. Conversely, a higher intake of nuts, vegetables, berries, beans, and non-fried fish may be linked to milder PD symptoms.42

The Mediterranean diet (MD) is a dietary pattern first described by Ancel Keys in the early ‘50s when observing the low cardiovascular risk of Neapolitan men.43 His observations sparked decades of scientific research. The MD is a plant-based diet, with a high intake of vegetables, fruits, whole cereals, legumes and nuts, a moderate fish, seafood and dairy intake and a limited consumption of meat, especially red and processed meats.44 All food is primarily cooked in olive oil, the primary source of fats.44 Various health benefits have been correlated with MD adherence, such as decreased cardiovascular risk, cancer prevention, better metabolic health, weight management and cognitive preservation.45

As regards PD, animal studies indicate that a high-fibre diet positively influences α-synuclein aggregation and microglial activation.46 Observational studies suggest that adherence to the MD may reduce the risk of developing PD,47 potentially lowering the risk of disease onset by 26%.47 Furthermore, longitudinal research indicates slower disease progression among those adhering to the MD.48 49 Recent longitudinal studies also link adherence to the MD to better motor and non-motor patient-reported symptomatology,50 as well as reduced overall mortality risk.42 Interventional studies suggested that the MD may improve executive function, language, attention, concentration, active memory and constipation in PD patients.51 52 Therefore, dietary patterns with a high intake of vegetables and fibre and a low intake of meat and processed food may be relevant for managing disease progression in PD. Additionally, compared with pharmacological interventions, the MD is not associated with adverse events other than flatulence.53 Known for its high adoption rate, palatability and long-term adherence, adopting the MD could potentially reduce the burden of common PD comorbidities, such as dementia. While new disease-modifying drugs are deeply needed, their development and availability will require a substantial amount of time. Conversely, longitudinal studies and randomised controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the MD’s effectiveness would provide more immediate evidence of its clinical utility. As such, the MD may help address several current unmet clinical needs in PD, posing the investigation of its impact on PD severity as a timely and valuable research priority.

Given the epidemiological evidence on dairy and PD, explored further in the intervention group section, and the recommendation to avoid dairy products, the food pattern focus of the intervention will be referred to as a modified MD (mMD). This 6-month RCT will be the longest study to investigate an mMD intervention on motor and non-motor symptoms experienced by PD patients.

Moreover, it will be the first trial to examine how mMD impacts PD patients’ immune system and gut and nasal microbiome alongside faecal and urinary metabolomics. The primary aim of this study is to assess the potential benefits of mMD adherence on PD patient-reported symptomatology. The hypothesis is that the intervention will slow down or stabilise PD symptomatology in high mMD adherents. Additionally, the study will investigate the correlation between immunophenotype, inflammatory status, gut and nasal microbiome and adherence levels to the mMD.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is an interventional, non-pharmacological, superiority, randomised, controlled, single-centre, masked study with two parallel groups. A total of 44 patients will be enrolled in the study, which will last 6 months, plus or minus 1 week. Each patient will be assigned either to the intervention arm (standard of care plus mMD) or to the control arm (standard of care). Using participants’ usual diet as the control arm aims to reflect a practical approach, to address difficulties and possible confounders of designing a placebo diet that could inadvertently affect study outcomes. Since we are based in Italy, a Mediterranean country, participants may naturally exhibit partial adherence to the MD.54 Randomisation is expected to balance baseline adherence levels, while dietary monitoring will account for variable adherence between participants. T0 is referred to as the baseline assessment visit. T0 includes informed consent, which is available in the online supplemental materials, eligibility screening and collection of baseline data, such as anthropometric parameters, physical activity levels and gastrointestinal symptoms. Between T0 and T1, before biological sample collection, clinical evaluation and allocation, part of the dietary baseline is assessed through a 3-day food diary.

T1, also called the pre-intervention evaluation and allocation visit. T1 takes place one to 2 weeks after T0. T1 includes biological sample collection and further clinical and dietary assessments. Allocation occurs at the end of T1. The mMD intervention starts 2 days after T1. Finally, T2 can also be referred to as the follow-up visit. T2 is scheduled approximately 6 months after T1 and mirrors the assessments conducted at T1, enabling longitudinal evaluation of parameters collected at baseline. Clinical dietary status is monitored across time points via food diaries to confirm adherence in the intervention group and dietary stability in the control group.

Study setting

The study will be conducted at the Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology, University of Insubria, where data and biological specimens will be stored and immunophenotyping will be conducted. The Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology is based in Italy, in Varese, near the Varesin Prealps. The study is conducted in a non-hospital environment, leveraging public facilities. Microbiome analyses will take place at the Centre for Microbiology and Environmental Systems Science, University of Vienna, Austria. Metabolomics analysis will be performed at the department of food and drugs, University of Parma, Italy. An accredited external laboratory will carry out routine laboratory analyses. Scheduled visits will be conducted at the Associazione Parkinson Insubria—AsPI section of Varese (http://www.parkinson-insubria.org, accessed on 06 March 2025). This association includes patients and caregivers living across the Italian province of Varese, in north-western Italy. Cooking classes will be held online and in person.

Enrolment

Patients will be recruited primarily through the association network of AsPI. Recruitment will also be conducted online through social media platforms, as well as via flyers and word of mouth.

Randomisation and masking

Patients will be randomised in the intervention or control group (1:1 allocation). Randomisation will be stratified by sex and by two Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) stage, namely, mild (stages 1 and 2) and moderate (stage 3).55

Random block randomisation will be performed. Patients will be randomised in order of consent at the first visit. Randomisation will be requested by the staff member responsible for recruitment to a member of the Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology, University of Insubria, who is not involved in enrolment, intervention, outcome assessment, data imputation and analysis of the study’s results. To reduce allocation predictability, researchers involved in the enrolment process will ignore block sizes. Allocation concealment will be ensured by using numbered in order of enrolment, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by the aforementioned Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology member. The envelopes will be prepared and delivered after participant recruitment, after completing all baseline assessments. Outcome assessors, such as the study neurologist and researchers analysing biological samples, will be masked to the patient’s allocation. Patients will be instructed to maintain the masking during the neurological visit.

Participants

Patients will provide written informed consent before any study procedure occurs.

Description of the intervention

Control group

The control group will be asked to maintain their current dietary habits and usual standard care. To assess whether control group participants maintain their dietary habits throughout the study, they will complete the same dietary assessments as the intervention group, namely, the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS)56 at T1 and T2, and the food diary at T0 and throughout the study. Moreover, they will undergo all the same routine examinations and check-ups as the intervention group.

Intervention group

The intervention group will undergo a 6-month intervention designed to encourage adherence to the mMD. At the beginning of the intervention, participants will receive a physical information booklet with a list of foods to favour or avoid. They will receive a monthly nutrition plan with recipes that highlight seasonal produce. Furthermore, patients will be offered cooking courses every 2 weeks, and caregiver participation will be encouraged.

The intervention is designed to be personalised to some extent. Throughout the cooking classes, participants will be educated on how to shop, read labels and organise the pantry to facilitate adherence to the mMD. They will receive personalised recommendations on how to adapt the intervention to individual preferences, current lifestyles and budgetary considerations. Patients’ adherence to the intervention will be monitored via the MEDAS at T1 and T256 and a 3-day food diary at T0 and throughout the study.

Avoidance recommendation of dairy products

Dairy is traditionally part of the MD. However, dairy consumption seems to increase the risk of developing PD.57 58 Recently, Fox et al found that dairy consumption may also worsen disease symptomatology.42 The association between dairy and PD risk and progression has been attributed to the urate-lowering effects of dairy products. Specifically, urate is a potent antioxidant and has been associated with decreased risk of PD and slower PD progression.59 Therefore, the study will recommend avoiding dairy products.

Changes and concurrent care

All patients’ medical events and any changes in PD and non-PD-related medications and dietary and medical supplements will be recorded throughout the study. Participants who will take medications that might interfere with the planned analyses, such as gut and nasal microbiome and immunophenotype, will be excluded from the final analysis. Every event and subsequent decision will be documented and justified.

Outcomes measures

Primary outcome

The Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) is a partly rater-based and partly patient-based questionnaire that assesses disease severity through 65 items divided into four domains: part I (non-motor experiences of daily living), part II (motor experiences of daily living), part III (motor examination) and part IV (motor complications). The MDS-UPDRS is designed to assess the presence and severity of clinically relevant problems in various domains. The primary outcome of this study is the (final-baseline) change in disease severity between the control and intervention groups at the end of the intervention, which will be assessed using the MDS-UPDRS60 I+II score. Part I of the scale investigates non-motor aspects of the disease, while part II refers to motor aspects. Each part contains 13 items. The assessment of the MDS-UPDRS I+II will enable the evaluation of patients’ perceived disability. This assessment will be carried out by the study neurologist during in-person visits at T1 and T2.

Secondary outcomes

Because of the exploratory nature of the study, several secondary outcomes are planned. The detailed schedule of outcome assessments is described in table 2.

Patient-reported outcomes

The MEDAS questionnaire is a self-reported 14-item questionnaire that investigates eating habits. The MEDAS questionnaire will allow the measurement of adherence to the MD, diet modification and effectiveness of the lifestyle intervention.56 Moreover, food diary analysis will aid in the assessment of diet modification, which will be based on the characteristics and quality of the collected data. The final analytic strategy will combine standardised scoring, such as the MEDAS, and qualitative assessment and will be reported in detail in subsequent publications. The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36)61 is a self-reported 36-item questionnaire that investigates emotional tone, health status and health-related quality of life in patients with PD. This will enable the evaluation of the mMD’s effect on participants’ physical, social and mental well-being.62

The Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS)63 is a self-reported 15-item questionnaire assessing the severity of gastrointestinal symptoms over the span of 1 week. The Patient Assessment of Constipation Quality of Life (Pac-QoL)64 is a self-reported 28-item questionnaire assessing the quality of life-related to constipation over the past 2 weeks.

For all the questionnaires mentioned above, baseline and final scores will be collected for each patient and for each domain within each of the two arms. The measure of effectiveness within each arm will be represented by the average of the before-and-after differences, while the comparison between the two arms will be represented by the difference between the averages of the before-and-after differences. Physical questionnaires will be administered in a controlled environment at AsPI by a researcher from the Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology, University of Insubria. The timing of questionnaire administration is outlined in table 2.

Clinician-reported outcomes

The Non-Motor Symptom Scale (NMSS)65 is a 30-item rater-based scale assessing non-motor symptoms severity and frequency across nine dimensions. A higher score represents a more severe condition. The full MDS-UPDRS and NMSS will be assessed by the study neurologist during inperson visits at T1 and T2. For each domain of the MDS-UPDRS and the total scores of the NMSS and MDS-UPDRS within each of the two study arms, baseline and final scores will be collected for each patient and each domain. The measure of effectiveness within each study arm will be represented by the average of the before-and-after differences, while the comparison between the two arms will be represented by the difference between the averages of the before-and-after differences.

Pharmacological history will be collected at T0 and T2 by clinicians from the Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology, University of Insubria. To assess the intensity of dopaminergic replacement therapy and facilitate the confrontation of different therapy schedules, levodopa equivalents will be calculated at T0 and T2 using the free online platform EQUIDopa.66

Height will be measured standing, without shoes. Weight will be assessed shoeless and lightly dressed with a digital scale (accuracy of 100 g). Body mass index will be calculated by dividing weight by height squared (kg/m²). Waist circumference will be measured according to the waist circumference midabdominal protocol, following WHO recommendations.

Laboratory assessed outcomes

Routine laboratory analysis

Peripheral venous blood samples will be collected in EDTA tubes at T1 and T2 after 12 hours of fasting. Samples will be stored at room temperature and processed within 24 hours. Each sample will be assigned a unique anonymous code. Part of the collected peripheral venous blood samples will be devoted to routine analysis, consisting of a complete blood count with formula, total cholesterol, high density lipoprotein and low density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, glycaemia, transaminases (aspartate transaminase and alanine transaminase), gamma-glutamyl transferase, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C reactive protein, creatinine, azotaemia and uricaemia.

Immunophenotype analysis

A second part of the sample will be devoted to the flow cytofluorimetric assay of the immunophenotype of circulating cells of the adaptive immune system. As described by Kustrimovic et al, whole blood samples will be stained and analysed with a BD FACS Celesta flow cytometer.16 The investigated subpopulations will include naïve/memory CD4+and CD8+ T cells, CD4+helper T subsets (Th1, Th2 and Th17), CD4+regulatory T cells (conventional, naïve and activated Treg), monocytes (classical, non-classical and intermediate) and Natural Killer (NK CD56 dim CD56 bright) cells, which will be analysed to investigate the potential immunomodulating effects of the mMD. Please see online supplemental materials - Staining and analytical procedure for immunophenotype analysis for details on staining and analytical procedures.

Gut and Nasal microbiome analysis

In this study, the effect of the mMD on the composition (taxonomy) and quantity of the microbiome will be evaluated.

Medical staff will collect nasal samples with a sterile swab. The swab will be inserted into the nasal passage between the septum and middle turbinate, directing it towards the upper part of the nasal cavity. Immediately after collection, the swab will be placed in sterile tubes and stored on ice. After collection, samples will be stored at −80°C until analysis.

Stool samples will be collected at home by the patients. At T0, the specialised staff involved in the study will provide containers for sample collection. Patients will store the samples for a maximum of 48 hours at 4°C. The samples will be transported on ice to the laboratory, where they will be homogenised, aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Both nasal and intestinal microbiomes will be analysed by sequencing the V1–V3 regions of the bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA gene, which will be amplified using universal bacterial primers to assess the composition and quantity of the intestinal and nasal microbiome. The sample will be processed using an Illumina Next-Generation Sequencing machine. We will examine operational taxonomic units as well as alpha and beta diversity.

Faecal and urinary metabolites analysis

Exposure to phenolic compounds will be assessed by analysing metabolites and catabolites of (poly)phenols in the collected faecal and urinary samples. A validated targeted ultra-performance liquid chromatography tandem triple quadrupole mass spectrometry method will be employed.67 Please see the online supplemental material- Urine metabolite extraction from urine samples - Sample analysis by UPLC-MS for details on faecal and urinary metabolite extraction and sample analysis.

Study timeline

All patients, regardless of their assignment, will be seen at T0, T1 and T2. Study enrolment started on 1768 January 2025 and is planned to finish in March 2026. The complete study timeline is described in table 2 and figure 1.

At T0, also referred to as the baseline assessment, after obtaining informed consent, in order to assess eligibility, the patient’s records will be reviewed. The patient’s diagnosis and staging will be confirmed, and anthropometric data (height, weight and waist circumference) will be recorded. Medical and pharmacological history will also be assessed and collected together with relevant variables like smoking. Physical activity levels will be assessed using the Saltin-Grimby Physical Activity Level Scale69 and the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly.70 The severity of gastrointestinal symptoms will be evaluated using the GSRS and Pac-QoL. At T0, a faecal collection kit and a urinary collection container will be provided, and the patient will be instructed on how to compile a 3-day food diary, which will be collected at T1.

Between 1 week and 2 weeks later, T1, also referred to as the preintervention evaluation and allocation visit, will be scheduled over 1 week. Blood, faecal, urinary and nasal samples will be collected, and a visit day will be scheduled at the patient’s centre. In a supervised environment, in order to assess eating habits and health-related quality of life, patients will complete the MEDAS and SF-36. The neurologist will administer the MDS-UPDRS and NMSS to evaluate the patient’s disease severity. At T1, all patients will receive an envelope containing the allocation details and physical diaries, namely, a food diary to be completed throughout the study, a food atlas to aid in the food diary completion, a pharmacological diary to be completed throughout the study, a laxative use diary and a constipation diary. 2 days after T1, patients in the intervention group will receive informational material to support the intervention.

T2, namely the follow-up visit, will be planned 6 months plus or minus 1 week after T1 and will include re-evaluating all parameters, scales and samples collected and administered at T1 and T0. Relevant parameters are clinical status, dietary status, anthropometric measurements and physical activity levels.

Sample size

The study is designed with sufficient statistical power to detect minimal clinically significant change in MDS-UPDRS I+II scores between the control and intervention groups. Through a comprehensive review of the existing literature, the minimal clinically significant change for MDS-UPDRS I+II scores in patients with an H&Y stage of 3 or less, as reported by Horvàt et al,71 has been identified. Considering an overestimation of 10% in the control group, the identified threshold has been determined to be 6.3 points.

To detect a difference of 6.3 points between the intervention and control groups, assuming a two-tailed significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power (β) of 80% and a SD of 6.3 points, a minimum sample size of 17 individuals per group has been calculated. Taking into account an estimated dropout rate of 20%, a treatment arm consisting of 22 patients and a total sample size of 44 patients has been determined.

Retention

Biweekly phone calls will be made to monitor adverse events, health status and ongoing therapy. These calls will also aim to improve compliance and mMD adherence in the intervention group. Physical diaries will be provided to monitor changes in drug therapy, diet and laxative usage throughout the study. Biweekly reminders for PAC-QOL completion and weight records will be sent out. Monthly, the patient will fill out a food diary, recording at least three non-consecutive days (two weekdays and one holiday) during the first and sixth months weekly and every 2 weeks in the remaining months. In order to ensure compliance, 1 day before the pre-established dietary record date, the patient will receive a reminder.

Statistical analysis

Intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses will be performed. Descriptive statistics, including mean, SD and median, will be calculated for baseline participant characteristics of each group. Where applicable, descriptive statistics will also be calculated for the primary and secondary outcomes. For each group, the mean (final minus baseline) change in MDS-UPDRS I+II scores will be calculated. The mean change between the control and intervention groups will be compared by means of a standard linear model.

If substantial baseline imbalances are observed, additional analyses adjusting for baseline MDS-UPDRS I+II score and other relevant covariates will be conducted.

The interpretation of results will primarily focus on the effect size, direction of the effect and its biological plausibility, with 95% CIs providing a measure of uncertainty. Before data analysis, the dataset will be anonymised a second time to enable a blind data analysis, following the methods proposed in.72

Data management and confidentiality

All data will be first collected via paper case report forms and then transferred to the electronic trial database. Each participant will be assigned a unique identifier to ensure confidentiality for all data collection. All the collected data will use a unique identifier and not contain patient personal data.

All records containing patients’ personal information, such as informed consent forms, will be stored separately from study records and identified by the study’s unique identifier. Patients’ personal information will be used to track consent, monitor intervention adherence, schedule visits and distribute study materials. Completion of source documents will be audited following each study visit. The MDS-UPDRS III assessment will be filmed. The physical archive, electronic database and biological specimen bank will be housed at the Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology at the University of Insubria. Data will be retained for at least 5 years. Biological specimens will be destroyed after 5 years.

No interim data analysis is planned, and trial termination can only be considered in the case of unforeseen adverse events. This protocol has been prepared according to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials reporting guidelines.73

Safety and adverse events

Even though no major risks are expected, each patient will be monitored routinely and have direct access to the research team. Throughout the study, patients will be asked to report any adverse events during bimonthly structured phone interviews conducted by Centre for Research in Medical Pharmacology members at the University of Insubria. Adverse events will be defined as specified by the guideline for good clinical practice of the International Council for Harmonisation as any unfavourable and unintended sign (including an abnormal laboratory finding), symptom or disease temporally associated with the use of a medicinal (investigational) product, whether or not related to the medicinal (investigational) product. When necessary, adverse events will be reported to the appropriate authorities, per current regulations. As per current regulations, civil liability insurance was contracted. An internal data monitoring committee (DMC) is deemed sufficient to manage the limited risks associated with this study. The internal DMC will periodically review the accumulating trial data to ensure participants’ safety, trial integrity and protocol adherence and monitor recruitment rates.

Discussion

The MED-PARK trial aims to evaluate the effect of adherence to mMD on PD severity. Moreover, the trial explores mMD’s impact on the patient’s immunophenotype, gut and nasal microbiome composition, faecal and urine metabolites and inflammatory and metabolic profile. This trial will be the longest MD trial in PD patients to evaluate the impact on motor and non-motor symptoms. Moreover, it will be the first trial to assess immunophenotype and gut and nasal microbiome changes alongside faecal and urine metabolites in relation to a mMD dietary intervention in PD patients. Positive results will support the theory that microbiota changes can influence the immune system and inflammation levels, potentially altering disease severity, as shown by previous studies on other neuroinflammatory diseases.74

Moreover, previous studies in PD analysing MD potential did not investigate the immunophenotype, faecal and urine metabolites and/or gut or nasal microbiome.

The randomised controlled design of the study can safeguard the results against chance and confounding factors. Additionally, the multidimensional approach of the study allows a more holistic understanding of mMD’s potential mechanism of action in PD. Furthermore, the hands-on planned intervention will hopefully lead to improved compliance. The study’s limitations include the small sample size and the single-centre design, which will diminish the result’s generalisability. Furthermore, participant masking was not feasible due to the nature of the dietary intervention. This, coupled with the use of a passive control group, without an active or a sham intervention, could increase the risk of expectation and performance bias. Moreover, we acknowledge the imbalance in contact and attention time between the intervention and control groups due to the biweekly cooking classes. However, we believe that this design choice reflects a pragmatic approach aligned with real-world implementation conditions. Although this 6-month study will be the longest interventional trial on mMD in PD to our knowledge, the study duration and design will not detect the mMD’s long-term effects. Finally, since the MDS-UPDRS scale developers advise against using a composite score, the choice of primary outcome could be critiqued.71 However, the existing literature suggests that the MDS-UPDRS I+II can be a feasible outcome to measure patients’ perceived motor and non-motor disability.71 75 Hopefully, this study will provide valuable insights into the mMD’s potential as a non-pharmacological, simple and accessible intervention to positively alter motor and non-motor symptomatology, quality of life, gastrointestinal symptoms and disease progression.

Ethics and dissemination

This protocol, along with the informed consent forms and the materials provided to participants, was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee on 17 September 2024. Informed consent will be sent via email before T0. At T0, to ensure complete understanding and voluntary participation, informed consent and the study’s objectives will be explained by trained personnel.

Only after formal approval by the ethics committee will any relevant amendments to the approved and published protocol affecting study results, procedures, data collection and participant safety be implemented. These changes will be disclosed at https://clinicaltrials.gov/. To facilitate patient outreach, we will collaborate with the association of patients and caregivers, AsPi Varese, and harness their network to reach individuals with PD.

The dissemination plan primarily targets PD researchers focusing on diet, immunity, metabolomics and the microbiota. Due to the study’s exploratory nature, physicians, patients and caregivers are secondary dissemination targets. The study results will be communicated to interested study participants. Once ready for publication, results will be disseminated in internationally indexed scientific journals. Authorship of publications reporting study results will follow the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors criteria.68 Individuals meeting authorship criteria will be granted authorship, with their roles transparently documented. A Publication Steering Committee (PSC), composed by F Marino, M Cosentino and E Pirovano, will be responsible for overseeing and guiding the publication process of research results, ensuring ethical and transparent practices. The PSCs will make key decisions regarding authorship, journal selection and publication timing. In particular, the PCS will be the forum for resolving any disputes regarding authorship, actively seeking agreement among all interested parties, taking into account international best practices and, specifically, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors guidelines.
