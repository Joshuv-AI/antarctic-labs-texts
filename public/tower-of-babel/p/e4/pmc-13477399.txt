Ventricular Arrhythmia Ablation in Inherited Cardiomyopathy: Phenotype Variability and Outcomes Based on Functional Genotypes
Authors: Arian Afzalian, Alireza Oraii, Timothy M Markman, Nosheen Reza, Rajat Deo, Anjali Owens, Andres Enriquez, Balaram Krishna J Hanumanthu, Matthew C Hyman, Maiwand Mirwais, Gustavo Guandalini, Vincent Y See, Robert D Schaller, Gregory E Supple, Michael P Riley, Saman Nazarian, David Lin, Fermin C Garcia, Sanjay Dixit, Andrew E Epstein, David S Frankel, David J Callans, Francis E Marchlinski, Poojita Shivamurthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInherited cardiomyopathies cause ventricular tachycardia (VT) and heart failure, yet genotype‐based substrate characterization is limited.MethodsPatients with inherited cardiomyopathy undergoing VT (n=80) or premature ventricular complex (n=25) ablation between 2010 and 2024 were evaluated for arrhythmia substrate, ablation, and heart failure outcomes.ResultsAmong 105 patients, the median age was 51 years, 72% were men, and the cohort comprised desmosomal (desmoplakin [DSP], 11%; non‐DSP, 24%), titin (22%), lamin A/C (LMNA; 20%), sarcomeric (14%), ion‐channel (5%), and cytoskeletal/Z‐disk (4%) genotype groups. In the VT cohort, low‐voltage substrate was predominantly septal in the LMNA (55%), titin (67%), and sarcomeric (75%) groups; both septal (75%) and lateral left ventricle (75%) in the cytoskeletal/Z‐disk group; perimitral (71%) and lateral left ventricle (57%) in the DSP desmosomal group; and right ventricular free wall (91%) in the non‐DSP desmosomal group. Ablation eliminated clinical VT in 90%, with noninducibility of any VT in 52 of 80 (65%) cases. VT‐free survival was 51% during 3 years (1.8–6.3 years). Among recurrences, 11 of 39 (28%) patients had a single VT episode. VT‐free survival was highest in the non‐DSP desmosomal group and lowest in the LMNA group (66% versus 20%, P=0.03). Residual VT inducibility (hazard ratio [HR], 2 [95% CI, 1.05–3.77]) and LMNA variant (HR, 2.25 [95% CI, 1.1–4.57]) predicted recurrence. In the premature ventricular complex cohort, the burden decreased from 12% (interquartile range [IQR]=Q3–Q1, 21–8) to 3.75% (IQR 3–1, 6–1), and 17 of 25 (68%) had ≤5% burden. End‐stage heart failure outcome (left ventricular assist device, transplant, or death) occurred in 29% overall and 52% in the LMNA group.ConclusionsGenotype correlates with VT substrate. Ablation successfully reduced VT and premature ventricular complex burden; however, long‐term VT recurrence was common except in non‐DSP desmosomal variants. Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Among patients with inherited cardiomyopathies undergoing catheter ablation for ventricular tachycardia, genotype‐specific patterns of late gadolinium enhancement and voltage abnormalities were evident, including predominant midmyocardial septal involvement in titin (TTN), lamin A/C (LMNA), and sarcomeric variants; subepicardial lateral involvement in desmosomal variants; combined septal‐lateral involvement in filamen C variants; and diffuse unipolar abnormalities in LMNA variants; however, a subset of patients demonstrated low‐voltage areas without late gadolinium enhancement in desmosomal (31%), TTN (21%), and LMNA (12%) variants. In addition, a predominance of left ventricular outflow tract premature ventricular complexes with periaortic voltage abnormalities was observed in the premature ventricular complex cohort.

Catheter ablation reduced premature ventricular complex and ventricular tachycardia burden, with a 3‐year ventricular tachycardia–free survival of 51%, which was highest in nondesmoplakin desmosomal variants (66%) and lowest in LMNA variants (20%). What Are the Clinical Implications?

Genotype‐based risk stratification in nonischemic cardiomyopathy is integral to optimizing the management of ventricular arrhythmia and heart failure, considering phenotypic heterogeneity and genotype‐dependent prognostic differences.

Inherited cardiomyopathies are largely caused by gene mutations that disrupt cellular or molecular function of cardiomyocytes (Figure 1). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants have been identified in genes encoding myocardial sarcomere proteins (myosin binding heavy chain [MYH7], myosin‐binding protein C3 [MYBPC3]), titin (TTN), desmosomal proteins (desmoplakin [DSP], plakophilin‐2 [PKP2], desmoglein 2 [DSG2]), structural cytoskeleton proteins (filamen C [FLNC]), ion channel proteins (sodium channel protein type 5 subunit alpha [SCN5A], ryanodine receptor [RYR2]), and nuclear envelope proteins (lamin A/C [LMNA]), with truncating TTN mutations being the most prevalent. 1 , 2 Including these in the phenotype‐based classification of all arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathies primarily based on the predominant ventricular involvement, ie, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), arrhythmogenic left ventricular, or biventricular cardiomyopathy, is inherently limited due to considerable phenotypic overlap, variable penetrance, and evolution of phenotypic expression over time. 3 , 4 , 5 For instance, ventricular arrhythmia (VA) is often the first clinical manifestation of disease in desmosomal cardiomyopathy, potentially taking years for heart failure (HF) to manifest, while LMNA P/LP variants present with either conduction disease, VA, or HF. 4 , 5

P/LP variants that interfere with cardiomyocyte structure and function facilitate focal and reentrant VAs due to atrophy, cell death, and interstitial fibrofatty replacement or triggered activity due to dysregulated intracellular signaling. 4 , 6 Voltage‐gated ion channel P/LP variants can facilitate slow conduction and reentrant VAs or early afterdepolarizations due to prolonged action potential duration. 7 Catheter ablation is being increasingly utilized to reduce VA recurrences, defibrillator shocks, and related hospitalizations. Nonetheless, recurrence is common in nonischemic substrates, especially in genetic cardiomyopathies due to complex pathology and disease progression. 5 , 8 , 9 Distinct genotypes have a predilection for fibrofatty replacement in specific myocardial segments and may vary dramatically with respect to presence and rapidity of substrate progression. 10 Septal fibrosis is frequently observed in LMNA and TTN cardiomyopathy, while DSP desmosomal cardiomyopathy involves the subepicardial basal lateral left ventricle (LV). 5 , 11 Understanding this genotype–phenotype relationship is essential for preprocedural planning, characterizing ventricular tachycardia (VT) circuitry and to improve ablation outcomes. Furthermore, it impacts critical therapeutic considerations such as timely device therapy, advanced HF therapy, and screening of family members. Limited large‐scale data exist on outcomes of VA ablation in inherited cardiomyopathy. 5 , 8 As such, we aimed to study the phenotypic diversity and impact of genotype on both immediate and longer‐term outcomes of VA ablation. By reporting on genotype‐based rather than traditional phenotype‐based classification, we intend to create a reliable framework for risk assessment, procedural planning, and long‐term prognosis.

METHODS

Study Population

We queried the institutional database and electronic medical records at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania to identify patients aged 18 or older who underwent percutaneous catheter ablation of VAs, including premature ventricular complexes (PVCs), PVC‐mediated ventricular fibrillation (VF), and VT, from January 2010 to December 2024. All patients undergoing ablation provided written informed consent according to institutional guidelines. All demographic, procedural, and follow‐up data prospectively entered a registry approved by the University of Pennsylvania's investigational review board for research use. The research reported in this article adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

From this ablation cohort, we identified a total of 364 patients who underwent panel‐based clinical genetic testing for cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia, of whom 132 (36%) had P/LP variants. To refine the functional genotype‐based analysis, infrequent and unique gene variants represented in a single patient were excluded from the final study cohort. These included CPT2 and SLC22A (carnitine deficiency), PPA2 (pyrophosphatase 2 enzyme deficiency), and P/LP variants affecting noncardiac skeletal muscles, including CNBP and DMPK (myotonic dystrophy) and EMD (muscular dystrophy). Given extensive noncardiac manifestations, we also excluded storage disorders such as Fabry (GLA), Danon (LAMP2), Pompe disease (GAA), and infiltrative disorders such as amyloidosis (TTR).

Genetic Analysis

Interpretation of genetic sequencing was based on the recommendations by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics. 12 Patients with P/LP variants were included in the study cohort. Those with variants of unknown significance, likely benign variants, and negative results were excluded.

Genotype Classification

The causative P/LP variants were categorized based on their function in the myocardial subcellular compartments into the following 6 genotype groups: desmosomal (DSP and non‐DSP), TTN, motor sarcomere, LMNA, structural cytoskeleton/Z‐disk, and ion channel (Figure 1). Similar genotype‐based classifications have been reported. 5 , 8

Clinical, Imaging, and Electrophysiological Data

Clinical characteristics at index presentation and ECG, Holter, and event monitor reports were analyzed. Imaging including echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance were reviewed. Electroanatomical mapping and ablation data for all the procedures were obtained from both procedural reports and by reviewing the maps primarily. Voltage abnormality or low voltage was defined as electrogram amplitude <1.5 mV in the bipolar map of the right ventricle and left ventricle, and as ≤8.27 mV for the left ventricle and ≤5.5 mV for the RV free wall in the unipolar map, according to previously published criteria. 13 Low voltage area (LVA) was defined as an area ≥2 cm2, including a minimum of 3 adjacent mapping points with voltage abnormalities. The American Heart Association's standard 17‐segment heart model was employed to describe the distribution of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on cardiac magnetic resonance, and LVA on electroanatomical mapping. 14

Follow‐Up, Definitions, and Study End Points

Data from postprocedural clinic visits, repeat ablations, Holter and event monitors, and defibrillator interrogations were collected. To ensure complete data collection, referring cardiology centers and outside institutions were systematically contacted to obtain and verify clinical status. Follow‐up concluded at permanent left ventricular assist device (LVAD) placement, heart transplant (HT) or death, whichever occurred first. In the VT ablation cohort, we evaluated procedural success based on noninducibility of clinical VT (partial success) or any VT (complete success) at the conclusion of the first ablation procedure and/or during predischarge noninvasive programmed stimulation. Intraprocedural VTs were identified as clinical VTs if their morphologies matched the ECG, stored intracardiac electrogram, and the rhythm strip of the presenting VTs or those of VTs induced during preprocedural noninvasive programmed stimulation. For the VT ablation cohort, long‐term freedom from major VA was analyzed, defined as sustained VT or VF >30 seconds or requirement of appropriate defibrillator therapy, or sudden cardiac death. In the PVC ablation cohort, PVC burden in the immediate postprocedural period and occurrence of major VA was studied. All patients were assessed for freedom from HT, LVAD, and all‐cause mortality.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, and comparisons were performed with Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test when cell counts were <5. Continuous variables are presented as means with SDs or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The comparison of continuous variables was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare the primary and secondary end points for the predefined groups. Univariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze the association between covariates and outcomes. Those with statistical significance (P<0.05) were included in the multivariable regression model. All statistical analyses were performed utilizing SPSS software, version 27 (IBM).

RESULTS

The final cohort included 105 patients who were grouped according to their functional genotype (Figure 1). The desmosomal group was the most prevalent (n=37, 35%), followed by TTN (n=23 [22%]), LMNA (n=21 [20%]), sarcomeric (n=15 [14%]), ion channel (n=5 [5%]), and cytoskeletal/Z‐disk (n=4 [4%]) groups.

Clinical Characteristics

The relevant demographics and clinical characteristics among the assigned genotype groups are depicted in Table 1. With a median age of 51 years (IQR, 37–59 years), the desmosomal group had the youngest patients at 37 years (IQR, 24–55 years), while the TTN group was the oldest at 59 years (IQR, 56–66 years) (P<0.001). The cohort was predominantly male (72%) and of White race (92%). The initial clinical presentation varied among the genotype groups (Figure 2). HF was the predominant initial clinical manifestation in the TTN (70%), sarcomeric (73%), and cytoskeletal/Z‐disk (74%) groups. In contrast, all non‐DSP desmosomal patients and half of the patients with DSP desmosomal presented with VA. The LMNA group exhibited the highest prevalence of third‐degree atrioventricular block (n=9, 43%) and were most likely to have a cardiac resynchronization therapy device (33%, P=0.005).

Echocardiographic and CMR Findings

The cytoskeleton/Z‐disk group had the lowest left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF; 33% [28–42]) and the desmosomal group had the highest (57% [37–60%], P<0.001) (Table 1). Longitudinal analysis over 35 months (15–68 months) comparing baseline (preablation) echocardiographic LVEF with the latest did not show a significant change in any of the groups (Figure 3). The sarcomeric group had the strongest trend towards a decline in LVEF (P=0.08).

A total of 93 patients (89%) underwent contrast‐enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging before ablation. LGE was observed in 75 of 93 (81%) patients, with a high prevalence among all subgroups (Table 1). Figure 4 shows the frequency and distribution of LGE among the predominant genotype groups. Among the genotypes primarily involving the left ventricle, the DSP desmosomal group was predisposed to LGE in the subepicardial basal lateral left ventricle. On the other hand, the TTN, LMNA, and sarcomeric groups were predisposed to midmyocardial basal to mid‐septal LGE. In the cytoskeletal/Z‐disk group (FLNC), all had inferolateral subepicardial LGE and 75% also had septal LGE (see Figure 5 for examples). Notably, a ring‐like LGE pattern, defined as either subepicardial or intramyocardial LGE in ≥3 contiguous LV segments in a single short‐axis plane, was observed in 14% of cases, with varied prevalence among genotypes (P=0.01). The LMNA group had the highest prevalence (38%), followed by the sarcomeric (29%) and cytoskeletal/Z‐disk (25%) groups. This finding was uncommon in the TTN (5%) and desmosomal (3%) groups. None of the 17 patients (16%) who underwent positron emission tomography and computed tomography had abnormal myocardial fludeoxyglucose F18 uptake suggestive of inflammation.

Electroanatomic Mapping and Ablation Findings

Ablation was performed for sustained monomorphic VT in 76% (80 of 105), for high burden PVCs/nonsustained VT in 22% (23 of 105), and PVC‐mediated VF in 2% (2 of 105).

VT Ablation

The distribution and frequencies of VT morphologies in 80 patients undergoing VT ablation are summarized in Table 2. In total, 223 VTs were inducible before ablation: 117 (52%) with left bundle branch block morphology and 106 (48%) with right bundle‐branch block (RBBB) morphology. The cytoskeletal/Z‐disk group had the highest number of inducible VTs (median, 3.5). The details of mapping and ablation are provided in Table 2, and the distribution of bipolar and unipolar LVA is depicted in Figure 4. LV septal bipolar LVA was observed in >50% of the TTN and LMNA groups. Periaortic bipolar LVA were common in the TTN (50%), LMNA (53%), and sarcomeric (73%) groups. Septal unipolar LVA suggestive of midmyocardial scar was seen in the cytoskeletal/Z‐disk (75%), LMNA (67%), TTN (58%), and sarcomeric (55%) groups. In contrast, 57% of patients with DSP desmosomal had lateral LV unipolar LVA, while only 14% had bipolar LVA, consistent with findings of epicardial LGE. Lateral LV unipolar LVA was also frequent in the cytoskeletal/Z‐disk (75%) group. Perimitral unipolar LVA extending to the lateral left ventricle was seen in most patients with DSP desmosomal (71%). In the right ventricle, septal LVA was seen in the TTN, sarcomeric, and ion channel groups (29%–43%). On the other hand, RV free wall unipolar LVA was seen in 91% of the non‐DSP desmosomal group (Table 2).

VT mapping strategy and ablation details are depicted in Table 2. The ablation locations correlated with the location of LVA, with more than half the patients requiring periaortic ablation in the TTN, LMNA, and sarcomeric groups. Septal ablation was more common than lateral LV ablation, reflecting the basal septal/periaortic substrate in these left ventricle–dominant cardiomyopathies (Figure 5). A high proportion (41%) of patients in the sarcomeric group underwent coronary venous mapping of the septal perforators to identify midmyocardial circuits. LV lateral epicardial ablation was performed predominantly in the cytoskeletal/Z‐disk (50%), DSP desmosomal (22%) and TTN (21%) groups. The non‐DSP desmosomal (ARVC phenotype) and ion‐channel groups underwent RV epicardial ablation in 48% and 67%, respectively (Table 2).

PVC and PVC‐Mediated VF Ablation

The preprocedural PVC burden was 12% (IQR 3–1, 21–8). PVC ablation at >1 site was performed in 6 of 25 (24%) patients. The PVCs were localized frequently to LV outflow tract (12 of 25, 48%), with 7 of them exhibiting LVA and/or LGE. The remaining PVCs were localized to RV outflow tract in 8 of 25 (32%), posteromedial papillary muscle in 6 of 25 (24%), para‐Hisian in 2 of 25 (8%), tricuspid annulus in 2 of 25 (8%), mitral annulus in 1 of 25 (4%), and RV apex in 1of 25 (4%) patients. One patient with the sarcomeric gene variant had posteroseptal RV outflow tract PVC‐mediated VF and 1 with the DSP variant had posteromedial papillary muscle PVC‐mediated VF, and both underwent successful ablation without recurrence.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Patients were followed for a median of 3 years (IQR, 1.8–6.3 years). In the VT ablation group, 41 of 80 (51%) remained free of VT recurrence. The mean time to recurrence was 2.2±1.1 years, with only 1 antitachycardia pacing or shock needed in 11 of 39 (28%) patients. VT‐free survival was the lowest (20%) in the LMNA group (P=0.03; Figure 6). Despite extensive substrate, 2 patients in the LMNA group were free of sustained VT for 6 to 8 years after the first ablation, 1 patient with ARVC had no recurrence for 12 years, and 1 patient in each of the FLNC, TTN, and DSP groups were VT‐free for 4 to 5 years. At repeat ablation, coronary venous ethanol ablation and bipolar ablation were employed for septal substrates, and sterotactic body radiation therapy was performed in 1 patient with the TTN variant and 2 patients with sarcomeric variants. When analyzed based on phenotypic classification, patients with ARVC had the highest VT‐free survival and those with the dilated/biventricular cardiomyopathies the lowest (20 of 28 [71%] versus 15 of 40 [38%], P=0.07) (Figure 6). Inducibility of any VT after first ablation (hazard ratio [HR], 2 [95% CI, 1.05–3.77]) and LMNA variant (HR, 2.25 [95% CI, 1.1–4.57]) predicted VT recurrence (Table 3). In the PVC ablation cohort (n=25), the PVC burden was reduced to 3.75% (IQR 3–1, 6–1), 17 of 25 (68%) had ≤5% burden. Repeat PVC ablation was performed in 4 of 25 (16%) patients, with only 1 requiring ablation at the same site as the index procedure (LV summit). Of interest during follow‐up, 9 of 25 (36%) patients who presented with frequent PVCs experienced VT/VF, at 27 months (IQR, 13–60 months). Of the 9 patients, 6 had previously implanted defibrillators for primary prevention and the remaining 3 patients received a defibrillator following PVC ablation for inducible VT or reduced LVEF.

In total, 75 of 105 patients (71%) remained free of the composite HF end point of LVAD, HT, or death. The LMNA group (48%) had the lowest HF end point‐free survival rate and the desmosomal group (89%) the highest (log‐rank P=0.007) (Figure 4). DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the most extensive single‐center report on VA ablation in patients with inherited cardiomyopathy. The significant findings include: (1) a high prevalence (132 of 364, 36%) of pathogenic gene variants in gene panel–tested patients with cardiomyopathy and VA; (2) HF was often the first clinical manifestation in the TTN, LMNA, and cytoskeletal/Z‐disk groups, whereas patients with non‐DSP desmosomal variants presented with VT; (3) high‐grade atrioventricular block was frequent in the LMNA group; (4) midmyocardial septal LGE was common in the TTN, LMNA, and sarcomeric groups, whereas lateral epicardial LGE predominated in the desmosomal group, and both lateral and septal LGE were observed in the cytoskeletal/Z‐disk group; (5) most patients in the nondesmosomal groups underwent periaortic and septal ablation; (6) ablation was effective in eliminating clinical VT and reducing VT recurrence; although LMNA variants and postablation inducibility of any VT predicted recurrence; (7) VT/VF occurrence was common in patients who underwent PVC ablation (36%); and (8) the LMNA group had the highest risk of LVAD, HT, and all‐cause mortality.

Although the majority of the genetic variants described herein have an LV predominant phenotype, we chose to perform a genotype‐based analysis to understand the consequences of impaired functionality of the various subcellular components (Figure 1). The TTN gene encodes the titin protein, the largest protein in humans. Titin differs from the rest of the sarcomeric proteins by maintaining sarcomere integrity, force transmission, and stretch sensing. 15 TTN variants are the most prevalent in dilated cardiomyopathy, with truncating variants linked to worse outcomes. 12 HF was universal in our patients, with an LVEF <35% in the majority, and 1 in 3 required LVAD/HT or experienced HF‐related death. This contrasts with findings of LV reverse remodeling and benign course described by others. 16 , 17 In addition, we found that RBBB and left bundle branch block morphology VTs were equally distributed. This argues against the reported predominance of RBBB VT morphology in LV cardiomyopathy by Belhassen et al. 18 However, their study population comprised patients with DSP variants characterized by LV lateral/epicardial substrate, resulting in VT with RBBB, whereas TTN‐ and LMNA‐associated cardiomyopathy more commonly demonstrate antero‐septal substrate and, thus, VT with left bundle branch block morphology. 19

Unlike TTN, the genes under the motor sarcomeric group (MYBPC3, MYH7, TMP1, and ALPK3) encode proteins responsible for muscle contraction. The MYBPC3 gene encodes myosin‐binding protein C, which acts as a “brake” by restricting interaction between actin and myosin filaments. The MYH7 gene encodes myosin heavy‐chain beta, which is responsible for muscle contraction by forming the thick filaments in the sarcomere. 20 , 21 Variants in these genes account for the majority of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, but dilated cardiomyopathy has also been reported. 5 , 20 Myocardial disarray, increased wall thickness, and complex intramural septal circuits result in lower VT ablation success rates due to ineffective lesion delivery. 22 Compared with other reports, our cohort had fewer apical hypertrophy variants but similar ablation success rates. 5 , 21 In our phenotype‐based analysis, the hypertrophic group had the lowest LVAD/HT‐free survival (Figure 6). One in 3 patients underwent LVAD/HT, which is higher than previously reported. 5 This suggests that advanced HF therapy evaluation should be triggered in sarcomeric variants with recurrent VA.

Desmosomal P/LP variants impair cell‐to‐cell adhesion, leading to cell detachment, cell death, and fibrofatty myocardial replacement. 23 Although phenotype depends heavily on the gene variant—ARVC in PKP2, JUP, DSC2, DSG2, or LV cardiomyopathy in DSP—lateral/free‐wall subepicardial scar is a common feature among these conditions. 8 , 11 While left bundle branch block VT with circuitry involving the RV free‐wall is most common in the ARVC phenotype, a small proportion of patients had RBBB VT requiring ablation at the RV inferior septum. We have previously demonstrated that the majority of RBBB VT cases in ARVC CMP, particularly with an early precordial transition to negative QRS by V3, originated in the right ventricle and exited at the apical septal aspect of the RV. Extensive free‐wall fibrofatty replacement may force activation towards the apical septum with delayed activation of the basal right ventricle, yielding RBBB VT. 24 Following the first ablation with an endocardial and adjunct epicardial approach, the long‐term VT‐free survival was 66% in this report, with only a single episode of VT in an additional 13% of patients. In 2015, we reported a similar VT‐free survival rate of 71% at 56±44 months. 25 In keeping with the previous findings, a quarter of our patients with DSP desmosomal underwent epicardial ablation at the inferolateral left ventricle, but the ring‐like subepicardial LGE was lower than previously reported. 5 , 8 , 26 Fibrofatty changes are localized to the lateral left ventricle with limited septal involvement, and LVEF is often preserved. The desmosomal group had the highest HT/LVAD‐free survival (89%) and no HF‐related deaths, findings identical to those reported by Cabrera et al. 8 Thus, effective catheter ablation can prevent LVAD/HT for arrhythmia management in these patients and delay LVAD/HT to later‐stage HF.

The LMNA gene encodes lamin A/C filaments, which are essential components of the protein network constituting the nuclear membrane. P/LP variants disrupt the nuclear membrane, impair mechanotransduction, activate cellular stress pathways and DNA damage response, and promote apoptosis. Our findings corroborate previous reports of a high incidence of atrioventricular block, midmyocardial ring‐like LGE, and VT storms, with the worse trends towards rapidly progressive HF, VT recurrence, and mortality. 16 , 27 , 28 , 29 Important additional observations include: (1) a high prevalence of diffuse unipolar voltage abnormalities (Figure 5) despite the absence of diffuse LGE, a predictor of worse outcomes 1 ; (2) intramural septal VT circuitry requiring unconventional techniques, ie, ethanol or bipolar ablation; (3) epicardial lateral LV VT circuitry was not uncommon, with ≈1 in 3 requiring epicardial ablation; and (4) myocardial reentrant VT was the most prevalent, but fascicular VT, bundle branch re‐entrant VT, and papillary muscle VT were also seen. LMNA variants are reported to account for 5% to 13% of all inherited cardiomyopathies. 5 These ominous findings underscore the advantage of genotype‐based risk assessment for an early collaborative approach between electrophysiologists and HF specialists.

The cytoskeletal/Z‐disk group encompasses FLNC variants and is a largely unexplored group. FLNC is an actin cross‐linking protein that anchors cell membrane proteins to the cytoskeleton and maintains sarcomeric structural integrity. 11 , 30 The prevalence of FLNC cardiomyopathy is lower than LMNA cardiomyopathy, with a high risk of malignant VA. 30 Subepicardial LGE is common, with a prevalence of 18% to 100%. 11 Despite previous reports of exclusive lateral LV epicardial ablation, 8 half of our patients received lateral epicardial ablation and half required ablation targeting extensive septal and perivalvular substrates. While all our patients had depressed LV function, a multicenter study observed that the risk of VA was not associated with severity of LV dysfunction. In their report, 23% of patients who experienced malignant VA had an LVEF >50%. 30 This again highlights the value of sudden cardiac death risk assessment based on genotype rather than phenotype.

Our study adds to the existing literature by providing a longer‐term assessment extending beyond ≈2‐year follow‐up, 5 , 8 not only on VT ablation but also HF outcomes, while offering new insights into outcomes of PVC ablation with future risk of VT/VF and the overall value of genotype‐based risk stratification. It is also the largest report on VA ablation in LMNA cardiomyopathy. Overall, despite excellent success in eliminating clinical VT, the inducibility of nonclinical VTs and recurrence rates highlight the complex pathology that remains inadequately researched. 31 The reason behind observed heterogeneity in time to VT recurrence, both within and across the genotypes, remains unclear and needs further investigation.

The higher prevalence of LV outflow tract PVCs compared with RV outflow tract PVCs supports the frequent finding of periaortic voltage abnormalities. Two such patients (TTN) who underwent PVC‐only ablation had inducible VT necessitating defibrillator implantation and both had sustained VT at follow‐up. Thus, inherited cardiomyopathy must be considered in the differential during evaluation of patients with LV outflow tract PVCs and LVAs.

Finally, imaging‐based risk stratification to guide defibrillator implantation can be challenging as several patients, despite the need for ablation with electrogram and substrate abnormalities, had no LGE (40% ion‐channel, 31% desmosomal, 21% TTN, 12% LMNA, 7% sarcomeric groups). Detection of layered epicardial scar may be limited with CMR imaging but can be identified by the presence of endocardial unipolar voltage abnormalities, which are recorded on the endocardium opposite the epicardial substrate. 32 , 33

Limitations

Although a larger study population than previous reports, our sample size was limited to a single‐center experience, and patients with P/LP variants who did not undergo catheter ablation were excluded. Nonetheless, our cohort provides valuable insights into patients at the highest risk, enhancing our understanding of the disease's natural course. Retrospective analysis and the absence of a standardized ablation protocol may introduce bias. We present procedural outcomes from a high‐volume institution with experienced operators in complex VT ablations, and the availability of advanced HF therapies may influence treatment pathways. Therefore, the results might not be universally applicable.

CONCLUSIONS

We highlight the distinguishing phenotypic features of functional genotype groups in patients with inherited cardiomyopathy presenting with VA. While the final common pathway is HF or refractory VA, the pathophysiology behind differential fibrofatty replacement and variable disease progression remains unclear. Translational work is necessary to learn if episodic myocardial injury or myocarditis, inflammation, autoimmune, or environmental triggers play a role in exacerbating VT or HF, and to consider targeted therapies. Catheter ablation can be used to eliminate PVCs and reduce VT burden; however, long‐term recurrence is common, particularly with LMNA variants. This report underscores the value of a genotype‐first approach for appropriate risk stratification and timely management of arrhythmias and heart failure in patients with nonischemic cardiomyopathy undergoing catheter ablation.
