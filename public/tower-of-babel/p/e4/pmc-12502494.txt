Preterm delivery is a risk factor for intrapartum complications in a subsequent term delivery
Authors: Rachel Gomez-Tolub, Ronit Silber, Shir Danieli-Gruber, Emilie Klochendler Frishman, Alyssa Hochberg, Ohad Houri, Gil Zeevi, Eran Hadar, Avital Wertheimer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm delivery before 37 weeks is a serious obstetrical complication and the leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality. We aimed to evaluate the association between preterm vaginal delivery and intrapartum complications in a subsequent term delivery.MethodsThe database of a tertiary hospital was retrospectively reviewed for all women with two consecutive deliveries between 2012 and 2020: a first vaginal delivery and a second term delivery. The cohort was divided by gestational age at first delivery: (1) before 34 weeks; (2) between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks; and (3) term (≥ 37 weeks).ResultsAmong 7264 women, 41 (0.6%) delivered before 34 weeks, 262 (3.6%) between 34 + 0–36 + 6 weeks, and 6961 (95.8%) at term. First preterm delivery was associated with higher rates of cesarean section at second delivery (17.0%, 7.3%, 5.4% in groups 1, 2, 3, respectively, p < 0.005), with higher urgent cesarean rates (85.0%, 78.9%, 48.4%, respectively, p = 0.0058), and higher assisted vaginal delivery rates (12.2%, 5.8%, 3.0%, respectively, p < 0.005). Multivariate analysis controlling for maternal age, gestational age at second delivery, assisted vaginal delivery at first delivery, and gestational diabetes showed first delivery before 34 weeks was an independent risk factor for subsequent cesarean or assisted delivery (aOR = 3.1, 95% CI 1.3–7.3 and aOR = 11.4, 95% CI 4.2–30.9, respectively).ConclusionsA first preterm delivery poses a risk of intrapartum complications in a subsequent term delivery, including cesarean section, urgent cesarean section, and assisted vaginal delivery.Keywords: Preterm, Delivery, Cesarean section, Assisted vaginal delivery, Intrapartum complications

Background

Preterm delivery (PTD) before 37 weeks of gestation, is a serious obstetrical complication and a leading cause of short and long term neonatal morbidity and mortality [1] It occurs in about 11% of births worldwide [2]. Despite extensive research, the pathogenesis of preterm labor remains poorly understood [3]. Suggested mechanisms include an exaggerated inflammatory response, altered genital tract microbiome [4], decidual placental blood vessel damage [5] and stress-induced premature activation of labor-triggering hormonal axis [4–6]. These factors may also relate to intrapartum complications, such as placental dysfunction causing both PTD and non-reassuring fetal heart rate leading to cesarean delivery [7].

Another proposed mechanism linking previous PTD to subsequent intrapartum complications is neonatal size. PTD involves passing a smaller neonate which potentially predisposes women to complications commonly associated with nulliparity during the second term delivery.

While most research in the field of preterm delivery has focused on preventing recurrent PTD [8, 9], the rate of subsequent term birth is as high as 70% [10]and the possibility that a previous PTD may affect subsequent term deliveries has been largely unexplored [11].

Indeed, these same pathophysiological pathways that are implicated in pre-term delivery, also directly contribute to intrapartum complications. For example, placental dysfunction and insufficiency can simultaneously trigger preterm delivery and cause non-reassuring fetal heart rate patterns, necessitating intrapartum cesarean delivery. Another proposed mechanism linking prior PTD to subsequent intrapartum complications is neonatal size. Delivering a smaller neonate in a previous PTD may predispose women to complications typically seen in nulliparous women during a subsequent term delivery.

Furthermore, there is data from Sweden and Netherlands demonstrating a 1.6-fold increase in subsequent stillbirth risk after PTD [11, 12], suggesting that prior PTD may indicate biological susceptibility to adverse pregnancy outcomes beyond recurrence.

Given the potential common etiology between PTD and intrapartum maternal and neonatal complications, this study aimed to investigate intrapartum complications in term deliveries following a prior PTD.

Methods

Study population

A retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary university medical center between 2012 and 2020. The cohort included all women with two consecutive deliveries during the study period: a first vaginal delivery followed by a second, term delivery. Exclusion criteria were age < 18 years, multiple gestation, first delivery by cesarean section, and second delivery before term (≤37 weeks). The study cohort was stratified into three groups according to gestational age at the first delivery: (1) early preterm, before 34 + 0 weeks; (2) late preterm, between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks; and (3) term, ≥ 37 weeks. The three study groups were compared with respect to baseline demographic and obstetric characteristics in both the first and second pregnancies (Table 1), as well as labor and neonatal outcomes of the subsequent term delivery (Tables 2 and 3).

Data collection

All deliveries at our center are registered in the computerized database of the delivery ward, and all diagnoses are coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9). For the present study, data on clinical and obstetrical characteristics and outcomes for both the first and subsequent deliveries were collected from the computerized database, as follows: maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index, gestational age at delivery, and inter-pregnancy interval; onset of labor, use of anesthesia, mode of delivery, and indication for assisted vaginal delivery or cesarean delivery; perineal tears, obstetrical and maternal complications including gestational diabetes mellitus, antepartum and intrapartum placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, postpartum hemorrhage, revision of the uterine cavity, and length of second and third stages of labor. Neonatal outcome parameters included birth weight, 1- and 5-min Apgar scores, umbilical cord pH, and neonatal intensive care unit admission.

Clinical definitions

Elective cesarean delivery was defined as a cesarean section that was planned before delivery occurred. Urgent cesarean delivery was defined as a cesarean section performed as a prompt intervention in an emergency setting. The principal factors that warrant an urgent caesarean section may be maternal (eclampsia and severe pre-eclampsia), obstetrical (suspected placental abruption, uterine rupture, hemorrhage due to placenta previa, protracted labor), or fetal (umbilical cord prolapse, non-reassuring fetal heart rate). Intrapartum cesarean delivery was defined as a subcategory of urgent cesarean section, performed in a laboring patient, usually because of protracted labor, arrest of dilatation, or non-reassuring fetal heart rate.

Operative vaginal deliveries were defined as assisted vaginal births performed using vacuum extraction only, as forceps are not used in our institution.

Clinical chorioamnionitis was diagnosed based on maternal fever (> 38.0 °C) accompanied by at least two of the following signs: maternal tachycardia (> 100 bpm), fetal tachycardia (> 160 bpm), uterine tenderness, foul-smelling amniotic fluid, or maternal leukocytosis (> 15,000 cells/mm³).

High-grade perineal tears were defined as third-degree lacerations involving the anal sphincter muscles and fourth-degree lacerations involving the anal sphincter complex and rectal epithelium.

Anesthesia type during labor was documented as part of the intrapartum management. This included regional anesthesia (such as epidural or spinal) and general anesthesia (when indicated, typically for urgent cesarean deliveries).

Statistical analysis

Continuous quantitative variables with a normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and with a non-normal distribution, as median and interquartile range. Continuous variables were compared between groups with Kruskal Wallis test; Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Categorical variables are reported as proportions and were compared between groups using chi-square or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Logistic multivariate regression analysis was used to test the association between gestational age at first delivery and risk of a cesarean or assisted vaginal procedure at the subsequent delivery, controlling for variables found to be significant on univariate analysis and other potential confounders. All statistical analyses were conducted with a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals. SAS, version 9.4 was used for analysis.

The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board, which waived the need for informed consent due to the retrospective design.

Results

A total of 7264 women met the inclusion criteria. Gestational age at birth at the first delivery was less than 34 + 0 weeks in 41 women (0.6%, group 1), between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks in 262 women (3.6%, group 2), and after 37 + 0 weeks in 6961 women (95.8%, group 3). Corresponding median (range) gestational age values for the three groups were 32 weeks (23–33), 36 weeks (34–36), and 39 weeks (37–43).

The demographic and baseline characteristics of the three groups at the first and second deliveries are presented in Table 1. Baseline characteristics were similar among the groups, except for rate of gestational diabetes which was significantly higher in group 2 (9.9%) than in groups 1 (2.4%) and 3 (6.4%) (p = 0.0408). Rates of intrapartum complications at first delivery were higher in group 1 than in groups 2 and 3; for placental abruption, 12.2% vs. 2.3% and 0.4% (p = 0.000); for chorioamnionitis, 2.4% vs. 0% and 0.1% (p = 0.000). The proportion of assisted vaginal deliveries increased with an increase in gestational age (7.3% in group 1, 15.3% in group 2, 21.0% in group 3 (p = 0.0085). Of the three assisted vaginal deliveries in group 1, only one was performed before 33 weeks (with a kiwi device) due to non-reassuring fetal heart rate. A similar trend was observed for anesthesia during delivery (Table 1).

Modes of the second delivery are shown by gestational age in Fig. 1. A first PTD was significantly associated with higher rates of subsequent cesarean and assisted vaginal deliveries in each of the three groups: for cesarean delivery -- 17.0% in group 1, 7.25% in group 2, 5.4% in group 3 (p < 0.005); for assisted vaginal delivery -- 12.2%, 5.7%, 3.0%, respectively (p = 0.000).

Mode of delivery in second term delivery

The intrapartum characteristics of the second delivery are presented in Table 2. Among the cesarean deliveries, the large majority were performed on an urgent basis: 85.0% in group 1, 78.9% in group 2, and 48.4% in group 3 (p = 0.006).

Among all deliveries, the rate of intrapartum cesarean section was higher at the second delivery among women who gave birth before term at the first delivery (2.4%, 3.0%, and 1.4% in groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively; p < 0.005).

The main reason for performing cesarean deliveries was contraindication to vaginal delivery, in 44% of cases—most commonly due to non-cephalic fetal presentation, which accounted for 30.3% of these cases—followed by fetal distress in 24% (14.2%, 36.8%, and 22.0% in groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively; p = 0.164).

The most common indication for assisted vaginal delivery was non-reassuring fetal heart rate (100.0%, 60.0% and 70.0% in groups 1, 2 and 3, respectively), followed by prolonged second stage, with no significant difference among the groups. Rates of placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, prolonged second or third stage of labor, postpartum hemorrhage, revision of uterine cavity, and perineal tears were similar in the three groups.

Neonatal outcomes at the second delivery are presented in Table 3. Neonatal birthweight was significantly correlated with gestational age at first delivery (3108.2 g in group 1, vs. 3125.7 g in group 2 and 3312.0 g in group 3, p < 0.0001). There were no differences among the groups in fetal well-being, including Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min, pH, and rate of NICU admission.

On multivariate regression analysis (Table 4), controlling for potential cofounders of maternal age, gestational age at the second delivery, assisted vaginal delivery at the first delivery, and gestational diabetes, a first PTD before 34 weeks was found to be an independent risk factor for cesarean section at the second (term) delivery compared to a first delivery at term (aOR = 3.1, CI 95% 1.3–7.3) and a first delivery between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks (aOR = 3.1, CI 95% 1.2–8.1). A first PTD before 34 weeks was also independently associated with a subsequent assisted vaginal delivery compared to a first delivery at term (aOR = 11.4, CI 95% 4.2–31.0%) and a first delivery between 34 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks (aOR = 4.0, CI 95% 1.3–12.2). Other risk factors that remained statistically significant after controlling for confounders were maternal age, early gestational age at second delivery, and assisted vaginal procedure at the first delivery.

Discussion

This study explored the association between a history of preterm birth and perinatal complications in a subsequent term delivery. The principal findings of our study demonstrate that a history of PTD is an independent risk factor for both urgent cesarean section and assisted vaginal delivery in subsequent term pregnancies. Notably, this risk increases as the gestational age of the initial preterm delivery decreases.

Previous studies reported an association between PTD and subsequent pregnancy complications beyond PTD recurrence. Ananth and Vintzileos [13] found that women with a spontaneous PTD were not only more likely to experience a recurrent spontaneous PTD, but also were at increased risk for a medically indicated PTD. These results suggest that although spontaneous and induced PTDs seem to have divergent etiologies, they may reflect common underlying pathological conditions. Moreover, Surkan et al. [11] reported a significant association between prior PTD or delivery of a small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infant and the risk of stillbirth in a subsequent pregnancy, with risk increasing as the gestational age of the first delivery decreased. Similarly, a large population-based study from Scotland by Smith et al. [14] demonstrated that women with a previous preterm birth had more than a twofold increased risk of unexplained stillbirth in the next pregnancy.

This current study confirms that prior PTD is a risk factor for obstetrical complications beyond recurrent PTD and provides novel insights into intrapartum complications following an initial PTD. Collectively, these findings establish that women with a history of PTD constitute a high-risk cohort requiring antenatal surveillance not only during pregnancy—to prevent recurrent PTD—but also during labor and delivery as they approach term gestation.

There are several possible explanations for our observation that previous PTD was a significant risk factor for subsequent intrapartum complications.

First, PTD and intrapartum complications may share a common etiology. Romero [15] introduced the term “great obstetrical syndrome” to describe the spectrum of clinical conditions responsible for maternal and perinatal morbidities. It emphasizes the idea that multiple obstetrical conditions in pregnancy, such as preterm labor, small for gestational age, and stillbirth are diverse expressions of a common and complex pathophysiological pathway [3]. The process of labor is also directly linked to this pathway [16]: Uterine contractions during labor may cause a decline in fetal oxygen [17]. Most healthy fetuses can withstand this effect, but when the pregnancy is not optimal, oxygenation may be inadequate and lead to intrapartum complications [17]. Likewise, abnormal placental development is directly linked to both PTD and non-reassuring fetal heart rate, resulting in intrapartum cesarean or assisted vaginal delivery [18]. Our study supports this explanation given the higher rates of cesarean and assisted vaginal deliveries among the women with a previous PTD and, especially, the higher rate of emergent cesarean delivery (85%) subsequent to previous PTD. In addition, the lower birthweights observed in neonates born to women with prior PTD in subsequent term deliveries, further support the hypothesis of persistent, subclinical placental dysfunction, consistent with the notion of a shared pathophysiological pathway. Notably, we did not find significant differences in the rates of hypertensive disorders between groups, and the absolute number of such cases in the second pregnancy was low. This may be related to our highly specific cohort, as women with recurrent or severe hypertensive complications were likely to have a first cesarean delivery or deliver preterm again and thus were not included in our pre-term-term cohort, or alternatively, improved preventive measures such as low-dose aspirin may have reduced recurrence, though this cannot be fully assessed based on the available data.

Building on this conceptual framework, Villar et al. recently introduced a revised classification of the preterm birth (PTB) syndrome. Rather than focusing solely on gestational age, their taxonomy groups PTB based on distinct underlying causes—such as inflammation, uterine overdistension, and cervical dysfunction—highlighting the biological diversity of the condition. This shift in perspective suggests that factors beyond timing alone may play an important role in determining the clinical course, including intrapartum events [19].

Second, our findings may be related to the theory of the “untested pelvis effect” which suggests that primiparas and multiparas behave differently in labor [20]. The first delivery is considered the “true test” of the pelvis and is therefore usually characterized by births that are more complicated, with higher rates of assisted vaginal and cesarean deliveries anal sphincter injury and lower Apgar scores [7]. PTD, which involves the passage of a smaller neonate, poses less of a challenge to the pelvis [21]. Accordingly, the subsequent term delivery may more closely resemble a first delivery than a second, with a higher-than-normal rate of intrapartum complications [22, 23].

The accurate identification of women at risk of adverse outcomes during labor is an integral part of intrapartum care [24]. A prior study of a low-risk pregnancy population in the United States found that 29% had an unexpected complication requiring non-routine obstetrical or neonatal care [25]. Our research seeks to provide clinicians tools for diagnosing high-risk patients in the delivery room, allowing better management during childbirth.

A significant strength of our study lies in its novelty. To our knowledge, this is the first study in the literature to evaluate the association between prior PTD and intrapartum complications during a subsequent term delivery. Our finding that a prior PTD is significantly associated with increased rates of cesarean section or assisted vaginal procedures at the next term birth aids in patient counselling and risk stratification during the subsequent pregnancies. This finding sheds light on potential shared pathophysiological mechanisms between preterm delivery and intrapartum complications, including in term pregnancies. Furthermore, our study cohort was comprised of a diverse and heterogeneous patient population, enhancing the generalizability of our findings and also allowed for the stratification by gestational age at the first delivery, which further substantiated the association between earlier PTD and the heightened risk of assisted or instrumental delivery in subsequent pregnancies. The main limitation of this study is its retrospective design, which restricted our ability to account for certain potential confounding factors. Specifically, data regarding the indication for the first preterm delivery and the presence of fetal growth restriction were not consistently documented and therefore could not be included in the analysis. In addition, the relatively small size of the early preterm subgroup limits the statistical power to detect meaningful differences in outcomes within this group. This may affect the reliability of comparisons involving this subgroup.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that a previous PTD, especially before 34 weeks, is associated with a higher risk of intrapartum complications in the subsequent term delivery. By providing clinicians with a tool for risk stratification, this study may help to refine patient management in the delivery room, thereby improving maternal and neonatal outcomes.
