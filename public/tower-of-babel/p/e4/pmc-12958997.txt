Associations between indices of body composition and metabolic status in normal-weight adults: a cross-sectional study of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study
Authors: Suzan Maleki, Farhad Hosseinpanah, Maryam Mahdavi, Amir Abbas Momenan, Seyyed Alireza Ebadi, Fatemeh Rahmani, Fereidoun Azizi, Majid Valizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate associations between body composition indices and metabolic status among normal-weight adults.DesignCross-sectional study using data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (phase VII: 2019–2021).SettingPrimary care and community health services in an urban Tehran population.Participants1298 adults (40.5% men, 59.5% women), aged 18–80 years, body mass index (BMI) 18.5–24.9 kg/m². Exclusions: known diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, malignancy, pregnancy or lactation, diuretic or glucocorticoid use. Participants were classified as metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW) or unhealthy (MUHNW).Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the association between body composition and anthropometric indices with metabolic status. The secondary outcome was identification of the strongest predictors of MUHNW. Body composition was assessed by bioelectrical impedance analysis to obtain fat mass (FM), body fat percentage (BFP), skeletal muscle mass percentage (SMM%), fat mass index (FMI), fat-free mass index, skeletal muscle indices and the fat-to-muscle mass ratio (FMR). Anthropometric measures included waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). Associations were examined using logistic regression adjusted for age, smoking and physical activity.ResultsMean age: 37.5 ± 12.8 y; MUHNW participants were older than MHNW (44.5 ± 13.2 vs 35.8 ± 12.1 years, p<0.001). MUHNW prevalence was 19.4% (higher in men, p<0.001). Adjusted mean BMI, WC, WHR, FM, BFP, FMR and FMI were higher in MUHNW in both sexes. SMM% was protective in men (OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.96). WHR was the strongest predictor of MUHNW (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.93 men; OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.61 women).ConclusionsBMI, WC, WHR and body fat indices were positively associated with metabolically unhealthy status among normal-weight adults of both sexes. WHR was the strongest predictor, highlighting its value for identifying at-risk individuals where advanced body composition tools are unavailable.

Introduction

Body mass index (BMI) is a common diagnostic indicator of obesity. However, defining obesity solely by BMI has been increasingly challenged. A major limitation of BMI is that it cannot differentiate central from peripheral adiposity or distinguish between fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass. Evidence suggests that body fat distribution, rather than excess weight alone, plays a key role in determining metabolic health.14

Some individuals with a normal BMI may still exhibit adverse metabolic profiles, often referred to as ‘metabolically unhealthy normal weight’ (MUHNW).5 6 In the 1980s, Ruderman et al described such individuals as being prone to hyperinsulinaemia and insulin resistance, and at increased risk of type 2 diabetes, hypertriglyceridaemia and premature coronary artery disease.7 Previous studies have shown that MUHNW individuals are at higher risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality compared with their metabolically healthy peers, either normal weight or with obesity.58 Identifying factors that contribute to abnormal metabolic health in the normal-weight population is therefore crucial.9

Body composition indices, including fat and muscle mass, are strongly associated with metabolic health. Higher body FM is related to greater visceral fat accumulation and a higher likelihood of adverse metabolic profiles.1012 The relationship between muscle mass and metabolic health has been extensively investigated in multiple studies.1317 Despite the increasing association between body composition and metabolic health in normal weight individuals, regional differences in study populations limit the generalisability of findings, with Middle Eastern cohorts remaining underrepresented.

This cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the association between body composition indices, measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), and metabolic health among normal-weight adults in the Iranian population. It was hypothesised that certain BIA-derived body composition indices are significantly associated with metabolically unhealthy status in this group.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study is a secondary analysis of data obtained from phase VII (2019–2021) of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), a large-scale, ongoing population-based prospective cohort aimed at identifying risk factors for non-communicable diseases in the Iranian population. The TLGS protocol has been previously published,18 and the study is registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) under the code IRCT20131110015488N20.

This cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from 9360 participants enrolled in phase VII of the TLGS. Individuals aged 18–80 years with a BMI between 18.5 and 25 kg/m² and available body composition data assessed via a BIA device were considered eligible. Exclusion criteria included known diabetes (n=73), heart failure (n=37), kidney failure (glomerular filtration rate (GFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m²) (n=302), history of malignancy (n=11), pregnancy or lactation (n=16) and use of diuretics (n=16) or glucocorticoid medications (n=36), with missing data on body composition (n=2775), unknown BMI (n=73), BMI<18.5 (n=101), BMI>25 (n=4609) and unknown metabolic status (n=13) were also excluded. Newly diagnosed cases of diabetes were not excluded. After applying the criteria, 1298 participants were included in the final analysis.

Clinical and laboratory measurements

Demographic information of people, including age, sex, education level, medical history, history of medication and smoking was collected by trained interviewers.

Physical activity data were collected using the Iranian version of the Multidimensional self-report measure (MAQ questionnaire) whose reliability and validity in Iranian urban adults have been previously established by Momenan et al.19 The participants were asked to report the frequency and duration of activities performed in each area in the previous week. Metabolic equivalents (MET) values were assigned to each activity, and total MET minutes per week were calculated. People were then grouped into two groups: low physical activity (<600 min per week) and appropriate/high physical activity (>600 min per week).

All anthropometric measurements were performed following the standard protocol of the TLGS, as described by Azizi et al.18 The weight of the individuals was measured and recorded with a digital scale with an accuracy of 100 g with minimal clothes and without shoes. Height was also measured with a tape measure with an accuracy of 1 cm while standing next to the wall and without shoes, while the shoulders of the subjects were in normal conditions. Each participant’s height was measured three times, and the average of these repeated measurements was used for analysis to increase measurement accuracy and reduce possible errors. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m2). Waist circumference at the umbilical area and hip circumference at the area of the largest diameter of the hips was measured without any pressure on the body and with an accuracy of 0.1 cm. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured with a standard mercury sphygmomanometer according to the standard TLGS protocol,18 after 15 min of rest in a sitting position and in the right arm, twice at 10 min intervals. Korotukov’s phase I and V sounds were considered as SBP and DBP, respectively, and then the mean of these two measurements was recorded as SBP and DBP.

Blood samples were taken from each participant after 12–14 hours of overnight fasting while they were in a sitting position. All analyses were performed in the laboratory of the TLGS unit on the day of blood sample collection, using Selectra 2 autoanalyzer (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, the Netherlands). Fasting blood glucose was measured using an enzymatic colorimetric kit with the glucose oxidase method. Serum triacylglycerol levels were measured using an oxidase-glycerol phosphate enzyme colorimetric method. Serum high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC) levels, indicating the cholesterol content of the corresponding lipoprotein particles rather than their number or size, were measured according to the standard TLGS laboratory protocol.18 HDLC was measured after apolipoprotein B precipitation by phosphotungstate acid, and LDLC was calculated using the equation of Friedewald, Levy and Fredrickson when triglycerol (TG) levels were <400 mg/dL. Inter-assay and intra-assay coefficients of variations at baseline were 2.2% for serum glucose, 2.0% and 0.5% for HDL-C, and 1.6% and 0.6% for TG.18

Body composition was assessed using a portable multi-frequency BIA device (model: InBody 570, InBody Co., Ltd, Seoul, South Korea), which is known for its validity and reproducibility in various populations, factors such as hydration status, body temperature and population-specific traits can affect the accuracy of the BIA measurements. The conditions that had to be followed before the body composition measurement included fasting for 2 hours, avoiding caffeine for 2 hours, avoiding exercise for 4–6 hours, emptying the bladder before the measurement and wearing light clothes without shoes.

Body FM (in kg), body fat percentage (BFP) (% BF/weight), skeletal muscle mass percentage (SMM%), fat mass index (FMI) (in kg/m2), fat-free mass index (FFMI) (in kg/m2) and skeletal muscle mass index (SMMI) (kg/m2) were also calculated by dividing each value by the square of height. The FMR was calculated by dividing the FM by SMM.

Definitions

MUHNW was defined as having a BMI between 18.5 and 25 (18.5˂BMI<25) kg/m² and meeting at least two of the following criteria: blood pressure≥130/85 mm Hg or taking antihypertensive medication, TG≥150 mg/dL or taking TG-lowering medication, HDL-C levels<40 mg/dL in men and<50 mg/dL in women, Pre-diabetes status, 100<fast blood sugar (FBS)< 125 mg/dL, newly diagnosed diabetes, FBS≥126 mg/dL, with no history of diabetes treatment. Metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW) was defined as 18.5˂BMI<25 kg/m² and having zero or one of the above criteria.20

Patient and public involvement

None.

Statistical analysis

All normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean (±SD). Otherwise, skewed continuous variables were presented as median and IQR (25th–75th percentile). Categorical baseline characteristics were reported as frequencies (percentages). Baseline characteristics between MUHNW individuals and MHNW individuals were compared using independent t-tests for normally distributed quantitative variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for skewed distributed quantitative variables and χ2 tests for qualitative variables. Body composition indices were also compared between women and men. Mean values of body composition indices in different phenotypes were compared with analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) after adjustment for age, smoking and physical activity. We estimated the adjusted mean for the total population and separately for men and women. To assess the association between metabolically unhealthy status and body composition indices, multivariable logistic regression models were used. The models were adjusted for age, smoking status and physical activity level, which were selected as potential confounders based on established evidence of their influence on both body composition and metabolic health.21 22

The assumptions underlying the statistical models were evaluated and confirmed. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. When minor deviations were indicated by statistical tests, clinical judgement was applied, consistent with previous studies. All variables demonstrated approximate normality except TG level, which showed a skewed distribution. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.20.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, USA). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant (two-tailed test). Finally, a post hoc power analysis was conducted to confirm the adequacy of our sample size. The analysis confirmed that with our sample of 1298 participants, the study had a statistical power >80% to detect the observed associations for our primary predictors at a two-tailed significance level of α=0.05.

To ensure the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed. In this analysis, the definition of metabolically unhealthy status was set as having at least one abnormal metabolic component (mentioned above), while metabolically healthy status was defined as the absence of any metabolic component. Body composition indices were then re-evaluated based on this revised definition to assess the consistency of the results.

Results

Of the 1298 normal-weight adults, 1046 (80.6%) were metabolically healthy (MHNW) and 252 (19.4%) were metabolically unhealthy (MUHNW). The proportion of MUHNW was significantly higher in men (61.5%) compared with women (38.5%, p<0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed between MHNW and MUHNW individuals regarding physical activity levels or smoking status. However, MHNW individuals reported significantly higher education, and MUHNW individuals were significantly older. MUHNW individuals exhibited significantly higher mean SBP and DBP compared with MHNW individuals. Similarly, FBS, TG and LDL-C were significantly higher in the MUHNW group (p<0.001). Association between body composition indices and metabolically unhealthy status

Based on univariate logistic regression analysis, it was found that BMI, WHR, WC, FMI, BFM, BFP and FMR were directly associated with metabolically unhealthy status in normal weight individuals in both men and women. The results were consistent after adjusting for age, physical activity and smoking.

In the adjusted model, indices with the greatest risk for MUHNW status were WHR and FMR (in men: OR (95% CI): 1.52 (1.20 to 1.93), 1.49 (1.20 to 1.87)), (in women: OR (95% CI): 1.31 (1.07 to 1.61), 1.29 (1.02 to 1.63)), respectively. SMM % was a protective factor for MUHNW status in men (OR (95% CI) 0.89 (0.83 to 0.95)) (tables4 5).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the associations between BIA-derived body composition indices and metabolic health among normal-weight Iranian adults. We found that the WHR was the strongest independent predictor of the MUHNW phenotype, whereas SMM% had a protective role in men. These findings emphasise the clinical and public health relevance of WHR as an accessible marker for early detection of metabolic risk in populations where advanced body composition tools are not routinely available.

Our observation that individuals with MUHNW exhibit higher values for indices of adiposity is consistent with previous research. For instance, Xia et al reported significantly higher BMI, WC and BFP in MUHNW individuals,1 while Wang et al found that FM and FMI were stronger predictors of this phenotype than BMI.15 Notably, a study by Fan et al identified FMI as the strongest predictor of MUHNW status.8 In contrast, our findings diverge by identifying a simpler anthropometric measure, the WHR, as the most potent predictor in our cohort.

WHR is a well-established surrogate for central obesity and visceral fat accumulation—key drivers of metabolic dysfunction.23 24 Esmaillzadeh et al reported that WHR is a better screening measure for cardiovascular risk factors than other anthropometric indicators in Tehranian adult men.25 Similarly, Gadekar et al found that WHR strongly correlates with visceral fat area,26 making it a valuable tool for assessing health risks associated with visceral obesity. The superiority of this simple anthropometric index over more sophisticated BIA-derived measures in our study, along with consistent evidence from recent research, highlights that body fat distribution rather than overall adiposity is the major determinant of metabolic risk.27 These results further support the clinical usefulness of simple and cost-effective anthropometric measures such as WHR for identifying high-risk individuals, even among those with normal weight. Moreover, modest increases in WHR were associated with higher odds of metabolic unhealthiness, underscoring its potential value as a practical indicator for metabolic risk screening.

In our study, SMM% was identified as a protective factor for metabolically unhealthy status in men. The adjusted OR for SMM% was 0.89 (95% CI 0.83 to 0.95), indicating that each one-unit increase in SMM% was associated with an approximate 11% reduction in the odds of being metabolically unhealthy. Meanwhile, although SMM% served as a protective indicator, SMMI was identified as a risk factor for metabolically unhealthy status in men. This paradoxical finding has been reported in other studies as well. Park et al and Hirasawa et al observed that SMM%, reflecting relative rather than absolute muscle mass, was inversely associated with metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance, while height-adjusted indices such as SMMI or ASMMI were positively related.28 29 Similarly, Xia et al identified SMM% as a protective factor for metabolically unhealthy status in normal-weight adults.1 While the exact reasons can be complex, Kim et al reported that such discrepancies might be due to differences in ethnicity, age-related factors or different methodology and site of measurements. They presumed that SMM % (calculated by dividing skeletal muscle mass by body weight) accounts for fat proportion within muscle mass, thereby minimising its confounding effect when assessing muscle mass. Consequently, whether muscle mass is adjusted for body weight or height may influence the results.30

In this study, the frequency of metabolically unhealthy status in normal-weight individuals was reported as 19.4%. In line with previous studies, we found that this condition was more frequent in men and older adults.31 32

While factors like smoking, education and physical activity have been linked to MUHNW in other studies,32 we found no significant differences for these variables between our metabolic groups. This finding suggests that other factors, not captured by conventional lifestyle metrics, may be more dominant in our population. Additionally, the observation of MUHNW even in paediatric populations further supports the hypothesis of a significant contribution from genetics and early-life environmental exposures.33

This study has some limitations; first, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to determine causality. Second, the energy intake and dietary patterns of the participants, which can influence both metabolic status and body composition, were not available. Third, although age was adjusted for as a covariate in all regression models, the relatively small sample size in older age groups prevented stratified analyses by age. Thus, associations between body composition indices and metabolic status could not be specifically examined in these subgroups. Fourth, the BIA device is not the gold standard for measuring body composition indices, but the results are closely related to DXA, which is known as the gold standard for measuring body composition.34 35 Finally, menstrual cycle phase and menopausal status were not considered, possibly introducing minor variability in women’s BIA measurements.

Besides all the above limitations, this study has some strengths. First, the data used in this study were derived from the TLGS, which is a well-designed longitudinal cohort study conducted in Iran, and the data about the participants have been carefully collected. Second, robust sensitivity analyses confirming result stability. Third, this is one of the few studies that has been conducted to investigate the association between indices of body composition and metabolic status in normal weight adults in non-East Asian populations.

Conclusion

According to the findings of our study, BMI, WHR, WC and body fat indices were positively associated with metabolically unhealthy status in normal-weight individuals of both sexes. In men, SMM% was negatively associated with metabolically unhealthy status. WHR was identified as the strongest independent predictor of metabolically unhealthy status, followed by FMR, which was the highest predictive index among the body composition indices.

A complementary and practical index that provides more accurate information about body fat status could be used alongside BMI to better identify normal-weight individuals with an unhealthy metabolic profile. However, whether body composition indices have a stronger association with metabolic status compared with anthropometric indices like WHR remains a subject of debate.
