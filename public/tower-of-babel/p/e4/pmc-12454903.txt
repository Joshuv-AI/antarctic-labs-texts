New strategies for tumor diagnosis and therapy with ultrasound assisted by nanomaterials☆
Authors: Wei Sang, Haoliang Fan, Yijun Huang, Junfeng Zhang, Weiqi Gao, Chaoran Zhao, Xiaoyao Liu, Linmei Zhao, Yulong Liu, Yunlu Dai, Ruiping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the development of nanomedicine and ultrasound technology, ultrasound-enhanced contrast agents and treatment platforms based on nanomaterials have become important tools for tumor diagnosis and treatment, owing to their high safety profile, low cost and excellent biocompatibility. This review comprehensively discusses the classification and mechanism of nanomaterials in enhancing ultrasound imaging and treatment. It encompasses organic nanomaterials, including nanobubbles and polymer nanoparticles, and as well as inorganic nanomaterials, such as metal nanoparticles and metal oxide nanoparticles. The application strategies and typical examples of combining nanomaterials with ultrasound technology for different tumors are elaborated, including brain tumors, breast cancer, lung cancer, liver cancer and pancreatic cancer. Numerous studies have demonstrated that integrating ultrasound technology with nano-delivery systems can improve drug delivery efficiency and overcome physiological barriers. This integration can also enhance anti-tumor effects by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Despite the significant progress in this field, challenges regarding the biosafety and clinical translation of nanomaterials remain. Future research should concentrate on optimizing the design of nanomaterials and strengthening clinical studies. This will promote the widespread application of ultrasound and nanotechnology in tumor treatment.

Introduction

Cancer, a principal cause of human mortality, primarily originates from the uncontrolled proliferation and malignant spread of tumor cells [1]. Early diagnosis is pivotal for effective cancer treatment. Despite the availability of a variety of clinical imaging techniques, each has significant limitations. For instance, fluorescence imaging is affected by limited tissue penetration and signal dispersion; magnetic resonance imaging is time-consuming; and positron emission tomography-computed tomography entails high costs [2]. Ultrasound, a conventional diagnostic imaging modality, stands out as the most widely used clinical method for disease diagnosis and therapy due to its non-invasive nature, real-time imaging capability, and convenience, when compared to other imaging modalities [3]. It generates images of internal organs by transmitting high-frequency sound waves and converting the reflected echoes into visual representations [4]. By analyzing features such as size, shape, and echogenicity, ultrasound can distinguish cystic from solid lesions, which is a crucial advantage in tumor diagnostics [5]. The emergence of ultrasound contrast agents (UCAs) has further accelerated the application of ultrasound in cancer diagnosis. The primary biophysical mechanism of UCAs is the cavitation effect also known as acoustic cavitation, which involves the oscillation of vapor or gas cavities under acoustic pressure. When the sound pressure surpasses a specific threshold, these microbubbles undergo expansion, collapse, and eventual bursting [6]. Cavitation manifests in two forms: stable cavitation and inertial cavitation [7]. Stable cavitation designates the stable oscillation of MB over time in response to applied acoustic waves and induces microstreaming. At higher acoustic pressures, inertial cavitation occurs. Inertial cavitation is the formation and the collapse of vapor-filled cavities in a liquid or solid due to the rapid changes in localized pressure [8]. Cavities formed by rapidly decreasing pressure will collapse when subjected to higher pressures. Additionally, cavitation initiates microstreaming: stable cavitation produces milder and more consistent microstreaming compared to inertial cavitation. While stable cavitation can lead to localized chemical and biological effects, inertial cavitation imposes a greater mechanical impact, possibly resulting in microjets and shockwaves [9]. In stable cavitation, microbubbles cyclically expand and contract in response to sound pressure variations, generating microstreaming with low shear forces [10]. In contrast, inertial cavitation is characterized by the overexpansion and violent collapse of microbubbles, producing strong shear forces. During ultrasound imaging for tumor detection using microbubble-based contrast agents, stable cavitation predominates. The periodic oscillation of microbubbles without rupture enhances echogenicity within tumor vasculature, thereby facilitating the differentiation between tumor and normal tissue. However, due to their relatively large size, microbubbles are incapable of passing through endothelial gaps smaller than 700 nm, which restricts their effectiveness in extravascular imaging [11].

In recent years, ultrasound has been increasingly developed for therapeutic applications [12]. Among the most extensively studied modalities are focused ultrasound (FUS), sonodynamic therapy (SDT), and ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. FUS leverages the focusing capability and deep penetration of ultrasound to precisely target lesions under imaging guidance. Its energy can be converted into thermal effects, inducing coagulative necrosis of targeted cells. According to the intensity of ultrasound, FUS can be divided into low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU). LIFU is a neuromodulatory technique that is based on mechanical energy to nondestructively and reversibly modulate neuronal activity, with a millimeter-scale spatial resolution, an adjustable depth of focus suitable for targeting deeper brain targets with high spatial precision [17]. HIFU is a minimally-invasive, radiation-free technique that leverages the thermo-mechanical capabilities of ultrasonic waves for the targeted treatment of malignant tumors, benign tumors, and other diseases [18]. Moreover, cavitation-induced microbubble collapse generates localized high temperatures and pressures, selectively ablating pathological tissues while sparing adjacent healthy tissues, thus offering a non-invasive therapeutic approach [19]. Unlike the thermal effect of FUS, SDT employs ultrasound to activate sonosensitizers, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), and converting acoustic energy into chemical energy to induce apoptosis or necrosis in target cells [20]. The use of sonosensitizers endows SDT with higher precision and specificity, enabling it to synergize with other therapeutic modalities to enhance treatment efficacy [2]. UTMD, for its part, combines UCAs with low-frequency ultrasound to induce cavitation of microbubbles within the body. This results in oscillation and rupture, generating mechanical forces such as fluid shear stress, acoustic streaming, and shock waves [21]. Compared to HIFU, UTMD operates at lower frequencies ranging from 20 kHz to 1 MHz, thereby minimizing thermal effects [21]. The introduction of microbubbles increases cavitation nucleus density and lowers the cavitation threshold, enhancing the safety profile of the treatment [22]. In addition, UTMD has significantly improved the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy when used in combination [13].

Thus, ultrasound therapy has garnered widespread attention owing to its non-invasiveness, safety, and potential for targeted drug delivery and gene transfection. Nevertheless, several challenges persist. For instance, FUS is primarily effective for tumors smaller than 2 mm in diameter and demonstrates limited efficacy against larger tumors. SDT alone often yields modest therapeutic outcomes and typically contributes more substantially when used in combination with other treatments [2]. Similarly, due to the relatively large size of microbubbles, UTMD is restricted in targeting and delivering agents to extravascular regions such as the tumor stroma [23]. Furthermore, the gas-filled structure of microbubbles results in poor stability and short circulation time in vivo, which is primarily attributed to the physicochemical properties of the shell materials and the limitations of current preparation methods.

The rapid advancement of nanomedicine has provided novel strategies for cancer detection and therapy, significantly enhancing both imaging contrast and therapeutic efficacy. This improvement is largely attributed to the diverse physical and chemical properties of nanoparticles, including size, shape, porosity, elasticity, stiffness, surface characteristics, composition, and targeting capabilities [24]. A key advantage of using nanobubbles (NBs) for ultrasound imaging lies in their ability to combine the extravascular transport potential of nanoscale drug carriers with the high safety and low cost associated with ultrasound imaging. NBs can circulate at high concentrations within narrow capillaries and, due to their small size, can traverse the vascular endothelium in conditions of vascular leakage, such as inflammation, making them suitable for advanced imaging applications [25]. Moreover, NBs benefit from the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, exhibiting prolonged circulation time and greater in vivo stability. They can also be engineered to specifically target cancer cells or components of the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby serving as a powerful supplement to overcome the limitations of conventional microbubbles. Molecular ultrasound imaging of cancer has been achieved in animal models using NBs decorated with targeting epitopes that bind to overexpressed biomarkers on tumors. For instance, Gao et al. utilized CA-125-targeted NBs for contrast-enhanced harmonic ultrasound imaging in mice bearing OVCAR-3 ovarian tumors, achieving faster tumor accumulation, higher signal intensity, and slower clearance rates [26]. In ultrasound-based therapies, nanoparticles can also act synergistically when combined with modalities such as FUS and SDT, facilitating deep tissue penetration and enhancing acoustic energy delivery to the interior of large tumors. This addresses the limited penetration capacity of FUS for larger tumor masses. Consequently, nanoparticles serve as highly effective adjuncts, playing a pivotal role in advancing ultrasound-assisted cancer diagnosis and therapy.

In this review, we systematically summarize recent advances in the application of ultrasound-responsive nanomaterials for tumor diagnosis and explore their potential and challenges as a next generation of ultrasound contrast agents. First, we classify and analyze various types of nanomaterials, such as organic-based and inorganic-based nanomaterials, focusing on their acoustic properties, imaging contrast enhancement capabilities, and biocompatibility. Second, we emphasize tumor-specific diagnostic and therapeutic strategies by discussing the advantages of different nanomaterials combined with ultrasound in various cancer types, including brain cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and pancreatic cancer. Finally, we highlight the key challenges currently facing the clinical translation of ultrasound nanomaterials and provide perspectives on their future development in tumor molecular imaging and personalized diagnosis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1This schematic illustrates the applications of ultrasound in cancer diagnosis and therapy. Classification of nanomaterials for enhanced ultrasound

Materials for ultrasound diagnosis and therapy must possess specific characteristics. In terms of acoustic properties, they need to enhance acoustic contrast by modifying local acoustic impedance to improve imaging quality. They should also maintain stable acoustic responses in ultrasound fields and exhibit adjustable acoustic properties to meet diverse application requirements. For biocompatibility, these materials must have minimal toxicity and be safely metabolized or excreted without disrupting normal physiological functions like blood flow and cell metabolism. Functionally, they must possess targeting ability to specifically identify and bind to tumor cells or tumor microenvironment (TME) components. They must also effectively load and precisely release drugs under ultrasound triggers for precise treatment [11]. Multifunctionality is crucial as it allows materials to integrate multiple functions such as diagnosis, therapy, and imaging guidance, achieving theranostics [27]. In terms of stability, they should be chemically and physically stable to ensure consistent performance during preparation, storage, and use. These characteristics collectively guarantee the effectiveness and safety of materials in ultrasound applications (Fig. 2). The materials that meet these criteria include organic nanomaterials such as NBs [28] and polymer nanoparticles (PNPs) [29], inorganic nanomaterials such as metal and metal oxide nanomaterials [30], and composite nanomaterials [31].Fig. 2This figure shows the key characteristics of ultrasound diagnostic and therapeutic materials, including acoustic properties, functionality, biocompatibility and stability.

Organic nanomaterials

Organic conjugated polymer nanomaterials, such as NBs and PNPs, demonstrate enhanced photostability and higher photothermal conversion efficiency after appropriate surface modification [11]. These enhanced properties are particularly evident when these nanomaterials are used in conjunction with ultrasound, thereby markedly enhancing imaging contrast.

Nanobubbles

NBs comprise an intact gas core and various shell nanomaterials. The gas core is typically composed of medical gases like oxygen, hydrogen peroxide, or perfluorinated compounds (e.g., C3F8 or SF6). When exposed to ultrasound, NBs undergo cycles of compression and expansion, resulting in backscattering, cavitation, and shell rupture [32]. This creates strong scattering and reflection, enhancing ultrasound imaging contrast and improving tumor detection sensitivity. This small feature allows NBs to enter areas that traditional therapies cannot reach, selectively target organs and tissues, and improve drug delivery efficiency [33]. NBs can increase ultrasound echo intensity, imaging sensitivity, and resolution. They can penetrate small capillaries, demonstrating strong stability and a longer half-life in the bloodstream. The ultrasound-induced cavitation temporarily creates tiny gaps between tumor vascular endothelial cells, increasing vascular permeability. This facilitates the transfer of drug-loaded NBs (DTX-R837@NBs) from blood vessels into tumor tissues, raising drug concentrations at the tumor site. In ultrasound-guided cancer therapy, targeted NBs excel in improving imaging contrast and therapeutic outcomes [[34], [35], [36]].

Liu et al. prepared DTX-R837@NBs via thin film hydration; these NBs encapsulated the chemotherapeutic agent docetaxel and the immunoadjuvant R837. By using targeted ultrasound to disrupt the NBs and promote drug accumulation in tumor tissues, DTX-R837@NBs has exhibited exceptional contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) imaging capabilities, enabling effective integration of diagnosis and treatment [37].

The encapsulated gas shell is made of materials with a high acoustic response, facilitating the generation of cavitation effects under ultrasonic stimulation. The shell materials are diverse, including lipids, proteins, polymers, and others, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified phospholipids [28,38]. These materials enhance the stability and biocompatibility of the NBs and allow their surfaces to be functionalized with targeting ligands, enhancing their specificity for tumor tissues [39]. Under ultrasound guidance, after crossing the endothelial barrier and entering the TME, the NBs can accurately deliver drugs to extracellular cancer cells. Their stability, biocompatibility, and targeting ability enable precise drug delivery and effective targeting of NBs in the body in real time, thereby facilitating real-time monitoring. Polymer nanomaterials

PNPs are predominantly formed via the spontaneous self-assembly of polymers, with sizes typically ranging from 1 to 1000 nm. The self-assembly of polymers, either on their own or under the direction of surfactants, gives rise to PNPs. Conventional PNPs include normal micelles and inverse micelles [41]. A normal micelle is characterized by a hydrophobic/oil core and a hydrophilic/water shell, whereas an inverse micelle possesses a hydrophilic/water core and a hydrophobic/oil shell. In addition to these normal and inverse structures, the self-assembly nanostructures of PNPs also encompass a variety of forms such as spherical, tubular, bottle-brush, rod-shaped, etc., among others.

The combination of PNPs with ultrasound can effectively enhance image clarity and extend imaging time. PNPs encapsulate substances to improve imaging performance. Gold nanoshells (AuNPs) and gadolinium-based nanoparticles (GdNPs) are employed to enhance cancer diagnosis imaging. Al Zaki et al. designed and optimized polymeric micelles (AuMs) where 1.9-nm-sized AuNPs were encapsulated within the hydrophobic core of micelles constructed from amphiphilic copolymer PEG-Poly (PEG-PCL) [42]. Ivan Zlotver and Alejandro Sosnik developed glucosylated hybrid TiO2/polymer nanomaterials for targeted sonodynamic cancer therapy. Their research shows these nanomaterials’ size and surface features can be precisely controlled, enhancing cancer treatment potential. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that these nanoparticles increase tumor accumulation and improve sonodynamic efficiency. Additionally, combining these PNPs with ultrasound shows promise for improving medical imaging [43].

When PNPs are subjected to ultrasound irradiation, they generate cavitation nuclei, which can enhance ultrasound energy and effectively destroy tumors. Polyethylenimine (PEI)-modified poly (lactic-co-glycolic) acid nanoparticles loaded with sodium bicarbonate (PEI-PLGA-NaHCO3-NPs) possess a positive surface potential [44]. Injected via the tail vein into 4T1 breast cancer-bearing BALB/c mice, these nanoparticles demonstrated tumor-homing ability through electrostatic adsorption with B. bifidum-colonized solid tumors (Fig. 4A). In the acidic microenvironment of solid tumors, PEI-PLGA-NaHCO3-NPs release NaHCO3 to produce CO2, acting as cavitation nuclei. Inorganic nanomaterials

In contrast to organic nanoparticles, inorganic nanoparticles exhibit superior stability and a broad spectrum of physicochemical, mechanical, magnetic and optical properties. These properties enable them to be readily modified with ligands to enhance their targeting capabilities. This versatility renders inorganic nanoparticles highly attractive for applications in bioimaging and drug delivery [46]. They also offer unique physicochemical functions that enhance the efficacy of ultrasound-based therapeutic diagnostics.

Metal nanomaterials

Ultrasound imaging can be enhanced through the use of metal nanomaterials. The stabilization of AuNPs with polymers is often employed in biomedical applications to improve imaging outcomes. Recent studies have demonstrated the effective use of Raman reporter molecules attached to the surface of polymer nanocomposites containing AuNPs. These composites have shown remarkable surface-enhanced Raman scattering enhancement and increased sensitivity [46]. Zhang et al. investigated Pt nanocrystals that can produce cavitation effects under ultrasound irradiation, thereby facilitating imaging [47].

Metal nanomaterials, when combined with ultrasound, can achieve targeting imaging. The biocompatibility and ease of functionalisation of AuNPs make them highly effective for the targeted imaging of cancer cells [48]. Furthermore, the combination of metal oxide nanomaterials with ultrasound can enhance imaging capabilities and enable real-time monitoring. Titanium dioxide, for example, can be used for both ultrasound imaging and SDT, achieving a dual therapeutic effect [47]. Magnetic nanoparticles, commonly used in biomedical applications, typically consist of an inorganic nanoparticle core coated with a suitable material [48]. This allows for real-time monitoring of the targeted area.

Composite nanomaterials

Composite nanomaterials usually refer to materials composed of two or more different types of materials combined at the nanoscale. These materials can include various combinations of inorganic substances, organic substances, metals, polymers, and more. Some composite nanomaterials can be used as contrast and imaging agents for cell tracking. Guo and colleagues explored the potential of polypyrrole-coated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (PPy@MWCNTs) as a novel sonosensitizer for melanoma treatment [47]. Their study demonstrated that PPy@MWCNTs can effectively generate heat and ROS under ultrasound irradiation, leading to significant cytotoxicity in melanoma cells in vitro and substantial tumor volume reduction in vivo. The research highlights the efficacy of combining ultrasound with PPy@MWCNTs to enhance ROS generation and thermal effects, offering a promising approach for melanoma therapy.

The specificity carried by a wide variety of nanomaterials combined with ultrasound offers a wide range of possibilities for cancer treatment. Nanomaterials offer new opportunities for cancer diagnosis and treatment. Multifunctional nanoparticles harboring various functions including targeting, imaging and therapy, have been intensively studied to overcome the limitations associated with conventional cancer diagnosis and treatment [49]. Notably, the integration of targeted strategies and multimodal imaging technology provides a strong impetus for advancing theranostics integration.

Synergistic strategies of nanomaterials with ultrasound for cancer diagnosis

The principle of ultrasound imaging is that high-frequency sound waves (>20 kHz) penetrate soft tissues and fluids while reflecting off dense structures (e.g., bone), thereby facilitating real-time anatomical visualization [50]. The main limitations of traditional ultrasound microbubble contrast agents are the large size limiting vascular penetration and the short circulation time due to instability [51]. In contrast, key advances in nanomaterial design comprise three critical dimensions: (1) Size optimization, which yields NBs (100–500 nm) capable of extravascular imaging [52,53]; (2) Significantly enhanced stability, demonstrated by perfluorocarbon-core NBs achieving circulation half-lives of 2–6 h—a substantial improvement over the minute-scale persistence of conventional microbubbles—while echogenic exosomes exhibit a 300 % increase in ultrasound brightness intensity at 5 mg/mL [54]; and (3) Inherent multifunctionality enabling integrated theranostic platforms that concurrently support diagnostic imaging and therapeutic modalities such as SDT [55].

Recent advances in targeted imaging leverage distinct nanoparticle designs to enhance tumor specificity. Comparative studies [56] reveal that phospholipid NBs (100–500 nm) utilize the EPR effect for superior tumor penetration, whereas larger commercial microbubbles (1–4 μm) provide lower tissue penetration. Furthermore, active targeting strategies demonstrate significant potential: folate-conjugated NBs (287 nm) achieve precise tumor localization in MCF-7 breast cancer models through specific receptor binding. This active targeting mechanism yields enhanced contrast under 9 MHz ultrasound imaging, confirming ligand-directed accumulation at tumor sites [57].

Advanced nano-contrast agents significantly enhance ultrasound echo signals by exploiting intrinsic acoustic impedance mismatches between their gas-filled cores and surrounding tissues, enabling precise tumor visualization. This principle is exemplified in innovative designs such as nanoparticles co-loaded with perfluorohexane (PFH) and AuNPs. Upon exposure to ultrasound or photothermal stimulation, the PFH core undergoes liquid-to-gas phase transition, generating intense echo signals. In vitro and in vivo evaluations confirm that these engineered agents achieve ultrasound contrast efficacy comparable to commercial agents (e.g., SonoVue®), providing clear visualization of tumor vascular perfusion. Further demonstrating versatility, biliverdin nanoparticles (100 nm) generate strong photoacoustic (PA) signals in lymph nodes, facilitating deep-tissue imaging [58]. Therefore, nano-contrast agents have overcome the limitations of traditional ultrasound, which is restricted to relatively superficial anatomical structures for diagnosis.

Nanoparticles serve as multifunctional carriers, which not only enhance single-modality ultrasound imaging, but also advance ultrasound-multimodal fusion. Iodine-loaded nanodroplets (60 nm) facilitate dual-modal US/CT imaging in hepatocellular carcinoma, significantly improving spatial resolution for tumor delineation [59]. Silica-gold core–shell nanoparticles (100 nm) serve as dual US/PA contrast agents, with performance validated in ex vivo models [60]. A more complex system—Au-SiO2 hybrid nanoparticles—demonstrates triple-modality imaging (US/CT/MR) in VX2 tumor models, exhibiting a 2.7-fold increase in ultrasound signal intensity compared to controls [61]. Collectively, these platforms establish nanoparticle design as a critical enabler of precision multimodal imaging.

Overall, combining ultrasound technology with nanomaterials holds significant promise for improving diagnostic accuracy. Nanomaterials significantly improve the performance of ultrasound imaging through size optimization, stability enhancement, and integration of versatility. Recent advances in targeted imaging and multimodal imaging have further expanded the application scope of ultrasound technology, providing new tools and methods for accurate diagnosis of cancer.

Synergistic strategies of nanomaterials with ultrasound for cancer therapy

Brain carcinoma

The treatment of central nervous system tumors, including primary and metastatic brain tumors, faces significant challenges due to the physiological barriers posed by the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and the blood-tumor barrier (BTB), which restrict the efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs [62]. The BBB, characterized by tight junctions and active efflux mechanisms, and the BTB, with its abnormal vasculature, both impede the penetration of chemotherapeutic agents. Currently, the combination of ultrasound with nanocarriers presents a novel therapeutic strategy. By adjusting ultrasound parameters to induce cavitation or thermal effects, ultrasound can transiently and reversibly open the BBB and BTB, creating a localized delivery window. Subsequently, nanocarriers can utilize passive or active targeting to enable efficient drug delivery and controlled release. Passive targeting takes advantage of the EPR effect, whereas active targeting employs ligand-receptor binding technique. This multi-modal strategy, which synergistically combines physical and biological approaches, is revolutionizing the treatment of brain tumors, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity (Fig. 5) [63].Fig. 5The key characteristics of ultrasound diagnostic and therapeutic materials in brain carcinoma, including BBB penetration, targeted delivery and tumor destruction.

Wu et al. introduced pulsed wave low-dose ultrasound hyperthermia (pUH) technology using 500 kHz-frequency ultrasound waves with 2.2 W acoustic power, a 50 % duty cycle, and a 1000 Hz pulse repetition frequency applied for 10 min [65]. This low-dose regimen is characterized by controlled thermal exposure, with a focal temperature of approximately 43 °C and a cumulative equivalent thermal dose of less than 2 min at 43 °C. In contrast, BBB disruption typically requires a focal temperature of approximately 48 °C and a cumulative equivalent thermal dose of less than 12.8 min at 43 °C. Through the dual effects of pulsed radiation pressure gradient and mild hyperthermia, pUH enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD) on brain metastases of breast cancer. Studies have demonstrated that pUH increases PLD concentration in tumor tissues by 1.7-fold while avoiding drug accumulation in normal brain tissues. Furthermore, it extends the median survival to 22 days compared to 11 days in the control group. This technology provides a novel strategy to overcome the BBB and achieve targeted therapy with improved efficacy and safety.

Breast carcinoma

Breast cancer’s heterogeneity, chemoresistance, and immunosuppressive TME complicate diagnosis and treatment [66]. Targeted ultrasound contrast agents and responsive drug activation are used in ultrasound imaging and therapy, offering precision [67]. Recent progress in imaging technology has enabled the development of ultrasound-responsive nano-platform that integrates multimodal diagnosis and treatment. Addressing the problem of chemotherapy-induced senescence escape, ultrasound-targeted controlled-release technology provides a novel paradigm for reversing drug resistance. Doxorubicin, a cornerstone in breast cancer therapy, often fails due to chemotherapy-induced senescence, a paradoxical state that enables tumor cells to evade apoptosis, promote immunosuppression, and drive relapse. Conventional strategies inadequately target senescent cells and may cause systemic toxicity. Guo et al. engineered DCR2-targeted ultrasound NBs (DCR2-FITC@Ver-NBs) to combat doxorubicin-induced senescence, a key driver of chemoresistance [70]. Ultrasound imaging guided the precise delivery of senolytic agent verteporfin to senescence-rich regions (Fig. 11A). Low-intensity ultrasound at 1.0 MHz triggered the release of verteporfin, suppressing senescence markers, including p16 and p21, and restoring Lamin B1 (Fig. 11B). In vivo, DCR2-FITC@Ver-NBs reduced tumor growth by 60 % (Fig. 11C), inhibited M2 macrophage polarization via iASPP/Nrf2/M−CSF axis (Fig. 11D), and demonstrated biosafety. Recent research has shown that the combination of ultrasound and nanotechnology can effectively overcome physiological barriers, enhance tumor imaging contrast, and strategically modulate the immune microenvironment to enhance antitumor efficacy. Wu et al. developed a novel therapeutic platform that integrates UTMD with programmed death ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) antibodies [71]. The ultrasound-induced cavitation effect offers dual therapeutic benefits: it physically disrupts tumor neovasculature to improve drug delivery efficiency while simultaneously upregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 and TNF-α detection. Experimental findings indicate that ultrasound-mediated mechanical stimulation promotes dendritic cell maturation in draining lymph nodes and tumor tissues, and significantly enhances CD8+ T infiltration. This lays a solid foundation for improving the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Ultrasound also plays a crucial role in immune modulation, particularly in sonodynamic immunotherapy. For example, the ultrasound-triggered activation of hematoporphyrin monomethyl ether/imiquimod loaded liposome‌ (HMME/R837@Lip) nanoparticles has been shown to induce the in situ release of tumor antigens, thereby triggering a systemic immune response [72]. In 4T1 breast cancer models, ultrasound intervention significantly increased the population of CD8+ T cells within the tumor while raising serum levels of IL-6 and TNF-α. Importantly, the spatiotemporal controllability of ultrasound not only inhibits the progression of primary tumors but also establishes durable immune memory to monitor and prevent distant metastases.

The mechanical regulatory potential of ultrasound further propels the reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages. Kong et al. utilized piezoelectric β-polyvinylidene fluoride to illustrate that mechanical stress generated by ultrasound activates the Ca2+-CAMK2A-NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 12) [73]. Ultrasound treatment significantly redirected tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) polarization towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, resulting in enhanced secretion of antitumor cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β. This ultimately contributed to prolonged survival in combination therapy cohorts. Collectively, these studies reveal the multifaceted immunomodulatory capabilities of ultrasound, encompassing cavitation effects, mechanical stimulation, and sonodynamic activation. These findings establish a transformative paradigm for overcoming therapeutic challenges in breast cancer treatment.Fig. 12Schematic illustration of the piezoelectric polarization process. Lung carcinoma

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is characterized by an immunosuppressive TME, imprecise drug delivery, and challenges in real-time monitoring [74]. These factors lead to treatment resistance and high recurrence rates. Ultrasound technology, which integrates diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, can enhance NSCLC therapy. It achieves this by guiding the localization of nanoparticles through contrast-enhanced imaging and triggering on-demand drug release and immune reprogramming via acoustic cavitation and sonoporation [75].

To tackle the immunosuppressive nature of the TME through the reprogramming of TAMs, Li et al. developed folate-modified ultrasound-responsive NBs (FA-PFNB-SIRPα siRNA) encapsulating Fe3O4 nanoparticles and SIRPα siRNA. These NBs are designed to reprogram TAMs, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy for NSCLC (Fig. 13A) [74]. LIFU-triggered acoustic droplet vaporization of perfluoropentane-loaded NBs generated microbubbles that enhanced CEUS imaging and promoted sonoporation-mediated cellular uptake (Fig. 13B). Under optimized LIFU parameters (1 W/cm2, 2 min), FA-PFNB-SIRPα siRNA achieved more than 95 % macrophage transfection efficiency, silenced SIRPα by more than 80 %, and reversed M2-to-M1 macrophage polarization. Ultrasound synergistically amplified macrophage phagocytosis of NSCLC cells by 45 % and enhanced intratumoral accumulation of nanoparticles, as validated by fluorescence and photoacoustic imaging (Fig. 13C, D). In vivo, LIFU-guided FA-PFNB-SIRPα siRNA prolonged survival and remodeled the immunosuppressive microenvironment through increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and reduced Treg infiltration. Dynamic CEUS confirmed ultrasound-triggered adenovirus (ADV)-mediated drug release, while transcriptomics revealed upregulated immune activation pathways. To combat post-therapeutic recurrence in NSCLC, Lv et al. engineered ultrasound-visualized gold-based nanocarriers (PGMP-siRNA NPs) that integrate STAT6 siRNA delivery with photothermal therapy (PTT) to suppress M2-like TAM polarization and enhance NSCLC treatment [76]. These nanocarriers feature a PLGA/PFP core encapsulated with gold nanoshells, enabling CEUS imaging for real-time tumor monitoring. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, PGMP-siRNA NPs achieve rapid photothermal conversion, while the phase transition of PFP under hyperthermia generates microbubbles that amplify CEUS signals. Ultrasound-guided delivery facilitated tumor accumulation via the EPR effect, with CEUS imaging confirming enhanced intratumoral contrast. Notably, STAT6 siRNA delivered by PGMP NPs silenced STAT6 mRNA in macrophages, inhibiting M2-like TAM polarization and downstream metastasis-promoting factors. In vivo, the dual-modality therapy combining PGMP-STAT6 siRNA and PTT achieves complete tumor ablation with minimal recurrence, as validated by suppressed CD206 expression in immunofluorescence. CEUS imaging further tracks therapeutic efficacy, correlating with reduced tumor volume. This study highlights the dual role of ultrasound in imaging-guided drug delivery and therapeutic monitoring, offering a synergistic approach to overcome NSCLC recurrence following PTT.

Hepatic carcinoma (HCC)

HCC confronts significant therapeutic challenges due to cancer stem cell-driven recurrence and immune evasion, particularly through the co-expression of PD-L1 and CD133 [77]. To overcome these limitations, ultrasound-based strategies have emerged as a promising approach, enabling precise tumor ablation, targeted drug delivery, and immune modulation [78]. Ultrasound not only provides real-time imaging but also serves as a therapeutic tool to enhance treatment efficacy and reduce systemic toxicity.

Shen et al. developed a neutrophil-mediated nanodrug delivery system (PLD@NEs) combined with HIFU ablation to suppress residual tumor recurrence [79]. The HIFU-induced thermal ablation generated localized inflammation (Fig. 14A), which recruited PLD@NEs to target residual tumors via chemokine gradients, as validated by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Fig. 14B, C). CEUS imaging confirmed the efficacy of HIFU, revealing perfusion defects post-ablation (Fig. 14D), while B-mode ultrasound guided precise tumor targeting. In vivo fluorescence imaging demonstrated rapid accumulation of PLD@NEs at HIFU-treated tumors within 4 h, leveraging neutrophil chemotaxis amplified by ultrasound-triggered inflammation. The combination of HIFU and PLD@NEs synergistically enhanced tumor suppression, reducing recurrence through localized drug release via neutrophil extracellular traps and inducing apoptosis (Fig. 14E). Importantly, ultrasound-mediated strategies minimized systemic toxicity, as evidenced by stable body weights and serum biomarkers. While Shen et al.’s HIFU strategy focuses on leveraging post-ablation inflammation to recruit drug carriers, Zhang et al.’s UTMD approach directly targets CSCs and immune checkpoints through functionalized NBs. Both methods utilize ultrasound for imaging guidance but differ in their underlying mechanisms. HIFU induces localized inflammation to prime the TME, facilitating the recruitment of neutrophil-mediated drug delivery systems. In contrast, UTMD enables precise spatiotemporal release of therapeutic agents directly at the tumor site. These innovations underscore the dual diagnostic and therapeutic potential of ultrasound in overcoming the challenges posed by HCC heterogeneity and resistance.

Pancreatic carcinoma

Pancreatic cancer is aggressive and often detected late, hindered by dense stroma and an immunosuppressive microenvironment [80]. Ultrasound provides a noninvasive solution by targeting the tumor accurately and boosting therapy locally. It triggers drug release from responsive nanocarriers and activates antitumor immunity through mechanical or thermal effects [81].

Kim et al. developed the ultrasound-sensitive liposome IMP301 to overcome the limited efficacy of gemcitabine and liposomal doxorubicin in pancreatic cancer, showing that FUS induces lipid membrane pore formation and releases the payload at the tumor site [82]. In pancreatic xenograft models, IMP301 plus FUS at 4 mg/kg and higher doses produced marked and sustained tumor suppression, outperforming monotherapy or Doxil plus FUS, without causing weight loss or systemic toxicity. Mechanistically, FUS triggers localized drug release from IMP301 and increases cell permeability through sonoporation, enhancing drug penetration; this combination of ultrasound-triggered release and mechanical augmentation offers a safe, image-guided strategy for precise therapy of deep tumors.

Chen et al. utilized titanium diselenide (TiSe2) nanosheets as a sonosensitizer for ultrasound-triggered SDT combined with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in hypoxic pancreatic cancer (Fig. 16A) [83]. At 1.0 MHz and 0.5 W/cm2, TiSe2 produced strong ROS under both normoxia and hypoxia, provoking immunogenic cell death shown by calreticulin exposure, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release, and ATP secretion (Fig. 16B, C). The resulting SDT matured dendritic cells and raised systemic cytokines, while dynamic ultrasound imaging verified TiSe2 accumulation in tumors. SDT plus anti-PD-1 shrank primary and distant tumors and curbed lung metastasis through increased CD8+ T cells and fewer Tregs, underscoring ultrasound’s dual role in ROS cytotoxicity and immune activation for precise pancreatic cancer therapy (Fig. 16D, E). Conclusions and perspectives

Ultrasound provides radiation-free, real-time, high-resolution imaging that can swiftly shift from observation to intervention [84]. By precisely tuning acoustic parameters, it induces controlled cavitation, localized heating, or mechanical stress to trigger on-demand drug release, transiently permeabilize cell membranes, and initiate immunogenic signaling within a single experimental session.

Nanomaterials amplify these capabilities through precise engineering of size, composition, and surface chemistry. Liposomes, polymeric vesicles, and metal or carbon-based nanoparticles can co-load contrast agents, chemotherapeutics, genes, or immunomodulators, circulate longer, and actively or passively accumulate in tumors [33]. Their sharp responsiveness to acoustic energy confines drug release, oxygen generation, or immune activation to the ultrasound focal zone, transforming conventional imaging into a powerful theranostic platform.

In this review we have summarized three persistent challenges because dense tumor pressure and disordered vasculature keep most nanoparticles at the periphery while rapid blood clearance further reduces intratumoral accumulation and excessive acoustic pressure may inadvertently harm healthy tissue. The long-term biosafety of nanomaterials should be assessed in detail before clinical implementation because most nondegradable nanomaterials persist and accumulate in the body for a long time, causing chronic inflammatory reactions [85].

(1) To address these challenges, researchers can develop carriers that swell or shrink in response to the microenvironment and apply matrix-degrading coatings that enzymatically remodel the extracellular matrix, while artificial intelligence steers each ultrasound pulse in real time to ensure acoustic energy converges precisely where it is needed. When these systems are paired with particles that naturally biodegrade once their mission is complete, deeper penetration, stronger therapeutic efficacy, and built-in safety that spares normal tissue can be achieved concurrently.

(2) Limited information is available about the toxicity of nanoparticles and their potential harm to human health. Under clinical requirements, nanoparticles need excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability. When the toxicity of nanomaterials reaches a controllable range, or when nanomaterials are combined with other materials, they will achieve enhanced clinical applicability.

(3) It cannot be ignored that nanomaterial-based therapeutic technology is still in its early stages. Further research on the potential and limitations of nanomaterials in the field of ultrasound should be the focus of future research. Future research can focus on the performance characteristics of various intracellular nanomaterials and promote the clinical implementation of therapeutic strategies combining nanomaterials with ultrasound. In that case, the use of dogs and monkeys, large animals with longer lifespans and growth environments more similar to humans than those of mice—will be more informative for evaluating nanoplatform studies and help accelerate clinical translation.
