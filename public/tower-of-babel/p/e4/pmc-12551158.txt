Identification of exosome-related gene biomarkers for parkinson’s disease: a multi-omics approach for early diagnosis and therapeutic targeting
Authors: Sihan Chen, Nan Feng, Yu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder linked to alpha-synuclein pathology and dopaminergic neuron loss. Exosomes play dual roles in PD progression, facilitating pathological protein spread or exerting neuroprotective effects. This study aimed to identify exosome-related gene biomarkers for PD diagnosis and therapy. Using multi-dataset analysis (Series: GSE42966, Platforms: GPL4133, Series: GSE99039, Platforms: GPL570, Series: GSE156926, Platforms: GPL19920) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), we integrated principal component analysis (PCA), differential gene screening (|logFC|>0.5, p < 0.05), and machine learning to identify PD-associated exosome-related genes. Functional enrichment revealed associations with oxidative phosphorylation, Huntington’s disease pathways, and immune responses. A predictive model comprising five genes—GNAS, TUBB2A, RPL22, RPL5, and WNT5A—was established and validated using ROC curves (Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) and nomograms. Immune profiling linked these genes to B cells, MDSCs, and CD4+/CD8 + T cells. Drug-gene network analysis highlighted interactions with compounds like NSC94017 and phosphine, while molecular docking identified key binding residues (e.g., GLN294, ASP295). These genes may serve as early diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Despite promising results, further validation in larger cohorts and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm their roles in PD pathogenesis and treatment. This study provides a foundation for developing precision gene therapies and non-invasive diagnostic strategies for PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04477-x.Keywords: Exosomes, Parkinson's disease, GEO, GSEA, KEGG

Introduction

PD is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by alpha-synuclein mislocalization and the loss of dopaminergic neurons [1]. Exosomes, nanoscale membrane vesicles secreted by cells, play a crucial role in cell communication by transporting proteins, nucleic acids, and other biomolecules [2]. Recent studies indicate that exosomes are closely related to PD pathological mechanisms: exosomes released by neurons or glial cells may contribute to the trans-cellular spread of alpha-synuclein, promoting pathological protein aggregation, exacerbating neuroinflammation, and causing neuronal damage [3–5]. Hou BR et al. showed that exosomes are important carriers of cell-to-cell communication. Microglia and neural stem cell-derived exosomes can mediate inflammatory injury and exert neuroprotective effects [6]. Zhanchi Zhu et al. said that Angiopep-2 and transactivator -modified well-defined small extracellular capsules with dual-targeted drug delivery and cell penetration functions can provide efficient and specific chemotherapy for gliomas [7]. Phosphorylated α-synuclein and other specific biomarkers have been identified in exosomes derived from the blood or cerebrospinal fluid of Parkinson’s disease patients, offering novel strategies for early diagnosis [3–5, 8]. Research has also explored the potential of engineered exosomes as drug carriers, such as for delivering neuroprotective factors or gene therapy agents, paving new avenues for PD treatment [5]. The dual role of exosomes makes them a significant focus for PD research, although their specific regulatory mechanisms require further exploration.

Research into PD-related exosome genes has opened new directions for diagnosis and treatment. Diagnostically, specific genes (such as the alpha-synuclein gene (SNCA) and LRRK2 mutation genes) carried by exosomes can serve as early biomarkers for minimally invasive screening through blood or cerebrospinal fluid to improve diagnostic sensitivity. Therapeutically, engineered exosomes can deliver gene-regulating tools (such as siRNA to silence mutated genes) or neurotrophic factors like GDNF (glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor). Additionally, the natural ability of exosomes to cross the blood-brain barrier makes them ideal drug delivery vehicles, paving new paths for developing precise and efficient PD gene therapies. This study aims to identify exosome-related genes co-expressed across multiple body regions that are associated with the occurrence and progression of Parkinson’s disease (PD). We searched the GEO database and selected three datasets—GSE42966 (substantia nigra), GSE99039 (blood), and GSE156926 (cortex)—each containing samples with corresponding control groups. The clinical information of these datasets is summarized in Table 1 [9]. Using correlation model analysis and molecular docking, we constructed a predictive model based on five exosome-related genes: GNAS, TUBB2A, RPL22, RPL5 and WNT5A [10–13]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that mining strongly PD-associated exosomal genes may serve as potential targets, thereby aiding in the early diagnosis and treatment of PD.

​Methods

Data acquisition

Gene expression data and clinical data for PD were downloaded from the GEO database. After conditional screening, we selected three datasets: GSE42966, GSE99039, and GSE156926 [9]. These datasets contain a large number of cases and rich gene chip data, making them highly suitable for this study. A total of 278 PD samples and 257 normal control samples were pooled across the three datasets, encompassing 2210 gene expression profiles [9].

Computational analysis tools

The analysis was conducted using R language (version: 4.4.1) [14, 15].

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis between PD samples and healthy controls was performed on the combined, batch-corrected dataset using the limma package (Version: 3.64.3). |logFC|≠0, adj.P.Val < 0.05 [16].

PCA (principal component analysis) analysis

PCA (Version: 2.20.0) analysis was performed to identify common genes across the datasets and visualize their distributions. PCA helps screen for genes expressed in all three datasets [17].

Variance analysis

Differential genes were screened using the criteria: |logFC| >0.5; adj.P.Val < 0.05. Results were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps [18, 19].

Exosome-related gene collection

Exosome-related genes were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) [20].

​Genetic screening criteria

Differential genes meeting the criteria |logFC| >0.5 and p-value < 0.05 were identified. PCA analysis and Venn diagram analysis were used to screen for exosome-related genes associated with PD.

​Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analyses, including GO (Gene Ontology), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), and GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis), were performed to determine the biological functions of the target genes [21–24].

​Logistic regression screening

Logistic regression analysis was used to screen out exosome genes closely related to PD. Filter: P < 0.05. OR ≠ 0. The results of logistic analysis were further performed for LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression analysis (10-fold cross-validation, alpha = 1). Further screening is associated with PD [25].

Machine learning validation of disease signature genes

Support Vector Machine (SVM) was used for cross-validation to obtain genes related to PD [26].

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis

ROC curves were used to evaluate the accuracy of the prediction model (95%CI) [27].

​Nomogram construction

Nomograms were used to assess the accuracy of the predictive model [28].

​Immune cell infiltration profiling

Immune cell infiltration analysis was performed using immune cell scoring methods to identify immune cells strongly associated with the genes of core (assessed using ssGSEA) [29].

Network pharmacology-based drug-gene interaction analysis

Analysis of the relationship between drugs and genes.

The relationship between drugs and genes was analyzed using the DSigDB database(FDR < 0.05)(https://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) [30].

Molecular docking simulation

Molecular structure and drug structure were downloaded using PubChem and RCSB Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB) databases, and then molecular docking was performed using CB-Dock2 [31–33]. For the molecular docking study, molecular docking analysis was performed between NSC94017 (PubChem ID: CID_6076) and GNAS (PDB DOI: 10.2210/pdb7e5e/pdb) [34, 35].

​Protein-protein interaction network analysis

The STRING database (version 12.0) was used to identify genes exhibiting comprehensive interaction profiles with the target genes and to generate protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks based on specific gene sets [36].

Calculation details

The detailed analytical files for each section have been uploaded to the supplementary materials.

Results and discussion

PD-related data, including gene expression and clinical data (GSE42966, GSE99039, and GSE156926), were downloaded from the GEO database. PCA analysis of common genes across these datasets was performed (Fig. 1). Screening results: A total of ​535 research samples​ and ​2210 co-expressed genes​ were identified. Exosome-related genes were collected through relevant literature and databases. Differentially expressed genes are displayed as volcano plots and heatmaps. Red indicates highly expressed genes, and blue indicates lowly expressed genes (77 differentially expressed genes were identified) (Fig. 2A, B). Exosome-related genes were collected through relevant literature and databases [37–43]. By utilizing a Venn diagram to identify overlapping genes between differentially expressed genes and endocrine-related genes, we discovered that ​8 exosome-related genes​ (GNAS, TUBB2A, GLIPR2, PRDX2, EEF2, RPL22, RPL5 and WNT5A) overlapped as co-expressed genes. The biological functions of 8 exosome-related genes were further analyzed, which are mainly enriched in steroid hormone response, cysteine and methionine metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis pathways. (Fig. 3A, B, C, D). The detailed data for the relevant enrichment files can be found in the supplementary materials. Logistic analysis explored the correlation between these exosome-related genes and PD. The results were further analyzed using Lasso regression analysis and SVM machine learning. Lasso regression identified 5 strongly correlated genes: GNAS, TUBB2A, WNT5A, RPL5, and RPL22. (Fig. 3E, F). Through SVM, we found that in the cross-validation, the highest accuracy and the smallest error value were 5 genes (Fig. 4). Therefore, we also verified the disease signatures using random forest (RF) analysis. Venn analysis of genes identified by SVM, LASSO regression, and RF confirmed the five core genes: GNAS, TUBB2A, RPL22, RPL5, and WNT5A. Clinical analysis indicated that GNAS, TUBB2A, RPL22, RPL5, and WNT5A could form a clinical prediction model (Fig. 5). Both nomogram and ROC analysis demonstrated the accuracy of this clinical model. (Fig. 6A, B). Their biological functions are more strongly related to drugs such as velcade and etodolac. Drug regulatory network analysis for these five genes showed they are primarily associated with NSC94017, phosphine, and 3,3’,4,4’,5-Pentachlorobiphenyl, among others. Immune cell analysis revealed that the model genes showed strong correlations with immune cells such as B cells, MDSCs, CD4+, and CD8 + T cells (Fig. 7A, B). The drug-gene interaction network constructed based on the DSigDB database [30] analyzes the associations between core exosome-related genes (GNAS, TUBB2A, RPL22, RPL5, WNT5A) and potential therapeutic compounds. This network reveals the pivotal role of core genes in PD-targeted therapy, with GNAS and WNT5A exhibiting higher node degrees, indicating their function as multi-target regulatory centers (Fig. 8A). Based on drug enrichment analysis, we found that ​NSC94017​​ exhibited the most significant association with ​GNAS​ in the protein-protein interaction network. Consequently, this drug-gene pair was selected for subsequent molecular docking analysis to elucidate their binding mechanisms (Supplementary documents). Finally, molecular docking simulations for the model-related genes were performed. Docking targeted residues including Chain A: GLY47, ALA48, GLY49, GLU50, SER51, GLY52, LYS53, SER54, THR55, LYS58, ASP94, ASN97, ASN98, LYS100, GLU101, GLU104, MET135, ASP173, CYS174, LEU198, ARG199, CYS200, ARG201, VAL202, LEU203, THR204, SER205, ILE207, ASP223, VAL224, GLY225, GLY226, GLN227, ARG228, ASP229, CYS237, ARG258, GLU259, GLU268, LEU272, LYS293, VAL367. Chain B: GLN227, ARG228, ASP229, GLU230, ARG232, LYS233, TRP234, GLN236, CYS237. Chain E: THR4, PHE5, ARG6. Chain, F: LEU2 (Fig. 9) [33]. The molecular docking fraction is shown in Table 2. By establishing PPI networks of the core genes via the STRING database, we observed limited interacting partners among the core genes themselves. However, significant associations were detected between GNAS and RIC8A, RPL22 and RPL5, as well as WNT5A and FZD1/AFM, respectively. The sparse network with limited interaction data may suggest that further exploration of its functional correlations is warranted (Fig. 10).

Discussion

PD is a common chronic neurodegenerative disease with a complex pathogenesis involving the interaction of multiple genes with environmental factors. Our model genes (GNAS, TUBB2A, etc.) synergize with exosomes’ BBB permeability to enhance therapeutic delivery. For instance, WNT5A-mediated cholesterol correction via exosomes may mitigate α-synuclein toxicity, as shown in our docking analysis (Fig. 9) [4, 44].

PD is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons and the pathological aggregation of alpha-synuclein. Despite advances in understanding its pathogenesis, early diagnosis and targeted therapies remain significant challenges. Exosomes, as key mediators of intercellular communication, have emerged as critical players in PD, influencing both disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies. This study leverages multi-omics approaches to identify exosome-related genes that could serve as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, offering new insights into PD mechanisms and treatment possibilities [2, 10, 11].

Our analysis identified five exosome-related genes—GNAS, TUBB2A, RPL22, RPL5, and WNT5A—that form a robust predictive model for PD. These genes were validated through rigorous statistical and machine learning methods, including LASSO regression, SVM, and random forest, demonstrating their potential as early diagnostic biomarkers. ROC curve and nomogram analyses further confirmed the model’s accuracy, highlighting its clinical applicability. Notably, these genes are enriched in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, steroid hormone response, and neurodegenerative diseases such as Huntington’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), suggesting shared molecular mechanisms across neurodegenerative disorders [45–47].

One of the most intriguing findings is the association of these genes with immune regulation. Immune cell profiling revealed strong correlations with B cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and CD4+/CD8 + T cells, implicating neuroinflammation as a key component of PD pathology. This aligns with growing evidence that immune dysregulation contributes to neurodegeneration, potentially through exosome-mediated signaling. For instance, exosomes released by glial cells can propagate inflammatory signals, exacerbating neuronal damage. Conversely, exosomes may also deliver neuroprotective factors, offering a dual role in PD progression. Our findings suggest that targeting these immune-related pathways could open new therapeutic avenues for PD [20, 23, 48, 49].

Compounds such as NSC94017 and phosphine were identified as potential modulators of the identified genes, providing a foundation for drug repurposing or the development of novel therapeutics. Molecular docking studies revealed specific binding sites (e.g., GLN294, ASP295) that could be targeted to disrupt pathological interactions, offering a structural basis for designing small-molecule inhibitors or gene-editing tools [46, 50].

The ability of exosomes to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) makes them particularly attractive for therapeutic delivery. Engineered exosomes could be used to deliver gene-regulating tools, such as siRNA to silence pathogenic mutations, or neurotrophic factors like GDNF, to promote neuronal survival. Our study provides a framework for leveraging exosome biology to develop precision therapies for PD, potentially overcoming the limitations of current treatments [51].

Our analysis of core genes through the STRING database revealed sparse protein-protein interactions among these genes themselves. However, several significant functional associations were identified: GNAS with RIC8A, RPL22 with RPL5, and WNT5A with FZD1 and AFM. The limited interaction data within the PPI network implies that the functional significance of these interactions requires further investigation.

These findings suggest its value as a potential PD diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Although the specific mechanism of RPL22 in PD remains unclear, it has proven useful for optimizing techniques to study the translated of brain endothelial cells (BECs) [52]. BEC dysfunction is associated with neurodegenerative diseases, including PD. The WNT5A-LRP1 axis was identified as a core mechanism for exosome-based PD therapy. By correcting neuronal cholesterol metabolism imbalance and lipid raft abnormalities, it mitigates α-synuclein (α-Syn) toxicity and restores synaptic function, ultimately improving motor and cognitive impairments. This provides a new strategy for targeted therapy [53]. Current literature reports on the direct mechanism of RPL5 in PD are limited. However, preliminary analysis of the Gene4PD database indicates differential expression between PD patients and healthy controls, warranting further investigation to validate its specific role [54].

We reviewed the relevant literature and found that the five key genes identified in this study have been rarely mentioned in published papers. The search results also indicate that their mechanisms have been partially elucidated primarily in the context of cancer-related research, while further investigation is still required in PD research.

Studies have shown that exosome miR-382-5p prevents the formation of pre-metastatic niches by suppressing the GPR176/GNAS–CXCR1/CXCR2 axis in colorectal cancer liver metastasis [55]. The long non-coding RNAs GNAS-AS1 and MIR205HG may contribute to the regulation of chemosensitivity to 5-fluorouracil in colon cancer cells by enhancing the extracellular release of exosomes [56].

Exosome-targeted BRIX1 impairs the interaction between MDM2 and ribosomal proteins RPL5 and RPL11, thereby inhibiting the activation of p53 in response to nucleolar stress and triggering chemotherapy resistance in cancer cells [57]. Knockdown of EXOSC8 in CRC cells induces ribosomal stress, leading to the release of nucleolar RPL5/11 into the nucleoplasm, where they sequester Mdm2 and block its E3 ubiquitin ligase function, resulting in p53 activation [58].

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. While our predictive model shows promise, its stability and generalizability need validation in larger, independent cohorts. Additionally, the functional roles of the identified genes in PD pathogenesis require further experimental validation, including in vitro and in vivo studies to elucidate their mechanisms of action. For example, gene knockout or overexpression experiments could clarify whether these genes act as drivers or bystanders in PD progression [59, 60].

In conclusion, this study highlights the dual role of exosomes in PD, both as carriers of pathogenic molecules and as potential vehicles for therapeutic delivery. By integrating multi-omics data and advanced computational methods, we have identified a set of exosome-related genes that could revolutionize PD diagnosis and treatment. These findings not only deepen our understanding of PD pathogenesis but also pave the way for innovative, exosome-based therapeutic strategies. Future research should focus on validating these biomarkers in clinical settings and exploring their potential in combination therapies, ultimately advancing effective interventions for this debilitating disease [51, 61].

Conclusion

PD remains a complex neurodegenerative disorder with significant challenges in early diagnosis and effective treatment. This study leveraged multi-omics approaches, including multi-dataset analysis, machine learning, and molecular docking, to identify five exosome-related genes—GNAS, TUBB2A, RPL22, RPL5, and WNT5A—as potential diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for PD. These genes were validated through robust statistical methods, including LASSO regression, SVM, and random forest, and demonstrated strong predictive accuracy via ROC curve and nomogram analyses. Functional enrichment revealed their involvement in critical pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, immune regulation, and neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting their potential role in PD pathogenesis.

The association of these genes with immune cells, including B cells, MDSCs, and CD4+/CD8 + T cells, underscores the importance of neuroinflammation in PD progression. Additionally, Drug-gene network analysis revealed potential therapeutic compounds, including NSC94017 and phosphine, whereas molecular docking elucidated critical binding residues, thereby furnishing a structural foundation for subsequent drug development. However, the subsequent treatment results still need to be further verified. The ability of exosomes to cross the blood-brain barrier further enhances their potential as vehicles for targeted therapy, including the delivery of neuroprotective factors or gene-editing tools.

Despite these promising findings, further validation in larger cohorts and mechanistic studies are necessary to confirm the functional roles of these genes in PD. Future research should focus on translating these discoveries into clinical applications, including the development of non-invasive diagnostic tools and precision therapies. This study not only advances our understanding of PD pathogenesis but also provides a foundation for innovative, exosome-based therapeutic strategies, bringing us closer to effective interventions for this debilitating disease.
