Clinical and Cost Outcomes Associated with Cardiologist Payment Models: A Qualitative Systematic Review
Authors: Sandeep Chand, Melissa Monaghan, Riley Martens, Derek S Chew, Satish R Raj, Cathy A Eastwood, Glen Sumner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPayment models for cardiologists create financial incentives that can influence clinical decision-making and healthcare costs. In addition, some cardiologists may order diagnostic tests and procedures on equipment they own, which increases the likelihood of such tests being ordered. We aimed to assess the influence of cardiologist payment models on clinical and health system cost outcomes.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, National Health Service Economic Evaluation Database, EconLit, and Web of Science, for the period from database inception to August 2025. Studies were included if they examined cardiologist payment models and reported clinical and cost outcomes using randomized controlled trials, controlled clinical trials, controlled before–after studies, interrupted time series, or observational study designs.ResultsOf 4156 identified studies, 6 met the inclusion criteria. Within studies that evaluated patient volume in a fee-for-service (FFS) model, high-volume FFS cardiologists ordered more diagnostic tests than did low-billing FFS cardiologists, and high-billing cardiologists billed a lower volume of procedures. We observed a higher incidence of hospitalizations in patients with atrial fibrillation in the highest billing cohort, but a lower incidence in patients with heart failure. Among studies that directly compared payment models, diagnostic test utilization was consistently higher in FFS models than in non-FFS payment models.ConclusionsFFS cardiologists order more diagnostic tests, which may lead to higher health system costs. Increased testing did not lead to improvement in key patient outcomes. Further research is needed to examine the relationship between cardiologist payment models, cost, and clinical outcomes of the care provided.Keywords: cardiology, payment model, utilization, fee-for-service, salary, capitation

Physician payment models stipulate a contract between the health system payer and the physician provider. These arrangements vary across countries. In the US, physician contracts are governed by both public and private payers: the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services sets payment rules for Medicare and Medicaid, and private insurers and hospital systems independently negotiate physician contracts and reimbursement rates.1,2 In Europe, physician contracts are governed by national or regional health authorities, social insurance funds, and medical associations.3 In Canada, physicians are represented by provincial medical associations that negotiate directly with provincial government health agencies.4

Payment models may be categorized broadly as either retrospective or prospective. Prospective payment models establish a full price prior to service delivery; the financial risk is therefore shared between physician and payer.5, 6, 7, 8 Salary is a time-based, prospective remuneration method that is independent of the volume of health services.5, 6, 7, 8 In a capitation model, physicians are paid on a population basis, risk-stratified per patient per designated time period.5, 6, 7, 8 In retrospective payment models, like fee-for-service (FFS), the physician receives payment for each service rendered, after the service has been performed, and therefore the financial risk lies fully with the payer.5, 6, 7, 8

Physician payment represents approximately 13.8% of all healthcare expenditure in Canada in 2024.9 However, through their clinical activity, such as the prescription of medications, the ordering of diagnostic testing, and referral to other physicians and allied health professionals, physicians are estimated to control up to 70% of all healthcare expenditure.10 Therefore, optimizing the physician contract to align with broad health system objectives may be of paramount importance.

In Canada, provincial and territorial governments have introduced alternative payment plans for physicians, but only 29% of physicians are enrolled in such plans.11 Alternative payment plans in Canada are predominantly salary-based.4 Studies have shown that physician payment can affect healthcare utilization and clinical outcomes.12, 13, 14 Therefore, a comprehensive understanding is required to inform policy decisions. A previous systematic review highlighted the finding that an FFS model was associated with increased utilization, fewer adverse outcomes, and better access to care. However, the review lacked studies that focused on cardiologists.15, 16, 17

The current systematic review synthesizes studies that focus on cardiologist payment models and their association with clinical and economic outcomes. We aim to identify the relationship between cardiologist payment models, healthcare utilization, and both clinical and cost-related outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review study is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (ID: CRD42024578595) and follows the Cochrane methodology to conduct the systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplemental Table S1).18,19 The study designs include randomized controlled trials, interrupted time series (ITS), and observational studies, to comprehensively capture both high-quality experimental evidence and real-world outcomes, enhancing the generalizability and applicability of the findings. The population studied in this systematic review consists of cardiologists and patients; the intervention is defined by the payment model; and outcomes are both cost and clinical outcomes. We excluded noncardiologists because practice settings and scopes of practice vary widely across countries, which influences how cardiovascular care, diagnostic testing, procedures, and follow-up are delivered. Restriction of the population to cardiologists reduced heterogeneity and excluded studies of noncardiovascular conditions. We made minor amendments by including the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) tool for assessment of the qualities of cohort studies, defining noncomparative studies, and providing detailed explanation to assess heterogeneity in case of quantitative analysis.20 The reason to amend the protocol was to expand our explanation and to fill gaps in some information in the systematic review methods. However, these amendments did not impact our search strategy, data collection, data synthesis, or overall outcomes (Supplemental Table S2).

Search strategy

We identified relevant articles by searching MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, The National Health Service Economic Evaluation Database, EconLit, and Web of Science. The search covered the period from the inception of the databases until August 2025, regardless of language.

The search strategy was based on 2 main themes or search terms: “cardiologist” and “payment models.” We validated search themes used in previous publications and found no substantive differences.15 For each theme, terms were searched using subject-specific terms and a series of keywords under each heading as text words or database-specific subject headings when available (eg, medical subject headings [MeSH]).

Theme 1 was to identify the physician speciality of interest: “cardiologist,” “cardiology,” “cardiology service,” “cardiac surgeon,” “cardiac specialist,” “heart surgeon,” and “heart specialist.” To identify our exposures of interest (theme 2), the following search terms were used: "capitation fee," "prospective payment," "sessional," "fees," "alternative funding," "alternative payment," "salary," "remuneration," "payment," "academic," "performance," "blended," "bundled," "episode," "episode-based remuneration," "reimburs∗," "reimbursement," "pay,∗" "fee or service∗," "remunerat∗," "bill∗," “episode,” “rate∗,” “salar∗,” and “reimbursement mechanisms.” We searched these themes separately using the Boolean operator ‘OR’ and then combined the themes with the Boolean operator ‘AND' (Supplemental Appendix S1).

Study selection

At the primary screening phase, 3 individuals (S.C., M.M., R.M.) independently reviewed each abstract to determine if a study met the following criteria: (i) pertained to physician payment models; (ii) was original research; and (iii) had a study design of either randomized clinical trial, controlled clinical trial, controlled before–after study, interrupted time series design, or any observational study. Studies that focused on hospital payments or payments to other healthcare providers were excluded. Reviewers also identified whether the article was about cardiologists or other specialists along with cardiologists. These studies were included in our full-text review. Any disagreements among reviewers during the title and abstract screening were resolved through discussion and consensus. These criteria ensured that only original quantitative studies examining payment models in the provision of care to patients with cardiovascular disease by cardiologists or other related specialists were advanced to full-text review.

Two individuals (S.C. and G.S.) conducted full-text reviews for articles that met the preliminary screening criteria. We retained the articles if they met our inclusion criteria. All types of physician payment models were considered. At this stage, we excluded studies that were not full text, were not focused on cardiologists, did not examine payment models, reported irrelevant outcomes, used a qualitative design, or were not original research. The study examined 2 primary outcomes: clinical outcomes and healthcare utilization. Utilization was defined as the use of cardiac tests, procedures, and subsequent follow-up care.15 Clinical outcomes included hospitalizations, emergency department (ED) visits, quality of life, and mortality. Costs were considered a secondary outcome and were defined as direct expenditures to the healthcare system, including consultations, cardiac tests, procedures, inpatient hospitalizations, and ED visits. These outcomes were selected because they comprehensively capture both the cost and potential clinical impacts related to specific physician payment models. Comparative effectiveness of payment models in respect to these outcomes was evaluated, when such data were available. In cases of disagreement regarding the eligibility of studies, resolution was achieved via consensus among the reviewers.

In this study, we implemented a rigorous publication review process to ensure the accuracy and consistency of our screening and selection procedures. Initially, 3 reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts to efficiently manage the large volume of records identified in the initial search (n = 3221). This approach was implemented to ensure timely completion of the screening process. To verify the reliability of our screening approach, we compared the approach with the screening method after the first 100 citations, to verify that all reviewers were applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria consistently. Full-text screening was conducted by 2 independent reviewers, as a substantially smaller number of studies (n = 93) met the criteria for full-text assessment and were deemed eligible by at least one of the investigators during the title and abstract screening. These criteria ensured that relevant studies were not excluded prematurely.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction from the selected publications for the systematic review was conducted in duplicate by 2 reviewers (S.C. and G.S.). The extracted data include the study design, year of publication, location of research, study time duration, type of payment model, patient demographics and comorbidities (if available), category of outcomes (clinical or utilization or cost), and specific outcomes and results as reported. The quality of the studies was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s recommended risk-of-bias criteria for effective practice and organization of care reviews, as applied in a study on previous systematic reviews.21 This method provides specific criteria tailored to study designs with a separate control group, including randomized trials, nonrandomized trials, controlled before–after studies, and interrupted time series. Each criterion was assigned a score (low risk, high risk, or unclear risk) based on the reporting of methods and results in accordance with the standards set by the Cochrane Collaboration.16 The cohort studies were assessed using the NOS because it is a widely used and validated tool for evaluating the methodological quality of observational studies, such as prospective and retrospective cohort studies.20 The scale assesses key domains, including selection of study groups, comparability of cohorts, and outcome assessment, providing a structured and transparent approach to risk-of-bias evaluation.20

Data synthesis and analysis

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed qualitatively (by examining variability in study populations, interventions, and outcomes).22 The synthesis of results was organized by clinical, utilization, and cost outcomes associated with non-FFS and FFS cardiologist payment models. Subgroup analysis also was reported by considering different payment models in the non-FFS category, and each was compared against FFS.

Results

Systematic literature search and identification of studies

A total of 4156 studies were identified using our search strategy initially from database inception to August 2025 (Fig. 1). Overall, after removing 935 duplicates, 3221 studies remained for title and abstract screening. All abstracts were found to be in English. Most exclusions (n = 3128) occurred during the title and/or abstract screening phase, based on predefined exclusion criteria, including a wrong study design, hospital payment models, and noncardiologist populations. Following this screening process, 93 studies were selected for full-text review. At the full-text screening stage, 87 studies were further excluded. Reasons for exclusion included exposure that was not related to physician payment models (n = 35), a population that was noncardiologists (n = 23), a study design that did not meet criteria (n = 13), no full-text was available (n = 8), relevant outcomes were not reported, (n = 6), and duplicates were identified (n = 2; Fig. 1). After full-text review, 6 studies were included for analysis.

When assessing quality using the NOS tool for observational studies, 3 studies, 2 from Bhatia and colleagues and one from Quinn and colleagues, were rated 9 stars, indicating the highest level of methodological rigour across all 3 domains, including selection, comparability, and outcomes (Supplemental Table S3).23, 24, 25 These studies demonstrated larger cohort selection using large administrative databases and adequate comparability of groups through adjustment for multiple baseline characteristics. The duration of follow-up was sufficient as per the objective of these studies (Supplemental Table S3).23, 24, 25 Two studies, from Saver and colleagues’ and Farrehi and colleagues’, rated 5 of 9 stars using the NOS tool because multiple potential risks of bias were identified.26,27 Specifically, the risk of bias included the selection of the cohort from different hospitals and organizations, limited adjustment for relevant confounding variables, and biases in outcome assessment (Supplemental Table S3).26,27 Because the NOS cannot be used for quasi-experimental study design, we assessed 1 study from Yuan and colleagues using Cochrane’s effective practice and organization of care, and we observed that the study might have a relatively high risk of bias because of the lack of adjustment for relevant confounding variables (Supplemental Table S4).28

Summary of included studies

After applying our inclusion criteria, 6 studies were available for analysis. These included 2 noncomparative (single payment model) studies, and 4 comparative (multiple payment models) studies (Table 1). Three of the studies were Canadian, and 4 originated in the US (Table 3).

Both noncomparative studies assessed clinical and utilization outcomes by stratifying patients into 5 quintiles, from the lowest (bottom 20%) to the highest-billing physicians (top 20%), and quintile 2 was used as the reference level based on annual cardiologist billings.23,24 A total of 409,793 patients were included across single-payment studies. One study focused on patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), and the other on patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) and heart failure (HF). Both studies had a higher representation of male patients and those from urban locations (Table 1).23,24 Patients with AF in the lower billing quintile had a higher proportion of HF and renal dysfunction, and they had 2 or more comorbidities, whereas in another study, patients with CAD and HF seen by low-billing cardiologists had higher incidences of acute myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease (Supplemental Table S5).23,24 A total of 467 cardiologists saw AF patients, and 413 cardiologists saw patients with CAD and HF, respectively (Table 1).23,24 Noncomparative studies primarily examined healthcare utilization, including diagnostics, procedures, hospitalizations, ED visits, follow-ups, and mortality. Billing quantiles were compared within the FFS model (Table 2).

For comparative studies (multiple payment models), we found 2 studies that examined utilization outcomes associated with FFS vs salaried payment models for cardiologists (Table 2).25,27 Only one study compared FFS vs capitation, and one study compared 3 payment models: FFS, salary, and capitation (Table 3).26,28 Multiple data items were missing on patients' and cardiologists' characteristics in the included comparative studies. Missing data included number of tests billed, number of procedures billed, number of cardiologists, age and sex of cardiologists, severity of patients, and the workload of cardiologists. We contacted the corresponding authors via e-mail to request additional information, including data specific to the cardiologists' cohort or any missing outcome variables, but we did not receive any responses. Studies comparing multiple payment models assessed differences in only diagnostics and procedures (Table 2).

Only 2 studies evaluated a single-payment model, making a meta-analysis inappropriate.23,24 Although 4 studies examined multiple-payment models, only 3 compared cardiac tests billed by cardiologists reimbursed under FFS, with those under salaried payment models. As a result, substantial heterogeneity remained across studies (Tables 1 and 2).25, 26, 27 Specifically, the types of tests varied. In the study by Quinn and colleagues, noninvasive tests were assessed. In contrast, Saver and colleagues and Farrehi and colleagues included invasive procedures.25, 26, 27 Additional heterogeneity was observed across patient populations, follow-up periods, outpatient and inpatient settings, and organizational contexts, which further limited the feasibility of quantitative synthesis. In addition, missing information on patient sample sizes, differences in endpoints, and measures of association led us to adopt a narrative synthesis approach (Tables 2 and 4). We summarized evidence by first presenting the noncomparative studies (single-payment model) and then the comparative studies (multiple-payment models).

Noncomparative studies (single-payment model)

Clinical and utilization outcomes

Two nnoncomparative studies examined the association of cardiologists under the FFS payment model with hospitalization, ED visits, mortality, diagnostics, and procedures (Table 3).23,24 All of the outcomes in both studies were adjusted for patients’ and physicians’ characteristics. Patients with AF treated by cardiologists in the highest billing quintile (Q5) had an 11% higher odds of hospitalizations due to cardiovascular disease, compared to Q2 (Table 3).23 In contrast, HF patients treated by the cardiologists in the higher billing quintile (Q4) had 6% lower odds of nonelective hospitalizations, compared to quintile 2 (Table 3).24 For ED visits, no statistical differences were present in adjusted odds reported across billing quintiles for patients with AF, CAD, and HF (Table 3).23,24 No significant differences in mortality were observed across cardiologist billing quintiles, except for patients with CAD in Q1, who had 16% higher odds of death (Table 3).23,24 Although overall patient volumes were higher in the highest billing quintiles, no significant differences were observed in visit rates across quintiles for CAD or HF patients.24

Patients with AF managed by the highest-billing (Q5) FFS cardiologists had higher odds of receiving diagnostic tests, including 26% higher odds of receiving echocardiography (echo), 28% higher odds of receiving stress testing, 25% higher odds of receiving Holter monitoring, and 79% higher odds of receiving a stress echocardiography, compared with those in lower billing quintiles (Q2; Table 3).23 Similarly, CAD patients in higher quintiles (Q5) had 65% higher odds of receiving echocardiography, and 50% higher odds of undergoing stress testing compared with those in lower-billing quintiles (Q2; Table 3).24 The HF patients in Q5 had 40% higher odds of receiving echocardiography, and 32% higher odds of undergoing stress tests, compared to patients in lower quintile Q2.24 In case of procedures, the odds of receiving ablation was 25% higher in the lowest quintile among patients with AF (Table 3).23 The odds of receiving cardiac catheterization and revascularization were higher among patients seen by cardiologists in the lowest quintile (Q1) among patients with CAD (Table 3).24

Cost outcomes

We observed that billing amount in noncomparative studies (Ontario, Canada) for cardiologists in the FFS payment model varied significantly. For example, in the cohort of cardiologists that cared for AF, the highest-billing group of cardiologists billed more annual payment (median) annually than the cardiologists in lower quintiles (Canadian dollars) (Q5 = $690,726 vs Q1 = $519,067).23 The median annual billing amount, calculated using claims data for every year billed by cardiologists based on professional fee codes, ranged from $18,438-$462,206 in Q1 to $1,128,539-$4,830,017 in Q5.23 Similarly, in the cohort of cardiologists that cared for HF patients, billing amounts ranged from $18,438-$463,560 in Q1, compared to $1,128,539-$4,830,017 in Q5.24

Comparative studies (multiple-payment models)

Clinical and utilization outcomes

Higher utilization of diagnostic testing and procedures by FFS cardiologists was also demonstrated in comparative studies. Patients with AF, chronic HF, and CAD under the care of FFS specialists (cardiologists and internists) had 2.07 times more tests ordered.25 When FFS interpreters (cardiologists who interpret the results for testing) were considered, this ratio was 2.87 times the rate of cardiac imaging tests, as compared to salaried specialists. This included adjustments for patient and physician characteristics (Table 4).25 Cardiac testing rates were higher under the FFS payment model, compared to the capitation model (141.9 vs 101.2 per 1000 patients annually), across 3 states in the US, with even higher rates among FFS cardiologists nationwide (215.5 per 1000 patients; Table 4).28 Following the reduction in Medicare FFS payments for the principal noninvasive cardiac tests in a cardiologist office setting, FFS patients increasingly underwent tests in hospital-based outpatient (HBO) settings.28 The HBO tests used among FFS patients grew from 22% in 2009 to 44% in 2015 in 3 states (Colorado, Washington, and Oregon).21 In contrast, capitation patients showed declining HBO test use, highlighting the influence of financial incentives on care settings.28

Saver and colleagues showed that patients managed by FFS cardiologists in a health maintenance organization setting (HMO) had nearly 3-fold higher odds of receiving left heart catheterization and more than 5-fold higher odds of receiving percutaneous coronary intervention, compared to those managed by salaried cardiologists (Table 4).26 In another HMO, FFS cardiologists ordered more than twice the number of coronary artery bypass grafting procedures, and almost 3 times the number of percutaneous coronary interventions associated with a diagnosis of myocardial infarction (Table 4).27 In another study, patients with chest pain in hospitals where cardiologists were paid via FFS had higher rates of coronary angiography (52.4 % and 36.1%, respectively), compared to academic teaching hospitals (33.4%) where cardiologists were paid a salary (Table 4).27

Cost outcomes

We did not find any comparative study that explicitly reported cost outcomes for healthcare utilization or cost-related patient management. However, the 4 comparative studies that we reviewed demonstrated that a higher volume of cardiac tests and procedures were received by patients seen by FFS cardiology specialists, compared to that for patients seen by non-FFS specialists.25, 26, 27, 28 Given that increased utilization of diagnostic tests and procedures is directly associated with higher healthcare expenditures, these findings suggest that FFS payment models may be associated with increased cardiac test costs relative to non-FFS models.25, 26, 27, 28

Subgroup analysis

Two studies analyzed differences in the diagnostic tests and procedures received by patients treated by cardiologists who practiced under the FFS vs the capitation model.26,28 In one study, cardiologists who practiced under the capitation payment model from 3 states in the US ordered a lower volume of diagnostic tests and procedures. This lower volume included echocardiography, nuclear imaging, stress electrocardiography, and stress echocardiography, compared to those in an FFS practice across all states in the study (Table 4).28 However, when the comparison was restricted to FFS cardiologists in the same 3 states, utilization of stress electrocardiography was higher among the capitation group (Table 4).28 A similar pattern was observed in another study, in which the odds of a coronary artery bypass grafting procedure was nearly 2-fold higher in the capitation model compared with the FFS model, although this finding was limited to a single HMO (Table 4).26

For a comparison of utilization of diagnostic testing and procedures between an FFS and a salaried group, 3 studies reported higher use of selected tests and procedures among FFS cardiologists, relative to those in salary-based model (Table 4).25, 26, 27

The evidence comparing capitation and salary models was limited to a single study, which found that the odds of performing percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty was more than 2-fold higher among cardiologists remunerated under capitation, compared with those under salary-based payment.26

Discussion

The aim of our study was to comprehensively review the association between cardiologist payment models and healthcare utilization. Related to this goal, we also evaluated the effects of payment models on both clinical and cost-related outcomes. We reviewed both single-payment (noncomparative studies) and multiple-payment (comparative studies) models.

Our findings suggest that although the financial incentives in an FFS payment model may improve access to care, no evidence indicates that the increased volume of care leads to improvements in clinical outcomes. For example, FFS cardiologists who treated AF patients in the highest-billing quintile (Q5) exhibited a higher risk of patient hospitalization, compared to those in lower-billing quintiles, even when controlled for patient comorbidities.23 However, the same trend was not observed for CAD and HF patients.24 Similarly, no statistically significant differences were observed for ED visits across cardiologists’ billing quintiles.24 The overall findings for hospitalizations, ED visits, and follow-ups from noncomparative studies are neutral (Table 3). The AF patients in the lowest-billing quintile experienced higher death rates, compared to those in high-billing quintiles, but this same observation was not significant for CAD or HF patients (Table 3).23,24

An interesting finding is that higher rates of ablation and cardiac catheterization in the lowest quintile were observed for patients with AF and CAD (Table 3).23,24 This finding may reflect the fact that FFS cardiologists in academic centres who order these procedures generally bill lower volumes than their community-based FFS colleagues. In contrast, we observed that FFS cardiologists in higher-billing quintiles were more likely to order diagnostic tests than the cardiologists in the lower-billing quintile (Table 3).23,24

In comparative studies among different payment models, we gathered more evidence on the higher utilization of diagnostic tests and procedures for the patients cared for by FFS providers, as compared to cardiologists in either salary or capitation models. Overall, a higher rate of cardiac diagnostic testing, such as echocardiograms or exercise stress tests, within the FFS payment model was observed. This difference may be because some cardiologists can bill for these tests at their place of practice. Such practice is known as physician-induced demand for services.29 Other cardiac procedures, such as cardiac catheterization, typically are performed in hospital settings where access to such tests may be constrained.

Canadian data have shown that AF, coronary HF, and CAD patients under the care of FFS cardiologists ordered 2.07-fold more diagnostic tests compared to salaried cardiologists.25 Similar findings were evident in the US comparative studies. These findings suggest that increased costs per patient may be attributable to FFS cardiologists.26, 27, 28 We found only one study that found that cardiologists under a capitation model billed more for percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty than those in a salary model. One reason for this is that these physicians often work in a mixed-payment model setting and may receive productivity-linked incentives.

One study found that an FFS payment model combined with ownership of cardiology diagnostic testing equipment led to higher number of such tests, compared to both salary and capitation payment models.26 Overall, a higher rate of cardiac diagnostic testing, such as echocardiograms or stress tests, within the FFS payment model may be the result of misaligned incentives, especially when cardiologists own the assets of the testing facilities and thereby create demand for testing services that they deliver. In the HBO program comparison in the US, FFS cardiologists ordered an increased number of diagnostic tests in the program from 2009-2015 (from 22% to 44%), compared to capitation model cardiologists, who ordered a lower volume of tests over the same period.28 This contrast may be explained by the volume-based incentive in place for FFS cardiologists.

This review identified only Canadian and US-based studies. Based on our findings, Canadian and American policymakers may have further reason to consider alternatives to a pure FFS payment model for cardiologists. Alternative payment models, such as salary or capitation, may curtail excessive healthcare utilization and costs. For example, an accountable care organization program in which providers are paid under a salary model demonstrated approximately US$200 in annual savings per cardiovascular patient—primarily due to reduced procedural and testing costs.30 Overall, in noncomparative studies, despite increased patient access to care in higher-billing quantiles, we did not find evidence of a strong association between clinical events and billing quantiles.23,24 Salary and capitation models were associated with fewer ordered elective procedures.25, 26, 27, 28 Thus, these latter 2 models may contribute to a reduction in healthcare costs through the mitigation of unnecessary interventions.

Previous reviews have shown that FFS payment models are associated with a higher volume of services, compared with capitation models, particularly in primary care settings.31 Under the FFS model, primary care physicians provide more patient visits, greater continuity of care, and a higher adherence to recommended visit schedules.31 However, these benefits were accompanied by lower patient satisfaction related to access to physicians, compared with salaried payment models.31 A recent systematic review focusing on specialist physicians reported that FFS payment was associated with increased utilization, and in some cases, fewer adverse outcomes, as well as improved access to ED services.15 The review also found that patients underwent fewer surgical procedures, such as cataract surgery and tubal ligation, when specialists transitioned from FFS to salary or capitation-based payment models.15 Evidence from oncology payment models further suggests that FFS systems can lead to inappropriate levels of care, with potential undertreatment when payments fail to account for patient complexity and overtreatment when services are financially incentivized.32 An important point to note is that higher treatment intensity does not necessarily translate into better quality of care, underscoring the need for robust quality measurement across all payment models.32

Overall, our review’s findings are broadly consistent with others’ results applicable to cardiologists. FFS reimbursement is associated with increased utilization of cardiac testing (Central Illustration). However, the impact of this increased utilization on quality and clinical outcomes remains uncertain and warrants further investigation. If higher utilization of diagnostic testing/procedures occurs in the FFS group without improvement in clinical outcomes, then this suggests that pure FFS models may need to be modified. In our study, pure FFS remuneration is associated with financial incentives that may lead to overutilization and therefore diminished value in cardiology care delivery. Future studies may explore the blending of cardiology payment models and prospectively evaluate such models through comparison of clinical and cost outcomes. Such mixed-payment models may better serve broad health policy goals, such as fiscal sustainability and high-quality service delivery, and improve both patient and provider satisfaction.

Our systematic review has limitations. A high degree of heterogeneity was present across the included studies. This heterogeneity included a wide variation in patient populations, sample size, follow-up time, organization settings, study designs, payment models, geographic location, diagnostic tests and procedures, and measured endpoints. As a result, generalizing the findings across such diverse contexts is difficult, both within North America and for other countries. Notably, all included studies were conducted in the US or Canada, which further limits the applicability of our results to other healthcare systems. Second, we used the NOS tool to assess quality of the studies included, and 2 studies received 4 of 9 ratings, which renders confident interpretation of those results challenging. Third, some studies included mixed-specialty cohorts (cardiologists and internists). Thus, segregation of cardiologist-specific outcomes is difficult.

Fourth, we excluded studies that examined the outcomes of interest without specifying whether cardiologists were included in their studies. Despite attempts to contact study authors for additional data, no responses were received. This exclusion contributed to a smaller effective sample size for our review. We also found an underrepresentation of alternative payment models. Only 2 studies compared capitation, and only 3 studies compared salary-based outcomes against FFS models. This limited representation further restricts the generalizability of our findings. Our systematic review is the first to focus specifically on cardiologists who are incentivized under the FFS model to perform diagnostic tests and procedures. Although evidence on clinical and cost outcomes is limited, studies included in this review support our findings of diagnostic test overutilization driven by financial incentives among FFS cardiologists (Central Illustration). Further comparative research is needed to evaluate the relationship between different payment models and clinical outcomes, to inform policy decisions.

Conclusion

This review suggests that cardiologists paid under an FFS model see a higher volume of patients and order more diagnostic tests and procedures, compared to their salaried or capitation counterparts. These findings support the premise that alternative payment models for cardiologists, such as salary, have a lower overall cost burden than a retrospective FFS model. Further, despite increased access to care in the FFS model, no clear evidence in the available literature indicates improved clinical and cost outcomes between FFS and alternative payment models. Although this study suggests that broad policy goals, such as fiscal sustainability, may be better supported by alternative payment models as compared to FFS, future work is required to improve our understanding of the relationship among cardiologist payment models, clinical outcomes, and overall cost of care.
