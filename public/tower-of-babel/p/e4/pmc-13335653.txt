From disability to daily life: functional status, quality of life and work productivity in real-world inflammatory arthritis
Authors: Ana C Fuhrmann, Dianne P Mosher, Susanne M Benseler, Wrechelle Ocampo, Maggie J Larché, Deborah A Marshall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveInflammatory arthritis (IA) can impair physical function, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and productivity. Associations among these domains are recognized; however, evidence quantifying them across clearly defined disability levels remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate HRQoL and productivity across functional disability levels in IA using standardized patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis included consecutive IA patients attending rheumatology clinics in Calgary/Canada, enrolled in the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry (2018–2026). The Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI = 0–3) assessed functional disability, categorized as no 0, mild-to-moderate (>0–1), moderate-to-severe (1–2) or severe-to-very-severe disability (2–3). EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L; utility = −0.148 to 0.948; Visual Analogue Scale [VAS] = 0–100) measured HRQoL. The Work Productivity and Activity Impairment: Specific Health Problem (WPAI: SHP) evaluated productivity. Differences across disability levels were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc comparisons and effect sizes. Minimum important difference (MID) thresholds supported the interpretation (EQ-5D-5L utility = 0.20; VAS = 9.3; productivity = 20%).ResultsOne thousand one hundred seventy-seven (1177) IA patients were included. Worsening functional disability was associated with progressively lower HRQoL and productivity (P < 0.001). Deterioration was evident starting at mild-to-moderate disability, with the moderate-to-severe disability representing a critical threshold for impairment compared with no disability (difference utility = 0.223; VAS = 25; productivity = 40%).ConclusionIn this robust real-world study using standardized PROMs, functional decline was associated with progressively worse HRQoL and productivity, with differences statistically significant and clinically meaningful. Moderate-to-severe disability appeared to mark a critical threshold. These findings underscore the importance of early identification and management of disability to prevent clinically meaningful impairments in quality of life and productivity.Keywords: arthritis, rheumatic diseases, functional status, work performance, health-related quality of life, patient-reported outcome measure

Introduction

Inflammatory arthritis (IA) profoundly affects the lives of millions worldwide, limiting individuals’ ability to perform daily activities due to pain, joint stiffness and fatigue [1]. IA encompasses a heterogeneous group of immune-mediated conditions, including RA, axial SpA (axSpA) and PsA [2]. Despite advances in treatment, IA continue to compromise physical function, quality of life and work participation, resulting in substantial individual and societal burden [1].

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly used to assess these impacts, providing a patient-centered approach beyond traditional clinical indicators. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and productivity are central to understanding the real-world burden of IA and are recommended for regular evaluation in research and clinical practice [3]. However, methodological limitations remain common. Inconsistent definitions of work outcomes, deviations from validated recall periods, incomplete reporting, small sample sizes and limited use of meaningful thresholds to aid interpretation [4] reduce comparability and generalizability of findings. Large real-world registries such as the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry [5–7] offer an important opportunity to address these challenges by systematically collecting standardized PROMs across diverse IA conditions within routine clinical care.

Despite growing evidence linking functional disability to impaired quality of life and productivity [8–11], limited research has examined the gradient of this impact across clearly defined disability levels or when HRQoL and productivity impairment become clinically meaningful, particularly in heterogeneous IA populations. Therefore, to address this evidence gap, this study aimed to evaluate HRQoL and productivity across functional disability levels in individuals with IA using standardized PROMs. Leveraging data from the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry, this study seeks to identify meaningful thresholds that can inform early, function-preserving care strategies and support optimal, patient-centered clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This cross-sectional analysis utilized prospectively collected data from the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry, an ongoing quantitative observational cohort launched in 2016. The registry includes individuals with suspected or confirmed IA receiving care at two tertiary rheumatology clinics operating within a centralized referral and intake program in Calgary, Alberta, Canada: the Richmond Road Diagnostic and Treatment Centre (RRDTC) and the South Health Campus (SHC) [5–7].

Consecutive adults (>18 years) with a diagnosis or suspicion of IA were eligible for the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry. Recruitment occurred through clinic-based posters, advertisements on the registry’s website or direct invitations from clinical staff. Individuals who were unable to complete questionnaires in English and did not have access to an interpreter were not eligible. Participants were excluded if IA was later ruled out as their final diagnosis.

The definition of IA included RA, PsA and axSpA. AxSpA comprised AS, ReA, undifferentiated SpA and arthritis associated with inflammatory bowel disease. The study was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB15-0628 and REB17-2107). All participants provided written informed consent.

Variables and measures

At enrolment in the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry, patients provided sociodemographic information, including age, sex, education, marital status, income and ethnicity. Clinical data, such as diagnosis, date of diagnosis, tender and swollen joint counts, and provider global health assessment (0 = very well to 10 = very poor), were entered into the secure, web-based platform by a healthcare provider at each clinic visit. To date, a total of 32 rheumatologists, one nurse practitioner and one pharmacist have contributed to clinical data collection. To ensure data quality and completeness, a study coordinator reviewed medical records and entered any missing clinical information directly into the registry.

Clinic visits were confirmed in advance by the study coordinator using the clinic patient list to help minimize cancellations and rescheduling. Patients received email reminders to complete PROMs, further reducing missing data. Standardized electronic PROMs assessing functional disability, HRQoL and productivity were completed either remotely within 1 week before the clinic visits or on tablets in the clinic. Overall, 61% of participants completed the questionnaires remotely and 39% in the clinic. Once submitted, PROM responses could not be modified.

Functional disability was evaluated using the Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI) derived from the Clinical HAQ (ClinHAQ). HAQ-DI measures difficulty in performing activities across eight domains: dressing and grooming, arising, eating, walking, hygiene, reach, grip and other activities. Scores range from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (unable to perform) [12, 13]. For interpretability, HAQ-DI scores were categorized into established disability levels as previously described in the literature: no disability 0, mild-to-moderate disability (>0–1), moderate-to-severe disability (1–2) and severe-to-very-severe disability (2–3) [14].

Participants also reported a global health assessment using a 0–10 scale (0 = very well, 10 = very poor). This measure was combined with provider global health assessment and tender and swollen joint counts to calculate the Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) for patients with RA and PsA. The CDAI ranges from 0 to 76, with the following disease activity categories: remission (≤2.8), low (>2.8–10), moderate (>10–22) and high (>22) [15]. Disease activity is not routinely assessed using CDAI in axSpA.

HRQoL was measured using the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) instrument, which assesses five domains of daily functioning and well-being: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression [16]. Each domain is rated on a five-point scale (no, slight, moderate, severe and extreme problems). Responses were converted into a utility score, using the Canadian value set, ranging from −0.148 (worse than death) to 0.949 (perfect health) [17]. The EQ-5D also includes a visual analogue scale (VAS) for overall self-rated health (0 = worst imaginable; 100 = best imaginable) [16].

The impact of the IA on productivity was evaluated by the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment: Specific Health Problem (WPAI: SHP) questionnaire, a six-item instrument with evidence for validity obtained in patients with RA and AS [18–20]. The WPAI: SHP measures four domains: absenteeism (work missed due to IA), presenteeism (reduced productivity while at work), work productivity loss (overall work impairment) and activity impairment (limitations in regular unpaid daily activities) [19]. Participants reported the number of h worked and missed, as well as the extent of productivity loss and activity impairment, using a 0–10 VAS, where higher values indicate greater impairment [19]. All responses reflected experiences over the preceding 7 days. Domain scores were calculated using the standard WPAI: SHP scoring algorithm, in which responses are converted to percentages (0–100%), with higher scores reflecting greater impairment. The WPAI: SHP questionnaire and scoring algorithm are presented in Supplementary Data S1. The WPAI: SHP has been considered one of the most relevant instruments for evaluating productivity by patients with IA in Europe and Canada [21], and its use is supported by Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) [22].

Study size and statistical analysis

This study included each participant’s first clinic visit with complete PROMs (ClinHAQ, EQ-5D-5L and WPAI) entered in the Rheum4U Precision Health Registry, limited to one observation per participant. The Registry was established in 2016; however, the EQ-5D-5L was implemented in February 2018, replacing the EQ-5D-3L version. Therefore, the ‘first clinic visit’ for this study refers specifically to the first registry entry with complete PROM data using the EQ-5D-5L. This visit does not necessarily correspond to the participant’s enrolment date in the Registry, time of diagnosis or their initial clinic appointment. This approach ensured all participants had complete and standardized outcome data measured with the same instruments. It also reduced bias from missing data and variable follow-up, enabling a cross-sectional analysis with one observation per participant. All participants previously completing the EQ-5D-3L also had EQ-5D-5L data available, so no missing EQ-5D-5L outcomes resulted from the instrument transition. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range for continuous variables, and frequency and percentages for categorical variables. Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Jarque–Bera test [23]. All quantitative variables were presented as continuous variables to preserve statistical power and reflect their natural distribution. Differences in HRQoL and productivity across functional disability levels were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing. Effect sizes (r) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of differences between groups, and were interpreted according to conventional benchmarks: small (0.1–0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5), large (≥0.5) [24]. Median differences were computed to describe the absolute differences in HRQoL and productivity scores between functional disability levels. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed in R [25].

To support interpretation of differences across functional disability levels, the concept of the minimally important difference (MID) was applied. The MID represents the smallest change in an outcome that patients perceive as important and that may prompt a change in management [26]. MID values can vary depending on condition, treatment, population and estimation method. For the EQ-5D-5L, previous studies have reported MID estimates ranging from 0.05 to 0.13 for utility and ∼6–9 for VAS in North American and chronic disease populations [27, 28]. In the absence of IA-specific thresholds, we adopted conservative MID improvement values of 0.20 for utility and 9.3 for VAS based on pooled anchor-based estimates from musculoskeletal conditions [28].

For WPAI: SHP outcomes, MID estimates were derived from phase 3 clinical trials in patients with PsA, using anchor-based methods supported by distribution-based approaches. A 15% improvement was considered the MID for overall work productivity loss, and a 20% improvement for presenteeism and activity impairment [29].

Results

Participants characteristics

A total of 1177 participants were included, of whom 66% were female. The median age was 53 [IQR 42, 62] years. Most participants (91%) reported having attained a high school certificate or higher level of education. The most prevalent ethnicity was European (82%) (Table 1). Regarding diagnosis, 679 (58%) had RA, 297 (25%) axSpA and 201 (17%) PsA. The median time since diagnosis was 8.3 [IQR 3.5, 13.6] years.

Disease activity was assessed in 880 patients with RA and PsA; axSpA patients (n = 197) were excluded due to a lack of available disease activity data. Among the included patients, 37.3% (n = 328) were in remission, 31.5% (n = 277) had low disease activity, 10.5% (n = 92) had moderate disease activity and 4.2% (n = 37) had high disease activity. Disease activity was missing for 16.6% (n = 146) of patients.

Regarding treatment, 40% (n = 709) of participants were receiving conventional DMARDs, 37% (n = 657) biologics, 16.5% (n = 295) NSAIDs, 3% (n = 54) corticosteroids and 4% (n = 67) had a joint injection at the study visit. The total percentage exceeds 100% as participants could be receiving more than one treatment concurrently.

Functional disability

In the total sample (n = 1177), for the HAQ-DI score, the mean (±S.D.) was 0.6 (±0.6), and the median [interquartile range] was 0.4 [IQR 0, 1]. Most participants reported mild-to-moderate disability (47.3%), followed by no disability (29.9%) and moderate-to-severe disability (18.9%). A small proportion had severe-to-very-severe disability (3.9%). Time since diagnosis was comparable across all functional disability groups.

Health-related quality of life and productivity

HRQoL and productivity were lower in participants with greater functional disability. While individuals with no disability reported the highest HRQoL, those with severe-to-very-severe disability had the lowest scores. Conversely, absenteeism, presenteeism, overall work impairment and activity impairment were lowest among participants without disability and highest among those with severe-to-very-severe disability (Table 2). The Kruskal–Wallis test revealed that EQ-5D-5L and WPAI: SPH scores differed significantly among functional disability levels (P < 0.001).

Significant differences in EQ-5D-5L utility and VAS scores were observed across functional disability groups compared with individuals reporting no disability (all Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001; Table 3). HRQoL declined progressively with increasing disability severity, demonstrating a clear graded relationship. Even mild-to-moderate disability was associated with significantly lower HRQoL compared with no disability, with differences in VAS exceeding established MID thresholds. Greater differences were observed in moderate-to-severe disability, where both utility and VAS differences exceeded established MID thresholds, with moderate to large effect sizes. The largest differences in HRQoL were seen in individuals with severe-to-very-severe disability, with moderate effect sizes. Pairwise comparisons between adjacent disability levels were significant for most comparisons, except for VAS between moderate-to-severe and severe-to-very-severe disability.

Significant differences in productivity were observed across all WPAI: SHP domains when comparing functional disability groups with individuals reporting no disability (Table 4). Productivity impairment increased progressively with greater disability severity. Compared with no disability, mild-to-moderate disability was associated with clinically meaningful increases in presenteeism, overall work impairment, and activity impairment, meeting or exceeding MID thresholds. Differences were most pronounced in the moderate-to-severe disability group relative to no disability, with moderate to large effect sizes. Severe-to-very-severe disability was associated with the greatest impairment, with small to moderate effect sizes. Absenteeism showed smaller effect sizes overall, although differences remained statistically significant.

Discussion

In this large real-world cohort of Canadians with IA using standardized PROMs, higher levels of disability were consistently associated with progressively poorer HRQoL and greater work and non-work productivity impairment. These differences were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful. Importantly, clinically relevant differences were evident as early as the mild-to-moderate disability stage, indicating that even modest functional limitations coincided with measurable impairments. Although the largest differences were observed among individuals with severe-to-very-severe disability, moderate-to-severe disability appeared to represent a critical threshold at which differences exceeded established MID and effect sizes reached moderate-to-large magnitudes.

The relationship between functional limitation and perceived health is not uniform across the spectrum of disability. The decline in HRQoL was not linear across disability levels for the utility scores, where a disproportionately larger decrease was observed at the most severe disability level. On the other hand, VAS scores decreased more linearly across categories. These patterns likely reflect differences in measurement properties, including the preference-based multi-attributed structure of utility scoring and the linear scaling of the VAS. Together, these findings underscore the complementary value of these instruments when evaluating HRQoL across the disability spectrum.

Perceived health status rapidly deteriorates once functional limitations emerge. Patients without functional disability reported HRQoL (mean utility: 0.900; VAS: 81.7) comparable to Albertan (mean utility: 0.845; VAS: 77.4) [30] and Canadian (mean utility: 0.864; VAS: 82.3) [31] general population norms. In contrast, even individuals with mild-to-moderate disability showed declines in HRQoL (mean utility: 0.815; VAS: 70), with further deterioration among those with moderate-to-severe disability (mean utility: 0.653; VAS: 56.7) and severe-to-very-severe disability levels (mean utility: 0.397; VAS: 45.3), whose HRQoL fell below population benchmarks.

Cross-disease comparisons further highlight the magnitude of this burden. Individuals with no disability reported higher HRQoL than patients with several major chronic conditions, including HIV infection (0.84), diabetes mellitus (0.83), cardiovascular disease (0.77), neoplasms (0.75), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (0.75), chronic kidney disease (0.70) and multiple sclerosis (0.56) [32]. However, HRQoL among those with mild-to-moderate disability was already worse than that reported for some of these conditions, and those with moderate-to-severe or severe-to-very-severe disability experienced reductions that were comparable to or worse than nearly all conditions examined.

Productivity showed similarly important patterns. Presenteeism and overall work impairment increased progressively with disability severity, whereas absenteeism rose markedly only at the most severe level. This suggests that many individuals remain employed despite substantial productivity losses, likely reflecting workplace adaptation, presenteeism or withdrawal from employment before sustained absenteeism occurs. Effect sizes were smallest for absenteeism, reinforcing that absenteeism alone underestimates the true burden of disease. Activity impairment showed larger incremental changes at specific disability transitions, suggesting heightened sensitivity to functional limitations outside of paid work. The clearest separation in score distributions was observed between individuals with no disability and those with moderate-to-severe disability, supporting the presence of a meaningful functional threshold affecting both perceived health and productivity.

Cross-condition comparisons of productivity are limited by the existing literature that primarily examines job-related determinants instead of condition-specific effects. Our analyses addressed this gap by offering a broader perspective on how functional disability is related to productivity in IA.

Clinically, these findings reinforce the importance of early detection and proactive management of functional decline. Routine assessment of functional status, HRQoL and work and non-work productivity using standardized PROMs may help identify individuals at risk of transitioning into clinically meaningful impairment. Such assessments extend disease management beyond conventional biomarkers, producing improvements in outcomes that matter to patients, as demonstrated by [33]. Furthermore, they can guide timely therapeutic, workplace and psychosocial support strategies to preserve functionality, sustain work participation and enhance quality of life for people living with IA.

Strengths of this study include the use of standardized measures with validation evidence (ClinHAQ, EQ-5D-5L, WPAI), enhancing internal validity and comparability with other research. The large, real-world Rheum4U Precision Health Registry cohort integrates clinical assessments with patient-reported data, providing comprehensive insight into the lived experiences of Canadians with diverse IA diagnoses. Importantly, WPAI: SHP data were collected irrespective of employment status, allowing capture of activity impairment among individuals who had left the workforce due to disease.

Although IA-specific MID values for EQ-5D-5L and WPAI: SHP are not yet established, we applied externally derived, evidence-based thresholds to ensure clinically grounded interpretation. These thresholds, drawn from related IA populations such as PsA, provide a reasonable reference point. Notably, the EQ-5D-5L MID values applied were relatively conservative, suggesting that our estimates of clinically meaningful impact are cautious and reinforcing the robustness of the observed associations. Future research establishing IA- and country-specific MID thresholds would further enhance interpretability.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cohort comprises adults with IA receiving specialist care in a large Canadian urban centre and is predominantly of European ancestry, with underrepresentation of certain ethnic groups, including Black and Indigenous populations, which may limit generalizability to more diverse populations. Structural barriers to specialist care, differences in disease prevalence and disparities in healthcare access may also influence registry participation and outcome reporting across ethnic groups. Selection bias may have arisen from excluding individuals unable to complete electronic English-language PROMs, and self-reported measures are subject to recall and response bias. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Ongoing longitudinal analyses within the Rheum4U cohort, including stratification by IA diagnosis, will be essential to clarify temporal relationships and identify predictors of HRQoL decline and productivity loss over time, as well as potential differences or similarities across IA diagnoses.

Conclusion

Functional decline in adults with IA is associated with both progressively worse HRQoL and work and non-work productivity. In this comprehensive, robust real-world study, using standardized PROMs, differences were both statistically significant and clinically meaningful. Measurable impairments were evident even in individuals with mild-to-moderate disability, indicating that negative consequences can emerge early in the disease course. The moderate-to-severe disability appears to mark a critical threshold, where differences exceed established MID values and effect sizes reach moderate to large magnitudes. These findings highlight the substantial real-world burden of functional limitation and underscore the importance of early identification and management of disability to prevent clinically meaningful impairments in quality of life and functioning.
