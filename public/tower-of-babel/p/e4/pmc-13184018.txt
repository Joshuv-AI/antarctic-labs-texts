A mixed methods study on prevalence and factors associated with depressive symptoms and mental healthcare use among adults in Kashmir, India
Authors: Tazkia Zainab, Prakash Babu Kodali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDepression remains a significant public health issue exacerbated by geopolitical uncertainty and socioeconomic vulnerability. We conducted this study to examine the prevalence of depressive symptoms, and explore the factors influencing mental healthcare access in Kashmir.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study employing a sequential explanatory design (quant-QUAL). A preliminary cross-sectional survey of 420 adults sampled from Budgam district, Kashmir was conducted to assess depressive symptoms using the patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). The qualitative phase followed included in-depth interviews of 17 (8 survey participants with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms, and 9 key informants) purposively sampled participants. Survey data was descriptively analyzed, qualitative interviews were deductively coded to generate themes.ResultsModerate to severe depressive symptoms were found in 14.3% of sample, and 9.3% reported thoughts of self-harm in the past two weeks. Female participants (16.4%), participants with chronic conditions (24.4%), and those reporting tobacco use (29.3%) reported higher prevalence of moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Qualitative analysis yielded four themes including (i) fear, uncertainty and trauma driving poor mental health, (ii) depression-substance use syndemic, (iii) availability and affordability of mental healthcare, (iv) stigma & lack of awareness.ConclusionGeopolitical environment coupled with poor mental healthcare access aggravates depression in Kashmir. Multi-layered interventions including building sense of security, addressing socio-economic divide, reducing stigma, strengthening mental healthcare support systems are required.

Background

Mental health disorders are on the rise globally and have significant public health implications [1]. Among these, depression is among most common, contributing to 46.9 million Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) globally in 2019 [2]. The lifetime risk of experiencing depression is estimated between 15 and 18% [3], and with substantial impact on quality of life [4], and extreme outcomes including self-harm and suicide [5]. Globally, depression affects approximately 350 million people which is equivalent to 3.8% of the population, with higher prevalence among females (6%) and older adults (5.7%), and majority burden concentrated in low-and middle-income countries [6, 7]. By 2030, depression is projected to be the third leading cause of disease burden in low-income countries and the second leading cause in middle-income countries [8]. In India, depressive disorders account for 37% of all mental, neurological, and substance use disorder-related DALYs [9]. The lifetime prevalence is estimated at 5.3%, with one in seven Indians having experienced some form of mental illness [10].

From a socio-ecological perspective, depression results from interaction between individual factors, with micro and macro-ecosystems [11, 12]. At an individual level gender, age, education, employment, and health and behavioral factors like perceived state of health, chronic diseases, tobacco use, and hospitalization are associated with depression [13–16]. The meso level factors like the household’s economic status, social vulnerability, community and social stigma drive depression [17–19]. At a macro level ecological factors like experience of violence, trauma, perception of safety attributed to geopolitical conflict and healthcare availability are known to impact depressive disorders [20–22]. These same factors also are known to influence use of mental healthcare services, with females, older adults, poorer households and those residing in geographies impacted by conflict having a poorer access to mental healthcare services [21–23]. Geopolitical and armed conflicts are known to have multi-fold effect on mental health as they directly and indirectly victimize the individuals causing trauma and psychological distress while simultaneously limiting the availability and access to mental healthcare [21, 22, 24]. Conflict aggravates depression which remains underdiagnosed and undertreated with less than 10% of those suffering with depression receiving minimally adequate care (Fig. 1) [25].

Conceptual framework of the study

Kashmir is one of the most heavily militarized regions, shaped by prolonged conflict, insurgency, and terrorism, which have deeply impacted its political, social, and economic landscape. High prevalence of mental health issues have been documented in Kashmir owing to high levels of trauma exposure, displacement, and socioeconomic challenges [20, 22]. More recent findings reported a 41% prevalence of depression and 12% suicidal ideation in the adult population [21]. Budgam district in Kashmir was noted as having one of the highest depression rates in a survey by Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) [26]. Despite initiatives like the National Mental Health Programme (1982), National Mental Health Policy (2014) and the Mental Healthcare Act (2017) critical gaps in care remain [17].

While the impact of conflict on mental health in Kashmir is well documented, most studies are quantitative, and few explore the barriers to accessing care. There is a dearth of research combining population-based survey estimates with qualitative exploration of barriers to mental healthcare in the region. We addressed this gap by assessing the prevalence of depressive symptoms, use of mental healthcare among individuals with mild to severe depressive symptoms, and explored contextual factors influencing access to mental healthcare in Budgam district, Kashmir. The findings offer community-level evidence from a conflict-affected setting and contribute to the limited body of research on depression in Kashmir, with implications for strengthening mental health policy and service delivery in similar contexts.

Methods

Study design and study setting

From December 2024 to April 2025, we conducted a mixed methods study employing a sequential explanatory design [quant-QUAL] grounded in post-positivist and interpretivist paradigms. The first descriptive quantitative phase, informed by a post-positivist epistemology and realist ontology, aimed at estimating the prevalence of depressive symptoms and utilization of mental healthcare services through a household survey of randomly sampled adults in Budgam district, Kashmir. The exploratory qualitative phase guided by an interpretivist epistemology and a constructivist ontology sought to provide deeper insights through follow-up interviews of participants with depression symptoms, and purposively sampled key informants to contextual factors influencing depression and access to mental healthcare in Budgam District. We used MENTOR guidelines to guide presentation of qualitative phase and triangulation of findings [27]. Finally, the findings from both strands were triangulated in the interpretation phase.

Study sample and sampling design

Quantitative phase

We estimated a sample size of 420 adults using Openepi (available at https://www.openepi.com/) with anticipated prevalence of 41% [21], precision of 5%, non-response rate of 10%, design effect of 1, and a 95% confidence level. We employed a multi-stage stratified random sampling approach to select the survey participants. In the first stage we stratified district into urban municipalities (n = 6) and rural panchayats (n = 296) as the primary sampling units (PSU). Among the PSUs, two municipalities and eight grama panchayats were randomly sampled using a computer programme. Further, two wards from each municipality and two villages from each panchayat were sampled as secondary sampling units (SSU) making a total of four urban SSUs and 16 rural SSUs. From each SSU, 21 households were identified using systematic random sampling, and from each household one eligible adult age 18 years and above was sampled using KISH method, totaling 82 participants from urban regions and 338 participants from rural regions. Individuals local to Kashmir, aged 18 years and above, and willing to provide an informed consent were eligible. Individuals with self-reported serious mental health conditions (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder), pregnant women, and those living in institutionalized settings were not included.

Qualitative phase

In qualitative phase we conducted a total of 17 in-depth interviews comprising of eight primary informants (i.e., the survey participants who have been identified to have moderate to severe depression in the survey) and nine key informants. The key informants comprised of Psychiatrists (n = 5), Psychologists (n = 2) and representatives of Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) working for mental health (n = 2) in the region. The sampling of participants was based on data saturation. Participants were selected employing purposive and snowball sampling methods, ensuring maximum variation (with respect to gender, position, experience, and geography) and relevance of the sample to study objectives.

Data collection tools and techniques

Quantitative phase

Household survey was conducted using structured survey questionnaire that captured socio-demographic characteristics, history of trauma attributed to conflict, health status, depression (measured using Patient Health Questionnaire [PHQ-9]) and mental healthcare access in the past two weeks. The survey questionnaire was pilot tested prior to the survey to refine the questionnaire and test for clarity and comprehension. Survey questionnaire was translated to Urdu and Kashmiri and was administered by the first author a Bachelor of Unani Medicine and Surgery graduate and a Master of Public Health trainee at the time of the survey.

Outcome variables

The outcomes studied included (i) depressive symptoms, and (ii) self-reported utilization of mental healthcare services. We defined depressive symptoms as low mood or loss of pleasure or interest in activities or thoughts of self-harm in the last two weeks, captured employing the PHQ-9. Based on PHQ-9 depressive symptoms was captured as a composite index of nine four point Likert type items with a composite score ranging from 0 to 27, with higher score indicating a greater severity of depressive symptoms [i) no-minimal depressive symptoms (0–4), ii) mild depressive symptoms [5–9], (iii) moderate depressive symptoms [10–14], (iv) moderately severe depressive symptoms [15–19] and (v) severe depressive symptoms [20–27] ] [28]. We further recoded depressive symptoms into a binary variable with the responses i) no to mild depressive symptoms (score of 0–9) and moderate to severe depressive symptoms (score of 10–27). In our study, the PHQ-9 had a Chronbach’s alpha of 0.81, indicating an acceptable internal consistency. The survey tools and individual items were reviewed by the subject experts external to the study who provided the feedback on conceptual validity of the items. The questionnaires including the PHQ-9 were translated to Urdu and Kashmiri and back-translated by the first author who is a native speaker, and the validation of the translation was done with help of another native speaker with professional level expertise in Urdu, Kashmiri and English. Prior to the main survey, a pilot survey was conducted in one ward of Magam Thesil of Budgam District to assess the interpretability of the survey items among our target population.

We defined the utilization of mental healthcare services among individuals with depressive symptoms as self-reported use of mental healthcare services in the past 15 days [29, 30]. We developed a binary item for mental healthcare service utilization based on the responses to two items, “in the past 15 days have you visited a hospital/clinic” (with responses yes/no), and “in your last visit to hospital/clinic did you receive any service (including counselling and pharmacotherapy) related to mental healthcare”.

Covariates

Socio-demographic characteristics include self-reported age across three age groups ‘18–39 years’, ‘40–59 years’ and ‘60 + years’; sex of the respondent (male/female); highest education level (no formal education, primary education, secondary education, higher secondary, bachelor’s and above); occupation (government/private employee, self-employed, student, unemployed/retired, home maker); marital status (never married, married, widowed/divorced/separated); and place of residence (rural/urban).

Economic status was captured as self-reported annual household income in Indian rupees (INR) (with responses < INR.142344/-, ~INR.142345/- to 568164/-), and ≥ INR.568165/-) and type of ration card (above poverty line[APL], and below poverty line[BPL]).

History of trauma attributed to conflict was captured as self-reported items including (i) experienced violence or trauma (yes/no); (ii) lost a loved one due to conflict (yes/no); (iii) perception of safety (very safe/ moderately safe/ not safe); (iv) impact of conflict on financial safety (yes/no).

Health status and healthcare use variables included (i) perceived health status (with responses excellent/very good/good/fair/poor), (ii) currently diagnosed with any chronic conditions [including diabetes, heart disease, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases] (yes/no), (iii) history of hospitalization in the last 365 days (yes/no) and (iv) tobacco use in past 12 months (yes/no).

Qualitative phase

The qualitative interviews were conducted employing semi-structured interview guides by the first author, a trained MPH candidate and a local resident of Kashmir ensuring reflexivity. The interview guides comprised of open-ended items that explored the participant’s views on mental health status in Kashmir, and the individual, economic, societal and health system factors that influence access and use of mental healthcare in Kashmir. The interviews were conducted in Urdu and Kashmiri. The participant interviews were carried out at their home, and key informant interviews were conducted at the key informant’s office ensuring privacy and confidentiality. The interviews were audio-recorded, with a prior consent of the participants. Each interview on average lasted forty minutes.

Data analysis

Quantitative phase

Survey data were cleaned and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 and Stata version 13. The data cleaning process involved developing the primary outcome variables (i.e., depression status and mental healthcare utilization) and recoding independent variables to facilitate structured analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize participant characteristics. Prevalence estimates were calculated for moderate to severe depressive symptoms among the study sample (n = 420), and for mental healthcare utilization among those with mild to moderate depressive symptoms (n = 60).

Qualitative phase

The qualitative data was analyzed using a thematic analysis approach [31]. Interviews were transcribed verbatim from Urdu/Kashmiri and translated to English. Consistency of translation was ensured by undertaking backward checks. The transcripts were familiarized and coded by both authors. A deductive coding process using the conceptual framework and theoretical models on healthcare utilization was applied to identify semantic themes [23]. Consensus between the authors was reached through rounds of consultative meetings and iterative process of coding, refining codes, generation of concepts and themes. Coding was conducted manually, and Microsoft Excel® was used to organize the codes.

Qualitative results

The in-depth interviews provided insights to the prevailing burden of depression and mental health issues in the region as the offshoot of long-standing geopolitical conflict and its spillover effects, the role of stigma in preventing mental healthcare access, and limited availability and affordability of care. Accordingly, the analysis yielded four inter-related themes (i) fear, uncertainty and trauma driving poor mental health, (ii) depression and substance use syndemic, (iii) availability and affordability of mental healthcare, and vi) stigma & lack of awareness (Fig. 3).

Overview of the themes developed from qualitative interviews

Theme 1: Fear, uncertainty, and trauma driving poor mental health

The participants described a prevalent mental health crisis across Kashmir, attributing it to the region’s long-standing situation of conflict, socio-economic uncertainty, and the resultant spillover effects. Key informants working with health and humanitarian aid organizations reported nearly half of the population had signs of depression and psychological distress.

“Since I work with < NAME of the ORGANIZATION”, we saw 47% people had probable depression across various districts and 45% of the population had one or another psychological issue”.(P7, Key Informant, Counsellor).

“Since I work with < NAME of the ORGANIZATION”, we saw 47% people had probable depression across various districts and 45% of the population had one or another psychological issue”.(P7, Key Informant, Counsellor).

Chronic fear, hyper vigilance, and trauma experience were reported by participants. The spillover effects including poor infrastructure, and lack of opportunities for employment and education only made the mental health impact of the conflict worse. The key informants reported it as ‘living in perpetual trauma’.

“The region is undergoing conflict so mental health disorders are bound to flare up…. So, we are in a perpetual trauma.” (P2, Key informant, Consultant Psychiatrist).

“The region is undergoing conflict so mental health disorders are bound to flare up…. So, we are in a perpetual trauma.” (P2, Key informant, Consultant Psychiatrist).

“Kashmir has always been a stressful place throughout… be it in the form of infrastructure, be it in form of business, be it in the form of education reforms or employment. So, Kashmir as a whole has always been under stress that clearly reflects in mental health.” (P10, Key informant, Senior Consultant TeleMANAS).

“Kashmir has always been a stressful place throughout… be it in the form of infrastructure, be it in form of business, be it in the form of education reforms or employment. So, Kashmir as a whole has always been under stress that clearly reflects in mental health.” (P10, Key informant, Senior Consultant TeleMANAS).

These observations establish that the long standing geo-political situations in the region contribute to poor mental health and high prevalence of depression.

Theme 2: Depression-substance use syndemic

In addition to depression, substance use was also prevalent in the region. The key informants reported on increasing cases of substance use particularly among youth. In addition to the use of tobacco and alcohol among underage youth, use of banned substances like hashish (concentrated form of cannabis), heroin and opioids was reported.

“The number is increasing day by day. We are dealing with the Heroin dependency, mostly and other drugs like opioids…” (P3, Key informant, Clinical Psychologist).

“The number is increasing day by day. We are dealing with the Heroin dependency, mostly and other drugs like opioids…” (P3, Key informant, Clinical Psychologist).

A reason attributed to increasing substance use was high prevalence of depression and vulnerabilities faced by the people in the region, sometimes pushing the individuals to use substances as a coping mechanism for untreated depression.

“Drugs are sometimes used as a way to cope with the depression. As I mentioned the brick kilns areas, they are actually the hotspots for the substance use” (P12, Key informant, Professor of Psychiatry).

“Drugs are sometimes used as a way to cope with the depression. As I mentioned the brick kilns areas, they are actually the hotspots for the substance use” (P12, Key informant, Professor of Psychiatry).

The intersection of geo-political conflict, socio-economic vulnerabilities, untreated depression, and poor mental healthcare access appears to foster a depression-substance use syndemic in the region.

Theme 3: Availability and affordability of mental healthcare

In conjunction with prevalent depression, limited availability of the mental healthcare services created a demand supply mis-match. Systematic limitations including infrastructure gaps, shortage of trained professionals, over reliance on tertiary care centres persisted despite recent efforts to improve mental healthcare services.

“For those who are aware and want to seek help, for them the biggest barrier is unavailability of services.”(P5, Key Informant, Senior Counselling Psychologist).

“For those who are aware and want to seek help, for them the biggest barrier is unavailability of services.”(P5, Key Informant, Senior Counselling Psychologist).

“At least in every district, we now have some mental health services, but given the very high burden of mental illnesses, services are inadequate and limited to tertiary hospitals only.”(P2, Key Informant, Consultant Psychiatrist).

“At least in every district, we now have some mental health services, but given the very high burden of mental illnesses, services are inadequate and limited to tertiary hospitals only.”(P2, Key Informant, Consultant Psychiatrist).

Further, cost of accessing mental healthcare services also prevented people from seeking care. Seeking care from private sector providers was unaffordable while mental healthcare services in public sector were either not available or inaccessible leaving individuals experiencing financial constrains to further neglect mental health.

“Last time, my wife had depression then I took her to mental hospital once. Then I couldn’t take her for the follow up because I couldn’t arrange money.” (P6, Participant).

Discussion

We investigated the prevalence of depressive symptoms and use of mental healthcare services among adults in Budgam district, Kashmir valley. At least 36.2% of participants had some degree of depressive symptoms and 14.3% had moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Participants reported the symptoms of persistent low mood, sadness, loss of pleasure, and even thoughts of harming oneself (9.3%). Our estimates are similar to that of earlier studies conducted in Kashmir valley between 2008 and 2022 which reported the prevalence of depression to be 33%-34.58% [22, 32]. Further, the thoughts of harming oneself (9.3%) are higher than those reported at the national average [33]. The high prevalence of depressive symptoms are attributed to the sensitive geographical border, sociopolitical uncertainties, economic constrains, and lack of adequate access to mental healthcare services similar to other conflict-affected regions across Asia, Africa, and Europe [34–36]. Moderate to severe depressive symptoms was more prevalent among females, individuals with a compromised health status, and those reporting tobacco use. Earlier studies in the Jammu and Kashmir region reported that irrespective of the age group, females tend to have a higher prevalence of depression [37, 38]. Similar to earlier studies, people with self-reported poor health had a higher proportion of moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms [39, 40].

Our study also presents the impact of macro ecosystem factors like geopolitical conflict on mental health status in the region. While the quantitative survey hinted towards a greater proportion of moderate to severe depression symptoms among people reporting experiencing violence or trauma, conflict effecting financial stability, and a feeling of unsafe, the qualitative interviews threw light on living with fear, uncertainty and trauma as drivers of poor mental health [34, 36]. Evidence points that longstanding geopolitical uncertainties could negatively impact social and economic support systems which would have further propagated poor mental health [21, 36]. This is further reflected in our another qualitative theme, which pointed towards the emergence of poor mental health and substance use as a syndemic in the region. Studies in Asia reported that people who experienced collective violence are more prone to substance use, tobacco use and depression [41]. While the quantitative survey couldn’t report substance use possibly owing to social desirability bias, a higher proportion of depressive symptoms among people reporting tobacco use corroborate our syndemic presence of depression and substance use in this region [18, 42]. In concurrence with our findings, studies in Kashmir reported an early age of initiation, poly-substance abuse, and co-morbid psychiatric disorders [24]. However, most research on substance use in Kashmir are institution based studies (i.e., based in hospital or de-addiction centers) while the problem of substance use at a population level remains to be investigated.

Similar to earlier studies, our findings indicate that the synergy of micro and macro-ecosystem factors like stigma, limited awareness, and health systems shortcomings hindered mental healthcare seeking [43, 44]. The survey reports, only 10%[n = 6] of individuals with moderate to severe depressive symptoms used mental healthcare services in past 15 days. The estimates for mental healthcare seeking is close to 13.1% reported in a 2014 study on treatment contact coverage among individuals screened to be positive for depression symptoms employing PHQ-9, in Sehore District of India [30]. Also, a study based on the 2017-18 National Sample Survey in India reported that only 0.65% of the last 15 days outpatient visits were attributed to mental health services [29]. Although the reliance on a 15-day recall period and the binary classification of mental healthcare utilization may be overly restrictive and could underestimate care-seeking behaviours, these findings nonetheless highlight persistent barriers such as stigma, lack of awareness, constrains in availability and affordability of mental healthcare which were reported in our qualitative phase. A systematic review of 144 studies published between 1980 and 2011 reported stigma as fourth prominent barrier in mental healthcare seeking [19]. Similar to other regions in India and elsewhere, stigma and lack of awareness function at multiple levels (individual, family, and community) and prevent individuals with depressive symptoms from seeking mental healthcare services [19, 43]. An earlier study in Kashmir also reported that poor mental health literacy and lack of awareness often resulted in self-medicating, taking help of a local medical shop or visiting faith healers [44].

Macro issues impacting access in terms of affordability, availability and adequacy of services are major barriers for mental healthcare utilization. It was previously reported that the mental healthcare systems in Kashmir are often fragmented and unequally distributed limiting the access to mental healthcare [45]. Cost of mental healthcare services has been a prominent barrier in India [43], with individuals seeking psychiatric care having up to 89.3% risk of being exposed to catastrophic health expenses [46]. Limited availability of mental healthcare workforce further aggravates the mental healthcare inequities. Additionally, non-specialist frontline health workers including physicians in public healthcare system are not adequately trained to assess for mental healthcare symptoms, as more than 68% of individuals with psychiatric morbidity were not referred to psychiatrist on first contact [44]. While the district mental health programme and initiatives like TeleMANAS have made some improvements, they are currently not sufficient to meet the growing demand for mental health services and needs to be scaled up [47, 48].

Limitations

To our knowledge, this study is among the few studies that comprehensively examines depression and mental healthcare utilization in Kashmir. Our study has its limitations including the cross-sectional design, limited generalizability beyond Budgam. The study also has several important methodological limitations. First, the sample size of 420, obtained using a multistage cluster sampling design (assuming a design effect of 1), would be insufficient to generate robust population-level estimates or to reliably examine inferential associations. Second, the forward–backward translation of study instruments (such as PHQ-9 and survey questionnaire) was conducted by the same author and did not involve an independent bilingual panel, cognitive interviewing, or formal assessment of construct equivalence; this may have affected the measurement validity of the outcomes (including depressive symptoms and mental health care utilization). Thirdly, while research involving participants who report thoughts of self-harm ideally requires active and sustained follow-up, we were unable to provide long-term follow-up to participants due to resource constrains.

Conclusions

Mental health outcomes in Budgam district, Kashmir are shaped by a complex interplay of geopolitical conflict, social dynamics, and systemic healthcare limitations. Bridging the mental healthcare gap necessitates targeted interventions including expansion of TeleMANAS, strategic task-shifting to engage non-specialist health providers, and the reinforcement of the district’s mental health infrastructure and workforce capacity. Equally important are culturally attuned approaches, including partnerships with grassroots organizations to combat stigma and foster trust in mental healthcare services. A comprehensive, context-sensitive framework is vital to ensuring equitable access and sustained engagement with mental healthcare in the region.
