A pragmatic approach to complex acid base disturbances of critical illness: the “Stewart light”
Authors: František Duška, Martin Krbec, Micah Heldeweg, Paul Elbers, Lorenzo Giosa
Published: 2026

Blood gas analysis is used daily in intensive care and emergency medicine. Many clinicians interpret results using bicarbonate or standard base-excess (SBE) then assess the adequacy of compensatory carbon dioxide tension (PCO2) responses via empirical formulas (e.g., the Boston rules) [1, 2]. This “classical” approach works well for a single dominant disorder. However, in ICU patients with multiple comorbidities, several metabolic and respiratory processes may coexist, offsetting each other and producing complex mixed acid–base disturbances (Example 1 in Supplementary Appendix) [3, 4].

In contrast, Stewart described acid–base from a physicochemical perspective using three independent variables: PCO2, strong ion difference (SID), and total weak acid concentration ([ATOT], mainly albumin and phosphate) [5, 6]. This approach disentangles mixed disorders because it analyses the acid–base determinants rather than the final bicarbonate state [3].

To enable bedside use of Stewart’s concepts while preserving the advantages of the “classical” approach, Story et al. introduced base-excess partitioning in 2004 [7]. This method ascribes metabolic SBE to deviations in SID (estimated as [Na⁺]–[Cl⁻]–35) and [ATOT] (0.25 × [42–Alb], Alb in g/L), with residual SBE attributed to unmeasured ions (UI). While intuitive, base-excess partitioning performs poorly outside the pH range 7.3–7.5 [8, 9], limiting applicability in ICU patients with severe acid–base disturbances.

Focusing on multicompartment acid–base physiology, we recently showed that the bias in base-excess partitioning can be corrected if pH-dependent transmembrane strong-ion shifts are considered and the [Na⁺]–[Cl⁻] reference point is adjusted accordingly [8, 9]. Therefore, here, we propose a pragmatic algorithm that combines long-standing acid–base principles with our novel findings. Notably, our intention is not to assert superiority over existing algorithms, but to offer a structured alternative that we consider useful for bedside practice and educational purposes.

The “Stewart light” algorithm

The approach we propose is pH-adjusted base-excess partitioning combined with assessment of compensation via the Boston rules. Clinical Assessment: diagnostic probability, severity and acute compensation

Step 1: Clinical presentation, pH, Boston rules

Step 1: Clinical presentation, pH, Boston rules

The medical history and clinical presentation provide pretest diagnostic probability and guide the indication to blood-gas analysis. Once this is performed, assessment begins by recognizing severity and relative clinical implications: for instance, extreme pH values (< 7.2 or > 7.6) should prompt rapid intervention. Similarly, failing compensation of a metabolic disorder may indicate respiratory muscle fatigue and an impending need for intubation. This can be assessed using the Boston rules: in metabolic acidosis, Winters’ formula [10] estimates the expected PCO2 (mmHg) as 1.5 × [HCO3⁻] + 8 ± 2; higher PCO2 indicates inadequate compensation or superimposed respiratory acidosis, while lower values suggest concomitant respiratory alkalosis.

Analysis of metabolic component

If lactate is available, subtract its contribution to determine whether additional UI are present [11, 12]. Common causes include ketones, uraemic anions (phosphate, sulphate), and toxin metabolites. The rare finding of positive SBEUI suggests the presence of unmeasured cations (e.g. Li⁺ or paraproteins).

Integrated interpretation

Step 5: Synthesis

Step 5: Synthesis

Once all individual acid–base disturbances are identified, the information is integrated with the clinical context to determine primary disorder(s), expected chronic compensatory responses, and superimposed secondary disorders. For instance, high-SID alkalosis unravelled by positive SBESID is consistent with a clinical presentation of upper gastrointestinal losses, but may also reflect the kidney response to chronic hypercapnia: renal compensation increases the [Na⁺] – [Cl⁻] difference by approximately 3–4 mmol/L for each 10 mmHg rise in PCO2 above 40 mmHg [13, 14], allowing assessment of whether SBESID is fully explained by compensation or reflects additional processes.

Clinical applications and limitations

The Stewart light is best applied when combined UI and SID alterations are suspected. Clinical examples are diabetic ketoacidosis, intoxications, or sepsis (Supplementary Appendix), where the presence of UI often triggers physiological responses (e.g., vomiting or renal excretion of organic acids as sodium salts) and treatments (e.g., fluids, diuretics or renal replacement therapy) that simultaneously alter SID. In such scenarios, while the “classical” approach remains a valid tool to detect isolated UI via the albumin-corrected anion gap [12], the Stewart light offers the additional advantage of identifying superimposed SID derangements, a critical step in interpreting acid–base changes during treatment and guiding resuscitation fluids choice. Limitations arise from omission of phosphate or minor cations (K⁺, Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺) and rounding of coefficients for SID and albumin effects on SBE [9].

Learning and teaching notes

The algorithm is best learned through clinical examples (Supplementary Appendix) whilst keeping the Gamblegram (Fig. 1) as a mental model of acid base status. We recommend learners first practise on cases with near-normal pH and only later apply the pH correction of the [Na⁺]-[Cl⁻] reference.

The way forward

The “Stewart light” combines SBE, electroneutrality, multicompartment physiology and standard respiratory compensation rules into a structured bedside algorithm which enables clinicians to decipher complex acid–base disorders and guide treatment. By integrating “classical”, Stewart’s and novel approaches, we emphasize the spirit of acid–base multilingualism rather than favouring any single method [15].
