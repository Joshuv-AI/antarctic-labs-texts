Dynamic machine learning prediction of persistent AKI after cardiac surgery with modifiable perioperative risk factors
Authors: Changho Han, Hyun Il Kim, Jong Wook Song, Heesoo Shin, Young-Lan Kwak, Sarah Soh, Dukyong Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEarly identification and prevention of persistent acute kidney injury (pAKI) remain challenging due to delayed biochemical markers and limited tools to differentiate between transient and persistent forms. We retrospectively analyzed data of 2,285 patients who underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and developed 3 machine learning (ML) models for predicting pAKI: model 1 (preoperative data); model 2 (intraoperative and immediate postoperative variables); and model 3 (data up to 48 h post-ICU admission). pAKI occurred in 168 patients. Predictive performance improved across models, reflecting the value of time-updated data. SHapley Additive exPlanations highlighted baseline factors (estimated glomerular filtration rate and hemoglobin) in model 1 and perioperative factors associated with pAKI risk (post-CPB perfusion pressure, transfusion volume, and hemoglobin trends) in models 2 and 3 as dominant contributors. Our dynamic ML model enables early risk stratification and identification of perioperative factors associated with pAKI risk, providing a foundation for hypothesis generation and future investigation.Subject areas: health sciences

Introduction

Cardiac surgery-associated acute kidney injury (CSA-AKI) is a common and devastating complication.1,2 Persistent AKI (pAKI)—AKI not recovering within 3 days—occurs in up to 44.7% of patients with CSA-AKI. pAKI is associated with significantly higher in-hospital mortality (IHM) and long-term mortality rates, prolonged hospitalization, and increased chronic kidney disease progression compared with transient AKI.3,4,5 Given these serious clinical implications and the limited therapeutic options currently available, early identification of high-risk patients is crucial to facilitate timely interventions.

Currently, AKI diagnosis predominantly relies on the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria using serum creatinine (SCr).6 However, SCr is a delayed marker of renal filtration rather than direct injury, which limits its efficacy in distinguishing between transient AKI and pAKI.7 Unlike conventional static preoperative risk models, dynamic models utilizing machine learning (ML) continuously integrate real-time patient data throughout hospitalization, enabling a more accurate risk assessment by reflecting physiological changes. Incorporating intraoperative features into ML models can enhance AKI prediction accuracy, better reflecting the risk fluctuations throughout the perioperative period.8,9

Furthermore, dynamic predictive models for CSA-AKI, integrating preoperative, intraoperative, and early postoperative data, may be specifically valuable, as they involve perioperative variables, including hemodynamic parameters, laboratory test results, transfusions, fluid balance, and medications, which are routinely monitored and managed in clinical practice.10,11 Additionally, these models may be applied for predicting pAKI, facilitating early risk assessment in patients with uncertain recovery trajectories, ultimately enabling timely intervention and improved long-term outcomes.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to develop an ML-driven dynamic prediction model for pAKI in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) by integrating preoperative, intraoperative, and early postoperative data to enhance pAKI risk prediction accuracy (Figure S1). Additionally, we utilized explainable artificial intelligence tools, including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), to identify key perioperative factors associated with pAKI risk based on clinical progression.

Results

Dataset characteristics

The study included 2,285 patients, of which 1,690 and 595 were included in the training/validation and test sets, respectively (Figure 1). In the selected cohort, 380 (16.6%) patients were diagnosed with AKI most commonly on postoperative day 1 or 2 (Figure S2). Among them, 168 (7.4%) patients developed pAKI, and 41 (1.8%) did not recover from kidney injury during hospitalization. Compared with patients without AKI, those with AKI—whether transient or persistent—were more likely to be male (51.5% vs. 63.2% and 61.9%, respectively) (Table 1). The median ages were 64 years in the no AKI group, 69 years in the transient AKI group, and 71 years in the pAKI group. As the duration of AKI increased, patients tended to have lower preoperative hemoglobin levels (median: 12.9 vs. 12.4 vs. 10.7 g/dL in the no AKI, transient AKI, and pAKI groups, respectively; p < 0.001) and lower baseline regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2; median: 62.8% vs. 59.3% vs. 56.6%, respectively; p < 0.001). Furthermore, pre- and post-CPB average perfusion pressure (PP) decreased with longer duration of AKI (pre-CPB median: 65.9 vs. 64.4 vs. 62.6 mmHg; p < 0.001; post-CPB median: 62.9 vs. 60.5 vs. 57.8 mmHg; p < 0.001), whereas the area under the curve (AUC) of PP < 60 mmHg showed the opposite trend. Model performance

The area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUROC) for the test set progressively improved across the three perioperative phases: 0.807 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.745–0.869) for model 1; 0.837 (95% CI: 0.778–0.897) for model 2; and 0.878 (95% CI: 0.834–0.922) for model 3 (Figure 2). All ML models outperformed the Thakar score (AUROC = 0.725, 95% CI: 0.663–0.802), as confirmed by the DeLong test (p = 0.044, 0.002, and <0.001, respectively). After Bonferroni correction (α = 0.017), models 2 and 3 remained significantly superior to the Thakar score (p = 0.002 and <0.001, respectively), whereas model 1 showed borderline significance (p = 0.044). The AUROC was consistent across demographic subgroups, including age (<60 vs. ≥ 60 years) and sex, with no statistically significant differences observed, suggesting broadly stable model performance across these populations (Table S3). Performance at the optimal cutoff improved across the three perioperative phases, with sensitivity rising from 0.756 to 0.791, positive predictive value (PPV) from 0.185 to 0.274, and F1-score from 0.297 to 0.407 (Table 2). To demonstrate how model 3—the best performing model—operates across a range of thresholds in clinical workflows, Table S4 summarizes its performance at multiple decision thresholds, highlighting the expected trade-offs between metrics and the need to select a threshold aligned with clinical priorities.

Calibration analysis demonstrated that all ML models showed good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Figure S3). Brier scores and the expected calibration errors (ECEs) decreased progressively from model 1 to model 3—0.060, 0.058, and 0.055 for the Brier score and 0.025, 0.022, and 0.021 for the ECEs, respectively—indicating improved probability estimation with the addition of perioperative variables.

Decision curve analysis further supported the clinical utility of the models (Figure S4). Across a wide range of threshold probabilities, all three models provided greater net benefit than the treat-all and treat-none strategies, with model 3 yielding the highest net benefit for most thresholds, followed by models 2 and 1. These findings indicate that the models—particularly model 3—may support perioperative risk stratification by providing favorable net benefit profiles across clinically relevant thresholds. The net reclassification index (NRI) values of models 1, 2, and 3 compared with the Thakar Score were 19.1% (95% CI: 8.3%–29.5%), 19.9% (95% CI: 9.3%–30.3%), and 29.2% (95% CI: 17.3%–40.3%), respectively, indicating improved risk stratification. Compared with model 2, model 3 showed an NRI of 9.5% (95% CI: 1.2%–18.1%), indicating that the addition of postoperative variables (within approximately 48 h after surgery) significantly improved risk stratification (Table S5).

Interpretation

Intraoperative biosignal features related to PP (post-CPB) were identified to be important in the SHAP analysis (Figure 3). A higher AUC of PP < 60 mmHg and lower average PP (both post-CPB) were associated with positive predictions in model 2, with the former also being important in model 3. Hemoglobin levels and transfusion-related variables, including lower baseline hemoglobin level, lower nadir intraoperative hemoglobin level, and higher intraoperative and postoperative red blood cell (RBC) transfusion volumes, were associated with positive predictions. The most significant predictor in models 2 and 3 was the immediate postoperative estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (measured within 1 h of ICU admission), with lower baseline and immediate postoperative eGFR being associated with positive predictions. High EuroSCORE II was linked to positive predictions across all phases, though its feature importance declined over the three phases.

Association with adverse clinical outcomes

We conducted a multiple logistic regression (MLoR) to assess the association between ML model predictions and IHM (STAR Methods). Table S6 presents the results of the MLoR analysis. For clearer interpretation, we adjusted the scale of ML model outputs from 0–1 to 0–10 by multiplying by 10. The odds ratios in Table S6 indicate the change in odds for a 10% absolute increase in ML model outputs. ML model outputs for models 1, 2, and 3 demonstrated significant associations with IHM. For instance, a 10% absolute increase in model 3 output increased the odds of IHM by 2.63 (95% CI: 1.87–3.92, p < 0.001) times after adjustments.

Model performance in the AKI subgroup

To further assess model robustness in a higher-risk population, we evaluated model performance within the AKI subgroup of the test set (n = 94), comparing patients with AKI that did not recover within 3 days (pAKI, n = 42) and those who achieved renal recovery (non-pAKI, n = 52).

In this subgroup, model 3 demonstrated reasonable discrimination, with an AUROC of 0.717 (95% CI: 0.613–0.817) and an AUPRC of 0.656 (95% CI: 0.499–0.802) (Figure S6 and Table S7). When compared across models, model 3 consistently showed higher discrimination than models 1 and 2, indicating preserved incremental performance in this more challenging clinical context.

In an exploratory secondary analysis, we also assessed model performance for predicting AKI without renal recovery by discharge or death. Model 3 showed moderate discrimination for this outcome; however, the limited number of AKI cases without renal recovery by discharge warrants cautious interpretation of these findings (Figure S7 and Table S8).

Discussion

In the present study, we developed a predictive model for pAKI, using a comprehensive dataset of perioperative clinical and biosignal data, incorporating intraoperative and early postoperative variables within the first 48 h. Given that early postoperative physiological changes are closely related to subsequent renal recovery trajectories, we developed a dynamic model incorporating not only intraoperative data but also early postoperative data, particularly patient blood management (PBM)-related factors. This approach was associated with improved pAKI prediction and allowed the identification of perioperative factors associated with the persistence of AKI. Model 3 was designed to support early postoperative risk stratification by integrating perioperative and early postoperative variables collected within the first 48 h of surgery, reflecting patients’ early physiological trajectory. The SHAP analysis revealed immediate postoperative and preoperative eGFR, intraoperative PP-related variables, transfusion-related variables, preoperative EuroSCORE II, and hemoglobin levels as key contributors to pAKI development. Notably, intraoperative PP- and transfusion-related variables emerged as prominent perioperative factors associated with pAKI risk. These findings highlight clinically plausible perioperative correlates identified through predictive modeling, which should be interpreted as reflecting predictive associations rather than causal relationships.

The pathogenesis of cardiac surgery-associated AKI is complex, primarily driven by renal hypoperfusion and oxygen imbalance.12 Numerous studies have reported associations between perioperative hemodynamic changes and postoperative AKI,13,14,15,16,17,18,19 with evidence suggesting that elevated central venous pressure (CVP) or PP < 60 mmHg, rather than hypotension alone, plays a more significant role.13,20,21,22 However, despite the well-established impact of hemodynamic instability on AKI, many predictive models have excluded intraoperative biosignal data or relied solely on static indices, such as mean values, limiting their ability to capture any short-term variability.23,24,25,26

Within the perioperative period, we focused on intraoperative hemodynamic time-series variables, including mean arterial pressure (MAP), CVP, PP, and cardiac index, to assess their association with pAKI. Notably, rather than analyzing the entire intraoperative period as a whole, we segmented the intraoperative period into pre-CPB, CPB, and post-CPB phases to identify the most critical phase contributing to pAKI risk. Our results highlighted the critical role of PP post-CPB, where both the duration and extent of PP reductions <60 mmHg emerged as key predictors of pAKI. The limited association of MAP or CVP with pAKI might have been influenced by several factors. Patients with low PP or elevated CVP before surgery likely already have several risk factors for pAKI.13,27,28 Additionally, a high preoperative PP in patients with chronic hypertension could increase the risk of AKI due to a right shift in the lower pressure limit of renal autoregulation.29 This may explain why pre-CPB hemodynamic factors exhibited limited association with pAKI. Conversely, post-CPB hemodynamic changes, particularly a decline in PP, potentially have a more direct impact on pAKI development than an increase in CVP.

The relationship between perioperative transfusion and postoperative AKI is complex. Studies have shown associations between preoperative anemia, intraoperative transfusion, and hemodilution during CPB with AKI risk, with both excessive and insufficient hemodilution contributing to AKI occurrence.30,31,32,33,34 In our study, patients who developed pAKI had significantly low hemoglobin levels at baseline, intraoperative nadir, and within the first 48 h postoperatively, suggesting that perioperative hemoglobin levels and transfusion needs are critical factors for pAKI development. However, the retrospective design limits the ability to determine a definitive temporal relationship or precise transfusion thresholds. Nevertheless, the SHAP dependence plots revealed trends in AKI risk relative to hemoglobin levels and transfusion amounts. These findings further support an association between perioperative hemoglobin levels, transfusion burden, and pAKI risk, and place PBM within an important perioperative context in which pAKI risk may evolve.

Our findings highlight the crucial role of intraoperative and early postoperative variables (up to 48 h) in predicting pAKI. While the feature importance of EuroSCORE II and preoperative eGFR decreased in models 2 and 3 compared with model 1, they remained significant predictors. This suggests that EuroSCORE II is a valuable indicator for identifying high-risk patients, with preoperative renal dysfunction being an important factor, consistent with the findings of previous studies. Furthermore, ML model outputs for all three models demonstrated significant associations with IHM, even after adjusting for EuroSCORE II, supporting their clinical relevance. This suggests that our models provide predictive insights beyond conventional risk scoring systems, enhancing risk stratification.

The strength of our approach lies not only in its ability to predict pAKI risk preoperatively but also in its integration of intraoperative and early postoperative variables into a dynamic model that reflects the impact of perioperative management on pAKI risk. This model incorporates essential intraoperative biosignal data, such as blood pressure and cardiac output, along with PBM strategies implemented during anesthesia and the early postoperative period. By integrating perioperative variables, our predictive model achieved a robust AUROC of 0.878, demonstrating its potential to support early postoperative risk stratification. Moreover, it highlights how perioperative clinical decisions can directly influence pAKI development, providing valuable insights for optimizing patient management during and after cardiac surgery. Our calibration, decision curve analysis, and NRI assessments indicated that the proposed ML models provided well-calibrated risk estimates and greater net benefit than traditional risk scores across clinically relevant thresholds, without implying causal or interventional benefit. While the model demonstrated strong discriminative performance, its relatively modest F1-score and PPV, possibly due to the low prevalence of pAKI, should be considered when evaluating clinical applicability.

In conclusion, we developed a predictive model for pAKI that did not resolve within 3 days in patients who underwent cardiac surgery with CPB. Our findings suggest that a decline in PP after CPB, lower perioperative nadir hemoglobin, and a higher volume of perioperative RBC transfusion are significantly associated with pAKI. By integrating these perioperative variables, our model enables early identification of patients at a high risk of pAKI, supporting perioperative risk stratification and providing a foundation for hypothesis generation in future studies. Importantly, model 3 maintained reasonable discrimination within the AKI subgroup, which supports its applicability in a higher-risk clinical context. In an exploratory analysis, we additionally evaluated model performance for predicting AKI without renal recovery by discharge or death. Model 3 demonstrated moderate discriminative performance for this outcome; however, the limited number of such events warrants cautious interpretation of these findings.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted using data derived from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. We were unable to identify an external institution with comparable high-resolution biosignal and clinical data suitable for independent validation. To partially address this limitation, we performed temporal validation by training the models on earlier surgical years and evaluating them on subsequent years, thereby assessing performance in a temporally distinct cohort. Second, although patients were prospectively enrolled in the institutional data registry, the present analysis was conducted retrospectively, which may introduce inherent biases associated with observational study designs. Accordingly, the perioperative factors identified through predictive modeling should not be interpreted as modifiable targets with proven interventional benefit, but rather as predictive associations that may serve as hypothesis-generating evidence for future prospective or interventional studies. In addition, this study was intentionally limited to routinely available perioperative clinical data and high-resolution biosignals and did not incorporate biomarker, transcriptomic, or renal perfusion imaging data; therefore, mechanistic insights into sustained tubular injury remain beyond the scope of the present analysis. Third, although early postoperative mortality could theoretically act as a competing event for pAKI, no deaths occurred prior to pAKI classification during the outcome assessment window in our dataset. Therefore, competing risk due to mortality did not influence outcome determination in this cohort. In addition, while partial and complete renal recovery after AKI may be associated with different long-term outcomes, the present study focused on early identification of pAKI defined by failure to recover within 3 days. Future studies with larger cohorts will be needed to evaluate recovery phenotypes in greater detail. Fourth, although the Thakar score was originally developed to predict dialysis-requiring AKI rather than pAKI, it remains one of the most widely validated and clinically adopted renal risk scores in cardiac surgery populations. In the absence of a universally accepted bedside clinical score specifically designed for predicting pAKI, we used the Thakar score as a pragmatic reference comparator. This comparison should be interpreted as illustrating the incremental value of a dynamic perioperative ML approach over traditional static preoperative risk stratification, rather than implying identical risk profiles between dialysis-requiring AKI and pAKI. Fifth, because model 3 incorporates postoperative variables collected up to 48 h after surgery, some predictors may be temporally close to the initial diagnosis of AKI in a subset of patients. Although all input variables were collected before pAKI classification and no data obtained after pAKI determination were included, model 3 may partially reflect early manifestations of renal dysfunction, in addition to baseline risk characteristics. Sixth, due to the lack of postoperative high-frequency hemodynamic data, immediate postoperative time-series hemodynamic variables were not included in the analysis. Seventh, the model developed in this study is computationally complex and not yet readily deployable in routine clinical settings, requiring dedicated infrastructure for automated data extraction and real-time prediction. Finally, although overall calibration performance was acceptable, conservative probability estimates were observed in higher-risk quantiles, suggesting that additional calibration refinement may be required for patients at the upper end of the risk spectrum.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics and consent

The Department of Anesthesiology at Severance Cardiovascular Hospital in Seoul, South Korea, has established a cardiac surgery registry that compiles perioperative clinical data and intraoperative biosignal data using VitalRecorder, a software for recording high-resolution biosignal waveforms and vital signs.35 The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Severance Hospital approved both the prospective data collection and subsequent use of the dataset for research purposes under protocols established in 2018 and further detailed in 2024 (approval numbers: 4-2018-1002 and 4-2024-1423). All data were utilized within the IRB-approved period, strictly adhering to anonymization standards and institutional oversight for retrospective analyses and predictive model development. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of enrollment.

Study population

Adult patients aged ≥19 years who underwent cardiac surgery with CPB between December 14, 2018, and December 31, 2023, were included (Figure 1). All patients were of Korean ethnicity, consistent with the population served by this single-center registry in South Korea. Perioperative clinical data, including preoperative variables (demographics, medical history, laboratory findings, and medications), intraoperative variables (procedural durations, fraction of inspired oxygen, medications, and fluid inputs and outputs), and postoperative variables (laboratory findings, transfusion history, and infused colloid and albumin volumes), were collected from the institutional cardiac surgery registry and electronic medical records (EMR). Variables used in the calculation of EuroSCORE II were excluded to avoid redundancy (Table S9).36 However, age, sex, and preoperative eGFR were retained, as they are key predictors of CSA-AKI.

Figure 1 illustrates the patient inclusion process. Patients with stage 5 chronic kidney disease, as defined by the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidelines,6 were excluded due to challenges in distinguishing disease progression from elevated serum creatinine. Patients with missing data on critical variables, such as intraoperative biosignals, or those who died within 2 days after surgery were excluded from the study, as persistent AKI could not be assessed in these individuals. Furthermore, to prevent data leakage, patients included in the training and validation sets were excluded from the test set. Table 1 summarizes the baseline patient characteristics, including age and sex.

Method details

Data labeling

Patients diagnosed with AKI within 7 days post-surgery based on the KDIGO criteria were classified as CSA-AKI-positive, whereas those who did not recover within 3 days were classified as pAKI-positive.3,4,5 Recovery was defined as the resolution of oliguria (without diuretics) and a return to baseline serum creatinine level or a 50% reduction.5 Patients who did not recover renal function throughout hospitalization were also included in the pAKI group. We additionally verified that no patients died before completion of the 7-day AKI assessment period. All deaths occurring on postoperative day 3 (n = 2) occurred after pAKI had already been diagnosed. Therefore, death did not act as a competing event affecting the classification of pAKI in this cohort. This definition of pAKI focuses on renal non-recovery within a predefined time window, so patients with partial recovery were included in the pAKI group as having persistent renal dysfunction.

Intraoperative biosignal feature calculation

We extracted biosignals recorded during intraoperative monitoring via VitalRecorder,35 including rSO2, MAP, CVP, and cardiac index (Table S9). PP was calculated as the difference between MAP and CVP (i.e., PP = MAP - CVP). The rSO2 values were obtained from both sides of the brain, with features derived from the average of these bilateral values.

The sampling frequency in the VitalRecorder file varied, with rSO2 exhibiting the lowest frequency at 0.2 Hz. Therefore, all study parameters were extracted at 0.2 Hz.35 This rate was selected because we hypothesized that sampling every 5 s would adequately capture the physiological status of the patient during the long operation while reducing the computation time for calculating study features.

Raw waveform data from VitalRecorder may contain artifacts that can negatively impact analysis, as they do not accurately reflect the patient’s physiological state and should be removed.35,37,38 We thoroughly examined the files to identify artifact patterns (e.g., from sensor detachment or disconnection) and found that significant artifacts could be addressed using a rule-based approach. Figure S8 shows examples, and Table S10 outlines the rules for removal.

We calculated features from intraoperative biosignal parameters for ML models (Table S9), capturing the overall or baseline characteristics of the patients (e.g., average or baseline values), the extent of adverse conditions experienced during surgery (e.g., AUC above or below a cutoff), and beat-to-beat variability in the data (coefficient of variation and average real variability [ARV]).39 Baseline values were defined as the average value recorded during the first minute after the start of anesthesia. The coefficient of variation, calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean, measures variability relative to the mean. ARV, calculated as the average of absolute differences between consecutive measurements, is an index of short-term, within-subject variability, designed to address the limitations of standard deviation, which only reflects dispersion around the mean.39 The AUC for a parameter is the area above or below a specified threshold.

Biosignal features were calculated separately for pre-CPB, intra-CPB, and post-CPB periods. During CPB, normal physiological functions are managed using the CPB machine through extracorporeal circulation. Consequently, most biosignal parameters do not reflect actual physiological status. Hence, features were not calculated for this period, except for those related to perfusion pressure (PP). For PP-related features, PP was substituted with mean arterial pressure during CPB, excluding periods of total circulatory arrest from feature calculation.

Features were calculated using a predefined method rather than an end-to-end approach for the following reasons. First, the raw data were highly heterogeneous, with considerable variation in operation time, type, and the presence of artifacts in the waveform data. Second, the sample size of our cohort was insufficient to handle this heterogeneity effectively with an end-to-end algorithm. A substantially larger dataset is required for artificial neural network-based end-to-end algorithms to extract meaningful features from complex datasets.40,41

Variable selection, data preprocessing and software

Variables with a missing rate of over 20% were excluded from the analysis. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded, and for missing values, a dummy variable was created (coded as 1 for missing, 0 otherwise), replacing the original one-hot encoded vector with zeros. Missing values in continuous variables were handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations with the random forest method.42 During cross-validation, the imputation model was trained on each fold’s training set and applied to the validation set. For the test set, final imputation values were averaged across all cross-validation fold imputation models. The missing value handling strategy allowed inclusion of patients with incomplete data, preserving sample size and reducing potential selection bias while maintaining model performance. Tree-based models, as used in this study, are not affected by feature normalization; therefore, we did not perform normalization. Regarding clinical variables, we did not conduct separate outlier treatment, as our exploratory data analysis and data review indicated that these values were not erroneous entries but rather reflected actual physiological measurements.

VitalRecorder files were extracted into CSV format using VitalUtils,35 and data preprocessing, feature calculation, model training, and evaluation were performed in Python (version 3.10.6) using Scikit-learn (version 1.3.0), whereas statistical analyses were conducted using the TableOne package (version 0.8.0).

Machine learning model development

Patients who underwent cardiac surgery between December 14, 2018, and December 31, 2022, were assigned to the training and validation sets, whereas those who underwent surgery between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023, were assigned to the test set. The input variables for the ML models are summarized in Table S9. We developed ML models using random forest, XGBoost, and extra trees classifier, optimizing hyperparameters via grid search with stratified 4-fold cross-validation (without patient overlap between folds). The hyperparameter combination that achieved the highest average AUROC was then used to retrain the corresponding model on the full training and validation sets before final testing on the test set. The same cross-validation folds were used throughout training and validation. To improve performance and address the weakness of each model,43,44 we developed a soft-voting ensemble classifier by averaging outputs of the three individual models, which was used as the final model. Table S11 summarizes the performance of the individual machine learning models before ensembling, including cross-validation results (mean ± standard deviation across the 4-folds) and test-set performance, while Table S12 presents the hyperparameters selected during cross-validation for each model.

We used the term “dynamic” to describe our modeling strategy that integrates progressively updated clinical and biosignal data at three time points (preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative within 48 h). This approach reflects how clinical decision-making evolves across perioperative stages, rather than implying real-time data streaming or model re-estimation. Accordingly, we developed ML models across three consecutive perioperative phases—Model 1, using only preoperative variables; Model 2, using preoperative, intraoperative, and immediate postoperative variables assessed within 1 h of intensive care unit (ICU) admission; Model 3, using preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables (approximately 48 h postoperatively)—to evaluate predictive performance and identify key features for prediction at each phase (Table S9). All model input variables were restricted to data collected within the first 48 h after surgery, ensuring that no postoperative information obtained after pAKI determination was used for prediction. Consistent with this outcome definition, Model 3 was designed to support early postoperative risk stratification by integrating perioperative and early postoperative variables collected within the first 48 h after surgery. The outcome of interest was pAKI.

Performance evaluation and interpretation

Performance metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, negative predictive value [NPV], and F1 score) were calculated using the threshold at the peak of the Youden J index, determined during cross-validation.45 We compared the performance of our model with the Thakar Score (also known as the Cleveland Clinic Score), a tool for predicting acute renal failure requiring dialysis after cardiac surgery,46 using a threshold of 4 at the peak of the Youden J index for dichotomization. We also evaluated the reclassification capability of our models compared with the Thakar Score, as well as between our models, using the NRI.47

Calibration performance was assessed by comparing predicted probabilities with observed event rates across quantile-based bins. Calibration curves were generated by plotting the mean predicted probability against the corresponding observed proportion of events within each bin. Overall calibration was quantified using the Brier score and the ECE, with lower values indicating better agreement between predicted and observed risks. Decision curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of the models across a range of threshold probabilities. Net benefit was calculated by weighing true positives and false positives at each threshold, and each model’s net benefit was compared with the default strategies of treating all patients and treating none.

The SHAP method was applied to interpret individual model predictions and quantify the relative contribution of each variable to the model’s predictive output.48 An SHAP dependence plot was generated for key variables to visualize how feature values were associated with changes in the model’s predicted risk, wherein the x and y axes represented the actual values of the feature and corresponding SHAP values, respectively. Features with SHAP values exceeding zero were associated with an increased predicted risk of pAKI within the model. A PDP was also generated to depict the marginal association between each feature and the model’s predicted risk by averaging predictions over the distribution of all other variables; in this plot, the y axis represents the partial dependence value, indicating the direction and magnitude of each feature’s contribution and corresponding to the model’s average predicted outcome at each feature value.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, the two-sample t test was used for normally distributed variables, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons among three or more groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Results are presented as “mean (standard deviation)” for normally distributed variables and “median [IQR]” for non-normally distributed variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The 95% CIs for the AUROC were calculated using DeLong’s method.49 Pairwise AUROC comparisons between each ML model and the Thakar Score were performed using the DeLong test (paired, two-sided).49 Given three pre-specified comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in a corrected significance threshold of p < 0.017. For subgroup analyses, between-group AUROC comparisons were conducted using the unpaired, two-sided DeLong test.49 The 95% CIs for the AUPRC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1-score, and NRI were estimated using 2,000 bootstrap samples, reporting the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. As a pre-specified secondary analysis, we conducted a MLoR to assess the association between model predictions and a real-world outcome—IHM. We adjusted for the EuroSCORE II, which is a tool for predicting the risk of IHM following cardiac surgery. Specifically, we performed MLoR with IHM as the dependent variable and the EuroSCORE II and ML model output as independent variables. IHM was defined as death from any cause during the index hospitalization, based on EMR data.
