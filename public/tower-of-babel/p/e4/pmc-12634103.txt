Socioeconomic inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality by sex/gender and age in Canada: a 13-year population-representative cohort study
Authors: Alessandra T Andreacchi, Erin Hobin, Arjumand Siddiqi, Brendan T Smith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividuals with low socioeconomic position (SEP) experience greater rates of alcohol-attributable mortality, contributing to health inequities in mortality and life expectancy. We examined the association between SEP and alcohol-attributable mortality by sex/gender and age in Canada. Census records from the 2006 Canadian Census Health and Environment Cohort (ages 12+; n = 5 038 790) were linked to mortality data from 2006 to 2019. SEP was measured by educational attainment and household income. Poisson and Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models estimated rate differences (RDs) per 100 000 person-years and hazard ratios (HRs). Both educational attainment and household income were inversely associated with alcohol-attributable mortality. Absolute SEP inequities were greater among men than women, with an RD of 30.81 (95% CI, 28.04-33.57) for men and 9.86 (95% CI, 8.49-11.22) for women when comparing the lowest to the highest income quintile. Age-stratified analyses showed absolute SEP inequities were most pronounced in middle and older adulthood, above age 30 for women and age 50 for men, with smaller RDs in ages 12 to 29. Relative SEP inequities were similar in women and men, with greater HRs at younger ages. Public health policies addressing social determinants and population-level alcohol policies should consider patterning of SEP inequities by sex/gender and age group.Keywords: health inequities, socioeconomic position, alcohol, alcohol-attributable mortality, CanCHEC, gender, aging

Introduction

Low socioeconomic position (SEP) is associated with an increased risk of alcohol-attributable mortality, contributing substantially to broader health inequities in mortality and life expectancy.1-7 Despite similar or lower reported alcohol use among individuals with lower compared to high SEP, they experience a disproportionate burden of alcohol-attributable harm, a phenomenon known as the “alcohol harm paradox.”3,8 The mechanisms underlying this paradox are complex and poorly understood.8,9 While research has predominantly focused on SEP differences in alcohol use and related lifestyle risk factors (eg, smoking), these factors only partially attenuate but do not fully explain the alcohol harm paradox.8,9 Additional hypothesized mechanisms include differential access to health care, variations in social support, and cumulative risk factors such as persistent disadvantage or stress, which may exacerbate the harmful effects of alcohol over time.9

To unravel the alcohol harm paradox, it is crucial to examine how socioeconomic inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality are patterned across different population segments, particularly by sex, gender, and age group. Alcohol use and attributable mortality exhibit distinct patterns across the life course. While alcohol use peaks in young adulthood around age 25, rates of alcohol-attributable mortality are greatest in older ages, beyond age 50.10-14 Heavy and chronic alcohol use over many years can lead to a range of health problems (eg, liver cirrhosis, cardiovascular diseases) that manifest later in life, contributing to higher mortality rates observed in older adults.15,16 Conversely, alcohol use also causes immediate and acute harm (eg, injuries, poisoning).15,16 Moreover, sex and gender differences are also evident, with men exhibiting greater rates of alcohol use and attributable mortality than women.14,17,18 Yet, little is known about the specific life stages when socioeconomic inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality are most pronounced. To comprehend these dynamics, socioeconomic contexts such as income and education must be considered alongside sex- and gender-specific factors that influence the risk of alcohol-attributable mortality across various life stages. This approach could provide insights into the cumulative risk factor hypothesis, such that individuals with lower SEP may accumulate greater risks over the life course due to cumulative experiences of disadvantage.9,19 By examining these inequities across the life course, we can offer valuable insights into the mechanisms contributing to the alcohol harm paradox.

Research from Canada and other high-income countries suggests that the relative association between low SEP and alcohol-attributable mortality is similar or stronger among men compared to women.2,20 However, fewer studies have assessed absolute differences, which are more meaningful for public health.21 Understanding both relative and absolute differences in the association between SEP and alcohol-attributable mortality is crucial: relative measures indicate the strength of association, while absolute measures provide a clearer picture of the actual burden by accounting for baseline rates. Together, these measures can guide more effective public health interventions. Moreover, research on the association between SEP and alcohol-attributable mortality by age group is limited, with most studies focusing on various measures of alcohol-related harm (eg, health care encounters, the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test scores) rather than mortality.22-25 Existing findings generally indicate larger relative SEP inequities in alcohol-related harm among younger individuals.22-25 Given that mortality is a critical indicator of the population-level burden due to alcohol, understanding these associations across different population segments is crucial to inform policies that seek to reduce alcohol-attributable mortality and related inequities.

This study aims to address these gaps in the literature by examining the association between SEP and alcohol-attributable mortality by sex, gender, and age group in Canada. Leveraging population-representative Canadian Census data linked to mortality records, encompassing over 5 million respondents, this study will estimate both relative and absolute measures to provide comprehensive insights into these associations. By describing the patterning of SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality across the life course, this study has the potential to inform evidence-based public health policies to mitigate alcohol-attributable health inequities and promote health equity for all segments of the population.

Methods

Data and study population

We conducted a population-representative cohort study using data from the 2006 Canadian Census Health and Environment Cohort (CanCHEC), which is a population-representative data set of 5.9 million Canadians who completed the 2006 long-form Census (~20% of Canadian households).26,27 The Canadian Census, mandatory and conducted every 5 years, provides a comprehensive demographic, social, and economic portrait of the country.26,27 The 2006 Census included residents of Canada aged 0+ who were Canadian citizens, landed immigrants, or nonpermanent residents. The institutional population (eg, those living in nursing homes, penitentiaries, and group homes) was not eligible.27 The CanCHEC data set was created by Statistics Canada through an individual-level record linkage between long-form Census records and the Canadian Vital Statistics—Death Database (CVSD), a data set that collects cause-of-death information from all provincial and territorial vital statistics registries in Canada.28,29 In 2006, the long-form Census had a response rate of 93.8%, and approximately 91% of these records were successfully linked to the Derived Record Depository in the Social Data Linkage Environment at Statistics Canada using probabilistic linkage methods.27,28 The CVSD was also linked to this environment based on surname, given names, birthdate, and geography, with a linkage rate of 99.8%.28 Linkage rates varied by sociodemographic characteristics, and Statistics Canada provided survey weights to account for any resulting sampling bias.29

A total of 5 871 337 residents of Canada aged 0+ on Census Day (May 16, 2006) with successful linkage to mortality records until December 31, 2019, were eligible for inclusion in the 2006 CanCHEC.26 We excluded respondents < 12 years old (n = 832 547) from our study population, as alcohol-attributable mortality was rare in this age group, and to align with the general age range studied in alcohol research.30 The final study population with no missing data included 5 038 790 Census respondents aged 12+ with successfully linked mortality records. Ethical clearance was received from the University of Toronto (protocol number: 44658).

Measures

The outcome was alcohol-attributable mortality, defined using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes from the Canadian Institute for Health Information indicator for causes that are 100% attributable to alcohol use (Appendix S1).31 Alcohol-attributable mortality was identified in the CVSD when a 100% alcohol-attributable code was listed as the underlying or contributing cause of death as contributing cause of death codes contain valuable information on alcohol-specific conditions.32 This approach captures deaths where alcohol played a role in a chain of events leading to death as death certificates often fail to capture the contribution of alcohol to mortality.32-34

The exposure was SEP, captured using 2 distinct indicators. (1) Household income was measured using total after-tax household income adjusted for household composition using a Statistics Canada scale. The scale assigns a factor of 1 to the oldest household member, then decreasing values to subsequent household members.35 Household income was adjusted by dividing the household’s income by the sum of the factors and was grouped into quintiles ranging from quintile 1(lowest) to quintile 5 (highest). (2) Educational attainment was assessed at the individual level for respondents aged ≥ 30 and at the household level for respondents aged < 30, in line with previous Canadian work on SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable harm.20 Educational attainment was categorized into 4 levels: less than high school; high school diploma/some postsecondary; trades, college or nonuniversity certificate, diploma or degree below bachelor’s degree; and bachelor’s degree or above.

We stratified our analyses according to variables collected in the Census at baseline, including sex (females, males) and age on Census Day grouped based on age-related developmental stages with unique drinking patterns in line with literature on alcohol use and harm across the life course (12-17, 18-29, 30-49, 50-64, 65+).30,36 Extensive research suggests that sex-based biological differences (including differences in alcohol absorption, distribution, and metabolism) and gender-related sociocultural factors (including alcohol marketing tactics, gender roles, norms, relations, identities, and institutionalized gender) influence alcohol use and attributable harm.37,38 While the Census only measures sex, we interpret our findings as the influence of both sex and gender and use the terms “sex/gender,” “women,” and “men.” We included additional covariates selected a priori, which were conceptualized as potential confounders not on the causal pathway. These covariates included immigrant status (born in Canada, born outside Canada), marital status (married/common law, separated/divorced/widowed, single/never married), rurality (urban, rural), and region of Canada (eastern, central, western, northern). Information on alcohol use or other lifestyle risk factors was not collected in the Census.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were stratified by sex/gender. Descriptive statistics were conducted to characterize the study population, including proportions of respondents, the number of alcohol-attributable deaths, and crude rates of alcohol-attributable mortality across baseline study covariates.

Rates of alcohol-attributable mortality were estimated using Poisson regression with robust standard error, including person-years (PYs) of follow-up as an offset.39 To assess absolute SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality, Poisson regression with robust standard error was used to estimate rate differences (RDs) between each lower category compared to the highest SEP category, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated using the SAS macro “NLMeans.”40 To assess relative SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality, Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard regression with competing risk (non-alcohol-attributable death) was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs between each lower relative category to the highest SEP category. The proportional hazard assumption was checked using Schoenfeld residuals and by testing the effect of each covariate over time using interaction terms. SEP indicators of education and household income were examined using separate models. All models were stratified by sex and then subsequently by age group. Unadjusted and covariate-adjusted models were estimated for all models, including age (continuous), age2, immigrant status, marital status, rurality, and region.

In addition to the primary analysis, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of the association between educational attainment and alcohol-attributable mortality by restricting the sample to individuals aged 30 and older. This adjustment was made to consider potential misclassification from using household education for respondents aged < 30.

Analyses were conducted in SAS Enterprise Guide version 8.1 (SAS Institute). All analyses applied Statistics Canada normalized survey weights, a weight variable directly included in all analytical procedures, enabling the study population to reflect the eligible Census population.27,41

Results

Study population

The study population was 51.3% women (mean age: 44.1) and 48.7% men (mean age: 42.7) at baseline (Table 1). Between 2006 and 2019, there were 3795 alcohol-attributable deaths among women (rate: 9.50 per 100 000 PYs; 95% CI, 9.14-9.87), where an alcohol-attributable ICD-10 code was listed as the underlying or contributing cause of death. The 3 most common underlying causes of an alcohol-attributable death in women were K703—alcoholic cirrhosis of the liver (21.3% of all alcohol-attributable deaths), F102—mental and behavioral disorders due to use of alcohol dependence syndrome (5.8%), and K704—hepatic liver failure (5.4%). Among men, there were 11 265 alcohol-attributable deaths (rate: 30.73 per 100 000 PYs; 95% CI, 30.06-31.41), where an alcohol-attributable ICD-10 code was listed as the underlying or contributing cause of death. The 3 most common underlying causes of alcohol-attributable deaths in men were K703—alcoholic cirrhosis of the liver (23.5%); F102—mental and behavioral disorders due to use of alcohol, dependence syndrome (7.0%); and I251—atherosclerotic heart disease without angina pectoris (4.2%). Rates of alcohol-attributable mortality were higher among older individuals; those who were separated, divorced, or widowed; individuals born in Canada; residents of rural and northern Canada; and those with lower household incomes and educational attainment.

Household income inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality by sex/gender

Among women and men, lower household income was associated with greater rates of alcohol-attributable mortality, with disproportionately greater rates in the lowest income quintile (Table 2). For women, the covariate-adjusted rate per 100 000 PYs in the lowest quintile was 15.24 (95% CI, 13.67-16.98) compared to 5.38 (95% CI, 4.63-6.26) in the highest quintile. The covariate-adjusted RD per 100 000 PYs between these quintiles was 9.86 (95% CI, 8.49-11.22), and the covariate-adjusted HR was 2.84 (95% CI, 2.51-3.23). Men in the lowest quintile had a covariate-adjusted rate per 100 000 PYs of 49.39 (95% CI, 46.18-52.83) compared to 18.58 (95% CI, 17.11-20.19) in the highest quintile. The covariate-adjusted RD per 100 000 PYs between these quintiles was 30.81 (95% CI, 28.04-33.57), and the covariate-adjusted HR was 2.68 (95% CI, 2.49-2.87). In summary, covariate-adjusted absolute RD estimates were larger for men, while relative HR estimates were similar for men and women. Unadjusted absolute and relative estimates are in Appendix S2.

Educational inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality by sex/gender

Among women and men, lower educational attainment demonstrated a similar pattern, with disproportionately greater rates of alcohol-attributable mortality in those with less than a high school education (Table 3). For women with less than a high school education, the covariate-adjusted rate per 100 000 PYs was 13.55 (95% CI, 12.10-15.19) compared to 4.96 (95% CI, 4.25-5.78) for women with a bachelor’s degree or above. The covariate-adjusted RD per 100 000 PYs between these levels was 8.60 (95% CI, 7.18-10.01), and the covariate-adjusted HR was 2.74 (95% CI, 2.36-3.18). Men with less than a high school education had a covariate-adjusted rate per 100 000 PYs of 45.09 (95% CI, 42.00-48.42) compared to 15.27 (95% CI, 13.94-16.73) for men with a bachelor’s degree or above. The covariate-adjusted RD per 100 000 PYs was 29.82 (95% CI, 27.00-32.64), and the covariate-adjusted HR was 2.97 (95% CI, 2.73-3.24). Unadjusted absolute and relative estimates are in Appendix S3. The sensitivity analysis, which restricted the study population to those aged 30 and older, revealed slightly larger absolute educational inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality, with similar relative inequities for women and men, suggesting that potential misclassification from using household education for individuals under 30 had a minimal influence (Appendix S4).

Household income inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality by age and sex/gender

Sex/gender-stratified absolute household income inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality were patterned by age (Figure 1; Appendix S5 and S6 for tables). For women, covariate-adjusted RDs per 100 000 PYs for alcohol-attributable mortality comparing quintile 1 to quintile 5 were greatest in ages 50 to 64 (RD: 27.38; 95% CI, 21.44-33.34), followed by ages 30 to 49 (RD: 11.28; 95% CI, 8.64-13.91), and was lowest in ages 12 to 17 (RD: 1.72; 95% CI, 0.04-3.41) (Figure 1A). RDs for quintiles 2 to 4 compared to quintile 5 were smaller yet showed a similar age pattern. Covariate-adjusted HRs among women were greatest in ages 18 to 29 (Figure 2A; Appendix S5 for tables). For men, covariate-adjusted RDs per 100 000 PYs for alcohol-attributable mortality comparing quintile 1 to quintile 5 were greatest in ages 50 to 64 (RD: 89.27; 95% CI, 76.99-101.55) and ages 65+ (RD: 92.17; 95% CI, 72.50-111.85) and lowest in ages 18 to 29 (RD: 7.28; 95% CI, 2.66-11.90) (Figure 1B). RDs for quintiles 2 to 4 compared to quintile 5 showed the same age pattern. Covariate-adjusted HRs among men were greater at older ages (Figure 2B; Appendix S6 for tables).

Educational inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality by age and sex/gender

Age patterns persisted in sex/gender-stratified absolute educational inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality (Figure 3; Appendix S7 and S8 for tables). For women, covariate-adjusted RDs per 100 000 PYs for alcohol-attributable mortality comparing those with less than a high school diploma to a bachelor’s degree or above were greatest in ages 30 to 49 (RD: 16.53; 95% CI, 12.93-20.12) and ages 50 to 64 (RD: 17.09; 95% CI, 12.04-22.14) and lowest in ages 12 to 17 (RD: 3.87; 95% CI, –0.04 to 7.79) (Figure 3a). RDs comparing those with a high school diploma and those with a trade or college certificate/diploma to a bachelor’s degree or above were smaller and showed an age pattern with greater RDs above age 30. Covariate-adjusted HRs among women were greatest under age 50 when comparing less than a high school diploma to a bachelor’s degree or above (Figure 4a; Appendix S7 for tables). For men, covariate-adjusted RDs for alcohol-attributable mortality comparing those with less than a high school diploma to a bachelor’s degree or above were greatest in ages 50 to 64 (RD: 82.50; 95% CI, 70.89-94.11) and 65+ (RD: 56.81; 95% CI, 41.93-71.69) and lowest in ages 12 to 17 (RD: 14.57; 95% CI, 6.82-22.33) (Figure 3b). RDs showed a similar age pattern when comparing those with a high school diploma and those with a trade or college certificate/diploma to a bachelor’s degree or above and were greatest above age 50. Covariate-adjusted HRs among men were greatest in ages 30 to 49 (Figure 4b; Appendix S8 for tables).

Discussion

In a population-representative cohort of over 5 million Canadian Census respondents with 13 years of mortality follow-up, we found an inverse association between lower SEP and alcohol-attributable mortality, using household income and educational attainment as SEP indicators. These SEP inequities were more pronounced among men on the absolute scale but similar between men and women on the relative scale, likely reflecting the higher baseline mortality rate in men. Furthermore, sex/gender-stratified SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality were patterned by age, with interpretations contingent on the scale used. Among women, absolute SEP inequities were greatest above age 30, while relative inequities were more prominent at younger ages. Among men, absolute SEP inequities increased with age, particularly above age 50. Conversely, among men, relative educational inequities were greatest in ages 30 to 49, and relative income inequities increased with age. These findings underscore the disproportionate burden of alcohol-attributable mortality among those with lower SEP in middle and older adulthood, particularly among men who experience far greater rates than women, emphasizing the intricate interplay between sex/gender, age, and socioeconomic factors.

Our study confirms the strong association between lower SEP and alcohol-attributable mortality, consistent with established literature from high-income countries.2,8 However, our contribution emphasizes the need to incorporate both absolute and relative effect measures for a comprehensive interpretation of health inequities. Prior studies have predominantly used relative effect measures, potentially influenced by implicit normative judgments regarding their perceived importance.2,8,42 Our findings show that these measures can be misleading and may lead to divergent conclusions or policy recommendations due to their dependence on the baseline prevalence (or rate) of the outcome, with estimates approaching the null as the baseline prevalence increases.43,44 By integrating absolute effect measures, we demonstrate that low SEP has a greater impact on alcohol-attributable mortality among men, as absolute inequities are larger in men despite relative inequities being similar between women and men. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of health inequities necessitates considering both absolute and relative effect measures.

We further contribute to the literature by uncovering that the most pronounced sex/gender-specific absolute SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality occur in middle and older adulthood. This contrasts with previous studies, which tend to show larger relative inequities in younger age groups, where the baseline rates of alcohol-attributable mortality are low (ie, < 10% of all alcohol-attributable deaths occurred in people under 29 years of age).22-24 Our findings confirm greater relative inequities in younger age groups and identify absolute SEP inequities starting between ages 12 and 29, although smaller in magnitude than in older ages.22-24 This underscores the importance of addressing social determinants early in life through public health policies. Moreover, best-buy population-level alcohol policies are effective in reducing overall harm (eg, controlling alcohol availability, price, and advertising) and may have differential impacts on population segments by gender, age, and SEP.45-47 Further research into these potential differential impacts can help align policies to mitigate SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality, particularly among those disproportionately affected and younger age groups.

We observed sex/gender differences in the age patterns of SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality. Women began experiencing the most pronounced absolute inequities around age 30, while men showed the greatest inequities around age 50 and above. This suggests the impact of low SEP manifests earlier in life for women than men, potentially due to different experiences of cumulative disadvantage or greater barriers to health care or addiction treatment.48 This finding also reflects the evidence that women experience alcohol-attributable harms sooner and at lower levels of alcohol use than men, which may be due to physiological differences in alcohol absorption and metabolism, as well as social norms influencing how alcohol affects women differently.18,37

Our findings of greater absolute SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality in middle and older adulthood support the cumulative risk factor hypothesis for understanding the alcohol harm paradox.9,19,20 Despite similar or less alcohol use, individuals with lower SEP may be more vulnerable to alcohol’s harmful effects due to cumulative disadvantages experienced throughout their lives, including prolonged exposure to stressors and deteriorating health over time.9,19 This vulnerability may be exacerbated by reduced access to health care and the potential loss of social networks and support systems associated with life course events like retirement and employment transitions, which can vary by SEP.9 The higher overall rate of alcohol-attributable deaths in older adults also contributes to widening the absolute gap in mortality rates between SEP groups, even if the relative rates remain constant. Although lower SEP individuals exhibit similar or lower average alcohol use, some evidence suggests greater alcohol use among lower education groups in midlife, particularly among men, coinciding with the age of the most pronounced SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality.49,50 While sex/gender- and age-specific drinking patterns contribute to the alcohol harm paradox, they do not fully explain it. As there is no single explanation for the alcohol harm paradox, future research should explore the hypothesis of cumulative risk factors and other potential causal mechanisms to better understand this phenomenon.

Our study has several strengths that support the robustness of our findings. First, we leveraged the 2006 CanCHEC, a large cohort of long-form Census respondents, enabling the precise assessment of SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality by sex/gender and age. Second, using Census data provides a reliable, representative sample, reducing the risk of selection bias common in many health surveys. Third, the application of survey weights ensures the generalizability of our findings to the eligible Canadian Census population. Lastly, linking Census data to mortality records allows for the measurement of individual-level SEP, a significant strength compared to studies that rely only on administrative records without such detailed information.

However, some limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, our outcome was based on 100% alcohol-attributable mortality, which underestimates the true mortality burden caused by alcohol as it does not account for deaths resulting from alcohol-related violence or where alcohol played a role but was not explicitly mentioned on the death certificate.33,51 To mitigate against this, we considered ICD-10 codes listed as the underlying or contributing cause of death. Furthermore, our outcome definition does not include partial alcohol-attributable mortality where alcohol may have contributed to the death (eg, cancer, injuries), as these deaths may only be estimated at the population level using population-attributable fraction methods.45,52 Second, although we used the 2006 Census, which is mandatory and population representative, it excludes certain demographic groups, such as the institutionalized population, who are potentially at greater risk of alcohol-related mortality, leading to a possible underestimation of our results.53 Third, we could not evaluate changes in SEP over time given our data. Although educational attainment tends to remain unchanged beyond young adulthood, not accounting for changes in SEP over the study period could influence our findings.

In conclusion, we observed SEP inequities in alcohol-attributable mortality in Canada. Absolute inequities were most pronounced in men and those in middle to older adulthood, while relative inequities were generally similar between women and men and varied by age. This highlights the importance of distinguishing between absolute and relative measures when interpreting health inequities. Our findings also support the theory that cumulative risk factors contribute to the alcohol harm paradox. This underscores the urgent need for public health policies that address the social determinants of health and target SEP inequities beyond those explained by alcohol use. Furthermore, maintaining and strengthening population-level alcohol policies is crucial, as they reduce overall harm by curbing per capita alcohol use and can disproportionately benefit individuals with low SEP.45-47
