Reproducibility of 3.0 T single-frequency cardiac MR elastography for quantification of left ventricular myocardial stiffness
Authors: Jun Wang, Yong Cheng, Yewen Jiang, Zijun Zheng, Zhanglei Li, Zhen Wang, Shutian An, Jun Chen, Kevin J Glaser, Arvin Arani, Yongqiang Yu, Ren Zhao, Xiaohu Li, MD, PhD
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) allows the non-invasive quantification of myocardial stiffness, but long-interval reproducibility data at 3.0 T single-frequency 140-Hz remain limited.PurposeTo evaluate the short- and long-interval reproducibility of 3.0 T cardiac MRE for left ventricular (LV) myocardial stiffness across healthy volunteers and patients with conditions associated with elevated myocardial stiffness.Materials and MethodsThis prospective single-center study included 69 participants: 60 healthy volunteers and 9 with cardiovascular disease (6 with aortic stenosis and 3 with long-standing hypertension). All participants underwent baseline cardiac MRE (scanner: Signa Premier 3.0 T, GE Healthcare, driver: Resoundant), an immediate short-interval rescan (SIR) within 5 minutes, and a long-interval rescan (LIR) after approximately 1 month. Left ventricular myocardial stiffness and octahedral shear strain signal-to-noise ratio were measured independently by 2 readers. Reproducibility was assessed using Bland-Altman analysis, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), within-subject coefficient of variation, standard error of measurement, and smallest detectable change at the 95% confidence (SDC95) level.ResultsBaseline LV myocardial stiffness was higher in the disease group than in healthy volunteers, 11.73 ± 1.01 kPa and 7.80 ± 0.70 kPa (mean ± SD; P < .001), respectively. Inter-reader agreement was excellent across all timepoints, with ICCs of 0.970 at baseline (95% CI, 0.953-0.982), 0.966 for SIR (95% CI, 0.945-0.979), and 0.962 for LIR (95% CI, 0.939-0.976). Within-reader scan-rescan reproducibility was also excellent. For Reader 1, stiffness ICC was 0.979 for the SIR (95% CI, 0.966-0.987) and 0.957 for the LIR (95% CI, 0.932-0.973), with SDC95 values of 0.616 kPa (95% CI, 0.528-0.741) and 0.869 kPa (95% CI, 0.744-1.044), respectively. Reader 2 showed excellent reproducibility, with ICCs ≤0.943.Conclusions3D single-frequency 140-Hz cardiac MRE at 3.0 T provides excellent short- and long-interval reproducibility for LV myocardial stiffness quantification. The SDC95 values may help distinguish true longitudinal stiffness changes from measurement variability.

3.0 T single-frequency 140-Hz cardiac MR elastography provides excellent short- and long-interval reproducibility for the non-invasive quantification of left ventricular myocardial stiffness and may support longitudinal assessment of myocardial mechanical remodeling.

Introduction

Myocardial stiffness is an important determinant of cardiac function and has been implicated in a range of cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and other causes of diastolic dysfunction.1,2 Increased myocardial stiffness is closely associated with impaired ventricular filling and has become an important target in conditions where left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction may remain preserved despite clinically relevant mechanical impairment.3,4 The reference standard for assessing myocardial chamber stiffness involves invasive pressure-volume loop analysis during cardiac catheterization, an invasive approach with procedural risks.5

Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) provides a non-invasive, contrast-free alternative by using externally applied mechanical vibrations to generate shear waves within tissue. The resulting displacement fields are measured with motion-encoded MRI and converted into stiffness maps using inversion algorithms.6,7 Compared with conventional cardiac MR techniques that assess morphology, function, or tissue composition indirectly, cardiac MRE provides a direct mechanical estimate of myocardial stiffness.8–10 Multifrequency approaches may additionally characterize both stiffness and viscosity.11–13

Previous cardiac MRE studies have demonstrated feasibility and short-term reproducibility, but long-interval reproducibility data for 3.0 T single-frequency 140-Hz cardiac MRE remain limited. Existing reproducibility studies have largely focused on healthy volunteers, leaving measurement reliability across a broader stiffness range incompletely characterized. Therefore, this prospective study assesses short-interval and long-interval reproducibility of 3.0 T single-frequency cardiac MRE for quantification of LV myocardial stiffness in healthy volunteers and patients with conditions associated with elevated myocardial stiffness.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective study was approved by the institutional review board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (PJ 2024-06-90), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Recruitment took place between May 2024 and April 2026 at a single institution.

Healthy volunteers were eligible if they had no personal or family history of cardiovascular disease. Normotensive status was confirmed by blood pressure measurement at the time of baseline scan. Available transthoracic echocardiography performed before enrollment was reviewed to confirm the absence of structural or functional cardiac abnormalities. Patients were identified by a cardiologist and enrolled if they met predefined clinical criteria for cardiovascular conditions associated with increased myocardial stiffness, specifically long-standing hypertension and aortic stenosis. Long-standing hypertension was defined as office blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg documented on at least 2 separate visits or current use of antihypertensive medication. Aortic stenosis was defined echocardiographically as an aortic jet velocity ≥2 m/s. These conditions were selected because both are associated with pressure overload, progressive LV hypertrophy, and increased myocardial stiffness.

Exclusion criteria for all participants included: (1) standard contraindications to MRI, including implanted cardiac devices and severe claustrophobia; (2) inability to perform adequate breath-holding for MRE acquisition; (3) any change in clinical status, medication regimen, or new cardiovascular event, including cardiac decompensation or hospitalization for heart failure, between baseline and long-interval rescan (LIR); (4) incomplete MRE acquisition at any of the scheduled timepoints.

To minimize physiological variability, all participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise and stimulant-containing beverages or foods, including coffee, tea, and chocolate, on the day of imaging. A 30-minute rest period before scanning was required to promote hemodynamic stability.

All participants underwent a baseline examination and an immediate short-interval rescan (SIR) within 5 minutes. Participants then returned for the LIR after more than 1 month.

MRE acquisition parameters

All examinations were performed on a 3.0 T MR scanner (Signa Premier 3.0 T, GE Healthcare) in an oblique slice orientation to obtain short-axis cardiac MRE images using a phase-contrast sequence with motion-encoding gradients and an AIR body coil array (GE Healthcare). Imaging parameters were as follows: spin-echo acquisition; repetition time synchronized to each participant’s heart rate via ECG gating; echo time = 69 ms; field of view 32 × 32 cm; acquisition matrix 64 × 64; parallel imaging acceleration factor of 2; 15 contiguous 5 mm-thick slices; 2 motion-encoding gradient pairs on each side of the refocusing pulse, matched to the vibration frequency; motion-encoding gradient directions along the x, y, and z axes, with a null encoding direction serving as a phase reference; and 4 phase offsets evenly distributed over one vibration period at 140 Hz. Images were acquired at the shortest possible delay following the R-wave ECG trigger (approximately 100 ms), corresponding to the most reproducible phase of the cardiac cycle. For both rescans, the passive driver was initially placed at the same anatomical position; following the localizer scan, placement was verified and adjusted if necessary to match the geometry of the original acquisition. Each cardiac MRE examination required approximately 5 minutes, including approximately 10-15 breath-holds of 12-20 seconds each, depending on heart rate and breath-hold duration.

Cardiac MRE was used to assess LV myocardial stiffness. An external active driver (Resoundant, Rochester, MN, United States) generated acoustic vibrations, which were transmitted through a flexible tube to a passive driver placed in direct contact with the participant. The passive driver surface was coated with acoustic gel and positioned adjacent to the left of the sternum, superior to the xiphoid process. An elastic tension strap secured the passive driver to enhance coupling between the driver diaphragm and the participant’s skin. Cardiac gating was performed using ECG leads placed on the posterior aspect of the left shoulder.

MRE postprocessing and image analysis

Wave inversion was performed using a 3D local frequency estimation (LFE) algorithm applied to the curl of the 3-dimensional displacement field, to isolate shear wave components and suppress compressional wave contamination.14 Postprocessing and LV segmentation were performed using commercial software (Cvi42 version 5.16.2; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., Calgary, Canada). Two readers performed independent segmentation and measurement: Reader 1 (a radiologist with 13 years of experience) and Reader 2 (a radiologist with 4 years of experience). Before analysis, all datasets were anonymized and assigned random study codes. The order of segmentation was randomized across participants and scan timepoints. Both readers were blinded to each other’s measurements and to the scan timepoint during analysis.

The LV myocardial boundary was first identified on the magnitude map, where myocardial contours are most clearly defined, and the region of interest (ROI) was delineated accordingly. To minimize partial-volume contamination from the adjacent blood pool, ROI boundaries were adjusted inward by approximately 10% toward the myocardial center. Regions of interest were then co-registered to the corresponding elastogram and OSS-SNR maps, from which mean LV stiffness (kPa) and mean OSS-SNR were extracted. OSS-SNR was calculated from the curl wave fields. An acquisition was considered technically adequate if mean OSS-SNR exceeded 1.17, as previously established for cardiac MRE applications.15 Representative ROI delineation for a healthy volunteer and a patient with aortic stenosis is illustrated in Figure 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) according to data distribution. Between-group differences in demographic and clinical characteristics were evaluated using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Scan-to-scan reproducibility was assessed for each reader independently across 2 intervals: the SIR (within 5 minutes) and the LIR (approximately 1-1.5 months). Inter-reader agreement was evaluated at all 3 timepoints (baseline, SIR, and LIR). The following metrics were computed for both myocardial stiffness and OSS-SNR: Bland-Altman analysis with mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA); intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)—a 2-way mixed-effects model (absolute agreement) for intra-observer reproducibility and a 2-way random-effects model (absolute agreement) for inter-reader agreement, consistent with the Shrout & Fleiss ICC taxonomy; within-subject coefficient of variation (wCV); standard error of measurement (SEM); and smallest detectable change at 95% confidence (SDC95 = 1.962×SEM), representing the minimum change distinguishable from measurement error at the 95% confidence level.

To determine whether measurement error increased with a longer interscan interval, absolute and relative differences between paired scans were compared between SIR and LIR using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Reproducibility metrics were additionally computed separately for the healthy volunteer and disease subgroups to assess consistency across the stiffness range. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 72 participants were initially recruited, including 61 healthy volunteers and 11 patients with cardiovascular disease. Three participants, including 1 healthy volunteer and 2 patients, were subsequently excluded because they were lost to follow-up and did not complete the LIR. Consequently, 69 participants were ultimately included in the analysis, comprising 60 healthy volunteers and 9 patients (6 with aortic stenosis and 3 with long-standing hypertension). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The healthy volunteer and disease groups were comparable with respect to sex, weight, height, and BMI, but differed significantly in age, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, LV ejection fraction (LVEF), and indexed myocardial mass. The mean interval between baseline imaging and the LIR was 44.4 ± 8.5 days and did not differ significantly between groups. Because of the small disease subgroup, patients with aortic stenosis and long-standing hypertension were pooled for primary analyses; disease-specific descriptive results are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Inter-observer reproducibility

Inter-reader agreement was excellent for myocardial stiffness across all 3 timepoints and good to excellent for OSS-SNR (Table 2). At baseline, the ICC was 0.97 [0.95, 0.98] for stiffness and 0.94 [0.91, 0.97] for OSS-SNR, with minimal mean bias for both metrics (Figure 2). Proportional bias was detected for stiffness and OSS-SNR; however, the proportional bias for stiffness explained only a small proportion of inter-reader variance. Most paired differences fell within the 95% limits of agreement (Figure 3). Stiffness ICCs remained stable at SIR and LIR, whereas OSS-SNR ICCs decreased modestly at later timepoints.

Comparison of short- and long-interval measurement error

Absolute and relative errors are summarized in Table 4, with distributions in Figure 5. For R1, absolute stiffness error increased significantly from SIR to LIR (0.207 [0.095, 0.329] vs. 0.311 [0.186, 0.504] kPa; P = .003), suggesting a physiological contribution to long-term variability. For R2, no significant SIR-to-LIR increase was observed (0.389 [0.202, 0.619] vs. 0.422 [0.159, 0.692] kPa; P = .948), consistent with short-term technical variability dominating at both intervals. Discussion

Principal findings

This prospective study demonstrates that 3D single-frequency 140-Hz cardiac MRE at 3.0 T provides excellent short-interval and long-interval reproducibility for non-invasive quantification of LV myocardial stiffness. Within-reader reproducibility was excellent over the short interval and remained high over the long interval of more than 1 month. Inter-reader agreement was also excellent across all timepoints. Mean baseline stiffness in healthy volunteers was consistent with previously reported normative values,16,17 whereas patients with aortic stenosis or long-standing hypertension showed higher stiffness values, as expected for conditions associated with pressure overload and myocardial remodeling. These findings support the feasibility of 3.0 T single-frequency cardiac MRE for longitudinal assessment of LV myocardial stiffness.

Comparison with prior cardiac MRE reproducibility studies

The reproducibility metrics observed in this study compare favorably with prior cardiac MRE studies. Previous studies18 reported lower reproducibility metrics at 1.5 T, with a concordance correlation coefficient of 0.77 and an ICC of 0.78. The higher ICCs in our study may reflect several factors, including field strength, acquisition strategy, postprocessing workflow, reader training, and cohort composition. This improvement is consistent with the higher intrinsic SNR at 3.0 T, which enhances displacement encoding sensitivity and reduces noise-driven variability in the LFE inversion output.

Previous 3.0 T cardiac MRE studies have demonstrated high test-retest and inter-reader reproducibility in healthy volunteers and selected patient cohorts.17,19 This study adds to the existing literature by evaluating both short- and long-interval reproducibility of single-frequency 3.0 T cardiac MRE in a larger cohort and through reproducibility tests that are directly relevant to longitudinal clinical assessment. These findings support the use of 3.0 T cardiac MRE for detecting myocardial stiffness changes over time.

From a clinical trial design perspective, the SDC95 values established here—0.616 kPa (R1, SIR) and 0.869 kPa (R1, LIR)—provide the 3.0 T, 140 Hz-specific thresholds for distinguishing true biological change from measurement noise. These thresholds may inform longitudinal study design, including sample-size planning and response interpretation in future interventional trials of myocardial mechanics.20

Inter-observer agreement and standardizing segmentation protocols

The observed proportional bias for stiffness (r = −0.26, P = .033) and OSS-SNR (r = −0.67, P < .001) reflects reader-dependent variation in ROI placement or boundary judgment. The stronger bias for OSS-SNR may reflect the greater sensitivity of this metric to ROI boundary placement. However, proportional bias in stiffness accounted for only 7% of inter-reader variance (r2 = 0.068), and all stiffness measurements remained within the 95% limits of agreement, supporting practical inter-reader consistency. These findings underscore the need for standardized segmentation protocols in cardiac MRE.

Interval-dependent measurement error and physiological stability

The significant increase in absolute stiffness error from SIR to LIR for R1 (P = .003), but not for R2 (P = .948), provides an important mechanistic insight. For R1, whose short-term technical variability is low (wCV = 2.71%), the LIR increment in error likely reflects genuine physiological fluctuation in myocardial stiffness over 1 month, driven by day-to-day variation in loading conditions, autonomic tone, hydration status, and circadian hemodynamic rhythms.21,22 This interpretation is supported by the pre-scan standardization protocol (30-minute rest, avoidance of stimulants), which minimized acute confounders but could not eliminate longer-term physiological variability.

For R2, the absence of a significant SIR-to-LIR error increase reflects a ceiling effect: short-term technical variability (wCV = 4.72%) already dominates measurement error at SIR, masking any additional temporal physiological contribution at LIR. This differential pattern has direct implications for longitudinal trial design: reader standardization is as critical as scan-rescan interval control for achieving the sensitivity needed to detect true biological change. Recent studies of quantitative CMR biomarkers have similarly identified reader training as the single most impactful modifiable factor in reducing longitudinal measurement variability across sites.23

Pathological stiffness elevation and disease group findings

The disease group demonstrated markedly elevated LV myocardial stiffness (11.73 ± 1.01 kPa) relative to healthy volunteers, consistent with the established pathophysiology of pressure-overload-induced myocardial fibrosis and passive stiffening.24,25 This magnitude of elevation aligns closely with values reported by Castelein et al.19 in their aortic stenosis cohort and with mechanical wave velocity data reported by Espeland et al.26 in patients with aortic stenosis at 3.0 T, where elevated LV wall mechanical wave velocities were reliably distinguished from healthy controls with good reproducibility.

The significantly lower OSS-SNR in the disease group (1.27 [1.26-1.35] vs. 1.40 ± 0.10; P = .025) is consistent with attenuated shear wave propagation quality in stiffer myocardium and may reflect altered wave propagation, increased attenuation, geometric heterogeneity, or reduced strain amplitude in stiffer myocardium.27,28 This observation has practical implications for clinical deployment: while the OSS-SNR > 1.17 criterion was met by all participants in this study, future work in more severely diseased cohorts should prospectively evaluate whether the current threshold requires disease-specific recalibration.

OSS-SNR at 3.0 T compared with prior 1.5 T data

OSS-SNR at 3.0 T was not substantially higher than values previously reported at 1.5 T, despite the theoretical SNR advantage of higher field strength. Several factors may explain this finding. First, B0 field inhomogeneity is more pronounced at 3.0 T, particularly in the thorax, where susceptibility differences among lung, myocardium, and blood may affect phase-based displacement measurements.29 Second, the 140-Hz vibration frequency used in this study was selected on the basis of prior cardiac MRE work, but the optimal frequency for balancing wave penetration, wavelength, and displacement sensitivity may differ across field strengths and patient populations. Third, mechanical wave delivery depends strongly on driver positioning, coupling efficiency, chest wall transmission, and myocardial geometry, factors that are not directly improved by higher magnetic field strength.30

The absence of a significant correlation between OSS-SNR and stiffness error above the predefined adequacy threshold suggests that OSS-SNR may function primarily as a quality-control criterion rather than a continuous predictor of measurement precision.31

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the disease group was older and had higher blood pressure than healthy volunteers. Disease-related stiffness elevation cannot be fully separated from age- and loading-related effects. Second, the disease subgroup was small and included patients with both aortic stenosis and long-standing hypertension. Although both are associated with increased myocardial stiffness, the sample size limited disease-specific reproducibility analyses. Third, biological stability during the LIR period was inferred from clinical stability rather than independently verified using biomarkers or additional tissue characterization imaging. Finally, the single-center, single-scanner design limits generalizability across vendors, field strengths, acquisition protocols, postprocessing procedures, and clinical sites. Multicenter validation will be necessary to document generalizability before broad clinical deployment.

Conclusion

3D single-frequency 140-Hz cardiac MRE at 3.0 T demonstrated excellent short- and long-interval reproducibility for the non-invasive quantification of LV myocardial stiffness. The derived SDC95 values provide practical reference thresholds for interpreting longitudinal stiffness changes in future studies.
