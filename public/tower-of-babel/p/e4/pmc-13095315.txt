Extensive peripheral immune cell phenotypes and nephrotic syndrome: Tracing causality through genetics – a bilateral Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Qiong Liu, Guojun Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nephrotic syndrome (NS) is a complex renal disorder characterized by significant proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, and hyperlipidemia. While its etiology involves genetic and environmental factors, the role of the immune system in NS’s pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. We adopted a bilateral Mendelian randomization approach, leveraging genetic variations as proxies to explore the causality between immune cell traits and NS. Utilizing genetic data sourced from the genome-wide association studies Catalog and FinnGen’s database, we analyzed the association of 731 immune phenotypes with NS. Instrumental variables were selected based on stringent criteria, and the analysis was augmented by the inverse-variance weighting method, alongside sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. Our analysis identified a significant causal association between CD8br natural killer T cell absolute cell count and NS, suggesting a novel immune-related risk factor for NS. In addition, decreased levels of 8 kinds of regulatory T cell phenotypes were observed in NS onset, suggesting a broader immunological impact of NS. These findings were supported by sensitivity analyses, which confirmed the stability and validity of the identified causal relationships. This pioneering study highlights the significant role of specific immune cell traits in the pathogenesis of NS, offering new insights into its immunopathological mechanisms. Future research should aim to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying these associations and explore their implications across diverse populations to enhance the generalizability and applicability of these insights.

1. Introduction

Nephrotic syndrome (NS), a multifaceted renal pathology, is distinguished by a triad of prominent proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, and hyperlipidemia, resulting in edema. Predominantly impacting the glomerular filtration mechanism, NS signifies a significant modification in the immune response, involving both innate and adaptive immunity in its etiology.[1–3] This disorder highlights the intricate interplay between immune system functionality and renal homeostasis, with diverse clinical presentations dependent on the degree and localization of renal impairment. Symptoms range from swelling and fatigue to severe complications such as renal failure if left untreated.[4,5]

The etiology of NS involves a combination of genetic predispositions and environmental triggers, resulting in a disease spectrum influenced by intricate immune interactions. While idiopathic forms are predominantly observed in children, in adults, NS often presents alongside systemic diseases such as diabetes and lupus, indicating a multifactorial basis characterized by abnormal immune responses.[3,6,7]

Diagnostic strategies for NS integrate clinical evaluation with laboratory and imaging modalities in order to establish a comprehensive understanding of the underlying pathology. Advances in diagnostic approaches, particularly the use of kidney biopsy, have improved the ability to delineate specific glomerular lesions and thereby support the development of more individualized therapeutic plans. Current treatment strategies aim to control clinical manifestations while addressing the primary pathogenic processes, with corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents remaining central components of pharmacologic management. Continued efforts to refine these strategies arise from the expanding knowledge of immune mechanisms involved in NS, which has stimulated the pursuit of therapies that achieve greater precision while reducing treatment-related adverse effects.[8,9]

A comprehensive analysis of the pathophysiology of NS necessitates a detailed investigation into the involvement of the immune system, particularly the intricate interactions among T cells, cytokines, and glomerular cells.[10–12] This intricate interplay not only elucidates the processes leading to proteinuria but also presents opportunities for novel therapeutic strategies to mitigate immune-mediated renal damage.

Recent progress in comprehending the immunopathogenesis of NS highlights the intricate relationship between immune function and renal physiology. Studies have illuminated the altered landscape of immune cells in NS, revealing significant changes in T cells, B cells, and monocytes, which suggest novel targets for therapy.[6] Moreover, investigations into membranous nephropathy linked to autoimmune disorders have underscored the possibility of overlap syndromes, underscoring the importance of comprehensive diagnostic evaluations in membranous nephropathy instances.[13] The advancing comprehension of the molecular pathways underlying podocytopathies such as minimal change disease and primary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis suggests the critical involvement of T and B cells, in conjunction with autoimmune mechanisms, in the development of these diseases.[14,15] This expanding body of evidence not only enhances our understanding of NS but also facilitates the development of more specific and efficient treatments, in line with the pressing demand for improved management approaches in NS.

The quest for understanding NS and improving patient care is continuous, with research endeavors focused on elucidating the intricate immune pathways implicated. This endeavor embodies the aspiration for more accurate diagnostic indicators and the creation of therapies with enhanced effectiveness and reduced side effects, heralding a new era in the management of NS.

Mendelian randomization (MR) applies genetic variants associated with specific exposures to investigate whether these exposures may have causal relevance for disease outcomes. By utilizing summary statistics derived from large genome-wide association studies (GWAS), this approach enables the evaluation of putative causal pathways in situations where conventional experimental designs cannot be implemented and provides a framework for examining the potential biological mechanisms that link immune-related traits to renal disease.[16]

Aiming to address the uncertainties surrounding the immunological determinants of NS, the present study applies a 2-sample MR framework to examine potential causal relationships between immune cell traits and disease susceptibility, and to evaluate whether genetic liability to NS is linked to systematic alterations in immune cell profiles. By integrating large-scale genome-wide association statistics, this work seeks to clarify how immune dysregulation may contribute to NS and to provide a basis for future advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

To explore the causal links between NS and the expression of 731 immune cell signatures, we adopted a 2-sample MR approach. This methodology makes use of genetic variations as proxies for potential risk factors, termed instrumental variables (IVs), to ascertain causality. For the MR analysis to hold validity, it is imperative that 3 essential criteria are met: Firstly, the genetic variants must demonstrate a strong association with the specific immune cell signatures under study[17]; secondly, these variants should not be associated with any confounding variables that might simultaneously influence both the immune cell signatures and NS[18]; and thirdly, the genetic variants must affect NS solely through their impact on the immune cell signatures, without any indirect mechanisms involved. 2.2. Immunity-wide GWAS data sources

In our genomic exploration, we gathered summary statistics on immune traits from the GWAS Catalog, including 731 unique immunophenotypes categorized as absolute cell (AC) counts, median fluorescence intensities, morphological parameters, and relative cell counts. These data provided a granular view across various immune cell categories such as B cells, conventional dendritic cells (CDCs), mature T cells, monocytes, myeloid cells, and panels of T cell, B cell, and natural killer cell panel (TBNK) and regulatory T (Treg) cells, with morphological parameters metrics further detailing the characteristics of CDC and TBNK cells.[19] The GWAS on 3757 Europeans used a Sardinian reference panel for the imputation of 22 million single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Adjustments were made for variables like sex and age to improve the association test precision.

2.3. GWAS data sources for NS

To mitigate the risk of biases inherent in subsample MR, we strategically utilized a distinct database for the GWAS summary statistics relevant to NS. These statistics were extracted from FinnGen’s latest publication, designated “DF10” and dated December 18, 2023. This particular database provides a thorough exploration of a cohort consisting of 406,992 individuals of European ancestry, identifying 917 cases of NS against a backdrop of 406,075 control subjects.[20]

For those seeking in-depth insights into this dataset, more comprehensive details can be found at FinnGen’s repository, accessible via https://r10.finngen.fi/.

2.5. Statistical analysis

In our comprehensive examination, we employed R software (version 4.3.1) and specialized packages (“TwoSampleMR” and “MRPRESSO” packages) tailored for MR analysis to investigate the intricate causal relationships between immunophenotypes and NS.[22] Our methodology is outlined as follows: Initially, we utilized a range of statistical methodologies, including inverse-variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median-based, and mode-based approaches, to combine data from numerous genetic variants. These methods are essential in estimating the overall causal impact of immunophenotypes on NS susceptibility, each offering distinct benefits in dealing with data heterogeneity and variable strength of IVs.[23–27]

Secondly, in order to evaluate diversity among the chosen IVs, we employed Cochrans Q statistic and associated P values. In cases where substantial diversity was identified, indicating the necessity for a more precise consideration of variability among genetic instruments, we transitioned to a random-effects IVW model.[24]

Thirdly, the examination of horizontal pleiotropy, wherein genetic variants may impact the outcome through pathways unrelated to the primary exposure, was deemed a crucial aspect of our analysis. We leveraged the MR-Egger method, particularly its intercept term, to adjust for and detect the presence of horizontal pleiotropy.[25]

Fourthly, pleiotropic outliers were identified and corrected for using the MR-PRESSO approach to maintain the unbiased nature of our causal estimates.[28]

Fifthly, to validate the reliability of our findings, funnel plots and scatter plots were utilized for visual inspection, aiding in the detection of outliers and ensuring the consistency of the IVs, thus reinforcing the credibility of our correlation results.

Furthermore, the Steiger test was applied to validate the directionality of the effects estimated by the SNPs. This was a crucial step in reinforcing the credibility of our causal inferences, ensuring that the direction of the causal relationship was accurately represented.[29]

Lastly, due to the comprehensive hypothesis testing conducted in our research, we implemented the false discovery rate (FDR) correction method to mitigate Type I errors.[30] This statistical adjustment plays a critical role in upholding the validity of our inferential conclusions, particularly in a study of such magnitude, thereby bolstering the reliability and scientific credibility of our findings pertaining to the causal relationships under investigation.

3. Results

3.1. Investigating the impact of immunophenotypes on NS through causal analysis

Our investigation into the causal impact of immunophenotypes on NS utilized an MR approach, with a primary focus on the IVW method. By applying the FDR method for multiple testing adjustments and setting a significance level at P_FDR < .05, we discovered a direct and statistically significant link between NS and a specific immune trait: the CD8br natural killer T (NKT) cell AC count, which presented an odds ratio (OR) of 1.5164 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) ranging from 1.2427 to 1.8504 and a P_FDR value of .0030. To validate the robustness of these causal associations, we relied on results from 3 additional techniques and a thorough sensitivity analysis. Horizontal pleiotropy considerations, which could potentially bias our findings, were addressed and dismissed based on the MR-Egger intercept and the global test from MR-PRESSO. Our confidence in the stability of these results was further supported by visual inspections using scatter plots and funnel plots (Figs. 3 and 4). The leave-one-out analysis contributed additional assurance, demonstrating that no SNP introduced a pleiotropic bias to our causal estimates (Fig. 5 and Tables S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R651). Finally, the MR Steiger test was conducted to affirm the correct directionality of our analysis, ensuring all SNPs were more strongly associated with the immune trait than with NS directly (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R651).

3.2. Exploration of the causal effect of NS onset on immunophenotypes

After multiple test adjustments based on the FDR method, no immune trait was identified at a significance of 0.05. However, at a less stringent threshold of 0.20, 8 suggestive immunophenotypes were identified, of which were all in the Treg panel. Specifically, decreases were observed in the levels of activated Treg %CD4 Treg (OR = 0.9201, 95% CI: 0.8739–0.9689, P_FDR = .1642) and activated Treg %CD4 (OR = 0.9151, 95% CI: 0.8701–0.9626, P_FDR = .0847) in NS patients. Moreover, a decrease was noted in CD28− CD25++ CD8br %T cells (OR = 0.9151, 95% CI: 0.7979–0.9446, P_FDR = .1242), along with a negative causal effect of NS on CD25 on CD39+ CD4 Treg (OR = 0.9060, 95% CI: 0.8608–0.96536, P_FDR = .0508). Similar associations were found for CD25 on CD4 Treg (OR = 0.9084, 95% CI: 0.8631–0.9561, P_FDR = .0508), CD25 on activated Treg (OR = 0.9147, 95% CI: 0.8646–0.9676, P_FDR = .1734), CD25 on CD39+ activated Treg (OR = 0.9089, 95% CI: 0.8633–0.9570, P_FDR = .0508), and CD25 on activated and secreting Treg (OR = 0.9151, 95% CI: 0.8701–0.9626, P_FDR = .0847). 4. Discussion

Our study revealed a statistically significant relationship between the AC count of CD8br NKT cells and the development of NS, indicating a potential novel immune-related risk factor. Furthermore, a decrease in activated Treg %CD4 levels and alterations in 7 Treg phenotypes were observed in NS patients, suggesting a broader immunological implication in the disease.

According to the current literature search results, there is a lack of explicit reference to the correlation between CD8br NKT cells and NS. This absence highlights the originality of our discoveries and indicates that the involvement of CD8br NKT cells in NS has not been thoroughly investigated to date. CD8br NKT cells, a distinct subset of NKT cells, possess a distinctive ability to engage with both the innate and adaptive immune systems, potentially impacting inflammatory reactions and autoimmunity.[31] Considering their dual functionality, it is reasonable to hypothesize that CD8br NKT cells may impact the pathogenesis of NS through processes involving immune modulation and inflammation. NS is distinguished by a compromised filtration barrier in the kidneys, resulting in substantial proteinuria, which may be exacerbated by an aberrant immune response orchestrated by CD8br NKT cells.[1] The identified OR suggests CD8br NKT cells may be a risk factor for NS, warranting further research into their role in the disease’s development. Further study of how these cells interact with the immune system in NS could provide new insights into the disease’s immunopathogenesis.

There is a growing body of evidence suggesting the involvement of Treg cells, specifically CD4+ Tregs, in the pathogenesis and modulation of immune responses in NS.[32,33] Studies have highlighted the role of Tregs in maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing autoimmune responses, which can be pivotal in diseases characterized by immune dysregulation, such as NS.[34–36] The decrease in levels of activated Treg %CD4 Treg and activated Treg %CD4 in patients with NS, as evidenced by our study, may suggest a disrupted regulatory mechanism that is crucial for maintaining immune homeostasis in NS. Tregs are essential for suppressing exaggerated immune responses and upholding immune tolerance, functions that are critical for preventing tissue damage in inflammatory conditions. In the context of NS, a condition marked by significant proteinuria and often associated with autoimmune components, a decrease in activated Treg cells might contribute to the pathophysiological mechanisms leading to podocyte damage and altered glomerular filtration.[37,38] The decrease in Treg subpopulations in NS can worsen the disease by not effectively controlling kidney inflammation, aligning with the overall immunopathogenesis model of NS. Hence, despite the scarcity of direct literature addressing the correlation between these particular Treg subpopulations and NS, the significance of Tregs in immune modulation and the consequences of their dysregulation in autoimmune and inflammatory conditions offer a compelling justification for exploring the potential impact of alterations in activated Treg %CD4 Treg and activated Treg %CD4 levels on NS development.[38] This line of inquiry presents an opportunity to gain insights into novel facets of NS immunopathology and potentially identify innovative therapeutic targets geared towards reinstating immune homeostasis in affected individuals.

The literature search indicates that while there is a growing understanding of CD8+CD28− T cells and Tregs in the context of immune regulation and autoimmune diseases, direct studies linking these specific cell subsets to NS are not readily available.[39,40] This gap in research highlights the novel aspect of our findings, which suggest that NS is associated with a decrease in specific T cell subsets, including CD28− CD25++ CD8br %T cells and various forms of CD25+ Tregs. The observed decrease in CD28− CD25++ CD8br %T cells, CD25 on CD39+ CD4 Treg, and other related Treg phenotypes in NS patients suggests a potential mechanism by which NS could influence immune regulation. CD28− CD25++ CD8br %T cells are a subset of T cells that lack the CD28 costimulatory receptor, which plays a crucial role in T cell activation and survival. Their decrease, alongside the reduction in activated and Treg cells expressing CD25 (the alpha chain of the IL-2 receptor), points towards an impaired Treg cell function in NS patients.[41,42] This impairment could lead to uncontrolled inflammation and immune responses, contributing to the pathophysiology of NS. Due to the crucial function of Treg cells in preserving immune equilibrium and averting autoimmunity, their reduction in NS may potentially exacerbate the advancement of the disease by inadequately inhibiting autoreactive T cell reactions and renal inflammation. More research is needed to understand the roles of T cells and Treg subsets in NS and how they are influenced by the disease.

Our study differs from traditional research on NS, which has mainly focused on inflammatory pathways and conventional immunosuppressive therapies. Instead, we have identified specific immune cells associated with NS, providing new insights into the disease’s immunopathology. This suggests that targeting these immune cell functions could lead to innovative treatment approaches, potentially reducing side effects associated with current therapies and improving patient outcomes.

Nonetheless, it is crucial to recognize the limitations of our study, notably the genetic homogeneity of our cohort, primarily of European descent. This restricts the generalizability of our results across different ethnic groups. Future directions should focus on elucidating the biological mechanisms through which the identified immune cells interinfluence with NS. This entails a combination of clinical and laboratory studies to confirm potential therapeutic targets.

In addition, longitudinal research examining how these immune cell alterations evolve with NS’s progression could offer deeper insights into its pathogenesis and treatment efficacy. By exploring these avenues, we can move towards developing targeted therapies that not only address NS more effectively but also offer a better quality of life for patients.

5. Conclusions

Our study highlights the significant role of specific immune cell traits in the pathogenesis of NS, offering new insights into its immunopathological mechanisms. Future research should aim to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying these associations and explore their implications across diverse populations to enhance the generalizability and applicability of these insights.
