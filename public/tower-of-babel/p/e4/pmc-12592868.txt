Introduction

The early phases of dental caries are clinically defined as opaque, white, flaky, and noncavitated lesions caused by mineral ion degradation in the enamel subsurface region.1,2 This initial phase of enamel caries can be reversed through a process known as remineralization, which replaces lost mineral ions in demineralized enamel.3 Saliva naturally promotes remineralization by transferring bioavailable calcium (Ca2+) and phosphate (PO4) ions to mineral-deficient carious lesions, a process known as biomineralization.4 However, saliva's natural remineralization process is slow and can be significantly hampered under severe acidic conditions, resulting in mineral ion depletion in saliva and the necessity for external remineralizing agents.4

Fluoride has long been the cornerstone of caries prevention and enamel remineralization. It not only promotes remineralization but also inhibits demineralization and has cariostatic properties.5 Community water fluoridation at an ideal level of 1 ppm has significantly reduced the frequency of dental cavities worldwide.6,7 However, technical, financial, and infrastructure obstacles make community water fluoridation projects difficult to implement in many developing nations. Water fluoridation has been associated with high rates of dental fluorosis, reaching up to 40% in certain geographic regions.7

The introduction of topical fluorides in dentistry has resulted in a greater use of concentrated fluoride applications, such as fluoride-containing toothpastes, mouth rinses, gels, and varnishes, either alone or in combination.8 The proper usage of fluorides is still debated despite their widespread use. Due to the ubiquitous availability of fluoride from different sources, questions have been raised about its actual effectiveness and potential dangers, including fluorosis, connected with various fluoride-based caries prevention treatments.4 Additionally, fluoride varnish is thought to be FDA-approved and needs to be used “off label” in order to prevent dental caries.9

Concerns about fluoride use and the risk of dental fluorosis have led to increased interest in non-fluoridated enamel remineralizing treatments. Grape seed proanthocyanidin (PAC) are an alternative. PAC, a type of polyphenol, have shown promise in enhancing dental health due to their high antioxidant potential, ability to bind to proteins, and chelation of calcium and phosphate ions required for remineralization.10 The purpose of this study was to evaluate and compare the remineralization effectiveness of a novel PAC biovarnish derived from grape seeds to that of fluoride varnish on artificially created dental caries in an in vitro setting resembling the oral cavity.

Methods

Extraction and Characterization of PACs from Grape Seeds

Indigenous fresh grape seeds (Vitis vinifera L.; Family: Vitaceae) identified by taxonomists were manually removed from the fruit and shade-dried for 10 days. The dried seeds were immersed in a glass beaker containing 10% ethyl acetate solution and left undisturbed for 24 hours. Following this interval, the liquid layer over the sediment was carefully removed and dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate. The obtained dry concentrate was combined with petroleum ether and filtered, resulting in unrefined liquid PAC11 (Fig. 1). Using gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (GC–MS), PAC were identified.

(A) Dry grape seeds (V. vinifera); (B) Grape seeds soaked in ethyl acetate; (C) Grape seed extract; (D) Decantation with Na2SO4; (E) Evaporated extract; (F) Mixed with ethyl acetate and petroleum ether; (G) Pure PAC extract; (H) Formulated PAC biovarnish

Formulation of a PAC Biovarnish

Composition

In this study, we developed a 30 mL dental varnish with 50 μg/mL PAC, ethanol, glycerin, shellac, xylitol, and grape flavor.

Procedure for Biovarnish Formulation

For the formulation of the varnish, 0.0015 gm PAC was dissolved in an ethanol solution. The remaining ethanol and glycerin were thoroughly combined in a separate container. Shellac, a film-forming ingredient, was gradually added to the mixture while stirring constantly until it was completely dissolved, which required gentle heating. Xylitol, a sugar substitute, was then added and thoroughly mixed. A PAC solution was added to the mixture to ensure consistent dispersion, followed by natural grape flavoring. Ultimately, 30 mL of distilled water was added, and the mixture was well-stirred to create a uniform varnish.

Sample Size

A priori power analysis for a one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) with fixed effects was performed using G*Power software 3.1 with an effect size of f = 0.50 and power of 0.90, based on similar in vitro studies.12 The sample size (n) was estimated to be 54, which was rounded off to 60, with 20 samples in each group. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Human Ethics Committee for Student Research CARE-IHEC-I/1243/22.

Preparation of Tooth Samples

Sixty primary molars with intact buccal and lingual/palatal surfaces, as well as caries-free primary molars requiring extraction (serial extraction), and exfoliating teeth were included in the study after obtaining parental consent. The extracted teeth were properly cleaned, soft tissue debris was removed with a scaler, and the teeth were disinfected by immersing them in 10% formalin for 2 days.

The intact buccal or lingual/palatal surfaces of the sample teeth were used to make a 0.3 × 0.3 cm square window using cellophane tape. The remaining tooth surface was painted with 25% phthalate solution.13

Baseline Microhardness Testing and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) Data

Vickers enamel microhardness testing (VEMH) was performed on all sample surfaces using a load of 1.96 N for 15 seconds, three times, to measure the mean surface hardness of the tooth enamel before the development of artificial caries-like lesions. SEM was used to analyze the baseline topography of tooth enamel.

Formation of Artificial Enamel Caries: Demineralization

The samples were exposed to acidic challenge using the following technique, which created an artificial caries-like lesion on the tooth enamel surfaces. The prepared tooth samples were immersed in a demineralizing solution for four days.14 The demineralizing solution contained 0.05 mM glacial acetic acid (CH3COOH), 2.2 mM anhydrous disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), and 2.2 mM calcium chloride dihydrate (CaCl2). The pH of the demineralizing solution was measured using pH paper and adjusted to 4.5 using 1 M potassium hydroxide (KOH). After 4 days, each sample was rinsed with deionized water for 20 seconds, dried, and placed in sterile containers. VEMH testing was performed, and the surface topography was analyzed under SEM following demineralization.

Experimental Groups

Group III test group: PAC varnish (n = 20).

Remineralization Treatment

All demineralized tooth samples were immersed in artificial saliva14 for 24 hours, which contained 0.65 gm of potassium chloride, 0.058 gm of magnesium chloride, 0.165 gm of calcium chloride, 0.804 gm of potassium dihydrogen orthophosphate, 0.365 gm of potassium dihydrogen phosphate, 2 gm of carboxymethyl cellulose sodium salt, and deionized water per liter. Following immersion, samples were treated twice daily for 7 days with test and control agents applied to the created window using a sterile cotton bud for 3 minutes, cleaned with deionized water for 20 seconds, and reintroduced into artificial saliva, which was freshly prepared every day. VEMH was performed on all samples following treatment. Three indentation marks were made on each sample for 15 seconds at a force of 1.96 N, and the mean results were statistically analyzed. Additionally, SEM was used to assess surface structural changes following remineralization.

Results

Quantitative Evaluation of Tooth Enamel Microhardness by Vickers

VEMH testing was used to measure the surface microhardness of tooth samples in the test and control groups at the baseline and demineralization and remineralization stages (Fig. 2). To identify significant differences in hardness values, data were analyzed using SPSS version 29, which included one-way ANOVA and post hoc Tukey's honest significant difference (HSD) tests.

One-way ANOVA revealed that the mean VEMH value at baseline and after demineralization was not significant. Qualitative Evaluation by SEM

Scanning electron microscopy images of enamel surfaces taken at 1000× magnification at three different stages—prior to demineralization, after demineralization, and after remineralization treatments in the three experimental groups—are shown in (Figs 4A to E). At baseline, images showed a smooth, intact enamel surface. Following demineralization, a rough, porous surface with evident mineral loss was observed. After remineralization treatment, both fluoride and PAC varnishes showed improved surface integrity with reduced porosity. The PAC-treated group exhibited more prominent remineralized areas, which were similar to sound enamel.

Discussion

Dental caries, a prevalent oral cavity condition during childhood, progresses because of many factors, including plaque accumulation, sugar consumption, and acidic environments. Initial enamel caries are white opaque lesions that indicate subsurface demineralization of the enamel. They are reversible through remineralization mechanisms that restore mineral content and halt the progression of the caries.2 While saliva promotes remineralization naturally, extremely acidic environments deplete mineral ions required for remineralization, such as calcium, phosphate, and fluoride, preventing enamel repair. Thus, external remineralization treatments are essential for reversing early carious lesions. Fluoride is the most commonly used remineralization agent because it keeps enamel intact by enhancing remineralization and preventing demineralization through the formation of acid-resistant fluorapatite crystals.5,15

Fluorides can be delivered both systemically and topically. Community water fluoridation is often regarded as the most effective strategy for preventing tooth decay in children.16,17 Topical fluorides that are professionally applied come in both varnish and gel forms and include sodium fluoride, difluorosilane, and acidulated phosphate fluoride. Varnish is a professional topically applied fluoride system that improves the synthesis of fluorapatite and increases the amount of fluoride ions in saliva, which promotes remineralization.18 A 5% NaF varnish is commonly used, whereas 0.9% difluorosilane varnish was found to be the most effective at preventing dental cavities.19 Despite being thought of as an efficient therapy for remineralizing caries, topical fluorides have the unfavorable effect of inducing dental fluorosis if not properly used.4 In developing nations, such as India, where fluorosis is endemic20 and fluoride is available in many food sources, the use of fluoride-containing varnishes increases the risk of fluorosis. In response to these limitations, nonfluoridated remineralization agents, particularly phytochemicals derived from plants, have received more attention.

Grape seeds include 40% fiber, 16% oil, 11% protein, and 7% complex phenols, mainly tannins. PAC, which are made up of epicatechin, epicatechin gallate, and catechin, are abundant in tannins.21 PACs are bioactive phytochemicals with antioxidant and anticaries properties due to their ability to bind with the amino acid proline.22 The organic components of primary tooth enamel contain type I collagen, which includes glycine, alanine, hydroxyproline, and proline. Carious enamel has high proline protein levels, likely due to the incorporation of proline-rich proteins from saliva.23 PACs interact with proline, forming fibrils through covalent bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and the formation of hydrophobic pockets. This interaction acts as a template for mineral deposition. PACs may bind calcium ions through their hydroxyphenyl groups, facilitating mineral deposition.24

Hardness testing indirectly measures the remineralizing efficacy. Specifically, Vickers and Knoop tests are designed to assess the microhardness of enamel. The Knoop test, which uses a diamond-shaped indenter with diagonals of different lengths, can introduce measurement errors due to elastic recovery at the shortest diagonal during indentation, hence Vickers microhardness testing was used.25

In vitro models that simulate early enamel caries are typically used to investigate the efficiency of remineralization treatments.26 This study used a demineralization pH cycling model based on the methods of ten Cate et al.14 By exposing primary tooth enamel to a demineralization cycle using an acetic acid solution at pH 4.5, artificial enamel caries-like lesions were generated. A white, chalky layer, which is a sign of early enamel caries, was formed on the enamel because of this 4-day process. Similar demineralization models have been used in a number of investigations.27–29 Alternative demineralization procedures have also been tried, with Nagi et al. using 40% hydrogen peroxide in three 45-minute cycles and Joshi et al. using lactic acid during a 2-day process.30,31 Acetic acid was used for this investigation because its pH of 4.76 is near the pH values that cause early enamel caries below 5.0.32

The VEMH values were reassessed after the samples underwent a demineralization pH cycle and were treated with artificial saliva and various remineralization agents following the recommendations of ten Cate et al.14 The mean (VEMH) values following remineralization were found to be in the following order: PAC biovarnish > fluoride varnish > control group. Previous research has shown that commercially available grape seed extracts containing PAC and other constituents, such as gallic acid, result in higher microhardness values than fluoridated dentifrices.28,30,33 In the current investigation, we employed a varnish with PAC extract without any additional ingredients and achieved greater microhardness values than those reported in earlier studies.

In dental research, SEM is a powerful and sensitive technology that is frequently used to observe the intricate details of enamel surfaces at the microscopic level. Before demineralization, the enamel on the primary teeth had a flat surface with grooves (Fig. 4A). The enamel structure showed a fish-scaled or honey-combed appearance (Fig. 4B) after 4 days of demineralization, which followed in earlier SEM investigations.34 After 7 days of immersion in the remineralization solution, the control group (Fig. 4C) revealed areas with plugged porosities and irregular masses. A nonhomogeneous surface with pits and porosities was observed in the fluoride varnish group (Fig. 4D). This surface was formed by the release of fluoride ions from difluorosilane in the interprismatic material, resulting in the formation of a fluorapatite layer over the enamel.35 The PAC group, on the contrary, had a dense, homogeneous layer with a globular, granular appearance (Fig. 4D), suggesting that demineralized enamel was plugged with remineralized matrix. PACs promote remineralization via two mechanisms: chelating inorganic calcium ions to form insoluble calcium complexes that deposit on the enamel surface and interacting with organic components, particularly proline-rich collagen, to form covalent bonds with carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, which act as ligands to bind calcium ions. Additionally, PACs prevent the activation of matrix metalloproteinases, which maintains the mineral deposition scaffold intact.24,36 As previously mentioned by Kim et al., the formation of globular structures in the SEM examination indicates extra-fibrollar mineralization37 PAC granules on the enamel were dense, irregular masses that effectively sealed the demineralized zone, leaving no open porosities, preventing acid penetration into the enamel and the formation of dental caries. Thus, PACs demonstrated superior extrafibrillar mineralization compared to fluoride groups under SEM.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that the PAC biovarnish from grape seeds exhibits superior remineralization properties compared to fluoride varnish, evidenced by increased hardness in (VEMH) testing and enhanced ultrastructural changes under SEM. As the first study to compare these agents in artificially induced early caries-like lesions in primary teeth, it highlights the safety and effectiveness of grape seed PAC biovarnish as a nonfluoridated alternative. This naturally derived varnish offers an affordable substitute to fluoride-based products, which can cause adverse effects. However, clinical trials are needed to validate its efficiency in real-world conditions.

Clinical Significance

The study demonstrates that PAC biovarnish is a highly effective alternative to fluoride varnish for enamel remineralization, potentially reducing the risk of dental fluorosis. PAC varnish showed superior remineralization by significantly increasing enamel hardness and improving surface topography. This offers a promising nonfluoridated option for caries prevention and management.
