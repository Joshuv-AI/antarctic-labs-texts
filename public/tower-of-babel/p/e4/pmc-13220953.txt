One-year impact of an ambulatory pulmonary curriculum for fellows leveraging adult learning theory
Authors: Kathleen A McAvoy, Katherine A Gielissen, Shyoko Honiden, Jennifer D Possick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) fellowship, inpatient training is often prioritized over outpatient learning, resulting in fellows’ dissatisfaction with their ambulatory education. Additional challenges limit the use of best practices for teaching and learning in graduate medical education.ObjectiveTo develop a 3-year ambulatory pulmonary curriculum leveraging principles of adult learning theory such as flipped classroom elements and interprofessional teaching, and to evaluate the first year of this curriculum.MethodsWe developed an ambulatory pulmonary curriculum at a large academic PCCM fellowship program including 24 topics over 3 years. Sessions emphasized case- and discussion-based learning in a small group setting, often incorporating flipped classroom elements. Most sessions also included interprofessional teaching for hands-on practical skill development. Assessment of the first year of the curriculum was performed using a mixed-methods approach, with pre- and postintervention surveys followed by fellow focus groups. Beginning with the third session, paired pre- and posttests for knowledge and trainee self-assessed comfort with the topic were evaluated. In-training exam (ITE) scores were compared to preintervention years.ResultsComparison of paired pre- and posttests demonstrated improved comfort in all 6 sessions, and improved knowledge in 5 of the 6 sessions assessed. ITE scores of all fellows following the first year of the curriculum were significantly increased compared to one of the 2 prior years, and ITE scores for the first-year fellows were significantly higher than both preceding academic years. Fellow and faculty surveys reflected that the curricular content, use of interprofessional teachers, small group setting, and use of presession reading all enhanced education. Fellow focus groups emphasized that content selection and teaching methodologies were strengths of the curriculum.ConclusionsIn its first year, the implementation of an ambulatory pulmonary curriculum grounded in adult learning theory and highlighting interprofessional teachers positively impacted fellow knowledge and comfort. Our curriculum is a first of its kind to demonstrate improvement in PCCM fellow knowledge and to expand the use of interprofessional teaching and flipped classroom elements to graduate ambulatory pulmonary education.Keywords: curriculum development, interprofessional teaching, flipped classroom, mixed-methods, focus groups

Introduction

For subspecialties with large inpatient and outpatient clinical footprints, such as pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM), the demands of inpatient care often take precedence for both learners and teachers.1 It is therefore unsurprising that PCCM fellows have historically expressed dissatisfaction with ambulatory training and rate their education inadequate to prepare them for independent practice.2,3 Busy trainee schedules pose challenges to incorporating best practices for teaching and learning in graduate medical education (GME).4 Adult learning theory posits that graduate trainees prefer relevance and application, immediate feedback and reflection, and learning within a social context.5 These factors merit careful consideration when designing education for fellows.

Kassutto and colleagues were the first to present a 24-month PCCM didactic curriculum highlighting 40 core outpatient topics in a standardized case-based format for small group learning.2,6 The curriculum improved learner and faculty perceptions of ambulatory education generally, but specific details of fellow reactions to the curriculum and effect on content knowledge could not be assessed due to low survey response rates.

Building upon this important work, we developed a 3-year ambulatory pulmonary curriculum using small group teaching and leveraging principles of adult learning theory by incorporating flipped classroom elements and interprofessional teaching, whereby professionals from different disciplines act as teachers.7,8 In this article, we describe impacts of the first year of this curriculum, which we assessed using a mixed-methods approach.

Portions of this data were previously presented in May 2023 at the American Thoracic Society (ATS) annual conference, both in poster format and oral presentation, at the ATS/Association of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine Program Directors (APCCMPD)/Pediatric Pulmonary Training Directors Association Clinician Education Forum.9,10

Methods

Study participants and setting

The first year of our curriculum was delivered to PCCM fellows at a single large academic medical center over 11 months. The study included all fellows (n = 19, 2020-2021; n = 18, 2021-2022) and faculty (n = 59, 2020-2021; n = 57, 2021-2022) involved with the PCCM fellowship program. The fellowship program is accredited by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) and includes 18 months each of clinical and research time. Fellows maintain an outpatient continuity clinic practice over the 3 years. Prior to this intervention, there was no dedicated ambulatory education; didactics occurred in two 1-hour sessions during weekly academic half-days, and ambulatory topics were included only if chosen by fellows for peer-delivered, lecture-based conferences. If on an inpatient rotation, fellows were excused from clinical duties to allow presence at education, with variable attendance.

This study was deemed exempt by the Yale Institutional Review Board (Protocol ID 2000031534). All fellows and faculty were included in the curriculum regardless of their participation in the study. Those who agreed to participate provided informed consent.

Needs assessment

A targeted needs assessment (NA) was performed by anonymously surveying PCCM fellows and faculty in May 2021, during the academic year (AY) preceding curriculum implementation (Tables S1-S4). A portion of the NA survey, including overall fellow and faculty impressions of ambulatory pulmonary education, was previously published in a manuscript detailing the parallel implementation of novel ambulatory blocks.3

Curriculum design and implementation

We selected 24 core topics for inclusion in a 3-year curriculum cycle (Table 1) based on results of the NA survey and the American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM) Pulmonary Disease blueprint.11 Specific learning objectives were guided by both subspecialty content experts and general pulmonologists, leading to development of teaching scripts and materials by study authors, which were distributed to curriculum teachers. Subspecialty faculty stakeholders also provided feedback on teaching content and accompanying teaching materials. Sessions were designed to emphasize case- and discussion-based learning. Didactics were delivered by PCCM faculty, mainly general pulmonologists with significant ambulatory presence. Flipped classroom elements were included with most sessions; fellows were assigned targeted prereading of practice-changing literature or society guidelines (expected to take approximately 20 minutes to complete). At dedicated time points during the case discussion, fellows were asked to either recall knowledge gained from this prereading, or present key findings from practice-changing clinical trials as relates to the patient case. Most sessions also incorporated hands-on practical skill development delivered by interprofessional teachers (pharmacists, respiratory therapists, etc) often over 20 to 45 minutes; study authors provided learning objectives for this portion of each educational session. The structure and balance of case-based discussion and hands-on practical skill development varied between curricular sessions (Table S5).

Curriculum implementation began in AY 2021-2022. Sessions were 2 hours long and delivered to small groups during dedicated ambulatory blocks to allow uninterrupted and protected ambulatory educational time.3 Groups consisted of 4 to 5 fellows and were deliberately mixed to include fellows at different levels of training. Following completion of a curricular session, teaching materials were made available to fellows on an internal file hosting service for reference and review.

The remainder of the 3-year curriculum highlighting flipped classroom elements and interprofessional teachers was delivered in AY 2022-2023 and 2023-2024; however, programmatic changes to fellow schedules altered curriculum delivery and environment, preventing proper analysis for inclusion in this manuscript.

Survey development

NA and postintervention survey items were developed by study authors and subsequently reviewed by internal medicine faculty not involved in the study but who have experience in medical education; survey questions were only included if consensus was achieved following several rounds of iterative feedback and revision. Surveys used 5-point Likert scales and included a sixth “insufficient contact to evaluate” option for faculty with little exposure to fellow education. All survey procedures were anonymous (Qualtrics).

Curriculum session pre- and posttests were developed through iterative revision by study authors (K.A.M., S.H., J.D.P.). Identical pre- and posttests included 3 knowledge questions and one comfort assessment of the topic, addressed using a 5-point Likert scale. Anonymous pairing was achieved by asking participants to provide the same nonidentifying word in each of the pre- and postsurveys.

Evaluation methods and reflexivity

Immediate knowledge transfer and changes in fellow self-assessed comfort were evaluated with pre- and posttests, beginning with the third curricular session. Additionally, de-identified scores (percentage correct) from the Pulmonary Diseases and Disorders section of the end-of-year in-service pulmonary and critical care examination (in-training exam [ITE]) for fellows (APCCMPD) were compared with the 2 academic years preceding the intervention; AY 2020-2021 was excluded, as ITEs were suspended due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Online surveys were distributed to all PCCM fellows and faculty 9 months following the curriculum implementation (March-April 2022). Separate additional survey questions about session content, efficacy, and provided teaching materials were administered to faculty instructors who taught curricular sessions. Survey questions are incorporated in Table 2, Table S4, and Table S6. Interprofessional teachers were not surveyed due to small sample size and the survey questions being related to the overall goals of the curriculum, rather than the individual sessions in which they participated.

Fellow focus groups were conducted 11 to 12 months after curriculum implementation (May-June 2022). Focus groups were conducted in-person in mixed-year groups of 3 to 5 fellows over 1 hour. Session moderators performed semi-structured interviews using a topic guide developed by study authors, in alignment with an explanatory mixed-methods approach.12 Moderators were internal medicine residents with no involvement in study design. Questions were specifically developed to delineate impacts of our pulmonary curriculum from concurrent implementation of ambulatory blocks. Further details on focus group semi-structured interview design, focus group organization, thematic analysis, and reflexivity were previously published.3 Fellows were invited to participate in member checking by receiving a draft of the focus group results and being invited to provide feedback. Eight fellows responded, which resulted in no changes in our focus group findings.

Statistical analysis

NA and postintervention survey data are reported in aggregate. For survey prompts using a 5-point Likert scale, agreement was defined as a response of “agree” or “strongly agree,” and comfort was defined as a response of “comfortable” or “very comfortable.” Comparative analysis of aggregate data was performed using χ2 or Fisher exact test, depending on sample size and response distribution.

Pre- and postsession tests of fellow knowledge were scored out of 3 possible correct answers. Comparative analysis was performed using one-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test. Comparison of ITE scores to the postintervention AY 2021-2022 was performed with one-tailed Mann–Whitney tests.

P values ≤.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 9 software.

Results

The NA survey was completed by 15 of 19 fellows (78.9%) and 36 of 59 faculty (61.0%). The postintervention survey was completed by 14 of 18 fellows (77.8%), 29 of 57 faculty (50.9%), and 5 of 8 faculty instructors (62.5%). Demographic data about participants were published previously.3

Survey results: needs assessment

In the NA survey, we found that learners lacked comfort with outpatient management in 13 of the 19 disease groups assessed (Table S1). Teaching methodologies most preferred by fellows included case-based learning with group discussion and faculty-driven lectures (Table S2). More than 72% of faculty expressed interest in providing references, supplying patient case examples, and developing content for the curriculum in their pulmonary subspecialty (Table S3).

Fellow survey results

In the postintervention survey, 100% of fellows agreed that the curricular sessions positively contributed to their ambulatory pulmonary education, and that receiving education from interprofessional teachers enhanced the educational experience (Table 2). There was also agreement in support of the use of a small group setting for teaching, having dedicated time for learning, and providing presession reading.

There was a significant improvement in fellow knowledge between pre- and posttests following 5 of 6 curricular sessions assessed (Figure 1A). Additionally, there was significant improvement in fellow self-assessed comfort between pre- and posttests for all 6 sessions evaluated (Figure 1B). There was a sustained significant increase in fellow self-assessed comfort with the outpatient management of 3 of the 5 pulmonary disease groups covered in the inaugural year of the curriculum by the time of postintervention survey completion (Table S4). ITE scores for all fellows from the postintervention AY 2021-2022 had the highest median compared to prior years, though were significantly increased compared only to AY 2018-2019 (Figure 2A). ITE scores for first-year fellows were significantly increased compared to both preceding AYs (Figure 2B).

Instructor survey results

In the postintervention survey, 100% of faculty instructors agreed that curricular sessions contributed positively to fellow education, that the small group setting improved learning, and that the use of session prereading and interprofessional teachers enriched the educational experience (Table 2). Nearly all instructors agreed that the curricular sessions filled an unmet need in the fellowship, that fellows were actively engaged in the sessions, that upper year fellows benefited from the sessions, and that ambulatory conferences should continue to be taught in this manner.

Regarding session content and teaching materials, 100% of instructors used the provided teaching scripts and rated them as well-written and factually accurate (Table S6). Instructors regarded the educational sessions as providing high-yield content. In line with our goal to have sessions taught by general pulmonologists, a minority of instructors (40%) considered themselves as having specific subspecialty expertise in the topic(s) presented.

Fellow focus groups

All Yale PCCM fellows (n = 18) participated in 4 focus groups consisting of 3 to 5 mixed-year trainees per group. The average length of focus group discussions was 44 minutes. Fellows commended the ambulatory pulmonary curriculum in 2 specific themes: curriculum content and teaching methodologies. Areas for improvement were organized into a third theme. Themes and subthemes, with representative quotes, are summarized in Table 3.

Theme 1: The curriculum exposed fellows to a broad selection of pulmonary content

Fellows emphasized the value of a dedicated and structured curriculum, in contrast to prior educational structures with more ad lib educational topic selection (Table 3). They also reported that synchronous learning on fundamental content areas helped normalize knowledge on topics ubiquitous to ambulatory pulmonary medicine. Fellows shared that practical approaches and “deep dives” into specific ambulatory topics were directly applicable to patient care (Table 3). Simultaneously, they also highlighted the curriculum’s breadth in balancing these fundamental topics with disease entities not commonly seen in the ambulatory clinic, as these topics were still anticipated during future practice and on pulmonary boards. Last, learners emphasized the value of the practical skills sessions, as they were facilitated by interprofessional teachers and promoted a multidisciplinary approach to patient care, covered content immediately applicable to patient care, and were not otherwise addressed by other aspects of fellowship training.

Theme 2: Curricular teaching approaches were effective for active learning

Fellows described the intimate and familiar feel of small group sessions—consisting of the same learners across the year—as particularly helpful to their learning, as it created a safe learning climate and increased their engagement. While instructors were provided teaching scripts and materials, fellows reported that the ultimate execution of sessions varied among instructors. Additionally, individual session structure varied: from a multidisciplinary tumor board simulation, to team-based cognitive exercises, to flipped classroom presentations on landmark studies. Fellows appreciated the variation in learning approaches to increase efficacy across learner types. Additionally, the prereading assignments were thought to be helpful as a method to scaffold learning, both in preparing background knowledge for curricular sessions, but also in promoting co-teaching among learners. Learners felt that the number and length of articles provided for prereading were reasonable given their busy clinical schedule. Last, fellows stated that many of the deliverables from sessions (eg, handouts, diagrams) were helpful to organize their learning and were resources they returned to multiple times during clinical care.

Theme 3: Areas for improvement

Fellows suggested more variation in session complexity by providing more foundational knowledge for first-year fellows and more nuanced sessions for fellows in their second and third years. Additionally, fellows requested that more sessions be taught by faculty subspecialty experts when possible; while learning was noted as effective with incorporating general pulmonary attendings, fellows thought discussions could be enhanced by the presence of content experts.

Discussion

In its first year, our ambulatory pulmonary curriculum resulted in significantly improved fellow satisfaction with ambulatory education,3 self-assessed readiness for practice, comfort with subject matter, topical knowledge, and ITE scores. Our work is the second published comprehensive curricular restructuring to improve PCCM fellow ambulatory pulmonary education,2 and the first to both demonstrate knowledge transfer and to explore impact through a mixed-methods approach. The unique aspects of our curriculum—incorporation of interprofessional teachers, focus on practical skill content, utilization of flipped classroom elements with high-impact prereading, durable reference materials, and delivery in small group learning environments—were highlighted in focus groups as highly valued by learners.

Interprofessional education, when students from different professions learn together, enhances collaboration and communication essential to patient care.8,13 It is encouraged in the ACGME fellowship milestones, though time and resource constraints limit implementation in GME.14,15 Alternatively, interprofessional teaching (IPT) has been proposed as an acceptable substitute in the GME setting to improve education and teamwork.8 For PCCM fellows, the intensive care unit is the primary setting for such training.15,16 Our study is a first of its kind in articulating a structure for and efficacy of IPT in a GME ambulatory clinic, with the benefits of IPT supported by improvement in content knowledge and self-efficacy. Additionally, the focus group feedback highlighted that hands-on IPT was directly applicable to patient care in both content and teaching structure; for example, in receiving teaching on inhaler techniques, fellows learned language applicable to instructing their own patients. We believe the success of IPT described in our study is heavily grounded in experiential and social adult learning theories.5

Our survey and focus group results also emphasized the importance of informing curricular strategies with adult learning theory principles, particularly the use of focused prereading as a flipped classroom element. The flipped classroom model requires trainees to complete homework to independently learn foundational knowledge, then to use this new knowledge during educational sessions.17 Its use has been expanded greatly in the undergraduate medical education (UME) space because of increased opportunities for learners to engage in critical thinking and apply knowledge, and is grounded in the social, behavioral, and constructivist learning theories.7 Though flipped classroom approaches have gained considerable traction in the UME setting, uptake in GME has been limited, possibly due to trainee time constraints and priorities for clinical learning.4,18,19 Despite these barriers, one study of flipped classroom education in diabetes for internal medicine residents successfully resulted in improved learner prescribing behavior.18 In our mixed-methods analysis, fellows emphasized the efficacy of the flipped classroom elements and provided session prereading, specifically commenting on its manageable length and importance in scaffolding learning. With the limited time available for ambulatory pulmonary education for PCCM fellows, teaching sessions should strive to be as effective and efficient as possible; utilizing flipped classroom elements with targeted presession materials offers opportunities for optimization. In our study, the success of session prereading was likely impacted by the ambulatory block structure.3

Unlike traditional ambulatory pulmonary curricula, which rely heavily on individual didactics delivered by content experts,2 we engaged expert stakeholders in content creation and revision, but included a larger pool of motivated clinician-educators with strong general pulmonary ambulatory background as instructors. As demonstrated in the NA survey, subspecialty faculty interest in curriculum development was high. By providing materials that can be effectively used by general pulmonologists and by also incorporating interprofessional teachers, session teacher availability was not a limiting factor for implementation and may be a successful approach for smaller fellowship programs. Additionally, incorporating general ambulatory faculty as instructors lowers the need for faculty stakeholders in niche subspecialties, expands the cadre of faculty educators engaging with learners, and leverages the on-the-job insights of ambulatory-heavy faculty.

We also felt it important to create a 3-year curriculum to match the length of PCCM fellowships and to align content with the ABIM blueprint to ensure that objective metrics, such as the ITE score, were targeted. Although the biggest ITE score gain was seen among first-year fellows, survey and focus group results suggested that even senior fellows in our program gained knowledge and found utility in curriculum participation. Focus group comments highlighted the breadth and depth of learning afforded by the curriculum, which helped novice learners and senior fellows alike. Notably, our improvement in ITE scores in AY 2021-2022 come despite a decline in scores following the COVID-19 pandemic seen at other institutions.20,21 Last, the delivery of curricular sessions in a small group setting was heavily valued by both learners and instructors. Benefits of the small group environment highlighted by focus groups, including improved learner engagement and a safer learning environment, are in line with the collaborative and social learning theories.5

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations to our study. First, one of the study authors (K.A.M.) was a fellow during this first year of curriculum implementation and the data collection included in this study, which has impacts on response bias. We tried to mitigate bias by using anonymous surveys and neutral focus group moderators, and by de-identifying focus group transcripts prior to analysis. Second, our NA survey was completed in May 2021 during the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic, which was known to ­negatively impact education and thus may have inflated the difference in responses from the pre- and postintervention surveys.22 Additionally, co-implementation with our ambulatory block model may have conflated postintervention survey results3; our use of a mixed-methods analysis sought to provide triangulation and contextualize survey findings. While our study demonstrates improvements in fellow attitudes and knowledge following curricular sessions, outcomes higher on the Kirkpatrick model such as changes in learner behaviors and patient outcomes were not assessed quantitatively; additional patient-centered outcomes could be the focus of future studies.23 We also acknowledge that demonstrating improvement in ITE score for just 1 year postintervention does not confirm durable curriculum impact. In this manuscript we included data from only the first year of the curriculum, as changes to fellows’ schedules altered curriculum implementation structure, thus introducing too many variables for proper analysis. Additional arenas for future exploration may include how to tier complexity for different learner levels, as well as identifying reinforcement strategies that best enhance learning.

Last, generalizability to smaller programs with fewer resources and alternative clinic structures remains unknown and should be explored. Our study was conducted at a single, large, academic medical center with a large faculty pool from which to draw curriculum instructors, though our curriculum was executed using just 8 faculty instructors. The first year of our curriculum was also implemented concurrently with the novel ambulatory block structure, which provided protected time for 2-hour educational sessions and for learners to complete session prereading, and likely improved session attendance.3 While ambulatory blocks themselves provided fellows with better impressions of ambulatory education, one focus group participant regarded the curriculum as “one of the most important parts of the ambulatory block.”

Conclusion

The first year of our ambulatory pulmonary curriculum, grounded in adult learning theory and highlighting interprofessional teachers, demonstrated improvements in fellow knowledge and comfort. Analysis of a multi-institutional study incorporating our curriculum into different ambulatory education models following completion will be important to fully understand its impact. Regardless, our study serves as a first-of-its-kind model to expand the use of interprofessional teaching and flipped classroom elements to graduate ambulatory pulmonary education.
