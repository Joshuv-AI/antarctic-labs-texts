Prevalence of food addiction and its association with ghrelin and metabolic parameters in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Muhammet Fatih Şahin, Sinem Kiyici, Deniz Sigirli, Said Karaca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to determine the prevalence of food addiction (FA) in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and examine its associations with serum ghrelin levels and metabolic control parameters.MethodsThe study included 160 patients with a median age of 32.0 years (range: 19.0–60.0), of whom 51.3% (n = 82) were female. After recording anthropometric measurements, sociodemographic data, medical histories, and detailed physical examinations were obtained. Following an 8-hour fasting period, venous blood samples were collected to measure serum ghrelin levels and biochemical parameters. Serum ghrelin levels were assessed using the ELISA method. FA was evaluated using the modified Yale Food Addiction Scale 2.0 (mYFAS 2.0).ResultsFA prevalence was 25.6% (n = 41). The FA group had higher median BMI (p = 0.047), while monthly income, diabetes duration, and insulin pump use were lower (p = 0.025, p = 0.009, and p = 0.046, respectively). Age, gender, education, and metabolic control indicators (HbA1c, lipids) were similar between groups. Serum ghrelin levels did not differ based on FA status. However, ghrelin showed modest negative correlations with BMI (r=–0.171; p = 0.030), waist circumference (r=–0.256; p = 0.001), and triglycerides (r=–0.168; p = 0.033).ConclusionOur findings indicate that the prevalence of FA is observed at considerable rates among patients with T1DM. Although no significant differences were observed in metabolic control parameters between patients with and without FA, the FA group exhibited a modestly higher BMI, suggesting a potential association between addiction-like eating behavior and adiposity in adults with T1DM.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02401-z.

Introduction

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by T-cell–mediated destruction of pancreatic β-cells, resulting in absolute insulin deficiency and lifelong dependence on exogenous insulin therapy [1]. Achieving optimal glycemic control in T1DM requires meticulous dietary self-management in addition to insulin replacement. T1DM is also a chronic disease that poses significant psychological challenges. Cognitive, emotional, and social factors are closely associated with treatment outcomes [2]. Although T1DM differs from type 2 diabetes mellitus in its primary pathogenesis, both conditions require strict dietary self-regulation, making behavioral and psychological factors central to long-term metabolic outcomes.

Food addiction (FA) is not currently recognized as a distinct disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). It refers to the compulsive consumption of palatable and high-carbohydrate foods that stimulate the brain’s reward system, and has been conceptualized in analogy to substance use disorders, sharing characteristics such as consuming larger amounts than intended, strong cravings, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and continued use despite negative consequences [3]. Individuals with FA are reported to have higher body mass index (BMI) compared to the general population [4, 5]. In observational studies, FA has been associated with obesity, with cross-sectional reports also showing associations with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and with psychological symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and stress [6–8]. Most of these associations, however, derive from cross-sectional designs and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships. The modified Yale Food Addiction Scale 2.0 (mYFAS 2.0) is a validated instrument used internationally to assess FA based on the DSM-5 substance-related and addictive disorders criteria [9, 10]. The scale evaluates 12 addiction-like symptoms using a brief 13-item structure. Compared with the full Yale Food Addiction Scale version 2.0 (YFAS 2.0), which consists of 35 items, the mYFAS 2.0 has a higher diagnostic threshold while maintaining strong psychometric validity, making it suitable for clinical and epidemiological studies.

Despite increasing interest in eating-related behaviors in individuals with T1DM, FA has not been extensively investigated in adult T1DM populations. Several eating-related behaviors have been previously studied in T1DM, including disordered eating, emotional eating, and insulin omission. However, FA represents a distinct construct grounded in DSM-5 substance use disorder criteria. To date, FA has not been systematically evaluated in adults with T1DM. Only one recent study has explored FA-related behaviors, focusing on adolescents with T1DM [11].

FA prevalence in the general population is estimated at approximately 10%, whereas rates increase to 20–25% in individuals with obesity [4, 5]. In cross-sectional studies of individuals with T2DM, FA has been associated with poorer glycemic regulation, higher BMI and waist circumference, and increased psychological burden [6–8], although the directionality of these associations remains unclear. These findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of addiction-like eating behaviors in populations requiring strict nutritional adherence.

Beyond the general importance of dietary self-management in T1DM, several mechanistic and behavioral pathways may link this condition to addiction-like eating behavior. First, the strict carbohydrate counting and dietary restraint required in T1DM may paradoxically increase the salience of palatable foods, potentially fostering disinhibited eating in response to chronic dietary restriction. Second, recurrent hypoglycemic episodes are typically managed by rapid intake of high-glycemic-index carbohydrates, which may repeatedly activate dopaminergic reward pathways and could, over time, reinforce reward-driven eating patterns. Third, the psychological burden of a lifelong chronic illness — including elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and diabetes-related distress — may promote emotional or comfort-driven eating, which overlaps phenotypically with FA. Together, these considerations suggest that FA is not only theoretically relevant but may have specific behavioral and neuroendocrine substrates in T1DM that warrant systematic investigation.

Ghrelin is gut-derived hormone whose most notable physiological function is the regulation of appetite and energy homeostasis [12, 13]. Furthermore, ghrelin can suppress insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells and promote lipogenesis, creating a bidirectional metabolic interaction that may be perturbed in insulin-deficient states. Together, these properties make ghrelin both metabolically relevant and mechanistically plausible as a hormonal correlate of FA in T1DM, providing the rationale for measuring fasting ghrelin in the present study [14, 15]. Beyond its role in hunger signaling, ghrelin activates dopaminergic projections in the mesolimbic reward pathway, thereby modulating the reinforcing properties of both addictive substances (e.g., alcohol, tobacco, opioids) and palatable, energy-dense foods [16, 17]. This dual role positions ghrelin as a candidate biomarker linking metabolic and reward-related processes — and, by extension, as a plausible mediator of addiction-like eating behavior such as FA. Direct empirical evidence linking ghrelin to FA in humans, however, remains limited and inconsistent: a study by Lopez-Aguilar et al. [18] reported a positive association between acylated ghrelin and FA scores in adults with normal weight and obesity, whereas a recent systematic review concluded that the overall evidence on ghrelin and FA is heterogeneous, in part due to methodological variability across studies [19].

The regulation of ghrelin in T1DM is complex and remains inconsistent across studies. Some reports suggest that insulin exposure is a major determinant of circulating ghrelin levels, with evidence showing that ghrelin suppression after meals requires adequate insulin signaling [20]. Hormonal and metabolic alterations associated with T1DM, including changes related to glycemic control, nutritional status, and insulin therapy, further contribute to the heterogeneity of ghrelin responses in this population [21]. Given ghrelin’s dual role in metabolic regulation and reward-driven eating, understanding its behavior in individuals with T1DM is particularly relevant.

Several T1DM-specific factors may also act as confounders in studies of eating behavior in this population. BMI is associated with both FA and T1DM and may mediate or modify their relationship. Total daily insulin dose can influence appetite, weight, and the frequency of hypoglycemia, which in turn requires repeated intake of high-glycemic-index carbohydrates and may reinforce reward-driven eating. Glycemic variability and the chronic burden of self-monitoring may further shape eating behavior in ways that overlap with addiction-like patterns. We accounted for these factors by recording BMI, total daily insulin dose, frequency of hypoglycemic episodes, and duration of diabetes, and by including key variables in our multivariable analyses.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has specifically reported the prevalence of FA in adults with T1DM or examined its association with circulating ghrelin levels in this population.

We hypothesized that FA would be prevalent among adults with T1DM and would be associated with adverse metabolic and clinical characteristics, including higher BMI and poorer metabolic control. We further hypothesized that serum ghrelin levels would differ between individuals with and without FA, based on existing — albeit limited and inconsistent — evidence linking ghrelin to addiction-like eating behaviors [18, 19, 22, 23]. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine the prevalence of FA in patients with T1DM and to examine its relationship with serum ghrelin levels and metabolic control parameters.

Methods

Study population

This was a single-center, cross-sectional study conducted among adult patients with T1DM to investigate the prevalence of FA and its association with ghrelin levels and metabolic parameters. Participants were consecutively recruited between January 2022 and December 2022, and all clinical, biochemical, and questionnaire-based data were collected during this predefined study period. The diagnosis of T1DM was confirmed based on medical records, including clinical history and physician diagnosis documented in the hospital electronic health system. Eligible participants were identified through outpatient clinic records and were consecutively invited to participate during routine follow-up visits. This study was conducted at a tertiary Endocrinology and Metabolism Outpatient Clinic with 160 patients aged between 18 and 65 years who had been diagnosed with T1DM. Patients with secondary diabetes, those who were pregnant or lactating, individuals using medications that could affect appetite, and those unable to comprehend the scales used in the psychological assessment were excluded. Of the 189 patients with T1DM screened for eligibility during the recruitment period, 29 were excluded: 15 declined to participate, 6 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 8 had incomplete clinical or questionnaire data. The remaining 160 patients constituted the final study sample (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the local ethics committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2011-KAEK-25 2022/01–12). All participants gave written informed consent before enrollment.

Measures

All participants underwent a detailed medical assessment, including sociodemographic information, personal and family history, eating habits, presence of minor or major hypoglycemic episodes, nutrition and exercise patterns. Dietary adherence and hypoglycemia frequency were assessed using study-specific operational definitions during structured face-to-face clinical interviews, rather than with formally validated standardized instruments. Main meals were defined as the three principal daily meals (breakfast, lunch, and dinner). Snacks were defined as any food intake occurring between main meals. Adequate dietary adherence” was defined as self-reported compliance with the prescribed diabetes-specific dietary recommendations as judged during the interview, and hypoglycemia frequency was based on the patient’s self-reported average monthly number of mild and severe hypoglycemic episodes over the preceding three months. Hypoglycemia frequency, dietary adherence, and eating habits were based solely on patient self-report and were not corroborated by CGM downloads, glucose meter records, food diaries, or other external data sources. As such, these variables are subject to recall and social desirability biases inherent to self-report methodology, and findings related to them should be interpreted accordingly. All participants were treated with intensive insulin therapy, either as multiple daily injections in a basal–bolus regimen (long-acting basal insulin combined with rapid-acting prandial insulin) or via continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (insulin pump). Sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, education level, marital status, monthly income, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, were collected using a structured questionnaire administered during face-to-face interviews.

Anthropometric measurements

Body weight and height were measured directly by trained healthcare personnel using standardized equipment, and BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Weight and body fat percentage were measured using a Tanita® device. A flexible tape measure was used to assess WC.

Laboratory measurements

Venous blood samples were collected in the morning after an overnight fasting period of at least 8 h to ensure standardized sampling conditions. Samples for routine biochemical analyses (fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, AST, ALT, and creatinine) and ghrelin measurement were collected into serum separator tubes (SST, yellow-top tubes with clot activator and separator gel), whereas samples for HbA1c and complete blood count were collected into K2-EDTA tubes (purple-top tubes). All laboratory analyses were performed at the Central Biochemistry Laboratory of Bursa Yüksek İhtisas Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary referral, university-affiliated training and research hospital. The laboratory operates under the institutional quality management system of the Turkish Ministry of Health and routinely participates in internal and external quality control programs to ensure analytical accuracy and reproducibility. Routine biochemical parameters were analyzed in serum on an Abbott Architect c16000 automated chemistry analyzer (Abbott Diagnostics, Abbott Park, IL, USA) using standard spectrophotometric methods. The Friedewald formula was used to calculate LDL-C levels if TG was less than 400 mg/dL [24]. HbA1c levels were analyzed chromatographically, by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Lifotronic H9 hemoglobin analyzer (Shenzhen Lifotronic Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Complete blood count was measured on a Mindray BC-6200 automated hematology analyzer (Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Shenzhen, China).

For ghrelin determination, a separate venous blood sample was collected into an SST tube. After complete clotting, samples were centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 10 min, and the separated serum was aliquoted, stored at − 80 °C, and thawed only once before analysis to minimize variability related to freeze–thaw cycles. Ghrelin concentrations were measured by ELISA on an Analytik Jena FlashScan S12 microplate reader (Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany) using a Human GHRL (Ghrelin) ELISA Kit (Elabscience, USA) with a sensitivity of 0.1 ng/mL and a detection range of 0.16–10 ng/mL (intra-assay variation: 3.51–6%). Readings were obtained at 450 nm ± 2. Ghrelin was selected as the primary hormonal biomarker of interest based on its established role in dopaminergic reward signaling and its prior association with addiction-related behaviors. Other appetite- and satiety-regulating hormones (e.g., leptin, insulin, peptide YY, GLP-1) were not assessed in the present study, as the central hypothesis specifically concerned the addiction-related rather than the satiety-related dimension of eating behavior. Serum ghrelin concentrations were measured as fasting total ghrelin levels only; acylated and des-acyl ghrelin isoforms were not assessed, and postprandial ghrelin responses were not evaluated in the present study.

Modified yale food addiction scale

FA was assessed using the mYFAS 2.0, a validated 13-item self-report instrument based on DSM-5 substance use disorder criteria [25]. The Turkish adaptation, including validity and reliability testing, has been published previously [26]. The scale assesses eating behaviors over the previous 12 months using a Likert-type response format. In the present study, the internal consistency of the mYFAS 2.0 was high, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.84. The scale evaluates addiction-like eating behaviors such as impaired control, continued consumption despite negative consequences, withdrawal, and clinically significant distress. Symptom counts were used to classify FA severity as follows: 0–1 symptoms: no FA; 2–3: mild FA; 4–5: moderate FA; ≥6: severe FA.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Variables that followed a normal distribution (e.g., waist circumference, total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C) are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution (e.g., age, BMI, duration of diabetes, total daily insulin dose, HbA1c, fasting glucose, triglycerides, serum ghrelin, frequency of hypoglycemic episodes) are presented as median with interquartile range (Q1–Q3), and group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons were performed using the Pearson chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or Fisher-Freeman-Halton test, as appropriate. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to examine relationships between continuous and ordinal variables. Subgroup analysis according to WHO BMI categories (underweight < 18.5; normal 18.5–24.9; overweight 25.0–29.9; obese ≥ 30 kg/m²) was performed using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test (because some cells had expected counts < 5), and the linear-by-linear association test was used to assess linear trends in FA prevalence across ordered BMI categories. Univariate group comparisons (Tables 1 and 2) and Spearman correlations (Tables 3 and 4) were treated as descriptive and hypothesis-generating analyses; consequently, formal correction for multiple testing was not applied. Borderline-significant findings (p ≈ 0.03–0.05) should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution and viewed as preliminary, requiring replication in larger and confirmatory studies.

To identify independent predictors of FA, a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in univariate analyses and those considered clinically relevant were included in the model. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Proportions were reported with 95% confidence intervals. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0.

Multicollinearity among predictors in the multivariable logistic regression model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values. All VIFs were below 5, and tolerance values were above 0.3, indicating that multicollinearity was within acceptable limits and did not compromise the stability of the model coefficients.

The minimum required sample size was calculated using a single-proportion precision method. Assuming an expected FA prevalence of 25%, a 95% confidence level, and a 7% margin of error, a sample of 149 participants was determined to be necessary. The expected prevalence of FA was assumed to be 25% based on previously published studies reporting lower prevalence rates in healthy populations and comparable prevalence rates in cohorts with T2DM. At the time of study design, no prevalence data were available for adult patients with T1DM; therefore, this estimate was selected as a literature-informed and conservative assumption for sample size calculation. A margin of error of 7% was chosen to achieve acceptable statistical precision while maintaining feasibility within the context of a single-center study. The final study sample (n = 160) exceeded this threshold. A post-hoc power analysis indicated that this sample size provided > 80% statistical power (at α = 0.05) to detect medium effect sizes in comparative analyses. Furthermore, the presence of 41 FA events provided approximately 6 events per predictor variable in the multivariable logistic regression model. While lower than the traditional rule of 10, methodological studies suggest that 5–9 events per variable are acceptable for maintaining model stability and validity in observational studies [27]. Effect size measures were calculated to complement p values and to provide information on the magnitude of group differences. Cohen’s d was used for normally distributed continuous variables, r (Z/√N) for Mann–Whitney U tests, odds ratios for binary categorical variables, and Cramer’s V for multi-category categorical variables, based on standard formulas derived from SPSS output.

Results

A total of 189 patients with T1DM were assessed for eligibility, of whom 160 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The final study cohort had a median age of 32.0 years (range: 19–60); 51.3% (n = 82) were female. According to the mYFAS 2.0 scale, 41 patients (25.6%) met the criteria for FA, of whom 16 (10.0%) had mild, 11 (6.9%) had moderate, and 14 (8.7%) had severe FA. Age and gender distribution were similar between individuals with and without FA (p = 0.865 and p = 0.996, respectively). The percentage of patients with low monthly income was higher in patients with FA than in those without FA (78.0% vs. 62.2%; p = 0.025), whereas education levels did not differ (p = 0.90) (Table 1).

Patients with FA had a higher BMI (24.0 vs. 23.0 kg/m²; p = 0.047), and WC (89.0 ± 10.1 vs. 85.9 ± 11.0 cm; p = 0.039) than those without FA. The median duration of diabetes was shorter in the FA group (8.0 vs. 12.0 years; p = 0.009). Insulin pump use was also substantially lower among patients with FA (2.4% vs. 15.1%; p = 0.046). No significant differences were observed regarding smoking status, alcohol consumption, dietary adherence, meal patterns, snacking behavior, or hypoglycemia frequency between the two groups (all p > 0.05) (Tables 1 and 2).

When stratified by WHO BMI categories, FA prevalence increased progressively from 7.7% in underweight (n = 13), to 23.1% in normal-weight (n = 78), 28.0% in overweight (n = 50), and 42.1% in obese (n = 19) participants. Although the omnibus comparison did not reach statistical significance (Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, p = 0.168), the test for linear trend was statistically significant (linear-by-linear association, p = 0.028), indicating a robust monotonic relationship between BMI category and FA prevalence (Supplementary Table S1).

No between-group differences were identified in vascular complications, glycemic control parameters, serum lipid profiles, or fasting ghrelin concentrations (all p > 0.05; Table 2).

A weak negative correlation was detected between FA severity and duration of diabetes (ρ = − 0.178, p = 0.024), whereas no meaningful correlations were found between FA severity and BMI, HbA1c, fasting glucose, lipid parameters, hypoglycemia frequency, or serum ghrelin levels (all p > 0.05) (Table 3). Serum ghrelin showed a weak inverse correlation with BMI (ρ = − 0.171, p = 0.030), waist circumference (ρ = − 0.256, p = 0.001), and triglyceride levels (ρ = − 0.168, p = 0.033), but not with other metabolic parameters (Table 4).

A multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to determine independent predictors of FA. The model included age, sex, BMI, waist circumference, diabetes duration, insulin pump use, and monthly income. Shorter diabetes duration remained significantly associated with FA (Adjusted OR: 0.93; 95% CI: 0.88–0.98; p = 0.009), indicating that the likelihood of FA decreases as disease duration increases. Age, sex, BMI, waist circumference, monthly income and insulin pump use were not independently related to FA (all p > 0.05; Table 5).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of adults with T1DM, FA was found to be relatively common and was associated with higher BMI and lower socioeconomic status. In contrast, FA was not associated with glycemic control, lipid parameters, or fasting serum ghrelin levels. These findings suggest that FA may be a behavioral factor of potential relevance in T1DM, although our data show that its associations with conventional metabolic indicators (HbA1c, lipid profile) and fasting ghrelin are limited. The regulation of metabolic outcomes in this population is therefore multifactorial, and the clinical significance of FA in T1DM warrants further investigation.

FA is characterized by compulsive consumption of high-glycemic index and fatty foods, driven by dopaminergic reward mechanisms similar to those observed in substance use disorders [28, 29]. Although not formally recognized in the DSM-5, FA has been consistently associated with weight gain, obesity, and psychological disturbances such as depression, anxiety, and stress [8, 30]. Several studies have also suggested a potential link between FA and the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus [6].

In contrast, limited data exist on FA in individuals with T1DM. Overweight and obesity are traditionally less common in T1DM than in T2DM; however, recent global trends indicate an increasing prevalence of obesity in T1DM populations [21]. Eating disturbances and psychological comorbidities are also frequently reported in these patients [31, 32]. At a conceptual level, FA has been described in the broader literature as overlapping with — but not identical to — other eating-related disturbances such as disordered eating patterns, binge eating disorder, and diabetes-specific maladaptive behaviors (e.g., insulin omission). Whether these constructs are empirically distinct or substantially overlapping in T1DM, however, could not be directly evaluated in the present study, as we assessed only FA (using the mYFAS 2.0) and did not concurrently measure disordered eating, binge eating, or insulin-related maladaptive behaviors. This conceptual–empirical distinction therefore remains a question for future research employing multiple validated instruments in T1DM cohorts.

In the present study, the prevalence of FA among adults with T1DM was 25.6%, as assessed by the modified Yale Food Addiction Scale 2.0 (mYFAS 2.0). For context, systematic reviews using the Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS) and its modified version (mYFAS) have reported pooled FA prevalence of approximately 10–15% in non-clinical populations and 20–25% in individuals with obesity [4, 7]. The 25.6% prevalence observed in our T1DM cohort is therefore comparable to that reported in obese populations and higher than that reported in non-clinical adult samples, although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in sample composition, recruitment setting, and the use of YFAS versus mYFAS [6, 8, 33, 34]. The relatively narrow confidence interval suggests adequate sample size and a stable prevalence estimate. To the best of our knowledge, there are no prior studies that specifically report the prevalence of FA in adults with T1DM. These findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of FA even in T1DM, a condition characterized primarily by insulin deficiency rather than insulin resistance.

Previous studies have reported that FA is more prevalent in women and tends to increase with age [4]. However, in our study, no significant differences were found between the FA and non-FA groups in terms of age or gender. In the present study, FA was more frequent among individuals with lower income, which may be related to the greater availability and consumption of inexpensive, high-calorie foods that strongly activate dopaminergic reward pathways. A Brazilian study found no association between occupation and FA [35]; our study, however, evaluated income level instead of occupation, suggesting that socioeconomic indicators such as income may be more relevant.

Both monthly income and insulin pump use were significantly associated with FA in univariate comparisons but lost statistical significance after multivariable adjustment. These changes are most consistent with confounding by clinical and sociodemographic factors that are themselves correlated with each other. Lower monthly income is typically associated with reduced access to advanced diabetes technologies and may also overlap with shorter educational attainment, less structured diabetes education, and greater psychosocial stress — factors that may independently increase the risk of addiction-like eating. Insulin pump use, in turn, is more frequent among patients with longer diabetes duration, higher socioeconomic status, and stronger self-management capacity. Once these correlated variables (e.g., diabetes duration, monthly income) are simultaneously adjusted for in the multivariable model, the independent contribution of either monthly income or insulin pump use becomes attenuated. This pattern suggests that monthly income and insulin pump use act primarily as markers of broader sociodemographic and clinical profiles rather than as independent determinants of FA.

Our findings also demonstrated that individuals with FA had shorter diabetes duration and lower insulin pump use compared to those without FA. Nicolau et al. [34] found no association between diabetes duration and FA in T2DM patients. This discrepancy may reflect pathophysiological differences: T2DM progresses gradually with insulin resistance, whereas T1DM typically presents acutely with rapid beta-cell loss. Patients with T1DM often receive structured diabetes education and initiate insulin therapy early, which may be associated with better dietary self-regulation in those with longer disease duration. However, given the cross-sectional design of our study, this association cannot be interpreted as evidence that FA prevalence decreases over time, and alternative explanations — such as differential selection of patients with shorter versus longer disease duration into the cohort, or generational differences in dietary environment — cannot be excluded. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between diabetes duration and FA. The lower rate of insulin pump use among patients with FA may reflect patient selection, since pumps tend to be prescribed to patients with stronger self-management capacity, higher socioeconomic status, and fewer psychiatric comorbidities [36, 37]. However, this interpretation is speculative; we did not directly assess prescribing decisions, adherence patterns, or psychiatric comorbidities in the present study, and the imprecise estimate for insulin pump use in our multivariable model further argues against drawing strong conclusions from this finding.

Patients with FA had a higher BMI and waist circumference than those without FA. However, both differences reached only borderline statistical significance (p = 0.047 and p = 0.039, respectively) and were associated with small effect sizes (r = 0.16 for BMI; Cohen’s d = 0.29 for waist circumference; Table 1). These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and viewed as suggestive rather than definitive evidence of an anthropometric difference between groups. Nevertheless, the consistent direction of effects across both anthropometric measures, together with the statistically significant linear trend in FA prevalence across WHO BMI categories (p = 0.028), supports a modest but coherent positive association between adiposity and FA in this T1DM cohort. This relationship has been demonstrated in the general population [4, 5] in individuals with obesity [28] and in patients with T2DM [34]. However, given the cross-sectional design of our study, the directionality of this association cannot be determined: FA may contribute to higher adiposity through impaired dietary self-regulation, or higher adiposity may itself increase the likelihood of developing addiction-like eating patterns, or both may share common upstream determinants such as psychosocial stress or genetic susceptibility. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these relationships [21]. Therefore, our study also investigated whether there were differences in metabolic control parameters and vascular complications between FA and non-FA groups.

Despite these anthropometric differences, no significant differences were found in HbA1c, fasting glucose, or lipid parameters between groups. Nicolau et al. [34] reported higher fasting glucose and HbA1c in T2DM patients with FA, though lipid profiles were similar. Our findings align with reports in individuals with obesity, where results have been inconsistent [7, 38]. Variability in study design, sample size, and the distinct pathophysiology of T1DM versus T2DM may explain these discrepancies.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the FA and non-FA groups regarding the prevalence of vascular complications. Numerically higher complication rates were noted in the FA group, but these differences did not reach statistical significance and should not be over-interpreted. Although Nicolau et al. [34] reported higher microvascular complication rates in T2DM patients with FA, our study was not powered to detect such associations in T1DM, and larger, adequately powered studies are needed to address this question. Hypoglycemia frequency did not differ significantly between groups, although a numerical trend toward more frequent hypoglycemic episodes in the FA group was observed. Given the lack of statistical significance and the self-reported nature of hypoglycemia data (see Limitations), this observation should be considered exploratory rather than indicative of a meaningful association.

In our study, serum ghrelin levels were negatively correlated with BMI, waist circumference, and triglycerides, consistent with previous findings in obesity and T2DM populations [22, 39, 40] supporting the role of ghrelin in appetite regulation and energy balance. However, no significant differences in ghrelin concentrations were observed between patients with and without FA. Prior studies in T1DM have reported inconsistent results, with some showing reduced ghrelin at disease onset and others describing elevated levels at diagnosis that declined following insulin therapy [20]. The fact that only fasting ghrelin was measured in our study may partly explain the lack of group differences.

Beyond its metabolic role, ghrelin has also been implicated in reward-related behaviors. Experimental studies demonstrate that ghrelin activates dopaminergic signaling in the mesolimbic pathway, thereby enhancing the rewarding value of food and addictive substances [16, 17, 23]. Human studies, however, have produced heterogeneous results. Lopez et al. [18] reported a positive association between ghrelin and FA in 130 individuals with obesity and individuals with normal weight, whereas a recent systematic review summarizing 16 studies concluded that the evidence linking ghrelin and FA is limited and inconsistent [19], largely due to methodological heterogeneity. In our study, no such association was observed in patients with T1DM, suggesting that FA in this population may involve additional or distinct mechanisms beyond ghrelin signaling. Several alternative explanations should be considered for the absence of a significant difference in fasting ghrelin levels between participants with and without FA. First, ghrelin has a short circulating half-life and exhibits considerable circadian, day-to-day, and inter-individual variability; a single fasting measurement may therefore have limited sensitivity to capture group-level differences. Second, although all samples were collected after at least 8 h of overnight fasting, longer or more standardized fasting durations might reveal different patterns of ghrelin secretion. Third, T1DM is a heterogeneous condition in which circulating ghrelin levels may be influenced by multiple factors including insulin regimen, residual β-cell function, glycemic state, and disease duration; this physiological heterogeneity may have obscured a potential association with FA. Finally, insufficient statistical power cannot be excluded: with 41 participants in the FA group, our study may have been underpowered to detect small between-group differences in ghrelin concentrations.

Only fasting total ghrelin levels were measured in the present study. Acylated ghrelin, which represents the biologically active form directly involved in appetite regulation and energy homeostasis, was not assessed. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously, as total ghrelin concentrations may not fully reflect biologically active ghrelin signaling. In addition, postprandial ghrelin dynamics were not evaluated. Since meal-related suppression and recovery responses of ghrelin may provide additional insight into eating behavior and food addiction mechanisms, the absence of postprandial measurements represents an important limitation. Future studies incorporating both acylated ghrelin and dynamic postprandial assessments may help clarify the complex relationship between ghrelin physiology and food addiction in patients with Type 1 diabetes mellitus. Taken together, our null finding regarding fasting total ghrelin and FA does not support — but also cannot rule out — a role for ghrelin signaling in food addiction in T1DM. Given the methodological limitations outlined above and the heterogeneous prior literature [23], this question remains open and warrants larger studies incorporating acylated ghrelin and postprandial sampling.

FA shares phenotypic and conceptual features with several psychological constructs that have been more extensively studied in T1DM. Depression and anxiety are well-documented comorbidities of T1DM and are themselves associated with disordered eating patterns and impaired glycemic self-management. Bidirectional relationships have been described in the broader literature, where depression may promote emotional or reward-driven eating, while addiction-like eating may further reinforce depressive symptomatology through weight gain, guilt, or repeated loss-of-control episodes. Antidepressant use, particularly with agents that affect appetite and weight (e.g., serotonergic and tricyclic compounds), may further modify eating behavior and complicate the assessment of FA in clinical samples. In T1DM specifically, eating disorder symptomatology — including subclinical features overlapping with binge eating disorder — and intentional insulin omission for weight control (sometimes referred to as ‘diabulimia’) represent additional behavioral phenotypes that may share or interact with FA-like patterns. Although these psychological constructs were not formally assessed in the present study, they are likely relevant contextual factors and should be incorporated in future research using validated instruments alongside FA assessment.

From a research perspective, our findings suggest that FA may be a behavioral phenomenon worth further study in T1DM, particularly in patients with higher BMI or lower socioeconomic status. However, our study did not demonstrate associations between FA and glycemic control, lipid profile, ghrelin levels, or vascular complications, and thus does not provide sufficient evidence to support routine or targeted FA screening as part of clinical practice in T1DM at this time. Whether the identification of FA could meaningfully inform clinical management or improve outcomes in this population remains an open question and should be evaluated in larger, prospective studies before any screening strategy is implemented.

It should be acknowledged that several mechanistic interpretations offered in this Discussion — including those regarding insulin pump prescribing patterns, the role of structured diabetes education over time, and dopaminergic reward-related vulnerability in T1DM — are inferred from the broader literature and were not directly tested in the present cross-sectional study. These explanations should therefore be regarded as hypotheses requiring confirmation in future longitudinal and mechanistic research.

Conclusion

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the relatively small number of FA cases (n = 41) limited the statistical power of subgroup and multivariable analyses, contributed to the wide confidence intervals observed for some predictors (notably insulin pump use), and resulted in approximately 6 events per predictor variable in the multivariable logistic regression model — below the conventional 10:1 ratio. Although methodological studies suggest that 5–9 events per variable can be acceptable for observational research [25], the present model may still be susceptible to overfitting and wider-than-optimal confidence intervals, and the reported adjusted odds ratios should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents causal or directional inferences, including with respect to the relationship between diabetes duration, BMI, socioeconomic status, and FA. Third, all behavioral, dietary, and clinical variables — including FA (assessed by mYFAS 2.0), dietary adherence, and hypoglycemia frequency — were obtained by patient self-report, without external corroboration (e.g., CGM data, food diaries, or validated psychological instruments such as those for depression, anxiety, or eating disorder symptomatology), introducing the possibility of recall and social desirability biases. Dietary adherence and hypoglycemia frequency were assessed using study-specific operational definitions rather than validated standardized instruments (e.g., SDSCA for dietary adherence; the Clarke or Gold scores for hypoglycemia awareness). Fourth, the available mechanistic data are limited: only fasting total ghrelin was measured, while acylated and des-acyl isoforms, postprandial ghrelin dynamics, and other appetite- and reward-regulating hormones (e.g., leptin, insulin, peptide YY, GLP-1) were not assessed; CGM-based glycemic variability metrics (e.g., glucose SD, MAGE, time-in-range) were also unavailable, as routine CGM use was not yet widespread during the recruitment period. Fifth, univariate group comparisons and Spearman correlations were treated as descriptive analyses, and no correction for multiple testing was applied; some of the borderline-significant associations (e.g., BMI, waist circumference, monthly income, ghrelin correlations) may therefore reflect type I error and require confirmation in independent cohorts. Sixth, psychological factors known to be associated with food addiction — including depression, anxiety, stress, and eating disorder symptomatology — were not formally assessed in the present study, preventing adjustment for these factors in the multivariable model.

The external validity of our findings is limited by several characteristics of the cohort. First, this was a single-center study conducted at one tertiary endocrinology clinic, which may not reflect the broader spectrum of T1DM care in different geographic regions, healthcare systems, or practice settings. Second, the cohort consisted predominantly of patients with very low to low monthly income, which may not be representative of T1DM populations in other socioeconomic contexts and may have influenced the prevalence and pattern of FA-related behaviors observed. Third, the proportion of patients using advanced diabetes technology — particularly continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (insulin pump) — was relatively low (~ 12% of the total cohort), reflecting the limited availability and reimbursement of such technologies during the study period in our setting. Findings related to insulin pump use and behavioral self-management may therefore not generalize to T1DM populations with broader access to technology, including those routinely using continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), automated insulin delivery, or hybrid closed-loop systems. Fourth, the absence of a non-diabetic comparison group prevents direct attribution of the observed FA prevalence or fasting ghrelin patterns to T1DM specifically. Fifth, a formal non-response analysis comparing participants with the 15 patients who declined participation could not be performed, as these individuals did not provide informed consent for the use of their clinical data; the possibility of selection bias therefore cannot be excluded. Replication in multicenter cohorts with diverse socioeconomic and technological profiles, and including matched non-diabetic and T2DM comparison groups, is needed to assess the generalizability of these findings.

To the best of our knowledge, and based on a non-systematic review of the available literature at the time of writing, this is among the earliest studies to investigate the prevalence and metabolic correlates of FA in adults with T1DM, and the first to examine the relationship between fasting ghrelin levels and FA in this specific population. We did not, however, perform a formal systematic literature search, and we acknowledge that the priority claim should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. We found that FA is relatively common and associated with higher BMI and lower socioeconomic status, but not with glycemic control, lipid parameters, or serum ghrelin levels. As the first study to directly assess the relationship between ghrelin and FA in this population, our work fills an important gap and highlights the need for larger, multicenter prospective studies evaluating both fasting and postprandial ghrelin responses to clarify this relationship. These findings highlight an overlooked behavioral component in T1DM management and suggest that screening for FA may be beneficial in selected patients. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether FA contributes to long-term weight gain, metabolic deterioration, or vascular complications in T1DM. Future research should also explore acylated and des-acyl ghrelin isoforms to clarify the biological pathways underlying these associations.
