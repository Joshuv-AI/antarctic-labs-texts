EPYC promotes laryngeal cancer carcinogenesis and angiogenesis by docking to VEGFR1: A novel therapeutic target
Authors: Dongmei Zhou, Fan Liu, Xiaoqi Li, Fei Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEPYC is a member of a small family of leucine-rich repetitive proteoglycans (SLRPs) and contributes to the carcinogenesis of different cancers. However, the role of EPYC in laryngeal cancer (LC) and angiogenesis is still unclear. This study aimed to determine the biological significance of EPYC in laryngeal carcinogenesis and angiogenesis. Here, we found that EPYC was significantly upregulated in LC tissues vs. normal tissues, both in public databases and in in-house LC tissue samples. EPYC knockdown in LC cells attenuated laryngeal carcinogenesis and angiogenesis, while the addition of rhEPYC partially restored tumor growth and neovascularization. Mechanically, EPYC may promote the degradation of VEGFR1 and reduce its ability to act as a decoy of VEGF-A to increase VEGF-A secretion and promote angiogenesis. In conclusion, we showed that EPYC is overexpressed in LC and promotes LC carcinogenesis and angiogenesis by docking to VEGFR1, suggesting EPYC as a novel therapeutic target for LC.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Cancer, Therapeutics

Introduction

The successful treatment of malignant tumors is an important obstacle in improving life expectancy worldwide.1 Approximately 5% of malignant tumors are head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs), 90% of which originate from the epithelium of the head and neck. Laryngeal cancer (LC) is the most common head and neck tumor; more than 25 thousand new cases are diagnosed every year, and 13 thousand deaths are reported every year in China.2 More than two-thirds (65%) of patients have stage III-IV disease, and the 5-year survival rate is less than 40%.3 The clinical treatment of LC usually involves a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. However, the progression of LC cannot be effectively inhibited. Therefore, it is important to explore the mechanisms underlying LC.

One of the hallmarks of malignant tumors is angiogenesis,4 a process by which blood vessels in tumors provide oxygen and nutrition for the tumor cells, enabling rapid tumor growth and providing routes for distant metastasis.5,6 A previous study proposed microvascular density (MVD) as a prognostic biomarker for LC.7 When vascular support is lacking, tumors may become necrotic or apoptotic, so antiangiogenic therapy is an important potential treatment option. However, the therapeutic value of antiangiogenic agents in LC remains unclear.8 Therefore, clarification of the mechanism of angiogenesis in LC is necessary to identify new therapeutic targets.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in the progression of malignant tumors, including LC. The interaction between LC cells and the tumor microenvironment promotes tumor growth and metastasis. The TME consists of stromal cells, among which vascular endothelial cells are important components, and the extracellular matrix (ECM). The ECM is a noncellular component of the TME that consists of fibrillar and structural proteins, proteoglycans, integrins, and proteases.9 Epiphycan (EPYC), also known as sulfated chitosan proteoglycan 3 (DSPG3) or proteoglycan Lb (PG Lb), is a proteoglycan that has been reported to participate in the development of cartilage, the cochlea, the cornea, and other tissues.10 There have been reports that EPYC expression is upregulated in malignant tumors such as pancreatic cancer11 and that it regulates biological processes such as cell adhesion, but there have been no reports of the association of EPYC with HNSCC.

According to the findings of this study, we explored EPYC’s expression in LC and its role in carcinogenesis and angiogenesis. These findings may inform studies on LC and the development of new antiangiogenic agents.

Results

Identification of key AR-DEGs in a public database

To investigate AR genes in LC, we identified 189, 512, and 581 upregulated genes in the GSE51985, GSE84957, and TCGA-HNSC datasets, respectively, and visualized them in a volcano plot (Figure 1A). By intersecting these genes from the three datasets, we obtained 30 differentially DEGs. GO function and KEGG pathway analyses were performed, and the most significant biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF) terms were “extracellular matrix organization”, “extracellular region” and “extracellular structural”, respectively (Figures S1A–S1C). The DEGs were involved in many ECM-receptor interactions and protein digestion and absorption pathways (Figure S1D), as shown by KEGG analysis.

To obtain angiogenesis-related DEGs (AR-DEGs), we intersected the 30 genes with angiogenesis-related genes obtained from the GeneCards database and obtained 19 AR-DEGs (Figure 1B; Table S1). To further identify key genes for LC angiogenesis and establish a diagnostic model, we employed three machine learning algorithms in the GSE65858 dataset, a GEO dataset containing gene expression and survival information. Using the LASSO regression algorithm, six genes among the DEGs were identified as potential key AR DEGs (EPYC, HOXD1, MMP1, MMP11, SERPINE1, and SPP1), and the risk score formula was as follows: LCA-RS = EPYC∗0.08 + HOXD10∗0.07 + MMP1∗0.07 + MMP11∗0.07 - NRG1∗0.13 + SERPINE1∗0.04 + SPP1∗0.15 (Figures 1C and 1D). Based on the LCA-RS, the patients in the GSE65858 dataset were divided into high- and low-risk subgroups; patients with a high LCA-RS had poorer survival than those with a low LCA-RS (Figures S2A and S2B). Similar results were obtained for the GSE27020 cohort, which was used as the validation cohort (Figures S2C and S2D). Using the RF algorithm and the MeanDecreaseAccuracy index as the importance score, we identified 10 potential key AR DEGs (MMP12, MFAP2, COL1A1, MMP11, SERPINE1, MMP1, EPYC, PLAU, SPP1, and BIRC5, Figure 1E). According to the SVM-RFE algorithm, the accuracy was maximized when the number of features was 18 and the top 5 variables out of 18 was identified (PLAU, SPP1, EPYC, FST, and MMP1, Figure 1F). Finally 3 genes (EPYC, SPP1, and MMP1) were found to be overlapped in the Venn diagram (Figure 1G); these 3 genes were selected as the key AR DEGs.

Validation of the key genes in LC patients

Among the 3 key AR DEGs, matrix metalloproteinases12 and phosphoproteins13 have been proven to be associated with angiogenesis, so we selected EPYC for further study. We explored the association between EPYC expression and somatic mutations in the TCGA-HNSC dataset via the maftools package in R because of the high heterogeneity and genetic factors of LC.14 The results showed that somatic mutations were similar between the EPYC-high and EPYC-low groups (Figure S2E).

Next, the expression of EPYC was detected in 172 LC tissues via immunohistochemistry. The patients’ ages ranged from 37 to 82 years, with a median age of 58 years, and the majority of the patients were male. A total of 32.6% (56/172) of the patients had stage IV disease, 24.4% (42/172) of the patients were node positive, and most of them had glottic cancer. These characteristics are consistent with the pathological characteristics of HNSCC.15 The expression of EPYC was upregulated in tumor vs. normal tissues for nearly half of the patients (48.3%, 84/172) (Figure 2A), and the expression of EPYC correlated with only lymph node metastasis, drinking history, and MVD (Figure 2B; Table 1), while not with other clinical parameters (including gender).

LC patients were followed up for 1 to 48 months, and the median follow-up time was 20 months. By the end of the follow-up, 26.7% (46/172) of the patients had died from disease progression, and the most common causes of death were cachexia, thrombotic events or pulmonary infections. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that age, T and N stage, tobacco history (Figures S3A–S3D), EPYC expression and MVD (Figures 2C and 2D) affected survival. Multivariate Cox analysis further demonstrated that age, T/N stage and EPYC expression were independent factors for predicting the prognosis of LC patients (Table S2).

The patient cohort appears limited in size, so we analyzed correlation between EPYC expression and survival in larger public clinical datasets. The results showed that in TCGA-HNSC and GSE65858 datasets, EPYC expression is not associated with survival time in the overall population, but in T4 stage and lymph node positive patients, EPYC overexpression corresponds to shorter overall survival time (Figure S4).

A nomogram was established using the independent prognostic factors to predict the 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS probabilities, and the relationships between age, T/N stage and EPYC expression are visually shown in the nomogram (Figure 2E). The performance of the predictive model was assessed via different methods. The area under the curve (AUC) values of the ROC curve provided a quantitative measure of the model’s performance (Table S3). The nomogram analysis showed good consistency (Figure 2F). The C-index of the nomogram was 0.816 (95% CI 0.755–0.877), and in the calibration plot, the predicted survival rates were consistent with the actual survival rates (Figure S5A). In addition, in Figure S5B, DCA illustrated the potential practical value in decision-making for predicting overall survival in LC patients.

EPYC knockdown inhibited the tumorigenesis of LC cells

To determine the biological function of the AR-key DEGs, we first detected EPYC expression in different cells. The results showed that TU212, an LC cell line, had the highest EPYC expression, while the EPYC expression in HUVECs was the lowest (Figure 3A). Therefore, we knocked down EPYC in TU212 cells, and the knockdown efficiency was examined (Figures 3B and 3C). EPYC knockdown decreased LC cell viability and colony formation ability, and this effect was partially attenuated by the administration of 50 μg/mL rhEPYC (Figures 3D and 3E). Annexin V-PI staining revealed that the proportion of apoptotic cells increased after EPYC knockdown but decreased after treatment with rhEPYC, mainly due to changes in the proportion of cells undergoing late apoptosis (Figure 3F). As shown in the EdU assay, siEPYC inhibited DNA replication, which was also partially abrogated by rhEPYC treatment (Figure 3G). Additionally, we found that siEPYC decreased anti-apoptotic protein (Bcl-2) and proliferation protein (proliferating cell nuclear antigen, PCNA) expression, who both increased by rhEPYC (Figure S5C).

We next explored the in vivo effects of EPYC on LC progression by establishing a subcutaneous LC-bearing model via transplantation of TU212 cells with stable EPYC/NC knockdown. Knockdown of EPYC slowed the growth of subcutaneous transplanted tumors (Figures 3H and 3I). After 21 days of transplantation, we sacrificed the mice and found that the staining of Ki-67 (a proliferation marker) and CD31 (a microvessel density marker) decreased after EPYC knockdown (Figure 3J; Figures S5D and S5E). For rescue experiments, 5 mg/kg of rhEPYC were intraperitoneal injected. Knockdown of EPYC slowed the growth of subcutaneous transplanted tumors while rescued by rhEPYC (Figures S6A and S6B). In summary, EPYC knockdown inhibited laryngeal tumorigenesis.

Previous studies have suggested that biglycan16 or decorin17 can regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway; therefore, we explored how EPYC affects the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. EPYC knocking-down induced a decrease in Wnt activity of nearly half (Figure S6C). According to western blotting and immunofluorescence analysis, we found that the level of β-catenin was decreased by EPYC knocking-down (Figures S6D and S6E). This may be the underlying mechanism of EPYC promotes tumorigenesis.

SiEPYC inhibited angiogenesis in LC

Next, we explored the effect of EPYC on angiogenesis. The key steps in angiogenesis are the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells toward tumor cells via a gap in the basal lamina; therefore, we added CM derived from siEPYC/NC-treated TU212 cells to HUVECs. Treatment with TU212/siEPYC-derived CM decreased HUVEC viability and colony numbers, while the addition of rhEPYC (50 μg/mL) resulted in colony formation (Figures 4A and 4B). Transwell assay also showed that the migration capability of HUVECs decreased in response to TU212/siEPYC-derived CM but was also partially abrogated by rhEPYC (Figure 4C). In the later stage of angiogenesis, vascular lumens develop, and capillary networks form. Therefore, we conducted a tube formation assay, and the results showed that siEPYC CM disrupted tube-like structures and vascular network formation, while rhEPYC CM had the opposite effect (Figure 4D). In addition, siEPYC decreased VEGF-A expression and secretion (Figures 4E and 4F). Neo-vascularization was unaffected in the TU212/siNC-derived CM group but dramatically suppressed in the TU212/siEPYC-derived CM group (Figure 4G), as verified by the CAM assay. These results indicated that the knockdown of EPYC could inhibit LC angiogenesis.

EPYC binds to VEGFR1 to promote angiogenesis

To determine the biological pathways and processes affected by EPYC, GSEA was conducted on the GSE51985 and GSE84957 datasets. The “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” gene set was enriched in genes upregulated in the EPYC high-expression group in both datasets (Figures 5A and 5B). To clarify the distribution of EPYC in LC tissues, we assessed EPYC expression at the single-cell level via scRNA-seq data in GSE172577 and GSE206332 datasets. As shown in Figure 5C, EPYC expression was prominent in vascular endothelial cells, indicating that EPYC in the LC might be secreted to vascular endothelial cells to regulate angiogenesis. To explore how EPYC affects angiogenesis in vascular endothelial cells by different cytokines in vascular endothelial cells, we added rhEPYC (50 ng/mL) to HUVECs directly and analyzed the activation of a variety of kinases via an RTK proteome profile array and VEGFR1 was downregulated after the addition of rhEPYC (Figure 5D). Immunofluorescent staining was performed and the results showed that the co-localization of EPYC with VEGFR1 was predominantly in membrane and cytoplasm of HUVECs and laryngeal cancer tissues (Figure 5E; Figure S7A).

EPYC may cause the degradation of VEGFR1 in HUVECs to promote angiogenesis, and we preliminarily validated this hypothesis by detecting the expression and secretion of VEGFR1 in HUVECs after the addition of rhEPYC. Western blotting, ELISA and immunofluorescence revealed that both the expression and secretion of VEGFR1 were decreased (Figures 6A–6C; Figure S7B). Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 diminished the reduction in VEGFR1 caused by rhEPYC (Figure 6D), while rhEPYC did not change VEGFR1 mRNA levels (Figure 6E), indicating that rhEPYC downregulated VEGFR1 expression via posttranscriptional upregulation. Previous studies have shown that VEGFR1 is degraded by the ubiquitin-proteasome system to maintain homeostatic levels,18 so we detected the ubiquitinated level of VEGFR1 in HUVECs after the addition of rhEPYC. The results showed that the level of ubiquitinated VEGFR1 increased after the addition of rhEPYC (Figure 6F; Figure S8A). Opposite changes in the expression and ubiquitination of VEGFR1 were observed after treatment with TU212/siEPYC CM (Figures 6G and 6H; Figure S8A). Next, the HUVEC lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation experiments, and the results indicated that EPYC can directly bind to VEGFR1 (Figure 6I). Biacore assays were conducted to determine the direct binding between the VEGFR1 protein and EPYC in Cellulo. In the Biacore assay, EPYC directly bound to VEGFR1 and had a micromolar binding affinity (KD = 224.5 pM, Figure 6J). Employing ITC analysis, the binding strength of protein-protein interactions could be measured by quantifying the thermodynamic changes and the thermodynamic binding parameters were calculated (Figure S8B): the enthalpy (ΔH) was −1355 ± 120.4 kcal/mol (Negative values of ΔH indicate that the bimolecular binding is spontaneous and exothermic) and the entropy change (ΔS) was −4.52 kcal/mol/deg. Finally, the binding of EPYC to VEGFR1 was verified via molecular docking. The EPYC and VEGFR1 D2 domains combined well without significant steric hindrance and formed hydrogen bonds (Val61 and Asp180; Lys217 and Asp293; Arg295 and Pro173) and π-π interactions (His241 and His214), with a Glide score (a scoring function for evaluating the strength of the interaction between ligands and receptors) of −209.8 kcal/mol (Figure 6K). The mutation analysis results showed that all calculated mutation energies for the specified mutations were positive (For example, the Arg315Ala mutation led to the loss of a salt bridge between Arg315 and Asp19, while the Arg295Ala mutation eliminated a hydrogen bond with Pro173, Figure S8C), that the mutations weakened the interactions between EPYC and VEGFR1. To explore the competitive binding of EPYC to VEGFA-VEGFR1, we conducted Biacore assay and molecular docking in VEGFA-VEGFR1. The results showed that VEGFA-VEGFR1 had similar affinities (KD = 179.9 pM) with EPYC-VEGFR1, and VEGFA also formed hydrogen bonds with VEGFR1 (His86 and Gln225; Tyr21 and Leu204; Asp63 and Arg224), with a Glide score of −220.7 kcal/mol (Figure S9). These results indicated that EPYC bound to VEGFR1 to promote its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation and VEGFA was released from VEGFR1 to promote angiogenesis.

Discussion

The prognosis and quality of life of patients with LC are poor, so it is necessary to find new diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Current studies have shown that abnormal gene expression and gene variation may be involved in LC19; however, the molecular pathogenesis of LC is not fully understood. Machine learning algorithms are used to analyze tumor transcriptome data generated from high-throughput sequencing technology.20 In this study, we employed the LASSO, RF, and SVE-RFE algorithms to identify EPYC as a key AR-DEG and verified these findings in LC tissues. EPYC is a member of a small family of leucine-rich repetitive proteoglycans (SLRPs), and its gene is located on human chromosome 12; it is a protein-coding gene that consists of seven exons.21 SLRPs are major soluble structural components of interstitial connective tissues ECMs and interact with growth factors, cytokines and receptors on the cell surface.22 For example, Wu’s study found that EPYC was the key regulators of chondrocyte ECM remodeling in osteoarthritis.23 SLRPs are soluble proteins of the extracellular matrix,22 and the SLRP decorin has been reported to play a bidirectional regulatory role in angiogenesis regulation: decorin promotes endothelial cell adhesion and migration in nonmalignant states to promote angiogenesis, while in cancer development, it plays a vascular inhibitory role.24 However, the role of EPYC in angiogenesis has not been reported. Here, after coincubation of HUVECs with medium from EPYC knockdown LC cells, the angiogenesis ability of HUVECs was attenuated; thus, EPYC may affect the whole stage of tumor angiogenesis.

VEGF is an important angiogenesis-related cytokine that can promote the penetration of vascular components through the vascular wall and widen the gap between capillary wall cells. It can activate plasminogen and metalloproteinases in the matrix, forming a network-like structure.25 VEGF is the most important regulatory factor in tumor angiogenesis26 and is reported to be upregulated in LC.27 In this study, we found that EPYC prevent VEGFR1 binding to VEGF-A.

VEGFR is a member of the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) family, among which VEGFR2 is a highly active receptor. The cascade signaling pathway activated by VEGF/VEGFR2 can regulate vascular endothelial cells, increase vascular permeability, and regulate angiogenesis.28,29 VEGF-A binds to VEGFR2 and regulates many endothelial cell functions.30 In contrast, VEGFR1 is a decoy receptor: VEGF-A has a high affinity for VEGFR1 but low tyrosine kinase activity, so the binding of VEGF-A to VEGFR1 inhibits VEGF-A mediated angiogenesis. VEGFR-1 exists in two isoforms: a membrane-bound isoform (mVEGFR-1) and a soluble one (sVEGFR-1) and both inhibits angiogenesis by binding to VEGF-A.31 In our study, we found that EPYC may also promote angiogenesis by binding to VEGFR1 directly to release VEGF-A.

By molecular docking, according to the energy and spatial structure complementarity of the receptor and ligand, the optimal binding mode of the receptor and ligand could be selected.32 Structurally, VEGFR1 consists of seven immunoglobulin-like transmembrane domains (D1-7), extracellular domains and intracellular domains, of which the intracellular domain containing the tyrosine kinase domain and VEGF-A can bind to the D2-3 domain.33 In our study, the crystal structure of the VEGFR1 D2 domain was downloaded from public datasets, and by molecular docking, we found that EPYC may form hydrogen and π-π bonds with this domain to promote VEGFR1 degradation.

In conclusion, our study showed that EPYC is overexpressed in LC and promotes LC carcinogenesis and angiogenesis. We hope our findings will deepen the understanding of LC and present a novel therapeutic target for further study in LC.

Limitations of the study

This is the first study to evaluate the function of EPYC in laryngeal carcinogenesis and angiogenesis; nevertheless, our research has several limitations. First and foremost, viral infections, especially human papillomavirus (HPV), also play a significant role in LC,34,35 but regretfully, in our study, the HPV status of patients was unknown. Second, we need to expand the sample size of LC patients, and prognostic models built from nomograms also need external validation. Third, some experiments involved in this study, including Biacore and ITC, have limitations in being sensitive to environmental factors and requiring high-quality biomolecule immobilization, which may lead to overdrawn conclusions. At last, the mechanism by which EPYC regulates VEGFR1 requires further exploration. Therefore, more on the role of EPYC in HNSCC is warranted to provide new therapeutic targets.

Public datasets

From the National Center of Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (NCBI GEO) database, we searched raw gene expression data of laryngeal cancer using the following key terms: (laryngeal OR larynx) AND (cancer OR tumor OR carcinoma). The publication time was not limited and ‘Homo sapiens’ was selected in organism and expression profiling by array under ‘type’. Altogether, 2123 datasets were brought into the initial search. The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the gene expression profiling data were included; (2) cancerous and noncancerous groups were involved, and at least 3 samples were included in each group.

Patients and tissue samples

A total of 172 LC and their non-cancerous tissues were obtained from surgical resection, all of which were examined by pathologist. Besides, we collected patients’ clinical information, including age, gender (male or female, defined as sex assigned at birth), TNM stages, smoking and driking history and followed up: patients or their relatives and clinicians were followed up until December 31, 2024. Our human data research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the research was in consent by Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University Ethics Committee (reference number: 2023PS651K). Informed consent were signed by all involved patients and complianced in sample collection.

In vivo experiments

5∗106 of TU212 cells, transfected with shEPYC or scramble lentiviral vectors, were injected to the female BALB/c nude mice subcutaneously. The mice were sacrificed after 21 days for further analyzing sample collection.

The in vivo study was performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of ‘National Institutes of Health’ and approved by the Ethics Committee of the China Medical University (reference number: KT2023052; the name of the ethics committee that approved the study: 5/each group) and the maximal tumour size was permitted by their the Ethics Committee.

Cell line culture

The LC cell lines TU212, TU686 and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purched from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy (Shanghai, China). The authentications of the cells were provided in our previous study36 and the cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination before the experiments conducted. The cell lines were cltured in standard cell culture medium and kept in a incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Method details

Collecting gene expression datasets of laryngeal cancer

GEO2R was used to screen DEGs (differentially expressed genes) between laryngeal cancer and noncancerous samples in the GEO datasets. P value <0.05 and absolute log fold-change (FC) greater than 2 for the DEGs were used as the cutoff criteria. We also obtained DEGs between laryngeal and noncancerous tissues from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database.

R package ‘VennDiagram’ was used to identify the overlapping DEGs among GEO, TCGA database and angiogenesis genes obtained from GeneCards database.37 Gene ontology (GO) functional annotation38 and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis39 of the up-regulated targets were performed using WebGestalt.40

The identification of key angiogenesis related (AR) genes

Three different machine learning algorithms were used to get key AR genes and ‘VennDiagram’ package was also used to identify the overlapping AR genes derived from the three algorithms.

(1)Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO): Using the glmnet package of R,41 LASSO analysis was implemented based on the DEGs. After identifying the minimum lambda, DEGs with coefficient ≠0 were further selected through predict.cv.glmnet function. The model or gene signature was defined as: angiogenesis related gene risk score (ARG-RS) = ∑Exp(i)∗coef(i), where i represents genes, exp represents the expression levels, and coef represents the LASSO coefficients. Patients were stratified into low- and high-ARG-RS groups with the median score. The reliability and stability of the model were proven in validation datasets.

The e1071 package was used to perform SVM-RFE to employ a ten-fold cross-validation strategy with grid search to select the best set of features and the average RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) was recorded for various sets of potential genes, and the set with the minimum RMSE was selected as the ultimate biomarker set during cross-validation.

Mutation of key gene EPYC in TCGA-HNSC

To identify variations of mutated genes in EPYC high and low expression groups, R package ‘maftools’ was used for the mutation analysis.43,44 Furthermore, the ‘mafCompare’ function in the ‘maftools’ package was used to compare the mutation levels between high and low EPYC expression groups.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed tissues were embedded for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunostaining. The expression of EPYC was assessed by immunohistochemical staining kit (Maixin, China). The tissues were blocked with normal goat serum for 20 min at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody, the tissues were incubated with the second antibody and biotin-labeled horseradish peroxidase. Subsequently, the antibody binding was visualized with a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) kit (Maixin, China) before brief counterstaining with hematoxylin.

Evaluation of immunohistochemistry and microvascular density (MVD)

EPYC immunoreactivity was semi-quantitatively evaluated by two pathologists independently. Five representative regions were randomly selected from the 400-fold field of view of the microscope. The score of each field of view was determined by the proportion of positive cells and the color rendering intensity. The expression was classified to Low-NO or Medium-High expression.

CD34-stained endothelial cells were surrounded by blood vessels, with a diameter of less than eight red blood cells are counted as MVs (micro-vasculars). MVD was determined by counting all immunostained MVs at 200x magnification.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA of LC cells was extracted, reverse-transcribed to synthetic cDNA and real-time PCR was carried out.

Western blotting

Western blotting was conducted as we described before.45 Briefly, proteins treated with different agents were extracted, quantified, separated by gels and transferred to membranes. The membranes were blocked, incubated with primary and secondary antibodies and visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence reagent.

MTT, colony formation, flow cytometry and EdU staining

The LC cell line TU212 was transfected with EPYC/NC-siRNA, with or without administration of rhEPYC, to conduct different assays to measure cell viability, proliferative ability, apoptosis and DNA replication, as our previous description.45 The flow cytometry results were analyzed by flowjo and the others’ were analyzed by ImageJ.

Transwell assay

To examine EPYC’s effect on migration of HUVECs, HUVECs, treated by siEPYC/NC CM (conditioned medium), adding rhEPYC or not, were performed transwell assay.

Tube formation

As described before,45 HUVECs, treated with different CM, were added to Matrigel (BD Biosciences) and tubes and branches were observed.

Chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model

As described before,45 briefly, different CMs were added into the chorioallantoic membrane and incubated. The neovascularization was observed and photographed.

Immunofluorescence

TU212 or HUVECs cells were fixed, blocked and primary antibody (VEGFR1) and Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody were applied to incubate the cells. Finally, cells were incubated with hoechst and visualized under an inverted fluorescent microscope.

Analysis of EPYC in HNSC using scRNA-seq data

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets were analyzed using Seurat R package.46 Firstly, the expression profiles were log-normalized. Then we removed the batch effects using FindVariableFeatures and FindIntegrationAnchors functions, and integrated data through IntegrateData function. Next, ScaleData function was conducted to scale data and identify the anchor for principal component analysis (PCA). Single cells were clustered with dim = 40 and Resolution = 0.5 based on FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions.47 After that, dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis were performed using the t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) and UMAP (uniform manifold approximation and projection) algorithms. Cell type annotation was performed by CellMarker database48 and EPYC’s expression in different cells was visualized by violinplot.

RTK array kit

10 μg/mL of rhEPYC was added to PBS buffer containing protease inhibitor and analyzed by the RTK-array kit as manufacturer’s instructions.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of EPYC associated gene sets

GSEA is conducted by using GSEA v2.2.2 to identify EPYC associated gene sets in GSE51985 and GSE84957 datasets.49 EPYC expression is first dichotomized as low and high categories to annotate phenotypes. GSEA of genes’ associations with the phenotypes is further tested by using C2: CP KEGG gene sets from MSigDB. The gene sets that are significantly enriched by the genes associated with high EPYC expression were selected as enriched gene sets.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Concentrations of secreted sVEGFR1 or VEGF-A were detected with ELISA Kit as manufacturer’s instructions. Recombinant human VEGFR1/VEGF-A (rhVEGFR1/rhVEGF-A) was used for standard curve.

Biacore assay for surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis

Referring to our previous research,50 surface plasmon resonance (SPR) affinity experiment51,52 for protein-protein interaction analysis was conducted employing a Biacore 100 T biosensor detector (GE healthcenter, USA).

Isothermal titration calorimetry analysis

rhEPYC was used to titrate rhVEGFR1 to study thermodynamic parameters. The initial volume of the rhEPYC solution for titration was 50 μL and the initial volume of the rhVEGFR1 solution (a solvent buffer as the control group) for titration was 300 μL. The reaction conditions included adding 20 drops of 2.5 μL each with a 120 s interval between drops at 25 °C and a stirring speed of 300 r/min.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to analysis interaction between EPYC and VEGFR1. VEGFR1 crystal structure (PDB ID: 1QSV) was prepared from RCSB53 and the amino acid sequence of EPYC was submitted to the SWISS-MODEL web server to generate the models. The EPYC-VEGFR1 complex analysis was performed using GRAMM-X Protein Docking Web Server (https://gramm.compbio.ku.edu/) and visualized by PyMol.

To assess the impact of mutations on binding affinity in protein-protein and protein-ligand complexes, we utilized the Calculate Mutation Energy (Binding) protocol. The mutation energy (ΔΔGmut) was determined as the difference in binding free energy between the mutated and wild-type structures. The total energy incorporated van der Waals (VDW) interactions, electrostatic effects, entropy changes from side-chain mobility, and non-polar solvation contributions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

We analyzed the experimental data by SPSS software (Chicago, USA), who was presented as the mean ± SEM. Quantitative analysis was performed by a t test, with the results shown in a bar graph (∗p < 0.05, n = 3∗).

In survival analysis, kaplan-meier and log rank test were performed. Nomogram was constructed by the foreign and rms packages in R according to the result of multivariate analysis.
