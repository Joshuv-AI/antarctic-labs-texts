Patient Education Strategies in Radiotherapy: A Narrative Review of Tools, Timing, and Therapist Roles
Authors: Mohammed Almutairi, Amer Al Ameer, Abdulrahman Al Otaibi, Areej Al Fayez, Sultan AlNujiman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe review was done with the aim of compiling the nature and efficiency of the patient education interventions used in radiotherapy, with a specific focus on the education materials, timing of delivery, and skill levels of therapists. A narrative synthesis was carried out across the 12 original studies involved, which used quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods. The five thematic areas were examined: modality, standardisation, timing of delivery, therapist involvement, and patient-reported outcomes. Educational strategies were delivered in face-to-face contact (58%) or video-based materials (33%), the majority following predetermined guidelines. In 75% studies, visual or multimedia support was offered, and its duration varied between 8.6 and 80 minutes. Approximately 67% of such interventions were conducted before radiotherapy initiation and as part of normal clinical practice. Despite significant heterogeneity in education delivery and therapist training, patient education interventions in radiotherapy were associated with better knowledge and, to a lesser extent, anxiety reduction and satisfaction.

INTRODUCTION

Radiotherapy is a central element in modern oncological treatment, utilized to induce remission in over 50% of cancer patients at diagnosis.[1] Though technological developments have significantly improved radiotherapy accuracy and effectiveness, intrinsic procedural complexity, multi-session treatment regimens, and concomitant side effects impose significant cognitive and emotional burdens on patients who receive such treatment.[2] As a result, patient education and effective communication are fundamental aspects of high-quality radiotherapy treatment, affecting knowledge gain and satisfaction, compliance and anxiety reduction, and clinical results.[3]

The radiotherapy treatment procedure includes a series of evaluations before the therapy begins, planning simulation delivery, and a series of exposures to high-energy ionizing radiation. Each of these steps poses logistical challenges and introduces new medical practices, which have been extensively reported to produce patient anxiety, confusion, and decision-making problems.[4] Such psychological impacts are most severely optimized among vulnerable populations, i.e., children, elderly individuals, or patients receiving head and neck radiotherapy, as immobilization, anatomical constraints, and communication deficits increase patient distress.[5] Therefore, there is a known need for education interventions intended to address the individual patient and his/her specific needs, treatment protocol, and information requirements.[6789]

Furthermore, the clinical staff members in charge of delivering the education have high heterogeneity, including radiation therapists, nurses, oncologists, allied health professionals, non-clinical facilitators, and digital technology.[10] The heterogeneity of educational qualifications, skills, and levels of involvement in preparing and delivering the educational materials by these educators can have a significant effect on both the fidelity and responsiveness of the intervention from the patient’s side.[1112] The present review, therefore, attempts to synthesize and summarize the features of patient education strategies applied in radiotherapy, with special reference to the education modalities, timing of treatment, the therapist roles and preparation, and the resulting outcomes in different patient populations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this review were established a priori to ensure methodological rigor and consistency. Original research articles were eligible if they evaluated a patient education intervention for patients receiving radiotherapy. Interventions that were considered eligible needed to deliver structured educational content intended to improve patient knowledge, anxiety reduction, satisfaction, or treatment compliance in the contexts of external beam radiotherapy or brachytherapy.

Excluded were studies that did not include a context of radiation therapy, did not have a formal educational intervention, or assessed general health education that did not include radiotherapy-specific content.

Database search protocol

A review of the literature was conducted using seven electronic databases, namely PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and PsycINFO. The search strategy employed Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) as well as free-text terms to retrieve relevant studies within radiotherapy patient education. Boolean “AND” and “OR” operators were used to connect conceptually related terms.

DISCUSSION

Incorporation of patient education into radiotherapy treatment is an important component of overall oncology care, particularly in the context of changing patient expectations for autonomy, shared decision-making, and individualized treatment plans. As radiotherapy methods have become more precise and advanced, so too have cognitive expectations on the part of patients to be informed not only why the treatment is being administered, but also how the procedure will be performed logistically, its potential side effects, and self-management techniques to maximize outcome.[5] Failure to meet these information needs has been associated with increased patient anxiety, reduced compliance, and lower satisfaction with care.[6]

Radiotherapy typically involves a series of appointments over weeks, each requiring precise positioning, technical skill of immobilization devices, and tolerance of emotionally and physically challenging procedures such as simulation, mask fixation, or breath-hold protocols.[4] Such experiences, unless adequately explained or contextualized, can lead to anticipatory anxiety, procedural distress, or avoidance behaviour, especially in vulnerable groups such as pediatric patients, cognitively impaired patients, and patients treated in high-anxiety anatomy sites such as the head and neck.[816] Education in these situations is more than information provision to encompass procedural preparation, emotional comfort, and support of self-efficacy.[9] This review was limited by heterogeneity of reporting of outcome measures between studies, specifically for recall accuracy, adherence to treatment, and long-term follow-up.

CONCLUSION

Education interventions aimed at patients who were receiving radiotherapy are moderately effective in increasing knowledge and decreasing anxiety, especially when interventions are incorporated early in clinical processes and are supported by well-organized content.
