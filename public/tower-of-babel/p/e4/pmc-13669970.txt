Understanding parents' perspectives of repeated blinded continuous glucose monitoring in children with early‐stage type 1 diabetes and an affected first‐degree relative
Authors: Alexandra S Tully, Sabrina K Binkowski, Brydie‐Rose Nevin, Megan A S Penno, Amanda J Anderson, Maria E Craig, Peter G Colman, Tony Huynh, Kelly J McGorm, Georgia Soldatos, John M Wentworth, Elizabeth A Davis, Jenny J Couper, Keely R Bebbington, Aveni Haynes, the ENDIA Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo explore the lived experiences of parents of children at risk of type 1 diabetes (T1D) undergoing repeated blinded continuous glucose monitoring (CGM).MethodSince 2021, children with persistent islet autoimmunity in the Environmental Determinants of Islet Autoimmunity (ENDIA) study have been invited to participate in a sub‐study involving blinded Dexcom G6 CGM. Parents with children undergoing repeated CGM participated in a semi‐structured audio call interview. Open‐ended questions captured their experiences of their child's repeated blinded CGM. Data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis.ResultsNineteen parents (18 mothers, 1 father) of children with a mean (SD) age of 7.5 (1.3) years, who had undergone a mean 5.5 (3.2) CGM assessments, were interviewed for a mean duration of 21.3 (8.0) minutes. Three main themes were identified: Challenges of CGM wear: All parents identified at least one difficult experience (e.g. pain with sensor insertion, anxiety of data loss) during CGM assessments Content and timing of feedback of CGM assessment findings: Parents described a range of experiences related to receiving feedback from health care professionals and their child's stage of T1D. Preparation for diagnosis of clinical (Stage 3) T1D and initiation of insulin treatment: Parents described feeling empowered by knowing their child's risk, allowing them to feel more prepared for progression to Stage 3 T1D.ConclusionDespite the challenges parents face while their child undergoes CGM assessments, they reported repeated blinded CGM as an acceptable form of monitoring that provided valuable knowledge regarding their child's risk of stage 3 type 1 diabetes.Keywords: CGM, children, early‐stage type 1 diabetes, monitoring, pre‐symptomatic

INTRODUCTION

Globally, screening efforts to identify children with pre‐symptomatic, early‐stage (stage 1 and 2) type 1 diabetes (T1D) are expanding. The goals of early identification include reducing the likelihood of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at time of clinical onset (stage 3 T1D), providing education, and enabling timely access to clinical trials or treatment with disease modifying therapies to prevent or delay disease progression to clinical onset. Furthermore, access to early education and psychosocial support may offer families a ‘soft landing’ at the time of progression to stage 3 T1D, a period often associated with significant overwhelm as families adjust to the demands of daily management following commencement of insulin replacement therapy.

To realise these goals, guidelines recommend children with early‐stage T1D are monitored for glycaemic progression according to their age and stage. 1 , 2 The role of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in care pathways for at‐risk children remains unclear and additional evidence is required to inform its use. 1 , 2 A growing literature highlights the psychological impact of participation in screening programs. A common finding is that caregivers experience increased symptoms of depression and diabetes‐specific anxiety, particularly those whose child is identified as having early‐stage T1D. 3 , 4 However, little is known about the potential psychological burden associated with monitoring for glycaemic progression. 5 , 6

The Environmental Determinants of Islet Autoimmunity (ENDIA) study is an Australia‐wide pregnancy‐childhood cohort study, following 1473 infants with a first degree‐relative diagnosed with T1D. 7 The ENDIA study includes comprehensive longitudinal data and biological sample collection 3‐monthly from birth to 2 years of age and 6‐monthly thereafter up to age 10 years. Testing for autoantibodies to insulin (IAA), glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GADA), tyrosine phosphatase‐like insulinoma antigen (IA2A) and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8A) is conducted at each study time point, to identify the development of persistent islet autoimmunity (defined as islet autoantibody detection on ≥ 2 more occasions at least 3 months apart). 7 Since 2021, ENDIA study children with persistent islet autoantibodies have been invited to participate in the ENDIA CGM sub‐study (ACTRN12620000947909), undergoing blinded Dexcom G6 CGM monitoring for a minimum of 14‐days (hereafter ‘CGM assessment’), repeated at 3 to 6 monthly intervals depending on the child's age and stage of T1D. 8 A qualitative analysis of semi‐structured interviews conducted with parents of ten ENDIA CGM children after their child's first CGM assessment, and prior to them receiving feedback on the CGM findings, found that parents did not perceive blinded CGM to impose a significant burden. 6 Furthermore, gaining knowledge of their child's glucose levels was perceived as a benefit, irrespective of CGM findings. 6 However, given the uncertain and variable timing of disease progression, families with a child identified as having early‐stage T1D living in countries where access to CGM is available may be given an option for longitudinal monitoring of their child via repeated CGM assessments across an extended period of time, and data on the lived experience of this are lacking.

Between January 2021 and December 2024, ENDIA CGM sub‐study participants with persistent islet autoantibodies underwent between one and 12 CGM assessments over a median follow‐up duration of 2 years. 9 We invited parents of these children to participate in a qualitative study to explore their experiences of their child undergoing repeated blinded CGM, and of receiving feedback on the CGM findings. This feedback is given by the child's study site clinical lead and includes being provided with a digital or paper copy of the ambulatory glucose profile (AGP) from the CGM assessment along with high‐level clinical feedback, for example no evidence of dysglycaemia, repeat assessment in 3 to 6 months' time according to the child's age, or some evidence of dysglycaemia which is stable/increasing compared to previous assessment and recommendation to repeat assessment in 3‐months' time or as clinically indicated. Blinded CGM allowed the collection of objective glycaemic data while minimising the likelihood of behaviour modification during the monitoring period. Feedback from participants supported the acceptability of blinded CGM and confirmed that the study design aligned with their preferences and expectations.

Following approval of the world's first disease modifying therapy to delay progression from early‐stage T1D to stage 3 T1D in 2022, 10 the landscape of early‐stage T1D research and now clinical practice has rapidly evolved. Therefore, the current study sought to explore the lived experience of longitudinal monitoring using blinded CGM in children with early‐stage T1D who have a first‐degree relative living with T1D. Understanding lived experiences of repeated CGM is essential to ensure early‐stage T1D monitoring strategies are both clinically effective and acceptable to families over prolonged periods.

METHODS

Between 2nd December 2024 and 19th June 2025, parents of all children enrolled in the ENDIA CGM sub‐study were invited to participate in an optional semi‐structured interview. Written informed consent was obtained from those willing to participate, and an interview was scheduled at a mutually convenient time.

An interview guide was developed by the study team comprising a clinical research nurse and psychologist with experience in qualitative research. It included seven open‐ended questions that were used as prompts to explore the parents' perspectives of their child undergoing repeated blinded CGM assessments and their experience of receiving feedback on the CGM findings (Appendix S1).

Interviews were conducted by the two trained interviewers (BN, OK) via Microsoft Teams audio call, with consent obtained for recording and transcription. The transcripts were imported into qualitative data management software NVivo 12 (QSR International, 2020). Data were analysed by three independent researchers (AT, SKB, BN) using inductive thematic analysis as outlined by Braun and Clark's six‐phase framework. 11 Data analysis commenced with repeated reading of transcripts and multiple reviews of the audio recordings to ensure thorough familiarisation with the data, independently by each researcher. Initial inductive codes were generated directly from the data and subsequently organised into themes and sub‐themes that reflected the underlying content and meaning of participants' accounts. For example, codes such as “fear of sensor insertion” and “lingering memory of discomfort” were grouped under the sub‐theme “Discomfort during sensor wear”. AT, SKB and BN engaged in regular discussions to explore alternative interpretations and refine theme and sub‐theme development. These collaborative discussions enhanced reflexivity and contributed to the depth and coherence of the analysis. Subthemes were iteratively reviewed and refined to ensure they accurately represented the data and aligned with the study's research objectives. The developing themes and subthemes were discussed within the research team and further refined to enhance clarity and analytic rigour. Once finalised, themes were clearly labelled and defined.

The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) were followed 12 (Appendix S2) and an audit journal was maintained to document analysis decisions and ensure a transparent and repeatable approach. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was achieved, with no new themes emerging in later interviews.

Ethics statement

Ethics approval was obtained nationally for the ENDIA CGM sub‐study from the Woman and Children's Hospital in Adelaide (2020/HRE01400) and Child and Adolescent Health Service in Western Australia (HREC RGS 0000002402). Parents provided written informed consent to be interviewed and have their interviews recorded for transcription and data analysis. Participants did not receive any reimbursement for taking part in the interview.

RESULTS

A total of 46 families were approached, 11 declined participation and four did not respond. Thirty‐one families provided consent; however, 12 were unable to be interviewed due to ongoing scheduling conflicts. A total of 19 interviews with a mean (SD) duration of 21.3 (8.9) minutes were completed with parents of 21 children (two mothers interviewed have two children in the ENDIA CGM sub‐study). Eighteen of the 19 parents (94.7%) were mothers, and over half self‐reported being of Australian ethnicity (Table 1). Fifteen of the 21 participants had confirmed multiple IA and six a single IA. Overall, children had undergone a mean (SD) of 5.5 (3.2) CGM assessments at the time their parent was interviewed (Table 1). Analysis generated three overarching themes: (1) Challenges of CGM wear, (2) Content and feedback of CGM assessments and (3) Preparation for diagnosis of clinical (stage 3) T1D and initiation of insulin treatment, and eight associated subthemes (Table 2).

Challenges of CGM wear

All parents reported experiencing at least one challenge during each CGM assessment period. The challenges experienced were variable, with some experienced repeatedly in repeated CGM assessments.

Discomfort during sensor wear

The most frequently reported issues related to sensor insertion and tape removal at the end of the CGM assessment, and some parents described their child having skin irritation at the sensor insertion site.

The first few times he did cry just from the insert. # 06 Mother of 7‐year‐old child with multiple islet autoantibodies

Managing device and data security

Some parents reported experiencing worries about potential data loss resulting from the sensor coming off prematurely and implemented strategies to ensure the longevity of the sensor wear.

In summer, because he's been in the pool a lot, it doesn't last as well and we've tried to use like other tape to try and reinforce it. #01 Mother of 6‐year‐old child with multiple islet autoantibodies

Parents also described concerns about losing CGM data when the receiver was not near the child and some parents identified technology and connection issues with the sensor as a source of frustration (Table 2).

Stigma and social acceptability

An additional challenge described by parents was that while CGM use may have become normalised within their family context, their child remained self‐conscious when wearing CGM outside the home.

He doesn't want to be seen to be different. He doesn't like to answer the questions. He's worried about being bullied about it. # 13 Mother of 7‐year‐old child with multiple islet autoantibodies

Content and feedback of CGM assessments

Reassurance and clarity from CGM results

Parents described variable preferences related to both the content and format of delivery of feedback from their health care professional following their child's CGM assessment. For example, some were satisfied with a general overview, whilst others wanted detailed reports with specific glucose values.

I think when you're getting a lot of information in one go, then afterwards to have something to refer back to would be helpful… # 01 Mother of 6‐year‐old child with multiple islet autoantibodies.

Anxiety associated with feedback

Parents described experiencing elevated anxiety during the interval between the CGM monitoring period and receiving the assessment findings. This anxiety was further intensified when this interval was prolonged.

I get concerned every time that [Dr.] rings me. Because I'm scared that she's giving me bad news. And I've told [Dr.] that and she understands. # 06 Mother of 7‐year‐old child with multiple islet autoantibodies

Limited child engagement

Most families chose not to share the feedback from the CGM assessment with their child, as parents perceived their child to be too young to understand. Those who did talk with their child about the CGM feedback varied in the information they shared.

She probably doesn't really know results side of things. We don't really go into that with her… # 03 Mother of 8‐year‐old child with multiple islet autoantibodies

Preparation for diagnosis of clinical (stage 3) T1D and initiation of insulin treatment

Despite the identified challenges of using CGM on their child, parents expressed a desire for their child to have regular CGM assessments due to the information offered about the status of their child's progression to stage 3 T1D. Some parents described feeling more prepared for progression to clinical diagnosis and commencement of insulin therapy. They expressed reassurance around the rapport built with the healthcare team who would provide care when their child progressed.

Familiarity with diabetes technology

Some parents believed this exposure to CGM would ease the transition at clinical diagnosis, making it less overwhelming compared to initiating CGM and insulin therapy simultaneously without prior experience.

I guess if you're familiar with the CGM then it's sort of just the insulin that's the newer part. # 01 Mother of 6‐year‐old child with multiple autoantibodies

Parents expressed a strong desire to obtain high‐quality and complete CGM data, as they perceived this to be critical for receiving accurate and reliable clinical feedback. Many parents felt that robust CGM data would provide reassurance that any changes in their child's glucose patterns were being appropriately identified and interpreted, particularly in relation to disease progression. Good‐quality data were seen as essential for informing clinical discussions, guiding monitoring decisions, and reducing uncertainty during the early stages of T1D.

I want the data. That's really what's important. We want to see the data, we want to have good data that is reliable and so I'm willing to wait to get that data. # 13 Mother of 7‐year‐old child with multiple autoantibodies.

DISCUSSION

This study reports findings from a qualitative study involving semi‐structured interviews conducted with parents of children with persistent islet autoimmunity undergoing repeated blinded CGM assessments as participants of the ENDIA CGM sub‐study. 8 , 9 While previous qualitative research in this population has focused on parental perspectives following a single blinded CGM assessment in their child, 6 this study addresses a gap in the existing literature on the lived experience of parents with children at risk of T1D being monitored longitudinally. 5 We identified three overarching themes, comprising eight subthemes, which provide valuable insights related to the feasibility and acceptability of CGM monitoring in this population. Although the use of CGM is not currently included in the standards of care for early‐stage T1D, current consensus guidance documents on monitoring these individuals highlight the need for further research on its use for disease staging and monitoring of progression. 1 , 2 Understanding the acceptability of using repeated blinded CGM is also essential for informing the development of future clinical guidelines.

Whilst parents interviewed in this study described repeated blinded CGM assessments in their child as being acceptable, they all reported experiencing a range of challenges related to the wearability of CGM recognised in children diagnosed with T1D, including discomfort, skin reactions and adhesion issues. 13 , 14 Although not part of routine clinical care for children being monitored for progression to clinical T1D, parents valued the opportunity to access CGM data on their child via participation in this research study, and receiving feedback on the CGM findings regarding their child's progression to clinical T1D. In addition, some perceived there would be an advantage to their child having exposure to CGM prior to developing clinical T1D, believing that already having familiarity with CGM could ease adjustment to daily management at the time of commencing insulin therapy.

Related to the importance described by parents of receiving information on their child's risk of progression to clinical T1D, some described the anxiety they experienced related to worries about potential data loss (e.g. due to early accidental sensor removal). Parents described feeling more prepared for their child's eventual progression to insulin‐requiring clinical T1D and reduced uncertainty about the future from receiving feedback on their child's CGM findings and discussion with their study site's clinical lead.

Notably, parents described the importance of receiving timely feedback on their child's CGM. Delays between completion of their child's CGM assessment and receiving feedback increased parental worry and concern. To mitigate this, feedback on blinded CGM findings should be provided to parents of children being monitored for progression to stage 3 T1D as close to completion of the CGM assessment as possible. Where this is not feasible, families need strategies and resources to manage uncertainty during the waiting period. 4 , 15 , 16 An additional finding related to receiving feedback on CGM findings was that parents expressed varying preferences for the level of detail provided regarding their child's blinded CGM assessment, underscoring the need to tailor feedback for families of children with being monitored for glycaemic progression. Importantly, parents requested resources they could revisit after receiving feedback which are provided in an appropriate format to share with their child. Studies have shown many parents feel uncertain about how to discuss increased risk of T1D with their children and may avoid these conversations, 17 , 18 reflecting a lack of clear guidance on effective risk communication for parents and children and the need for resources to support families with this.

Whilst providing new insights on parents' perspectives of their young child living undergoing repeated blinded CGM, the findings of this study need to be considered within its limitations. Firstly, this study was conducted within the ENDIA longitudinal T1D at‐risk cohort study. Therefore, all parents interviewed either live with T1D themselves or have a first‐degree family member living with the condition and would thus also have prior knowledge and experience of CGM. Hence, this may limit the generalisability of this study's findings to families without a first degree relative living with T1D, or those without prior knowledge or experience with CGM, as well as families not engaged in clinical follow‐up in a research context. Further, the majority of parents interviewed were mothers and Australian, and all of Caucasoid ethnicity. An additional limitation is that this study is based on the experience of using blinded CGM and cannot be generalised to the use of real‐time, unblinded CGM. An additional limitation is that the current study did not explore parents' perspectives of the frequency of CGM monitoring periods and this warrants attention in future studies. Although clinical guidelines will naturally be informed by emerging data on glycaemic trends and patterns associated with rate of disease progression, such guidelines should also take psychosocial factors into account, including the potential risk of monitoring fatigue or burnout, particularly in families for whom CGM use does not yet have a direct clinical management function. Furthermore, anecdotally, members of the study team are aware of families electing to postpone subsequent monitoring sessions following feedback that CGM monitoring did not provide evidence of glycaemic progression. This highlights the role that risk perception plays in health behaviour and the importance of fostering an accurate perception of risk for families. 4 Finally, children's perspectives were not directly captured and warrant future study. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study provides novel insights for informing the development of future monitoring protocols that include using repeated blinded CGM in children at risk of stage 3 T1D.

CONCLUSION

Despite the practical and emotional challenges associated with repeated blinded CGM, parents describe it as an acceptable and valuable tool for ongoing monitoring of their children. Parents' willingness to continue with CGM assessments was driven by the importance of gaining insight into their child's T1D stage and preparing for their potential progression to clinical (stage 3) T1D. To ease the burden of CGM, strategies include timely provision of feedback following blinded CGM assessments with the format and content tailored according to parental needs and preferences, and the provision of family‐friendly resources to refer to after receiving feedback. In addition, proactive discussion with parents regarding potential challenges whilst undergoing CGM assessments could help address expectations and equip parents with practical strategies to manage difficulties if they arise. This study provides important insights related to the clinical care of children with early‐stage T1D being offered repeated blinded CGM and further demonstrates the necessity of involving people with lived experience to inform and optimise delivery of their health care.
