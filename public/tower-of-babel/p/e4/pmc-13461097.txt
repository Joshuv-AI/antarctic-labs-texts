Spadmiss with basal ganglia calcification and multilocus genetic disease: a novel phenotypic expansion
Authors: Maryam Kachuei, Shayan Eghdami, Sarah Eyvaz-Ziaei, Azita Tavasoli, Amir Ghadipasha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:SpADMiSS syndrome (SPOUT1-Associated Developmental delay, Microcephaly, Seizures, and Short stature) is a recently described autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder caused by biallelic variants in SPOUT1/CENP-32. The phenotypic spectrum of this condition is still evolving. Reporting novel clinical features and complex genetic architectures is essential to refine genotype–phenotype correlations, particularly in consanguineous populations.Case presentation:We describe a 5.5-year-old girl born to consanguineous parents who presented with refractory early-onset seizures, severe global developmental delay, growth failure, and microcephaly. Neuroimaging revealed marked cerebral atrophy and previously unreported bilateral basal ganglia calcification. The patient also developed bilateral cataracts requiring surgical intervention. Whole-exome sequencing identified a recurrent homozygous missense variant in SPOUT1/CENP-32 (c.292 G > A; p.Gly98Ser), confirming the diagnosis of SpADMiSS. In addition, homozygous pathogenic variants were detected in XYLT2 and EYS, indicating multilocus genetic disease.Clinical discussion:This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of SpADMiSS by documenting basal ganglia calcification, a feature not previously reported in affected individuals. The presence of early-onset cataracts and ocular involvement is best explained by concurrent pathogenic variants in XYLT2 and EYS, illustrating a blended phenotype due to multilocus inheritance. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive genomic analysis in consanguineous families to avoid misattribution of clinical features to a single genetic disorder.Conclusion:We report a novel presentation of SpADMiSS syndrome associated with basal ganglia calcification and multilocus pathogenic variation. This case underscores the expanding neuroimaging phenotype of SPOUT1/CENP-32-related disease and emphasizes the critical role of whole-exome sequencing in accurately delineating complex phenotypes in consanguineous populations.

Background

The identification of SPOUT1/CENP-32 as a disease-causing gene in humans is a recent development, with the associated disorder first delineated in 2024–2025. SPOUT1 (also known as CENP-32 or C9orf114) encodes a putative RNA methyltransferase belonging to the SPOUT superfamily[1]. Pathogenic variants in genes regulating centrosome integrity and mitotic spindle function have long been implicated in severe neurodevelopmental disorders, including autosomal recessive primary microcephaly, microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism type II, and Seckel syndrome[2]. These disorders predominantly manifest with microcephaly and intellectual disability, reflecting the vulnerability of the developing brain to defects in cell cycle regulation and neural progenitor proliferation[3]. Mechanistically, centrosome dysfunction leads to spindle disorganization, mitotic delay, and chromosome segregation errors, ultimately resulting in premature depletion of neural progenitor cells through aberrant differentiation and apoptosis[1].

SPOUT1/CENP-32 was initially identified through proteomic and bioinformatic analyses of proteins associated with mitotic chromosomes[1]. Depletion of SPOUT1/CENP-32 by siRNA in human cells results in characteristic centrosome detachment from spindle poles, delayed anaphase onset, and chromosome segregation errors. Independent large-scale CRISPR-Cas9 screening confirmed SPOUT1/CENP-32 as essential for cell viability and linked it to RNA modification pathways[1,4]. Structural studies reveal a conserved SPOUT-domain methyltransferase core with an inserted oligonucleotide-binding fold, enabling S-adenosyl-L-methionine–dependent RNA methylation activity[1,4].

In 2024–2025, Dharmadhikari and colleagues characterized biallelic variants in SPOUT1/CENP-32 in 28 individuals from 21 unrelated families with diverse ancestries, establishing a novel autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder termed SpADMiSS (SPOUT1-Associated Development delay, Microcephaly, Seizures, and Short stature)[1]. The core clinical phenotype includes global developmental delay (100%), intellectual disability (100%), seizures (71%), microcephaly (86%), short stature (83%), and failure to thrive (79%). Seizures in this cohort were often severe, with approximately 43% meeting criteria for epileptic encephalopathy, including Lennox–Gastaut syndrome and infantile epileptic spasms. Neuroimaging abnormalities were present in 64% of individuals, characterized by cerebral atrophy with ventricular enlargement, white matter hyperintensities, and corpus callosum thinning[1]. Notably, the original description did not include basal ganglia calcification as a feature.

In accordance with the CAse REport (CARE) guidelines and the Transparency In The reporting of Artificial INtelligence (TITAN) checklist[5,6], we present a case of SpADMiSS associated with basal ganglia calcification and multilocus pathogenic variation. This report expands the neuroimaging spectrum of SPOUT1/CENP-32-related disease and highlights the diagnostic value of whole-exome sequencing (WES) in delineating complex phenotypes.

Case presentation

Patient demographics and chief complaint

A 5.5-year-old girl, the daughter of consanguineous parents, was referred to the hospital for evaluation of refractory seizures and developmental delay.

History of present illness

Seizure disorder

The patient’s seizures began at 12 months of age, initially manifesting as myoclonic episodes. Subsequently, at approximately 3 years of age, the seizure semiology evolved into infantile spasms. The seizure episodes were frequent and poorly responsive to conventional antiepileptic medications.

Antiepileptic drug trial

The patient received sequential trials of multiple antiepileptic agents without substantial clinical response. Levetiracetam (initial therapy), sodium valproate, vigabatrin, topiramate, clobazam, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and, at last, phenobarbital – which was added upon transition to an infantile spasm diagnosis – showed no meaningful response.

This pattern is consistent with drug-refractory epilepsy, requiring escalation of therapy and investigation for underlying genetic etiologies.

Developmental milestones

Global developmental delay was evident. By 5.5 years of age, the patient had not achieved age-appropriate developmental milestones; she was unable to hold her neck independently and could not sit without support. Speech and language development were severely delayed, with the patient remaining largely non-verbal.

Family history

Parents are consanguineous (first-degree relatives), which increases the recurrence risk for autosomal recessive disorders. No other family members are reported to have similar phenotypes.

Past medical and physical examination findings

The patient demonstrated severe global developmental delay and profound hypotonia. The cranial nerve examination was grossly normal.

Fundoscopic examination was unremarkable at the time of the initial evaluation. Yet, importantly, the patient developed bilateral cataracts that were surgically addressed at 2.5 years of age. This early-onset cataract presentation indicates an important genetic contribution.

Postoperatively, the patient failed to demonstrate normal fix-and-follow behavior, suggesting either residual visual impairment or cortical visual impairment secondary to neuronal dysfunction.

Hearing assessment

Age-appropriate audiometric testing revealed normal hearing, ruling out syndromic sensorineural hearing loss.

Neuroimaging findings

Computed tomography of the brain

Genetic analysis

WES identified three distinct genetic findings.

Primary finding: SPOUT1/CENP-32.

Variant: c.292 G > A; chr9-128827108 C > T (hg38); p.Gly98Ser; NM_016390.4; and rs771869476.

Zygosity: Homozygous.

Classification: Likely pathogenic (recurrent variant; see below).

This homozygous missense variant results in a glycine-to-serine substitution at codon 98 within the conserved catalytic SPOUT methyltransferase domain. The p.Gly98Ser (p.G98S) variant represents a recurrent mutation, reported in 9 of 21 families in the original SpADMiSS cohort described by Dharmadhikari et al[1]. Zebrafish in vivo complementation assays confirmed the pathogenicity of this variant[1]. This makes SpADMiSS a newly recognized disease entity.

The presence of this variant correlates with the patient’s core phenotype of neurodevelopmental disorder, early-onset refractory seizures (including infantile spasms), severe global developmental delay, growth failure, microcephaly, and cerebral atrophy, all consistent with SpADMiSS.

Secondary finding: XYLT2.

Variant: c.2080A > G; chr17-50358345 A > G (hg38); p.Thr694Ala; NM_022167.4; and rs145098209.

Zygosity: Homozygous.

Classification: Likely pathogenic.

XYLT2 encodes a xylosyltransferase involved in proteoglycan biosynthesis and plays critical roles in maintaining extracellular matrix integrity. Pathogenic variants in XYLT2 are associated with a spectrum of skeletal dysplasia and connective tissue phenotypes, including cataracts that typically emerge in the second decade of life. The presence of this variant provides a genetic explanation for the patient’s bilateral cataracts, which appear earlier than typically reported for XYLT2-related disease but are consistent with more severe allelic combinations.

Tertiary finding: EYS.

Variant: c.2137 + 1 G > A; chr6-65057613 C > T (hg38); NM_001142800.2; and rs199740930.

Zygosity: Homozygous.

Classification: Likely pathogenic.

EYS (Eyes Shut Homolog, Drosophila) encodes a photoreceptor-specific protein essential for normal retinal development and function. Biallelic EYS variants cause autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa and progressive retinopathy, typically manifesting as progressive night blindness and visual field constriction, with symptoms expected in the second decade of life or later. This variant explains the potential for progressive cataracts in this patient.

Interpretation of multilocus genetic findings

The identification of three distinct biallelic pathogenic variants across separate genes is consistent with a multilocus genetic disorder arising in the context of consanguinity, which increases genome-wide homozygosity. Such multilocus inheritance patterns are increasingly recognized in genomic studies of consanguineous populations, particularly in the Middle East and South Asia. In this case, the patient harbors homozygous recessive variants underlying three independent genetic conditions:

1. SpADMiSS (primary neurodevelopmental disorder).

2. XYLT2-related disease (contributing to the cataract phenotype).

3. EYS-related retinopathy (contributing to ocular involvement).

This multilocus inheritance explains the phenotypic complexity and severity of this patient’s presentation, extending beyond what would be expected from SPADMISS alone.

Phenotypic novelty and clinical significance

Basal ganglia calcification: a new feature

The most striking and previously unreported finding in this patient is the presence of bilateral basal ganglia calcification. Although the original SpADMiSS cohort of 28 individuals did not include basal ganglia calcification as a recognized feature[1], the current case demonstrates this finding definitively on CT imaging. Ocular phenotype: evidence for multilocus disease

The presence of bilateral cataracts extends beyond the SpADMiSS phenotype and represents evidence of additional genetic contributions. While ocular abnormalities were noted in 39% of the SpADMiSS cohort (including microphthalmia, nystagmus, slit-like eyes, and leukocoria), congenital cataracts were not described[1]. The identification of pathogenic XYLT2 and EYS variants specifically explains the ocular findings and demonstrates the importance of comprehensive genetic analysis in consanguineous families, where multiple recessive conditions may co-segregate[7,8].

Discussion

We report a novel presentation of SpADMiSS syndrome in a 5.5-year-old girl, confirming the core phenotype of global developmental delay, microcephaly, and infantile spasms caused by the recurrent homozygous SPOUT1 p.Gly98Ser variant. Beyond verifying the recently established syndrome, this case provides two critical insights into the clinical and genetic architecture of neurodevelopmental disorders in consanguineous populations: the identification of basal ganglia calcification as a potential new feature of SpADMiSS and the demonstration of a blended phenotype resulting from multilocus pathogenic variation.

Basal ganglia calcification: a novel phenotypic expansion

The most significant finding in this patient is bilateral basal ganglia calcification, a feature absent in the cohort of 28 individuals described by Dharmadhikari et al[1]. In pediatric populations, basal ganglia calcification is a radiologic hallmark with a wide differential diagnosis, ranging from infectious etiologies (such as TORCH complex, HIV) to metabolic disorders (such as hypoparathyroidism) and monogenic interferonopathies like Aicardi–Goutières syndrome[9,10]. Having ruled out infectious and metabolic causes, and given the absence of pathogenic variants in known primary familial brain calcification genes (e.g., SLC20A2, PDGFB, and MYORG), we propose that basal ganglia calcification represents a genuine phenotypic expansion of SPOUT1-related disease.

The mechanism underlying this calcification remains speculative but biologically plausible. SPOUT1 is expressed in the basal ganglia, and its loss of function disrupts mitotic spindle organization, leading to aneuploidy and cell death[11]. We hypothesize that the calcification observed here is likely “dystrophic,” which is secondary to chronic neuronal apoptosis and tissue degeneration in the striatum, rather than a primary defect in phosphate homeostasis. This phenomenon is analogous to calcifications seen in other severe neurodegenerative conditions, like mitochondrial encephalopathies (Leigh syndrome)[12].

Multilocus inheritance and the “blended phenotype”

This case demonstrates the complexity of “blended phenotypes” in consanguineous families. While the basal ganglia calcification likely arises from the SPOUT1 defect, the patient’s bilateral cataracts and ocular involvement are best explained by the concurrent homozygous variants in XYLT2 and EYS, respectively.

Distinguishing between true phenotypic expansion and multilocus inheritance is critical[13]. Without WES, there would be a significant risk of attributing the cataracts to SPOUT1 by mistake, thereby contaminating the clinical description of SpADMiSS for future clinicians. Recent large-scale cohort studies suggest that 2.4–7% of individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders in consanguineous populations harbor diagnoses involving two or more distinct genetic loci[14,15]. This case serves as a cautionary tale: features that appear to be outliers in a known syndrome (such as early-onset cataracts in SpADMiSS) should trigger a search for a second genetic etiology rather than an automatic assumption of variable expressivity.

Clinical implications and conclusion

This report expands the phenotypic spectrum of SpADMiSS to include basal ganglia calcification, suggesting that SPOUT1/CENP-32 should be considered in the genetic differential diagnosis of childhood intracranial calcifications, particularly in the presence of microcephaly and refractory seizures. Furthermore, these findings highlight the importance of comprehensive genomic approaches, such as whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing, over targeted gene panels in consanguineous families. As illustrated in this case, a single-gene diagnosis may not fully account for the patient’s clinical presentation, whereas characterization of the overall mutational burden is critical for accurate prognostication, informed genetic counseling, and appropriate surveillance for comorbidities.
