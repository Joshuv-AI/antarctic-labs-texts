Association of stress hyperglycemia ratio on right ventricular dysfunction and biventricular coupling in patients with acute coronary syndrome assessed by CMR feature tracking: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Pei-Lun Han, Ji-Er Ma, Ye Deng, Ge Zhang, Jing Liu, Wei-Feng Yan, Li-Ting Shen, Zhu-Lin Feng, Xiao-Ling Wen, Zhi-Gang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmong individuals diagnosed with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) has been demonstrated to correlate with an elevated risk of unfavorable cardiovascular events. However, the relationship between SHR, right ventricular (RV) function and biventricular interdependence has not yet been reported. The present study aimed to explore the association of high SHR with RV deformation and biventricular coupling in ACS patients via cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR).MethodsThis study consisted of 103 patients with ACS and 57 age- and sex-matched controls. Based on the median of SHR values, ACS patients were stratified into low (<1.07, n=51) and high (≥1.07, n=52) SHR groups. Biventricular myocardial deformation, encompassing global peak strain (PS) in the radial, circumferential, and longitudinal directions for both left ventricle and right ventricle, was quantified and compared across groups. Linear regression and exploratory mediation analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between SHR and RV PS and to examine whether LV PS may partly explain this association.ResultsACS patients, irrespective of SHR level, had significantly decreased left ventricular (LV) and RV PS, compared to controls (all P<0.001). Among ACS patients, the high SHR group had lower LV radial PS [14.32 (10.70, 21.39) vs. 21.45 (11.02, 28.84)], LV longitudinal PS [−7.92 (−10.77, −6.75) vs. −10.26 (−13.00, −6.34)], RV radial PS [25.03 (18.55, 35.02) vs. 33.31 (20.57, 44.66)], and RV longitudinal PS (−11.92±5.40 vs. −16.60±6.57) than the low-SHR group (all P<0.05). Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that LV radial and longitudinal PS may statistically account for part of the observed association between RV radial and longitudinal PS between the low- and high-SHR groups [indirect effect of LV radial PS: −6.159 (−8.982, −3.641) for RV radial PS and 1.859 (0.970, 2.828) for RV longitudinal PS; indirect effect of LV longitudinal PS: 1.859 (0.970, 2.828) for RV radial PS, and 1.617 (0.888, 2.444) for RV longitudinal PS].ConclusionsHigher SHR was associated with more impaired RV radial and longitudinal PS in patients with ACS. Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that LV radial and longitudinal PS may partly explain the association between higher SHR and impaired RV radial and longitudinal PS.

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is characterized by the sudden onset of myocardial ischemia due to a rapid decrease in coronary blood flow. Despite significant advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of ACS, it continues to be a leading cause of mortality and morbidity globally (1,2). Evaluating the systolic function of both left and right ventricle is a critical component of managing patients after ACS, mainly due to its important prognostic value (3). Notably, the role of right ventricular (RV) function in multiple cardiovascular diseases has been gradually emphasized due to its prognostic values. Recent studies have identified adverse RV remodeling in ACS patients (4,5), and the prognosis of ACS patients is closely linked to RV performance (6). These observations emphasize the necessity of a precise and comprehensive evaluation of RV mechanics for patients with ACS.

Stress hyperglycemia is a transient metabolic response in emergency status. This stress status often occurs in ACS patients, and has been proven to be associated with poor prognosis (7). In recent years, stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), which is an emerging marker reflecting stress-induced hyperglycemia, has been gradually recognized as a better relative glycemic indicator though correcting admission blood glucose concentration by background glycemia [glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c)] (8). Recent large cohort studies have shown that SHR is associated with both short-term and long-term adverse outcomes in patients with ACS, supporting its potential value as a relative glycemic stress marker in this population (9). In acute myocardial infarction populations, elevated SHR has also been associated with worse long-term outcomes irrespective of diabetes status, suggesting that SHR captures acute metabolic stress beyond chronic glycemic burden alone (10). Some studies have demonstrated significant association between left ventricular (LV) remodeling indices such as LV volume size, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), as well as LV strains, and SHR in ACS, which may contribute to an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events to some extent (11,12). However, the relationship between SHR and RV deformation in patients with ACS has not been well characterized.

Ejection fraction is a simple and most frequently used index to evaluate ventricular systolic function, but it cannot sensitively reflect the overall cardiac function. cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) feature tracking (CMR-FT) enables the assessment of subtle myocardial alterations that are undetectable with conventional indicators (5,13,14). This method can provide objective and quantitative deformation indices for both left and right ventricles. In addition, the ventricular interdependence in ACS, especially in hyperglycemic states, is not well described, which may help inform possible preventive strategies for adverse outcomes. Therefore, the aims of the present study are to evaluate: (I) the association between SHR and RV function; and (II) whether LV dysfunction may partly explain RV strain impairment among ACS patients with higher SHR, using CMR-FT. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0267/rc).

Methods

Study population

A total of 426 consecutive ACS patients who underwent CMR between January 2012 and August 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. ACS diagnoses, including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and unstable angina, were established according to current clinical practice guidelines (3). All ACS patients received standard emergency management according to clinical indications, including PCI when appropriate. CMR was performed after emergency treatment and after the patients’ clinical condition had stabilized. The interval from the index ACS event to CMR acquisition was recorded for each patient. Exclusion criteria comprised known cardiac conditions [e.g., primary cardiomyopathy (n=1), severe valvular heart disease (n=13), congenital heart disease (n=1)], disorders potentially impacting right heart function [e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (n=6), pulmonary embolism (n=1), pulmonary hypertension (n=1)], incomplete essential clinical data (n=296, mainly due to the lack of HbA1c information), and poor image quality precluding analysis (n=4) (Figure S1). The control group consisted of sex- and age-matched individuals meeting the inclusion criteria, defined as the absence of cardiovascular symptoms (dyspnea, chest pain, palpitations) and no detectable abnormalities on CMR (such as wall motion abnormalities, perfusion defects, or myocardial late gadolinium enhancement). Ultimately, 103 ACS patients (12 females, 91 males; mean age 57.88±10.84 years) and 57 controls (8 females, 49 males; mean age 57.58±10.08 years) were enrolled. Because no universally accepted SHR cut-off has been established for ACS patients undergoing CMR, patients were stratified into low- (<1.07, n=51) and high-SHR (≥1.07, n=52) groups according to the cohort-specific median SHR value. In addition, SHR was also analyzed as a continuous variable in correlation and regression analyses. Admission blood glucose was obtained at hospital admission as part of the initial clinical evaluation for ACS, before or during emergency management according to the clinical workflow. HbA1c was measured during the index hospitalization and used as an indicator of chronic background glycemia. SHR was calculated as admission blood glucose divided by the estimated average glucose derived from HbA1c, according to the formula proposed by Roberts et al. (8): SHR = admission blood glucose (mmol/L)/[1.59 × HbA1c (%) − 2.59]. In this equation, HbA1c was expressed as percentage (%), and [1.59 × HbA1c (%) − 2.59] represents the estimated average glucose concentration in mmol/L. The subtraction term “−2.59” is the intercept of the HbA1c-derived estimated average glucose equation rather than an arbitrary correction factor. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (approval No. 2019-756), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

CMR scanning protocol

CMR imaging was carried out using 3.0-T whole-body scanners (Tim Trio and MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). Cine imaging was performed using a balanced steady-state free precession sequence. Standard long-axis cine images, including 2-chamber, 3-chamber/LVOT, and 4-chamber views, together with 8 to 12 contiguous short-axis slices covering both ventricles from base to apex, were acquired throughout the cardiac cycle. Acquisition parameters included repetition time (2.8 or 3.4 ms), echo time (1.22 ms), flip angle (40° or 50°), slice thickness (8 mm), field of view (250×300 or 340×285 mm2), and matrix size (208×139 or 256×166 pixels). A gadolinium-based contrast agent was administered intravenously at 0.2 mL/kg with an injection rate of 2.5 to 3.0 mL/s, followed by a 20 mL saline flush delivered at 3.0 mL/s. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging was performed 10 to 15 minutes following contrast administration using a segmented turbo FLASH phase-sensitive inversion recovery sequence. Imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time (750 or 512 ms), echo time (1.18 or 1.24 ms), flip angle (20° or 40°), slice thickness (8 mm), field of view (240×300 or 288×360 mm2), and matrix size (256×184 or 256×125 pixels).

Imaging analysis

CMR image analysis was conducted using CVI42, version 6.2.2 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Inc., Calgary, Canada). Measurements were carried out independently by two experienced radiologists who were blinded to clinical data. Any discrepancies in their assessments were resolved through consensus discussion.

Cardiac structure and function analysis

Ventricular volumes, ejection fraction, and myocardial mass were analyzed using the Short-3D module of CVI42, version 6.2.2. Cine images acquired for each patient comprised 8 to 12 contiguous short-axis slices, along with 2-chamber, 3-chamber/LVOT, and 4-chamber long-axis views obtained throughout a single cardiac cycle. Endocardial and epicardial borders of the left and right ventricles were manually delineated on serial short-axis slices at both end-diastole and end-systole. The LV/RV end diastolic volume (EDV), end systolic volume (ESV), ejection fraction (EF) and myocardial mass were automatically derived. LV/RV mass index (LVMI/RVMI) was calculated by indexing LV/RV myocardial mass to body surface area. LV/RV remodeling index (LVRI/RVRI) was calculated by indexing LV/RV myocardial mass to LV/RVEDV.

Feature tracking analysis

Myocardial deformation was evaluated using the feature-tracking module of CVI42 (Figure 1). Endocardial and epicardial borders of the left ventricle were manually traced on end-diastolic short-axis, 4-chamber, and 2-chamber long-axis views, while those of the right ventricle were traced on end-diastolic short-axis and 4-chamber long-axis views. Feature tracking analysis was subsequently conducted with end-diastole set as the reference phase. Global peak strain (PS) values for radial, circumferential, and longitudinal directions were automatically obtained for both the left and right ventricles.

To assess the comparability between conventional 2D feature-tracking strain and the originally reported global strain measurements, 2D strain analysis was additionally performed in a randomly selected 20% subset of the study population (n=32). Two-dimensional LV strain was measured from standard short-axis and long-axis cine images, and 2D RV strain was measured from the 4-chamber cine view where appropriate. Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between 2D and global feature-tracking-derived strain values.

LGE analysis and infarct characterization

Myocardial scar was defined as a region with signal intensity exceeding the mean signal intensity of remote normal myocardium by five standard deviations (15). For LV infarct assessment, the endocardial and epicardial borders of the left ventricle were manually contoured on contiguous short-axis LGE images, and normal-appearing remote myocardium was selected as the reference region (16). The global LV infarct mass was automatically calculated, and LV infarct size was expressed as the percentage of infarct mass to LV myocardial mass. LV infarct territory was determined according to the American Heart Association 16-segment model (17) and categorized as interventricular septal, anterior, inferior, or lateral wall involvement.

RV infarction was assessed on short-axis and long-axis LGE image (18). RV infarction was defined as focal or regional LGE involving the RV free wall, distinguishable from adjacent blood pool, epicardial fat, and artifacts. Isolated RV insertion-point LGE was not considered definite RV infarction (19,20). For quantitative RV infarct assessment, the RV endocardial and epicardial borders were manually contoured on LGE images in slices with visible RV myocardium. Hyperenhanced RV myocardium was identified using the same five-standard-deviation threshold method when image quality allowed reliable delineation. RV infarct mass was calculated as the mass of hyperenhanced RV myocardium, and RV infarct size was expressed as the percentage of RV infarct mass to total RV myocardial mass. RV infarct territory was categorized according to the involved RV region, including anterior, inferior, lateral wall involvement. All LGE analyses were performed by experienced observers blinded to clinical and strain data, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Reproducibility analysis

Inter- and intraobserver reproducibility of LV/RV PS was assessed in 30 randomly selected individuals (20 ACS patients and 10 controls). One observer measured LV/RV once, and a second observer who was blinded to the first observers results measured LV/RV at two time points 1 month later.

Statistical analysis

The normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%). Comparisons of continuous variables among three groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA with LSD post hoc test or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Categorical data were compared using the chi-squared test. Associations between high SHR and LV or RV PS were examined using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. Linear regression analysis was employed to identify independent correlates of ventricular strain. Variables including age, sex, clinically relevant factors, and those with a P value <0.1 in univariate analysis were entered into a stepwise multivariate linear regression model. Stepwise regression was used to reduce model complexity, and multicollinearity was separately assessed using variance inflation factors. To assess the incremental association of SHR beyond admission blood glucose and HbA1c, correlation analyses and multivariable linear regression models were performed by entering admission blood glucose, HbA1c, and SHR simultaneously. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Exploratory mediation analysis was performed to examine whether LV strain parameters could statistically account for the association between SHR group and RV strain parameters. SHR group was treated as the exposure, LV radial or longitudinal PS as the explanatory variable, and RV radial or longitudinal PS as the outcome. Indirect effects were estimated using nonparametric bootstrapping. Sensitivity analyses were performed with adjustment for age, sex, diabetes status, admission blood glucose, HbA1c, LV infarct size, and beta-blocker use. Given the retrospective cross-sectional design, these analyses were considered hypothesis-generating and were not interpreted as evidence of causality. Interobserver and intraobserver reproducibility for ventricular strain measurements was assessed using Bland-Altman analysis and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate the potential influence of scanner heterogeneity on CMR-FT-derived strain measurements, a subgroup comparison of LV and RV global PS parameters was performed between subjects scanned using the Tim Trio and MAGNETOM Skyra systems. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 and R Version: 4.4.2.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Table 1 shows the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the subjects. There was no significant difference between the 3 groups in terms of age and sex (both P>0.05). Body surface area was significantly higher in ACS patients compare with controls (P<0.001). ACS patients with low SHR showed significantly higher heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared with controls (all P<0.05). ACS patients with high SHR showed significantly low diastolic blood pressure, higher prevalence of diabetes, and higher admission blood glucose level (all P<0.05). Regarding medical therapy, the use of major cardiovascular and glucose-lowering medications was generally comparable between the low- and high-SHR groups. Although beta-blocker use was numerically more frequent in the low-SHR group than in the high-SHR group, the difference was not statistically significant. All ACS patients underwent CMR after standard emergency management and clinical stabilization. The interval from the index ACS event to CMR acquisition was 3.50 (2.00, 88.00) days in the low-SHR group and 3.00 (2.00, 5.00) days in the high-SHR group, with no significant between-group difference.

Comparison of CMR indices between ACS patients and controls

A comparison of CMR indices between groups is presented in Table 2. Compared with controls, ACS patients, regardless of the level of SHR, showed increased LVEDV, LVESV and LVMI, and decreased LVEF and LV radial, circumferential, and longitudinal PS (all P<0.05). ACS patients with high SHR showed significantly increased LVRI compared with controls (P<0.001). Regarding traditional RV global structure and function, there was no significant difference between ACS patients and controls (all P>0.05). Whereas, RV radial, circumferential, and longitudinal PS was significantly decreased in ACS patients (all P<0.001).

Comparison of CMR indices between ACS patients with low and high SHR

Among ACS patients, those with high SHR showed decreased LVEDV and LVESV (both P<0.05). Both LV and RV radial and longitudinal PS was significantly decreased in ACS patients with high SHR compared with those with low SHR (all P<0.05). Other parameters of LV and RV global structure and function, LV and RV infarct size and territory did not differ significantly between low and high SHR groups (all P>0.05).

Association of glycemic indices with biventricular deformation

Correlation analysis showed that SHR was significantly correlated with LV radial and longitudinal PS and RV radial and longitudinal PS (all P<0.05) (Figure 2). LV radial, circumferential and longitudinal PS were significantly correlated with RV radial, circumferential and longitudinal PS (all P<0.05). Univariate linear regression analysis showed that SHR and LV radial, circumferential and longitudinal PS were significantly associated with RV radial and longitudinal PS (all P<0.05) (Table 3). Multivariable linear regression analysis showed that SHR and LV longitudinal PS were independent factors associated with RV longitudinal PS (all P<0.05). In addition, beta-blocker use was independently associated with RV radial PS, suggesting that medical therapy may influence RV deformation parameters. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and no severe multicollinearity was observed among the variables retained in the final models.

To assess whether SHR provided additional information beyond admission blood glucose or HbA1c alone, correlation and multivariable regression analyses were further performed. As shown in Figure 2, SHR showed generally stronger correlations with biventricular strain parameters than admission blood glucose or HbA1c, especially for RV radial and longitudinal PS. In multivariable models in which admission blood glucose, HbA1c, and SHR were entered simultaneously, SHR remained independently associated with RV longitudinal PS, whereas admission blood glucose and HbA1c were not independently associated with these RV strain parameters (Table 3). No severe multicollinearity was observed among these glycemic variables. Together, these analyses indicate that SHR retained a stronger relationship with RV deformation than either admission glucose or HbA1c alone.

The ICCs for interobserver reproducibility ranged from 0.991 to 0.999 for LV PS and from 0.981 to 0.990 for RV PS. Detailed ICC values with 95% confidence intervals are presented in Figure 3. In the randomly selected validation subset, conventional 2D feature-tracking-derived strain parameters were compared with the corresponding global feature-tracking-derived strain parameters (Table S2). Strong correlations were observed for LV radial, circumferential, and longitudinal PS (r=0.784, 0.945, and 0.870, respectively; all P<0.001). For RV strain, the correlations were weaker: RV longitudinal PS showed a moderate correlation (r=0.530, P=0.002), RV circumferential PS showed a weak but significant correlation (r=0.370, P=0.037), and RV radial PS showed a weak, non-significant correlation (r=0.295, P=0.102). These findings indicate that the 2D and global feature-tracking-derived measurements were generally comparable for LV strain and partially comparable for RV strain, although RV radial PS should be interpreted cautiously

In the scanner subgroup analysis, most LV and RV global PS parameters did not differ significantly between subjects scanned using the Tim Trio and MAGNETOM Skyra systems (all P>0.05) (Table S3). Overall, CMR-FT-derived strain measurements were broadly comparable between scanners. However, difference in RV circumferential PS in ACS patients was observed (P=0.011), and therefore scanner heterogeneity was further acknowledged as a potential limitation.

Discussion

The current study examined the differences in biventricular structure and function between individuals with ACS exhibiting different levels of SHR and healthy controls, utilizing CMR feature tracking techniques. Given the observed association between LV and RV deformation, we further explored whether LV strain may partly explain differences in RV strain between ACS patients with low and high SHR. Our data showed that (I) in patients with ACS, RV strain was reduced in ACS patients than in normal controls, despite comparable RVEF; (II) ACS patients with high SHR had lower RV radial and longitudinal PS than those with low SHR, and SHR remained independently associated with these strain parameters after adjustment; (III) the difference in RV radial and longitudinal PS between ACS patients with high and low SHR was partly explained by LV radial and longitudinal PS in exploratory mediation analysis.

RV dysfunction and pathophysiological mechanisms in ACS patients

ACS may be associated with impaired myocardial deformation in both ventricles during the early post-ACS period, though it more commonly affects the left ventricle, as our results indicated (21). Cardiomyocytes affected by ACS undergo ischemic challenges that disrupt metabolic processes and elevate oxidative stress levels (22). It has been documented that oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species are critical contributors to the pathogenesis of ischemia and reperfusion injury (23). These factors can directly inflict cellular damage and initiate signaling pathways that lead to cellular dysfunction (24,25), thereby initiating apoptosis and necrosis in cardiac tissue (26). Several other mechanisms, such as coagulation, inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and autonomic dysregulation, are implicated in the pathophysiology of myocardial ischemia. We speculate that all these mechanisms may collectively contribute to not only LV but also RV functional alterations after ACS, which are characterized intricate architectural modifications that lead to the reorganization of the existing myocardial structure (23).

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed towards the function of the right ventricle owing to its critical role in determining functional status and clinical outcomes (27-29). Previous literatures have demonstrated that patients with ACS exhibited significantly lower RV strains compared to healthy controls (5,30,31), and our findings corroborate their statements. Nevertheless, the RVEF of patients with ACS was comparable with those of healthy controls. These findings suggest that myocardial strain may serve as a more sensitive indicator of RV dysfunction than RVEF. Consequently, the evaluation of RV strain could be a valuable complement to traditional clinical indices and RV function assessments in determining the optimal timing for the management of patients with ACS.

Association between SHR and RV dysfunction in ACS patients

Stress hyperglycemia reflects the severity of an emergency and, to some degree, suboptimal glucose regulation. Furthermore, stress hyperglycemia has been associated with adverse acute cardiac conditions through several mechanisms (32), including the enhancement of endothelial dysfunction (33), reduction of platelet nitric oxide responsiveness (34), aggravation of microvascular obstruction (35), and the induction of additional hyperglycemia-mediated vascular damage pathways (36). Recently, SHR has emerged as a novel and better biological marker for identifying true acute hyperglycemia, compared to absolute hyperglycemia at the time of admission (8). Some studies have highlighted the prognostic value of SHR in patients with ACS, particularly those experiencing acute myocardial infarction (9,36-38).

The RV analysis conducted in the present study revealed a significant reduction in RV radial and longitudinal PS in ACS patients with elevated SHR compared to those with lower SHR. Both the LV and RV longitudinal myocardial fibers are predominantly located in the subendocardium, and these fibers particularly vulnerable to early ischemic events and hyperglycemia-induced damage to the endocardium (32,39,40). This may be one of the main causes of the more severe deterioration of the longitudinal strain in the right ventricles of patients with ACS and high SHR. Additionally, increased SHR was identified as an independent factor associated with diminished RV radial and longitudinal PS. These results may provide imaging-based evidence linking higher SHR to impaired RV deformation and support the need for further evaluation of RV function in ACS patients with elevated SHR.

Beyond ACS, SHR has also been linked to adverse outcomes in severe infection (41-43), heart failure (44), and ischemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries (44), supporting its role as a marker of acute metabolic stress and systemic vulnerability. These observations are consistent with our imaging findings, in which higher SHR was associated with impaired biventricular deformation. However, those studies focused mainly on clinical outcomes, whereas the present study evaluated CMR-derived strain; therefore, direct extrapolation should be avoided.

Medical therapy may also affect ventricular deformation. In our cohort, the use of major cardiovascular and glucose-lowering medications was not significantly different between the low- and high-SHR groups. However, beta-blocker use tended to be more frequent in the low-SHR group and was independently associated with RV radial PS in multivariable analysis. Beta-blockers may influence RV strain by reducing heart rate, myocardial contractility, myocardial oxygen demand, sympathetic activation, and ventricular loading conditions. They may also affect metabolic responses, including glucose metabolism, although these effects may vary according to the specific beta-blocker used and patient characteristics (45).

Relationship between LV strain and RV strain in ACS patients with higher SHR

The results of our study showed significant associations between LV and RV strain parameters in patients with ACS, consistent with previous findings by Wen et al. (5). These associations may be partly explained by ventricular coupling through shared anatomical and hemodynamic mechanisms (46). Specifically, the anatomical substrate includes common interventricular septum, myocardial tracts encircled the ventricles (47), and pericardium limiting pericardial flexibility. Of these, the interventricular septum is a key component since it is involved directly in the contraction and relaxation of both ventricle (48,49). Besides, changes in LV stroke volume can affect the loading conditions of the right ventricle. Changes in LV structure and function may be associated with RV deformation in ACS patients; however, shared myocardial injury and global ventricular dysfunction may also contribute to the observed LV-RV strain correlations. Furthermore, whether LV strain alterations may partly explain the association between elevated SHR and reduced RV strain remains unclear. In the present exploratory analysis, impaired RV radial and longitudinal strain in ACS patients with higher SHR was statistically associated with LV strain parameters in exploratory mediation analyses. Given the interdependent nature of the left and right ventricles, assessment of LV dysfunction may help identify patients with concomitant RV strain impairment. However, whether improvement in LV function can mitigate RV dysfunction requires confirmation in longitudinal studies.

The observed correlations between LV and RV strain parameters should be interpreted with caution. Although these associations are consistent with the concept of ventricular coupling, they do not by themselves prove a specific mechanism of biventricular interdependence. In ACS patients, LV and RV strain impairment may also reflect shared effects of global myocardial injury, infarct burden, ischemia-related myocardial dysfunction, hemodynamic loading conditions, or overall disease severity. Therefore, the association between LV and RV deformation observed in this study may represent both ventricular coupling and common myocardial damage. Similarly, the exploratory mediation analysis should be regarded as a statistical assessment of whether LV strain partly explains the association between SHR and RV strain, rather than evidence of a causal LV-to-RV pathway.

The mediation findings should be interpreted with caution. Although the unadjusted exploratory mediation analysis suggested that LV radial and longitudinal PS may partly explain the association between higher SHR and impaired RV strain, the adjusted mediation sensitivity analyses showed that the indirect effects were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after accounting for age, sex, diabetes status, admission blood glucose, HbA1c, LV infarct size, and beta-blocker use. These results indicate that the observed mediation pattern may be influenced by clinical, metabolic, and infarct-related factors. Therefore, the mediation analysis should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and does not establish a causal LV-to-RV pathway. We did not perform reverse mediation analysis because the primary focus of this study was RV deformation in relation to SHR. Nevertheless, LV-RV interactions may be bidirectional, and the cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding temporal sequence or causal direction (46,50).

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although consecutive ACS patients undergoing CMR were screened, a substantial proportion of patients were excluded mainly because of missing HbA1c data required for SHR calculation; this may have introduced selection bias.

Second, residual confounding may still exist. Although several clinical and metabolic factors were considered in the revised analyses, including diabetes status, admission blood glucose, HbA1c, LV infarct size, and beta-blocker use, the limited sample size restricted more detailed subgroup analyses according to different glucose metabolic states. In addition, medication timing, dosage, duration, and adherence were not fully available because of the retrospective design. Furthermore, although variable selection procedures were applied to reduce model complexity, the relatively limited sample size may still increase the risk of model overfitting in the multivariable analyses. Therefore, the identified associations require confirmation in larger independent cohorts.

Third, CMR-related factors may have influenced strain measurements. Although CMR was performed after standard emergency treatment and clinical stabilization, the timing of CMR acquisition was not prospectively standardized. Myocardial strain parameters during the early post-ACS period may still be affected by acute ischemia, myocardial stunning, edema, infarct extent, and reperfusion-related changes. Moreover, two scanner models with different acquisition parameters were used. Although most LV and RV global PS parameters were comparable between scanner subgroups and reproducibility analysis showed good measurement reliability, scanner heterogeneity and protocol-related variability could not be completely excluded. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and mitral annular plane systolic excursion (MAPSE) were not included because standardized annular displacement measurements were not part of the original imaging protocol.

Fourth, the mediation analysis should be interpreted cautiously. Because of the retrospective cross-sectional design, temporal sequence and causal relationships among SHR, LV strain, and RV strain could not be established. Furthermore, adjusted sensitivity analyses showed that the indirect effects through LV strain were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after adjustment for clinical and metabolic covariates. Therefore, the mediation findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a causal pathway.

Finally, clinical outcome data were not available in the present study. Therefore, we could not evaluate whether SHR-related RV strain impairment was associated with mortality, heart failure hospitalization, arrhythmias, or other adverse cardiovascular events. The clinical and prognostic relevance of impaired RV strain in relation to SHR remains uncertain and should be investigated in future prospective longitudinal studies.

Conclusions

Higher SHR was associated with lower LV and RV radial and longitudinal PS in ACS patients. LV strain parameters were statistically associated with this relationship in exploratory mediation analyses, although the indirect effects were attenuated after covariate adjustment. These results support combined LV and RV deformation assessment in ACS patients with elevated SHR, while longitudinal studies are required to determine causal and prognostic relevance.
