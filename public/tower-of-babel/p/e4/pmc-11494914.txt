Bayesian sparse regression for exposure–response analyses of efficacy and safety endpoints to justify the clinical dose of valemetostat for adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma
Authors: Masato Fukae, James Rogers, Ramon Garcia, Masaya Tachibana, Takako Shimizu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractValemetostat is an oral inhibitor of enhancer of zeste homolog (EZH) 2 and EZH1 approved in Japan for the treatment of adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL). To support the approved daily dose of 200 mg and inform dose adjustments in patients with ATLL, Bayesian exposure–response analyses were conducted using data from two clinical trials. The analyses included two efficacy endpoints, overall response by central and investigator assessments in patients with ATLL (n = 38, 150–200 mg), and six safety endpoints in patients with non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 102, 150–300 mg), which included grade ≥3 laboratory values for anemia, absolute neutrophil count decreased, and platelet count decreased; any grade ≥3 treatment‐emergent adverse event (TEAE); and dose reductions and dose interruptions due to TEAEs. A slightly positive relationship was observed between unbound exposure and efficacy endpoints. A steeper relationship was observed in safety endpoints, compared with efficacy. Candidate covariate effects, except intercepts of the baseline laboratory values, were regularized via spike and slab priors in a Bayesian framework; only the laboratory values for corresponding hematologic TEAEs were shown to be of substantial impact. The target exposure range was established by defining a modified region of practical equivalence (184–887 ng·h/mL), which was expected to provide satisfactory efficacy and acceptable safety within the range of available exposure data. The simulated exposure range considering inter‐individual variability showed that 200 mg could reach target exposure in the overall population and across subpopulations of interest, supporting the use of valemetostat 200 mg in patients with ATLL.

INTRODUCTION

Adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) is a form of non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) that is characterized by a poor prognosis. For patients with the aggressive acute and lymphomatous ATLL subtypes, median overall survival times are 4.1 and 10.2 months, respectively, and 4‐year overall survival rates are 10% and 4%, respectively. 1 , 2 , 3 These forms of ATLL exhibit chemotherapy resistance and immunosuppression, 4 and most patients who do respond to aggressive frontline therapy will experience disease relapse, often within months of completing chemotherapy. 2

Valemetostat tosylate (valemetostat; formerly DS‐3201b) is a novel, potent, and selective dual inhibitor of enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) and EZH1, two enzymatic subunits that catalyze for a repressive transcriptional mark, which silences genes involved in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis, and is involved in the regulation of lymphocyte activation and differentiation. 5 , 6 , 7 Mutations and/or overexpression of EZH2 are responsible for the development and progression of many NHLs, including ATLL. 8 , 9 Valemetostat was approved in September 2022 in Japan for the treatment of patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) ATLL, 10 , 11 and is currently under investigation in Japan, the United States, Europe, and elsewhere for the treatment of R/R NHLs and solid tumors with aberrant EZH2 expression.

Valemetostat monotherapy has been investigated in two open‐label clinical trials for the treatment of patients with R/R NHLs, including ATLL: the phase I DS3201‐A‐J101 study (“J101”; NCT02732275), and the phase II DS3201‐A‐J201 study (“J201”; NCT04102150). Both studies demonstrated favorable tolerability, safety, and efficacy in patients receiving valemetostat 200 mg/day. In J201, the overall response rate (ORR) was 48% (12/25; 90% confidence interval [CI], 30.5%–65.9%), inducing complete response (CR) in five patients and partial response (PR) in seven patients. 12 , 13

This article presents exposure–response (E‐R) analyses using data from the J101 and J201 clinical trials, which were performed to assess the valemetostat exposure range associated with acceptable efficacy, safety, and tolerability in patients with R/R ATLL, in order to justify the valemetostat dose for a typical patient with ATLL and in subpopulations of interest that might warrant a dosing regimen other than the standard recommended dose of 200 mg. Moreover, this study aimed to investigate whether the E‐R relationships for efficacy and safety endpoints were different among patient subpopulations with different values of covariate. A novel approach for E‐R analysis with a full covariate model in a Bayesian framework, with spike and slab priors, was employed for this purpose. 14

METHODS

Study design and patient eligibility criteria

The E‐R analyses included pooled data from the J101 and J201 clinical trials of valemetostat monotherapy in patients with R/R NHLs, including ATLL.

The first‐in‐human, multicenter, open‐label, phase I trial (J101) enrolled adult patients in Japan and the United States with R/R NHLs. The study included a dose‐escalation phase, which assessed the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), and preliminary efficacy of valemetostat dose levels of 150, 200, 250, and 300 mg, followed by a dose‐expansion phase further assessing the clinical activity of valemetostat 200 mg. 13 The dose‐escalation phase comprised of patients with various R/R NHLs; the subsequent dose‐expansion phase was limited to patients with R/R ATLL or other peripheral T‐cell lymphomas. Valemetostat was administered orally once daily until progressive disease or unacceptable toxicity.

The multicenter, open‐label, single‐arm, phase II trial (J201) was conducted in Japan and enrolled adult patients with R/R ATLL. All patients received oral valemetostat 200 mg/day until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. 12

In both trials, clinical responses to valemetostat in patients with R/R ATLL were defined per modified ATLL 2009 response criteria, 15 and the ORR was the proportion of patients achieving CR (including uncertified CR) or PR. Responses were determined by central assessment in J201, and by investigator assessment in both trials. Laboratory hematology values and TEAEs were assessed in patients who received at least one dose of valemetostat.

Concentrations of total and unbound valemetostat in plasma were serially assessed in all patients 16 and included in a population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) model, 17 which was used to predict individual exposure metrics in the E–R models.

Exposure–response models

Exposure–efficacy and exposure–safety models were developed and comprised of patients enrolled in the two clinical trials with at least one valemetostat concentration observation in the PopPK analysis, and with non‐missing specific efficacy or safety endpoint data.

Each model employed a logistic regression in a Bayesian framework with spike and slab priors for covariate effects. 14 The exposure–efficacy models included clinical response endpoints for patients with R/R ATLL, determined by central assessment in J201 (n = 25; valemetostat 200 mg) and by investigator assessment in both trials (n = 38; valemetostat 150–200 mg). The exposure–safety models included patients with R/R NHL in either trial (n = 102; valemetostat 150–300 mg) and assessed the following safety endpoints: any grade ≥3 TEAEs (per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grading); grade ≥3 laboratory value abnormalities of anemia, absolute neutrophil count (ANC) decreased, or platelet count (PLT) decreased; dose interruptions due to TEAEs; and dose reductions due to TEAEs. The explored patient‐specific exposure metrics were total and unbound valemetostat areas under the concentration–time curve for a dosing interval at steady state (AUCSS), total and unbound maximum concentrations in the dosing interval at steady state (C max,ss), and total and unbound minimum concentrations in the dosing interval at steady state (C min,ss). Unbound AUCSS was chosen for the model development process.

For the exposure–efficacy analysis, the following covariates were considered as potential modifiers of the logit‐intercept and/or the logit‐slope with respect to the exposure metric: sex (male/female), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) (0/1+), and NHL disease stage (I–III/IV), as categorical covariates; and age, weight, and lactate dehydrogenase concentration (LDH) as continuous covariates. For the exposure–safety analysis, geographic region (Japan/United States), sex (male/female), race (Asian/white/black), hepatic impairment (normal function/mild impairment), and NHL disease stage (I–III/IV), were considered as candidate categorical covariates, baseline laboratory values for corresponding hematologic TEAEs (ANC, PLT, and hemoglobin [Hb]) were included as continuous covariates.

The efficacy endpoints were modeled as binary responses using logistic regression as follows: logitPYi=1Ei,xi=μ+βE*Ei+xiTβ1*+xiTβ2*Ei, where Yi is equal to 1 if patient i is a responder and 0 if a non‐responder, Ei is the exposure value of unbound AUC SS for patient i; xi is the vector of baseline covariates of patient i; μ is the average ORR in the population on the logit scale; both E (exposure) and x (continuous covariates only) were centered and scaled at approximate population median and standard deviation values; βE* is the effect (slope) of exposure in the logit scale; β1*=β1l*…β1J* is the vector of effects of xi on the intercept in the logit scale; β2*=β2l*…β2J* is the vector of effects of xi on the slope in the logit scale. Model fitting was carried out in a Bayesian framework using weakly informative priors 18 , 19 , 20 for the intercept μ and for the slope with respect to exposure βE*. For the other effects, β1* and β2*, a normal mixture spike and slab prior was used. These priors allow for stable predictions and estimates in the presence of many covariate effects while applying shrinkage that can be controlled through a mixing parameter λ. 14 , 21 The full prior specification for regression parameters was: μ∼Nslab βE*∼Nslab β1j*∣λ∼λNspike+1−λNslab β2j*∣λ∼λNspike+1−λNslab λ∼Betaa,b where λ is the mixing parameter that denotes the prior probability that a covariate effect belongs to the spike; the slab prior Nslab was set to Normal (0, 2.5), and the spike prior Nspike was set to Normal (0, 0.1); Beta (a, b) is a beta‐distribution with parameters a and b and consequently has mean a/(a+b). Beta (1, 1), corresponding to Unif (0, 1), was used for primary analyses while other values for a and b were considered in sensitivity analyses. Posterior samples were obtained from four Markov chains starting from dispersed initial values. Following 2000 iterations as a warm‐up sequence, iterations were continued until an effective sample size of 1000 was attained. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) convergence was assessed using the Gelman‐Rubin diagnostic. 22 In addition, trace plots and density plots of post‐warm‐up samples were generated to assess convergence.

For the exposure–safety analyses, grade ≥3 laboratory values (Hb, ANC, or PLT), any grade ≥3 TEAEs, dose interruptions due to TEAEs, and dose reductions due to TEAEs were modeled as binary responses. The models were developed in a similar way as was done for the efficacy endpoints analysis, with the only difference being that for the laboratory value‐based TEAEs, (anemia, PLT decreased, and ANC decreased) the regularization on the baseline laboratory values (Hb, PLT, and ANC) on the intercept was removed; given the self‐evident a priori relevance of these baseline values, only a slab prior was used for their covariate effects.

Posterior diagnostics and sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure that final inferences were not unduly influenced by hyperparameters governing the degree of regularization.

Model evaluation

The goodness of fit of the models was assessed using two methods: (1) the randomized quantile residuals method, in which randomly generated residuals are plotted against model predicted values and model covariates 23 ; and (2) the visual predictive check method, in which a model and parameters derived from an observed data set should produce simulations of the data (n = 1000 posterior simulation replicates) that are similar to the original observed data. 24

Model‐based simulations

The fitted models were used to predict efficacy and safety endpoints at exposure ranges predicted under 200 mg daily dosing of valemetostat in subpopulations of interest. The fitted models were also used to establish a region of practical equivalence (ROPE). As a working definition, two different exposure levels were considered to be practically equivalent if they were both expected to result in satisfactory efficacy and safety/tolerability. For ORR, a ROPE for efficacy endpoints, a lower limit of exposure associated with satisfactory efficacy was defined as the lowest value e such that: PXPOR|E=e,X≥π≥p

In other words, “e” is defined as the exposure level that is associated with 100*p% of patients each having a patient‐specific probability of at least π for achieving an endpoint. A satisfactory ORR endpoint was defined at a threshold (π) of 30%, and the proportion of patients (p) set to achieve this threshold was defined at 50%. The exposure “e” is therefore equivalent to the exposure providing an expected ORR of 30% in a typical patient, which was consistent with the response rates assumed to calculate sample size in the pivotal phase II clinical trial (J201).

Dose reductions due to TEAEs were selected as a unique safety endpoint used in the ROPE analyses where the upper limit of exposure “e”, associated with acceptable safety, was defined as the highest value of “e” such that: PXPDose Reductiondueto TEAE|E=e,X≤π≥p

A satisfactory endpoint was defined at a threshold (π) of 50%, and the proportion of patients (p) set to achieve this threshold was defined at 90%, that is, 90% of patients receiving an initial dose that is likely (probability >50%) to be maintained.

Ethics statement

The trials were conducted in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and Good Clinical Practice guidelines outlined by the International Council for Harmonization, and in accordance with applicable local regulatory requirements. The study protocols were approved by corresponding Institutional Review Boards. All participants provided written informed consent prior to any trial procedure.

SOFTWARE

The E–R modeling for efficacy and safety endpoints was implemented in CmdStanR, version 0.4.0, using the default no‐U‐turn sampler with the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo method. 25

Data assembly and all other pre‐ or post‐processing was conducted in R version 4.0.3 (http://www.r‐project.org).

RESULTS

Patient populations

During the data assembly for the exposure–efficacy models 39 patients with R/R ATLL were available; however, one patient had missing efficacy outcomes, resulting in 38 patients with R/R ATLL with available clinical response data during valemetostat treatment (Table 1). Most patients were from Japan (n = 34, 87.2%) and five were from the United States. The population comprised a similar proportion of males (51.3%) and females (48.7%). At baseline, the median age was 69 years (range, 37–84) and median weight was 61.5 kg (34.5–111). The clinical response assessments used for the analyses included 12 responders (ORR 48.0%) and 13 non‐responders per central assessment, and 22 responders (ORR 57.9%) and 16 non‐responders by investigator assessment.

For the exposure–safety models, all 102 patients with R/R NHLs in the J101 and J201 trials were included (Table 1). Seventy‐one patients (69.6%) were Asian from Japan, the remaining were White (21.6%), Black (5.9%) or Asian from the United States (2.9%). The population included a slightly higher proportion of males than females (55.9% and 44.1%, respectively). Median (range) age and weight were 69.0 (37–88) years and 63.4 (34.5–114) kg, respectively. Approximately, two‐thirds of all patients (n = 69; 67.6%) experienced a grade ≥3 TEAE during valemetostat therapy. Grade ≥3 anemia, ANC decreased, and PLT decreased (laboratory values) were reported in 21 (20.6%), 33 (32.4%), and 25 (24.5%) patients, respectively. TEAEs led to valemetostat treatment interruptions for 45 patients (44.1%) and dose reductions for 10 patients (9.8%).

In the E–R model for anemia, the reference patient had a predicted 15% (95% CrI, 8%–23%) probability of experiencing grade ≥3 anemia laboratory values while receiving valemetostat. Being from the United States had a 9% higher odds of anemia compared with being from Japan (OR 1.09 [95% CrI, 0.81–2.41]). Rates for grade ≥3 anemia were predicted to be higher in patients with grade 2 anemia values at baseline (posterior median estimate 34% [95% CrI, 20%–51%]), but were otherwise comparable across all subpopulations (Figure S7A).

In the E–R model for ANC decrease, the reference patient had a predicted probability of 33% (95% CrI, 23%–44%) for experiencing a grade ≥3 ANC decrease with valemetostat. In the E–R model for PLT decrease, the reference patient had a predicted probability of 17% (95% CrI, 9%–26%) to experience a grade ≥3 PLT decrease while receiving valemetostat. In these exposure–safety models assessing laboratory value changes, a 250 ng·h/mL increase in unbound valemetostat AUCSS from the reference value (375 ng·h/mL) was associated with a marked 103% increase in the odds of grade ≥3 PLT decreased (OR 2.03 [95% CrI, 1.23–3.68]), 52% higher odds of grade ≥3 anemia (1.52 [0.90–2.54]), and 38% higher odds of grade ≥3 ANC decreased (1.38 [0.87–2.31]).

The model for any grade ≥3 TEAEs resulted in the following predictions: the reference patient had a predicted probability of any grade ≥3 TEAE of 65% (95% CrI, 55%–75%) while receiving valemetostat; a 250 ng·h/mL increase in unbound valemetostat AUCSS was associated with 51% increased odds for any grade ≥3 TEAE (OR 1.51 [95% CrI, 0.89–2.51] vs. reference); a 300 U/L increase in baseline LDH was associated with 42% higher odds for any grade ≥3 TEAE (OR 1.42 [95% CrI, 0.96–6.95]). Rates of dose interruptions/reductions due to TEAEs were comparable across all evaluated subpopulations (Figure S7E,F).

Region of practical equivalence (ROPE)

A ROPE was established to justify the recommended valemetostat dose for typical patients and subpopulations considering inter‐individual variability in PKs. Model‐based estimates resulted in a theoretical ROPE extending beyond the observed exposure range, from 0 to 1255 ng·h/mL. To avoid extrapolation beyond the range of the available data, the model‐based ROPE was narrowed to extend only to the 5th and 95th percentiles of the observed exposure distribution, from 184 to 887 ng·h/mL, namely, modified ROPE (Figure S8). Based on the empirical Bayes estimates distribution obtained from the safety dataset, valemetostat exposure levels within the proposed ROPE provided satisfactory ORR by investigator/central assessment, as well as acceptable events of dose reduction due to TEAEs. Predicted distributions of unbound AUCSS at dose levels of 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 mg are shown in Figure S9. The predicted exposure interval between 5th and 95th percentiles at 200 mg of daily dose matches a modified ROPE, which supports the approved 200 mg dose compared with the other dose levels.

Simulated exposure assessments suggested that the valemetostat 200 mg dose level would achieve exposures within the ROPE for subpopulations of interest (Figure 4). Simulations showed that 200 mg of valemetostat provides drug exposure within the modified ROPE in any subpopulation of interest. Age ≥ 65 years does not impact the relationship between exposure and response. Finally, no differences were observed in patients with mild hepatic impairment or with non‐optimal creatinine clearance compared with patients with normal liver/kidney functions. Simulations supported the standard dose of valemetostat 200 mg/day in patients with ATLL and no dose adjustment for the observed subpopulations.

DISCUSSION

This study conducted Bayesian E–R analyses using data from two clinical trials to justify the valemetostat dose for typical patients with ATLL and for subpopulations of interest. Valemetostat exposure range associated with satisfactory efficacy and acceptable safety or tolerability was determined. Moreover, this study investigated whether the E–R relationships for efficacy and safety endpoints were different among patient subpopulations with different values of covariate.

Because of limited efficacy and safety data, particularly for the efficacy analyses (n = 38), it was challenging to estimate the effect of covariates of interest and establish a priori ones would be influential. However, estimating covariate effects is essential to adequately assess the E–R relationship under the presence of possible confounders. Therefore, all endpoints were modeled using logistic regression in a Bayesian framework using spike and slab priors to include and simultaneously estimate all covariate effects. This methodology implements a “full model” approach in the sense that no covariates are excluded on an algorithmic basis but does so using regularization, intentionally applying shrinkage to covariate effects that have negligible magnitudes to stabilize estimation overall, while applying minimal regularization to effects with non‐negligible magnitudes. In this way, a full covariate model approach was employed while still achieving stable estimation. This approach can be helpful to evaluate the E–R relationship in cases where the amount of data are limited but potential confounders need to be controlled, such as in the development of an orphan drug.

Among patient‐specific exposure metrics, unbound valemetostat AUCSS was chosen as the primary predictor for E–R analyses as unbound exposure was theoretically more relevant to drive efficacy and safety than total exposure, and AUCSS was expected to be more accurately estimated than other exposure metrics considering sampling points of the clinical trials.

Probably due to the more limited sample size used for efficacy endpoints, a steeper exposure–efficacy relationship was estimated for ORR by investigator assessment (22% greater odds of response) vs. by central assessment (8%), per 250 ng·h/mL increase of unbound valemetostat AUCSS. However, shallower covariate–response relationships were estimated in the model for ORR by investigator assessment and most covariate effects were well regularized.

For the exposure–safety analyses, attention was given to the J201 most common grade ≥3 TEAEs: PLT decreased (32%), anemia (32%), and ANC decreased (12%). 12

The effect of baseline Hb, ANC, and PLT on their corresponding TEAEs clearly have high prior plausibility and so were not regularized. The risk of laboratory‐value‐defined TEAEs varied substantially depending on the baseline laboratory values, even with exposure held constant as expected. Steep exposure–safety relationships were estimated for all endpoints, in particular for grade ≥3 PLT decreased, for which a 250 ng·h/mL increase in unbound valemetostat AUCSS from the reference value produced the highest increase of odds of TEAEs (103%).

For these models, the primary objective was the identification of an exposure range that would manifest only warranted risk, in particular for the dose reductions due to the TEAEs model, since maintenance of the initial dose following a TEAE plausibly represents the acceptance of warranted risks. According to the results, the probabilities of a dose reduction due to a TEAE were comparable among all tested subpopulations, with estimated rates below 10% (Figure S7F).

The ROPE approach employed in the present work has the following advantages: (1) the pre‐defined criteria for efficacy and safety allow for objective determination of the safe and efficacious dose; (2) the necessity for dose adjustment in subgroups that have different exposure ranges can be judged considering inter‐individual variability; and (3) once established, the ROPE may inform target exposure ranges for subsequent indications. However, interpretation should be made with caution due to the limited sample size causing wide 90% prediction intervals. The shallow exposure–efficacy relationships and desirable safety generated a ROPE extending beyond the range of observed exposures, indicating satisfactory efficacy and acceptable safety of valemetostat across the entire studied dose/exposure range. Under these conditions, any attempt to estimate the minimum exposure threshold for efficacy would necessarily involve extrapolation. Since the model‐based application beyond the data range might not be reliable, a modified ROPE with direct empirical support needed to be generated to provide a more confident range.

In summary, the novel approaches allowed us to assess the exposure–response relationships of valemetostat with limited data, remaining uncertainty beyond the available exposure ranges. However, a recommended dose of valemetostat 200 mg was shown to produce exposure ranges that provide favorable benefit–risk balance within the modified ROPE in the overall population and across subpopulations, supporting the use of a daily dose of 200 mg for the treatment of patients with ATLL.
