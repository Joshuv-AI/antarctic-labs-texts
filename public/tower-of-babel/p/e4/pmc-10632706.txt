Experimental study of a natural convection indirect solar dryer
Authors: Jaouad Ennissioui, El Mahjoub Benghoulam, Tarik El Rhafiki
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the effectiveness of an indirect solar dryer (ISD) specifically designed for the geographical and climatic conditions of Meknes (Morocco). The constructed ISD system incorporates a solar air collector (SAC) inclined at 34° to the ground, reaching a maximum outlet temperature of 58 °C. During the drying process, banana slices experienced a substantial reduction in mass, decreasing from 549.76 g to 138.41 g. The calculated mean efficiency of both the SAC and the dryer stood at 23.37 % and 18.8 %, respectively. In terms of moisture removal, ISD outperformed open solar drying (OSD), achieving a substantial moisture reduction of 74.83 %, whereas OSD yielded a reduction of 39.08 %. The process of drying resulted in a decrease in the moisture content (MC) of banana slices from an initial value of 3.5771 kg/kg (db) to a final value of MC varied depending on the tray used for drying, with values of 0.0397, 0.1292, 0.1745, and 0.2597 kg/kg (db) recorded for trays 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. By focusing on the development and evaluation of an ISD system optimized for the local conditions, this research contributes valuable insights into efficient and sustainable food drying.

1. Introduction

Drying food products corresponds reduction of the water content inside the product, down to values preventing the formation of bacteria, and thus allowing their conservation in ambient conditions [1,2]. But the use of fossil fuels for drying poses a major cost and environmental protection problem, hence by using renewable energies such as solar energy [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]] give a good alternative and sustainable solution for to this problem. Solar drying, utilizing solar heat, is considered the most efficient, sustainable, and environmentally friendly technology [10].

In solar drying, two primary methods utilizing the sun's rays are employed: solar drying conducted directly and solar drying conducted indirectly. Direct solar drying exposes the products directly to the sun, which can cause vitamin degradation and susceptibility to weather, insects, and dust. Open solar drying (OSD) is a traditional technique employed for preserving food. However, it has certain limitations, including low efficiency and direct exposure of the product to the sun, which can adversely affect product quality. In contrast, indirect solar drying provides a superior solution by allowing controlled drying processes that preserve the quality and nutritional content of the products [[11], [12], [13]]. Indirect solar dryer (ISD) is a conventional device [14]. It is essentially composed of a solar air collector (SAC) [15] its role is heating air fluid for removing moisture from product, and a drying chamber [16] where products were disposed [17].

In recent times, there have been notable advancements in the solar drying and food preservation. Many studies have been done for drying several products. In a study conducted by Gilago and Chandramohan [18], an assessment was carried out on the effectiveness of an ISD specifically engineered for dehydrating ivy gourd. The collector efficiency averages stood at 62.56 % for natural convection and 77.2 % for forced convection. Correspondingly, the average drying efficiency was found to be 6.62 % under natural convection and 7.8 % under forced convection. L. Hidalgo et al. [19] studied a self-sustained solar dryer that operates directly powered by a PV module for energy generation. The drying kinetics exhibited distinct stages, starting with a consistent rate phase followed by a declining rate period, observed under both operational conditions. Negligible color alteration was detected between dried and fresh green onions for the two operating scenarios, aligning with the objective of preserving the natural green color of the material. Pragnan Lad et al. [20] examined three configurations involving phase change material (PCM) integration within a solar dryer. The first configuration represented a traditional indirect dryer, the second featured a solar dryer modified with PCM integrated into the collector. The third showcased a solar dryer modified with PCM embedded inside the chamber. Upon analyzing the outcomes, the modified solar dryer with PCM integrated into the drying chamber demonstrated more efficient maintenance of the requisite thermal conditions for the selected crops in comparison to the other configurations. Pangavhane and Sawhney [21] developed an ISD operating in natural convection for drying grapes. They observed that using ISD method significantly reduced the time of drying by 43 % when it was compared to the conventional OSD technique. Furthermore, the study found that the OSD method required a longer drying period of 7 days, whereas the ISD method only took 4 days to achieve similar levels of moisture removal. This significant difference in drying duration further emphasizes the superiority of the ISD technique in terms of time efficiency. Ringeisen et al. [22] studied tomatoes by applying them to the solar drying process facilitated by a solar dryer. They aimed to compare tomatoes quality by studying the impact of drying on tomatoes and specifically focused on the changes in mass throughout the drying process. The findings of the study revealed that the final dried tomato slices exhibited no significant changes in quality when compared to their initial mass. This indicates that the drying was successful in preserving the quality of the tomatoes. H. Essalhi et al. [23] developed an ISD for drying pears by natural convection. Throughout the drying duration, the original mass of pears, initially measured at 0.9973 kg, underwent a significant reduction, ultimately reaching 0.1351 kg after 24 h of drying. This indicates that a substantial amount of moisture was successfully removed from the pears, resulting in a significant reduction in mass. The computed average value of thermal efficiency of the dryer was determined to be 11.11 %. César et al. [24] studied an indirect-direct passive solar dryer, that it was implemented, and evaluated for the drying of Solanum lycopersicum (tomato). The efficiency of the SAC ranged from 52.30 % to 55.45 Additionally, the study measured the temperature average value during drying. An observation was made that as solar radiation penetrated through a section of the cabinet, the average chamber temperature increased significantly. The maximum temperatures recorded reached up to 73.6 °C. The elevated temperatures within the chamber demonstrate the heating capabilities of the dryer, providing the essential heat required for dehydration of the tomatoes. Hegde et al. [25] designed and studied an ISD, and they compared the performance of a SAC with flat plate for two configurations of upper and lower flux for drying banana slices. Their findings revealed that the lower flux configuration yielded temperatures within the chamber that were approximately 2.5 °C higher than those achieved with the higher flux configuration, despite both configurations receiving the same amount of solar energy input. The lower-flux configuration was 38.21 % more efficient than the higher-flux configuration, which was 27.5 %. M. El-sebaey et al. [26] studied two indirect solar dryers, namely a chimney dryer and a fan dryer, were investigated for their performance in drying banana slices. The study revealed that the chimney dryer resulted in the lowest moisture content (MC) of banana slices at 10.59 %. This demonstrated a significant 81 % improvement in drying operation compared to the conventional OSD. The chimney dryer proved to be more efficient in removing moisture from the banana slices, leading to a superior final product. The average values of thermal efficiencies obtained through the utilization of a chimney dryer and a fan dryer were 14.45 % and 12.76 %, respectively. These values indicate the effectiveness of both dryers in converting solar energy into useable heat for the drying. The chimney dryer's collector achieved an average efficiency value of 34.14 %, while the fan dryer's collector had an average efficiency value of 29.54 %. The results obtained indicate that when combined the chimney dryer with SAC, it demonstrated the highest thermal efficiency for the overall drying system. R. Jain et al. [27] investigated on study of a conductive solar dryer. They used solar reflectors as closure covers to extend life of the lining material. Inside the dryer. Their findings revealed that the highest recorded temperature was 77.4 °C, 61.2 °C using solar reflectors, and 59.1 °C, 51.8 °C without employing solar reflectors during both no-load and full-load conditions, respectively. The drying efficiency was calculated and it was reached to be 59.4 % for the developed solar dryer when solar reflectors were utilized, significantly higher than the average drying efficiency of around 35 % typically seen in other general solar dryers. When the solar reflectors were not used, the drying efficiency was 50.09 %. Furthermore, the drying rate ranged from 1.525 to 0.005 g when the solar reflectors were employed. Without the use of reflectors, the drying rate ranged from 1.397 to 0.005 g. These findings demonstrate that the presence of solar reflectors resulted in more consistent and efficient drying rates. Y. D Kokate et al. [28] invested on study of performance of an ISD and it was compared to results obtained using OSD for drying onion and garlic products. The study reported a maximum temperature value of 47.6 °C recorded inside the dryer during the drying period. Over 24 h of drying, the MC of the onion and garlic products was reduced to 25 % and 60 %, respectively. In comparison, when subjected to OSD, the MC was found to be 67 % for onions and 63 % for garlic. They concluded that the utilization of indirect drying proved to be more efficient than using the conventional OSD and the product quality was preserved in contrast to direct exposure to the sunlight. M. Tagne et al. [29] conducted a study focused on assessing the effectiveness of a solar dryer specifically designed to dry cocoa beans. The dehydration procedure involved the use of both natural convection through the night and forced convection through the day. The researchers aimed to assess the drying properties and the dryer efficiency. The initial MC of the cocoa beans was found to be 1.2 kg/kg (db). Through the combined use of natural and forced convection, the drying took approximately 32 h to achieve a final MC of 0.15 kg/kg (db). This significant reduction in MC indicates the effectiveness of the dryer in extracting moisture from the cocoa beans. The humidity and temperature gradients within the dryer were sufficient to facilitate homogeneous drying in the layers of cocoa beans. The arrangement of the product inside the dryer allowed for a consistent drying process throughout the beans, ensuring uniform moisture removal. Jain and Tewari [30] have developed a novel design an ISD under natural convection and it incorporated a PCM thermal energy storage unit. During sunshine until midnight in June in the Jodhpur region, they observed that the temperature value within the chamber was 6 °C higher than the surrounding environment. By analyzing the economic performance of the dryer, they found a return on investment and a period of 0.65 in payback, indicating a positive return on the investment made in the dryer on the optimum cost of the design raw materials and the study found that the duration for cost recovery was 1.57 years depending on the price of selling the product. Bhavsar and Patel [31] investigated the operational efficiency of a solar dryer using both natural and forced convection mechanisms for dehydration fruit, specifically focusing on drying ginger. The aim was to analyze the loss of MC and loss of weight in ginger through the drying period. Their finding revealed that the initial value of MC presented in ginger product was 80 % with a corresponding weight of 4000 mg. Regarding the scenario of drying through natural convection, the mass of the ginger product decreased to 830 mg after the three-day drying period, and in comparison, during the same three-day drying period using forced convection, the mass of the ginger product decreased further to 620 mg. The forced convection drying method appeared to be more efficient in terms of achieving a higher weight loss within the same drying period.

Lately, there has been a heightened interest in the development and optimization of ISDs for various agricultural products. However, additional research is still warranted to explore the performance and potential of these systems, especially in specific geographical and climatic circumstances. This study aims to assess the effectiveness of the ISD for drying banana slices under natural convection tailored to the unique geographical and climatic conditions of the Meknes region (Morocco).

The study encompasses the measurement and analysis of crucial parameters, including solar radiation, temperature distribution, collector and dryer efficiencies, moisture content, and drying rate. Furthermore, a comparative analysis was conducted between OSD and ISD techniques to evaluate their respective effects on banana drying. The experimental results will provide valuable insights into the effectiveness and efficiency of the ISD for drying agricultural products in the Meknes region.

By employing an indirect solar drying approach, we aim to contribute to the advancement of sustainable and efficient drying methods, offering a viable solution for preserving food products while minimizing environmental impact. The subsequent sections of this paper provide a detailed description of the experimental setup, methodology, results, and comparative analysis between OSD and ISD techniques.

Conception and realization of the solar dryer

The experimental device was concepted and designed for preserving food, especially vegetables and fruits like grapes, bananas, grain, potatoes, tomatoes, onions, etc., and it was constructed from inexpensive and environmentally friendly materials to make it easily duplicated in rural areas.

The schematic ISD and its dimensions are represented in Fig. 1. It is consisted of two principal elements; a solar air collector (SAC) and a drying chamber.Fig. 1Schematic representation of ISD and its elements, dimensions are given in cm.Fig. 1

Schematic representation of ISD and its elements, dimensions are given in cm.

The main element of the dryer is the solar air collector (SAC) which is represented in Fig. 2-a and 2-b. It consists of an absorber that heats the air-fluid and a glass cover that creates a greenhouse effect. The confined air between the glass and the absorber helps to collect the maximum heat provided by the solar radiation, and it will transport its energy to the product in the drying chamber. The SAC is constructed using various materials. The absorber was constructed from iron and coated with a matte black finish to increase heat absorption; it has a thickness of 0.2 cm. The glass cover is 0.4 cm thick and covers an area of 0.57 m2. The rear insulation is achieved using a layer of 4 cm thick polystyrene covered by two wooden plates with a total area of 0.858 m2.Fig. 2Experimental device; (a), (b) Solar collector, (c) Drying chamber, (d) Chimneys, (e) Experimental setup.Fig. 2

Experimental device; (a), (b) Solar collector, (c) Drying chamber, (d) Chimneys, (e) Experimental setup.

The outlet of the SAC is connected to the inlet of the drying chamber (Fig. 2-c), which contains the food products intended for drying. The drying chamber is made of wood and has dimensions of 50 × 60 × 100 cm3. It is divided into four trays (1, 2, 3, and 4) separated by 20 cm. The trays are rectangular and made of iron mesh. The heat transfer fluid rises between the trays and exits through two PVC chimneys (Fig. 2-d), each measuring 10 cm in diameter and 30 cm in height.

The components of the system are securely joined using a fixing glue that binds the wood, polystyrene, absorber, and glass. The dryer is titled at an angle of 34°, which corresponds to the latitude of the Meknes site.

The actual ISD is represented in Fig. 2-e.

The air from outside first passes under the collector heated by the received solar radiation through the glass and by the greenhouse effect created in the part located between the glass surface and the absorber plate.

During this passage, the air will receive heat exchange obtained by convection and conduction transfers from the SAC, and therefore the air will enter the drying chamber with a high temperature than the inlet. As it passes through the drying chamber, it will transfer some of this heat to the products in the trays, causing a quantity of water in them to evaporate. After passing through all the trays, the dried air will exit through the chimneys positioned at the upper part of the drying chamber.

Results and discussion

Performance of the indirect solar dryer

This section presents the temperature distribution inside the indirect solar dryer, highlighting its sensitivity to variations in solar radiation. The temperature distribution plays a crucial role in understanding the overall performance of the dryer and its efficiency in utilizing solar energy. By closely examining the impact of changing solar radiation levels, we acquire valuable understanding of the system's dynamic behavior.

In Fig. 4, the solar radiation was measured over a period of 8 h during the drying process from 9:00 to 17:00. The measurements indicate that there is a significant fluctuation in the solar radiation levels during this period. At the start of the process, at 9:00 in the morning, the solar radiation was measured at its lowest value of 341 W/ m2. This low level of radiation can be attributed to the fact that the sun is still low on the horizon at this time of the day. As the day progressed, the solar radiation levels gradually increased, and by 13:00, the solar radiation had peaked at a maximum value of 635 W/ m2. This high level of radiation can be attributed to the fact that the sun is at its highest point in the sky at this time of the day, which leads to maximum exposure to the sun's rays. The average solar radiation measured during the day was 397.8 W/ m2. This average value takes into account the fluctuations in solar radiation values levels throughout the day, and provides a good representation of the overall solar radiation levels during the drying. The peak radiation value observed at 13:00 indicates the optimal conditions for drying during this period. However, it's crucial to highlight that diminished radiation levels in the morning and late afternoon may lead to slower drying rates during these times.Fig. 4Variation of solar radiation values versus day time (W/m2).Fig. 4

Variation of solar radiation values versus day time (W/m2).

Fig. 5 provides valuable insights into the temperature changes within the dryer through day time. Inside the dryer, the measurements show that the temperature varies depending on the location within the system. Specifically, the temperature was found to be higher at the SAC outlet and gradually decreased as the air flowed through trays 1, 2, 3, and 4. This pattern can be attributed to the fact that the air passes through each tray and transfers heat to the material being dried, resulting in a decrease in temperature as it flows through the trays. These results are contrary to what one might think, as OSD is often thought to be a quicker and more effective method of drying. However, the ISD technique utilizes indirect thermal solar energy to dry the product, providing a more controlled drying environment and offers a maximum gain in time of 4.2 h compared to the OSD technique. The use of indirect heating in the ISD technique prevents direct exposure of the product to sunlight. Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study examined the effectiveness of an ISD in drying banana slices through natural convection. The ISD maintained higher temperatures within the drying chamber, creating favorable conditions for moisture evaporation and leading to noteworthy decrease in the MC of the banana slices. The average solar collector efficiency and dryer efficiency were measured at 23.37 % and 18.8 %, respectively. The drying process led to a reduction in the MC from an initial value of 3.5771 kg/kg (db) to final values ranging from 0.0397 to 0.2597 kg/kg (db), depending on the tray location. The comparison with other studies in the literature also confirmed the improved performance of the ISD. Future research can focus on optimizing the design parameters of the ISD and exploring its application for drying other food products. Additionally, investigating the potential integration of energy storage technologies could enhance the efficiency and versatility of drying processes.

Overall, the study offers valuable insights into the capabilities of the ISD as a sustainable and efficient solution for food preservation. These findings contribute to the continual endeavors in developing environmentally friendly drying technologies.
