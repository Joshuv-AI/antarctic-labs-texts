Patient With Missed Right Ventricular Rupture From Blunt Trauma Remains Hemodynamically Stable for 3 Days
Authors: Mohammad Bilal Memon, Taylor Kirkman, Michael Wahl, Rami Akel, Ali Dahhan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac chamber rupture from blunt trauma is rare but can be fatal. Surprisingly, in some subsets of patients, it can be subtle and rather easily missed. Rapid recognition and management are essential. Percutaneous closure can be successful in iatrogenic chamber perforation (during pericardiocentesis) but possibly not in traumatic chamber rupture. (Level of Difficulty: Intermediate.)Key Words: blunt trauma, cardiac chamber rupture, cardiac injury, percutaneous closure, right ventricular perforation, right ventricular rupture, surgical repair

History of Presentation

A 71-year-old man was found unconscious as an unrestrained driver in a motor vehicle collision against a utility pole. He regained consciousness en route to the emergency department and endorsed mild anterior chest wall tenderness. His initial heart rate was 97 beats/min and blood pressure was 100/69 mm Hg, which improved with 500 mL of intravenous fluids. Glasgow Coma Scale was 15. Multiple abrasions were noted in the bilateral arms and face. He had garbled speech because of comminuted fracture of the right mandibular body. Electrocardiography showed atrial fibrillation with right bundle branch block. Serum alcohol level was elevated at 246 mg/dL. Hemoglobin was 12.3 mg/dL, and troponin I was elevated at 10.8 ng/mL.

Chest computed tomography (CT) with intravenous contrast showed fracture of the left clavicle, bilateral first ribs, and left second rib. It raised concerns for possible right ventricular (RV) rupture with pericardial hematoma (Video 1).

Past Medical History

The patient’s history included coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery 20 years earlier.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included cardiac tamponade physiology and non–ST-segment myocardial infarction.

Investigation

Emergent transthoracic echocardiography showed no pericardial effusion/hematoma or evidence of pericardial tamponade. Emergent transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) did not identify any evidence of RV rupture (Video 2). Left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction was 50%. The cardiothoracic surgery service suspected that the saphenous venous graft to the right coronary artery (SVG-RCA) might have been aneurysmal and ruptured into the extrapericardial space after trauma and then thrombosed, preventing active bleeding. Troponin I peaked at 12.9 ng/dL. Although hemoglobin dropped to 9.9 mg/dL, the patient remained hemodynamically stable over the weekend without any vasoactive drug support. Left heart catheterization showed normal LV diastolic pressure, which was not consistent with tamponade physiology from a chamber rupture. There was occlusion of the proximal SVG-RCA, but no evidence of active extravasation (Video 3).

Hemodynamic stability, along with troponin elevation (rise and fall consistent with myocardial infarction), and lack of “button sign” on SVG angiography all supported absence of cardiac injury and the possibility of recent traumatic SVG rupture, with thrombus formation preventing active bleeding. Therefore, no additional cardiac workup was deemed urgently needed for 48 hours after presentation.

Within the next 48 to 72 hours, the patient was transferred to our care, and careful review of CT results with the radiologist raised more concerns that the extravasation was, indeed, from the RV. Transthoracic echocardiography with ultrasound enhancing agent injection showed extravasation of contrast (Video 4). Emergent structural cardiac CT angiography, gated to the pulmonary artery, confirmed the RV rupture. The defect was irregular, measuring 3.3 × 6.7 cm, located in the RV outflow tract 2 cm below the pulmonic valve. The extravasation was contained within the mediastinum as a large pseudoaneurysm measuring 6.5 × 4.8 × 2.5 cm (Video 5, Figure 1).

A 3-dimensional rendering of the heart using computed tomography confirmed right ventricle rupture with a large defect (blue arrow) and a large pseudoaneurysm (purple arrow).

Management

We explored percutaneous vs surgical approaches. Given the size and location of defect, we decided to pursue surgical repair.

Intraoperative TEE showed a defect in the RV free wall with a pseudoaneurysm (Video 6). Dense adhesions were noted after opening the sternum, and the anatomy was not very clear. A hematoma was overlying the anterior aspect of the RV, which was dissected carefully, and then the RV and defect were identified (Figure 2). The defect was successfully closed primarily with multiple pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures. TEE confirmed successful repair.

Intraoperative Evaluation

Intraoperative evaluation revealed the defect that was detected on CT (green arrow). A drain is placed through it.

Postoperatively, the patient was in cardiogenic shock from RV failure and needed significant inotropic support. Surprisingly, his pulmonary pulse pressure started to rise within 24 hours, signifying early signs of improved RV contractility. Inotropes were weaned off by postoperative days 4 to 5. His hospital course was prolonged and complicated by liver and renal failure needing temporary hemodialysis. He was discharged to rehabilitation on postoperative day 30.

Discussion

Most cases of blunt trauma to the heart are with minimal morbidity and mortality. However, significant cardiac injury frequently results in mortality and should be suspected if there is severe blunt trauma and multiple rib fractures. Common mechanisms of trauma include motor vehicle collision (75%), pedestrian struck by an automobile (15%), and falls from height (10%).1 Cardiac injuries can include rupture of cardiac chambers (65%), cardiac laceration (25%), pericardial laceration (5%), hemopericardium (5%), valvular laceration (0.1%), coronary artery rupture (0.1%), and papillary muscle rupture (0.1%).2 Cardiac chambers that commonly rupture are the RV (20%), LV (15%), right atrium (10%), interventricular septum (10%), left atrium (5%), and interatrial septum (5%).2 Rupture can involve single or multiple cardiac chambers in 80% and 20% of cases, respectively.2 Patients with rupture of 2 or more cardiac chambers have very high mortality.

The majority of patients with significant cardiac injuries are either found dead at the scene or arrive with severe hemodynamic instability. Although some of those patients are deceivingly hemodynamically stable initially, this may change abruptly, and overall mortality can be as high as 90% to 100%.1 Therefore, early recognition is crucial, and emergent management can improve survival.1

Different imaging modalities (such as real-time 3-dimensional echocardiography and TEE) can be used.3,4 However, echocardiography has limited sensitivity in general screening unless a specific structure is of interest. Use of ultrasound enhancing agents (i.e., microbubbles) may improve sensitivity.5 Nonetheless, CT should be used in all trauma cases, and any abnormality should be thoroughly investigated.6

The RV wall defect from rupture in our case was large and not expected to be compatible with life. The extravasation from the RV is normally into the pericardial sac (resulting in cardiac tamponade) or into the intrathoracic cavity (resulting in hemorrhagic shock). Interestingly, the significant adhesions and scarring that developed in our patient’s thorax after his CABG 20 years earlier contained the bleeding and formed a pseudoaneurysm, which minimized blood loss. The pericardiectomy from CABG may have also prevented development of hemopericardium and tamponade. Fortunately, he remained hemodynamically stable for more than 72 hours until surgical repair was performed. However, intraoperatively, the blood was noted to be dissecting through the scarring tissue and reaching the pleura of the right lung, which supported the notion that conservative management of such injury would have only led to certain mortality.

Determining the appropriate repair approach, percutaneous vs surgical, can be very challenging. Successful percutaneous closure using different devices (such as the Amplatzer Vascular Plug II and Angio-Seal Vascular Closure) has been reported, but that was in selective cases where the pseudoaneurysm resulted from iatrogenic perforation of the RV during emergent pericardiocentesis.7,8 Defects after iatrogenic perforation are expected to be smaller with intact edges. Unfortunately, limited literature is available regarding percutaneous closure of RV rupture after blunt trauma and does not report success.9 Such a low success rate can also be expected in traumatic rupture given the larger size of defects, irregular shape of borders, and friability of tissue at edges.

Despite the high mortality rate, prompt surgical repair can improve survival.10 Atrial injuries were primarily oversewn with 2-0 or 3-0 sutures, and ventricular injuries were repaired with deep pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures.

Follow-Up

Remarkably, the patient continued to do well upon follow-up at 1.5 years.

Conclusions

Significant cardiac injury is common after severe blunt trauma, especially if multiple rib fractures are present. It causes high mortality; therefore, early recognition and emergent management are crucial, even in patients who appear hemodynamically stable. Single cardiac chamber (RV and LV) rupture is the most common injury. Echocardiography has limited sensitivity, which may improve with ultrasound enhancing agents. Nonetheless, CT remains the cornerstone in establishing diagnosis.

Percutaneous closure can be successful in iatrogenic perforation of the RV. However, it is challenging with limited success in traumatic RV rupture; therefore, emergent surgical repair should be considered. Cardiogenic shock can ensue after extensive RV repair; nevertheless, the RV can recover.

Special attention should be paid to the subset of patients who underwent pericardiectomy and/or intrathoracic surgeries (such as CABG). Intrathoracic scarring can help form a pseudoaneurysm and contain active extravasation. Hemopericardium may not develop after pericardiectomy; therefore, hemodynamic stability and the absence of tamponade physiology do not necessarily rule out cardiac chamber rupture.
