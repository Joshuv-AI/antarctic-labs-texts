NTRK fusion in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer treated with entrectinib: a case report
Authors: Shuichi Tatarano, Akihiko Mitsuke, Yoichi Osako, Takashi Sakaguchi, Satoru Inoguchi, Ryosuke Matsushita, Hirofumi Yoshino, Yasutoshi Yamada, Hideki Enokida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitors have demonstrated substantial efficacy in solid tumors harboring neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) gene fusions. Although NTRK fusions have been sporadically reported in prostate cancer, their clinical significance and the therapeutic efficacy of TRK inhibitors in this disease remain unclear. We report a case of NTRK fusion-positive metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer treated with the TRK inhibitor entrectinib.Case presentationA 68-year-old Japanese male with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) experienced disease progression after androgen deprivation therapy, enzalutamide, abiraterone, and docetaxel. Because sufficient tumor tissue was unavailable for comprehensive genomic profiling, circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analysis using FoundationOne® Liquid was performed, and a PBX1-NTRK1 fusion was identified. Based on this result, treatment with the TRK inhibitor, entrectinib, at a daily dose of 600 mg was initiated. Despite two months of therapy, prostate-specific antigen levels increased from 22.7 ng/mL to 70.2 ng/mL, and bone scintigraphy demonstrated radiographic disease progression with new metastatic lesions. Although the patient reported transient improvement in bone pain, entrectinib was ultimately ineffective. No severe adverse events were observed.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first reported case of NTRK fusion-positive mCRPC treated with entrectinib. This case highlights the potential limitations of cfDNA-based detection of NTRK fusions in prostate cancer and suggests that NTRK fusions detected in cfDNA without tissue validation may not reliably predict clinical benefit from TRK inhibitors in mCRPC. Further studies are required to clarify the biological relevance of NTRK fusions and optimal patient selection for TRK inhibitors in prostate cancer.

Background

Prostate cancer harbors genetic abnormalities, including AR, TP53, PTEN, MYC, PIK3CA, and BRCA1/2 [1]. Precision oncology-based therapeutics are available in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), including poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors for homologous recombination repair deficiency. In addition, pembrolizumab is also administrated in cases with high microsatellite instability and high tumor mutation burden [2]. TMPRSS2-ERG fusion is one of the most prevalent gene fusions in prostate cancer. Overexpression of the ERG protein can affect the expression of other genes, potentially promoting cancer development. However, there is no targeted therapy that inhibits the fusion product or downstream signaling pathway in TMPRSS2-ERG fusion abnormalities. On the other hand, the neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) gene fusion is relatively rare in prostate cancer compared to different types of solid tumors. The tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitors, such as larotrectinib and entrectinib, have shown promising results in clinical trials for NTRK fusion-positive solid tumors. However, the clinical efficacy of TRK inhibitors in prostate cancer harboring NTRK fusions remains unclear because of the small number of cases. We report a case of NTRK fusion-positive mCRPC treated with entrectinib, highlighting the clinical challenges of TRK-targeted therapy in prostate cancer.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old Japanese male visited his previous clinic with a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) value of 190.00 ng/mL four years ago. He underwent a prostate biopsy and was diagnosed with adenocarcinoma with a Gleason score of 4 + 5 in all the specimens. Computed tomography (CT) and bone scintigraphy showed left external iliac lymph node and pelvic bone metastases. He was diagnosed with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, and androgen deprivation treatment and bicalutamide were started. PSA nadir of 0.385 ng/mL was obtained at nine months of treatment. However, PSA failure was confirmed at 15 months. Based on the diagnosis of CRPC, estramustine phosphate sodium hydrate, ethinylestradiol, enzalutamide, and abiraterone were introduced sequentially. When he was referred to our hospital with left pelvic pain, his PSA level had increased to 20.94 ng/mL. CT showed no lymph node metastasis, but bone scintigraphy revealed multiple bone metastases, including bilateral involvement of the pelvic bones and ribs (Figs. 1A, B, 2). The left pelvic bone biopsy was performed because it is the most progressive site among all the metastases. Pathological examination revealed pelvic bone metastasis of prostate cancer. Palliative radiotherapy delivered 60 Gy to the bilateral pelvic bone metastases, including the prostate. One month after radiation, the PSA level was 16.6 ng/mL, and docetaxel treatment was initiated. Although comprehensive genomic profiling using FoundationOne® was attempted on a bone biopsy specimen, the tumor sample was insufficient for analysis. Therefore, a liquid biopsy-based assay (FoundationOne® Liquid) was performed. Genomic analysis of the liquid biopsy revealed a PBX1-NTRK1 fusion. Based on these results, entrectinib was initiated after confirming radiological progression in the right pelvic bone metastasis and experiencing pelvic bone pain following six courses of docetaxel. Entrectinib was initiated at a daily dose of 600 mg and maintained at this dose for two months without dose reduction. Treatment response was evaluated according to the Prostate Cancer Clinical Trials Working Group 3 (PCWG3) criteria, including PSA progression and radiographic progression assessed by bone scintigraphy. Although the PSA level increased from 22.7 ng/mL to 40.8 ng/mL after one month, the patient experienced improvement in pelvic bone pain. The patient was receiving NSAIDs for bone pain, and the dosage remained unchanged during entrectinib therapy. The patient's performance status also remained stable during this period. However, after two months of entrectinib treatment, the PSA level increased to 70.2 ng/mL. In addition, bone scintigraphy showed disease progression with new metastases in the ribs and vertebrae. In this case, the only adverse event associated with entrectinib was dysgeusia (CTCAE grade 1), with no severe adverse effects. Discussion

Gene fusions play an important role in the molecular landscape of prostate cancer, with TMPRSS2-ERG being the most common fusion. However, no targeted therapy directly inhibits the fusion product or its downstream signaling pathway. In contrast, NTRK gene fusions generate constitutively active TRK fusion proteins that act as oncogenic drivers in several solid tumors and are actionable with selective TRK inhibitors.

NTRK fusions are exceedingly rare in prostate cancer, with reported frequencies ranging from 0% to 0.22% [3, 4]. High frequencies of NTRK gene fusion have been observed in tumors such as infantile fibrosarcoma (90.56%), secretory breast cancer (92.87%), and secretory salivary gland carcinoma (79.68%), whereas prostate cancer is not considered an NTRK-driven malignancy [3]. Only a limited number of prostate cancer cases harboring NTRK fusions have been described, and clinical outcomes following TRK inhibitor therapy have not been well documented [5, 6].

TRK inhibitors such as larotrectinib and entrectinib have demonstrated substantial efficacy across multiple NTRK fusion-positive solid tumors [7]. In a pooled analysis of three phase 1/2 clinical trials of larotrectinib, the objective response rate was 79%, although only one prostate cancer case was included, and its treatment outcome was not reported [8]. Entrectinib has also shown favorable efficacy and a manageable safety profile in patients with NTRK fusion-positive solid tumors, leading to its regulatory approval; however, no prostate cancer cases were enrolled in the pivotal trials [9, 10].

In the present case, NTRK1 is fused with pre-B-cell leukemia homeobox 1 (PBX1). PBX1 encodes a transcription factor involved in prostate cancer biology, and previous studies have shown that PBX1 overexpression promotes prostate cancer cell proliferation and contributes to therapeutic resistance [11, 12]. Because sufficient tumor tissue was unavailable, confirmation of TRK protein expression by immunohistochemistry or ribonucleic acid (RNA)-based fusion analysis could not be performed. Therefore, the biological functionality of the PBX1-NTRK1 fusion detected by cfDNA remains uncertain. This limitation significantly restricts the interpretation of the therapeutic response to entrectinib in this case. Several biological explanations may account for the lack of response to entrectinib in this case. First, the PBX1-NTRK1 fusion detected by cfDNA may represent a non-functional passenger alteration rather than an oncogenic driver, particularly because tissue-based validation was not available. Second, the tumor-derived cfDNA fraction may have been low, potentially affecting the reliability of fusion detection in this case. Third, mCRPC is typically driven by dominant AR signaling pathways, which may limit the biological impact of rare kinase fusions such as NTRK. These possibilities highlight the importance of cautious interpretation of rare gene fusions detected by cfDNA alone when considering targeted therapies in prostate cancer.

Although the patient experienced transient improvement in bone pain during entrectinib treatment, objective disease progression was evident based on PSA progression and radiographic findings on bone scintigraphy. This discrepancy suggests that entrectinib may have exerted limited or non-durable biological activity without achieving meaningful disease control. In addition, the relatively short duration of entrectinib exposure (two months) represents another limitation, although treatment discontinuation was driven by clear biochemical and radiographic progression. This case highlights the importance of tissue-based confirmation when interpreting rare gene fusions detected by liquid biopsy in prostate cancer.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first case report of NTRK fusion in mCRPC treated with entrectinib. Entrectinib did not demonstrate clinical efficacy in this case, highlighting the limitations of cfDNA-based NTRK fusion detection in mCRPC. However, further studies are needed to clarify the biological relevance of TRK inhibitors and to define appropriate patient selection for TRK inhibitor therapy in mCRPC. Our findings emphasize that tissue-based validation may be essential when interpreting rare NTRK fusions detected by liquid biopsy in prostate cancer.
