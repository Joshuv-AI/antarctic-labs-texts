Desidustat’s cardioprotective mechanisms in heart failure: a network pharmacology, molecular docking and dynamics approach
Authors: Mohammed Fayaz Sadiqbasha, Arjun Gunasekaran, Jeevak Chander Thirulokachandar, Sarumathy Sundararajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart failure (HF) remains a significant global health issue. Current guideline-directed therapies often focus on neurohormonal modulation and may not fully address the metabolic, oxidative, and inflammatory components central to the disease. Desidustat, a hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, has shown benefits in chronic kidney disease anemia, alongside early evidence of cardiovascular effects in preclinical models. Its complete mechanistic fingerprint in HF is not fully elucidated. This study aimed to generate a robust mechanistic hypothesis by identifying the molecular targets and pathways through which Desidustat may exert cardioprotective effects in HF. An integrated computational approach comprising network pharmacology, molecular docking, and advanced molecular dynamics (MD) simulations was employed. Drug-disease target intersection identified 348 shared targets, which were subjected to topological analysis to pinpoint central hub genes (HSP90AA1, STAT3, ESR1). Molecular docking determined binding affinities to these hubs. The structural stability and binding thermodynamics of the strongest complex (Desidustat–HSP90AA1) were rigorously evaluated using extended MD simulations (up to 200-ns) and Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) calculations. Functional enrichment analysis linked the 348 shared targets to crucial processes in HF, including oxidative stress response, angiogenesis, and the regulation of apoptosis. Topological metrics strongly prioritized HSP90AA1, STAT3, and ESR1 as key regulatory nodes, all of which exhibited favorable binding affinities in docking. The MD simulation confirmed the exceptional structural stability of the Desidustat–HSP90AA1 complex, characterized by consistent low RMSD and a strong binding free energy ΔGbind (Total) of –75.10 ± 5.03 kcal/mol, calculated via MM-GBSA. This hypothesis-generating in silico study suggests that Desidustat acts via a polypharmacological mechanism, potentially modulating key metabolic and inflammatory pathways through central targets like HSP90AA1. These findings provide a mechanistic basis that warrants further experimental and preclinical investigation to evaluate Desidustat’s non-hemodynamic cardioprotective potential as a complementary strategy in HF treatment.Keywords: HIF-PHD antagonist, Heart failure, Network pharmacology, Desidustat, Cardio protectionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a global urgent healthcare issue: the number of people affected with heart failure in the world has reached 56.5 million in the year 2021 (95 percent UI: 49.7–63.7 million), and in the age-standardized prevalence increased since 1990: it was 647.9 per 100 000 in 1990 and 682.7 per 100 000 in 20211. This is a diverse syndrome with high morbidity and mortality,1 year mortality is 23.6% with acute HF, and 5 years mortality is still present regardless of the newer therapies2. Pathophysiological features are the cumulative ventricular dysfunction, inappropriate cardiac remodeling, and aberrant metabolism with oxidative stress, inflammation, and aberrant cellular energy metabolism3.

The field of expertise that has recorded tremendous gains in prognostics is the field of guideline-directed medical therapy. The paradigm-HF study (n = 8399) showed ARNI (angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors) reduced the risk of cardiovascular mortality or HF hospitalization by 20% in comparison with enalapril4. SGLT2i has proven to be a powerful agent: The DAPA-HF (n = 4,744) and EMPEROR-Reduced (n = 3,730) studies demonstrated equal 26% and 25% reductions in primary endpoints. Meta-analysis of these landmark studies indicated an all-cause mortality risk reduction of 13% and a 26% risk reduction in cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization with SGLT2i treatment5,6. However, present-day reviews show that recent pharmacological therapies primarily use neurohormonal reprogramming points, failing to comprehensively use molecular roots, such as metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory cascade reactions3,7–10.

Hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors (HIF-PHIs) are an exciting new modality as they have shown clinical efficacy in chronic kidney disease (CKD) induced anemia11. The small-molecule HIF-PHI Desidustat was utilized to treat anemia in CKD patients in both dialysis-dependent and non-dialysis cohorts. Functional in vitro experiments show a potent stabilization effect of both isoforms of HIF-1alpha (HIF-1alpha, EC50 = 32.6 μM constant and HIF-2alpha, EC50 = 22.1 μM constant) and a maximum therapeutical trough (Emax = 119% with both isoforms), thus inducing endogenous erythropoietin synthesis and erythropoiesis11. Rodent studies also confirm a cardiovascular profile: in animal models of myocardial infarction, HIF-PHD1 knockout mice showed increased angiogenesis and capillary density as well as increased release of VEGF, and increased myocardial perfusion. In addition, HIF mediated signalling affects central signalling involved in HF progression, viz., redox sensitive gene expression, inflammatory response HF pathways by regulation via NF-κB, and regulation of metabolic stability via up regulation of glycolytic enzyme expression12.

Though there is already an emerging body of evidence, a number of gaps in mechanistic knowledge still exist. Although there exists evidence of HIF-mediated cardioprotection during ischemic preconditioning13, there is no firm clinical evidence that Desidustat is connected to cardiovascular outcomes. HIF-PHI class cardiovascular safety debates have grown more sophisticated;

The HIF-PHI class is of high concern to address a cardiovascular safety issue. Significantly, the PRO2TECT trial showed that vadadustat vis-à-vis darbepoetin alfa was associated with a much higher risk of MACE (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.01–1.36) that raised some concern about vadadustat’s cardiovascular profile14. Comparatively, daprodustat versus darbepoetin alfa pooled analyses demonstrate no meaningful difference in MACE events out to 52 weeks [OR 0.95 (95% CI 0.82–1.11); p = 0.55] indicating an absence of cardiovascular effect. Taken together, these results emphasize that cardiovascular effects of HIF-PHIs can be drug specific and not drug category-wide, and taking their individual differences into account is critical in regulatory and clinical practice15.

Since desidustat has HIF-mediated effects on a multitude of molecular targets, its efficacy and non-adverse mechanisms have to be comprehensively quantified at the systems level in a complex biological scenario of HF pathophysiology. The main research gap of Desidustat is that there is a lack of comprehensive, mechanism-based analysis of its possible cardiovascular therapeutic potential. Whereas the erythropoietic mechanism in the clinical development of the drug is well documented, the alleged cardioprotective effects have been progressively explored. Molecular targets, signaling pathways, and protein–protein interactions networks in which Desidustat might alter the process of heart failure have not been explored according to system. This is a severe source of knowledge deficit as it is a substantial barrier to rational development of cardiovascular drugs and limits translation of its potentially useful therapeutic actions into the clinical setting. The main objective of this research is to employ network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics tool to predict the therapeutic efficacy of Desidustat on cardioprotection in heart failure by performing an in-depth molecular target prediction, pathway analysis.

Methodology

Study design overview

This study employed a comprehensive integrative network pharmacology and molecular docking strategy to elucidate the potential therapeutic mechanisms of Desidustat in heart failure (HF). Initially, putative Desidustat-associated targets and HF-related genes were systematically retrieved from multiple high-confidence databases, followed by the identification of overlapping drug–disease targets representing the core therapeutic space. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was then constructed to delineate the molecular interplay among these targets, with hub genes identified through topological analysis as central regulators within the network. To gain functional insights, Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and pathway enrichment analyses were performed, highlighting key biological processes and signaling cascades potentially modulated by Desidustat in the context of HF. Finally, molecular docking simulations were conducted to validate the binding affinities and interaction modes between Desidustat and the most critical hub proteins, thereby providing structural evidence in support of the network-based predictions.

Target identification

Potential molecular targets of Desidustat were systematically retrieved by integrating multiple complementary resources to ensure comprehensive coverage and reliability. SwissTargetPrediction http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/ was utilized for probability-based prediction of small molecule–protein interactions16, while PharmMapper http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/ provided pharmacophore mapping to identify potential binding targets17. PubChem https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ was queried to extract experimentally validated chemical–biological activity associations, and PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ was mined for literature-reported targets to capture prior evidence on Desidustat-related mechanisms. In addition, the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD) http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/ was employed to incorporate curated therapeutic target records18. Together, these databases enabled the establishment of a robust and diverse target repertoire, serving as the foundation for subsequent network pharmacology analyses.

In order to minimize the loss of targets and reliability of the results, disease-related genes were thoroughly extracted across various established databases to enhance adequate coverage of the heart failures associated genes. Particularly, curated therapeutic targets were investigated in Therapeutic Target Database (TTD), gene-disease association was queried with DisGeNET https://disgenet.com/, genetically verified disease-associated genes were downloaded with Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) https://www.omim.org/, and the standardized information on proteins was searched in UniProtKB, whereas gene-focused multi-omics data was retrieved with the GeneCards https://www.genecards.org/, These resources were searched by the keyword of the “Heart Failure” and “Congestive Heart Failure”. All retrieved gene sets were subsequently merged, followed by rigorous deduplication suitable for downstream network pharmacology analysis.

Identification of common targets

The curated target sets of Desidustat and HF were systematically compared to identify their intersection using the Interactive Venn platform https://www.interactivenn.net/. The overlapping genes obtained from this analysis were regarded as the putative therapeutic nodes19, representing potential mediators through which Desidustat may exert its pharmacological effects in HF.

Protein–protein interaction network construction

The common drug and disease targets obtained were uploaded in the STRING database (https://string-db.org/, version 12.0) to generate a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. In order to fulfil the conditions of reliable and comprehensive connectivity, the analysis was limited to Homo sapiens. A medium confidence threshold (0.4) was selected to capture a broad range of potential interactions, while interactions with a score < 0.4 were excluded to reduce false-positive associations. The resulting network was downloaded in TSV format and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.9.1).

Hub gene identification

CytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape was used to rank gene as per topological features by primarily Degree centrality. Hub genes were ranked into three level: top 100, top 30, top 10. These were regarded as important candidates for further examination.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to help describe in a systematic manner the functional role of the overlapping targets in the context of Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC), and Molecular Functions (MF). Analysis was done with DAVID bioinformatics resources https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/ background reference was homo sapiens. Data filtering was performed using a significance set at p < 0.05 to be statistical rigorous and an enrichment score of 3 and higher to be strict and indicate only the most relevant and high enrichment GO terms that can be considered biologically critical. The results of the Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis were visualized using the online tool available at Bioinformatics.cn http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/, which enabled the generation of high-quality bar plots and bubble plots.

Pathway enrichment analysis

These top 10 hub genes with highest centrality and enrichment relevance were identified in the PPI network and then pathway enrichment analysis of these hub genes were conducted via Metascape https://metascape.org/, which is integrated KEGG, WikiPathways, and GO Biological Process datasets. The enrichment parameters were defined as follows: minimum overlap of three genes, p-value cutoff of 0.01, and q-value cutoff of 0.05 with Benjamini–Hochberg correction to control for false discovery rates. The top KEGG pathways highlighting key molecular processes potentially involved in the cardioprotective mechanism of Desidustat. Where KEGG pathway maps were reproduced www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html, formal permission was obtained from the Kanehisa Laboratories20,21.

Preparation of ligand and proteins

The three-dimensional structure of Desidustat was retrieved from the PubChem database (Compound ID: CID 75,593,290) in SDF format. The structure was imported into Chem3D (PerkinElmer Informatics, version 20.0) for energy minimization and geometry optimization. Energy minimization was performed using the MMFF94 force field, which is well-established for accurately predicting molecular geometries and conformational energies of drug-like organic molecules. The optimization was conducted using a two-step approach: initial minimization with the steepest descent algorithm (100 iterations) followed by conjugate gradient refinement until convergence. The convergence criterion was set at a root mean square (RMS) gradient of 0.01 kcal/mol·Å with a maximum of 1000 iterations. A dielectric constant of 1.0 was employed to simulate a vacuum environment during minimization. The energy-minimized structure was visually inspected to ensure the absence of steric clashes or unrealistic geometries, then saved in PDB format.

The crystal structures of hub proteins were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank https://www.rcsb.org/ (resolution ≤ 3.0 Å) and prepared using PyMOL (v2.5.0). All water molecules, ions, and non-essential ligands were removed. Protonation states of ionizable residues were assigned at pH 7.4 using PROPKA 3.0, with histidine tautomers (HID/HIE/HIP) determined based on local hydrogen bonding environments and predicted pKa values. Aspartate and glutamate residues were deprotonated, while lysine and arginine were protonated. Hydrogen atoms were subsequently added using PyMOL’s “Add Hydrogens” function. Missing residues in short loops (< 5 residues) were modeled using PyMOL’s “Build” command with iterative refinement. The structures were energy-minimized using the MMFF94s force field with harmonic restraints on Cα atoms (500 kcal/mol/Å2) until convergence (gradient < 0.05 kcal/mol/Å). Final structures were validated using MolProbity, ensuring > 95% of residues in favored Ramachandran regions.

Molecular docking and visualization

The docking experiments were performed on CB-Dock http://cao.labshare.cn/cb-dock/ with blind docking mode (where cavity detection is done automatically) with the AutoDock vina scoring function that was used to sort the docked poses according to the affinity of binding (kcal/mol). To validate the reliability of the blind docking results, an additional site-specific (locked) docking approach was employed. The predicted binding pockets were independently identified and downloaded from the https://proteins.plus/ website. These pocket files were visualized in PyMOL, where pocket “isosurfaces P0surf” were generated, and the spatial boundaries of each binding site were determined using the “get_extent” function. The centroid coordinates of each pocket were calculated using the formula: (max + min) / 2, The calculated centroid coordinates were subsequently used to define the docking grid for locked docking simulations performed with Glide. Subsequent docking visualization and interaction analysis was carried out using PyMOL (v2.5) and Discovery Studio Visualizer (BIOVIA) software compounds whereby hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and π-π stacking interactions were determined and labeled to offer important insights into the molecular recognition of Desidustat with the major hub proteins.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The conformational stability of the top-ranked protein–ligand complexes was evaluated using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations performed with the Desmond module of the Schrödinger Suite (2022–1) for simulation durations of 100 ns and 200 ns. The systems were prepared using the OPLS3e force field to accurately describe protein–ligand interactions. Each complex was solvated in an orthorhombic TIP3P explicit water box with a minimum buffer distance of 10 Å from the solute. System neutrality was achieved by the addition of appropriate counter ions (Na⁺ or Cl⁻), and a physiological ionic strength was maintained by incorporating 0.15 M NaCl. Energy minimization and equilibration were carried out using the Desmond default multi-stage protocol as follows: (1) Minimization—2,000 steps of steepest descent energy minimization with solute-heavy-atom restraints (50 kcal/mol/Å2); (2) NVT equilibration—12 ps at 10 K with Brownian thermostat (tau = 0.1 ps) and solute restraints; (3) NPT equilibration—12 ps at 10 K using Nosé–Hoover thermostat (tau = 1.0 ps) and Martyna-Tobias-Klein (MTK) barostat (tau = 2.0 ps, reference pressure = 1.013 bar); (4) NPT equilibration—24 ps at 300 K with same thermostat/barostat settings and reduced solute restraints (5 kcal/mol/Å2); (5) NPT equilibration—24 ps at 300 K with no restraints, allowing full relaxation of the system. Production MD was then conducted under NPT conditions at 300 K and 1.013 bar using the Nosé–Hoover thermostat and MTK barostat with the RESPA integrator and a 2.0 fs time step. Post-simulation trajectory analyses were performed using the Simulation Interaction Diagram (SID) tool in Maestro, including calculations of root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and protein–ligand contact profiles. These analyses provided comprehensive insights into the structural stability of the complexes, residue-level flexibility, and dynamic conformational behavior during the simulation period.

Post dynamics simulation study

To investigate the conformational dynamics of the protein–ligand systems, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Free Energy Landscape (FEL), and Dynamic Cross-Correlation Matrix (DCCM) analyses were performed utilizing the Bio3D and MDAnalysis toolkits. MM-GBSA calculations were performed using an implicit solvation approach based on the Generalized Born (GB) model to estimate polar solvation contributions, while nonpolar solvation energy was calculated using a solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) model. The OPLS3e force field was applied for molecular mechanics energy evaluation, with solute and solvent dielectric constants set to 1.0 and 80.0, respectively. Binding free energies were computed from equilibrated MD snapshots using the Prime MM-GBSA module (Schrödinger Suite 2022–1). The total ΔGbind was decomposed into the following Prime MM-GBSA output components: van der Waals (ΔGbind_vdW), Coulomb electrostatic (ΔGbind_Coulomb), generalized Born polar solvation (ΔGbind_SolvGB), lipophilic/nonpolar solvation (ΔGbind_Lipo), hydrogen bond correction (ΔGbind_HBond), and covalent strain (ΔGbind_Covalent). The ΔGbind_SolvGB term represents the polar desolvation penalty, which partially offsets the favorable electrostatic contribution. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to extract dominant eigenvectors and describe global motions within the trajectory. The initial two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were employed to create 2D and 3D scatter graphs illustrating the significant conformational shifts throughout the 200 ns simulation. The Free Energy Landscape (FEL) was obtained by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) projections utilizing Gibbs free energy profiles to pinpoint energetically advantageous metastable conformations and local minima. The DCCM analysis was used to assess correlated (positive) and anti-correlated (negative) motions among residues, offering insights into collective motions and inter-domain communication affected by ligand binding. These investigations jointly clarified the structural flexibility, stability, and dynamic coupling of the protein–ligand systems.

Target prediction

Desidustat target prediction was carried out using multiple databases. A total of 100 potential targets were identified from SwissTargetPrediction, 301 from PharmMapper, 1 from PubChem, 10 from PubMed references, and 1 from the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD). After removing duplicate entries, 413 unique targets were initially identified, which were further refined to 376 core targets of Desidustat.

Heart Failure pathogenic genes were collected from diverse and reliable databases. Specifically, 95 targets were retrieved from TTD, 448 from DisGeNET, 481 from OMIM, 599 from UniProt, and 16,991 from GeneCards. After deduplication, a total of 17,230 unique genes associated with Heart Failure were retained.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To assess the structural stability and binding dynamics of the Desidustat–HSP90AA1 complex, we performed two separate unbiased molecular dynamics simulations of 100 ns and 200 ns duration, respectively. Both simulations were initiated independently from the same energy-minimized and equilibrated starting structure (the docked complex after system preparation), using identical force field (OPLS3e) and solvation conditions (TIP3P explicit water, 0.15 M NaCl). The 100 ns simulation served as an initial stability assessment, while the 200 ns simulation provided extended conformational sampling to ensure thermodynamic convergence and to support the post-dynamics MM-GBSA analysis. These are not independent replicates with varied initial velocities; rather, they represent progressively extended simulations designed to assess stability at different timescales. The structural drift was quantified by calculating the RMSD of the protein backbone (Cα atoms) and the ligand heavy atoms (after fitting on the protein reference), as shown in Fig. 9. In both simulations, the protein Cα RMSD equilibrated rapidly, stabilizing at 2.5 ± 0.3 Å and 2.8 ± 0.4 Å for the 100 ns and 200 ns runs, respectively. The ligand RMSD profiles demonstrated a stable binding conformation. In the 100 ns simulation, the ligand RMSD equilibrated around 2.5 Å. In the extended 200 ns simulation (Fig. 9B), the ligand showed a slight conformational adjustment in the first 50 ns, stabilizing at an RMSD of approximately 3.0–3.5 Å for the remainder of the trajectory. Crucially, no dissociation event was observed, and the RMSD values remained within a range consistent with a stable protein–ligand complex.

RMSD analysis of Desidustat and HSP90AA1 in 100 ns and 200 ns.

Residue-wise root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis (Fig. 10) revealed that ligand-interacting residues exhibited comparatively lower fluctuations, indicating stable protein–ligand interactions throughout the simulation. Key binding residues including PHE22, ASN51, ASP54, LYS58, TYR61, and TRP162 showed restrained backbone and side-chain mobility. In contrast, terminal and loop regions displayed elevated RMSF values, consistent with their intrinsic flexibility.

RMSF analysis of Desidustat and HSP90AA1 at 200 ns.

The protein–ligand interaction profile was further evaluated by analyzing the total number of contacts and residue-specific contact persistence throughout the 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation (Fig. 11). The total contact plot demonstrates that the ligand maintains a stable interaction network across the entire simulation, with the number of contacts fluctuating primarily between 3 and 8 contacts, and occasional peaks exceeding 10 contacts, particularly during the early equilibration phase. Importantly, no abrupt loss of interactions was observed, indicating absence of ligand dissociation and suggesting sustained binding stability within the active site.

Protein–Ligand contact timeline.

These observations are further supported by the protein–ligand interaction map, which reveals a well-organized hydrogen-bonding and water-mediated interaction network stabilizing the ligand within the active site. The ligand forms direct hydrogen bonds (Fig. 12) with ASN51, GLY135, ASP54, and LYS58, consistent with their high contact persistence observed in the timeline analysis. Notably, LYS58 and ASP54 participate in multiple interactions involving both direct and water-bridged hydrogen bonds, underscoring their dominant role in electrostatic and polar stabilization of the complex. Several conserved water molecules contribute to bridging interactions between ligand heteroatoms and surrounding residues, particularly involving ASN51 and GLY135, indicating that water-mediated contacts significantly complement direct protein–ligand interactions.

2D protein–ligand interaction analysis.

Hydrophobic and π-related interactions further reinforce ligand anchoring, with PHE138 and ALA111 engaging the aromatic and nonpolar regions of the ligand, thereby stabilizing its orientation within the binding cavity. Residues such as LEU107 and MET98 are positioned in proximity to the ligand scaffold, providing additional hydrophobic support despite exhibiting lower contact persistence over time. The coexistence of stable direct hydrogen bonds, dynamic water bridges, and hydrophobic contacts suggests a multimodal interaction mechanism, allowing the ligand to maintain a favorable binding pose while accommodating local flexibility within the pocket. Collectively, this interaction pattern corroborates the residue-level contact timeline results and explains the sustained binding stability observed throughout the molecular dynamics simulation.

Post dynamics analysis

To further quantify the binding affinity and thermodynamic stability of the Desidustat–HSP90AA1 complex, we performed Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) calculations on the equilibrated 200 ns molecular dynamics trajectory. The computed total binding free energy (ΔGbind = − 75.10 ± 5.03 kcal/mol) indicates a robust and highly favorable thermodynamic interaction between Desidustat and HSP90AA1, as detailed in Table 5. The mean MM-GBSA binding free energy values and their standard deviations, computed across equilibrated trajectory snapshots from the 200 ns simulation, are summarized in Table 5 and visualized in Fig. 13. Figure 13 presents a bar chart of each energy component (ΔGbind_vdW, ΔGbind_Coulomb, ΔGbind_SolvGB, ΔGbind_Lipo, ΔGbind_HBond, ΔGbind_Covalent, and total ΔGbind), with error bars representing the standard deviation across sampled frames. The relatively small standard deviations (≤ 5.11 kcal/mol for all components) indicate that the binding free energy remained consistent and stable throughout the equilibrated simulation period, underscoring the thermodynamic robustness of the Desidustat–HSP90AA1 complex. Decomposition of the binding energy revealed that van der Waals interactions and electrostatic contributions were the primary stabilizing forces, complemented by substantial lipophilic and moderate hydrogen-bonding terms.

Principal component analysis and free energy landscape analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to characterize the dominant conformational motions of the protein–ligand complex during the 200 ns simulation (Fig. 14).

Each data point represents a trajectory frame projected onto the space of the first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3), Data points are colored by simulation time (blue = early; red = late), illustrating the temporal evolution of the conformational sampling. The convergence of data points into restricted regions at later time points indicates conformational stabilization of the complex.

The first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) captured the dominant collective motions of the system, collectively accounting for approximately 83.8% of the total conformational variance (PC1: 52.3%, PC2: 21.7%, PC3: 9.8%). The conformational space projected onto the 3D PCA scatter plot (Fig. 14) uses PC1, PC2, and PC3 as the three orthogonal axes. The time-colored distribution indicates an initial equilibration phase followed by convergence into restricted conformational regions, suggesting stabilization of the complex without large-scale structural drift. To further assess the thermodynamic preference of the sampled conformational states, a 3D free energy landscape (FEL) was constructed using PC1 and PC2 as the two reaction coordinates (Fig. 15), which together account for 74.0% of the total variance and represent the most dominant conformational transitions. The FEL reveals pronounced low-energy basins with free energy minima of 15–17 kJ/mol, separated by moderate energy barriers (Fig. 15). These minima correspond to the most densely populated PCA regions, demonstrating that the complex predominantly occupies energetically favorable conformations throughout the simulation. The presence of multiple shallow basins indicates limited conformational flexibility while preserving overall structural stability.

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis

To characterize the inter-residue communication and collective dynamics of the Desidustat–HSP90AA1 complex during the 200 ns simulation, a Dynamic Cross-Correlation Matrix (DCCM) was computed and visualized as a hierarchically clustered heatmap (Fig. 16). Pairwise cross-correlation coefficients ranged from − 1.0 (perfectly anti-correlated, blue) to + 1.0 (perfectly correlated, red), with near-zero values (white) representing independent motion. The DCCM revealed three dominant correlated clusters that are clearly delineated by the dendrogram. Cluster I, comprising primarily N-terminal and mid-chain residues including GLU:18, LEU:64, THR:99, LEU:103, PHE:170, THR:171, SER:165, ILE:187, ASN:83, THR:88, ILE:214, and TYR:216, displayed strong intra-cluster positive correlations (r > 0.5), indicative of concerted domain motion. Cluster II encompassed residues in the ligand-binding region including ASN:51, ALA:55, VAL:92, ILE:131, GLY:132, ARG:182, THR:184, GLN:159, ALA:161, LYS:224, ASP:127, LEU:122, SER:113, and PHE:213, many of which correspond to key binding site residues identified in the MD interaction analysis (ASN51, ASP54, LYS58, PHE138, ALA111, GLY135). These residues exhibited moderate-to-strong positive correlations within the cluster, reflecting coordinated binding-site motion that is consistent with sustained ligand anchoring throughout the trajectory. Cluster III, comprising C-terminal residues GLU:205, ARG:201, GLN:194, LEU:143, SER:39, ASP:66, LEU:29, and THR:36, formed a compact, internally correlated block with strong red signal along the sub-diagonal, suggesting a rigid C-terminal lobe.

Hierarchically clustered Dynamic Cross-Correlation Matrix (DCCM) of the Desidustat–HSP90AA1 complex derived from the 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation. Each cell represents the cross-correlation coefficient between the Cα atom motions of residue pairs, ranging from + 1.0 (perfectly correlated, red) to − 1.0 (perfectly anti-correlated, blue). Residues are ordered by hierarchical clustering (dendrograms shown on both axes), revealing three principal dynamic clusters. Binding site residues (ASN51, ALA55, VAL92, ILE131, GLY132, LYS224, ASP127, LEU122, SER113, PHE213) in Cluster II exhibit internally correlated motion, while pronounced anti-correlations (blue) between Cluster I and Cluster III reflect domain-level breathing dynamics of HSP90AA1 upon Desidustat binding.

Notably, pronounced negative cross-correlations (blue regions, r < − 0.3) were observed between Cluster I and Cluster III, indicating anti-correlated breathing motions between the N-terminal and C-terminal domains—a pattern consistent with a “clamp-like” dynamics mechanism previously reported for HSP90 family proteins. The binding-site residues of Cluster II showed minimal anti-correlation with either flanking cluster, suggesting that the ligand-occupied pocket acts as a dynamic buffer that decouples the opposing domain motions. Collectively, the DCCM analysis demonstrates that Desidustat binding induces a well-defined, structurally organized pattern of correlated and anti-correlated motions within HSP90AA1, supporting the notion of stable, non-disruptive engagement with the chaperone active site over the full 200 ns simulation period.

Discussion

The interactomes of Desidustat and heart failure related pathways analysis screens were overlapped to create a dataset of 348 shared proteins; the HSP90AA1, STAT3, and ESR1 proteins stand out as molecular hubs. The finding that HSP90AA1 was the most strongly bound target agrees with known literature that reveals HSP90AA1 as an essential element in cardiac protection22. Proteins in the HSP90 family act as molecular chaperones that are required to maintain the integrity of cardiomyocytes in stress situations. Empirically, HSP90 modulation can reduce cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis by regulating the RIP1-RIP3-MLKL necroptosis signaling, in addition to maintaining cardioprotective Akt-signaling in doxorubicin-induced heart failure models23,24. In addition, extracellular HSP90 also regulates TGF-1 signaling, which can regulate directly the collagen synthesis in cardiac fibroblasts and myocardial remodeling25. The extended molecular dynamics simulations (100 ns and 200 ns) provided nuanced insights into the dynamic behavior of the Desidustat-HSP90AA1 complex. While the protein backbone RMSD rapidly equilibrated and remained stable within 2.5–3.0 Å throughout both simulations, the ligand RMSD exhibited an early increase after 20–30 ns, reflecting a conformational repositioning of Desidustat within the binding pocket rather than complete dissociation from the protein. This interpretation is strongly supported by residue-wise RMSF and protein–ligand contact analyses, which demonstrated sustained interactions between Desidustat and key binding site residues, including ASN51, ASP54, LYS58, TYR61, GLY135, ALA111, PHE138, and TYR139, across the entire 200 ns trajectory. These residues displayed restrained fluctuations, indicating a stable interaction environment despite local ligand rearrangements. Persistent hydrogen bonds, water-bridged interactions, and hydrophobic contacts collectively maintained ligand anchoring, highlighting a multimodal stabilization mechanism within the HSP90AA1 pocket.

Importantly, post-dynamics MM-GBSA analysis revealed a highly favorable binding free energy (ΔG_bind –75.10 ± 5.03 kcal/mol), dominated by van der Waals and electrostatic contributions, confirming strong thermodynamic stability of the complex over the equilibrated trajectory. Complementary PCA and free energy landscape analyses further demonstrated convergence toward confined low-energy basins, indicating that the Desidustat-HSP90AA1 complex predominantly occupies energetically favorable and dynamically stable conformations without large-scale structural perturbations. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that Desidustat can stably engage and functionally modulate HSP90AA1, a central regulatory hub in heart failure–associated signaling networks. This systems-level molecular evidence provides a strong biological rationale linking Desidustat’s mechanism of action to broader cardioprotective pathways, including regulation of inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress responses, angiogenesis, and metabolic adaptation, thereby suggesting therapeutic potential beyond its established erythropoietic effects. Further corroborating these observations, the DCCM analysis revealed a well-organized network of correlated and anti-correlated residue motions within the Desidustat–HSP90AA1 complex, wherein binding-site residues of the central dynamic cluster exhibited internally coherent motion while pronounced domain-level anti-correlations between the N-terminal and C-terminal lobes of HSP90AA1 highlight a “clamp-like” allosteric communication mechanism that is preserved—rather than disrupted—upon stable Desidustat engagement, suggesting that the ligand does not perturb the native chaperone dynamics but instead integrates into an intrinsically organized inter-domain communication network.

STAT3 emerged as the most central hub gene within our network in accordance with its known, significant cardioprotective mediating action26,27. As such, STAT3 has dual action as it not only acts as a transcription factor through gene regulation but also has non-genomic effects including mitochondria28, where it regulates oxidative phosphorylation, calcium regulation and oxidative phosphorylation and dynamics of mitochondrial permeability transition pores. JAK2/STAT3 signaling can contribute to cardioprotection (BCL2‑regulated Apoptotic Process in Myocardial Ischemia‑reperfusion Injury29, including through pro-apoptotic inhibition and survival signaling cascade activation. In paradigms of ischemic preconditioning and postconditioning, activation of STAT3 can be essential in attenuating apoptosis of cardiomyocytes and limiting infarct size26,30.

The target ESR1 has especially promising medicinal resides. The cardioprotective responses via the ESR1 involve several mechanisms, i.e., vasodilation, the inhibition of apoptosis of cardiac cells, and the promotion of neovascularization31,32. Clinical data prove ESR1 gene polymorphism to determine the predisposition to coronary artery diseases, with low activity of ER a correlate to a elevated risk to cardiovascular problems33. Strong binding capacity (-8.3 kcal/mol) of Desidustat to ESR1 indicates the possibility of altering estrogen-dependent cardioprotective mechanisms34,35, which may be of particular interest in terms of cardiovascular failure disparities between communities of women before and after menopause.

The roles of angiogenic signaling pathways36, activation of VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling (Y.37) and urotensin II-mediated signaling38, are consistent with known mechanisms of HIF-PHI cardioprotection. Prolyl hydroxylase inhibition further HIF-1α to be able to induce pro angiogenic factors, such as VEGF, not only to drive coronary angiogenesis but also to increase myocardial oxygen delivery, vital in the failing heart39.

Heart failure is marked by the up production of reactive oxygen species, mitochondrial dynamic, and weakening of antioxidant defense components. Treatment of HIF-PHI has been proved to reduce oxidative stress with various mechanisms, which include increased expression of antioxidant enzyme and mitochondrial activity. The key roles of the identified hub genes, in addition to STAT3 and HSP90AA1 during the cellular stress response27,40, and protection on mitochondrial state supports the therapeutic rationale of HIF-PHI treatment in heart failure41,42.

Apoptosis is a factor that positively contributes to heart-failure pathogenesis43. Our networked analysis shows overrepresentation of apoptotic regulatory pathways, namely, necroptosis and caspase-mediated apoptosis, with key role of BCL2, CASP3, and AKT129. These findings indicate that Desidustat can mediate the cardiomyocyte survival pathway by balancing the pro- and anti-apoptotic signaling pathway (X.44), which supports the earlier findings that activation of HIF-1α leads to a decrease in cardiomyocyte apoptosis and enhanced post-ischemia survival (J.45).

Mechanistic hypothesis

Anemia and iron deficiency are common comorbidities of heart failure and have a prevalence of more than 50%; they demonstrate an independent relationship to lowering functional status and a greater risk of hospitalization46. Pathophysiologically, systemic inflammation during heart failure causes hepcidin elevation, which, in turn, induces impairment of intestinal iron absorption and iron entrapment of macrophages and hepatocytes, reducing the amount of iron available in the serum to be transferred to the bone marrow47. As a result of this, patients may have under functional iron deficit where standard oral or IV iron preparations no longer work and can instead lead to iron toxicity overload48,49.

Desidustat demonstrates a new mechanism of action combating this pathophysiological cascade by inhibiting the hepcidin expression in a HIF-α-dependent mechanism50, promoting an increase in erythropoietin transcription and stimulates downstream regulators of iron homeostasis. This process restores dietary iron uptake and increases the production of more stored iron in macrophages and hepatocytes, increasing the iron bioavailability significantly needed in erythropoiesis51. Since metabolic reprogramming, oxidative stress, and angiogenesis improve concomitantly in response to Desidustat that mitigates oxidative stress, the clinical expansion of such benefits past hemoglobin correction are posited11.

Literature support

Contemporary guideline-directed medical therapy that comprises angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), SGLT2 inhibitors, beta-blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, has significantly reduced mortality in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction 52,53,54). Nevertheless, significant heart-failure mortality is still in place, as 5-year survival rates show that there is still a lot of room to grow. Network topology indicates a multi-target, multi-pathway profile in which Desidustat is well placed to complement current therapies by targeting novel, unrelated molecular pathways. The metabolic reprogramming ability of HIF-PHIs, together with angiogenic and cytoprotective effects is an innovative approach with particular treatment of the underlying pathophysiological processes in heart failure.

Desidustat safety profile is promising as compared with other HIF-PHIs. Despite failure of vadadustat to show non-inferiority in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in the PRO2TECT trials55, and increased risk of thrombotic side effects associated with Roxadustat (Q.56), Desidustat clinical trials with more than 1500 participants had demonstrated therapeutic efficacy without relevant off-target cardiovascular or thrombotic adverse events, supporting the potential use of Desidustat in cardiovascular medicine11.

Limitations & future directions

However, it is important to note that these findings are subject to a number of limitations, including: 1) While this study utilized both 100 ns and 200 ns simulations and duplicate runs to ensure convergence, a standard triplicate simulation protocol could further statistically validate these conformational observations. 2) The in-silico nature of network construction requires verification through experimental and clinical studies; 3) Transcriptional networks are fixed snapshots that cannot observe changes over time in disease progression; and 4) The clinical studies do not include true random control groups, which limits comparability. These predicted therapeutic mechanisms require validation through in vitro and in vivo experimental models to establish clinical relevance.

Conclusion

This integrative network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics study reveals that Desidustat exerts potential cardioprotective effects in heart failure through multi-target modulation involving HSP90AA1, STAT3, and ESR1. The strong and stable binding of Desidustat to HSP90AA1 supports its role in maintaining cardiomyocyte integrity and attenuating apoptosis, while STAT3 and ESR1 contribute to mitochondrial regulation, anti-inflammatory responses, and estrogen-mediated signaling. Functional enrichment further highlights pathways related to oxidative stress reduction, angiogenesis, and necroptosis inhibition. Collectively, these findings suggest that Desidustat may provide pleiotropic cardiovascular benefits beyond erythropoiesis, offering a mechanistically grounded therapeutic avenue to complement current heart failure treatments.
