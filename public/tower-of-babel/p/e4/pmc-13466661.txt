Robot-assisted vesical neck h-shaped flap plasty for vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis: a case report
Authors: Xingyuan Xiao, Kuibiao Wang, Qiuxuan Yu, Hang Yin, Mengxin Lu, Xincheng Gao, Bing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS) is a challenging complication, occurring in 2%–7% of patients following radical prostatectomy. Endoscopic treatment fails in some patients, severely impairing their quality of life. Open reconstructive surgery is technically demanding with a long learning curve. Thus, to address a complication known as vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis following radical prostatectomy, we propose a new robot-assisted vesical neck H-shaped plasty.Case DescriptionA 57-year-old patient with prostate cancer developed vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (with a stenosis segment length of 2 cm) 4 months after laparoscopic radical prostatectomy. Following short-term recurrence of the condition after three transurethral incisions for VUAS, the patient received robot-assisted vesical neck H-shaped plasty for posterior urethral reconstruction. The surgical technique primarily involves constructing an H-shaped bladder flap, tubularizing it to reconstruct a funnel-shaped posterior urethra, performing a tension-free anastomosis between the vesical neck and the posterior urethra, and constructing a retrocurvature structure of the urethra. Treatment success was defined by both anatomical and functional criteria, unobstructed passage of a 17 Fr cystoscope or a urinary flow rate >15 mL/s. The patient in this study achieved adequate early urinary patency and no related complications.ConclusionsBased on short-term follow-up, robot-assisted vesical neck H-shaped flap plasty is feasible for vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis, but long-term follow-up is needed for verification.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We report the first novel surgical technique using an H‑shaped flap for posterior urethral reconstruction to address vesicourethral anastomotic stricture after radical prostatectomy.

What is known and what is new?

• Vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis poses significant challenges for conventional surgical reconstruction because of its deep location, high tension, and prior endoscopic procedures treatment.

• This report introduced a novel approach robot-assisted vesical neck H-shaped plasty for vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Robot-assisted vesical neck H-shaped flap plasty may be a safe and effective surgical method for the treatment of vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis.

• This technique still needs to be further validated in larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up results.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignant neoplasms among aged men, and its incidence rate has been steadily increasing recently (1). Radical prostatectomy (RP) stands as the primary therapeutic option for localized PCa, but it may cause a series of complications after the operation. Among these, bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) is a challenging mid-to-long-term postoperative complications, including bulbar urethral stenosis resulting from radiation and vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS) (2). With the increasing proficiency of surgical techniques and the widespread application of the Da Vinci robotic system, the incidence of VUAS after RP currently ranges from 2% to 7% (3-7). Currently, the main treatment methods for VUAS are endoscopic therapies, such as urethral balloon dilation, direct vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU), and DVIU combined with mitomycin or steroids. However, the success rate of these therapies varies greatly (40–90%) (5,7), and they are accompanied by a relatively high risk of recurrence. The recurrence rates at 1 year and 5 years after surgery can reach 34% and 42% respectively (8). In this context, urethral reconstructive surgeries, including open surgeries (via transabdominal, transperineal, or combined transabdominal-transperineal approaches) and robot-assisted surgeries [including in situ vesicourethral anastomosis, vesical neck Y-V plasty, and buccal mucosal grafting (BMG)] (2,9,10), have emerged as important alternative options for patients who have failed endoscopic treatment. However, such reconstructive procedures are technically demanding and associated with a relatively high incidence of postoperative complications. Current surgical patency rates range from 50% to 100%, with an average patency rate of approximately 80%, and the rate of de novo urinary incontinence is 17% (11). We report a case of robot-assisted laparoscopic H-shaped bladder neck flap plasty for VUAS with satisfactory short-term follow-up outcomes, which may provide a novel surgical strategy for the clinical management of this condition. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://acr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/acr-2026-0084/rc).

Case presentation

The patient, a 57-year-old man, was admitted to Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University in November 2024 with a chief complaint of “15 months after radical prostatectomy, intermittent dysuria for over 11 months, and aggravation for 1 month”. Specifically, the patient’s detailed medical history was as follows: in August 2023, he underwent laparoscopic radical prostatectomy for localized prostate cancer. In January 2024, he developed progressive dysuria and was treated with transurethral incisions for VUAS. In May 2024, he suffered from recurrent urinary retention and thus underwent a second transurethral resection for the same condition. The stenosis relapsed in August 2024, followed by a third transurethral incision. In October 2024, the patient presented with recurrent dysuria and was referred to our hospital after receiving six sessions of intermittent urethral catheterization at another hospital. Cystoscopy revealed a pinhead-like stenosis in the prostatic portion of the urethra (Figure 1A), and MRI revealed edema formation at the posterior urethra (Figure 1B). The stricture does not involve the membranous urethra. Prior to indwelling catheterization, the patient required three incontinence pads per 24 hours. He had no family history of relevant diseases. He was diagnosed with VUAS. The patient was severely dependent on an indwelling urinary catheter with impaired quality of life, so urethroplasty was planned. Preoperatively, suprapubic cystostomy was performed. After 20 days of urethral rest, the patient remained unable to void spontaneously and declined to undergo urethrography. So he underwent transabdominal robot-assisted H-shaped flap plasty of the bladder neck, with the operative duration of 295 minutes. All procedures performed in this case were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Surgical procedure

The patient was placed in the Trendelenburg position and operated on with the Da Vinci IV Surgical System. An 8 mm incision was made at the upper margin of the umbilicus, and an 8 mm camera trocar was inserted. Subsequently, an 8 mm incision was created at the lateral border of the rectus abdominis muscle on both sides at the umbilical level, with a robotic operating trocar placed through each incision respectively. Thereafter, a 12 mm and 5mm incision were made at the midclavicular line on both sides at the position forming an isosceles triangle with the camera port and operating ports, and an auxiliary trocar was inserted through each incision (Figure 2).

Image of robotic port placement.

Details of the surgical procedure are shown in Figure 3. Utilizing the Da Vinci IV robotic surgical system, the pelvic peritoneum was incised, and the anterior wall of the bladder was dissected free to the prostatic segment of the posterior urethra (Figure 3A,3B). A transverse incision was made on the anterior bladder wall, approximately 1cm above the bladder neck. The mucosa of the proximal bladder neck appeared relatively intact, while significant scar stricture was noted in the distal bladder neck and posterior urethra. Both ureteral orifices were identified, and a ureteral stent was placed bilaterally through the trocars under direct vision, extending from the renal pelvis to the bladder. Stent placement was smooth with unobstructed drainage, and no injury to the ureteral orifices was confirmed. The strictured segment was longitudinally incised from the bladder neck to the urethra until normal urethral mucosa was reached. Partial mucosa of the bladder neck and posterior urethra appeared pale with luminal rigidity. A transverse incision was made at the normal mucosa of the distal urethra. The incisions on the anterior bladder wall, vesical neck and posterior urethra were examined and confirmed to be in an H shape (Figure 3C,3D). The cicatricial tissue of the stenotic segment was circumferentially excised, and an F26 catheter was transurethrally inserted to the vesical neck, showing unobstructed passage without resistance. Continuous mucosal-to-mucosal interrupted sutures were performed with 5-0 absorbable sutures to restore continuity between the posterior urethra and bladder neck, forming a tubular structure that constitutes the posterior urethra (Figure 3E,3F). The anterior bladder wall was closed with continuous sutures using 4-0 barbed suture (Figure 3G,3H). Approximately 200 mL of sterile normal saline was injected through the catheter, and no bleeding or urine leakage was observed at the anastomosis site (Figure 3I,3J). A drainage tube was placed in the anterior bladder wall through the auxiliary trocar and securely fixed. The pelvic peritoneum was closed with continuous sutures using 3-0 barbed suture.

(A,B) Exposure of the anterior bladder wall and posterior urethra, followed by creation of an H-shaped incision; (C,D) appearance of the H-shaped incision; (E,F) anastomosis of the tubular bladder flap with the posterior urethra; (G,H) continuous suture of the anterior bladder wall; (I,J) overall view after completion of suturing.

Follow-up

The urethral catheter was removed from the patient 2 weeks after surgery. Cystoscopy revealed that the vesicourethral anastomosis was unobstructed (Figure 4A). At the two-month postoperative follow-up, uroflowmetry results were as follows: maximum flow rate (Qmax) 14.6 mL/s, voided volume 174.8 mL, and post-void residual urine volume 0 mL.

Images of follow-up examination. (A) A follow-up examination at 2 weeks postoperatively showed unobstructed posterior urethra; (B) a follow-up examination at 3 months postoperatively demonstrated unobstructed posterior urethra without cicatricial hyperplasia.

At the three-month follow-up, cystoscopy demonstrated that the vesicourethral anastomosis was unobstructed (Figure 4B). At the four-month follow-up, uroflowmetry revealed the following parameters: Qmax 17.0 mL/s, voided volume 167.6 mL, and post-void residual urine volume still 0 mL.

In the short term following the operation, the patient’s urethra was unobstructed, allowing for independent urination. It is recommended that the patient undergo follow-up examinations every three months during the first two years postoperatively, every six months starting in the third year, and annually after five years.

Discussion

Risk factors for VUAS include advanced PCa, large prostate volume, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, nerve-sparing status during surgery, anastomotic fistula, postoperative radiotherapy, and the surgeon’s operative experience (12). Magnetic resonance urography can identify the location, length, and severity of the stricture, cystoscopy or retrograde urethrography, as well as excretion cystourethrography, are the gold standards for diagnosing whether there is VUAS. Symptomatic VUAS patients are mainly characterized by dysuria, recurrent urinary tract infection, or stress urinary incontinence (SUI). The patient had undergone repeated urethral dilatation and transurethral incisions for VUAS without improvement, and his dysuria worsened. The stricture length of this patient was approximately 2 cm, with no history of radiotherapy.

Urethral reconstructive surgery is required when patients have undergone unsuccessful endoscopic treatment or have complete occlusion. In such cases, orthotopic vesicourethral anastomosis can be performed via transabdominal, transperineal, or combined transabdominal-transperineal approaches. However, owing to the stricture is situated deep within the pubic symphysis and in proximity to critical structures like the external urethral sphincter, cavernous nerves, and rectum, the complexity of the reconstruction surgery increases, leading to a heightened risk of postoperative complications (6). Despite the reported 87% success rate of TPRA in the literature, postoperative urinary incontinence rates have been documented to reach 100% (13), usually necessitating a secondary surgery for the insertion of an artificial urethral sphincter. Transabdominal redo anastomosis can preserve the bulbous urethra but often requires concomitant pubectomy, which increases the incidence of SUI (14). Robotic surgical systems provide magnified, three-dimensional visualization and articulated instrument movement, features commonly reported in pelvic reconstructive procedures. Compared with open reconstruction, robot-assisted reconstruction avoids perineal dissection and is associated with a lower incidence of postoperative urinary incontinence (15,16). Accordingly, robot-assisted reconstruction has been increasingly adopted for urethral reconstruction in patients with VUAS.

A multicenter retrospective study of robotic bladder neck reconstruction showed that at a median follow-up of 12 months, 80% of the patients were unobstructed postoperatively, but at a 30-day follow-up, 30 patients (59%) developed new or aggravated stress urinary incontinence (17). Shakir et al. reported the latest advances in robotic technology for the treatment of VUAS. Their cohort included 15 cases of in situ vesicourethral anastomosis and 17 cases of bladder neck Y-V plasty. At a median follow-up of 12 months, 24 patients (75%) showed unobstructed status post-surgery, and 26 patients (81%) were able to void through the urethra (18). Lee et al. introduced a novel technique of robot-assisted transvesical bladder neck reconstruction (RTV-BNR) for VUAS. The approach avoids dissection of the posterior bladder neck and reduces the risk of rectal injury. In their study group, 11 patients with short-segment VUAS (<2 cm) were included. After a median follow-up of 22 months, 10 patients (91%) exhibited unobstructed postoperative condition (19). Robot-assisted laparoscopic bladder neck YV plasty has become an effective surgical method for the treatment of bladder neck contracture (BNC) after benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) surgery, yet it is associated with high postoperative recurrence rates and numerous complications in the management of VUAS (20).

Based on the above-mentioned robot-assisted surgery, this study innovatively designed and implemented the formation of the bladder neck H-shaped plasty. Compared with traditional surgical methods, robot-assisted reconstruction surgeries, H-shaped plasty adopted in this study, offer several advantages. First, it utilizes a larger bladder flap, which alleviates tension at the anastomosis site, thereby facilitating a tension-free anastomosis and diminishing the risk of anastomotic fistula formation. Second, the increased length of the reconstructed urethra and the reconstitution of the pubo-urethral posterior curvature contribute to enhance patients’ ability to control urination independently. Third, by increasing the diameter of the reconstructed urethral lumen, the procedure may reduce the incidence of restenosis. Fourth, this surgical approach completely excises scar tissue, making it also suitable for the treatment of occlusive. The innovation in the suture method is a major feature that distinguishes this bladder neck H flap plasty from previous robot-assisted reconstruction surgeries. Based on the fact that the postoperative conditions of the patients in this study achieved adequate early urinary patency and no related complications, it can be inferred that bladder neck H-shaped plasty can indeed reduce the risk of anastomotic fistula and restenosis, promote the healing and recovery of body tissues, and show strong development potential in reducing the postoperative recurrence rate.

Conclusions

Based on short-term follow-up, robot-assisted vesical neck H-shaped flap plasty provides a feasible reconstructive approach for patients with VUAS. However, this technique still needs to be further validated in larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up results.
