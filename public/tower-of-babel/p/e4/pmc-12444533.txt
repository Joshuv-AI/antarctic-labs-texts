Polymer-Based Microstructured Photonic Membrane for Passive Heating Textiles
Authors: Mohamed Boutghatin, Salim Assaf, Pascal Tilmant, Parastesh Pirasteh-Charrier, Bahram Djafari-rouhani, Petr Janíček, Petr Němec, Joël Charrier, Vincent Thomy, Yan Pennec, Michèle Carette
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The development of textiles for personal thermal management is one of the most promising technological solutions to reduce the energy consumption in heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems. These technologies offer a potentially low-cost solution that can help to limit greenhouse gas emissions. In this paper, we propose the design and fabrication of a polyimide (PI)-based microstructured photonic membrane (MSPM) for personal passive heating textile. Using a photolithographic process, the membrane is drilled with a triangular array of holes. We showed theoretically and experimentally that the MSPM can decrease the transmission of mid-infrared (MIR) radiation emitted by the human body. The thermoregulator effect of the MSPM is demonstrated by using a thermal camera and a thermocouple. This fabric can efficiently warm the human body, enabling lower room temperature while still maintaining personal comfort. Moreover, the microholes enable air permeability and promote water-wicking. The performance of this membrane provides a new opportunity to deploy thermal management textiles for everyday use and targets one of the major forms of energy consumption.

Introduction

The building sector is the dominant part of the total energy consumption worldwide. In the European Union, more than 37% of this energy is attributed to temperature control through Heating, Ventilation and Air Conditioning (HVAC) systems. Consequently, numerous efforts are being made to reduce this huge consumption and develop a novel approach to control human body temperature and keep people in thermal comfort.

The concept of “personal thermal management” using a new generation of textiles is emerging as an excellent strategy to manage the temperature around the human body (more precisely in the area between the skin and the textile). − At a normal body temperature of 34 °C, our skin emits infrared (IR) radiation with a maximum at the wavelength of 9.5 μm, and this thermal radiation contributes to more than 50% of the total heat loss from the body. Therefore, the ability to control IR radiation has become a strategy in adapting and designing radiative thermo-regulating textiles.

Since their discovery, photonic crystals have attracted intense interest as a solution for the control of thermal radiation. For instance, a number of research studies have been focusing on the use of photonic structures for wave modulation in the mid-infrared (MIR) and the development of thermal management designs for daytime and solar cell radiative cooling. −

Recently, smart textiles based on photonic structures for advanced personal radiative thermal management have drawn more attention. In this context, it appears that one major application remains the development of structures dedicated to cooling and heating the human body. ,− To date, a few periodic structures have been proposed to achieve personal thermoregulation, the majority of which are made of periodically arranged fibers. For cooling textiles, Tong et al. proposed structured synthetic polyethylene fibers sufficiently small in order to maximize IR transparency via weak Rayleigh scattering. This same structure also allows one to obtain visible opaqueness via strong Mie scattering with an intrinsically low infrared absorption. Zhang et al. have experimentally demonstrated a dynamic design for dual thermal functionality by using bimorph fibers coated with carbon nanotubes. They succeeded in controlling the IR emissivity in reaction to the relative humidity of the underlying skin. Recently, Abebe et al. have studied theoretically a dynamic structure constituted of metallic fibers arranged in a periodic geometry. The dynamic aspect in this approach makes it possible to achieve the cooling and heating functions by adjustment of IR transmission according to the external thermal conditions. Unlike the majority of work in the field of personal temperature management, our work has focused for several years on polymer-based photonic membrane structures for the purpose of passive heating, which remains a major economic and social problem in parts of Europe (maintaining the microclimate at around 34 °C). Our strategy of developing structured polymer membranes is original compared with the state of the art, which proposes the use of periodic-based structures on textile fibers. Our choice is justified based on two trigger points: large-scale replication of structured membranes can be easily envisaged from an industrial point of view, using either screen-printing or roll-to-roll techniques. In the long term, the use of active polymers (responding to stimuli such as temperature) will enable dynamic modulation of the thermal properties of the membrane, which will be able to adapt to its environment. In this perspective, in a previous study, we showed theoretically that photonic crystal structuration of a synthetic polymer made of benzocyclobutene (BCB) was able to decrease the transmission and increase reflection in the infrared domain by varying only the geometrical parameters, i.e., without any metallic embedding.

In this work, we are pushing this strategy one step further by designing and fabricating a microstructured photonic membrane (MSPM) of polyimide (PI) by the photolithography process for passive personal thermoregulation in indoor areas. Both simulation and characterization of these photonic membranes have been carried out for their optical (in the mid-infrared range) and thermal properties. We show that MSPM can increase the microclimate temperature, leading to a reduction of the ambient set point temperature. Unlike previous work using metal components to provide heating functionality, , our approach aims to demonstrate that it is possible to increase the microclimate temperature simply by developing metal-free polymer engineering. Our work shows that a judicious choice of geometric parameters makes it possible to obtain a photonic grating that can control the behavior of electromagnetic waves in the mid-infrared and therefore control the thermal environment close to the membrane. The fact that no metal is added and periodic structuring can be achieved using other industrial techniques (such as roll-to-roll) will make it easier to integrate this solution into textile processes.

MSPM Fabrication Process

To produce patterned polymer films, various micro machining processes (such as laser drilling, wet/dry etching, holographic/soft lithography, etc.) have been proposed. − However, it remains a challenge to create ordered microholes with small distances between the adjacent holes. PI is an excellent polymer for the preparation of membranes because of its outstanding heat resistance and good mechanical strength, as well as its chemical resistance to many solvents. Moreover, PI is a high refractive index polymer in the infrared range, offering significant contrast with the air hole. In our case, we have used the HD 4110 series of products (from HD MicroSystems), which is a photosensitive PI precursor and a negative resist. Several methods have demonstrated their effectiveness in structuring PI films, including ion etching process, laser drilling, breath figure, and photolithography methods. To our knowledge, the creation of an array of micro holes (approximately 8 μm in diameter and 3 μm spacing) in a PI membrane using photolithography process has never been achieved.

Here, the patterned PI membranes were fabricated using a photolithography method. Figure c describes the different processing steps to fabricate the PI membrane: first, the silicon wafers are properly cleaned using a piranha (3:1 mixture of sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)). Then, we start wet thermal oxidation of the silicon substrate to grow a 100 nm-thick SiO2 film (temperature = 1100 °C – O2/H2 flow rates = 1500/2500 sccm – growth rate = 3.3 nm·min–1). The HD4110 precursor is poured onto the wafer and slowly spread out after waiting 10 min. This is a very important step to ensure that any small bubbles introduced during the pouring stage have enough time to evaporate. Finally, a base PI layer is formed by spin coating HD4110 on the substrate, followed by a soft bake on a hot plate at 90 °C for 3 min, and then at 110 °C for an additional 3 min. The spin speed and time are determined by the desired film thickness. Four thin nonstructured PI membranes were obtained with thicknesses of 13, 19, 23, and 28 μm before curing. The parameters used to obtain the different PI thicknesses before and after curing are shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

The HD4110 series is a photocurable PI that acts like a negative photoresist. An optical chrome mask was therefore used to create a periodic array of elementary hole patterns on a 2.3 × 2.3 cm square area of a PI membrane (t m = 6.5 μm) with a period of 12 μm and a hole diameter of 8.5 μm. These parameters were chosen due to the constraints of the UV exposure (dimension of the holes vs thickness of the membrane). This patterned mask was then applied to the surface to block UV light (365 nm), so that only unmasked regions of the material will be exposed to light and the holes are formed. After testing several lithographic parameters (exposure time and feature dimensions), the typical exposure settings chosen are two exposure cycles of 5 s followed by an 8 s delay. After a second annealing at 80 °C for 60 s, the substrate was developed with a PA-400 D developer for 5 min and followed with a PA-400 D rinse for 2 min. Then, a postdevelopment bake was used to remove the residual volatiles. Figure a shows scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (left) and Keyence numerical microscopy (right) images of the fabricated photonic membrane on the Si substrate. Finally, the sample was placed in a programmable oven under a nitrogen environment (to prevent incorporation of H2O in the layer), where the temperature was ramped up to 200 °C following a 10 °C/min ramp rate and held for 30 min and then further increased to 310 °C and maintained for 60 min. At the end, the SiO2 layer under the networks of holes is etched by hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching to obtain a self-supported membrane. The numerical microscopy Keyence image of the MSPM after SiO2 etching demonstrates the preservation of the shape of the holes and the array after the HF attack (see Figure b).

(a) Cross-sectional SEM (left) and numerical microscopy Keyence (right) image of the MSPM before SiO2 etching. (b) Numerical microscopy Keyence image of the MSPM after SiO2 etching. The period of the array, the diameter of the holes, and the thickness of the membrane are approximately 12, 8.5, and 6.5 μm, respectively.

Refractive Index Characterization

The complex refractive index N = n + ik of the spin-coated HD4110 was measured using the ellipsometry technique (see Methods). The optical properties of polymer layer were studied exploiting variable angle spectroscopic ellipsometer IR-VASE, J.A. Woollam Co., which covers the spectral range 500–6200 cm–1 (NIR-MIR). Ellipsometric data for angles of incidence of 50, 60, and 70° were recorded (measuring 25 scans, 15 spectra per revolution, with wavenumber steps of 4 cm–1). For the measurements, the samples were placed on a glass substrate holder using double scotch tape, which avoid unwanted backside reflections. Before ellipsometry data acquisition, the same instrument was utilized for transmission measurements where polymer foil samples were attached onto drilled substrate. In the case of measured polymer foils, simple Azzam’s theory for a single interface could be used to determine the spectral dependence of the refractive index n and extinction coefficient k from ellipsometry parameters ψexp and Δexp. For HD4110 foil, a total of 25 Lorentz oscillators were used to model the absorption bands found by using a combination of ellipsometry and transmission measurements. The real (n) and imaginary (k) part of the refractive index are shown in Figure . The obtained optical functions are very similar to PI data reported earlier by Zhang et al.

Mid-Infrared Properties

The MSPM has been characterized using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (see Methods) to evaluate its MIR transmission T properties, and the results have been compared with numerical simulation. The three optical coefficients (reflection R, transmission T, and absorption A) of the membranes are calculated by employing the finite element method (FEM) to solve the Maxwell’s equations using COMSOL Multiphysics software (see Methods). The optical coefficients are then used to calculate the efficiency factor by integrating the emissivity spectrum over the 5–15 μm range of the human body at 34 °C. This type of coefficient was used extensively in our previous work (see the Supporting Information, Supporting Note 2, eq 10). ,

Figure reports the transmission spectra obtained both experimentally (solid line) and numerically (dashed line) for, respectively, the unstructured membrane taken as reference (Figure a) and the MSPM (Figure b). Good agreement is obtained between simulation and measurements with, as a general trend, transmission dips well reproduced in both simulation and experiment. Slight amplitude differences can be observed around certain wavelengths, which can be related to a variation in the geometrical parameters between the simulated and fabricated membranes. For both membranes, several sharp transmission peaks and dips are present at low wavelengths, from 5 to 9 μm. In the range 10–13 μm, the MSPM (t m = 6.5 μm, P = 12 μm, and D = 8.5 μm) presents a significant decrease in the transmission spectrum compared to the unstructured membrane, over a large frequency range. To go deeper in the analysis, we have represented the reflection R (Figure c) and the absorption A (Figure d) spectra for, respectively, the unstructured membrane (dashed line) and the MSPM (solid line). One can see that the large decrease of T between 10 and 13 μm is related to the occurrence of large reflection R peaks that can be attributed to the periodic structuration, as already demonstrated in our previous work. In addition, the numerous sharp peaks/dips at a low wavelength (<9 μm) are generated by the intrinsic absorption peaks, which is consistent with the imaginary part behavior of the refractive index (see Figure b). Quantitatively, for the unstructured membrane, the calculation of the efficiency coefficient in transmission leads to 51% for the experimental curve compared to 50% for the simulation one. For the MSPM, the efficiency coefficient in transmission drops to 43% for the experiment, close to the simulation value of 41%. Therefore, the results revealed that periodic structuration has a significant influence on MIR properties, resulting in a decrease of the transmission coefficient coming from periodic structuration of the membrane.

Thermal Analysis

The photonic membrane’s thermal properties have been investigated using a unidimensional thermal analytical model given the required room temperature to maintain the human body in a thermal comfort, i.e., at T s = 34 °C (see Supporting Note 2). The corresponding ambient set point has been calculated for the structured and unstructured membrane. As we can see in Figure , when the body is covered by the MSPM (P = 12 μm, D = 8.5 μm, t m = 6.5 μm), for a 2 mm thickness between the body and the fabric, the targeted skin temperature of 34 °C can be reached with an ambient temperature of 23.3 °C versus an ambient temperature of 23.8 °C for an unstructured membrane of the same thickness. As a matter of comparison, the room temperature jumps to 26.2 °C for bare skin. This means that the structured membrane provides a reduction of nearly 0.5 °C at room temperature to maintain the thermal comfort of the human body. To summarize, the radiative heat losses have been decreased thanks to the lower transmission achieved from the microstructuration of the membrane as reported in the previous section (see Figure ).

In order to evaluate qualitatively the thermal properties of the membranes, we used an IR camera. To this end, the membrane was handled cautionally and positioned precisely at a fixed distance from the skin. A physical support has been realized with a rigid and thick ring aperture manufactured on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) with a 2 mm thickness in order to recreate the microclimate. The samples and their supports are left on the hand palm for at least 5 min to ensure the thermal stability. The ambient temperature and humidity were measured at 25 °C and 60%, respectively. Figure represents the schematic protocol (left) and the corresponding thermal snapshot (right) of the human hand covered with an unstructured membrane (Figure a) and the MSPM (Figure b). With a dark-red color inside the circle, it appears that the optical radiations are higher for the unstructured membrane compared to the MSPM one. As discussed previously, this result is a consequence of the photonic structuration, which minimizes the transmission of the human hand radiations. Consequently, the reflection in the MIR increases for the MSPM leading to additional heating of the 2 mm air spacer between the skin and the membrane.

To quantitatively demonstrate the indoor radiative heating performance of the photonic membrane, we carried out direct thermal measurements. To this end, the temperature of the air spacer or microclimate for each membrane was measured by a K-type thermocouple taped between the body forearm and the membranes, at a distance d from the skin (Figure a). All measurements were performed at a constant ambient temperature of 25 °C and a relative humidity of 60%.

Conclusions

In this work, which combines a design and technological manufacturing phase with a characterization step (optical and thermal), we demonstrated, both theoretically and experimentally, a personal thermal regulation fabric based on photonic polymer membranes providing personal heating by modulating optical radiations from the human body to the ambient environment. The design of a photonic grating with microstructure dimensions combined with the development of a photolithography process has resulted in a 5 cm2 microstructured polymer photonic membrane with holes around 8.5 μm in diameter and a hole spacing minimum distance of 3.5 μm. This membrane leads to a modulation of the optical transmission amplitude by 8%, increasing the reflection signal. Because of this IR photonic effect, this membrane allows a reduction of room heating by 0.5 °C without compromising personal thermal comfort. The heating effect of the photonic textile was demonstrated using an infrared camera and a K-type thermocouple. Moreover, this microstructure photonic fabric allows wicking of sweat away from skin and promotes air permeability due to the presence of pores in the membrane.

We believe that this strategy, based on the development of a polymer membrane, is of real interest as a layer complexed with an existing textile in order to improve its thermal properties. Although the temperature gain may seem small, on the scale of an entire building, the energy gain could be significant. The results presented relate to the use of the PI polymer but could be transposed to other polymers by adapting the geometry of the photonic grating to the optical properties of the polymer and possibly modifying the manufacturing process. Finally, the use of active polymers, whose physicochemical properties change as a function of stimuli such as temperature, could lead to the development of membranes whose optical and therefore thermal properties adapt to their environment.

Methods

Numerical Simulation

The optical coefficients R, T, and A were simulated using the finite element method (FEM) implemented in COMSOL Multiphysics software. We consider an incident electromagnetic plane wave radiating in air from the source (human body) and normally interacting with the elementary unit cell of a PI membrane. The source and the detector are placed in air, respectively, before and after the membrane. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) are applied on each side of the unit cell to build the total periodic structure. In addition, perfect matching layers (PMLs) are applied at the finite boundaries to avoid any reflections of outgoing waves.

Fabrication

The HD4110 thin layers were deposited using spin coater equipment (model: RC8, manufacturer: SUSS MicroTec) at the IEMN clean room laboratory. The samples were exposed to UV light using a mask aligner of MA6/BA6 model (manufacturer: SUSS MicroTec). The amount of exposure energy required for optimum resolution or wall shape depends on the coating thickness, underlying surface reflectivity, structure size, and feature uniformity. Most applications use the 150–400 mJ/cm I-line. Depth of focus will impact resolution, feature size, and sidewall shape. Top side alignment (TSA) was down to 0.5 μm, bottom side alignment (BSA) was down to 1 μm, and the exposure mode was hard contact.

Morphology Characterization

Surface imaging and analysis was determined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) Zeiss Ultra 55 at 0.7 kV and digital microscope Keyence that can observe uneven surfaces and 3D objects with clear images thanks to its large depth of field and long observation distance.

Refractive Index Measurement

The complex refractive index was measured using the ellipsometry method. Spectroscopic ellipsometry is an indirect optical characterization method, where the measured values ψexp and Δexp are compared to the values calculated from the model ψmod and Δmod. Two parameters, amplitude ratio, ψ, and phase shift, Δ, are defined by using the Fresnel reflection coefficients for p- (rp) and s- (rs) polarized light and express the change of the polarization state: rp/rs = tan­(ψ)•exp­(iΔ). Fitting procedure is used with the aim of minimizing Mean Square Error (MSE).

Optical Characterization

The infrared measurements were carried out with an integrating sphere (from PIKE Technologies). The sphere diameter is coated by a high reflectance diffuse gold coating. The entrance port, with a diameter of 2.3 cm, is located on the side of the sphere. A detector records transmitted radiation by the sample in the spectral range from 2 to 20 μm. The proportional measurement of specular and diffuse components of the transmittance of the analyzed samples can be performed, as well. To ensure that the incident beam impacts the structured surface, the surface area has to be of 4.15 cm2 minimum, which corresponds to the top or the opening window of the entrance port side of the integrated sphere. The sample is placed in front of the entrance port. The radiation from the internal source of the spectrometer passes through the sample into the integrating sphere system, where it is repeatedly reflected until it leaks through the detector port onto the detector. The measurement was automatically performed under the control of the IR Solutions software provided by Shimadzu.

Thermal Characterization

The thermal images were taken by an infrared camera (testo 865). All temperature measurements were collected using a BK Precision 715. This instrument is a digital thermometer for use with any K-type thermocouple as a temperature sensor. The relative error among the thermocouples is less than 0.03 °C.
