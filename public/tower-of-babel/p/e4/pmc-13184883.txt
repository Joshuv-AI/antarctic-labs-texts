While the Ross procedure is broadly recognized as one of the optimal surgical options for aortic valve pathologies in relatively young patients, its application in patients with Marfan syndrome (MFS) is not only debatable but may even be considered a contraindication. We report the case of a 31-year-old woman with MFS who underwent the Ross procedure for failure of valve-sparing root replacement (VSRR) after 2.5 years. No ethics review is required for the implementation of case reports at our institution. Case Presentation

A 31-year-old woman with a surgical history of VSRR (David-V reimplantation) and ascending aorta replacement for an aneurysm of the sinus of Valsalva 2.5 years earlier, presented to our institution for evaluation and treatment of asymptomatic severe aortic insufficiency (AI). Her family history included significant MFS in her father and older brother.

The initial surgery had been performed via partial sternotomy. Cardiopulmonary bypass with femoral artery and vein cannulation was established. The measured diameter of the sinus of Valsalva was 42 mm, the basal ring was 24 mm, and a 24-mm Valsalva graft (Gelweave Valsalva Ante-Flo gelatin impregnated woven Dacron graft, Sulzer Vascutek) had been used. Twelve 2-0 braided polyester with spaghetti horizontal mattress subannular stitches were placed, and the graft was seated. Remnant native tissue of the sinus of Valsalva was fixed with a continuous 5-0 polypropylene suture. The effective height was 12 mm. Immediately after surgery, transthoracic echocardiography showed trivial AI, but mild AI with a central jet was present at 6 months postoperatively. After 2 years, severe AI developed, prompting the consideration of redo aortic root surgery (Figure 1). Cardiopulmonary bypass with ascending aorta cannulation and bicaval venous drainage was initiated after resternotomy. Aortic cross-clamping was performed, and cardiac arrest was achieved using antegrade cardioplegia. The native aortic valve exhibited a shortened right coronary cusp, along with disproportionate enlargement of the basal ring on the right coronary cusp side, resulting in prolapse of all leaflets (Figure 2A). The basal ring diameter was 23 to 24 mm. Next, the main pulmonary artery was dissected and divided. The pulmonary valve was examined to confirm no damage, and an autograft with an annular diameter of 23 mm was harvested. After harvesting the autograft, the septal surface of the right ventricular outflow tract was carefully checked to ensure hemostasis, and a Xenomedica patch (Baxter Healthcare Corp) was sutured in place. The coronary button was detached from the previous Valsalva graft, and the graft was transected, leaving just the lowest portion (Figure 2B). The autograft was sutured to the basal ring using 28 stitches of 4-0 braided polyester interrupted suture. A Xenomedica strip was inserted during suture tie-down (Figures 2C and 2D, Video 1). Each coronary button was sutured in situ to the sinus of Valsalva of the autograft using 5-0 polypropylene. The anastomosis between the autograft and the remaining distal part of the 24-mm Valsalva graft was performed using a continuous suture after securing it with three 5-0 polypropylene sutures reinforced with a felt strip. Postoperatively, the patient's hemodynamics were stable, and transthoracic echocardiography showed no AI or gradient across the neo–aortic valve. The patient was discharged home after 2 weeks. At 2 years postoperatively, mild AI due to an eccentric jet from between the right and noncoronary cusps was observed, but left ventricular function was preserved (ejection fraction: 64%, left ventricular diastolic dimension/systolic dimension: 44/29 mm). The diameters of the sinus of Valsalva and the annulus of the autograft have remained stable at 25 mm and 18 to 19 mm, respectively, without evidence of dilatation. Over the past 2 years, the patient has been able to live a normal daily life without symptoms, and her progress is being closely monitored on an outpatient basis (Figure 1).

Discussion

The durability and long-term survival after VSRR for aortic valve and aortic root pathology remain favorable even in patients with connective tissue disorders such as MFS.1, 2, 3 The choice of VSRR in this patient's initial surgery appears entirely appropriate. It seems to be the first choice for relatively young patients who want to have a baby, and to avoid complications associated with prosthetic valves and oral coumadin treatment. However, some cases of reoperation have been reported during the follow-up period after VSRR,4,5 and meticulous follow-up is obviously desirable even with good durability.

On the other hand, the Ross procedure is considered to be superior to aortic valve replacement techniques in terms of hemolysis, infection, and hemodynamics, as it eliminates the need for oral coumadin treatment associated with prosthetic valve replacement and avoids serious complications such as thromboembolism and bleeding.6,7 Based on such data, it is widely acknowledged that the efficacy of the Ross procedure has been re-evaluated in recent years, particularly in Europe and the U.S./Canada, leading to a dramatic increase in the number of cases performed.

In the present case, the patient presented with severe AI 2.5 years after VSRR and was a young woman desiring future pregnancy. Regarding the failure of the initial VSRR, intraoperative findings revealed prolapse in all 3 leaflets, with the right coronary cusp being particularly shortened and exhibiting very poor valve coaptation. During the initial surgery, a deep Ross-plane dissection was adequately performed before placing first-row stitches, suggesting no technical issues. Identifying a clear cause for the failure proved difficult.

The choice of the Ross procedure for MFS is indeed debatable, and the latest expert consensus concluding that Ross procedures should be avoided in patients with connective tissue disorders such as MFS.8 This is supported by a case report of a giant pulmonary aneurysm in a patient with MFS9 and by a multicenter study finding that patients with MFS tend to have larger pulmonary arteries than normal subjects.10 However, the case report involved a patient with pulmonary hypertension, while the multicenter study did not include a time-series comparison and did not examine exposure to systemic blood pressure after actual transplantation. Therefore, the evidence completely ruling out the Ross procedure for patients with MFS appears limited.

Our patient had an autograft sutured to the basal ring portion, which was surrounded by the previous graft at the bottom and the 24-mm graft from the ascending aorta at the top. No dilatation of the autograft was observed on echocardiography over time. Given that the AI was eccentric, an abnormality in the pulmonary valve rather than recurrence of AI due to enlargement of the autograft or annulus is more likely. If performing a Ross procedure on a patient with MFS currently, it is important to at least prevent annular enlargement with reinforcement procedures, and wrapping the autograft with a vascular graft may also be worth considering. It may be premature to rule out the Ross procedure for MFS solely because of the potential for autograft dilation; nevertheless, meticulous follow-up, similar to that required after the initial VSRR, is desirable.

Conclusions

We report the 2-year postoperative outcomes of the Ross procedure in a patient with MFS after VSRR. Although mild AI is present, there has been no significant change in the left ventricular diameter, ejection fraction, or symptoms, and we plan to continue monitoring the patient's condition.
