Site-selective valence isomerism as a unified electronic trigger for magnetism, chirality, and catalysis in Ni–carboxylate MOFs
Authors: Jin-Huang Peng, Haitao Zhang, Zhenwei Wei, Yang Cao, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractValence modulation at metal nodes provides a fundamental route to couple electronic structure with functional properties in metal–organic frameworks (MOFs). Achieving precise and stimulus-independent control of metal oxidation states within an isostructural lattice remains a central challenge. Here we demonstrate valence isomerism in a Ni–carboxylate framework, Ni3(OH)2(Cam)2(H2O)4, by combining pH adjustment with vortex-driven stirring. This approach yields an isostructural pair consisting of a Ni(II) parent phase and a mixed-valence Ni(II)/Ni(III) analog, in which approximately 25% of the Ni centers are oxidized to low-spin Ni3+ selectively at the Ni(1) sites. Subtle valence reconfiguration reorganizes the intrachain interactions within the [Ni3(OH)2] chains, reinforcing ferrimagnetic order and enhancing net magnetization. Concomitantly, the short-wavelength circular dichroism signals invert, and the oxygen-evolution overpotential decreases by ~100 mV. These results establish valence isomerism as an electronic trigger that coherently couples magnetic, chiroptical and catalytic properties in MOFs, offering a general design principle for multifunctionality in reticular materials.Subject terms: Coordination chemistry, Inorganic chemistry

Introduction

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), constructed from metal nodes and organic linkers, represent a prototypical class of porous crystalline solids characterized by structural diversity, high surface areas, and chemically tunable environments. These features have enabled their broad exploration in catalysis, gas storage and separation, sensing, magnetism, and electronics1–3. Within this family, metal–carboxylate frameworks, pioneered by Yaghi’s seminal work on IRMOF series (e.g., MOF-5 and MOF-74)4–6 and further enriched by diverse series such as UiO7,8, MIL9,10, and PCN11,12, constitute one of the most robust and extensively studied structural motifs, providing a reliable platform for functional design. Notably, the incorporation of redox-active metal nodes allows deliberate modulation of oxidation states and electronic configurations, thereby directly coupling reticular chemistry with electronic functionality13.

A number of strategies have been developed to regulate the valence states of MOFs, including redox-driven counterion incorporation14–16 and defect engineering17–19, which can modulate oxidation states and thereby influence conductivity, electrocatalytic activity, gas adsorption, and magnetism20–22. These approaches are often accompanied by structural distortions or compromised crystallinity. Another route relies on intramolecular electron transfer, either between redox-active metal centers or between metals and coordinated ligands, as exemplified by cyano-bridged coordination polymers and cobalt–dioxolene complexes23–26. These examples illustrate how valence modulation can reconfigure dipole moments, magnetic exchange interactions, and guest recognition27–31. However, such phenomena typically involve transitions between two discrete electronic configurations, which rarely coexist under identical conditions and generally require external stimuli such as temperature, light, or pressure32–37. Thus, achieving stimulus-independent valence-state control in isostructural MOFs without compromising lattice integrity remains a central challenge.

In this work, we report a controllable synthetic approach to access valence isomerism in Ni–carboxylate MOFs by combining pH modulation with vortex motion. Using camphoric acid as the bridging ligand, we synthesized an isostructural pair: a Ni(II) parent framework obtained under hydrothermal conditions and a mixed-valence Ni(II)/Ni(III) analog formed under alkaline stirring, in which low-spin Ni3+ ions are selectively incorporated at the Ni(1) sites of the [Ni3(OH)2] chains. The introduction of Ni3+ centers triggers pronounced multifunctional responses: reorganization of intrachain antiferromagnetic interactions into ferrimagnetic order, inversion of short-wavelength circular dichroism signals, and a reduction of approximately 100 mV in the overpotential for the oxygen evolution reaction. By directly coupling carboxylate coordination chemistry with site-specific valence modulation, this work establishes valence isomerism as a unifying electronic mechanism that coherently governs multifunctionality in MOFs.

Results

Synthesis and structural characterizations of Ni(Cam) MOFs

The crystalline Ni(Cam)-MOFs, Ni3(OH)2(ᴅ-Cam)2(H2O)4, and Ni3(OH)2(ʟ-Cam)2(H2O)4, were synthesized by reacting Ni(NO3)2·6H2O with ionized ᴅ-/ʟ-camphoric acid using Na2CO3 under hydrothermal conditions (pH = 5.5, 120 °C, 5 days), hereafter denoted as Ni(Cam)-HY (HY = hydrothermal). The single-crystal structures of Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY reveal that both compounds crystallize in the chiral monoclinic P21 space group (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) with one-dimensional (1D) [Ni3(OH)2(H2O)4]n4n+chains extending along a-axis as secondary building unit (SBU) (Supplementary Figs. 1–3 and Supplementary Data 1). These chains are bridged by camphoric acid units, forming a distorted prism-like packing arrangement that ultimately results in a three-dimensional network (Fig. 1a, b). Within each chain, three nickel ions are coordinated in an octahedral NiO6 geometry and share two µ3-OH groups with each other, creating trimers of Ni(1), Ni(2), and Ni(3) (Supplementary Fig. 3). The two crystal structures, Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY, thus represent an enantiomorphic pair arising from the chiral configurations of ʟ- and ᴅ-camphoric acid.

Because all three Ni centers are initially water-coordinated, the reaction pH critically governs Ni2+ stability and ligand deprotonation. Systematic pH control was therefore achieved by varying the ratio of NaOH to ligand (Supplementary Fig. 4). Low pH conditions (≤5.19) led to impurity phases due to insufficient ligand deprotonation (Supplementary Fig. 5), whereas higher pH values (≥7.44) caused rapid Ni(OH)2 precipitation (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 6). An intermediate pH of 7.21 was thus selected, under which Ni2+ remains stable while the ligand is sufficiently deprotonated, enabling the reproducible formation of phase-pure Ni(ᴅ-Cam) upon stirring at 80 °C for 2 days. Under these optimized stirring conditions, controlled deprotonation and partial oxidation of Ni2+–OH2 to Ni3+–OH were induced, yielding Ni(Cam)-ST (ST = stir) (Fig. 1c), which retains the same overall structural framework of Ni(Cam)-HY. Figure 1d displays the PXRD patterns for the Ni(Cam) MOFs, confirming that the pristine (Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY) and valence-modified (Ni(ʟ-Cam)-ST and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST) samples are isostructural and highly crystalline. Both Ni(Cam)-HY and Ni(Cam)-ST samples displayed rod-like morphologies with size variations likely caused by solution perturbations during synthesis (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Thermogravimetric analysis under N2 at ambient pressure revealed that Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY showed a 9.5% weight loss upon heating to ~200 °C, corresponding to the release of four H2O molecules (calc. 10.6%). By contrast, Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST exhibited a slightly lower weight loss (7.8%), consistent with conversion of part of the coordinated water into hydroxyl groups (OH⁻) during partial nickel oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Species analysis and valence state characterization of Ni(Cam) MOFs

To compare the composition of the two reaction systems and clarify the reaction pathways of valence-variable Ni sources, we analyzed the reaction intermediates before and after synthesis using nano-electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (Nano-ESI-MS)38–40. Several growth species were identified in the negative-ion spectra (Supplementary Figs. 10–13). In the hydrothermal system (Fig. 2a, b), the most abundant Ni2+-containing growth species was [Ni(NO3)3]⁻ (m/z 243.9019, base peak). Intermediates such as Ni2+-ligand complexes [Ni(ᴅ-Cam)(NO3)]⁻ (m/z 318.0184), [NiH(ᴅ-Cam)(NO3)2]⁻ (m/z 381.0134), and [Ni2(ᴅ-Cam)(NO3)3]− (m/z 499.9339) were also detected, indicating the formation of metal–ligand coordination bonds prior to crystallization. Additionally, the peak observed at m/z 197.9101 was assigned to the elemental composition [NiN2O7]−. This species is interpreted as an oxo-nickel nitrate adduct, possibly [NiO(NO3)2]⁻, and its presence may indicate the formation of reactive nickel-oxo intermediates in the reactions (Fig. 2b).

a, b HRMS spectra of the reaction solution prepared via hydrothermal method. c, d HRMS spectra of the reaction solution prepared via stirring method. Source data are provided as a Source Data file.

In the stirring system (Fig. 2c, d), a broader range of species was observed compared with the hydrothermal system. In addition to the species observed in the hydrothermal system, the stirring system also produced sodium-containing species such as [Na(ᴅ-Cam)]⁻ (m/z 221.0827), [NaH(ᴅ-Cam)(NO3)]⁻ (m/z 284.0812), [Na2(ᴅ-Cam)(NO3)]− (m/z 306.0627), and [NiNa(ᴅ-Cam)(NO3)2]− (m/z 402.9977), highlighting the impact of stirring and higher pH on ligand stability and coordination environments. Based on these observed growth species, together with previous studies on Mg-MOFs using camphoric acids39, Ni-ligand complexes generated in situ from Ni2+ and camphorate in both reaction systems serve as precursors for the solid-state assembly of Ni(Cam)-MOFs. However, the higher pH and rotational shear flow in the stirring system facilitated the formation of additional sodium-containing species, which may be associated with the incorporation of Ni3+ into the final product. This hypothesis is supported by the marked decrease in the relative intensities of sodium-containing species and oxo-nickel nitrate adduct [NiO(NO3)2]⁻ after reaction (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13).

The X-band EPR spectra of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST exhibited markedly distinct spectral features (Fig. 3a). The spectrum of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST displayed a well-resolved anisotropic signal centered at g ≈ 2, with principal g-values of g1 = 2.00 and g2 = 1.93, which is characteristic of low-spin Ni3+ complexes (S = 1/2). In contrast, no corresponding EPR signal was observed for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY in this g-value region, indicating the absence of detectable Ni3+ species within this sample. Thus, the hydrothermal method predominantly stabilizes Ni2+ species, whereas the stirring method prefers Ni3+, reflecting the distinct chemical environments and reaction pathways of the two synthesis routes. The valence states of nickel ions in the Ni(Cam) MOFs were also investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) analyses of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST (Fig. 3b, c). The Ni 2p XPS spectra of both samples exhibit similar overall features (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 14), but the peak maxima of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST are shifted to higher binding energies relative to Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY. This shift toward higher binding energy indicates that a fraction of Ni atoms in Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST adopt higher oxidation states. Curve-fitting results of the Ni 2p spectra show that the dominant peaks corresponding to the Ni2+ species appeared at 856.2 eV (2p3/2) and 873.7 eV (2p1/2) for both Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST. However, for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST, additional peaks at 857.8 eV (2p3/2) and 875.3 eV (2p1/2) can be assigned to Ni3+ species, consistent with reported Ni3+ binding energies and fitting procedures in nickel-based compounds41,42.

Electron paramagnetic resonance and electronic spectroscopy analysis of the Ni(Cam) isostructural pairs.

a X-band EPR spectra, b Ni 2p XPS, and c Ni LMM Auger spectra of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST. Source data are provided as a Source Data file.

To further confirm the valence state changes and exclude potential contributions from other Ni2+ species, such as nickel hydroxide (Ni(OH)2), which may form under alkaline conditions and exhibit high binding energy similar to NiO(OH), Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST were conducted (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 15). The Ni LMM Auger spectra show that the main peak of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST is shifted by 0.8 eV to higher kinetic energy compared with Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY. Combined with XPS and AES results, the modified Auger parameter α′ (defined as α′=Eb+Ek, where Eb and Ek are the binding and kinetic energies of the dominant core electron and Auger electron lines, respectively) can be calculated to investigate chemical states for Ni43. The α′ value of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST is 1.2 eV higher than that of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY, which provides additional evidence that partial Ni ions in Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST are oxidized to higher valence states. Thus, the hydrothermal method predominantly stabilizes Ni2+ species, whereas the stirring method prefers Ni3+, reflecting the distinct chemical environments and reaction pathways of the two synthesis routes.

Magnetic properties of Ni(Cam)-MOFs

For the transition-metal organic complex, the distribution of valence electrons and the coordination environment are closely correlated with the spin state, which directly affects its magnetic moment and exchange interactions. The temperature dependence of field-cooled magnetization (FCM) was measured in the temperature range 1.8–300 K under a 1 kOe dc field, and the molar susceptibility (χm) was evaluated. The temperature-dependent χm and χmT for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST show similar trends, indicating comparable magnetic coupling and interactions (Supplementary Fig. 16). The χm value of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY is larger than that of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST, suggesting that Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST contains fewer unpaired electrons (i.e., a smaller spin quantum number), leading to a smaller total magnetic moment. Upon cooling, both compounds exhibit a gradual decrease in χmT, reaching a minimum around 30 K, which is indicative of intrachain antiferromagnetic coupling between nickel ions. Upon further cooling, the χmT value increases rapidly and reaches a maximum of 7.56 and 7.06 emu mol−1∙Oe−1 K at 2 K for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST, respectively, suggesting the emergence of ferromagnetic behavior. This low-temperature spontaneous magnetization is attributed to spin canting within the weakly antiferromagnetically coupled trinuclear Ni units, arising from the non-collinear magnetic geometry of the bent Ni–Ni–Ni arrangement mediated by µ3-OH and carboxylate bridges44.

Figure 4a and Supplementary Fig. 17 present the temperature-dependent χm−1 curves for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST. Fitting the linear paramagnetic region of the χm−1−T curves (300–50 K) using the Curie-Weiss law yields Curie constants (C) of 3.75 emu Oe−1 K mol−1 for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and 3.18 emu Oe−1 K mol−1 for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST, with corresponding Curie-Weiss temperatures θCW of −21.3 K and −22.8 K, respectively. Based on the fitting results, the effective magnetic moment (μeff) of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY is calculated as 5.48 μB, exceeding the theoretical spin-only moment of μcal=2μB∑SS+1=4.90μB for one formula unit (three Ni2+, S = 1, g = 2.00), suggesting an additional orbital contribution to the magnetic moment. For Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST, μeff is smaller, at 5.04 μB, attributable to the partial conversion of Ni2+ (S = 1) to low-spin Ni3+ (S = 1/2), consistent with the spin states observed in other Ni3+ systems with octahedral oxygen coordination45. The large negative θCW for both samples indicates predominant antiferromagnetic exchange interactions between nickel centers.

To further probe the low-temperature magnetic behavior, ZFC and FC magnetization measurements were carried out under an applied field of 1 kOe (Supplementary Fig. 18). The coincidence of the ZFC and FC curves excludes long-range ferromagnetic ordering and spin-glass-like freezing, indicating negligible interchain magnetic interactions within the investigated temperature range.

We further measured the magnetization (Mf.u., magnetization per formula unit) as a function of the magnetic field (H) (Fig. 4b; for molar magnetization Mm, see Supplementary Fig. 19). The M-H curve for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY measured at 1.8 K (Fig. 6b), shows that Mf.u. reaches a plateau at around 30 kOe, attaining 2.17 μB at 70 kOe. This value is approximately one-third of the saturated magnetization of 6 μB (theoretical spin-only value for three isolated Ni2+ ions with S = 1 and g = 2.00). Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST exhibits similar M–H behavior, with a Mf.u. of 2.57 μB at 70 kOe, suggesting that the conversion of partial Ni(1)2+ (S = 1) to low-spin Ni(1)3+ (S = 1/2) increases the net magnetic moment per formula unit (inset of Fig. 4b). Figure 4c shows the nickel hydroxide chains and their magnetic lattice as derived from the crystal structure. In the formula unit (enlarged view on the right), three nickel atoms (Ni(1), Ni(2), Ni(3)) are connected via O(9) and O(10) atoms. The bond angles between nickel centers, such as Ni(1)–O(9)–Ni(2) (117.8°) and Ni(1)–O(9)–Ni(3) (127.7°), are much larger than 90°, while the angle Ni(2)–O(10)–Ni(3) (95.0°) is closer to 90°. These geometries suggest that the net spin directions of Ni(2) and Ni(3) align, while Ni(1) spins are antiferromagnetically coupled to those of Ni(2) and Ni(3). Consequently, the nickel centers thus form a one-dimensional diamond-chain magnetic network46 The Ni3+ spin aligns opposite to the net magnetic moment when combined with the diamond-like magnetic lattice structure (Fig. 4c, lower panel) and the observed antiferromagnetic coupling. These results indicate that Ni3+ preferentially occupies the Ni(1) site, as illustrated in the inset of Fig. 4b. The thermodynamic stability of the oxidized species at different oxidation scenarios was further addressed by DFT calculations, as documented in Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, along with the relevant discussion on site selectivity.

The fraction of Ni3+ was quantitatively estimated by comparing the effective magnetic moments (μeff) derived from Curie–Weiss fits in the 50–300 K range (see Supplementary Information for details). Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY exhibits μeff = 5.48 μB, whereas Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST shows a reduced value of 5.04 μB. Assuming selective oxidation at the Ni(1) site, whereby Ni2+ (S = 1) is partially converted to low-spin Ni3+ (S = 1/2), the decrease in μeff2 corresponds to an average oxidation occupancy of ~0.74 at Ni(1), corresponding to approximately 25% of the total Ni centers being oxidized to Ni3+.

We also used DFT calculations to explore the magnetic and spin-state properties of the structure. The (Ni2+)4(ᴅ-Cam2−)6(OH−)4·(H2O)6 cluster was employed to evaluate the magnetic coupling constants (J) between adjacent nickel ions, revealing predominantly antiferromagnetic interactions, with Ni(1)–Ni(2) (J = −36.58 cm−1) and Ni(1)–Ni(3) (J = −0.42 cm−1) both antiferromagnetic, whereas Ni(2)–Ni(3) exhibits significant ferromagnetic coupling (J = 36.23 cm−1) (Supplementary Fig. 21; Supplementary Tables 5–10, and Supplementary Data 2). The spin density distributions and energy differences between high-spin (S = 3/2) and low-spin (S = 1/2) states of the nickel ions show that the low-spin state configuration is more stable for all three nickel sites, with energy differences of 0.29 eV, 0.22 eV, and 0.23 eV for Ni(1), Ni(2), and Ni(3), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 22). In particular, the stabilization of the low-spin configuration at the Ni(1) site (Fig. 4d) is consistent with both the antiferromagnetic coupling scheme and the experimental spin-state analysis. These results underscore the critical role of magnetic measurements in qualitatively identifying oxidation-state changes in Ni(Cam) MOFs and provide detailed insight into how these changes govern spin states and magnetic exchange within the framework.

Chiroptical properties of Ni(Cam)-MOFs

The chirality of the synthesized Ni(Cam) MOFs was identified using CD spectroscopy (Fig. 5). The CD signals can be categorized into three distinct wavelength regions: approximately 250, 400, and 700 nm (Fig. 5a). The CD peaks in the long wavelength regions (400 and 700 nm) are primarily determined by the chiral configuration of the camphoric acid ligands. In these regions, Ni(ʟ-Cam)-MOFs exhibit a positive Cotton effect, whereas Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-MOFs display a negative Cotton effect. In contrast, at short wavelength (~250 nm), the orientation of CD signals depends on both the ligand configuration and the synthetic conditions. For the pristine Ni(Cam)-HY, Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY displays a positive Cotton effect at 250 nm, while Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY exhibits a negative signal. However, in the valence-modified Ni(Cam)-ST, the short-wavelength CD signals are inverted, becoming consistent with those observed in the long-wavelength regions. Specifically, Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST shows a negative Cotton effect, while Ni(ʟ-Cam)-ST exhibits a positive Cotton effect at 250 nm. Given the relatively weak CD intensity and the alignment of nickel hydroxide chains along the a-axis, linear dichroism (LD) measurements were performed (Supplementary Fig. 23). The negligible LD signals across the measured range confirm that the observed chiroptical features originate from intrinsic chirality rather than linear dichroism artifacts.

The one-dimensional heatmap representation of CD intensity is provided in Fig. 5b, with positive and negative signals distinguished by color coding. This visualization shows that samples prepared with the same camphoric acid configuration exhibit consistent signal orientations in the long-wavelength regions. In contrast, the short-wavelength CD signals of the valence-modified Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST and Ni(ʟ-Cam)-ST samples are inverted compared to their pristine counterparts. This observation reveals that the local inversion of CD signals is induced by valence isomerism within the isostructural framework. As the CD signals are highly sensitive to the surrounding dipole moment, we performed computational simulations of the chiroptical properties for the Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY based on its crystal structure. The hole-electron distributions (Supplementary Tables 11–13) and the interfragment charge transfer (IFCT) analysis (Supplementary Tables 14–16) indicate that the CD peaks (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25) in long wavelength regions (400 and 700 nm) can be assigned to two d-d transition bands: 3A2g→3T1g(3F) and 3A2g→3T1g(3P)47. In the region below 300 nm, the dominant CD signals associated with the three nickel sites originate from ligand-to-metal and ligand-to-ligand charge-transfer processes, specifically from OH− to Ni2+ and to ᴅ-Cam2−. The short-wavelength CD signals are highly sensitive to the local electronic structure and coordination environment of the metal centers. The site-selective valence isomerism from [Ni(II)(H2O)2]2+ to [Ni(III)(OH)(H2O)]2+ modifies the local ligand field and metal–ligand covalency without changing the overall framework chirality, leading to an inversion of the associated rotational strengths.

To elucidate the relationship between the local inversion of the CD signal and associated with the valence state of Ni(1), we conducted a mechanistic analysis by integrating the coordination environment of the nickel metal centers with DFT-calculated rotatory strengths. As shown in Fig. 5c, the coordination environments can be classified into two types. Ni(1) adopts a highly symmetric geometry, with bridging carboxylates, water, and hydroxyl groups arranged in a para disposition. In contrast, Ni(2) and Ni(3) share an identical coordination environment, characterized by a distorted asymmetric octahedron comprising both bridging and chelating carboxylates. These distinct coordination geometries result in site-dependent differences in rotatory strength. Figure 5d compares the maximum rotatory strengths of the three Ni centers across three wavelength ranges. In the short-wavelength region (<300 nm), all Ni centers generate strong rotatory strengths of comparable intensity, and their peaks overlap in wavelength, leading to additive contributions. Thus, both the intensity and the sign of the CD signal in this region are co-determined by all Ni sites, allowing for signal inversion when the valence state of Ni(1) changes. By contrast, in the 400–600 nm and >600 nm regions, which correspond primarily to d–d transitions, the disparity in coordination geometries results in Ni(1) contributing significantly weaker rotatory strength than Ni(2) and Ni(3). Consequently, the CD signals in these regions are dominated by Ni(2) and Ni(3) and remain unaffected by changes in the Ni(1) valence state.

Electrochemical performance of Ni(Cam)-MOFs in the oxygen evolution reaction

The composition and oxidation state of the catalysts can affect their electrocatalytic OER performance. The intrinsic catalytic activity of the Ni3+ centers plays a key role in promoting the electrocatalytic OER48. We therefore compared the OER activities of Ni(Cam)-MOFs. Figure 6a displays the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves for the four Ni(Cam)-MOF samples, with carbon fiber cloth (grey curves) serving as a blank control. The pristine Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY exhibit relatively large overpotentials of ~450 mV at a current density of 10 mA cmgeo−2. In contrast, the valence-modified Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST and Ni(ʟ-Cam)-ST demonstrate improved OER activity, with overpotentials reduced to ~350 mV, a significant reduction of nearly 100 mV. As is commonly observed in Ni-based electrocatalysts, the oxidation peak corresponding to the transition from Ni2+ to Ni3+ appears around 1.37 V vs. RHE15,41. In the valence-modified Ni(Cam) MOFs, this anodic peak shifts to a lower potential and increases in intensity, indicating a reduced activation barrier for generating active Ni3+ catalytic centers. This enhancement can be attributed to the incorporation of Ni(1)3+, which, owing to its higher acidity, withdraws electron density from the neighboring Ni(2) and Ni(3) sites through an inductive effect. This electron withdrawal weakens the Ni–O bonds and lowers the energy barrier for the oxidation of Ni2+ to Ni3+, thereby facilitating the generation of active Ni3+ catalytic centers. The Tafel plots (Fig. 6b) reveal that the Tafel slopes for the valence-modified Ni-MOFs (137 mV dec−1 for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST and 139 mV dec−1 for Ni(ʟ-Cam)-ST) are significantly lower than those of the unmodified Ni-MOFs (285 mV dec−1 for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and 262 mV dec−1 for Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY). A lower Tafel slope indicates a higher electron transfer efficiency in the water oxidation process, suggesting that the valence-modified Ni-MOFs exhibit superior OER activities.

The stability of the catalysts was assessed by performing long-term stability tests at a constant current density of 10 mA⋅cmgeo−2 for 3 h (Supplementary Fig. 26). The results show that Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY maintained a stable overpotential difference of ~100 mV throughout the testing period, indicating good stability for the valence-modified MOF. Overall, the OER catalytic activity of the enantiomeric Ni(Cam)-MOFs is comparable, with the valence-modified MOFs exhibiting lower overpotentials and Tafel slopes. These results clearly confirmed the presence of valence-variable nickel and its role in enhancing OER activity.

Furthermore, by adjusting the metal-to-ligand ratios during synthesis, we were able to modulate the crystallinity of the Ni(Cam)-MOF samples (Supplementary Fig. 27). Despite large variations in crystallinity, the CD signals and OER performance (overpotentials and Tafel slopes) remained consistent (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29), indicating that chirality and catalytic activity are governed primarily by the nickel valence state rather than crystallinity. Under standard conditions, Ni3+ is introduced into the MOFs through the combination of higher pH and stirring. Control experiments (entries 5 and 6) further demonstrate that either raising the pH or applying stirring alone can also induce CD signal inversion at 250 nm together with a reduction in OER overpotential (Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 30–32).

These experiments reveal a clear correlation between the presence of Ni3+, the sign of the CD signals, and the OER overpotentials, independent of the synthesis conditions. Specifically, the pristine Ni(Cam) MOFs without Ni3+ exhibit intrinsic CD responses accompanied by relatively high overpotentials, whereas the valence-modified Ni(Cam) MOFs containing Ni3+ show partially inverted CD signals at 250 m together with enhanced OER activity. The incorporation of low-spin Ni3+ at the Ni(1) site alters the local dipole moment and influences charge-transfer processes associated with the metal nodes, thereby inducing the observed inversion of the 250 nm CD signal. At the same time, the electron-withdrawing nature of Ni(1)3+ creates electron deficiency at the Ni(2) and Ni(3) sites, which facilitates the pre-OER Ni redox transition and enhances catalytic activity. In addition, the chiral factors inherent in the structure may also facilitate the valence transition process27,49. Importantly, these changes in CD signals and OER activities occurred within an isostructural lattice, thus further revealing the pivotal role of metal-center valence states in simultaneously modulating chirality and electrocatalytic activity in Ni(Cam) MOFs.

Discussion

The precise modulation of valence states at metal nodes is essential for establishing a direct correlation between electronic structure and functional properties in MOFs. In this work, we combine pH adjustment with vortex motion to realize valence isomerism in a Ni–carboxylate framework. This approach affords an isostructural pair comprising a Ni(II) parent phase and a mixed-valence Ni(II)/Ni(III) analog, in which approximately 25% of the Ni centers are oxidized to low-spin Ni3+, selectively occupying the Ni(1) sites within the [Ni3(OH)2] chains. This site-specific oxidation triggers multifunctional responses, including modification of intrachain antiferromagnetic interactions, inversion of short-wavelength circular dichroism signals, and reduction of the oxygen-evolution overpotential by ~100 mV. These findings establish valence isomerism as a lattice-preserving mechanism that coherently couples magnetic, chiroptical, and catalytic properties in a single framework. This work demonstrates site-selective valence tuning as a general design principle for multifunctional MOFs, offering a synthetic foundation for integrating electronic structure control with applications in molecular magnetism, chiral photophysics, and energy catalysis.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis via hydrothermal method (Method A, Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY as an example)

Under ambient air, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (292.7 mg, 1.0 mmol, 2.0 equiv), ᴅ-Camphoric acid (100.2 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv), Na2CO3 (54.3 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv), and 7 mL of deionized water were added to a 20 mL sealed tube equipped with a magnetic stirring bar. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 15 min until all reactants dissolved, resulting in a clear green solution (pH = 5.5). The solution was transferred to a 25 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 5 days. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol, and dried under vacuum at 40 °C for 10 h to yield Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY (118.0 mg, 70% yield) as green crystals. General procedure for the synthesis via the stirring method (Method B, Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST as an example)

Under ambient air, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (72.7 mg, 0.25 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and Na2(ᴅ-Cam) (1.0 mL 0.25 mol/L in H2O, 1.0 equiv, prepared from camphoric acid, sodium hydroxide, and deionized water) and 2 mL of deionized water were added to a 4 mL sealed tube and stirred at room temperature. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 min to dissolve all reactants, resulting in a clear green solution (pH = 7.2). The sealed tube was then placed in a dry bath heater and heated at 80 °C for 48 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water, and dried under vacuum at 40 °C for 10 h to yield Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST (17.5 mg, 31% yield) as a green powder. Control experiment for the synthesis of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-CE1 (Table 1, entry 5)

Under ambient air, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (145.0 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv), Na2(ᴅ-Cam) (2.0 mL, 0.25 mol/L in H2O, 1.0 equiv), and 4 mL of deionized water were added to a 20 mL sealed tube and stirred at room temperature. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 min to dissolve all reactants. The resulting solution was transferred to a 25 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 5 days. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol, and dried under vacuum at 40 °C for 10 h to yield Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-CE1 (38.2 mg, 34% yield) as a green powder.

Control experiments for the synthesis of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-CE2 (Table 1, entry 6)

Under ambient air, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (290.8 mg, 1.0 mmol, 2.0 equiv), ᴅ-camphoric acid (100.0 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv), Na2CO3 (54.2 mg, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and 7 mL of deionized water were added to a 20 mL sealed tube and stirred at room temperature. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 15 min until all reactants dissolved, forming a clear green solution. The solution was then divided into two 4 mL sealed tubes, each equipped with a magnetic stirring bar, and stirred at 80 °C in a dry bath heater for 48 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water, and dried under vacuum at 40 °C for 10 h to yield Ni(ᴅ -Cam)-CE2 (40.4 mg, 24% yield) as a green powder.

Crystal structure analysis

The single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY was performed on a Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy Custom diffractometer using Cu−Kα radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å). Using Olex250, the structure was solved with the SHELXT51 structure solution program using Intrinsic Phasing and refined with the SHELXL refinement package using Least Squares minimization. Structural diagrams were prepared using Diamond software52. The CIF files for Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY and Ni(ʟ-Cam)-HY have been deposited at the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) under the Supporting Information numbers CCDC−2407334 and 2407333, respectively. Copies of the data can be obtained free of charge from the CCDC via www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/data_request/cif. The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) was measured on a Rigaku Smartlab 9kW diffractometer operated at 45 kV, 200 mA using Cu Kα (λ = 1.5418 Å) with a scan speed of 10°/min. The simulated PXRD patterns were calculated from the CIF files of Ni(ᴅ-Cam) and Ni(ʟ-Cam) using Mercury 3.8 software.

Solid-state CD spectra measurement

All of the solid-state circular dichroism (sCD) spectra used in this research were measured on a JASCO J-1500 spectrometer equipped with DRCD-575 attachment and PML-534 detector to obtain an accurate signal. Approximately 2.0 mg of dry sample was mixed with 48.0 mg of KCl in a mortar, followed by grinding into a fine powder (1∶25 ratio in KCl). The resulting mixture was pressed into a tablet using the dedicated sample holder of the DRCD-575 attachment. Measurements were performed with optimized parameters: 200 nm/min scan speed, 1 nm data pitch, and 10 nm bandwidth. Linear dichroism, high tension voltage, and absorption measurements were performed simultaneously on the same samples and sample holder.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements

The EPR measurements were carried out using a Bruker ELEXSYS-II E500 CW-EPR. The instrumental parameters were set as follows: the sample temperature was controlled at 100 K using an ER4131VT digital temperature control system with liquid nitrogen cooling. The microwave frequency was fixed at 9.395 GHz with a power of 20 mW. The magnetic field was scanned with a resolution of 0.5 Gs around a center field of 3480.00 Gs, and each scan was completed in 80 s. The g-factors were determined from the resonance condition using the formula g=hν/μBB, where h is Planck’s constant, ν is the microwave frequency, μB is the Bohr magneton, and B is the resonant magnetic induction intensity.

Nano-electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (Nano-ESI-MS) measurements

Nano-ESI-MS studies were carried out using a SCIEX X500R QTOF (AB SCIEX Ltd., USA). The instrumental parameters were set as follows: Curtain gas: 25 psi, spray voltage: −1.5 to −3 kV, collision energy −25 V, acquisition time: 0.1 s. The spray voltage was supplied by a high-voltage power supply (Dongwen High Voltage Power Supply Co., Ltd., Tianjin). All nano-ESI emitters were pulled from borosilicate tubing (B150-86-10, Sutter) using a micropipette puller (P-97, Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA). The emitter orifice was about 3 μm.

Magnetic property measurements

Magnetic measurements were performed using a Quantum Design SQUID magnetometer (MPMS3) in the temperature and d.c. field ranges of 1.8 to 300 K and −70 to 70 kOe, respectively. The polycrystalline samples of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY (24.8 g) and Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-ST (24.4 g) were mounted in gelatin capsules for field-cooled magnetization (1 kOe, 300 to 1.8 K), FC-ZFC (0 Oe, 30 to 1.8 K; 1 kOe, 1.8 to 30 K), and field-dependent magnetization (1.8 K, −70 to 70 kOe) measurements. Magnetic data were analyzed in the CGS (centimeter-gram-second) unit system, and no diamagnetic correction has been made.

Electrochemical characterizations

Electrochemical tests were carried out on a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation using a standard three-electrode setup in 1.0 M KOH electrolyte. High-purity KOH was used to prepare the electrolyte and stored in a polypropylene bottle. The working electrode consisted of carbon fiber cloth loaded with electrocatalysts, the counter electrode was a graphite rod, and the reference electrode was a Hg/HgO electrode. The catalyst powders were applied to the carbon fiber cloth via drop-casting a catalyst ink with a loading of 0.75 mgcal.⋅cmgeo−2. The ink was prepared by ultrasonication of a 600 μL ethanol solution containing 0.1% Nafion and 3 mg of finely ground catalyst powder for 30 min. For OER tests, the working electrode was conditioned with 40 potential cycles between 0.2 and 1.0 V (vs. Hg/HgO) at a scan rate of 500 mV s−1 to stabilize the voltammogram. Linear sweep voltammetry was performed at a scan rate of 2 mV s−1 from 0.2 to 1.0 V (vs. Hg/HgO). Tafel slopes were obtained from the linear region of the overpotential vs. current density plot. Catalyst stability was evaluated by chronopotentiometry at a current density of 10 mA⋅cmgeo−2. All potentials were calibrated to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) using the equation: ERHE = EHg/HgO + 0.098 V + 0.0592·pH V.

DFT calculations

Theoretical investigations were conducted based on the SCXRD data of Ni(ᴅ-Cam)-HY. Unless otherwise specified, cluster-based single-point calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 software package53. For these computations, the def2-TZVP basis sets were employed54, with the B3LYP functional utilized for analyzing magnetic properties and the CAM-B3LYP functional for simulating the CD spectra55,56. Numerical integrations were performed with an ultrafine integration grid to ensure computational precision, and the self-consistent field procedure was configured to achieve tight convergence with a root mean square density matrix threshold of 10−8. Wavefunction analyses and visualizations of electronic and magnetic characteristics were conducted using Multiwfn 3.8 and VMD 1.9.3 software packages57,58. Unless otherwise stated, isosurface values for spin density and hole-electron distribution visualizations of the excited states were standardized at 0.005 a.u (verses 6 and 8 in supporting information for details).
