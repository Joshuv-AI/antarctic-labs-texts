The effect of malicious and benign envy on career plateau in nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Şehrinaz Polat, Tuğba Yeşilyurt Sevim, Nilgün Göktepe, Leyla Afşar-Doğrusöz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnvy and career plateau are common among nurses. Envy can result from upward social comparisons and negatively affect individuals’ behaviors, emotions, and organizational perceptions. This study aimed to determine the effect of nurses’ personal and professional characteristics and workplace envy levels on perceptions of career plateau.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted using an online survey distributed to nurses working in university, private, and public hospitals in Türkiye. The study’s sample consisted of 431 nurses, and data collection tools included a Nurse Information Form (including demographic and work-related variables), the Career Plateau Survey, and the Benign and Malicious Envy Scale (BeMaS-T). Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis, and multivariate linear regression analysis (enter) were used to analyze the data.ResultsThe mean scores of the nurses’ job content career plateau, hierarchical career plateau, BeMaS-T malicious envy, and benign envy scales were 3.24 ± 0.66, 3.39 ± 1.17, 4.19 ± 1.42, and 4.12 ± 1.21, respectively. It was found that the nurses’ marital status influenced the perception of job content (R2 = 0.043, F = 3.784, p = 0.002), and the status of voluntarily choosing the nursing profession, the type of institution, and the perception of malicious envy influenced the perception of hierarchical career plateau (R2 = 0.165, F = 8.290, p > 0.001).ConclusionsMalicious envy and high levels of perceived career plateau are not desirable situations in hospitals because they have the potential to negatively affect patient care, nurses, and the organization, and effective interventions should be put in place to reduce them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03817-z.

Introduction

The concept of career plateau was first described by Ference et al., who defined it as “the point in a career where the likelihood of additional hierarchical promotion is very low” [1, p.602]]. Nowadays, measures of career plateau focus on employees’ subjective evaluations [2, 3] and are analyzed as hierarchical and job content plateau [3, 4]. Subjective career plateau, which is frequently referred to in the literature, is the individual’s perception that they feel limited in their job position or progression [5].

Hierarchical plateau refers to promotions in the pyramidal structure of an organization and occurs when opportunities for advancement diminish or become unattainable. Job content plateau, on the other hand, occurs when employees have completely mastered their jobs and feel that there is little or no challenge left [2]. In a study by Kim and Lee [6], clinical nurses reported the characteristics of hierarchical career plateau as an unmet need for vertical mobility and acceptance of promotion restrictions, and the characteristics of content career plateau as job repetition, involuntary job fixation, job lethargy, low expectations for nursing career development, and disinterest in care recipients.

Career plateau has been reported to be related to many behaviors, attitudes, perceptions, and workplace outcomes. It affects the retention of experienced nurses and is an important determinant of nurses’ job satisfaction, promotability, and nursing competency [7]. A meta-analysis found that career plateau was linked to counterproductive work behavior, job insecurity, psychological distress, and higher turnover intentions. It was also found to be associated with age, sex, tenure, race, and role ambiguity [3]. Most employees strive to be seen as competent, high-performing employees who are worthy of promotion, greater access to resources, career opportunities, rewards, and recognition. Opportunities for such recognition serve as a powerful motivation when comparing one’s own performance achievements with those of one’s peers [8]. Studies have reported that both nurses and healthcare professionals perceive a high level of career plateau [9, 10]. As a result of upward social comparison, they may perceive some people as more advanced in their careers and superior to them, which can lead to negative emotions.

Emotions felt by employees in the workplace play an important role in the overall productivity of both employees and organizations [11]. Envy is a negative feeling that arises when people see others like themselves with something they want, making them feel inferior or disadvantaged [12, 13]. An individual’s poor relationship with their supervisor and personality traits, socio-demographic characteristics, and many other individual, group, and organizational factors have also been shown to predict envy [14–17]. The literature shows that envy in the workplace is associated with dysfunctional outcomes for both individuals and organizations [16]. It has been reported that envy can have a negative impact on individuals’ behaviors, emotions, attitudes and perceptions of the organization [18–22]. Although the first studies on envy considered it to be a negative emotion only [13], there is now a recognition that there are two distinct forms of envy, benign and malicious envy [11, 17, 23]. Benign envy involves a longing for self-improvement and imitation of the envied person, entailing feelings, thoughts, and tendencies meant to improve the envious person’s own outcome. Malicious envy, on the other hand, involves hostile feelings, thoughts, intentions, and tendencies meant to damage the superior position of the envied [23, 24]. Benign envy is linked to hope for success, whereas malicious envy is linked to fear of failure and diminished hope of success [25]. Both forms of envy, however, are an attempt to overcome the competitive disadvantage that one experiences [24].

According to the literature, envy is always based on upward social comparison [23, 25]. According to social comparison theory, people try to understand their own position in relation to others through comparison [26]. As a result of social comparison, individuals have an idea about their own characteristics, how they perceive the world, and their place in it [11]. Zurriaga et al. [16] reported that upward social comparison and unpleasant evaluation of oneself as compared with others (those whom one thinks are superior to oneself) were precursors of envy.

It has been reported that several factors may result in the formation of either benign or malicious envy. Research indicates that malicious envy is less prevalent when individuals regard the superior party as competent, well-liked, and deserving of their status. Furthermore, this tendency decreases if individuals believe they can attain similar levels of achievement and perceive themselves as possessing strong self-efficacy [11, 27–29]. It has been shown that an individual’s poor relationship with their superior, personality traits, and socio-demographic characteristics predict envy, and many other individual, group, and organizational factors predict or influence envy [14–22, 24, 30].

Social comparison and malicious envy are common among nurses, and envy is a powerful emotion that affects human communication and behavior in organizations [12]. Envy is reported to occur among nurses [12], yet the nursing literature lacks sufficient research on its antecedents or consequences, which limits understanding of envy and the development of strategies to address it. However, nurses also make social comparisons in their environment. Opportunities for recognition, such as greater access to resources, rewards and recognition, promotion and career opportunities, serve as powerful motivators when comparing one’s own performance to that of peers [8]. Individuals try to make sense of their position in the organization by comparing themselves to their peers who have greater access to resources, are rewarded and valued, have career opportunities and are promoted. As a result of upward social comparison, they may perceive some individuals as more advanced and superior in their careers, and their feelings of envy may be triggered. As a result, they may feel that they cannot develop their careers as much as their superiors (job content plateau) or that they can no longer advance in the hierarchy (hierarchical plateau).

According to Evans et al. [31], envy in nursing is an emotion that can negatively affect effective care delivery by distracting nurses and preventing them from fully concentrating on their work and therefore can have harmful consequences in the healthcare system [31]. Understanding the causes and consequences of envy in nursing is imperative [12]. Interestingly, there are no studies on the development of career opportunities and envy in the workplace and in the field of nursing, or whether envy plays a role in the formation of the perception of a career plateau. This research aims to advance the literature on envy and career plateau by examining the association between these two factors among nurses, thereby addressing a gap in our understanding of their relationship. A better understanding of the relationship between envy and career plateau and related factors may benefit both organizations and their employees. The aim of this study was to determine the effect of nurses’ individual characteristics, job characteristics, and levels of workplace envy on their perceptions of career plateau.

Purpose and research questions

The purpose of our study was to determine the effect of nurses’ personal and professional characteristics and workplace envy level on perceptions of career plateau.

The research questions of this study were:

Is there a relationship between malicious envy, benign envy, and career plateau among nurses?Do nurses’ personal and professional characteristics, malicious envy, and benign envy predict career plateau?

Methods

Study design, sample, and settings

This cross-sectional and exploratory analysis study was conducted using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).

Data were collected between November 2nd, 2021, and March 15th, 2022. This study was conducted across the country, not in specific hospitals. The inclusion criteria were having at least 1 year of experience in the current job, and being willing to participate in the study. Individuals with less than 1 year of experience in the current job were excluded.

According to the Statistical Yearbook of the Ministry of Health [32], there are 227,292 nurses actively working in Türkiye (Ministry of Health, 2021). This study’s population consisted of 227,292 nurses who were actively working in Türkiye [32]. The formula of n = Nt²pq/(d²(N-1) + t²pq) was calculated with a 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, and 50% incidence (p) rate, which is the maximum value in sample calculation. According to the sampling method with known population, the minimum sample size of the study was determined as 384 nurses, with a 95% confidence interval and 5% error. The final sample consisted of 431 nurses who voluntarily completed the questionnaire. The study used convenience sampling. All participants gave informed consent for participation and were informed that their anonymity was protected.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

Participants were asked about their sex, age, marital status, educational level, total years of experience in nursing, total years of experience in their current hospital, hospital type, and working unit.

Career plateau survey (CPS)

The CPS, developed by Milliman [33] and adapted into Turkish by Ak & Soybali [34], is a self-report scale consisting of 12 questions and two sub-dimensions. The first six questions measure job content plateau, and the last six questions measure hierarchical plateau. The scale is a 6-point Likert-type scale (1 - Strongly Disagree, 6 - Strongly Agree). Responses to the first, second, fourth, fifth, sixth, eighth, and twelfth statements in the scale were reverse scored. The mean scores of the sub-dimensions (rather than the total score of the scale) were used in the regression analysis. High mean scores indicate a high level of perceived internal/external career plateau. Scale averages vary between one and six. The CPS Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficient was 0.86 in the Turkish form of the scale [36] and 0.72 in this study’s total scale.

The benign and malicious envy scale (BeMaS-T)

Developed by Lange and Crusius [25] and adapted into Turkish by Çırpan and Özdoğru [35], BeMaS-T is a self-report, 6-point Likerty-type scale (1 - Strongly Disagree, 6 - Strongly Agree). The scale consists of 10 items concerning benign and malicious envy, with five items in each category. The internal consistency coefficient for the malicious envy factor was 0.89 in the original version of the scale and 0.86 in the Turkish adaptation. For the benign envy factor, the internal consistency coefficient was 0.85 in the original form and 0.78 in the Turkish form. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient was 0.75 for benign envy and 0.85 for malicious envy. High scores in the scales indicate a high perception of malicious or benign envy. Scale averages can vary between one and six.

Turkish versions of the scales were used in the study. Permission to use the scale was obtained via email from the authors who conducted the validity and reliability study and adapted the scale to Turkish.

Data collection

Data were collected using an online Google form. Nurses were invited to participate in the study via social media, and an online questionnaire was sent three times in 15-day intervals. The first page of the questionnaire contained the “Informed Consent Form,” the end of which included “I agree” and “I do not agree” options, indicating participants’ consent to participate in the study. Participants were able to access the questions only after confirming consent for participation. The questionnaire contained no personal identifiers of the participants and took approximately 10 min to complete.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in Brazil, 2013) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Istanbul University Faculty of Medicine (Application date: 01.10.2021, Number: 512239, File number: 2021/1718, Approval date 25.10.2021–568870). All participants gave informed consent for participation and were informed that their anonymity was protected. The first page of the online survey informed potential participants about the purpose of the study and clarified that participation was voluntary. Those who volunteered to participate in the study could access the survey after clicking the “yes, I agree” box, and the survey did not include any identifying information.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 software package (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analyses in this study. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test the normality of scores obtained from continuous variables. In addition to descriptive statistical methods (number, percentage, mean, median, standard deviation), the independent sample t-test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to test differences between the groups. In groups for which the difference was significant as a result of ANOVA analysis, multiple comparisons were made using the Tukey test. The level of relationship between two continuous variables was examined using Pearson’s correlation test. In addition, multiple linear regression analysis was used to determine the effect of independent factors on dependent variables. The results were evaluated with 95% confidence intervals, and significance was evaluated at the p < 0.05 level.

Discussion

This study investigated how nurses’ personal and professional attributes, as well as their levels of malicious and benign envy, influenced perceptions of career plateau. The findings indicate that marital status significantly impacted on perceptions of job content career plateau and malicious envy. Additionally, factors such as the employing institution and willingness to enter the profession were found to affect nurses’ experiences of hierarchical career plateau.

According to the results of the study, only marital status is included among the predictors of job content plateau, which is one of the dependent variables. In the present study, as in the meta-analytic study by Hu et al. [3], marital status was found to influence job content plateau and being married increased job content plateau. A study by Liu et al. [10] also showed that married primary caregivers were more likely to encounter career plateau. Although the finding that being married increases job content career plateau was supported by some studies [3, 36], others reported that there was no relationship between the two [3, 37]. Considering that work-life conflict has a significant positive effect on both hierarchical plateau and job content career plateau [38], and that work-life conflict of married nurses is higher than that of single nurses [15], and the desire for work-life balance may lead to career plateau [27], it can be concluded that being married is a variable that may affect job content career plateau. In addition to their roles and responsibilities in the hospital, nurses try to achieve a work-life balance because their roles and responsibilities increase with marriage.

Nurses must cope with the workload and stress of their professional lives, as well as the responsibilities of caring for their homes and children. Developing or changing job content may not be attractive to individuals for several reasons (e.g., expected work-life conflict and increased work demands, possibility of increased workload, enjoyment of current job) [39, 40]. Taking on multiple roles can result in time and energy constraints, which limits the resources available for professional development. Married nurses may have to devote time and energy that would otherwise be spent on professional development to family responsibilities. This can reduce internal motivation, increase burnout, and make it difficult to find new goals or meaning in one’s career. In such a situation, nurses may not be able to make demands such as changing and improving their job content, even if they want to, and may feel that they are in a plateau of job content. Therefore, it can be said that the underlying mechanism behind the relationship between marriage and career plateau is disruption to work-family balance. Providing organizational support to nurses who desire professional development may help prevent job content career plateaus.

Predictors of perceived hierarchical plateau, which was the other dependent variable of this study, included willingness to enter the profession, working institution, and perceived malicious envy. We found that entering nursing reluctantly increased nurses’ perceptions of hierarchical career plateau. However, there is no study in the literature that directly examines this relationship. Nurses may choose the nursing profession for reasons such as personal interest, desire to help others, ease of finding a job, living conditions, family pressure, and chance or suitability according to university entrance exam scores [41, 42]. Some studies in Türkiye have reported that most nursing students prefer nursing because of the ease of finding a job [43, 44]. Love for the profession is one of the values of the nursing profession. This has important implications for the profession and the provision of professional services [45]. In a comparative study of American and Turkish nursing students, it was found that American students primarily considered job fit and goals when choosing a career, whereas Turkish nursing students primarily considered living conditions [46]. Zhu and Li [20–23] reported that those who did not value their job too much were more likely to reach a career plateau [47].

As career interest is a component of career adaptation, nurses who choose nursing without career interest may have difficulty adapting to their careers and may not make the necessary investments to advance in the profession. Shaber et al. [48] argued that career adjustment reduced career plateau. Hu et al. [3] also confirmed that both career and job adjustment affected career plateau. Zhu et al. [47] reported that nurses who entered the profession without much enthusiasm might have low motivation to learn the profession. Nurses who enter the profession willingly may have invested more in the profession, which may result in higher motivation to learn, career adjustment, and job satisfaction. Conversely, individuals who enter the nursing profession without strong motivation may experience limited career alignment and engagement. This lack of investment in professional development or participation in activities that enhance promotability can contribute to an increased perception of hierarchical career plateau.

The present study also found that nurses working in university hospitals displayed higher perceptions of hierarchical career plateau than those working in private and public hospitals. No studies to date have compared perceptions of career plateau in employees working in different types of organizations. However, in a qualitative study examining the reasons for nurse resignations in private, public university, and public state hospitals, nurse managers of public university hospitals pointed to the absence of objective criteria for promotion and inequalities present in the promotion process [49]. In Türkiye, hierarchical progression in university hospitals is more limited than in public and private hospitals. This is because, while nurses working in public hospitals can transfer to another public hospital if hierarchical progression is not possible, there is much less opportunity to transfer to other hospitals for nurses working in university hospitals. Resignation is typically not considered a preferred course of action for nurses employed in university hospitals. This is due to the fact that, similar to their counterparts in public hospitals, these nurses hold ‘permanent staff’ status and benefit from job security protections. Choosing to resign would mean relinquishing these established job security rights. Dissatisfaction with career opportunities [50] has been identified as a factor influencing turnover. Working in university hospitals may be associated with an increased perception of hierarchical career plateau among nurses becase nurses in these settings may remain in their positions even when they are not satisfied with their career progression.

According to our results, as malicious envy increases, hierarchical career plateau decreases. To date, no study has directly focused on malicious envy and hierarchical career plateau. It has been reported that in a work environment where personal resources and equal opportunities are accessible, individuals’ career plateau levels may be low, whereas in the presence of perceptions of favouritism and discrimination, career plateau levels may be high [51], and career barriers increase career plateau [52, 53]. When the presence of career barriers and undesirable practices such as favouritism and discrimination are perceived, perceptions of career plateau may increase in nurses who do not have the resources to overcome these barriers and believe that these barriers cannot be overcome. Conversely, as highlighted in the literature, an individual’s assessment of whether a person perceived as superior or advantaged (for instance, someone receiving a promotion) merits the advancement, possesses the necessary competence, and has control over the factors contributing to this advantage may be shaped by feelings of envy [11, 25, 27]. Gan (2022) emphasized that injustice, not being deserving, feeling that one’s control was not sufficient, could trigger envy in nurses [12]. In such a situation, nurses’ negative perceptions may lead to high levels of malicious envy because of social comparisons with those experiencing hierarchical advancement. However, high levels of malicious envy do not necessarily correspond to high perceptions of a hierarchical plateau. Individuals who value promotion, believe they have adequate resources, and consider obstacles in the work environment surmountable may not view promotion as unattainable. In addition, it is known that people with high levels of malicious envy may not feel hopeless about their career progression or perceive a career plateau because they may resort to organizational tactics (even unethical ones) that can turn the current situation in their favor, in order to have the advantages of people they see as superior to them. Malicious envy in nurses is also a predictor of turnover [53, 54]. If nurses with high malicious envy perceive that there is no possibility of hierarchical advancement in the hospital where they work, they may not want to work with a high career plateau because they are likely to leave their job. Therefore, nurses with high malicious envy who continue to work may be more likely to try other methods of hierarchical advancement and may perceive less of a career plateau.

Limitations

Because the study is cross-sectional, it reflects a momentary situation and does not examine changes and developments over time. Longitudinal studies may provide more comprehensive information about the relationship between the main variables in the study. The limited number of studies on envy and career plateau in nurses caused limitations in the discussion of the findings.

Implications and recommendations

Career plateau is an important problem in nursing and may negatively affect nurses’ job satisfaction, potentially leading to the loss of experienced and skilled nurses. Therefore, it is important that institutional and nurse managers assess nurses’ perceptions of career plateau and the factors affecting it. To prevent nurses from reaching a plateau in their careers, institutions must play an active role in supporting their professional development.

Based on the findings, it appears that married nurses need organizational support to mitigate the effects of work-family conflict. Family-friendly policies, such as flexible working hours and shift arrangements that consider family responsibilities, short-term or part-time work options, flexible leave policies, and childcare support, can help married nurses achieve work-family balance. These policies enable nurses to obtain career advancement opportunities more easily, allowing them to devote more time to their families and professional development. This reduces the risk of career stagnation related to job content. Hospital management can support nurses in experiencing a sense of renewal and progress in their work content by offering continuous education, mentoring, and opportunities to acquire new skills, as well as certification and rotation opportunities that encourage professional development. These factors increase internal motivation, and diversity in the distribution of tasks and responsibilities prevents monotony and stagnation. Additionally, a fair and empathetic managerial approach, coupled with strengthened psychosocial support mechanisms, fosters married nurses’ institutional commitment and helps them overcome professional development obstacles. On the other hand, given the impact of personal factors, such as marital status, on career plateaus, nurse managers may find it beneficial to implement individualized career counseling and development policies within their institutions.

Before deciding to become a nurse, factors such as interest in nursing, compatibility of nursing with personal goals, knowledge of the profession, and compatibility with professional values should be assessed. Providing guidance on these matters to prospective nurses before they enrol in nursing schools may help individuals who are interested in the profession make informed choices and could reduce career stagnation after entering the field. Special strategies should be employed during vocational education training to help students who did not voluntarily choose nursing adopt professional values and principles, thereby fostering a strong professional identity. Additionally, programs that foster professional identity in the workplace help individuals align their career goals with their values, interests, and abilities.

In line with these findings, nurse managers may plan interventions designed to reduce career plateau and improve both nurse and institutional outcomes. In this context, some suggestions can be made to nurse managers and hospital managers to reduce envy and career plateau: Factors that may lead to career plateau and envy should be investigated by evaluating the work environment. In this way, areas for improvement can be identified and improved, and a positive working environment can be created. Human resource practices in line with management ethical principles can be implemented to improve nurses’ perceptions of organizational justice. To prevent career stagnation among nurses, healthcare institutions should establish transparent and fair promotion systems, clarify promotion criteria, and provide regular feedback. In addition, management roles, clinical expertise, nursing education, and horizontal career paths should be supported. Nurses’ development should be encouraged through rotation, temporary leadership roles, mentoring, coaching programs, and transparent announcements of vacant positions to provide employees with opportunities. Efforts can be made to reduce competitive practices among staff. To help nurses get to know each other better, activities can be planned to improve communication between nurses. Managers can be sensitive to the feelings, attitudes, and perceptions of the nurses with whom they work.

The finding that career plateau is low in environments where malicious envy is high should not be ignored because it may be caused by different antecedent or mediating factors affecting this relationship that were not examined here. Therefore, it is suggested that future quantitative and/or qualitative research on the relationship between envy and career plateau should include different factors that may affect this relationship.

Conclusions

This study showed that the status of being married increased nurses’ perception of job content career plateau, and having reluctantly chosen the profession, employment at a university hospital, and decreased levels of malicious envy increased hierarchical career plateau. Malicious envy and high levels of perceived career plateau are not desirable situations in hospitals because they have the potential to negatively affect patient care, nurses, and the organization, and effective interventions should be put in place to reduce them.
