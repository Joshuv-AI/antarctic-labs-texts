Rising trends in mortality due to cocaine use and cardiovascular diseases in the United States: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Muneeb Khawar, Sardar Muhammad Imran Khan, Muhammad Waqas, Muhammad Usman Mahmood, Abdul Qadeer, Muhammad Aizaz Ashraf, Hasnain Farooq, Malik Sohail Riaz, Mirza Muhammad Hadeed Khawar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cocaine use and cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related mortality trends in the U.S. are underreported. Using the CDC’s Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research database, we analyzed the relationship between cocaine use and CVD-related deaths.Methods:We analyzed death certificates from the Multiple Cause of Death dataset to identify CVD-related deaths involving cocaine use. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 1 000 000 and annual percentage changes (APCs) were calculated using Joinpoint Regression.Results:From 1999 to 2022, 82 944 deaths were linked to cocaine use and CVDs. AAMRs rose from 9.6 in 1999 to 32 in 2022. Mortality trends varied by demographics and regions. Males and females showed early increases (1999–2006), declines (2006–2012), and sharp rises (2012–2022), with APCs of 11.8% and 12.8%, respectively. Non-Hispanic Black individuals exhibited consistently higher rates than non-Hispanic Whites, though both groups experienced sharp increases starting in 2014 (APCs: 12.5 and 12.7%). Geographic trends revealed early 2000s increases, mid-2000s declines, and rapid rises beginning in the 2010s. The Northeast and South had notable APCs of 13.5 and 12.6% (2013–2022). Rural areas initially showed steeper increases than urban areas but displayed steady upward trends since 2011 (APC: 9.5%). Age-wise, older adults experienced a particularly sharp rise from 2012 to 2022 (APC: 15.0%).Conclusion:Cocaine use and CVD-related mortality rose significantly from 1999 to 2022, with pronounced increases among men, non-Hispanic Black individuals, older adults, urban residents, and those in the South. These findings highlight a growing public health crisis.

Introduction

Cocaine use is associated with various cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), such as myocardial infarction, heart failure, cardiomyopathies, arrhythmias, aortic dissection, and endocarditis[1–3]. Extended cocaine use negatively impacts the cardiovascular, neurological, and mental health of individuals[3]. Approximately 0.5% of the global population has been reported to have used cocaine in the last year, with recent data indicating that 4.77 million Americans have used cocaine in the past year, and 40.9 million Americans have used cocaine at some point in their lives[4]. Over the last several years, the number of deaths due to cocaine overdose has increased in the United States, with nearly 27 569 deaths reported in 2022[5].

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the harmful effects of cocaine use on the cardiovascular system[6]. Trends in mortality associated with substance use and CVDs have also been discussed[7]. Nonetheless, the changes over time in CVD-related mortality linked to cocaine have not been thoroughly investigated. Although prior studies have established the physiological effects of cocaine on the cardiovascular system, such as accelerated atherosclerosis and increased risk of acute coronary events, there remains a gap in understanding long-term temporal trends in cocaine-associated CVD mortality across diverse demographics in the United States. This study justifies the need for updated epidemiological insights by analyzing trends from 1999–2022 using CDC WONDER data, stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, age, urban-rural status, and census regions. Unlike previous research focused on general substance use and CVD, this analysis specifically quantifies cocaine’s role, revealing pronounced increases in vulnerable groups (e.g., non-Hispanic Black individuals and older adults) and estimating annual percentage changes (APCs) to inform targeted public health interventions. This adds novel value by highlighting geographic and demographic disparities, which can guide policy amid rising cocaine overdose deaths (e.g., 27 569 in 2022). Moreover, it remains uncertain whether the trends in cocaine use and mortality from CVDs differ according to sex, ethnicity, and geographic location within the United States.

Consequently, we analyzed these trends in cocaine use and deaths related to CVDs in the United States by using the Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC-WONDER) database from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Methods

Study setting

This is a retrospective type of study in which we obtained and used the data provided by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), available through the CDC-WONDER Database, to observe annual mortality trends from 1999 to 2022[8]. The data are annually updated using death certificates of U.S. residents, which provide knowledge about both the underlying cause of death and associated demographic information. The data were extracted for individuals aged 25 years and above, with a population size exceeding 1 000 000. Utilizing the final Multiple Cause of Death Public Use Record and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes– specifically I00-I99 for CVD-related mortalities and F14, R78.2, and T40.5 for mortalities associated with cocaine use– similar codes and age cutoffs were used by a previous study as well[7]. These ICD-10 codes were specifically used to query the CDC-WONDER database for deaths where CVDs (I00-I99) were listed as the underlying or contributing cause, and cocaine involvement was indicated by F14 (mental and behavioral disorders due to cocaine use), R78.2 (finding of cocaine in blood), or T40.5 (poisoning by cocaine). We identified death certificates listing these conditions as underlying or contributing causes of death. Our research study did not require approval from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) as it was based on anonymized and publicly available data. Furthermore, the research strictly adhered to the STROBE guidelines[9]. To ensure the transparency of the article, we adhered to the TITAN 2025 checklist on the declaration and use of artificial intelligence in research[10].

Data extraction

The extensive dataset used for analysis included a wide range of demographic variables, which encompassed sex, race/ethnicity, age groups, census region, and urban-rural classification. Race/ethnicity groups were categorized as non-Hispanic (NH) White and NH Black or African American. Mortality trends for gender, racial, and age groups were calculated from 1999 to 2022, whereas trends for rural-urban and census region disparities were calculated from 1999 to 2020. To classify age-related disparities in trends, age groups were divided into young adults (25–54 years old) and older adults (55 years and above). Additionally, mortality trends from cocaine use and CVDs were interpreted based on different census regions in the United States (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West) from 1999 to 2020, and specific county-level urbanization classifications (rural: micropolitan and noncore regions; urban: large central metro, large fringe metro, medium metro, and small metro regions) from 1999 to 2020[11].

Statistical analysis

Cocaine use and CVDs related to AAMRs were obtained. AAMR checks for the population’s change in age distribution, allowing data comparison, and was plotted using the direct method of adjustment with the 2000 standard population[12]. The Joinpoint Regression Program (Joinpoint version 5.1.0, National Cancer Institute) was employed to analyze age-adjusted mortality trends from 1999 to 2022[13]. This program utilizes serial permutation tests to examine repeated time trends and identifies up to a single inflection point where the rate of change in mortality is statistically significantly different. Successively, the program calculates the weighted average APC for each time segment in the AAMR, along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). An APC estimate was determined to highlight an increase or decrease if the slope of the trend significantly differed from zero; otherwise, the trend was denoted as stable. A pairwise comparison was conducted to determine whether the differences in APCs were significantly different across various subgroups (sex, race, census regions, and urbanization). A statistically significant trend change was denoted by a P-value of < 0.05.

Results

Mortality rates across all demographic groups exhibited significant temporal trends throughout the study period. From 1999 to 2022, a total of 82 944 deaths among adults (25 years of age and above) were reported due to cocaine use and CVDs. From 1999 to 2022, men displayed a total of 63 124 deaths, whereas women had 19 820 deaths during this period (Supplemental Digital Content Table 1, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296). In race-stratified groups from 1999 to 2020, the total recorded deaths for the NH White and NH Black populations were 33 390 deaths and 39 409 deaths, respectively.

The overall AAMR for cocaine use and CVD-related deaths was 9.6 in 1999 and increased to 32 in 2022 (Supplemental Digital Content Table 3, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296). Overall mortality rates exhibited a comparable trajectory, rising from 1999 to 2006 (APC: 11.5, 95% CI: 9.6 to 13.3), declining from 2006 to 2009 (APC: −12.7, 95% CI: −15.4 to −7.7), and experiencing a sharp rise again from 2014 to 2022 (APC: 12.9, 95% CI: 11.7 to 14.7) (Fig. 1 and Supplemental Digital Content Table 8, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296). Gender disparities

Males consistently presented with higher AAMRs compared to females (Supplemental Digital Content Table 2, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296). An initial increase in mortality rates was noted from 1999 to 2006 (APC: 10.8, 95% CI: 8.3 to 13.9), followed by a decline from 2006 to 2012 (APC: −8.4, 95% CI: −11.8 to −5.9). However, from 2012 to 2022, there was a considerable rise in male mortality rates (APC: 11.8, 95% CI: 10.7 to 13.1) (Fig. 1).

Female mortality rates displayed a similar pattern, with an increase from 1999 to 2006 (APC: 11.6, 95% CI: 9.7 to 14.2), accompanied by a decline from 2006 to 2009 (APC: −13.8, 95% CI: −16.7 to −7.7), and a marked increase from 2014 to 2022 (APC: 12.8, 95% CI: 11.4 to 15.2).

Racial disparities

Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) for NH Black individuals were consistently greater than those for NH White individuals throughout the entire study duration (Supplemental Digital Content Table 4, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296). Among NH Black individuals, mortality rates increased from 1999 to 2006, showing an APC of 8.4 (95% CI: 6.6 to 10.9). This was followed by a significant decline from 2006 to 2009 (APC: −13.8, 95% CI: −16.8 to −7.0) and a phase of relative stability from 2009 to 2014 (APC: −0.9, 95% CI: −5.5 to 8.1). Subsequently, a considerable rise was noted from 2014 to 2022, with an APC of 12.5 (95% CI: 11.1 to 15.2).

For NH White individuals, the mortality rate also saw a significant increase from 1999 to 2006, with an APC of 15.8 (95% CI: 11.8 to 22.0). This was followed by a decrease from 2006 to 2012 (APC: −8.2, 95% CI: −14.2 to −4.8). Starting in 2012, mortality rates for NH White individuals demonstrated a noteworthy rise, with an APC of 12.7 (95% CI: 11.1 to 15.0), which continued until 2022 (Fig. 2). Urban-rural disparities

The analysis identified notable trends in mortality rates and APCs across three periods: 1999–2006, 2006–2012, and 2012–2020 for metropolitan areas, and 1999–2006, 2006–2011, and 2011–2020 for non-metropolitan areas. Metropolitan areas showed persistently higher AAMRs compared to non-metropolitan areas (Supplemental Digital Content Table 5, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296).

In metropolitan areas, there was an initial rise in mortality rates from 1999 to 2006, with an APC of 10.4 (95% CI: 7.9 to 14.0) (Fig. 3). This trend was followed by a significant decline in mortality rates from 2006 to 2012, exhibiting an APC of −8.5 (95% CI: −13.3 to −5.8). However, beginning in 2012, mortality rates in metropolitan areas again increased, reflecting a considerable rise with an APC of 11.3 (95% CI: 9.3 to 14.1) through to 2020. For non-metropolitan areas, the initial rise in the mortality rate registered an APC of 15.8 (95% CI: 12.1 to 21.8) between 1999 and 2006. This trend was followed by a phase of decline from 2006 to 2011, during which the APC was −11.0 (95% CI: −21.1 to −5.6). Subsequently, from 2011 to 2020, there was a renewed increase in the mortality rate, with an APC of 9.5 (95% CI: 7.0 to 13.7).

Age group disparities

Between 2000 and 2006, younger adults showed an initial increase in mortality rates, with an average annual percent change (APC) of 10.2 (95% CI: 8.3 to 12.5) (Fig. 4). This upward trend was subsequently followed by a significant decrease between 2006 and 2010, reflected by an APC of −12.9 (95% CI: −16.9 to −7.7). Mortality rates then stabilized between 2010 and 2014, exhibiting a minimal APC of −0.4 (95% CI: −8.4 to 7.6). However, beginning in 2014, mortality rates among younger adults began to rise again, displaying a marked APC of 11.1 (95% CI: 9.5 to 14.1) through to 2022. For older adults, mortality rates demonstrated a similarly significant increase from 2000 to 2006, with an APC of 17.6 (95% CI: 14.2 to 23.3), notably higher than that observed for younger adults during the same period. From 2006 to 2012, however, mortality rates among older adults stabilized, indicating a near-zero APC of 0 (95% CI: −6.1 to 3.8). Mortality rates started rising substantially in 2012 and continued to do so until 2022, when they reached their highest rate of increase throughout the study period, with an APC of 15.0 (95% CI: 14.0 to 16.5).

In conclusion, both younger and older adults have exhibited fluctuating patterns of rising and decreasing AAMRs (Supplemental Digital Content Table 7, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296). However, the period from 2012 to 2022 reveals a strong, concurrent upward trend in mortality across both age groups. During this time, the APC is slightly higher for older adults, indicating a steeper rise in mortality within this population.

Census region disparities

Each region exhibited distinct mortality rate trends (Supplemental Digital Content Table 6, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B296) and APCs across various periods, highlighting both similarities and differences in mortality patterns. In the Northeast, the mortality rate initially increased with an APC of 9.0 (95% CI: 5.6 to 18.4) from 1999 to 2006 (Fig. 5). This was followed by a slight decline between 2006 and 2013, with an APC of −1.4 (95% CI: −11.1 to 1.7). However, from 2013 onward, the Northeast experienced a significant rise in mortality, with an APC of 13.5 (95% CI: 11.6 to 16.3) up to 2022, marking one of the steepest increases among the regions in recent years. The Midwest also witnessed an initial increase from 1999 to 2006, reflected by an APC of 9.5 (95% CI: 6.5 to 13.0). From 2006 to 2012, the mortality rate decreased significantly, with an APC of −10.2 (95% CI: −14.9 to −6.9). Similar to the Northeast, the Midwest experienced a notable rise in mortality from 2012 to 2022, with an APC of 11.5 (95% CI: 10.0 to 13.3).

In the South, mortality rates initially increased from 1999 to 2006 at an APC of 15.0 (95% CI: 10.8 to 19.4), the highest initial APC among the regions. Between 2006 and 2010, the region saw a sharp decline with an APC of −11.5 (95% CI: −15.7 to −6.6), followed by a period of modest growth from 2010 to 2014 with an APC of 3.3 (95% CI: −1.5 to 8.3). From 2014 onwards, the South experienced a pronounced rise in mortality, with an APC of 12.6 (95% CI: 11.4 to 13.9) extending to 2022.

The West demonstrated a more moderate pattern, with an initial increase from 1999 to 2006, reflected in an APC of 6.4 (95% CI: 3.0 to 11.5). This was succeeded by a substantial decrease from 2006 to 2013, with an APC of −10.2 (95% CI: −16.7 to −7.0). However, similar to other regions, the West also displayed a significant increase from 2013 to 2022, with an APC of 11.6 (95% CI: 9.4 to 15.0) (Fig. 6). While all regions experienced fluctuations in mortality rates, the most recent years (2013–2022) have displayed a trend of rising mortality across all four regions, with particularly high APCs noted in the Northeast, South, and West. Discussion

The data reveals a significant increase in cocaine-related CVD mortality in the United States over the years, with notable peaks and declines. For example, between 1999 and 2006, there was a marked rise in mortality rates, followed by a temporary decline from 2006 to 2009, only to surge again from 2014 to 2022. A study by Hedegaard et al (2020) reported that the rate of drug overdose deaths involving cocaine was stable between 2009 and 2013, then nearly tripled from 1.6 per 100 000 in 2013 to 4.5 in 2018[14]. This pattern suggests potential shifts in cocaine use trends, changes in public health policy, or socio-environmental factors influencing drug availability and consumption[15]. This trend is consistent with reports from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, which noted a resurgence in cocaine use over the last decade (SAMHSA, 2020).

In terms of demographic disparities, our analysis indicates that mortality rates are significantly higher in men than in women. For example, by 2022, the AAMR for men reached 49.1 per 100 000, compared to 15.4 per 100 000 for women, highlighting a gender disparity. This disparity may be partially attributed to men’s higher prevalence of cocaine use, societal pressures, and increased cardiovascular susceptibility. According to the National Institute on Drug Abuse, men are more likely than women to use illicit drugs, including cocaine, which correlates with higher instances of overdose and emergency department visits among men[16]. However, our data show women experiencing sharper recent increases (AAPC 12.8% vs. 11.8% in men post-2012), possibly due to rising female cocaine use amid mental health stressors.

Additionally, racial analysis reveals pronounced mortality differences, particularly between NH Black and NH White populations. NH Black individuals experienced an AAMR of 117.2 per 100 000 in 2022, significantly exceeding the rate of 21.3 per 100 000 among NH White individuals. A previous study reported that in 2018, the rate of drug overdose deaths involving cocaine in the non-Hispanic Black population (9.0 per 100 000) was nearly twice that of the non-Hispanic White population (4.6) and three times that of the Hispanic population (3.0)[14]. Socioeconomic inequities, including limited access to preventive healthcare, systemic discrimination, and urban poverty concentration, likely contribute to these disparities. Research by Valasek et al (2023) underscores that structural socioeconomic factors amplify overdose disparities, particularly among NH Black populations[17]. This aligns with recent trends where non-Hispanic Black individuals had the highest cocaine-involved overdose rates (24.3 per 100 000 in 2023), driven by polysubstance use with opioids. Furthermore, higher incidences of hypertension and diabetes in NH Black adults increase their vulnerability to CVD[18]. These findings underscore the need to examine not only cocaine’s direct physiological impact but also the broader social determinants influencing mortality in these groups.

Geographic disparities show elevated mortality in urban metropolitan areas compared to rural areas. Metropolitan mortality rates rose from 10.5 per 100 000 in 1999 to 26.5 in 2020, while non-metropolitan rates peaked at 12.7 in the same year. This trend may be attributed to the greater accessibility of cocaine and emergency health services in urban settings, leading to higher documented mortality rates, as per the Urban–Rural Differences in Drug Overdose Death Rates, 1999–2019. Rural settings, with limited healthcare infrastructure, might underreport cocaine-related deaths, further accentuating the apparent urban-rural mortality divide. National data confirm higher stimulant-involved CVD mortality in non-metropolitan areas (AAPC 9.5% post-2011), linked to limited treatment access. These patterns suggest that targeted interventions must consider region-specific healthcare gaps, especially in metropolitan areas heavily impacted by drug use.

Age-stratified data reveal significant increases in cocaine-related CVD mortality among older adults (55 years and above), whose rates grew from 3.3 per 100 000 in 1999 to 37.6 in 2022, compared to younger adults. According to a previous study, in 2018 the rate of drug overdose deaths involving cocaine was highest for adults aged 35–44 and lowest for those aged 65 and over[14]. This trend could reflect a cumulative effect of prolonged substance use over time, compounded by aging-related vulnerabilities to CVD. Chronic cocaine use is associated with accelerated atherosclerosis and an incremental risk for acute coronary events, as indicated by elevated levels of C-reactive protein and von Willebrand factor[19]. Additionally, the sympathomimetic effects of cocaine, including increased heart rate and blood pressure, can exacerbate age-related cardiovascular vulnerabilities, leading to higher mortality rates in older populations[20]. These physiological effects underscore the heightened risk faced by older adults, as cumulative exposure to cocaine compounds the age-related cardiovascular vulnerabilities they experience.

The APC analysis shows periods of decline, notably between 2006 and 2009 (−12.7% overall), followed by significant increases, particularly post-2014. This pattern may correspond with shifts in drug availability and potency, as well as public health response measures that varied over time. Studies have noted similar fluctuations, highlighting the complex interplay of drug policy, enforcement, and prevention strategies[21]. The sharp rises in mortality from 2020 to 2022 may also be influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic, which exacerbated substance use disorders and CVD risks. During 2020 to 2022, U.S. CVD deaths increased by 9.3% overall, with excess deaths linked to disrupted healthcare access, increased isolation, and higher drug use[22–24]. Specifically, cocaine-involved overdoses rose amid pandemic stressors, with a 23% increase in stimulant use reported. In our data, the post-2019 acceleration (e.g., AAPC 12.9% from 2014 to 2022) aligns with national trends where COVID-19 compounded vulnerabilities, particularly in non-Hispanic Black and urban populations.

Limitations and strengths of the analysis

Data inconsistencies and reporting variability over time and across states may affect reliability. Additionally, the lack of granular data on socioeconomic status and specific drug use patterns by demographic subgroups limits our ability to fully attribute observed disparities to specific socioeconomic factors. The observational nature of the data restricts causal inference, and concurrent substance use of an unaccounted factor may influence cardiovascular mortality rates. Additionally, the CDC WONDER database lacks detailed clinical data, such as biomarkers (e.g., C-reactive protein or von Willebrand factor levels), which could provide mechanistic insights into cocaine’s cardiovascular effects[19]. Changes in ICD-10 coding practices over time, such as the October 2020 updates to synthetic opioid codes [e.g., splitting T40.4X into more specific categories including fentanyl (T40.41)], may have influenced reporting of drug-related deaths, potentially underestimating cocaine-specific trends if polysubstance use was miscoded[22]. These coding shifts could introduce bias in later years (e.g., 2020–2022), though our focus on cocaine-specific codes (T40.5, etc.) minimizes this for our analysis. Despite these limitations, the extended time frame of this analysis from 1999 to 2022 allows for a comprehensive view of trends over time, highlighting both periods of decline and resurgence in cocaine-related mortality.

Future directions

Future studies should explore the long-term cardiovascular effects of cocaine use in high-risk groups to understand cumulative impacts over time. Research focusing on socioeconomically disadvantaged communities could reveal specific environmental and systemic factors influencing mortality disparities. Additionally, evaluating the efficacy of community-based harm reduction services could offer insights into strategies that mitigate cocaine-related mortality. Investigating the combined effects of comorbid conditions, such as hypertension and diabetes, alongside cocaine use could inform tailored preventive health strategies.

In conclusion, the evolving trends in cocaine-related CVD mortality underscore the necessity for continuous monitoring and adaptive public health strategies. Addressing the multifaceted factors contributing to these trends is essential for mitigating the impact of cocaine on cardiovascular health.

Potential public health implications:

The findings have several implications for public health policy and targeted interventions. The persistently high mortality among certain demographics highlights the need for strategies tailored to high-risk groups, including non-Hispanic Black populations and urban residents. Programs aimed at increasing healthcare accessibility and education on drug risks in these communities are essential. Furthermore, policies that address the root causes of drug misuse, such as socioeconomic inequities and lack of community resources, could play a crucial role in mitigating these health outcomes. Community support systems, including harm reduction programs and accessible addiction treatment services, could reduce the burden of cocaine-related CVD mortality.
