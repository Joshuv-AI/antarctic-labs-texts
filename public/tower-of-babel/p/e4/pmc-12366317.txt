Variants of NLRP genes encoding subcortical maternal complex components are linked to biparental placental mesenchymal dysplasia
Authors: Ayaka Murase, Hiroyuki Mishima, Saori Aoki, Satoshi Hara, Musashi Kubiura-Ichimaru, Takashi Ohba, Koh-ichiro Yoshiura, Hidenobu Soejima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlacental mesenchymal dysplasia (PMD) is a placental abnormality resembling partial hydatidiform moles without trophoblastic proliferation. Although many PMD cases involve androgenetic/biparental mosaicism or chimerism, we recently reported that approximately 30% of cases retain biparental genomes (BiPMD) and exhibit aberrant methylation at multiple imprinted differentially methylated regions (DMRs). This resembles multilocus imprinting disturbances (MLIDs) and biparental hydatidiform moles (BiHMs), which are associated with pathogenic variants in subcortical maternal complex (SCMC) genes. However, the involvement of SCMC variants in BiPMD pathogenesis remains unclear.ResultsWe performed whole-exome sequencing on seven mothers with BiPMD during pregnancy, focusing on SCMC-related and DNA methylation maintenance genes. We identified compound heterozygous frameshift and missense variants in NLRP5 in one mother, and a heterozygous missense variant in NLRP2 in another. In silico predictions suggested that the NLRP5 frameshift variant was pathogenic, whereas the missense variants were deemed likely benign. Methylation specific-multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MS-MLPA) of placental tissues revealed aberrant methylation patterns in multiple imprinted DMRs. The affected DMRs varied between cases and within the same case, with abnormalities also observed in macroscopically normal placental regions.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that maternal variants in NLRP genes, which encode components of the SCMC, may contribute to the development of BiPMD with MLIDs. SCMC dysfunction due to SCMC gene mutations may cause aberrant methylation at imprinted DMRs in early embryos with cell-to-cell variation in the affected DMRs among cells, leading to a mosaic pattern of abnormal cells and normal cells. Through differentiation into placental tissues in this mosaic condition, BiPMD with aberrant methylation of multiple DMRs can occur. Taken together, our findings support the hypothesis that MLID in live-born individuals, BiPMD, and BiHMs may collectively represent a continuum within the MLID spectrum. Background

Placental mesenchymal dysplasia (PMD) is a placental abnormality characterized by cystic changes resembling a hydatidiform mole but without abnormal trophoblastic proliferation. It is often mistaken for a partial hydatidiform mole or a complete one with a coexisting normal fetus [1]. Although the fetus is typically normal, there is a high risk of complications, including fetal growth restriction, preterm delivery, fetal demise, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [2]. Additionally, approximately 20% of fetuses have Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (BWS), and about 20% of fetuses with BWS are associated with PMD [3]. The estimated prevalence of PMD is 0.02% [3, 4].

PMD is often associated with androgenetic/biparental mosaicism or chimerism [1]. However, our research has shown that ~ 30% of PMDs retain a biparental genome (BiPMD) [5]. In BiPMD, multiple placenta-specific and ubiquitous imprinted differentially methylated regions (DMRs) exhibit methylation abnormalities, mostly hypomethylation, resulting in abnormal expression of imprinted genes associated with these DMRs [5]. These multilocus methylation abnormalities resemble multilocus imprinting disturbances (MLIDs) and biparental hydatidiform moles (BiHMs). In mothers with MLIDs or a BiHM, pathogenic variants of subcortical maternal complex (SCMC)-related genes have been reported [6]. However, the relationship between methylation abnormalities in BiPMD and SCMC gene variants remains unclear.

The SCMC is a protein complex with core components (NLRP5, TLE6, OOEP) and additional components (KHDC3L, NLRP2, NLRP4f, ZBED3, PADI6) [7–9]. These are exclusively expressed from the maternal genome in oocytes and early embryos and degrade during further embryonic development without compensation by the embryonic genome. SCMCs and periodic filaments together form cytoplasmic lattices (CPLs), which have a large surface area [10]. CPLs associate with many proteins critical for embryonic development, including those involved in epigenetic reprogramming during preimplantation embryonic development [7–9]. Pathogenic variants of SCMC-related genes NLRP2, NLRP5, NLRP7, and PADI6 have been identified as causative of MLIDs and BiHMs [6, 7]. Homozygous or compound-heterozygous pathogenic variants of NLRP7 occur in approximately 75% of patients with a BiHM, whereas pathogenic variants of KHDC3L account for 5–10% of such cases [7]. Variants of PADI6 have also been reported in patients with a BiHM [11–13].

We performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) on seven mothers with BiPMD pregnancies to investigate the involvement of SCMC gene variants in BiPMD, revealing variants of NLRP genes. Methylation analyses identified aberrantly methylated DMRs in BiPMD tissues resembling those in MLIDs and BiHMs. Further, the patterns of aberrantly methylated DMRs varied among tissues analyzed. These findings suggest that NLRP gene variants contribute to BiPMD and its pathogenesis.

Methods

We collected the data and samples of 49 patients diagnosed with PMD across Japan between 2009 and 2021 [2, 5]. Of these, the peripheral blood of seven mothers with BiPMD pregnancies was available for DNA extraction (Supplementary Table S1). The mother in case PMD-020 had a history of miscarriage, whereas no miscarriages were reported in the other cases, although one mother had an unknown history. We also extracted DNA from the paternal peripheral blood, PMD placental tissue, and umbilical-cord blood, when available. DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Of the seven cases, six were identified as BiPMD using the CytoScan HD Array Kit and one using the GlobalFiler PCR Amplification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) [5]. For case PMD-020, since the placenta included macroscopically normal (macro-normal) and macroscopically PMD (macro-PMD) regions, we took samples from two sites each in the macro-normal (macro-normal 1 and 2) and macro-PMD (macro-PMD A and B) regions. For PMD-030, in which the entire placenta was macro-PMD, we sampled a single site.

We performed WES on all seven mothers. Libraries enriched using the SureSelect Human All Exon V8 + NCV System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeqX System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The read data were processed using the NVIDIA Clara Parabricks software package version 3.7.0–1 (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the hg38 reference genome. For candidate genes (NLRP2, NLRP5, NLRP7, KHDC3L, PADI6, TLE6, OOEP, UHRF1, DPPA3, ZAR1, ZFP57, TRIM28, and ZNF445), variants meeting the following two criteria were prioritized: [1] rarity in populations (< 1% in both 38KJPN v2 and gnomAD v3.1.2) and [2] a SnpEff-predicted impact of HIGH or MODERATE. Variants thus identified were validated using Sanger sequencing, using the primer sequences listed in Supplementary Table S2. The pathogenicity of identified variants was evaluated using the following prediction tools: MutationTaster, SIFT, ENTPRISE-X, PolyPhen-2, Mutation Assessor, FATHMM, PROVEAN, M-CAP, PrimateAI, DEOGEN2, ClinPred, LRT Detector, and CADD (https://cadd.bihealth.org). CADD PHRED scores were defined as follows: BP4 (strong) ≤ 0.15; 0.15 < BP4 (moderate) ≤ 17.3; 17.3 < BP4 (supporting) ≤ 22.7; 25.3 ≤ PP3 (supporting) < 28.1; and PP3 (moderate) ≥ 28.1 [14, 15].

We performed multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) using the ME034 kit (MRC Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands), following the manufacturer’s instructions, to analyze the methylation status and copy number of 13 imprinted DMRs scattered across the genome. For methylation specific-MLPA (MS-MLPA), genomic DNA (500 ng) was subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA).

Results

After filtering the WES data, we identified compound-heterozygous frameshift and missense variants of NLRP5 (NM_153447:c.[2572_2573insT, p.C858Lfs*2];[2895C > G, p.N965K]) in the mother in case PMD-020 and a heterozygous missense variant of NLRP2 (NM_001174081:c.1287C > G, p.F429L) in the mother in PMD-030. We conducted Sanger sequencing to verify whether these variants were transmitted to the placenta or umbilical-cord blood (Fig. 1). We detected them in the mothers’ peripheral blood but not in the fathers. The NLRP5 frameshift variant, but not the missense variant, was found in the two macro-PMD regions and umbilical-cord blood in PMD-020. The NLRP2 missense variant was identified in the placental tissue in PMD-030.

Results of Sanger sequencing. A Both the frameshift variant (c.2572_2573insT) and the missense variant (c.2895C > G) of NLRP5 were detected in the mother but not in the father in case PMD-020. The frameshift variant was transmitted by the mother to macro-PMD placental tissues A and B and to the cord blood, whereas the missense variant was not transmitted to any tissues. B The missense variant of NLRP2 (c.1287C > G) was detected in the mother but not in the father in case PMD-030. It was transmitted by the mother to the macro-PMD placental tissue.

These variants were not registered in the ClinVar archive. We therefore analyzed them using in silico prediction tools and evaluated them according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG-AMP) criteria (Supplementary Table S3) [15]. The NLRP5 frameshift variant was predicted as damaging by MutationTaster, SIFT, and ENTPRISE-X and met ACMG-AMP criteria PVS1, PM2, and PP3, resulting in a "very strong" pathogenicity classification. For the two missense variants, some prediction tools classified them as damaging or nonfunctional, while others classified them as benign, tolerated, or functional, corresponding to ACMG-AMP criteria PP3/BP4 ("supporting"). To classify them, we calculated their CADD PHRED scores (v1.7) following the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group recommendations [14]; the NLRP5 variant scored 0.004 (BP4_strong) and the NLRP2 variant scored 14.21 (BP4_moderate). We therefore assumed both to be benign.

We analyzed the relationship between these variants and methylation abnormalities at imprinting loci using MLPA and MS-MLPA. MLPA analysis revealed the normal two copies in all tissues analyzed (Supplementary Figure S1). MS-MLPA revealed no methylation abnormalities in the peripheral-blood DNA of the parents in either case (Supplementary Figure S2). In PMD-020, methylation abnormalities were detected at multiple imprinting loci within the macro-PMD tissues (Fig. 2). At macro-PMD-A, we observed hypomethylation in three DMRs (H19/IGF2:IG-DMR, KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR, and MEG8:Int2-DMR) and hypermethylation in one DMR (GNAS-AS1:TSS-DMR). At macro-PMD-B, we identified hypomethylation in three DMRs (GRB10:alt-TSS-DMR, GNAS-XL:Ex1-DMR, and GNAS A/B:TSS-DMR) in addition to those identified in macro-PMD-A. We also detected methylation abnormalities at macro-normal-1 (two DMRs: H19/IGF2:IG-DMR and MEG8:Int2-DMR) and macro-normal-2 (two other DMRs: GRB10:alt-TSS-DMR and GNAS A/B:TSS-DMR). In the umbilical-cord blood, we found hypomethylation in three DMRs (GRB10:alt-TSS-DMR, KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR, and GNAS-XL:Ex1-DMR). In PMD-030, the macro-PMD region exhibited hypomethylation in two DMRs (H19/IGF2:IG-DMR and MEG8:Int2-DMR) and hypermethylation in two DMRs (MEG3:TSS-DMR and GNAS-AS1:TSS-DMR).

Results of MS-MLPA. The placental tissues and cord blood of case PMD-020 exhibited aberrant hypomethylation at several ubiquitous imprinted DMRs and aberrant hypermethylation at one DMR. The DMRs with aberrant methylation varied among the analyzed samples from case PMD-020. The placenta in case PMD-030 exhibited aberrant hypomethylation at two DMRs and aberrant hypermethylation at three others. Asterisks indicate aberrantly methylated DMRs.

Discussion

Here, we used WES analysis of peripheral-blood DNA of seven mothers with BiPMD to identify a compound-heterozygous NLRP5 frameshift variant (pathogenic) and missense variant (possibly benign) in one mother, and a heterozygous NLRP2 missense variant (possibly benign) in another. Previously, heterozygous and homozygous pathogenic variants of NLRP5 and NLRP2 have been reported in mothers with early embryonic arrest or MLIDs [6, 8]. NLRP5 is one of the core components of the SCMC, and the pyrin domain of NLRP5 is essential for SCMC stability [16]. Additionally, the CPLs of both mouse and human oocytes store UHRF1, which forms a protein complex with DNMT1, essential for maintaining DNA methylation in early embryos [10]. Further, NLRP5 binds to and stabilizes UHRF1; in fully grown NLRP5-null oocytes, UHRF1 becomes unstable and decreases in both cytoplasm and nucleus [17]. Therefore, NLRP5 may influence methylation levels in imprinted DMRs.

NLRP2 is another component of the SCMC. In mice, females lacking Nlrp2 are subfertile, exhibiting early embryonic loss and offspring with diverse developmental phenotypes and abnormal DNA methylation at imprinted loci [18]. Transcriptome analysis of Nlrp2-null oocytes revealed increased expression of Tet2 and Kdm1b [19]. Tet2 induces DNA demethylation [20], and in Kdm1b-null mice increased H3K4me levels lead to abnormal DNA methylation at imprinted loci [21]. Thus, pathogenic variants of NLRP2 may affect the expression of these factors, contributing to hypomethylation at imprinted loci. Although the identified variant was inferred to be benign, further investigation of NLRP2 gene variants should be conducted in additional PMD cases.

Variants of NLRP7 were reported in two mothers who exhibited MLIDs and spontaneous abortion, with indications of PMD [8, 22]. One (NM_001127255.1:c.2010_2011del) delivered a child with Beckwith–Wiedemann spectrum (BWSp) symptoms, MLIDs, and hypomethylation of KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR after PMD was suspected on ultrasound [22]. The other (NM_001127255.1:c.[2161C > T];[2573 T > C]) delivered a child with omphalocele and PMD [8]. Histological PMD was also observed in a patient with multiple hydatidiform moles and a homozygous pathogenic PADI6 variant [23]. Although no molecular genetic analyses of placental tissues were performed in these studies, those findings combined with ours strongly suggest that SCMC gene variants cause BiPMD.

We previously found that methylation abnormalities at imprinted loci in BiPMD occur more frequently at placenta-specific, maternal allele-methylated DMRs than at ubiquitous DMRs [5]. Our current MS-MLPA analysis revealed variations in aberrantly methylated DMRs across cases and within the same case at different collection sites. Since the relationship between gene variants and affected DMRs (genotype–epigenotype correlation) remains unclear in MLIDs [6], this suggests the following mechanism for BiPMD: SCMC dysfunction due to SCMC gene mutations causes aberrant methylation at imprinted DMRs in early embryos, but the DMRs affected vary among cells. A mosaic pattern of cells with different methylation abnormalities and normal cells differentiates into placental tissues, resulting in variations in affected DMRs across and within samples. In PMD-020, macro-PMD regions also tended to have more methylation abnormalities than macro-normal regions. This suggests that methylation abnormalities already exist in macro-normal regions and macro-PMD regions form where these abnormalities accumulate. This hypothesis is supported by the higher numbers of methylation abnormalities in placenta-specific DMRs in macro-PMD regions than normal regions [5]. Further, in the umbilical-cord blood in PMD-020, we observed methylation abnormalities in three DMRs, including KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR hypomethylation, and the fetus was diagnosed with classical BWS with MLIDs (BWSp score: 5) [5]. This probably reflects a mosaic distribution of cells with these methylation abnormalities in the inner cell mass. These phenomena are consistent with our proposed mechanism of BiPMD pathogenesis.

We also observed abnormalities in paternally methylated DMRs. In MLID cases, variants of maternal-effect genes can alter the methylation of both maternally and paternally imprinted genes [6, 7]. Further, although most methylation abnormalities here involved hypomethylation, some DMRs exhibited hypermethylation. These aspects are similar to MLID cases.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of cases analyzed via WES was small. Second, only one pathogenic variant (NLRP5 frameshift) was identified, and the two missense variants were inferred as benign. Third, methylation analysis was limited to imprinted loci, without genome-wide analysis. In mice with heterozygous Padi6 missense variants, incomplete DNA demethylation was observed in two-cell embryos, but imprinted loci were largely unaffected [24]. We may therefore have overlooked aberrant methylation patterns outside imprinted loci. Finally, genetic variants were identified in only two cases, leaving the causes of the other five cases unknown. Given the rarity of PMD, accumulating more cases, using advanced genomic techniques, and performing in vitro and in vivo single-cell functional analyses of the SCMC are essential.

Conclusions

Variants of maternal-effect NLRP genes that are components of the SCMC were suggested as causative factors in two BiPMD cases. Similar variants have been identified in MLIDs, implying that BiPMD may be a form of MLID in the placenta, representing an intermediate phenotype between miscarriage and live-born children with MLIDs. Taken together, these findings suggest that MLIDs in live-born individuals, BiPMD, and BiHMs may collectively represent a continuum within the MLID spectrum. Elucidating the mechanisms by which variants of maternal-effect SCMC genes induce imprinting errors remains a challenge. Clarifying the role of SCMC genes in maintaining methylation imprinting and the pathogenesis of MLIDs will contribute to improved understanding, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of the MLID spectrum, including PMD.
