Scleroderma renal crisis: avoiding the diagnostic pitfalls of an elusive condition
Authors: Isaac K S Ng, May Shuen Tang, Faith Huiwen See, Jiacai Cho
Published: 2026

Scleroderma renal crisis (SRC) is a life-threatening manifestation of systemic sclerosis (SSc) characterized by malignant hypertension, acute kidney injury (AKI) and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Its pathophysiology involves endothelial injury that triggers intimal proliferation and narrowing of renal vasculature, leading to hypoperfusion and unbridled activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [1]. SRC seems more prevalent (2–7%) in the Western region (e.g. United States, Europe and Australia) than Asia (2–3%), which may be due to higher prevalence of anti-RNA3 polymerase antibodies in the former [2]. Although survival outcomes have drastically improved with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) and renal replacement therapy, it still carries a 1-year mortality rate of 36%, with a quarter of patients remaining on dialysis [3]. While the importance of prompt recognition and early treatment cannot be overstated, the diagnosis of SRC remains challenging with no universally accepted criteria. We herein discuss the major pitfalls in SRC diagnosis and provide practical tips to recognize this elusive, yet highly dangerous condition.

Current clinical pitfalls in SRC diagnosis

SRC is characterized by a triad of AKI, malignant hypertension and TMA, and usually occurs <4 years of diagnosis [1]. However, there is significant heterogeneity in clinical presentations. Patients with SRC usually present with non-specific symptoms (e.g. dyspnea, chest discomfort, headache), and many do not have the full clinical ‘triad’. For instance, microangiopathic hemolytic anaemia (MAHA) is only seen in 30–50% of cases and 10% of SRC patients are normotensive at presentation [4, 5], a phenomenon attributed to pre-existing ACEI/calcium channel blocker use [6] or underlying myocardial involvement [7]. Generally, patients with normotensive SRC have a worse prognosis, likely due to delayed diagnosis and poorer response to ACE-inhibition [7]. Moreover, late-onset SRC (>5 years) occurs in 25% of cases [8]. Besides SRC, other causes of AKI in SSc patients must be considered (Table 1), including (i) hypovolemia as a result of gastrointestinal losses or anorexia commonly seen in SSc patients; (ii) drug-related causes (e.g. calcineurin inhibitors); (iii) sepsis-related; (iv) direct injury from malignant hypertension; (v) renal artery/venous thrombosis in concomitant antiphospholipid syndrome and (vi) other causes of renal TMA such as hemolytic-uremic syndrome (HUS)/thrombotic thrombocytopenia purpura (TTP). Additionally, > 10% of SSc patients have systemic lupus erythematosus [2], and 2% have antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)-associated vasculitis overlap [5], which can cause glomerulonephritis.

Practical tips for the timely diagnosis of SRC

Firstly, SRC must always be considered in SSc patients presenting with AKI, especially if there are supporting features such as hypertension or MAHA, or known risk factors. For patients with malignant hypertension (SBP > 180 mmHg), assess for end-organ damage, including neurological (hypertensive encephalopathy, stroke, intracranial haemorrhage), ophthalmological (hypertensive retinopathy, papilledema) and cardiovascular (acute coronary syndrome, flash pulmonary oedema, aortic dissection) sequelae through relevant clinical examinations, laboratory tests, electrocardiogram and radiological investigations. MAHA is diagnosed by the identification of ≥1% schistocytes on peripheral blood films [9]. Regardless, the absence of the full clinical triad (AKI, hypertension, TMA) does not preclude the diagnosis of SRC. For ‘normotensive’ patients, review their home/outpatient blood pressure trends, for comparing current readings with their baseline (to assess for relative increase in systolic pressure >30 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure >20 mmHg) [10]. Additionally, check for pre-existing cardiac disease or blood pressure-lowering medications (especially ACEIs) that could mask a hypertensive response. In patients with absence of frank MAHA at presentation, clinicians should look for early laboratory evidence of hemolysis (e.g. elevated reticulocyte count, bilirubin, low haptoglobin, elevated lactate dehydrogenase), and consider repeating full blood count and blood films regularly. Additionally, assess for presence of risk factors of SRC: <4–5 years of SSc diagnosis, DcSSc, rapidly progressing skin disease (use the modified Rodnan skin score), tendon friction rubs, anti-polymerase III antibody positivity, antitopoisomerase-I antibody and prednisolone ≥15 mg/day [1].

Secondly, assess for counter-diagnostic features of SRC, or alternative explanations for the patient’s clinical presentation. For example, as SSc/SRC is not characterized by an inflammatory state, persistent fevers or markedly elevated inflammatory markers could point to an alternative diagnosis. Moreover, as SRC is primarily a vasculopathic process rather than a form of glomerulonephritis, heavy (e.g. nephrotic-range) proteinuria or significant red cell casts would not be expected on urinalysis [7]. Likewise, consider the clinical evidence that may suggest an alternative disease process. For example, a patient with poor oral intake, appears clinically dehydrated (dry mucous membranes) and has an elevated urea/creatinine ratio (>20) or hypernatremia, likely has pre-renal AKI. Acute tubular necrosis (ATN) is another possibility in patients with sepsis or recent use of nephrotoxic drugs. In patients with AKI and TMA who have prominent neurological manifestations (e.g. altered mental state), TTP must be considered. For patients who acutely develop multi-organ failure with evidence of thrombotic events, catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) must be excluded. Overlap syndromes should be considered in patients with clinical features of vasculitis or lupus manifestations such as alopecia, oral/nasal ulcers, malar rash/photosensitivity and cytopenias. In these contexts, serological testing such as anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) and anti-extractable nuclear antigens (anti-ENA), ANCA (anti-proteinase 3 and myeloperoxidase antibodies) and antiphospholipid antibodies may be considered. Additionally, ADAMTS-13 antibodies and activity levels can be assessed if TTP is a consideration. However, serological tests have a significant turnaround time—hence, treatment should not be delayed if there is a high index of clinical suspicion.

Third, multidisciplinary management is key, especially the timely involvement of renal specialists whenever SRC is suspected. While a kidney biopsy is neither required nor definitively able to establish the diagnosis of SRC, it is a useful modality that should be considered in atypical cases, for instance, to rule out competing aetiologies with distinct treatment implications, such as an immune-mediated glomerulonephritis [7]. Classically, histopathological findings of SRC cases include intimal proliferation and thickening with obliteration of small vessel lumen, ‘onion-skin’ hypertrophy of interlobular arteries and/or thromboses [1]. However, histopathological findings of TMA of renal vessels on kidney biopsy may also be seen in conditions like malignant hypertension and HUS/TTP.

Conclusion

While SRC is treatable, its long-term renal/mortality outcomes remain significant, underscoring the importance of timely diagnosis. However, SRC remains difficult to diagnose due to atypical presentations, competing causes of AKI and a lack of awareness of its heterogeneous manifestations. Careful clinical assessment with appropriate utilization of adjunctive investigations is key to timely identification of this elusive condition.
