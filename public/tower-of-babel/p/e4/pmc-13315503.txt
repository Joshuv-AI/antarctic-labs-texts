miR-26a-5p inhibits the proliferation and myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs by targeting MDFIC
Authors: Shuibing Liu, Yada Lu, Linxi Wu, Zijian Qin, Changbin Zhao, Zhen Zhou, Yongxiang Song, Xiquan Zhang, Dexiang Zhang, Hongmei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coordinated development and homeostasis among bone, muscle, and adipose tissue are essential for the sustainable advancement of the broiler industry. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) possess multi-lineage differentiation potential and provide an in vitro model for investigating regulatory mechanisms of the “bone-muscle-adipose” balance. Nevertheless, microRNA (miRNA)–mRNA regulatory network and the functions of key miRNAs during myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs remain unclear. We profiled mRNA and miRNA expression during this process to identify key miRNAs regulating BMSC proliferation and differentiation. mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) and miRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) before and after 5-azacytidine (5-Aza) induction identified 2,233 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 67 differentially expressed miRNAs (DE miRNAs). DEGs were mainly enriched in cell cycle and DNA replication pathways, whereas predicted targets of DE miRNAs were predominantly enriched in calcium signaling pathway. A candidate miRNA–mRNA regulatory network was constructed using 13 skeletal muscle-related DE miRNAs and inversely expressed target mRNAs, with target genes enriched in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum and Wnt signaling pathways. RNAhybrid prediction and dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that miR-26a-5p directly targets MyoD Family Inhibitor Domain Containing (MDFIC). Functionally, miR-26a-5p overexpression suppressed proliferation markers (CCNB2, CCND2, CDK8) and myogenic markers (MYOD1, MYOG, MEF2C, MYHC, Desmin), and reduced MYOD1 protein, whereas miR-26a-5p inhibition produced opposite effects. Collectively, this study delineates the miRNA–mRNA regulatory network during myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs and demonstrates that miR-26a-5p negatively regulates proliferation and myogenic differentiation by targeting MDFIC. These findings identify candidate targets for understanding the post-transcriptional regulation of myogenesis in chicken BMSCs.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle development, skeletal growth, and adipose deposition are important biological processes involved in the growth and development of broiler chickens. However, the long-term genetic selection for high growth performance and feed efficiency can lead to unfavorable phenotypes, such as excessive fat deposition, impaired skeletal development, and muscle fibrosis, which negatively impact the poultry industry (González-Cerón et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2025). These tissue-related processes are closely associated with the lineage specification of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), including myogenic, osteogenic, and adipogenic differentiation, thereby providing a cellular basis for investigating regulatory mechanisms related to muscle lineage specification (Gimble and Nuttall, 2012; Cao et al., 2015; Kusuyama et al., 2018). These cell fate decisions are regulated by transcriptional and post-transcriptional networks, including microRNA (miRNA)-mediated regulation (Li et al., 2019). Consequently, understanding the regulatory mechanisms involved in myogenic differentiation of BMSCs may provide useful insights into muscle development and the “bone–muscle–adipose” balance in broiler chickens.

BMSCs are multipotent stromal cells with the capacity for self-renewal and multilineage differentiation (Muruganandan et al., 2009). Their potential to differentiate into myogenic, adipogenic, and osteogenic lineages makes BMSCs a valuable in vitro model for investigating the regulatory mechanisms related to the “bone–muscle–adipose” balance (Engler et al., 2006; Attia and Mashal, 2021). Their mesenchymal stem cell identity is commonly characterized by positive expression of mesenchymal or adhesion markers and low or negative expression of hematopoietic markers, supporting their use in subsequent functional studies (Chamberlain et al., 2007).

In vitro, distinct induction conditions can drive BMSCs toward different lineage programs. 5-Azacytidine (5-Aza), a demethylating agent, has been reported to promote the differentiation of BMSCs toward myocyte-like and cardiomyocyte-like phenotypes by enhancing the expression of muscle-specific genes and proteins, although complete sarcomere formation was not observed (Burlacu et al., 2008; Li et al., 2021). However, research on its application in chicken BMSCs remains limited. Our previous work systematically evaluated 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation in chicken BMSCs, revealing that differentially expressed genes were markedly enriched in signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt and TGF-β using transcriptome sequencing (Zhou et al., 2022). Nevertheless, as our previous study mainly focused on mRNA-level changes, the miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms involved in 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs remain unclear. miRNAs are endogenous small non-coding RNAs, approximately 18-25 nucleotides in length, that are widely present in eukaryotes. They primarily function by binding to the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of target mRNAs, thereby mediating mRNA degradation and/or translational repression and fine-tuning gene expression at the post-transcriptional level, with critical roles in processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and development (Ambros, 2004; Correia de Sousa et al., 2019). In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated that miRNAs play important regulatory roles in the multilineage differentiation of BMSCs, including adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation (Zhu et al., 2018; Breulmann et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025). Therefore, the miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulatory network underlying 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs warrants further investigation.

To identify miRNA–mRNA regulatory networks involved in 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs, transcriptome sequencing was employed to profile differentially expressed mRNAs and miRNAs before and after this process, and a miRNA–mRNA interaction regulatory network was constructed. At the in vitro cellular level, we provide evidence that the key candidate miRNA, miR-26a-5p, inhibits the proliferation and 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs and directly targets MDFIC 3′ UTR. These findings provide candidate targets for understanding post-transcriptional regulation of myogenic differentiation in chicken BMSCs and may provide potential molecular targets for future genetic studies.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All animal procedures were conducted under a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of South China Agricultural University (approval number SCAU#0017). All procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the committee’s regulations and guidelines to ensure the humane treatment of all chicks.

Isolation, culture, and identification of cells

Chicken BMSCs were isolated from the bone marrow of the femurs and tibias of 1-day-old chicks as previously described (Zhou et al., 2022). Briefly, six 1-day-old chicks were humanely euthanized, and the legs were dissected under aseptic conditions, disinfected by immersion in 75% ethanol, and then placed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). The muscles and connective tissues surrounding the tibias and femurs were carefully removed, the epiphyses at both ends were excised, and the marrow cavities were flushed with PBS to collect a bone marrow cell suspension. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm sterile cell strainer (Biosharp, Beijing, China) and centrifuged at 450 × g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in complete BMSC culture medium (mMSC PRO, XRbio, Hangzhou, China) supplemented with 0.2% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) and seeded into 60 mm cell culture dishes (JET Biofil, Guangzhou, China). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂. After 24 hours, the medium was completely replaced to remove dead cells and non-adherent cells, thereby enriching primary BMSCs. When BMSCs reached approximately 90% confluence, cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco) and subcultured. Cells after the first and second subcultures were designated as passage 1 (P1) and passage 2 (P2), respectively. All subsequent experiments involving chicken BMSCs were performed using P2 cells unless otherwise stated.

Chicken fibroblasts (DF-1) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Bio-channel, Nanjing, China) and 0.2% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂.

To assess the expression of BMSC surface markers, immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry were performed. P2 BMSCs in the proliferation phase were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/well for immunofluorescence staining. When the cells reached 80%–90% confluence, they were fixed with paraformaldehyde, permeabilized, blocked, incubated with primary antibodies, and stained with secondary antibodies. Primary antibodies included anti-CD29 (LS-C413122, rabbit, LSBio, Seattle, WA, USA; 1:400), anti-CD45 (MA5-28682, mouse, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; 1:400), anti-CD105 (bs-0579R, rabbit, Bioss, Beijing, China; 1:400), and anti-CD166 (bs-1251R, rabbit, Bioss; 1:400). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for detection, including goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (ab6785, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:40,000) and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (ab6717, Abcam; 1:40,000), and nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Servicebio). Images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Flow cytometry was further performed to assess BMSC surface marker expression. P2 BMSCs were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco), and the cell pellet was collected. Cells were stained in separate tubes with primary antibodies against CD34 (bs-0646R, rabbit, Bioss), CD44 (clone AV6, mouse, SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL, USA), CD45 (MA5-28682, mouse, Invitrogen), CD73 (bs-4834R, rabbit, Bioss), and CD105 (bs-0579R, rabbit, Bioss). After incubation for 30 min at room temperature, cells were washed and resuspended in PBS (Servicebio). Subsequently, a cyanine 3 (Cy3)-conjugated secondary antibody (PE561, BaiQianDu Bio, Wuhan, China) was added, followed by incubation for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. After washing, cells were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (B75442, Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). An unstained control consisting of cells resuspended in PBS without primary antibodies or the Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody was used as a negative control to set the positivity gate for flow cytometric analysis.

Induction of myogenic differentiation in chicken BMSCs

Previous studies have shown that 5-Aza can induce myogenic differentiation of BMSCs (Wakitani et al., 1995). In the present study, P2 BMSCs were induced toward myogenic differentiation when they reached approximately 90% confluence. Based on our previous work on 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation in chicken BMSCs (Zhou et al., 2022), the induction medium consisted of DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 0.2% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), 2% horse serum (Gibco), and 10 μM 5-Aza (Macklin, Shanghai, China). The medium was replaced every 2 days. Cell morphology was monitored and recorded using an inverted microscope (DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Sample collection

For qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses used to verify myogenic differentiation, untreated proliferating BMSCs at approximately 90% confluence were collected as the GM group, and BMSCs induced toward myogenic differentiation for 2 and 4 days were collected as the DM2 and DM4 groups, respectively. For transcriptome sequencing analysis, P2 BMSCs derived from the pooled bone marrow cell suspension of six chicks were seeded into six 60 mm cell culture dishes, with three dishes maintained under proliferative culture conditions as the GM group (GM1–GM3) and the other three dishes treated with myogenic induction medium for 4 days as the DM group (DM1–DM3). For qRT-PCR analysis of the expression pattern during myogenic differentiation, cells were collected after 2, 4, 6, and 8 days of induction. Proliferation-related assays were performed under proliferative culture conditions without 5-Aza treatment. For experiments assessing the regulatory effect of miRNA on myogenic differentiation, BMSCs were induced toward myogenic differentiation 24 h after transfection and collected after 48 h of induction for qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses.

RNA extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA samples were stored at −80°C until use. For mRNA reverse transcription, total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (R333-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). For miRNA, cDNA was synthesized using a stem-loop RT approach with the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (FSQ-101, TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). Briefly, miRNA-specific stem-loop RT primers were used to bind to mature miRNAs and extend the short miRNA sequences during reverse transcription, thereby generating cDNA templates suitable for subsequent qPCR amplification. Before reverse transcription, total RNA was denatured at 65°C for 5 min and immediately chilled on ice. Each 10 μL reverse transcription reaction contained 1 μg of total RNA, 2 μL of 5 × RT Buffer, 0.5 μL of RT Enzyme Mix, 0.5 μL of miRNA-specific stem-loop RT primers, and RNase-free ddH₂O to a final volume of 10 μL. The reverse transcription reaction was performed at 37°C for 15 min, followed by 98°C for 5 min. The resulting cDNA was diluted three-fold and used as the template for qPCR. qPCR was performed using the 2 × ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme) on an ABI QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher). Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. GAPDH and U6 were used as internal reference genes for mRNA and miRNA quantification, respectively. Relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Four biological replicates were included for each group, and each biological replicate was analyzed in technical triplicate. mRNA primers were designed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1) and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The Bulge-loop™ miRNA qPCR primer sets (including stem-loop RT primers and corresponding qPCR primer pairs) were designed and synthesized by RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Western blotting

Protein expression levels were assessed by Western blotting. Total protein was extracted from BMSCs using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (C0100, BaiQianDu Bio) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (C0112-1ML, BaiQianDu Bio). Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (BCA-1500, Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein samples, 30 μg per lane, were separated using 4%–20% Smart PAGE™ Precast Protein Gel Plus gels (SLE015, Smart Lifesciences, Changzhou, China) and then transferred onto PVDF membranes at 200 mA for 90 min in an ice bath.

After blocking, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against MYOG (AF7542, rabbit, Beyotime; 1:500), MYOD1 (bs-2442R, rabbit, Bioss, Beijing, China; 1:1000), β-Actin (AC026, rabbit, ABclonal, Wuhan, China; 1:100,000), and GAPDH (bsm-33033M, mouse, Bioss; 1:10,000), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (AS014, ABclonal; 1:5000) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (HA1006, Huabio, Hangzhou, China; 1:50,000). Bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (PMK0448, Pumoke, Wuhan, China) and imaged using an Odyssey FC Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ v1.8.0 (Schneider et al., 2012). Each Western blot experiment was independently repeated three times.

When β-Actin was used as the loading control, membranes were first incubated with anti-MYOD1 antibody overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and developed. Membranes were then treated with stripping buffer (C0302, BaiQianDu Bio) to remove antibodies from the previous probing. After re-blocking, membranes were incubated with anti-β-Actin antibody to detect the loading control.

When GAPDH was used as the loading control, membranes were co-incubated with anti-MYOD1 (rabbit) and anti-GAPDH (mouse) primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies, followed by development to obtain band signals for both MYOD1 and GAPDH.

mRNA and miRNA library preparation and sequencing

Six BMSC samples, comprising three biological replicates of proliferating BMSCs in the GM group and three biological replicates of BMSCs in the DM group corresponding to day 4 of induced myogenic differentiation, were lysed in TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa) and submitted to Biomarker Technologies Corporation (Beijing, China) for RNA extraction, library preparation, mRNA-seq, and miRNA-seq. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, while RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) with the RNA 6000 Nano kit. All RNA samples used for sequencing had RIN values ranging from 8.7 to 8.9. The mRNA-seq and miRNA-seq raw data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA), National Genomics Data Center (NGDC), under accession number CRA035774 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/search?searchTerm=CRA035774).

For mRNA library preparation, the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

For miRNA library preparation, libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Small RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (NR811-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Briefly, total RNA was sequentially ligated to 3′ and 5′ adapters, followed by reverse transcription and PCR amplification. The PCR products were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (N411-03, Vazyme), size-selected by 6% TBE polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and bands within the designated size range were excised and recovered. The recovered small RNA libraries were quality-checked using a Qsep-400 system. Finally, 50-bp single-end sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina) using the NovaSeq™ X Series 25B reagent kit. After adapter trimming and quality filtering, clean reads within the 18–30 nt size range were retained for downstream miRNA analysis.

Identification and analysis of differentially expressed mRNAs and miRNAs

Quality assessment of raw sequencing reads was performed using FastQC. For mRNA-seq, raw reads in FASTQ format were processed using Perl scripts provided by Biomarker Technologies Corporation to remove adapter-containing reads, poly-N-containing reads, and low-quality reads. The clean reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome (Gallus_gallus.GCF_016699485.2.genome.fa) using HISAT2 v2.0.4. mRNA expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). For miRNA-seq, raw reads were processed using Perl scripts to remove adapter-containing reads, poly-N-containing reads, and low-quality reads. Reads shorter than 18 nt or longer than 30 nt were removed, and clean reads within the 18–30 nt size range were retained for downstream analysis. Bowtie was used for read alignment and filtering before miRNA identification. miRNA expression levels were normalized as transcripts per million (TPM). For differential expression analysis, raw count data were used as input for DESeq2 v1.30.1 (Love et al., 2014). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed miRNAs (DE miRNAs) were identified by comparing the DM group with the GM group. Significant differential expression was defined as false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1.

Protein-protein interaction network (PPI)

DEGs were imported into the STRING database (http://string-db.org/) to obtain predicted protein-protein interaction (PPI) results. Protein interaction pairs with a confidence score ≥ 0.15 were retained. To focus on myogenic differentiation-related candidate genes, muscle-related DEGs were then selected from the filtered DEG-derived PPI results, and a focused muscle-related PPI subnetwork was constructed. The PPI network was visualized in Cytoscape v3.8.2 (Shannon et al., 2003).

Construction of the miRNA–mRNA interaction regulatory network

Target genes of DE miRNAs were predicted using miRanda v3.3a (Betel et al., 2008) and TargetScan v5.0 (Lewis et al., 2003) based on chicken gene sequence information. Candidate miRNA–mRNA pairs were retained only when they were predicted by both miRanda and TargetScan, met the thresholds of TargetScan_Score > 95 and miRanda_Energy < −14, and had target genes that overlapped with differentially expressed mRNAs identified from the mRNA-seq dataset (FDR < 0.05). To further improve network confidence, only miRNA–mRNA pairs showing inverse expression patterns were retained, including upregulated miRNAs paired with downregulated mRNAs and downregulated miRNAs paired with upregulated mRNAs. The up- or down-regulation status of miRNAs and mRNAs was determined based on log2FC values. Analyses focused on 13 skeletal muscle–related DE miRNAs (gga-miR-10b-5p, gga-miR-133a-3p, gga-miR-143-3p, gga-miR-148a-3p, gga-miR-181a-5p, gga-miR-1a-3p, gga-miR-205a, gga-miR-20b-5p, gga-miR-21-3p, gga-miR-218-5p, gga-miR-26a-5p, gga-miR-7, gga-miR-9-5p) and their corresponding inversely expressed target genes. The miRNA–mRNA interaction regulatory network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape v3.8.2 (Shannon et al., 2003), and node color represents the log2FC value of each miRNA or mRNA.

Functional enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs and predicted target genes of DE miRNAs was performed using clusterProfiler v4.4.4 based on the Wallenius non-central hypergeometric distribution. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the above gene sets was performed using KOBAS in combination with clusterProfiler v4.4.4. For GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, all annotated chicken genes were used as the gene background list.

Plasmid construction, RNA oligonucleotides, and transfection

To generate pmirGLO dual-luciferase reporter constructs, DNA fragments containing the wild-type (MDFIC 3′ UTR-WT) or mutant (MDFIC 3′ UTR-MT) sequences of the MDFIC 3′ UTR were synthesized by Tsingke and cloned into the pmirGLO vector. Plasmids were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), with nucleic acids diluted in Opti-MEM (Gibco). All transfections were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The miR-26a-5p mimic, mimic negative control (mimic NC), miR-26a-5p inhibitor, and inhibitor negative control (inhibitor NC) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Oligonucleotide sequences are provided in Table 1. For BMSC transfection, cells were transfected when they reached approximately 70%–80% confluence. These RNA oligonucleotides were transfected into BMSCs using Lipofectamine 3000 at a final concentration of 150 nM, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Prediction of binding interactions and dual-luciferase reporter assay

To predict the binding site of miR-26a-5p within the MDFIC 3′ UTR, RNAhybrid v2.2.3 was used. When DF-1 cells reached approximately 70%–80% confluence, each well was co-transfected with 500 ng of pmirGLO-MDFIC 3′ UTR-WT or pmirGLO-MDFIC 3′ UTR-MT plasmid and miR-26a-5p mimic or mimic NC at a final concentration of 150 nM using Lipofectamine 3000. At 48 h post-transfection, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the TransDetect® Double-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Transgen, Beijing, China) on a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity (Firefly/Renilla). The dual-luciferase reporter assay was independently repeated three times, with four replicate wells per group in each experiment.

CCK-8 and EdU assay

For the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, at 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post-transfection, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (GK10001, GLPBIO, Montclair, CA, USA) was added to BMSCs cultured in 96-well plates. After incubation at 37°C for 2 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek). The CCK-8 assays were independently repeated three times.

For the EdU assay, at 36-48 h post-transfection, BMSCs cultured in 24-well plates were incubated with 50 µM 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU, MeilunBio, Dalian, China) at 37°C for 2 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized, and stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were acquired from four randomly selected fields per group using an inverted fluorescence microscope (DMi8). EdU-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ v1.8.0 (Schneider et al., 2012). The EdU assays were independently repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) in SPSS v26.0. Significance thresholds were set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Different letters indicate significant differences among groups (P < 0.05).

Results

Identification and myogenic differentiation induction of chicken BMSCs

Chicken BMSCs were isolated from the bone marrow of tibiae and femurs of 1-day-old chicks and cultured in growth medium. Approximately 24 h after seeding, the medium was replaced to remove most non-adherent contaminating cells. At this stage, a small number of sparsely distributed adherent cells were observed, exhibiting fibrous or spindle-shaped morphologies of varying lengths (passage P0; Fig. S1A). When confluence exceeded 90%, cells were passaged. After two consecutive passages, contaminating and dead cells were largely eliminated. Cells at passages P1-P2 displayed a typical fibroblast-like morphology with a swirling pattern and appeared relatively homogeneous (Fig. S1B,C). Immunofluorescence staining showed that chicken BMSCs were positive for CD29, CD105, and CD166 but negative for CD45 (Fig. S1D). Flow cytometric analysis showed high positivity for CD105, CD44, and CD73 at 99.19%, 85.24%, and 97.69%, respectively, whereas CD34 and CD45 were detected at very low levels at 0.54% and 0.20%, respectively (Fig. S1E). Together, these results indicate that the isolated cells exhibit a typical MSC surface marker profile with high purity and are suitable for subsequent functional studies.

Compared with the GM group, cells in the DM group exhibited marked morphological changes after induction. With increasing induction time, cells became more elongated and aligned in bundles, resembling myoblast-like morphology (Fig. 1A–C). qPCR showed that mRNA levels of myogenic markers (MYHC, Desmin, MYOG, and MYOD) were markedly higher in the DM group than in the GM group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D–G). Western blotting further demonstrated that MYOG and MYOD protein levels were considerably increased on day 4 of induction (DM4) compared with GM (P < 0.05; Fig. 1H,I). Differential expression of mRNAs and functional enrichment analysis

The mRNA-seq libraries yielded more than 46 million clean reads per sample. Unique alignment rates to the chicken reference genome exceeded 92.06% for all samples (Supplementary Table S2), supporting the overall quality of the sequencing data.

Principal component analysis (PCA) showed tight clustering within groups and clear separation between GM and DM (Fig. 2A). Using FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1, 2,233 DEGs were identified, including 1,261 upregulated and 972 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S4). The distribution of DEGs is shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 2C). Among the upregulated DEGs, 24 genes associated with skeletal muscle growth and development were highlighted, including MYOC, MYO1C, MYO1D, MYO1E, MYO5A, MYO5C, MYH11, and MYH15 (Fig. 2C). Six DEGs were randomly selected for qPCR validation. Proliferation-associated genes (CCNB2, CCND2, and CDK8) were quantified by qPCR after transfection. miR-26a-5p overexpression statistically reduced CCNB2, CCND2, and CDK8 mRNA levels (P < 0.05; Fig. 7C). Discussion

Improving poultry meat yield and quality remains a central goal of the poultry industry. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms associated with muscle development and the balance among bone, muscle, and adipose tissues may provide useful insights for broiler biology (Zhang et al., 1995; Abdalla et al., 2018; de Almeida Mallmann et al., 2019). BMSCs, which possess multilineage differentiation potential (including myogenic, adipogenic, and osteogenic lineages), are increasingly used as an in vitro cellular model to investigate molecular mechanisms underlying myogenesis (Olenic et al., 2025). In this study, integrated transcriptome profiling was performed to characterize mRNA and miRNA expression changes in primary chicken BMSCs before and after 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation. A miRNA–mRNA interaction regulatory network was constructed by integrating DEGs and DE miRNAs. Moreover, the functional role of the candidate miRNA miR-26a-5p in regulating BMSC proliferation and 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation was evaluated in vitro.

The source, purity, and phenotypic characteristics of BMSCs are prerequisites for establishing a reliable in vitro model. Previous studies have widely used density gradient centrifugation, differential adhesion, flow cytometric sorting, and immunomagnetic separation for BMSC isolation (Conget and Minguell, 1999). In recent years, protocols for BMSC isolation, expansion, and in vitro multilineage differentiation have become increasingly established across livestock and poultry species, including chickens (Adhikari et al., 2019), pigs (Bie et al., 2019), cattle (Cortes et al., 2013), and sheep (Stamnitz et al., 2025). We isolated primary BMSCs from the bone marrow of one-day-old chicks and assessed their surface marker profile. Immunofluorescence showed positive staining for CD29, CD105, and CD166, whereas the hematopoietic marker CD45 was negative. Flow cytometry further indicated high positivity for CD44, CD73, and CD105, with minimal expression of hematopoietic/immune markers (CD34 and CD45). This marker profile is consistent with commonly used MSC identification criteria and prior reports on chicken BMSCs (Dominici et al., 2006; Brinkhof et al., 2020). Collectively, these results support the suitability of the isolated cells for downstream analyses of myogenic differentiation in chicken BMSCs.

BMSCs possess myogenic potential under specific in vitro induction conditions. Studies have demonstrated that BMSCs from multiple species, including humans, pigs, cattle, sheep, and chickens, can upregulate the expression of skeletal muscle markers like MYOG, MYOD, and MYF5 following treatment with compounds such as 5-Aza or exposure to conditions like hypoxia or heat shock (Ramírez-Espinosa et al., 2015; Kulesza et al., 2016; Milner et al., 2018; Adhikari et al., 2020; Archacka et al., 2021). In this study, chicken BMSCs were induced with 10 µM 5-Aza to promote myogenic differentiation. We observed increased mRNA expression of MYHC, Desmin, MYOG, and MYOD1 after induction. Similarly, MYOG and MYOD1 protein levels were also significantly upregulated. Notably, multinucleated myotube-like structures were not observed under our induction conditions, suggesting that this in vitro system primarily captures early-stage events associated with myogenic differentiation rather than the complete process of myotube maturation. This represents a limitation of the present study. Nevertheless, this model provides a workable in vitro platform for subsequent investigations into molecular regulators associated with 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs.

Transcriptomics provides a crucial tool for analyzing cellular state transitions and molecular regulatory networks at a holistic level, enabling the systematic untangling of large-scale gene expression changes and their underlying biological significance (Lockhart and Winzeler, 2000). In this study, we identified 2,233 DEGs during 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs. Key candidate factors upregulated and associated with skeletal muscle growth and development, including MUSK, MYO5A, MYH15, MYH11, MYL3, MYO1C, MYO1D, MYO5C, and MYLK, were screened. These changes support the notion that chicken BMSCs activated myogenic differentiation-related transcriptional programs under our in vitro induction conditions. Notably, several DEGs are also implicated in broader BMSC biological processes, beyond canonical myogenic markers. For example, miR-155 has been reported to directly target MYLK, thereby reducing MYLK expression and suppressing migration of human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (Wang et al., 2018b). GO and KEGG analyses showed that many downregulated genes were enriched in proliferation-associated pathways, including Cell cycle and DNA replication. This pattern is consistent with the concept that skeletal muscle cells typically undergo cell cycle exit prior to differentiation (Halevy et al., 1995; Walsh and Perlman, 1997). During myogenic differentiation, precursor cells typically shift from a proliferative state to a more quiescent differentiated state, a transition often accompanied by reduced Cyclin/CDK activity and increased expression of CDK inhibitors such as p21 and p27 (Zhang et al., 1999; Kitajima et al., 2018). In our dataset, cell cycle marker genes including CCNA2, CCNB1, CCNB3, CCND3, CDK1, and CDK2 were pronouncedly downregulated, while CDKN1C was substantially upregulated. Together, these transcriptional changes suggest reduced proliferative activity and a shift toward cell cycle withdrawal during 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs. Thus, suppression of cell cycle progression and DNA replication may be associated with activation of downstream myogenic programs.

With increasing insight into miRNA biology, miRNAs have been recognized as important post-transcriptional regulators in skeletal muscle development and BMSC multilineage differentiation (Guo et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021). For instance, miR-99a-5p promotes proliferation while suppressing differentiation of chicken skeletal muscle satellite cells by targeting MTMR3 (Cao et al., 2020). In BMSCs, miR-124 has been reported to regulate cardiomyogenic differentiation of rat BMSCs by targeting the STAT3 signaling pathway and to inhibit myogenic differentiation of mouse BMSCs by targeting DLX5 (Cai et al., 2012; Qadir et al., 2014). However, miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation during 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs remains unclear. From the 67 DE miRNAs, we highlighted multiple miRNAs previously implicated in myogenesis, including miR-143-3p (Dreher et al., 2021), miR-26a-5p (Peggion et al., 2025), and miR-1a-3p (Wang et al., 2018a). These miRNAs represent plausible candidates for regulating myogenic differentiation in chicken BMSCs under our in vitro induction system. Functional enrichment indicated that predicted targets of the 67 DE miRNAs were enriched in the calcium signaling pathway, whereas the 380 differentially expressed target genes in the constructed miRNA–mRNA network were enriched in 10 pathways, including protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, Wnt signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway. These enrichment results suggest that calcium signaling, endoplasmic reticulum-related protein processing, Wnt signaling, and MAPK-related signaling may be associated with the miRNA-mediated regulatory network involved in 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs. Future studies could further investigate whether these pathways directly participate in miRNA-mediated regulation during this process.

To further explore miRNA-mediated regulation during in vitro myogenic differentiation, we selected miR-26a-5p and its predicted target MDFIC from the miRNA–mRNA network for functional validation. CCNB2, CCND2, and CDK8 are key members of the cell cycle family, playing crucial roles in cell proliferation and serving as important marker genes for this process (Wood and Endicott, 2018). The results of this study demonstrated that miR-26a-5p significantly downregulated the expression of CCNB2, CCND2, and CDK8, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation, as confirmed by CCK-8 and EdU assays. This is consistent with reports that miR-26a-5p suppresses proliferation of goat skeletal muscle satellite cells (Zhan et al., 2024). MYHC encodes a major component of thick filaments and is widely used as a marker of myogenic differentiation (Tajsharghi and Oldfors, 2013). MYOD and MYOG are core myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs) that drive myogenic gene expression programs during skeletal muscle formation (Adhikari et al., 2021). MEF2C, a member of the MEF2 family, cooperates with MRFs to regulate myogenic transcription and is required for proper myogenic differentiation (Liu et al., 2014). Desmin is specifically expressed in myoblasts, with high expression levels during the proliferation phase, making it a marker protein for identifying myoblasts (Yablonka-Reuveni and Nameroff, 1990). This study found that overexpression of miR-26a-5p substantially downregulated the mRNA levels of myogenic marker genes such as MYOD1, MYOG, MEF2C, MYHC, and Desmin, and inhibited MYOD1 protein expression. Inhibition of miR-26a-5p, conversely, produced the opposite effects. These results indicate that miR-26a-5p attenuates myogenic marker expression during the induced myogenic differentiation of chicken BMSCs. The MDFIC protein contains an I-mfa protein inhibitory domain, and I-mfa is involved in muscle cell differentiation by regulating the activity of transcription factors such as the MyoD family (Chen et al., 1996). In fetal bovine skeletal muscle-derived progenitor cells, MDFIC has been shown to inhibit myogenic differentiation (Hu et al., 2020). Conversely, overexpression of MDFIC promotes both proliferation and myogenic differentiation in chicken myoblasts (Huang et al., 2019). The biological function of MDFIC may differ across species and cell types. In our study, RNAhybrid prediction and dual-luciferase reporter assays supported an interaction between miR-26a-5p and the MDFIC 3′ UTR, while MDFIC mRNA expression was altered after miR-26a-5p overexpression or inhibition in chicken BMSCs. Taken together with our functional assays, these data suggest that MDFIC may be involved in miR-26a-5p-associated regulation of BMSC proliferation and myogenic marker expression under our in vitro conditions. In summary, miR-26a-5p participates in the negative regulation of the cell cycle progression and 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation process in chicken BMSCs by targeting MDFIC, laying a foundation for further elucidating the role of the miR-26a-5p/MDFIC axis in chicken skeletal muscle development.

Conclusions

Using a 5-Aza-induced in vitro myogenic differentiation model of chicken BMSCs, we systematically profiled mRNA and miRNA expression changes and constructed a miRNA–mRNA interaction regulatory network, establishing a foundation for elucidating the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms. Functional assays further showed that miR-26a-5p inhibits chicken BMSC proliferation and attenuates 5-Aza-induced myogenic differentiation, and target validation supports MDFIC as a direct target of miR-26a-5p (Fig. 9).
