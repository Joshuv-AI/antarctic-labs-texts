iNOME-seq: in vivo simultaneous genome-wide mapping of chromatin accessibility, nucleosome positioning, DNA-binding protein sites, and DNA methylation in Arabidopsis
Authors: Nosheen Hussain, Ryan Merritt, Julia Engelhorn, Javier Antunez-Sanchez, Anjar Wibowo, David Latrasse, Travis Wrightsman, Maximillian Collenberg, Ilja Bezrukov, Hidayah Alotaibi, Elsa Carrasco, Moussa Benhamed, Detlef Weigel, Nicolae Radu Zabet, Jose Gutierrez-Marcos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present iNOMe-seq, a novel method for in vivo simultaneous profiling of chromatin accessibility, nucleosome occupancy, DNA-binding protein sites, and DNA methylation in living tissues. iNOMe-seq utilizes an m5C methyltransferase to mark accessible cytosines in a GpC context, bypassing nucleosome-restricted regions. Using Arabidopsis thaliana, we demonstrate that iNOMe-seq improves chromatin accessibility quantification compared to existing methods. Furthermore, it allows for the spatial and temporal analysis of chromatin dynamics, transcription factor binding, and DNA methylation, offering insight into the role of epigenetic components in transcriptional regulation across tissues and genetic variations in natural populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03760-x.

Background

Quantitative and qualitative control of gene expression is crucial for all living beings. In multicellular organisms, including plants, spatial and temporal regulation is critical to drive cellular differentiation and development, and it is essential for accurate morphogenesis [1, 2]. The primary goal of gene regulation is for genes to be expressed only when and where required. Control of gene expression can take place on different levels, i.e., transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, and post-translational stages [3]. However, for many genes, transcriptional regulation is the first opportunity to control gene expression and therefore is the most important determinant for the expression of any gene. One part of transcriptional regulation is DNA accessibility. DNA accessibility plays a vital role in the control of transcription genome-wide and is controlled by many genomic features, such as DNA methylation, nucleosome positioning, and protein binding [4, 5]. Knowledge of genome-wide DNA accessibility has greatly enhanced our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms that control gene expression [6]. Recent developments and improvements in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have allowed for the sequencing of whole genomes, providing vast amounts of information that were previously out of reach [7, 8]. These improvements have led to the development of many methods, including the detection of DNA accessibility by ATAC-seq [9], whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) [10], the identification of nucleosome occupancy by MNase-seq [11], and the discovery of protein binding sites such as transcription factor binding sites by (ChIP–seq) [6]. These methods enable the profiling of many genomic features, providing great insight into their role in controlling gene expression. However, the control of transcription is often linked to interactions between multiple genomic features. As a result, using a combination of methods is usually required to obtain a deep understanding of the role these features play in gene expression. Methods designed to profile multiple features at once were developed to enable a more accurate and straightforward analysis of these features. One such method is Nucleosome Occupancy and Methylome Sequencing (NOMe-seq), which allows for simultaneous identification of nucleosome occupancy, DNA accessibility, and methylation levels in mammals [12, 13]. While this method has been extensively used in vitro in mammals, it requires the titration of both isolated nuclei and the m5C methyltransferase M.CviPI enzyme that modifies cytosines in a GpC context, followed by bisulfite conversion and sequencing library construction, but other methods to measure DNA methylation (such as EM-seq or ONT) can also be used. NOMe-seq is well suited for mammalian genomes, which primarily contain methylation at CpG [14]. However, other multicellular organisms, such as plants, require optimized computational analysis as their genomes contain abundant non-CpG methylation (in CHG and CHH contexts) [15, 16].

Here, we describe a new method called in vivo nucleosome occupancy and methylome sequencing (iNOME-seq) that can be used to study genome-wide chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation profiles, nucleosome occupancy, and transcription factor binding in living tissues. This method supports the temporospatial mapping of multiple chromatin features over a period of time in living cells, enabling the detection of short bursts in chromatin accessibility. As such, our novel approach acts as a memory repository, preserving past and present information about the epigenetic status of regulatory regions. Using this method, we mapped the Arabidopsis chromatin landscape by identifying accessible chromatin regions, nucleosome positioning, DNA methylation levels, and potential transcription factor binding sites. Our findings show that iNOME-seq can simultaneously detect multiple chromatin features also across different genotypes, thus streamlining comparative epigenomic studies that will further our understanding of epigenetic regulation on evolutionary processes in plants, and likely in other organisms.

Results

A new methodology for the dynamic profile of chromatin in living tissues

It has been shown that the in vitro treatment of chromatin with a recombinant m5C methyltransferase from Paramecium bursaria Chlorella virus NYs1 (M.CviPI) [17], which modifies cytosines in a GpC context, enables the simultaneous mapping of chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation after bisulfite conversion followed by sequencing (BS-seq) [12, 18]. We reasoned that the controlled expression of M.CviPI methyltransferase (“indMCviPI”) might enable the measurement of the specific chromatin states underpinning different expression levels in living tissues (Fig. 1A). To test this idea, we used plants as a model system because contrary to animals they accumulate methylation in three different cytosine contexts (GC, GHC, and CHH). We generated transgenic plants carrying an engineered construct that enabled the chemically inducible expression of a codon-optimized M.CviPI methyltransferase (see Methods). We selected lines with a controlled expression by phenotypic selection, reduction of root growth, followed by quantitative expression profiling after exposure to β−17-estradiol (EST) (Fig. 1B, C). We found that a minimum induction of 6 h was required for M.CviPI to accumulate and reach a stable level of expression (Additional file 1: Figure S1). We then treated plants from homozygous indMCviPI lines with EST or mock solution and performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing to determine the genome-wide landscape of GC methylation (Fig. 1A). The chemical induction of M.CviPI resulted in the accumulation of methylation exclusively at GC sites in all cytosine contexts at non-centromeric genome regions (Additional file 1: Figure S2). To select the most suitable transgenic line for further analysis (indMCviPI_1a), we measured the accumulation of GC methylation (Additional file 1: Figure S2) and the inhibition of root growth after EST treatment (Fig. 1B, C). We defined the dynamics of GC methylation accumulation in different organs by comparing BS-seq data from control (Col-0) and chemically induced transgenic lines. We found that M.CviPI activity was evident by a notable increase in DNA methylation at GCG, GCHG, and GCHH sites after 6 h in roots and after 12 h in shoots (Additional file 1: Figure S3). The deposition of GC methylation was detected at all cytosine contexts primarily in gene-rich regions of the genome, while centromeric regions accumulated very low levels of GC methylation even after long M.CviPI induction (Fig. 1D, E). iNOME-seq accurately profiles accessible chromatin at single base-pair resolution

To test whether iNOMe-seq recapitulated known patterns of accessible chromatin, we first monitored the accumulation of DNA methylation in different organs at different time points of M.CviPI induction. We found that DNA methylation accumulated primarily in euchromatic regions of the genome (Fig. 2A) and that it was strongly associated with regions proximal to the transcriptional start of annotated genes (Additional file 1: Figure S4). Therefore, we hypothesized that cytosine methylation in living cells of our transgenic plants could also report dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility. Because plants have endogenous methylation in three distinct cytosine contexts (CG, CHG, and CHH), we first defined the optimal parameters to quantify endogenous CpG methylation and the newly deposited GpC methylation associated with chromatin accessibility. To this aim, we computed the methylation profile from control and indMCviPI mock- and EST-treated plants in different organs. We defined the parameters to quantify methylation at each cytosine context with the most stringent criteria (distance between cytosines, number of reads and statistical threshold) (see Methods and Additional file 1: Figure S5–6). We then tested the relationship between iNOMe-seq and a conventional method for mapping chromatin accessibility genome-wide, Assay for Transposase Accessible Chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq). Genomic regions detected by iNOMe-seq had features that are characteristics of active transcription, because independently of cytosine context, iNOMe-seq regions were enriched near the transcriptional start sites of genes (log2(obs/exp) in Leaf, 1.49; in Root, 1.11) and intergenic regions (log2(obs/exp) in Leaf, 0.29; in Root, 0.49) (Fig. 2C–E) and they were associated with the accumulation of RNA polymerase II (polII). In addition, there were significant amounts of iNOMe-seq positive regions that did not overlap with ATAC-seq positive regions (CG, 32.6 Mb; CHG, 22.6 Mb; CHH, 20.6 Mb) (Fig. 2B and S2). Despite these differences, the accessible chromatin detected by either method is primarily at sequences flanking actively transcribed genes (upstream, 1.47 Log2 E/O; downstream, 0.89 Log2 E/O) (Fig. 2F–H). Markedly, the active chromatin detected exclusively by iNOMe-seq included many intergenic regions of the genome (0.40) as well as a small proportion at transposable element sequences (Fig. 2G, H).

The latter observations led us to consider that iNOMe-seq might be able to report on chromatin associated with transcripts independently of their expression level. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the expression of genes detected by different chromatin profile methodologies. We found that genes identified by ATAC-seq had on average a medium level of expression, whereas genes marked exclusively by iNOMe-seq had substantially lower expression levels (Fig. 2I). Expression levels of the genes tagged by the new regions identified only by iNOMe-seq were, however, still higher than those of genes with chromatin that was not accessible by either ATAC-seq or iNOMe-seq. Collectively, our data show that iNOMe-seq is a methodology capable of uncovering accessible chromatin associated with both high and low levels of transcription (Additional file 1: Figure S7).

The induction time may influence the number of regions identified as accessible by iNOME-seq. To investigate this, we analyzed regions detected at different time points (6, 12, and 24 h) and observed that extended induction of M.CviPI expression in plants results in an increased number of regions identified as accessible (Additional file 1: Figure S8A–F). This outcome is expected, as all regions that transition to an accessible state during M.CviPI induction are marked by DNA methylation, which the plant’s cellular machinery preserves until sequencing (BS-seq). Additionally, due to the slower induction of M.CviPI in leaves, longer estradiol treatments (12–24 h) are necessary to detect these accessible regions effectively.

Finally, we compared the accessible regions detected by iNOMe-seq at 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h with regions detected as accessible by enzymatic NOMe-seq [12]. We performed enzymatic NOMe-seq on seedlings and called DNA-accessible regions following the same method we used for iNOMe-seq (see Materials and methods). Our results showed that there is a good overlap between the regions detected by iNOMe-seq and NOMe-seq, in particular, for longer induction times (see Fig S8G). In CpG context, iNOMe-seq identifies more regions than NOMe-seq, but the numbers become similar for non-CpG contexts. This provides additional evidence of the reliability of our method to detect DNA-accessible regions.

iNOME-seq accurately determines nucleosome phasing

The profile of GpC methylation adjacent to transcription start sites suggested that iNOMe-seq also captures nucleosomes flanking these regions, which is less apparent in ATAC-seq data (see correlation values in Fig. 3A, B). This observation is supported by the phased distribution of iNOMe-seq signal at transcriptionally active genes, which resembles the nucleosome phasing identified by MNase-seq (Fig. 3A). To define the sensitivity of iNOMe-seq in the analysis of nucleosome dynamics, we split genes into four quantiles according to their level of expression (high, medium, low, and none). The genome-wide nucleosome phasing profile detected by iNOMe-seq was highly correlated with the profile of MNase-seq, independently of the level of expression (Fig. 3B). The average distance between nucleosome midpoints as determined by correlating the methylation status between cytosines at GpC pairs was 145–150 bp (Fig. 3B). Identification of protein footprints on DNA with iNOME

Previous studies have shown that NOMe-seq coupled to nanopore sequencing can detect potential binding sites of transcription factors (TFs) [19]. We hypothesized that iNOMe-seq data could do the same. To this end, we extracted unmethylated gaps processed from iNOMe-data (see Methods). We found that their sizes aligned with the expected footprints of bound proteins. The distribution of unmethylated runs revealed a higher density of gaps at ~ 50 bp (potential TF binding events) and ~ 150 bp (potential mononucleosomes) in root tissue (Fig. 4A). We annotated the ~ 50 bp gaps detected in roots and found that these short gaps are enriched in exons and upstream of transcription start sites (TSS) but depleted in transposable elements and introns (Fig. 4B, C). To further study the link between potential TF binding sites inferred from iNOMe-seq signal and genes, the distribution of the short gaps was plotted around genes, which revealed an enrichment upstream of genes, predominantly in two discrete regions proximal to the TSS (Fig. 4D). To quantify the link between potential TF binding sites and genes, the correlation between these sites and DNase I hypersensitivity sites (DHSs) was calculated. We found a positive correlation between the short unmethylated gaps of iNOMe-seq data, indicative of TF binding, and DHSs (Pearson correlation coefficient R = 0.79, p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 4E). To investigate the link between potential TF binding sites and gene expression, we considered the expression of genes with and without such sites within 1 kb upstream of the TSS. While most genes do not have nearby potential TF binding sites (Fig. 4F), those that do are expressed at higher levels than genes without (Fig. 4G) (Anova; p < 5.1e-4). Next, we performed a motif enrichment analysis [20], which revealed the most enriched motifs to correspond to transcription factors that have high expression and that are linked to tissue-specific expression (Fig. 4H and Additional file 1: Figure S9). As a proof of concept, we focused our analysis on “operon-like” metabolic gene clusters because the constituent genes are typically co-expressed in specific tissues and because the clusters are under strong selection and prone to genomic re-organisation [21, 22]. We first conducted a transcriptome analysis on genes of the thalianol, marneral, tirucalladeniol, and arabidiol gene clusters [23–25], finding notable differences in expression between accessions (Fig. 5B), including failure to detect expression for several genes in the thalianol and tirucalladeniol gene clusters in some of the accessions. We then investigated the chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation profile of the four gene clusters in the four accessions as well as the reference accession. We found structural variations along with differences in chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation (Fig. 5C, D and Additional file 1: Figures S12 and S13). As expected, accessible chromatin reported by iNOMe-seq was associated with expression levels. While most transposon-related sequences had high levels of DNA methylation, some had accessible chromatin according to the iNOMe-seq signal. When looking at the structural variations present in different accessions, we found small insertions, deletions, and translocations that affected genes in the tirucalladeniol and thalianol gene clusters (Fig. 5C, D). Collectively, our data shows that iNOMe-seq facilitates comparative genomic and epigenomic analyses in natural plant populations.

Discussion

Chromatin accessibility plays a central role in the control of gene expression. Its levels of accessibility are controlled by various features such as DNA methylation and nucleosome occupancy [5], and patterns of chromatin accessibility inform on the regulatory mechanisms that control gene expression [6]. Here we describe a new in vivo method that reports chromatin structure in plants based on an inducible GpC methyltransferase construct. We first demonstrate that despite the presence of naturally occurring non-CpG methylation, iNOMe-seq can effectively and accurately detect chromatin regions with high and low accessibility throughout the genome. iNOMe-seq detects regions that appear to be only transiently accessible, providing a record of accessibility in an organ, and it can accurately and efficiently profile nucleosome occupancy. The detection of both DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, and potential TF binding sites could improve our understanding of the mechanisms involved in the regulation of gene expression.

Typically, the study of DNA accessibility, DNA methylation, nucleosome occupancy, and transcription factor binding sites is completed using multiple sequencing methods. These methods include the detection of DNA accessibility by ATAC-seq [9], mapping of DNA methylation by BS-seq [26], the identification of nucleosome occupancy by MNase-seq [11], and the discovery of protein binding sites by ChIP–seq [6]. Because iNOME-seq achieves this with a single DNA sequencing modality, this method greatly reduces the cost and complexity of such multi-omic studies.

To highlight the potential of iNOMe-seq, we compared our results to ATAC-seq and MNase-seq. iNOMe-seq not only detects ATAC-seq accessible chromatin with a similar effectiveness, but because of its higher sensitivity it also detects regions around genes with low expression and lower accessibility. These genes may be transiently transcribed or only in a few cells [27, 28]. The superior power of iNOMe-seq to detect these comes from the DNA methylase being active for several hours in living cells and thus providing an integration over a longer time than ATAC-seq, which profiles accessibility at a single time point after DNA extraction. Notably, genes that are only sporadically expressed can have essential functions [29]. Our data showed an increase in the number of accessible regions detected following prolonged induction of the M.CviPI methylase. Notably, the majority of regions identified during shorter induction times were also detected under longer induction conditions. It is important to highlight that enzymatic NOMe-seq has been demonstrated to identify comparable quantities of accessible DNA to those detected by ATAC-seq, with substantial overlap in the accessible regions detected by both methods [30]. We have performed enzymatic NOMe-seq and found that indeed iNOMe-seq detected a larger number of accessible regions compared to NOMe-seq in CpG context. Nevertheless, for non-CpG context, the number of regions detected as accessible is comparable.

Furthermore, unlike ATAC-seq, iNOMe-seq signal is strongly correlated with that from MNase-seq, indicating that iNOMe-seq can detect the positioning of nucleosomes, which plays an important role in gene expression [31, 32]. In vitro modification of chromatin with EcoGII methyltransferase, which deposits m6A at accessible chromatin, coupled to long-read nanopore sequencing has also been shown to allow the simultaneous mapping of accessibility and DNA methylation in plants [33]. However, this approach requires the isolation of plant nuclei from fixed tissue and the optimization of methyltransferase activity.

As part of the iNOMe-seq workflow, the genome-wide cytosine methylation landscape offers valuable insights into the temporal regulation of genes and transposable elements [34, 35]. Accessible chromatin is identified by comparison with the BS-seq profile of un-induced and induced plants. Importantly, we can detect small stretches of reduced accessibility nested within the larger accessible regions. We interpret the reduced accessibility as being due to potential TF binding, which precludes binding of the induced DNA methylase [19]. In agreement with these stretches of DNA sequences being transcription factor binding sites, we detected the enrichment of motifs for several highly expressed tissue-specific transcription factors, such as PLT1 and STOP1 [36, 37]. However, the sensitivity of iNOME-seq is limited at TF binding sites not covered by GC sites and for TFs that display low occupancy of binding sites. Since the density of GC sites is the main factor affecting the sensitivity of iNOME-seq in identifying TF binding sites and the fact that iNOMe-seq and enzymatic NOMe-seq detect a similar number of regions in non-CpG context suggests that the two methods would perform similarly in detecting most TF binding sites. However, iNOMe-seq detects more regions as accessible in CpG context compared to enzymatic NOMe-seq, which suggests that at those TF binding sites overlapping GCG sites, iNOMe-seq could perform better. Nevertheless, this is specific for each binding site and needs to be investigated systematically in future research. In conclusion, iNOMe-seq is a new method that can profile DNA accessibility, DNA methylation, nucleosome occupancy, and TF binding sites with a single DNA sequencing modality, with an accuracy that matches that of other methods commonly used to measure chromatin information. Furthermore, we demonstrated that iNOMe-seq facilitates comparative genomic and epigenomic analyses in natural populations.

Conclusions

We show that iNOMe-seq represents a notable advancement in epigenomic profiling by enabling the simultaneous in vivo analysis of chromatin accessibility, nucleosome occupancy, DNA-binding protein localisation, and DNA methylation. By leveraging m5C methyltransferase to mark accessible GpC cytosines in vivo, iNOMe-seq effectively distinguishes open chromatin regions from nucleosome-occupied sites. Its application in Arabidopsis thaliana demonstrates greater accuracy in chromatin accessibility quantification compared to existing approaches. Additionally, the method facilitates high-resolution spatial and temporal mapping of chromatin dynamics, facilitating our understanding of the interplay between epigenetic modifications and gene regulation across diverse tissues and genetic backgrounds.

Material and methods

Plant lines and growth conditions

The plant lines used in this study were derived from A. thaliana wild-type accessions—Col-0 (Columbia-0), Ler (Landsberg erecta), Ws-2 (Wassilewskija-2), Cvi-0 (Cape Verde Islands), and C24—to generate MCviPI transgenic lines. Transgenic and WT seeds were grown at 20 °C in long photoperiod conditions (16-h/8-h light/dark cycles). Seeds were sterilized with 10% sodium hypochlorite (V/V) for 5 min and washed in sterile water thoroughly five times. Plant growth media were prepared with half-strength MS medium (Murashige and Skoog, Duchefa Biochemie), 1% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.8% phytoagar (Duchefa Biochemie). Seedlings of MCviPI transgenic and control plants were subjected to β-estradiol induction and mock treatment for 6, 12, and 24 h; the stock of estradiol was 10 mM in DMSO, with mock being DMSO in water. Eight-day-old seedlings were transferred to Whatman paper soaked with either induction or mock solution in plates, so that the plants were in contact with the solution and to avoid drought stress. The plates were wrapped with cling film before being moved to a growth cabinet.

Generation and selection of indMCviPI transgenic lines

We modified the plant transformation vector pMDC7, which enables the controlled ectopic expression upon exposure to β−17-estradiol [38]. We removed the OLE1-RFP visual seed selection reporter after digestion of the pUbiCAS9-Red [39] with SpeI and PacI, and overhangs were blunted. The OLE1-RFP insert was cloned in pMDC7 previously digested PmeI restriction site, to generate the pMDC7-OLE1. The MCviPI sequence was codon optimized for Arabidopsis and chemically synthesized (Genosys) including a nuclear localization signal and a HA-tag at the N and C terminus, respectively. The M.CviPI transgene was cloned into pDNOR207 and then cloned into pMDC7-OLE1 by Gateway recombination.

Arabidopsis plants were transformed using the floral dip method [40]. T0 seeds (RFP +) were selected and grown to the T1 generation. From the subsequent lines that showed a 3:1 RFP + to non-RFP ratio, seeds were selected and grown to the T2 generation. In the T2 progeny, homozygous lines (lines with 100% RFP + seeds) were selected and propagated as stably expressing lines. To select a responsive and tightly controlled indMCviPI line, seedlings (4 days old) from homozygous lines were transferred to 40 µM β−17-estradiol for 10 days and monitored for root length differences compared to control plants. Root growth was calculated by measuring the distance between the root tip position immediately after transferring and after 10 days of treatment using ImageJ, and statistical analysis was performed (t-test, p-value ≥ 0.05) to compare the differences in root growth. To identify the most responsive indMCviPI line in the Col-0 background, we selected 4 independent homozygous lines and carried Out BS-seq analysis after 48-h 10 mM β-estradiol induction. Finally, indMCviPI −1a was selected for further study.

For the induction of MCviPI expression to map accessible chromatin, seedlings of transgenic and control plants were subjected to β−17-estradiol induction and mock treatment for 6, 12, and 24 h (10 mM β−17-estradiol in DMSO and DMSO in water for mock). Eight-day-old seedlings were transferred to a Whatman paper soaked wet with a film so that the plants were in contact with the solution and to avoid drought stress. Approximately 30 seedlings were placed on one plate in two rows. Plates were wrapped with cling film before induction in a growth cabinet.

Enzymatic NOMe-seq preparation from isolated nuclei

Nuclei were isolated from 200 to 400 mg of 14-day-after-germination seedlings using gentleMACS. Pre-cooled gentleMACS C Tubes were loaded with 2 mL nuclei isolation buffer (Miltenyi Biotec) followed by immediate transfer of tissue pieces. Samples were dissociated using the gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) running program 4C_nuclei_1, with tubes kept on pre-chilled Octo Coolers throughout. Tubes were placed on ice and incubated for an additional 5–10 min after dissociation to optimize cell lysis. The resulting suspension was passed through a 50-µm cell strainer, washing the nuclei with washing buffer. The suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at 4 °C, removing supernatant afterwards. The nuclei pellet was resuspended in final buffer, and concentration was determined using flow cytometry (Sony MA900). One million nuclei were resuspended in 75 µl of 1 × GpC methylase buffer (NEB), and we added 7.5 µl 10 × GpC methylase buffer (NEB), 1.5 µl 32 mM SAM, 50 µl GpC methyltransferase (4U/µl) (NEB) and 16 µl H2O, incubating the reaction for 8 min at 37 °C and adding 25 µl GpC Methyltransferase (100 U) and 0.75 µl of 32 mM SAM, mixing and incubating at 37 °C for 8 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 500 µl of 1X PBS, mixing, and centrifuging for 5 min at 800 g at 4 °C, followed by phenol/chloroform treatment for DNA isolation.

BS-seq library preparation and sequencing

For whole genome bisulfite sequencing, after estradiol or mock treatment, seedling root and leaf samples were collected from 20–30 seedlings at each treatment time. Root and leaf tissues were separated by cutting the seedlings in two at the lower part of the hypocotyl with a scalpel blade. The tissue samples were collected in 1.5-ml Eppendorf tubes and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. DNA extraction was carried out using the Qiagen Plant DNeasy kit (Qiagen), and DNA was quantified using Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Invitrogen). Approximately 100 ng of genomic DNA in total 100 µl volume was sonicated using a Bioruptor (Diagenode Inc.) with 30 s ON and 30 s OFF at a “low” setting to generate 350 bp DNA fragments. The libraries were generated using the Illumina TruSeq Nano kit (Illumina, CA, U.S.A) according to the manufacturer’s manual. Size-selected and adapter-ligated DNA was subjected to bisulfite treatment using an Epitect Plus DNA Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s manual. PCR amplification was performed using Kapa Hifi Uracil + DNA polymerase (Kapa Biosystem, MA, U.S.A) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To check size distribution of DNA fragments, samples were run on bioanalyzer (Agilent 2100). Indexed bisulfite treated libraries were sequenced with 2 × 150-bp paired-end reads on an Illumina HiSeq2000 instrument. Maximum of 16 different indexed libraries were pooled in a single lane run. The Illumina Real Time Analysis 1.13.48. software was used for image analysis and to extract sequence data.

ATAC-seq library preparation and sequencing

Plant leaves were finely chopped with a razor blade in a petri dish with 1 mL of nuclei extraction buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, protease inhibitor cocktail EDTA-free (Roche)). Chopped tissues were filtered with a 50-µm filter and centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed 3 times with nuclei extraction buffer. After washing, the pellet was resuspended in sucrose buffer (0.25 mM sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM MgCl2, protease inhibitor cocktail EDTA-free (Roche)) and DMSO was added to 10% final. Samples were then placed at − 80 °C in CoolCell freezing containers (Corning) for cryopreservation and storage. For each ATAC-seq experiment, 75,000 nuclei were thawed and centrifuged at 600 g for 5 min to remove storage buffer. Transposition reaction, DNA purification, and PCR amplification were performed using the ATAC-seq kit from Active Motif. Libraries were purified using 0.9 × SPRISelect (Beckman Coulter) and size-selected for 150–500 bp fragments using PippinHT (Sage Science). Finally, the quality of the libraries was assessed using 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and then subjected to 2 × 150 bp paired-end high-throughput sequencing by NovaSeq 6000.

De novo genome assembly and annotation

For the extraction of HMW-DNA, leaf tissues were ground with liquid nitrogen, and the powder was incubated for 45 min at 55 °C in freshly prepared, pre-heated lysis buffer (1% sodium metabisulfite, 1% PVP40, 0.5 M NaCl, 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, 50 mM EDTA pH 8, 1.5% SDS, 2% β-mercaptoethanol). We added to the lysate 60 µl of 20 mg/ml PureLink ™ RNAseA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated the mixture for 10 min. To precipitate proteins, we added 600 µl of 5 M potassium acetate followed by 2.4 ml of 25:24:1 (v/v/v) phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol and incubated for 10 min. After centrifuging at 4400 g for 10 min, the upper phase was transferred to a new tube and mixed with 24:1 (v/v) chloroform:isoamyl alcohol for 10 min with slow rotation. Following a second centrifugation at 4400 g for 10 min, the upper phase was transferred to a new tube and cleaned twice with SeraMag SpeedBeads® Magnetic Beads (GE Healthcare) using a magnet. HMW-DNA was eluted with 50 µl EB (Qiagen) after incubation at 37 °C for 30 min.

For the construction of CLR libraries, 10 µg of HMW-DNA that had been sheared twice with a needle (FINE-JECT 0.45 × 25 mm, Henke Sass Wolf) was prepared with the SMRTbell Express Template Preparation Kit 2.0 (PacBio). The libraries were size-selected with the BluePippin system (SageScience) with a 30 kb cutoff in a 0.75% DF Marker U1 high-pass 30–40 kb vs3 gel cassette (Biozym). The library was sequenced with sequencing primer v4 and no pre-extension time on a single SMRT Cell (30-h movie time) with the Sequel II system (PacBio) using the Binding Kit 2.0 (PacBio).

For genome assembly, we used CLR subreads larger than 10 kb for assembly using Canu v2.0 [41]. For the evaluation of accuracy and completeness, we scaffolded contigs > 100 kb with RagTag v1.1.1 [42] using a hard-masked version of TAIR10 as the reference genome. We mapped gene annotations to new genome assemblies using Liftoff [43] using TAIR10 as the reference genome. We annotated transposable elements (TEs) and other repeat regions using Extensive de-novo TE Annotator (EDTA) v1.9.7 [44].

Data processing for iNOMe-seq, NOMe-seq, and ATAC-seq analysis

Methylation correlation for each of the GCG, GCHG, and GCHH was computed for both leaf and root estradiol-treated and control samples. Our data showed a strong methylation correlation in GCG and GCHG contexts; however, although the methylation in the GCHH context was also correlated, it was weaker as compared to that of symmetrical contexts. Based on this observation, a bins method was used to compute differentially methylated regions (DMRs). Initially, DMRs from iNOMe-seq data were computed with varying methylation differences (10–100%) and bin sizes (50–5000 bp). After selecting the optimal bin size and methylation difference, we computed DMRs as accessible chromatin values. To compare these DMRs with ATAC-seq-identified accessible regions, precision, recall, and F-score were calculated (Additional file 1: Figure S6). Each set of identified DMRs was compared to the ATAC-seq accessible regions, and for each of them, the proportion of true positives (TP—regions detected by both iNOMe-seq and ATAC-seq), false positives (FP—regions detected only by iNOMe-seq), and false negatives (FN—regions detected 35only by ATAC-seq) was calculated. We assumed that the regions detected by ATAC-seq were true positives. Using these calculated proportions, we computed the recall (TP/(TP + FN)), precision (TP/(TP + FP)) and F-score (2T P/(2T P + F P + F N)). Finally, we selected the methylation difference threshold and bin size that lead to the highest F-score. Based on calculation, the precision, recall, and F-score, a value of 30% for methylation difference and 700 bp bin size were found to be the optimum for the comparison of accessible chromatin regions by both methods.

For enzymatic NOMe-seq, we performed the same analysis and found that a value of 60% for methylation difference and 700 bp bin size resulted in the optimum set of parameters for the comparison of accessible chromatin regions by ATAC-seq and NOMe-seq methods (Additional file 1: Figure S14). It is worthwhile noting that, unsurprisingly, enzymatic NOMe-seq led to a higher level of methylation than an in vivo inducible system (iNOMe-seq) and, thus, the methylation difference is higher for the former.

Differentially methylated regions/accessible regions identified by iNOMe-seq were compared to accessible open chromatin identified by ATAC-seq. So, any difference in accessibility observed in control and induced samples around the targeted features was compared to the accessibility of these features by ATAC-seq data. In order to carry Out iNOMe signal detection around targeted features, the proportion of methylation percentage at each individual cytosine was used to compute a moving average with a window size of 500 bp for all samples. The moving averages were then used to determine the methylation difference between control and treatment samples. The difference was calculated by subtracting the moving average of control samples from the moving average of treatment samples. This process allowed for the easier visualization of induced GC methylation across the genome by removing naturally occurring background methylation. As iNOMe-seq detects a larger amount of accessible DNA than ATAC-seq, the distribution of iNOMe peaks throughout the genome was checked. To do this, the R package ChIPpeakAnno [45] was used to annotate DMRs and to identify the closest gene associated. This package also calculated the distance between genes and DMRs; these distances were used to determine the distribution of iNOMe peaks around genes. By comparing these data to various features, the iNOMe peaks were separated into different categories; these categories included genes, transposable elements, upstream, downstream, and intergenic regions.

Transcription factor binding site identification

These cytosines are categorized based on their context and compared with the corresponding differentially methylated regions (DMRs). Any cytosines falling Outside of these DMRs are excluded from further analysis. Next, the average methylation score is calculated using the remaining cytosines, and any cytosines below this value are removed. Gaps between the remaining cytosines are identified, merged if overlapping, and then filtered to retain only gaps within the range of 10–60 base pairs in width. These remaining gaps are compared with all cytosines in the genome, and only gaps that overlap with at least one cytosine are retained. Finally, the gaps are overlapped with CpG islands, and only gaps that do not overlap with CpG islands are kept. The resulting gaps that meet these criteria are considered potential transcription factor (TF) binding sites. Using XSTREME [46], motifs that are enriched inside potential TF binding sites compared to DMRs that do not overlap potential TF binding sites are identified. The enriched transcription factors (TFs) are cross-referenced with RNA-seq data to determine their expression levels.
