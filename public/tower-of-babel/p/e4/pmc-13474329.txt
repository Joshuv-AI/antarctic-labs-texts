Catecholamine-Induced Cardiomyopathy Initially Misdiagnosed as Myocarditis in a Patient With Pheochromocytoma
Authors: Shunyu Yao, Bi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy (CICMP) is myocardial injury from excessive catecholamine secretion by pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma (PPGL), with reversible cardiac dysfunction post-tumor resection.Case SummaryA 29-year-old female initially hospitalized for severe myocarditis recovered but presented 3 years later with marked blood pressure fluctuations. She was diagnosed with CICMP secondary to pheochromocytoma and managed with aggressive blood pressure control and successful tumor resection, achieving favorable outcomes with stable blood pressure off medications.DiscussionLiterature review shows CICMP is occult. The reminder for clinicians from this case is that for atypical myocarditis, especially in patients experiencing blood pressure fluctuations, PPGL should be considered, and relevant examinations should be promptly completed.Take-Home MessageBlood pressure lability in a young patient with suspected myocarditis should immediately raise suspicion for PPGL and prompt biochemical screening.Key words: catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, heart failure, pheochromocytomas

History of Presentation

A 29-year-old female office worker was admitted due to “headache accompanied by chest pain, nausea, and vomiting for 13 hours.” Thirteen hours prior to admission, the patient awoke with a sensation of “intracranial vessel rupture,” followed by tolerable headache, chest pain, palpitations, nausea, and vomiting. Self-measured blood pressure at home showed a maximum systolic value of 160 mm Hg. There were no significant emotional triggers. She presented to a local hospital, where electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus tachycardia and T-wave inversions in V2-V4. Cardiac enzymes were negative. White blood cell count was 15.06 × 109/L. Head computed tomography was unremarkable. After symptomatic treatment with antibiotics and fluid replacement, her symptoms partially improved, and she was discharged. Two hours later, her chest pain, palpitations, and nausea worsened significantly, leading her to our hospital's gastroenterology clinic. Blood tests again showed elevated white blood cell count and neutrophils, but amylase and lipase were normal. Subsequently, due to persistent chest pain and palpitations, she was evaluated in the cardiology clinic. ECG revealed sinus tachycardia and ST-T changes (ST-segment depression in over 6 leads, ST-segment elevation in aVR) (Figure 1). Cardiac enzymes were elevated: creatine kinase isoenzyme MB (CK-MB) 60.7 ng/mL (normal range <4.9 ng/mL) and cardiac troponin T 1.370 μg/L (normal range <0.03 μg/L). She was admitted with a provisional diagnosis of “suspected myocarditis.”

•This case highlights the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for pheochromocytoma-associated catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy in patients presenting with unexplained severe myocarditis and labile blood pressure, even when initial manifestations mimic viral myocarditis.

Past Medical History

The patient had no history of recent upper respiratory infection, no history of hypertension, diabetes, or coronary heart disease. She denied family history of genetic disorders.

Investigations

Physical examination showed temperature: 36.9 °C, pulse: 133 bpm, respiration: 22 breaths/min, blood pressure: 143/89 mm Hg. Her neck veins were not distended, and hepatojugular reflux was negative. Lungs were clear to auscultation. Heart rate was 133 bpm with no murmurs.

Laboratory tests showed PO2 of 55 mm Hg and lactate 3.6 mmol/L. Cardiac troponin I was 4.17 ng/mL (normal range <0.05 ng/mL), and brain natriuretic peptide 336 pg/mL (normal range <100 pg/mL). ECG showed sinus tachycardia with widespread ST-segment depression, but the ST-segment elevated in aVL and aVR leads. Echocardiogram revealed severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 25% and moderate mitral and tricuspid regurgitation.

Management

This patient was diagnosed with severe myocarditis and was admitted to the cardiac care unit. Her condition deteriorated rapidly 4 hours post admission with acute pulmonary edema, hypotension (blood pressure 80/41 mm Hg), and wide-complex tachycardia. Despite receiving aggressive treatments including mechanical ventilation and vasopressors, her condition continued to deteriorate, with hypotension, tachycardia, and progressively rising lactate levels. Serial echocardiography showed a progressive decline in LVEF to 12%. The patient was diagnosed with severe myocarditis complicated by cardiogenic shock and was treated with veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). Over the following days, her hemodynamics gradually stabilized but was accompanied by fluctuations in blood pressure, with systolic pressure ranging from 90 to 150 mm Hg and diastolic pressure from 50 to 90 mm Hg. A comparison of bedside chest x-ray showed that pulmonary edema significantly improved compared with that at admission (Figure 2). ECMO was successfully removed on day 4, and serial echocardiograms showed progressive recovery of LVEF to 47%. She was discharged after 26 days of hospitalization, with no residual symptoms at the time of discharge.

Over the next 3 years, the patient experienced no palpitations, shortness of breath, or other discomforts, and both blood pressure and heart rate remained normal. However, the patient was readmitted due to palpitations, dizziness, and headache 3 years later. On admission, the blood pressure was 190/110 mm Hg with a heart rate of 123 beats per minute. ECG showed sinus tachycardia (Figure 3). Echocardiogram revealed an LVEF of 67%. Secondary hypertension workup revealed elevated catecholamine metabolites (Table 1). Adrenal computed tomography identified a 3.8 × 5.0-cm left adrenal mass suggestive of pheochromocytoma (Figure 4).

Contrast-Enhanced Abdominal CT

A well-circumscribed, round soft-tissue mass measuring approximately 3.8 × 5.0 cm was observed in the left adrenal region. The lesion exhibited heterogeneous density on noncontrast images, with the solid component showing intermediate attenuation. Following contrast administration, the mass demonstrated marked heterogeneous enhancement. Multiple cystic areas and punctate high-density foci were noted within it. The mass was supplied by the left adrenal artery. These imaging features are suggestive of a neoplastic lesion, likely a pheochromocytoma. Outcome and Follow-Up

Following the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma, preoperative preparation was initiated with the nonselective α-adrenoceptor antagonist phenoxybenzamine. The starting dose was 10 mg twice daily, which was gradually titrated over 14 days to a final dose of 30 mg twice daily along with the beta-blocker propranolol to achieve target blood pressure (<120/80 mm Hg sitting) and heart rate (60-80 beats/min). Concurrent volume expansion was implemented preoperatively through a high-sodium diet and intravenous normal saline. Intraoperatively, volume expansion was maintained with infusion of hydroxyethyl starch and succinylated gelatin to prevent hemodynamic instability.

Finally, she underwent successful left adrenalectomy, with histopathology confirming pheochromocytoma (Figure 5). During the next 3 years of follow-up, her blood pressure remained in the normal range without antihypertensive medications.

Discussion

Catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy (CICMP) represents a reversible form of acute heart failure resulting from excessive catecholamine secretion, most commonly from pheochromocytoma or paraganglioma.1 The pathophysiological mechanisms involve direct myocardial toxicity, microvascular dysfunction, and calcium overload.2,3 It is critical to distinguish the pathophysiology underlying CICMP from that of true myocarditis. While viral/lymphocytic myocarditis is characterized by lymphocytic infiltration and myocyte necrosis, catecholamine-induced myocardial injury produces contraction band necrosis, which is a direct toxic effect of excess catecholamines on cardiomyocytes.2 Previous studies have extensively characterized the systemic effects of catecholamine excess in pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas, emphasizing the cardiovascular manifestations that may mimic other conditions.4

Our case highlights a typical diagnostic challenge. The initial presentation closely mimicked acute viral myocarditis, leading to a delayed diagnosis of the underlying tumor. In retrospect, the patient's marked blood pressure variability during her first admission (systolic pressures ranging from 90 to 150 mm Hg) should have prompted earlier biochemical screening for catecholamine excess. Per Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guidelines, unexplained paroxysmal hypertension, headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis warrant immediate measurement of plasma-free or urinary fractionated metanephrines.5 This testing was not performed during the initial hospitalization, representing a missed diagnostic opportunity.

Management of CICMP requires a dual approach: aggressive hemodynamic support in the acute phase and meticulous preoperative preparation before tumor resection.5, 6, 7 The use of VA-ECMO in our patient underscores the potential utility of mechanical circulatory support in severe cardiogenic shock. Preoperative α-blockade remains essential to minimize perioperative cardiovascular morbidity.8

This case reinforces the need for clinicians to consider pheochromocytoma in the differential diagnosis of unexplained acute myocarditis, particularly in patients with labile hypertension, paroxysmal symptoms, or incomplete recovery. CICMP can present subtly and is potentially reversible. However, the most important thing is to increase awareness of CICMP.

This case has several limitations. First, the patient did not undergo myocardial biopsy or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging to rule out classic viral myocarditis. Second, although the patient was ultimately diagnosed with pheochromocytoma, no genetic testing was performed. The absence of genetic testing for hereditary syndromes (MEN2, VHL, NF1, SDHx) in this young patient (<45 years) represents an additional limitation, as current guidelines mandate such testing to identify at-risk family members and guide surveillance.5 Most importantly, at the time of the initial hospitalization, because the doctors only focused on cardiovascular symptoms such as myocardial injury and hemodynamic instability and overlooked the clinical manifestations caused by endocrine disorders, plasma-free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines were not adequately tested, leading to a missed diagnosis.

The most important educational takeaway from this case is the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas in patients presenting with unexplained blood pressure lability, even when the clinical picture is dominated by cardiac manifestations. Such hemodynamic instability should prompt immediate biochemical screening, as early diagnosis and treatment are critical to prevent life-threatening cardiovascular complications and achieve curative outcomes.

Conclusions

This case underscores that severe myocarditis can be the initial manifestation of CICMP caused by pheochromocytoma. Given the potential for cure with tumor resection, routine screening for pheochromocytoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients with unexplained severe acute myocarditis, particularly those with suggestive features like significant blood pressure lability.
