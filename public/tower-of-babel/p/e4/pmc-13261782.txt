Dental Implants in Adults With Intellectual Disabilities: A Multicenter Retrospective Study. Part 1: Implant Outcomes
Authors: Márcio Diniz‐Freitas, Manuel Bratos‐Bobo, Manuel Bratos‐Morillo, Denise Faulks, Marie‐Sophie Bogner, Melanie Derchu, Caroline Eschevins, Emmanuel Nicolas, Pierre Yves Cousson, Chantal Auduc, Yoo Soo‐Yeon, Pedro Diz‐Dios, Pablo Gasamáns‐García, Jacobo Limeres‐Posse, Paolo Appendino, Simone Buttiglieri, Francesco Della Ferrera, Roberto Rozza, Fabio Brusamolino, Javier Fernández‐Feijoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDespite the potential benefits of implant‐supported rehabilitation, data on implant performance and peri‐implant health in adults with intellectual disabilities (ID) are limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate dental implant outcomes in adults with non‐syndromic ID and to identify variables associated with peri‐implant health, complications, and implant loss.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study included adults with non‐syndromic ID who received at least one dental implant and had a minimum follow‐up of 36 months. Dental implants placed in 137 patients across 7 specialised centers in Spain, France, Italy, and South Korea were analysed. Standardised data were collected on patient characteristics, oral health status, implant design, prosthetic variables, and follow‐up care. Clinical outcomes were categorised as peri‐implant health, mucositis, peri‐implantitis, or implant loss.ResultsOf the 453 implants placed, 418 (92.3%) remained functional at a mean follow‐up of 75.9 ± 42.7 months (range: 36–211 months). Among the 445 implants evaluated for clinical outcomes, 63.6% were classified as healthy, 23.4% as presenting with mucositis, 4.4% with peri‐implantitis, and 7.3% were lost during the follow‐up period. Tissue‐level implant type, cement‐retained fixed prostheses, and the use of general anaesthesia during the impression step were significantly associated with peri‐implant clinical outcomes. No variables were identified as statistically significant predictors of implant loss.ConclusionsDental implant therapy represents a viable long‐term treatment option for adults with non‐syndromic ID. Despite the clinical challenges of this population, high survival and success rates can be achieved, particularly when appropriate implant selection, prosthetic retention systems, and behavioural management techniques are implemented.

Introduction

Effective dental care is essential for the rehabilitation of individuals with disabilities, playing a critical role in improving their overall quality of life. Patients with intellectual disabilities (ID) or cognitive impairment (CI) often face considerable challenges in maintaining adequate oral hygiene and implementing preventive measures. They present a high prevalence of dental caries and periodontal disease and higher levels of unmet dental need than the general population [1]. These difficulties, combined with behavioral challenges, mean that they often present as adults with multiple extracted or unrestorable teeth. In recent years, improvements in life expectancy and quality of life, along with greater social inclusion of individuals with ID, have led to a growing demand for dental care within this population. However, these patients often face disparities in access to care and are less likely to receive advanced treatment options compared to the general population [2, 3].

Dental implants are widely considered the first‐line treatment for the replacement of missing teeth, with an average 10‐year success rate of approximately 95% in the general population [4]. However, only a limited number of case series and retrospective studies have evaluated implant therapy in individuals with ID [5]. Some of these suggest that implant success rates may be comparable to those observed in individuals without disabilities [6]. Nonetheless, existing studies often involve heterogeneous samples of patients with special needs, including individuals with varying degrees of intellectual, physical, and systemic conditions, limiting the generalisability of their findings [7].

The present study aims to analyse the clinical outcomes of dental implants in patients with non‐syndromic intellectual disability, and to identify potential risk indicators associated with implant failure and biological and prosthetic complications in this specific population. It was hypothesised that biological complications and dental implant survival in adults with non‐syndromic intellectual disability would be clinically acceptable over long‐term follow‐up, and that peri‐implant clinical outcomes would be associated with dental implants and prosthesis‐related factors, particularly those influencing plaque control, access for oral hygiene, and maintenance.

Part 1 of this study focuses on implant survival and biologic complications, while Part 2 will address prosthetic rehabilitation and mechanical complications.

Materials and Methods

A multicenter retrospective study was conducted to evaluate implant survival rates and the incidence of biological complications associated with dental implants in patients with non‐syndromic intellectual disability (ID). The study included patients from 7 clinical centers across 4 countries: France (Université Clermont Auvergne, CROC, and CHU Clermont‐Ferrand, Service d'Odontologie, Clermont‐Ferrand), Italy (Azienda Ospedaliera Mauriziano di Torino; University of Milan, University Hospital Santi Paolo e Carlo; Azienda Socio Sanitaria Territoriale dei Sette Laghi, Varese), Spain (Clínica Dental Bratos, Madrid; and Health Research Institute of Santiago de Compostela, University of Santiago de Compostela), and South Korea (Department of Prosthodontics & Dental Research Institute, Seoul National University Dental Hospital, School of Dentistry, Seoul National University, Seoul).

Inclusion criteria comprised (i) adults (≥ 18 years); (ii) with a confirmed diagnosis of non‐syndromic ID or diagnosis of ID in patients with syndromes non‐related to poor dental implants outcomes (i.e., exclusions include Down syndrome, Papillon‐Lefevre syndrome and ectodermal dysplasia for example) or diagnosis of CI in patients diagnosed before implant placement (e.g., acquired brain damage or Alzheimer's disease); (iii) the placement of at least one dental implant; and (iv) a minimum follow‐up period of 36 months—except in cases of implant failure, where follow‐up was concluded at the time of implant loss.

Exclusion criteria included (i) serious systemic complications (e.g., immunosuppression, cancer); (ii) medical conditions related to poor dental implants outcomes (e.g., diabetes); (iii) bone diseases related to poor dental implants outcomes (e.g., osteogenesis imperfecta, osteopetrosis); (iv) tobacco use; (v) previous radiotherapy in the head and neck area.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the research ethics committees of the four countries in which the participating centers were located. In addition, approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Santiago for coordination of the multicenter protocol [University of Santiago de Compostela (USC 10/2024)]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to inclusion. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable local regulatory requirements.

To ensure uniformity in methodology and comparability of results, a standardised data collection protocol was established and followed across all participating centers. Data was collected retrospectively, from patient records. This included a coding manual, standardised case report forms, and quality control procedures, all of which were discussed and agreed upon by the investigators. Regular meetings were held with research teams from the participating sites to calibrate criteria, review data collection progress, resolve discrepancies, and ensure adherence to the protocol. All data were submitted to a central coordinating center (University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain) for analysis.

Baseline data were collected at the time of first implant placement and included variables related to the patient and oral health status. During subsequent clinical visits—whether for surgical procedures, prosthetic rehabilitation, or routine follow‐up—data pertaining to the surgical procedure, prosthetic treatment, and clinical outcomes were systematically recorded.

Patient‐related variables included age, sex, systemic diagnosis (i.e., intellectual disability of unknown aetiology, perinatal encephalopathy, neurodevelopmental disorders, rare diseases, acquired brain injury, or autism spectrum disorders etc.), presence of comorbidities (i.e., epilepsy or hypothyroidism), and current pharmacological treatments potentially associated with implant prognosis (e.g., proton pump inhibitors, serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or anti‐hypertensive drugs) [8].

Oral hygiene status was assessed using a modified version of the Simplified Debris Index [9], categorised into four levels: score 0 indicated no visible debris or staining; score 1 corresponded to mild accumulation of soft debris on up to one‐third of the tooth surface; score 2 represented moderate accumulation covering between one‐third and two‐thirds of the surface or the presence of localised plaque or calculus; and score 3 reflected poor oral hygiene, with soft debris on more than two‐thirds of the exposed surfaces or generalised plaque or calculus. Additional indicators included the Decayed‐Missing‐Filled (DMF) index, the presence of periodontal disease [10], signs of bruxism, and harmful oral habits such as repetitive finger insertion and pica syndrome.

Surgical variables encompassed the behavioural management technique used during implant placement, categorised as no sedation, light sedation, deep sedation, or general anaesthesia. The timing of implant placement was classified according to established protocols as immediate (performed at the time of tooth extraction), early (approximately 4 weeks post‐extraction), early delayed (12–16 weeks post‐extraction), or delayed (> 16 weeks post‐extraction) [11]. Additional variables recorded were the implant site (classified by sextant), implant design (cylindrical or tapered), implant type (bone‐level or tissue‐level), implant diameter and length, and whether the case was a reimplantation (i.e., placed in a site of previous implant failure). The use of guided bone regeneration techniques—specifically, bone grafts, membranes, or both—was also documented, along with whether the implant was left submerged or non‐submerged during the osseointegration period.

Prosthesis‐related variables included the number of years since prosthesis placement; prosthesis type (fixed or removable), and fixed prosthesis subtype (single crown or bridge). Retention method was classified as screw‐retained or cement‐retained for fixed prostheses, and as ball‐retained, bar‐retained, or locator‐retained for removable prostheses. The loading protocol was categorised as immediate (< 1 week after implant placement), early (1–8 weeks), or conventional (> 8 weeks) [12]. Additionally, the behavioural management techniques applied during prosthetic procedures—including impressions, framework try‐in, and delivery—were recorded using the same sedation categories described for the surgical phase.

At the final follow‐up visit, further variables were recorded to assess long‐term maintenance and oral hygiene practices. These included the frequency of maintenance visits, categorised as at least once per year, biennial, triennial, or only in response to specific issues. Diet consistency was classified as normal/chewable, mashed/minced, or creamy/pureed. Daily oral care practices were documented, including whether oral hygiene was performed regularly or irregularly, the toothbrush type (manual or electric), the level of assistance during brushing (assisted, partially assisted, or independent), and the use of adjunctive hygiene aids such as interdental brushes, dental floss, oral irrigators, or antiseptic mouthwash. Oral hygiene status was reassessed using the same modified version of the Simplified Debris Index as that applied at baseline [9].

Handling of Correlated Data

Outcome variables included several clinical endpoints related to implant performance and peri‐implant tissue health. “Implant survival” was defined as the presence of the implant in the oral cavity at the time of the final follow‐up visit. “Peri‐implant health” referred to the absence of clinical signs of inflammation at the peri‐implant site, specifically erythema, bleeding on probing, swelling, or suppuration [13]. “Peri‐implant mucositis” was diagnosed when bleeding occurred upon gentle probing, with or without accompanying erythema, swelling, or suppuration [13]. “Peri‐implantitis” was defined as the presence of inflammatory clinical signs (e.g., bleeding on probing and/or suppuration), increased probing depths and/or mucosal recession, together with radiographic evidence of bone loss compared to previous assessments. In cases where baseline radiographs were unavailable, peri‐implantitis was diagnosed when the bone level was ≥ 3 mm apical to the most coronal portion of the intraosseous implant, in combination with bleeding on probing [13]. “Implant failure” referred to implant loss due to mobility, infection, or progressive bone loss, and was further categorised as pre‐loading failure (occurring before prosthetic loading) or post‐loading failure (occurring after prosthetic loading) [14].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Quantitative data were summarised using measures of central tendency (mean, median) and dispersion (standard deviation, range), while qualitative variables were reported as absolute and relative frequencies. Group comparisons were conducted using p‐values. For categorical data, the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test was applied. For continuous data, the Anderson–Darling test assessed normality and the Fligner–Killeen test evaluated homogeneity of variances. Based on these results, appropriate parametric (Student's t‐test, Welch's t‐test, ANOVA, Welch's ANOVA) or non‐parametric (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis) tests were selected. Implant cumulative survival was analysed using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared via the log‐rank (Cox–Mantel) test. Effect sizes were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all factors included in the models. Odds ratios were reported relative to the reference outcome category (healthy implant); therefore, an OR < 1 indicates reduced odds of being classified as healthy and, consequently, higher likelihood of biological complications or implant failure.

Due to the hierarchical structure of the dataset—where observations at lower levels (implants, prostheses) are nested within higher‐level units (patients)—dependency among observations was addressed using mixed‐effects models. These models account for shared characteristics within clusters (e.g., oral hygiene in a given patient or implants supporting the same prosthesis), which can otherwise bias estimates.

Modelling was conducted in three sequential stages. First, variable preselection was carried out through univariate analyses and univariate multinomial logistic regression models to identify predictors associated with the probability of belonging to specific outcome categories, using “Health” (for implant‐level outcomes) and “Undamaged and successful” (for prosthesis‐level outcomes) as the respective reference categories. Second, multinomial logistic regression models with random effects were fitted to estimate the probability of classification into each outcome category while accounting for hierarchical clustering and interdependence among observations. Finally, binomial logistic regression models with random effects were developed using a one‐vs‐all strategy to model each outcome category independently, enhancing interpretability and discrimination. To evaluate and compare the performance of the proposed models’ accuracy, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‐ROC), and Cohen's Kappa coefficient were calculated.

The present study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies.

Results

A total of 137 patients with ID were included in the study, comprising 69 females (50.4%) and 68 males (49.6%), with a mean age of 44.1 ± 13.8 years (range: 18–76y). A total of 453 dental implants were placed and recorded. At the time of the final follow‐up, 418 implants (92.7%) remained functional, with a mean follow‐up period of 75.9 ± 42.7 months (range: 36–211 m). Of the 35 (7.3%) implants that failed, 13 (37.1%) failed prior to prosthetic loading and 22 (62.9%) after loading. Notably, 17 of the failed sites (3.8% of the total implants) received a new implant in the same anatomical location. Regarding patient characteristics that could influence implant prognosis, the most common primary diagnosis was non‐syndromic intellectual disability of unknown aetiology (n = 70; 51.1%), with epilepsy being the most frequent comorbidity (n = 16; 11.7%). In terms of prescribed medications, the most commonly used drugs were antihypertensives (n = 19; 13.9%), followed by selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (n = 14; 10.2%).

At the time of implant placement, oral hygiene was rated as excellent (score 0) in 5 patients (3.6%), mild (score 1) in 38 cases (27.7%), moderate (score 2) in 35 patients (25.5%), and poor (score 3) in 46 individuals (33.6%). Oral hygiene could not be assessed in 13 patients (9.5%) due to incomplete or missing clinical data. The mean DMFT index was 16.4 ± 8.9, mainly driven by missing teeth (10.8 ± 9.0; range: 0–28). Periodontal disease was present in 51 patients (37.2%), bruxism was identified in 46 patients (33.5%), and harmful oral habits were observed in 13 individuals (9.4%).

The number of implants placed per patient ranged from 1 to 19, with a mean of 3.3 implants per patient. A total of 55.8% of the implants were placed in the maxilla (n = 253) and 44.1% in the mandible (n = 200). Implant characteristics included a cylindrical design in 320 cases (70.6%) and a tapered design in 133 (29.4%). Most implants were bone‐level (n = 315; 69.5%), while the remainder were tissue‐level (n = 138; 30.5%). The most common implant diameter was 4.1 mm (n = 128; 28.3%), and the most frequent length was 10 mm (n = 212; 46.8%). The majority of implants were placed using a non‐submerged protocol (n = 403; 88.7%). Regarding timing, 241 implants (53.2%) were placed in delayed healing extraction sockets, 137 (30.2%) immediately post‐extraction, 40 (8.8%) at 4–8 weeks post‐extraction, and 32 (7.1%) at 12–16 weeks. In three cases (0.7%), this information was not available. Sinus lift procedures were performed in 6 cases (1.3%), and guided bone regeneration was applied in 34 implants (7.5%). Surgical complications were recorded in 51 implants, with bleeding being the most common complication (n = 48; 10.6%). General anaesthesia was the most commonly used pharmacological behavioural management technique during implant placement (n = 91; 66.4%) and during the impression stage (n = 52; 37.9%).

Detailed information regarding the implant‐supported prostheses delivered to patients in this cohort is presented in the second part of this article (Part 2).

At the final follow‐up visit, oral hygiene scores were similar to those recorded at baseline, with 62% of individuals presenting scores of 2 or 3. In 47.4% of cases, patients attended dental check‐ups at least once a year, while 33.6% attended biannually. A normal‐consistency diet was tolerated by 84.7% of patients. However, only 65% underwent routine oral hygiene procedures, most commonly using a manual toothbrush (62.0%), with assisted or partially assisted brushing techniques reported in 63.5% of cases. Additionally, 24.7% of patients used some form of adjunctive hygiene aid, most commonly mouthwashes (10.9%).

Of the 453 implants placed, 35 (7.3%) were lost before the final follow‐up visit. Among the 418 surviving implants, 8 could not be clinically or radiographically assessed due to behavioural limitations. A total of 410 implants were evaluated and classified into the predefined clinical outcome categories: 261 implants (63.6%) were considered clinically healthy, 96 (23.4%) presented with peri‐implant mucositis, and peri‐implantitis was diagnosed in 18 implants (4.4%). Additionally, implant failure after prosthetic loading was recorded in 22 implants (4.9%), while failure prior to prosthetic loading occurred in 13 implants (2.9%). For analytical purposes, implant losses before and after loading were grouped under a single “failed implants” category.

In the univariate analysis of patient‐related variables, significant associations with implant outcomes were observed for age at first implant surgery, epilepsy, and use of proton pump inhibitors (Table 2). Regarding oral health status at the time of implant placement, implant outcomes were significantly influenced by baseline oral hygiene, DMF index, periodontitis, bruxism, and the presence of harmful oral habits (Table 3). Among implant‐related variables, the following showed statistically significant associations with clinical implant outcomes: implant design, implant type, implant diameter, sinus lift procedures, guided bone regeneration techniques, and the healing protocol (Table 4). With regard to prosthetic variables, significant associations with clinical outcomes were found for the retention subtype of fixed prostheses, the loading protocol, and the pharmacological behaviour support technique applied during the impression procedure (Table 5). Finally, oral hygiene score at the last follow‐up visit was the only follow‐up related variable that reached statistical significance (Table 6).

Nonetheless, these trends are consistent with previous findings and highlight potential areas of clinical relevance.

In contrast to mucositis and healthy implant outcomes, none of the studied variables showed statistically significant associations with peri‐implantitis or implant loss in this model. Although the direction of some coefficients—for example, larger implant diameter for peri‐implantitis or poor oral hygiene for implant loss—was biologically plausible, wide confidence intervals and non‐significant p‐values (all > 0.2) indicated limited discriminatory power. This is likely attributable to the relatively small number of cases in the peri‐implantitis (n = 18) and implant loss (n = 35) subgroups, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect true associations.

Binomial logistic regression models with random effects demonstrated a reasonable and clinically informative performance, with good discriminative ability (AUC = 0.731), acceptable overall accuracy (accuracy = 69%), and fair agreement (Cohen's Kappa = 0.381). Within these models, tissue‐level implant type, screw‐retained fixed prostheses, and the use of general anaesthesia during the impression step were significantly associated with the “healthy” outcome category.

Discussion

The current study provides one of the largest multicenter evaluations of dental implants in patients with non‐syndromic ID, including 453 implants placed in 137 individuals across 7 clinical centers in 4 different countries. Despite the elevated clinical complexity of this population, the overall implant survival rate was 92.3%, which is within the range of prior studies involving individuals with cognitive impairments [6, 15, 16]. These results reaffirm that dental implant therapy is feasible in patients with non‐syndromic ID when appropriate case selection and support protocols are implemented.

This survival rate compares favourably with earlier findings by Ekfeldt [17], who reported the lowest survival rate (85.8%) among the reviewed studies. That investigation included a heterogeneous sample of 27 patients with diverse developmental conditions, including Down syndrome, autism, and schizophrenia. A total of 12 implant failures were documented over a 3‐year follow‐up period, with 3 occurring before prosthetic loading and 9 afterward.

These results highlight the variability in implant outcomes across different subgroups of patients with cognitive and developmental disabilities, and reinforce the importance of focusing on homogeneous populations—such as individuals with non‐syndromic ID—to better understand condition‐specific risks and predictors of success.

However, implant failure and biological complications—specifically peri‐implant mucositis and peri‐implantitis—were not negligible. These findings are consistent with previously published data and underscore the critical importance of peri‐implant maintenance. Epidemiological studies have reported that, in the general population, peri‐implant mucositis affects approximately 43%–47% of implant patients, while peri‐implantitis is observed in 20%–22% of cases [18, 19, 20]. It should be noted that the prevalence of mucositis and peri‐implantitis in the present study may be underestimated due to its retrospective design, potential variability in diagnostic criteria among research teams, and the clinical and radiographic examination challenges posed by poor patient cooperation in some cases.

In the present study, patient‐related variables did not have a significant impact on implant outcomes. In the univariate analysis, epilepsy was significantly associated with an increased risk of implant complications, particularly post‐loading failure. However, this association was not sustained in the subsequent binomial or multivariate logistic regression models. These findings are consistent with those reported by Cune et al. [21], who concluded that dental implant therapy in patients with severe epilepsy and additional disabilities appears to be a viable treatment option.

It has also been suggested that certain pharmacological agents—such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and proton pump inhibitors—may negatively influence implant osseointegration [22]. In the present cohort, proton pump inhibitors use was associated with an increased risk of implant complications—specifically peri‐implantitis and pre‐loading failure—in the univariate analysis; however, this association did not persist in the binomial or multivariate models.

In our cohort, poor oral hygiene—both at the time of implant placement and throughout the follow‐up period—was strongly associated with mucositis and peri‐implantitis, emphasising the need for individualised hygiene protocols and active caregiver involvement [23, 24, 25].

Our results also underscore the influence of specific implant‐ and prosthesis‐related variables on peri‐implant health. Tissue‐level implants were significantly associated with better clinical outcomes, with a lower probability of mucositis and a higher likelihood of peri‐implant health, corroborating findings from Taheri et al. [26] and Wang et al. [27] in the general population. This association is biologically plausible, as tissue‐level implants position the implant–abutment junction away from the crestal bone and result in a more superficial peri‐implant sulcus, potentially reducing plaque accumulation and facilitating access for oral hygiene and professional maintenance. These characteristics are particularly relevant in adults with intellectual disability, in whom effective plaque control and regular supportive peri‐implant care may be challenging. Poor oral hygiene and lack of maintenance have been consistently identified as major risk indicators for peri‐implant diseases, whereas implant positioning and designs that facilitate hygiene access appear to confer a protective effect [13, 28]. From a clinical perspective, the use of tissue‐level implants may therefore represent a pragmatic strategy to mitigate biological risk in patients with limited capacity for self‐performed oral hygiene.

Additionally, tapered implant design was protective against mucositis, possibly due to more favourable emergence profiles and/or reduced plaque retention. It is possible that other prosthetic factors not assessed in the present study—such as the use of unsplinted implants, platform‐switched abutments, increased abutment heights, a one abutment‐one time protocol, and optimised emergence angles and profiles—may also contribute to the observed benefits, as these have been previously associated with reduced peri‐implantitis risk [29].

Screw‐retained fixed prostheses were associated with a higher risk of mucositis and poorer peri‐implant health outcomes compared to cemented restorations in the present cohort. Although this finding is consistent with previous reports of greater marginal bone loss around screw‐retained prostheses [30, 31], it warrants cautions interpretation. Residual submucosal cement is a well‐established risk indicator for peri‐implant disease; however, available evidence suggests that biological complications are primarily related to the presence, apical position, and inadequate removal of cement rather than to cement retention per se [28, 32, 33]. In addition, the marked imbalance between cement‐retained (n = 51) and screw‐retained restorations (n = 303) and the non‐randomised selection of retention mode raise the possibility of selection bias, as cement‐retained prostheses may have been preferentially used in cases with more favourable anatomy, hygiene access, or patient cooperation. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that cement retention is inherently protective; rather, they suggest that, in selected patients, cement‐retained restorations combined with meticulous cementation protocols and accessible margins may achieve acceptable peri‐implant outcomes, while overall evidence indicates that cement‐ and screw‐retained restorations confer a similar risk of peri‐implant disease and that retention choice should be guided by clinical, technical, and aesthetic considerations rather than by retention type alone [34, 35].

The impression step performed under general anaesthesia was positively associated with peri‐implant health. There are currently no studies directly addressing the impact of sedation regimen on mucositis incidence. Nonetheless, evidence from the literature demonstrates that in patients with ID, the use of general anaesthesia or deep sedation substantially improves patient cooperation—enabling more precise procedural execution and reducing technique‐related errors or tissue trauma [15]. These operational advantages—though not explicitly related to peri‐implant mucosa health in the literature—provide a plausible rationale for the indirect association observed in our cohort. In the present series, using the implant as the unit of analysis, 76% of implant placements and 61% of impression procedures were performed under general anaesthesia, confirming that this modality remains essential for the safe delivery of implant therapy in patients with severe cognitive impairment [7, 36].

In the binomial logistic regression models, no significant predictors were identified for the implant loss outcome. However, from the perspective of implant‐related variables, implant diameter was significantly associated with implant loss in the univariate analyses and showed a statistical trend in the logistic regression models. Narrow‐diameter implants may be associated with increased biomechanical risk, particularly in patients with bruxism or elevated occlusal loads—both of which are common traits in this population [37, 38]. Accordingly, occlusal planning and prosthetic design must be carefully tailored to account for these risk factors in order to mitigate the potential for biomechanical overload.

Despite the study's retrospective nature and limitations in follow‐up consistency and radiographic assessment, the multicenter design and standardised data collection strengthen the generalisability of the findings. However, this study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, its retrospective design may have introduced selection bias and limited the ability to control for confounding factors. Secondly, the degree of ID was not systematically recorded, which may have led to underrepresentation of more severely affected individuals [39]. Thirdly, some outcome categories had a low number of events, limiting the statistical power of regression models—particularly for peri‐implantitis and implant loss. Lastly, validated tools to assess patient‐reported outcomes could not be applied [40], and the clinical relevance of peri‐implant complications in this population may be difficult to interpret without such measures.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, the long‐term survival of dental implants in patients with non‐syndromic ID is confirmed, despite the clinical complexity inherent to this population. Among the variables analysed, tissue‐level implant type, cement‐retained fixed prostheses, and the use of general anaesthesia during the impression step were most significantly associated with healthy peri‐implant tissue conditions; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as these variables may have been influenced by clinical complexity and patient‐related factors, introducing potential selection bias. In contrast, no variables were identified as significant predictors of implant loss.
