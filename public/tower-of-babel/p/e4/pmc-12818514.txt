Effectiveness of Dynamic Noise Monitoring Combined with Staged Nursing Care on Nocturnal Motor Symptoms in Patients with Parkinson’s Disease
Authors: XiaoYu Zhang, YinXia Dong, Kun Gao, XuXia Wu, HongMei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:The effectiveness of dynamic noise monitoring combined with staged nursing care on nocturnal motor symptoms was evaluated in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD).Methods:This retrospective clinical study enrolled 200 patients with PD, who were hospitalised from March 2021 to December 2024. The patients were divided into the following two groups based on the nursing methods they received: dynamic noise monitoring group (n = 102), which underwent dynamic noise monitoring and received staged nursing care, or the staged nursing care group (n = 98), which received staged nursing care alone. The primary outcomes comprised the nighttime scores of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (UPDRS-III) and the Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale-2 (PDSS-2). The assessed secondary outcomes included heart rate (HR), blood pressure, salivary cortisol levels, Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) and Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire-18 (PSQ-18). We used independent samples t-tests for intergroup comparisons, paired t-tests for intragroup comparisons and χ2 tests for categorical variables.Results:The dynamic noise monitoring group exhibited markedly lower noise levels and reduced noise exposure events exceeding 50 and 60 dBA in comparison to the staged nursing care group (P < 0.001). Compared with the staged nursing care group, the dynamic noise monitoring group exhibited markedly reduced UPDRS-III nighttime scores and PDSS-2 ratings (P < 0.001). Moreover, notable enhancements were noted in HR, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and salivary cortisol levels. The PDQ-39 Summary Index scores and PSQ-18 total satisfaction scores demonstrated significant enhancements in the dynamic noise monitoring group (all P < 0.05).Conclusion:Dynamic noise monitoring, when integrated into staged nursing care, considerably enhances nocturnal motor symptoms, sleep quality and overall well-being in patients with PD.

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterised by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, affecting approximately 1–2% of individuals over 60 years of age worldwide.[12] Classical motor symptoms, such as tremor, rigidity and bradykinesia, are well-established indicators of PD, and nocturnal motor symptoms pose considerable challenges to disease management, adversely affecting patients’ quality of life and increasing caregiver burden.[3] Nocturnal motor symptoms in PD include movement-related disturbances that manifest during the night, such as nocturnal akinesia, early morning dystonia, sleep-related movement disorders and restless legs syndrome.[4] Notably, 60% of patients with PD present with nocturnal motor symptoms.[5] The repercussions include sleep disruption, elevated fall risk, reduced functional independence, increased psychological distress and accelerated disease progression due to compromised restorative sleep processes.[5] Current nursing strategies have numerous limitations, such as inconsistent implementation protocols, inadequate individualisation based on symptom severity, insufficient real-time monitoring capabilities and suboptimal integration of environmental factors that may aggravate nocturnal symptoms.[6]

Environmental noise considerably affects nocturnal motor symptoms in patients with PD. Noise levels in hospital settings range from 45 to 65 dBA during nighttime hours, exceeding the World Health Organization’s recommended maximum of 30 dBA for sleep environments.[7] Excessive noise exposure can activate the sympathetic nervous system, elevate cortisol levels and disrupt sleep architecture, potentially exacerbating motor symptoms in neurologically susceptible groups.[8] Studies investigating the effects of noise on patients with PD have shown association of elevated nighttime noise exposure with increased motor symptom severity, reduced sleep efficiency and heightened medication requirements.[9] Comprehensive hospital ward renovation has been proven effective in reducing ward noise levels, but this approach is restricted by substantial capital costs, architectural constraints and limited feasibility in resource-limited healthcare settings.[10] Therefore, alternative methods that can accomplish noise reduction without requiring extensive facility alterations are essential.

This study employed a dynamic noise monitoring system integrated with real-time nursing protocols to regulate environmental noise exposure in hospitalised patients with PD. We hypothesised that dynamic noise monitoring combined with staged nursing care, would be more effective than staged nursing care alone in improving nocturnal motor symptoms, sleep quality and overall patient outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This retrospective clinical study examined the effects of dynamic noise monitoring combined with staged nursing care on nocturnal motor symptoms in patients with PD. A study was conducted at Tianshui Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine from March 2021 to December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Tianshui Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine (approval number: IRB-2025-0606), and all participants or their legal representatives provided informed consent. Patients were assigned to either the dynamic noise monitoring group or the staged nursing care group according to the nursing methods they received during their hospitalisation. The dynamic noise monitoring group (n = 102) received dynamic noise monitoring technology combined with staged nursing care, whereas the staged nursing care group (n = 98) received only the staged nursing care protocol in accordance with established clinical guidelines.

Located on the same floor level, the staged nursing care group and dynamic noise monitoring group were housed in separate but adjacent ward units within the same hospital building. The units are approximately 50 m apart, have identical ward configurations, including room layout, bed specifications, lighting systems, ventilation, staffing protocols and standard nursing care procedures, but differ in the dynamic noise monitoring system.

Patient Selection

Between March 2021 and December 2024, a total of 243 patients were assessed for eligibility. Of them, 18 were excluded because of incomplete medical records, 12 were excluded because of severe cognitive impairment, 8 were excluded because of concurrent acute medical conditions and 5 were excluded because of the inability to complete outcome assessments. In the end, 200 cases were included in the final analysis.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age of 45–80 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of idiopathic PD according to Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria; (3) Hoehn and Yahr stage I–III; (4) nocturnal motor symptoms as reported by patients or caregivers; (5) stable dopaminergic medication regimen for at least 4 weeks prior to enrolment; (6) proficiency in understanding and adhering to study procedures; (7) complete medical records and (8) hospitalisation for at least 7 days.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) secondary parkinsonism or atypical parkinsonian syndromes; (2) severe hearing impairment; (3) diagnosed sleep disorders unrelated to PD; (4) severe cognitive impairment; (5) use of sleep medications or major changes in antiparkinsonian medications during the study period and (6) severe psychiatric disorders requiring active treatment.

Noise Measurement

Environmental noise levels were continuously monitored using precision-calibrated acoustic measurement devices (Model SL-4013, REED Instruments, Wilmington, NC, USA). The noise level metres were positioned at the patient’s bedside at a height of 1.2 m above the floor and were kept at a minimum distance of 1.0 m from walls, medical equipment surfaces and other noise-reflective structures to minimise acoustic interference. Throughout the entire hospitalisation period, the devices recorded continuous noise data at 1-minute sampling intervals during each 24-hour cycle. The acoustic analysis focused on the following three key parameters: the average noise energy over each 1-minute interval, represented by the A-weighted equivalent continuous noise level; maximum A-weighted noise pressure level, which captures instantaneous peak noise events and minimum A-weighted noise pressure level, which identifies the quietest periods during nighttime hours.

Nursing Methods

Staged Nursing Care Protocol

The staged nursing care protocol was implemented as a three-phase nursing method by trained registered nurses during a 7-day hospitalisation period. All nursing staff members received standardised training on PD management and the staged care protocol. The phases were as follows: (1) nurses conducted comprehensive evaluations, including motor symptoms severity, sleep patterns, nocturnal motor symptoms and recorded regular vital signs on Day 1 of hospitalisation. Individualised care plans were developed within 48 hours in response to the assessment results, which included the optimisation of medication timing, development of specific nocturnal symptom management strategies and provision of sleep hygiene education. (2) Nurses conducted daily education sessions (30–45 minutes) during Days 2–6. These sessions concentrated on self-management techniques, such as proper body positioning, turning techniques in bed, timing of evening medications, gentle stretching exercises and caregiver training on appropriate assistance methods during nocturnal episodes. These sessions were conducted by nurses. (3) Nursing effectiveness was assessed using baseline instruments on Days 6–7. Discharge planning included provision of written care guidelines, demonstration of techniques by the caregiver and establishment of follow-up schedules. Standardised protocols, monthly staff education sessions and weekly case reviews were implemented to ensure quality assurance.

Dynamic Noise Monitoring System

Throughout their hospitalisation, the dynamic noise monitor group received the complete staged nursing care protocol as previously described and a noise monitoring and management system.

Patients in the dynamic noise monitor group underwent a baseline noise exposure assessment during their first night of admission, and continuous monitoring was implemented on Day 1. The immediate identification of excessive noise events was facilitated by the transmission of real-time data to a central nursing station display system. Automated alerts were transmitted to the mobile devices of the nursing staff when noise levels exceeded 50 dBA for more than 2 consecutive minutes during nighttime hours. Immediate protocols were implemented within 3 minutes for levels that exceeded 60 dBA. The noise reduction protocol comprised the following: (1) the immediate identification and elimination of controllable noise sources (equipment alarms, staff conversations and trolley movements); (2) implementation of quiet hours with staff notification systems; (3) coordination with housekeeping and maintenance staff to minimise nighttime disturbances; (4) use of noise-absorbing ear plugs when feasible and (5) the strategic timing of necessary medical procedures to prevent sleep disruption. The nursing staff participated in an additional 8-hour training session that focused on rapid response protocols, source identification and noise awareness.

The total nighttime noise exposure, peak noise events and nursing response times were all documented in the daily noise exposure reports that were generated automatically. Throughout the hospitalisation, these reports were incorporated into the daily nursing assessments and employed to modify environmental management strategies. The post-nursing analysis included the correlation of noise events with documented sleep disturbances and nocturnal motor symptom episodes as documented in patient sleep diaries.

Data Collection

General Information

The patients’ demographic and clinical baseline data were collected using patient self-reported questionnaires and electronic medical record (EMR) system. We obtained the following information from the EMR system: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), education level, disease duration, Hoehn and Yahr stage, medication regimen (L-DOPA equivalent daily dose) and comorbidities. Standardised data collection forms were employed to gather all baseline information within 24 hours of admission. Structured interviews were conducted by trained research nurses to obtain self-reported information, whereas medical information was verified by attending neurologists.

Disease severity and progression were assessed using the Hoehn and Yahr staging system. The staging criteria were as follows: stage I (unilateral involvement only), stage II (bilateral involvement without impairment of balance), stage III (bilateral disease with mild to moderate disability and postural instability), stage IV (severely disabling disease but still capable of walking or standing unassisted) and stage V (restricted to a wheelchair or bed unless assisted). To standardise dopaminergic medication exposure at different drug combinations, the L-DOPA equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was determined by employing established conversion formulas.

Primary Outcomes

The UPDRS Part III comprises 14 items that assess a variety of motor functions, such as bradykinesia, postural instability, rigidity and tremor.[1112] The total scores of each item range from 0 (normal) to 4 (severe), with a range of 0–56. High scores indicate severe motor impairment. Nocturnal motor function was from 22:00 to 06:00 for this study. The Cronbach’s α of the UPDRS Part III used in this study was 0.89. The Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale-2 (PDSS-2) is a 15-item patient-rated scale that is specifically designed to assess sleep disturbances in PD. Each item is scored on a scale of 0 (never) to 4 (very frequent), yielding a total score of 0–60. High scores indicate poor sleep quality.[1314] The PDSS-2 has a Cronbach’s α of 0.82. Both assessments were conducted by trained neurological nurses at baseline (Day 1) and at discharge (Day 7).

Secondary Outcomes

(1) Physiological indicators included heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and salivary cortisol levels. HR, SBP and DBP were evaluated at standardised 4-hour intervals. Three consecutive readings were obtained at 2-minute intervals for each measurement using the automated oscillometric method, and the average was calculated to guarantee accuracy. The trained nursing staff adhered to standardised protocols when measuring all vital signs using calibrated electronic monitors (Mindray VS-900, Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China). Prior to measurement, patients were required to remain in a supine position for 5 minutes, and measurements were prohibited within 30 minutes of medication administration or physical activity. Saliva samples were collected between the hours of 7:00 and 8:00 using Salivette tubes (Sarstedt AG & Co., Nümbrecht, Germany). Prior to sample collection, patients were advised to refrain from eating, drinking, smoking or brushing their teeth for 30 minutes. The samples were immediately stored at −20 °C and analysed using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (DRG Salivary Cortisol ELISA, DRG International Inc., Springfield, NJ, USA). The assay exhibited intra-assay precision of <8% and inter-assay precision of <10%. The detection range is 0.09–30 ng/mL. Average physiological parameters at baseline (Day 1) and at discharge (Day 7) were used for the comparison of the groups.

(2) Quality of life was assessed using the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39).[1516] The PDQ-39 is a disease-specific quality of life instrument that consists of 39 items across 8 dimensions: mobility (10 items), activities of daily living (6 items), emotional well-being (6 items), stigma (4 items), social support (3 items), cognition (4 items), communication (3 items) and bodily discomfort (3 items). Dimension scores were calculated as percentages ranging from 0% to 100%, with higher scores indicating a greater negative impact on quality of life and each item was scored on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The total PDQ-39 Summary Index was calculated as the mean of all eight-dimension scores. The PDQ-39 has Cronbach’s α of 0.93.

(3) Patient satisfaction was evaluated using the Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire-18 (PSQ-18). This 18-item instrument has been validated and is specifically designed for neurological care settings.[1718] The PSQ-18 assesses satisfaction in six domains: technical quality (three items), interpersonal manner (three items), communication (three items), financial aspects (two items), time spent with healthcare providers (three items) and accessibility and convenience (four items). Negatively worded items were reverse-scored, and each item was scored on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The domain scores were calculated by averaging the scores of pertinent items, and the total satisfaction score is between 18 and 90 points. High scores indicate a high level of satisfaction with care provided. The Cronbach’s α is 0.87 for the PSQ-18.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and tables were prepared in Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Shapiro–Wilk tests were implemented to evaluate the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups with independent samples t-tests; within-group comparisons across time points were conducted using paired t-test procedures. For categorical variables, frequency counts with percentages were reported and performed analyses using χ2 tests. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (two-sided tests).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective analysis uncovered significant correlations between dynamic noise monitoring and enhanced nocturnal motor symptoms in hospitalised patients with PD. Potential relationships between environmental noise management and clinical outcomes are suggested by the observed reductions in UPDRS Part III nighttime scores and PDSS-2 ratings in the dynamic noise monitoring group, coupled with decreased physiological stress markers, including salivary cortisol levels and cardiovascular parameters. The observed improvements in UPDRS-III nighttime scores and PDSS-2 were significant. The minimal clinically important difference for motor symptoms in PD decreased by three to four in the UPDRS-III. A decrease of 3.44 points in PDSS-2 total scores was considered clinically significant.[19] These results indicated that the nursing interventions not only produce statistically notable outcomes but also result in substantial enhancements in patients’ nocturnal symptoms.

This study illustrated that nocturnal motor symptoms in PD patients were significantly enhanced by dynamic noise monitoring combined with staged nursing care. A stress response was initiated by the sympathetic nervous system, which was activated by excessive noise exposure. This response was characterised by an elevated cardiovascular response and increased cortisol secretion.[2021] This physiological mechanism was corroborated by the reduced salivary cortisol levels and enhanced HR and blood pressure in the dynamic noise monitoring group. The stress response induced by noise exposure exacerbated dopaminergic neuronal dysfunction, as cortisol has been demonstrated to modulate dopamine transmission and increase oxidative stress in the substantia nigra, potentially accelerating neurodegeneration.[22] Additionally, environmental noise disrupted sleep architecture by increasing sleep fragmentation, reducing slow-wave sleep and prolonging sleep onset latency.[23] The restorative functions of sleep that were particularly critical patients with PD were impaired by these disruptions because their dopaminergic systems required overnight recovery to maintain optimal function.[24] The improvements observed in PDSS-2 scores suggested that reducing noise exposure improves the quality and continuity of sleep, potentially facilitating the overnight replenishment of dopamine stores and improving motor function. This finding is consistent with previous research that demonstrated a direct correlation between the severity of motor symptoms in patients with PD and the quality of their sleep.[25] Additionally, the pharmacodynamics of drugs can be influenced by noise-induced stress and sleep disruption.[26] Stress hormones can affect the absorption and bioavailability of levodopa by altering gastrointestinal motility and blood flow.[27] In the dynamic noise monitoring group, the improved UPDRS-III scores are likely due to the optimisation of medication efficacy during nighttime hours. The differential improvements across PDQ-39 dimensions provide insights into the specific mechanisms of our nursing measures. The substantial enhancements in mobility, activities of daily living, emotional well-being and bodily discomfort are likely indicative of the direct physiological benefits of enhanced sleep quality and reduced noise-induced stress because these domains are closely associated with psychological arousal, physical comfort and motor function. Conversely, the absence of substantial modifications in the stigma, social support and communication domains suggests that these psychosocial aspects of quality of life may be less susceptible to short-term environmental changes and may necessitate more comprehensive, long-term psychosocial nursing interventions that address disease-related social challenges and interpersonal relationships. Our nursing methods appeared to have established a therapeutic healing environment by addressing environmental noise, which had a positive effect on the physiological and psychological aspects of PD management. Several limitations of our investigation necessitated serious consideration. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias through non-randomised group assignment based on received nursing methods, while the single-centre implementation limits generalisability across diverse healthcare environments with varying acoustic profiles and institutional protocols. The absence of parallel daytime symptom assessment protocols is a critical methodological gap that prevents the determination of whether the benefits of environmental noise control extend beyond nocturnal improvements to influence comprehensive motor function throughout the care continuum. The follow-up period was restricted to the duration of hospitalisation, which prevented the assessment of the long-term clinical efficacy and durability of nursing measures after discharge. The subjective perception of noise exposure by patients was not systematically evaluated, which may have resulted in the neglect of individual sensitivity variations that could be associated with objective acoustic measurements and treatment responses. Despite efforts to reduce the frequency of evaluations, the unavoidable disruption of sleep architecture that resulted from the necessity of conducting UPDRS-III nighttime assessments could potentially confound primary outcome measurements. While unmeasured confounding factors related to ward placement and patient-specific environmental preferences may have influenced treatment outcomes, variability in the implementation of noise reduction protocols by nursing staff may have influenced the consistency of nursing measures. Prospective randomised controlled designs with extended post-discharge follow-up periods, parallel daytime and nighttime outcome assessments and the development of evidence-based guidelines for environmental noise thresholds through multi-centre validation studies that can establish optimal monitoring protocols for diverse neurological populations should be implemented in future investigations.

CONCLUSION

Combined with staged nursing care, dynamic noise monitoring significantly enhances the quality of life, physiological parameters, sleep quality and nocturnal motor symptoms of hospitalised PD patients. These nursing methods offer practical, non-pharmacological solutions for the management of nocturnal symptoms. Assessment of the applicability to other healthcare environments necessitates additional validation. This method has the potential to alleviate the burden of nocturnal symptoms and enhance the overall management of PD.
