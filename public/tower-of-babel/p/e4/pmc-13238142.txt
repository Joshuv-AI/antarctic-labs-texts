Rapid reduction of temporomandibular joint dislocation with a modified and optimized patient-guided technique: a retrospective case series
Authors: Wei-qing Lin, Yu-hua Wang, Yi Lu, Fei Lin, Hai-dong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo report a modified and optimized patient-guided maneuver for the rapid and physiological reduction of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dislocation.MethodsA retrospective case series was conducted at a single Emergency Department from January 2017 to December 2022. The study evaluated 83 consecutive patients with acute TMJ dislocation treated with a modified and optimized patient-guided technique. The maneuver follows a two-step sequence: Step 1 involves concurrent mouth opening and head extension; Step 2 involves mouth closing and head flexion while the clinician applies gentle, sustained downward traction. Reduction time was recorded by an independent observer.ResultsThe overall success rate for achieving anatomical goals was 100%. Specifically, for simple dislocations (n = 81), a 100% reduction success rate was achieved within a mean time of 150 s. For dislocations complicated by condylar fractures (n = 2), the maneuver was 100% successful in achieving temporary palliative alignment, which served as a crucial bridge to definitive surgical intervention. No iatrogenic injuries or complications were observed in either group.ConclusionThis patient-guided approach utilizes active patient movement to facilitate reduction with minimal force. It appears to reduce iatrogenic risk and improve patient tolerance compared to traditional high-force maneuvers. While 100% success was observed in this series, the results are limited by the single-center, single-operator design. Further multi-center prospective trials are necessary to validate these findings and assess long-term outcomes.Clinical significanceThe patient-guided technique provides a controlled, less traumatic alternative for TMJ reduction, potentially reducing the need for anesthesia and improving the overall patient experience in emergency settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08232-7.

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dislocation (also referred to as luxation in some literature) is a common condition, affecting approximately 25 individuals per 100,000 annually [1–4]. This condition can significantly impact a patient’s quality of life and often requires prompt medical intervention.

According to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD), dislocation of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is defined as a condition where the condyle is displaced out of the articular fossa and remains locked out, preventing normal jaw movement [5]. This condition can be classified into different types based on the direction of the dislocation:Anterior Dislocation: The condyle is displaced forward out of the articular fossa.Posterior Dislocation: The condyle is displaced backward.Lateral Dislocation: The condyle is displaced to the side.

The DC/TMD criteria emphasize the importance of a comprehensive clinical examination and, when necessary, imaging techniques such as MRI or CT scans to confirm the diagnosis [6]. The criteria also include the assessment of pain, functional limitations, and any associated psychosocial factors to provide a holistic understanding of the patient’s condition [6].

The Hippocratic maneuver, a widely used technique, involves the clinician standing in front of the seated patient. Proper positioning is crucial, with the patient’s mandible no higher than the clinician’s elbows to optimize leverage. An assistant typically stabilizes the patient’s head. The patient is instructed to maximally open their mouth to facilitate muscle relaxation. The clinician then applies downward pressure on the mandible with their thumbs placed intraorally on the lower molars or the external oblique ridge. Concurrently, the clinician’s remaining fingers provide extraoral support by encircling the mandible. Following downward pressure, a backward force is applied to guide the condylar repositioning. Upward pressure on the mentum can also be employed to assist in mandibular rotation. The reported success rate of this technique ranges from 86.2% to 86.7% [7].

However, this technique is often perceived by patients as unpleasant and distressing for the patient, and the results are not always satisfactory [8], with reported success rates ranging from 86.2% to 86.7% [9, 10]. Furthermore, the ergonomically challenging angles required by the Hippocratic method can place undue stress on the clinician’s ligaments and wrists [7], often necessitating favorable outcomes observed in this cohort force and skill. This can lead to reduction failures and potential injury to the operator’s thumb due to the triggering of the myotonic reflex during forceful muscle overstretching.

In some instances, local anesthesia via auriculotemporal nerve blocks or intra-articular anesthetic distribution within the glenoid fossa might be employed to minimize muscle spasm before manual manipulation. However, severe myospasm may necessitate general anesthesia, occasionally including the use of intravenous paralytics [11].

Despite the existence of numerous alternative reduction methods explored since the description of the Hippocratic technique around 1500 BC [7, 8, 12–14], no universally accepted approach has emerged. This is likely due to these methods being either too technique- and force-demanding for widespread adoption, or excessively painful for patients, hindering cooperation and repeat attempts. Additionally, many require an assistant to stabilize the patient’s head, further increasing the complexity of the procedure.

Motivated by the need for a more patient-centric and less demanding reduction technique, this study investigates a modified method for TMJ dislocation reduction.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is a retrospective case series conducted at the Emergency Department of our hospital, spanning from January 2017 to December 2022.

Recruitment and sample size estimation

Patients presenting with acute temporomandibular joint dislocation (TMJ dislocation) who were treated with the modified and optimized technique were consecutively included in the study. The final cohort consisted of 83 eligible patients. The sample size was not pre-calculated for statistical power but represents a consecutive series of eligible patients treated during the study period, aiming for sufficient clinical representation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged 16 years and older diagnosed with acute TMJ dislocation. Imaging (CT/X-ray) was performed in cases of suspected fracture, high-energy trauma, or when the clinical diagnosis was unclear. While radiographic imaging (CT or X-ray) was the standard protocol for suspected traumatic dislocations, a pragmatic exception was made for 12 cases of lateral dislocation. These patients presented with severe distress and a clear clinical history of failed manual reduction attempts prior to arrival, leading to immediate guided reduction to prioritize urgent pain relief. In all such cases, post-reduction CT scans were performed to confirm successful repositioning and rule out occult fractures.

Consent to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Previous history of TMJ tumor, or systemic diseases affecting the temporomandibular joint.

Chronic TMJ dislocation.

Previous TMJ surgery.

Inability to follow instructions due to cognitive impairment.

Diagnosis of dislocation

TMJ dislocation was initially diagnosed based on clinical examination (inability to close the mouth, pain, and palpable condyle displacement). Dislocation types were classified as anterior, posterior, or lateral. The diagnosis was confirmed by radiographic imaging (X-ray, CT) when deemed clinically necessary, specifically in cases of non-anterior dislocations or where an associated fracture was suspected, to ensure an accurate baseline diagnosis and rule out concomitant pathology.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital.

Standardization and calibration

All procedures were standardized according to a protocol developed by the lead investigator. Investigators underwent calibration sessions to ensure consistency in technique and data collection.

Operational Definitions and Outcomes The primary outcome was successful reduction, defined as the complete anatomical repositioning of the condyle into the articular fossa within a 5-minute observation window, achieved without procedural sedation or anesthesia. Reduction time was recorded by an independent observer (Fei Lin) from the initiation of traction to audible/palpable re-entry. If reduction was not achieved within 5 min, the protocol mandated a transition to conventional manual reduction under local anesthesia or sedation.

Technique description

Patient Positioning and Preparation: Patients were seated facing the clinician, with the mandible at the clinician’s elbow level. This positioning facilitates the application of optimal reduction force.

Maneuver execution

To maximize leverage, the clinician places both thumbs intraorally, as posteriorly as possible on the external oblique ridge. The remaining fingers are positioned extraorally to provide stability and support. Step 1 (Unlocking/Disengagement): The patient is instructed to open their mouth while simultaneously raising their head. Concurrently, the clinician applies gentle, continuous downward traction on the mandible with the thumbs, aiming to relax the masseter and pterygoid muscles and disengage the condyle from the temporal bone.

Step 2 (Repositioning): Following sustained downward pressure, the patient is then instructed to close their mouth while simultaneously lowering their head. This coordinated, active movement facilitates the gliding of the temporomandibular condyle back into the articular fossa. Once reduction is achieved, the clinician’s thumbs can be slowly withdrawn without the risk of being bitten.

In 3 cases of anterior dislocation (3.6%), an extraoral variant of the guided technique was employed due to patients’ extreme intraoral sensitivity. In these instances, the clinician applied pressure externally over the mandibular condylar area while the patient followed the same movement protocol. Our preliminary observation suggests that this extraoral method might be even more convenient and rapid, achieving reduction in approximately 30 s while minimizing clinician exposure to bite injuries. (See Supplemental Video 1) However, owing to the limited number of cases (n = 3) using this variant in our current series, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary clinical insights. The intraoral approach remains the primary recommendation of this study, though the extraoral variant represents a promising alternative that warrants further validation in a larger cohort.

Reduction Time Measurement: The reduction time was recorded by an independent observer (Fei Lin) and was measured precisely from the moment the clinician began the sustained downward traction (Step 1) to the point of successful, audibly or palpably confirmed condyle re-entry (end of Step 2).

Technical Standardization and Operator Consistency To ensure reproducibility and minimize variability, all 83 procedures in this series were performed by a single senior clinician with over 20 years of experience in oral and maxillofacial surgery. While the technique was developed to be easily learned, this single-operator design was employed to maintain strict technical consistency during the initial validation phase of the modified maneuver. An independent observer (Fei Lin) recorded all reduction times to ensure objective data collection.

Investigator calibration

Investigators participated in calibration sessions to align on the technique and data recording procedures, ensuring uniformity across all cases.

Post-reduction Care and Follow-up: Following successful reduction, all patients received standardized discharge instructions: a soft-diet for 2 weeks, avoidance of wide yawning or biting into hard foods, and supportive analgesics if needed. Due to the emergency nature of the presentations, patients were discharged immediately without a mandatory scheduled hospital follow-up, unless symptoms recurred.

Advantages and applicability

This patient-guided technique offers several advantages. It is readily learned by novice clinicians due to its simplicity. Additionally, the minimal pain reported by the majority of patients nature of the maneuver enhances patient cooperation and tolerance for repeat attempts if necessary. Notably, this method demonstrates efficacy not only in reducing anterior TMJ dislocations but also in managing posterior and lateral dislocations.

Results

Patient population and clinical characteristics

A total of 83 consecutive patients (24 males, 59 females) with acute temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dislocation were enrolled, with a mean age of 44.64 ± 18.87 years (range: 19–85 years). Anterior dislocation was the predominant type, occurring in 62 cases (74.7%). Notably, although clinically rare, lateral and posterior dislocations were also observed in 16 (19.3%) and 5 (6.0%) cases, respectively (Fig. 1). Detailed demographics are presented in Table 1.

The etiologies of TMJ dislocation included excessive mouth opening (n = 65; specifically yawning in 39, laughing in 23, and biting hard objects in 3 cases), iatrogenic factors (n = 10), blunt trauma (n = 5), and drug-induced dystonia (n = 3). The mean duration from the onset of dislocation to arrival at the emergency department was 4 h (range: 1–12 h).

Among the cohort, two traumatic cases were associated with concomitant fractures (one anterior and one posterior dislocation). As detailed in the Methods, these cases were managed with the patient-guided technique for temporary stabilization prior to definitive surgical intervention. Among the cohort, two cases involved concomitant fractures. In these specific instances, the technique was utilized not as a definitive treatment, but as a palliative maneuver to achieve temporary anatomical alignment and symptomatic relief prior to definitive surgical intervention.

Treatment outcomes

A total of 83 consecutive patients (24 males, 59 females) with acute TMJ dislocation were enrolled. The modified patient-guided technique achieved a 100% primary success rate, with all reductions completed within the specified 5-minute window. Specifically, for simple dislocations (n = 81), a 100% reduction success rate was achieved, while for those complicated by condylar fractures (n = 2), the maneuver provided successful palliative alignment as a crucial bridge to definitive surgery. No iatrogenic injuries or complications were observed in either group (Fig. 2).

The process demonstrated high efficiency across all case types; the mean time to successful reduction was 150 ± 51.78 s (range: 28–280 s), with a median of 146 s (IQR: 124–183 s). Notably, no cases reached the 300-second protocol limit, confirming that the 100% success rate was achieved without censoring bias.

This high success rate likely reflects favorable case selection and operator experience and may not be generalizable to all clinical settings.

Regarding patient tolerance, the procedure was reported as minimally traumatic. Although validated scales such as the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) were not prospectively recorded in this retrospective series, clinical nursing notes and physician charts were reviewed for descriptors of patient tolerance. Most patients were documented as experiencing ‘minimal discomfort’ during the active phase of the maneuver and ‘significant relief’ upon reduction. The technique circumvented the requirement for procedural sedation or anesthesia in all simple dislocation cases (n = 81). Pharmacological intervention was reserved exclusively for the two cases involving concomitant fractures. In these two specific instances, the maneuver was utilized to achieve temporary stabilization and anatomical alignment (Fig. 3) in preparation for definitive surgical management. Notably, the maneuver proved effective in 12 cases (14.5%) that had been previously refractory to conventional manual reduction methods (failed at least two attempts at referring facilities). No iatrogenic complications, such as secondary fractures or ligamentous injuries, were observed during or after the procedure.

Refractory cases prior to presentation

Among the 83 cases, 12 patients (14.5%) were referred from primary care clinics or dental offices following unsuccessful reduction attempts. These initial attempts were performed by general dentists or emergency residents (average experience 5 years) using the traditional Hippocratic maneuver (downward and backward intraoral pressure). Each case had undergone at least two documented unsuccessful attempts. The mean time interval from the last failed attempt to successful reduction with our guided technique was 4.2 h (range 2–12 h). While muscle fatigue from prior manipulations could theoretically facilitate subsequent reduction, the persistent spasm and increased patient anxiety observed upon arrival suggest that the biomechanical advantage of our patient-guided technique was the primary driver of success.

Discussion

Proposed biomechanical rationale

The efficacy of this modified and optimized patient-guided technique is hypothesized to stem from the synergy between clinician-applied traction and active patient movement. This dynamic approach leverages several biomechanical principles to enhance reduction efficiency while minimizing trauma.

Muscle relaxation and synchronized traction

When TMJ dislocation occurs, particularly in an anterior direction, a reflex muscle spasm ensues, hindering condylar repositioning [15–17].Unlike the Hippocratic maneuver, which often employs sudden, forceful downward pressure that may trigger a myotonic reflex (reflex muscle spasm), our technique relies on gentle, sustained traction. By synchronizing this traction with the patient’s voluntary mouth opening and head extension, the maneuver facilitates the relaxation of the primary elevators of the mandible—specifically the masseter and medial pterygoid muscles. This state reduces “protective guarding” and allows for a more physiological return of the condyle to the articular fossa.

Dynamic modification of the condylar-skull angle

The patient’s coordinated head movements (extension and flexion) actively modify the condylar-skull angle. This adjustment creates a more favorable orientation for the condyle to bypass the articular eminence during re-entry. Furthermore, utilizing the patient’s own neck strength allows for a more individualized level of traction force, further minimizing the risk of over-stretching the surrounding soft tissues.

Preservation of joint stability

The TMJ capsule and the temporomandibular ligament (TML) are critical for maintaining joint stability [11, 18]. Conventional high-force maneuvers carry an inherent risk of overextending these structures, potentially predisposing the patient to chronic instability. By minimizing external force and leveraging the patient’s proprioception, this technique theoretically reduces the risk of iatrogenic ligamentous strain. While long-term follow-up is needed, this preservation of anatomical integrity suggests a potential advantage in maintaining post-reduction stability.

Clinical considerations for atypical dislocations and fractures

While anterior dislocations are the most common presentation, our series included lateral (n = 16) and posterior (n = 5) cases. These types are clinically rare and often associated with high-energy trauma, such as fractures of the condylar neck or the external auditory canal [19, 20]. Our technique successfully managed these atypical cases by utilizing coordinated movement to navigate the condyle around bony obstacles. However, we must emphasize that imaging (CT or MRI) is mandatory in all traumatic presentations to screen for fractures before any manipulation, as forceful movement could exacerbate an underlying injury.

Clinical observations and procedural pragmatism

It is noteworthy that in this series, lateral dislocations appeared to be influenced by, or occurred secondary to, unsuccessful self-reduction attempts or improper manual maneuvers performed by non-specialized clinicians prior to hospital presentation. We speculate that these initial attempts may have inadvertently shifted the condyle from an anterior to a lateral position. While the exact biomechanical transition requires further kinematic validation, our clinical observations suggest a possible association between improper external force application and these atypical presentations. Due to the severe distress and the clear clinical diagnosis (e.g., severe lateral mandibular deviation), a subset of these lateral cases proceeded with immediate guided reduction to prioritize urgent pain relief without pre-procedural imaging. While this represents a pragmatic clinical decision in an emergency setting, we emphasize that radiographic confirmation remains the standard, especially in traumatic presentations, to mitigate medicolegal risks and rule out occult fractures.

The deviation from the ‘imaging first’ protocol in 14.5% of our cohort (12/83 cases) was based on specific clinical criteria: (1) non-high-energy trauma etiology, (2) obvious clinical deformity consistent with lateral displacement secondary to prior manipulation, and (3) intolerable pain. However, we emphasize that this represents a pragmatic clinical decision in an emergency setting and does not replace the requirement for pre-reduction imaging in cases of high-energy blunt trauma where fractures are highly suspected.

Ease of learning and patient tolerance

The simplicity of this approach facilitates rapid proficiency, potentially allowing for its application in diverse clinical settings. Furthermore, by avoiding high-velocity manual force, the procedure is better tolerated, enhancing patient cooperation and reducing the psychological distress often associated with traditional reduction methods.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the high success rate observed in this series, several limitations must be acknowledged:

Study Design and Bias: As a retrospective case series, this study is inherently limited in its ability to establish definitive causality. The findings are subject to selection and information bias, as data collection relied on existing clinical records from a single emergency department. Furthermore, the high success rate (100%) observed in this study may be influenced by single-operator bias, as all reductions were performed by an experienced senior specialist. The generalizability of this technique to novice clinicians or different clinical settings remains to be validated in multi-center trials.

Post-reduction protocol and follow-up limitations

Following successful reduction, all patients were provided with standardized post-operative instructions, including a soft-diet for two weeks and restrictions on maximum mouth opening (e.g., supporting the chin during yawning). While this retrospective series primarily evaluated immediate reduction success, we conducted a secondary review of the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system for a period of up to six months post-procedure. No patients from this cohort returned to our emergency department with recurrent dislocations within the first 30 days. However, we lack prospective, long-term data regarding functional recovery (e.g., precise range of motion), chronic pain, or recurrence managed at other facilities. It is important to note that while this technique effectively addresses the acute dislocation event, it does not modify underlying anatomical predispositions—such as ligamentous laxity or articular eminence morphology—that may contribute to chronic joint instability.

Thirdly, the assessment of pain during the procedure remains semi-quantitative. The absence of standardized pain measurement tools (e.g., VAS or NRS) at the time of treatment limits our ability to objectively compare the discomfort level of this technique with traditional methods. Future prospective studies should incorporate these validated scales to provide more rigorous data on patient experience.

Concomitant Fractures and Critical Clinical Caution Regarding cases involving concomitant fractures, we must emphasize that our observations are limited to only two specific instances. In these cases, the modified maneuver was not intended as a definitive or curative treatment for the fractures themselves. Instead, it was utilized as a palliative measure solely to achieve temporary anatomical alignment and symptomatic relief as a bridge to definitive surgical intervention.

Given the high risk of displacement or exacerbation of injury, we do not recommend the routine application of this technique in the presence of suspected or confirmed fractures. Pre-reduction radiographic evaluation (CT or MRI) is mandatory in all traumatic presentations. Any attempt at guided reduction in fracture-associated cases must be approached with extreme clinical caution and should only be considered by experienced clinicians as an adjunct to the broader surgical management plan.

Theoretical mechanism

The proposed biomechanical mechanism—centered on active muscle relaxation and dynamic condylar-skull angle modification—remains primarily theoretical. At present, we lack objective validation through advanced modalities such as dynamic imaging (cine-MRI) or electrophysiological monitoring (EMG) to confirm the specific muscle-sparing effects hypothesized.

Future research

Prospective, randomized controlled trials are necessary to compare the efficacy and safety of this patient-guided approach against conventional maneuvers. Further investigations incorporating long-term follow-up and objective physiological data are critical to validating the clinical utility and safety profile of this technique.

Conclusion

This modified and optimized, patient-guided approach offers favorable outcomes observed in this cohort advantages in reducing TMJ dislocations, including ease of use, minimal pain, high success rate, and reduced risk of complications. By empowering patients to actively participate, this technique provides a less traumatic and more patient-friendly alternative. Further prospective comparative research with a larger cohort is warranted to definitively establish its clinical effectiveness and long-term outcomes.
