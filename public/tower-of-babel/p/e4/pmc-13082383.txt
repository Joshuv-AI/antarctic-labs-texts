Getting better or worse? How trends in healthy life expectancy within working age are shaped by diverging trends among age groups
Authors: Juliane Tetzlaff, Lieselotte Mond, Batoul Safieddine, Stefanie Sperlich, Susanne Steffens, Fabian Tetzlaff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgainst the backdrop of population ageing and growing labour shortage, Healthy Life Expectancy (HLE) in working age represent a key resource for individual wellbeing and economic growth. This study investigates how age-specific trends shape the development of HLE within working age and whether these trends differ between health indicators. We used the German Socioeconomic Panel to calculate HLE between age 18 and 64 for three periods between 2002 and 2022 (N = 232 393) based on Self-rated Health (SRH), mental and physical Health-related Quality of Life (p/mHRQoL). We decomposed changes in HLE over time into the contributions by age group and distinguished between morbidity and mortality contributions. For men, HLE increased in terms in SRH but remained largely unchanged in terms of mHRQoL and pHRQoL. For women, HLE decreased in terms of SRH and pHRQoL while it remained stable for mHRQoL. Deteriorating health in younger working-age groups strongly contributed to decreases in HLE while improving health at older working age fostered increases in HLE. Mortality contributions were minor. We found divergent contributions to trends in HLE by age group with the younger working-age population fostering decreases in HLE while older age groups fostered an increase. Furthermore, trends differed by health indicator, which underlines the importance of analysing more than one indicator whenever possible. The findings are worrying and suggest that the labour force may decline not only due to population ageing, but also due to the deteriorating health, posing growing challenges for both health and labour market policies.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of prolonged working lives and the growing shortage of skilled workers, health at working age is not only an important resource for individual well-being but also for a country’s economic development [1]. Healthy Life Expectancy (HLE) is a key measure of the number of years in good health [2]. It can be determined for the entire life span, for older age, as well as for working age (e.g. [3, 4]) and defined based on various health indicators [5, 6].

While there are numerous studies that describe the development of HLE at older age (e.g. [2, 5]), studies with a specific focus on the healthy life years within working age are rare [7]. So far, we also know too little about the contribution of age groups to trends in HLE [7]. This holds especially true for working age. In general, it is conceivable that health in some age groups develops better than in others, and that some age groups contribute stronger to increases in HLE [8]. This means that a growth in HLE could, in extreme cases, be borne by strong improvements in health in a few age groups only, while health in other age groups remains constant or even deteriorates over time. From a public health perspective, the knowledge of the age-specific contributions on trends in HLE is important. This knowledge can help to identify age groups with a particular need for prevention and show how many healthy years within a specific age group should be gained to prevent a downward trend in overall HLE in a population. This paper investigates trends in HLE across the last two decades in Germany as an example of one of the oldest population in the world [9], with a rapidly ageing workforce [10] and strong increases in statutory retirement age [11]. Some earlier studies have suggested that the prevalence of poor health might have developed somewhat more unfavourably in younger age groups than in older ones beyond working age [12–14]. So far, however, the effect of how age-specific trends shape the development of healthy years at working age and how many healthy years are lost due to deteriorating health at younger age groups (or gained due to improving health) remains largely unknown.

The WHO defines health as ‘a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity’ [15], which illustrates the multifaceted nature of health. Depending on the health indicator used, trends in HLE can differ over time [6]. In line with this, we analysed three different widely used health indicators in this paper to cover a broad picture of health: Self-rated Health (SRH) as a global health indicator [16] and physical and mental Health-related Quality of Life (p/mHRQoL) to depict physical and mental components of health and wellbeing [17, 18]. Using three indicators also allowed us to examine whether patterns of age contributions are consistent or differ between the indicators. Different patterns could indicate, for example, a more favourable (or less favourable) development in the number of ‘healthy’ years of life among younger (or older) people in one health dimension compared to another.

Previous studies showed that poor health in terms of these indicators is correlated to reduced work ability, early retirement and more sick-leave days [19–22]. Against the background of the shortage of skilled workers in many countries, this emphasizes the importance to study trends in HLE during working age based on these indicators.

The paper is guided by three research questions:

Methods

Data and health indicators

We used the data of the German Socioeconomic Panel (GSOEP V.39). With about 30 000 respondents per year aged 18 years and older living in private households the GSOEP is one of the largest surveys in social science in Europe [23]. Mental and physical Health-related Quality of Life (m/pHRQoL) is based on the Short-Form-Health Survey, Version 2.0 (SF-12v2) questionnaire [18], which has been asked every 2 years since 2002 in the GSOEP survey. The SF-12 depicts eight health domains, which can be summarized into a physical and a mental subscale of HRQoL. The SF-12 spans a norm-based score range from 0 to 100 and is standardized to 50, which depicts the population average of m/pHRQoL in the GSOEP in 2004. For this standardized score, a 10 score point difference marks a meaningful up-/downward deviation from the national norm value of 50 [18]. Self-rated Health (SRH) is based on a 5-point scale ranging from ‘very good’ to ‘bad’ [24] and is included in all GSOEP survey waves.

In this paper, we accounted for the multifaceted nature of health by analysing three health indicators and by using more than two health states for the Health Expectancy calculation (which usually is ‘healthy’ and ‘unhealthy’). We therefore extended the analyses to a middle category of health for all three indicators. This also allows to capture changes over time in intermediate health states. For SRH, we classified good SRH by combining ‘very good’ and ‘good’ SRH, satisfactory SRH, and poor SRH (combining ‘poor’ and ‘bad’ SRH). For m/pHRQoL, score values of 60 and above were classified as ‘above average’ values <60 and >40 as ‘average’, and values of 40 and below as ‘below average’ m/pHRQoL. To provide sufficient case numbers within each health state by gender and age group, the pooled data of single survey years were used to analyse time trends in Health Expectancies (2002–08, 2010–16, 2018–22). As the SF-12 questionnaire is only answered every second year, only the even years of the survey were considered. The survey on which this study is based on was carried out by the German Institute for Economic Research (Deutsches Institut für Wirtschaftsforschung, DIW). The data are available for scientific purposes on request from the DIW. The DIW is responsible for all legal requirements and ethical aspects regarding these data. Therefore, it was not necessary for the authors to obtain the approval of an ethics committee or informed consent of the participants for this study. All data were fully anonymized when we received them.

Statistical analysis

We used the Sullivan method to calculate Health Expectancies in the three different health states for SRH, mHRQoL, and pHRQoL. The Sullivan method combines life table information with prevalence proportions [25]. The analyses are restricted to the working age, i.e. all Health Expectancies reported in this paper are partial expectancies at age 18 up to age 64 (which means that at most 47 ‘healthy’ years would be possible if morbidity and mortality rates between the ages of 18 and 64 were zero). We used the official population and death statistics of Germany to calculate life tables for all three periods considered [26, 27] and weighted the life years lived within the respective period and 5-year age group with the prevalence proportion of the same period and age group drawn from the survey. The age- and period-specific prevalence proportions were weighted with the cross-sectional survey weight provided in the dataset to account for differences between the unweighted respondent structure and the population structure. Furthermore, we decomposed the difference in Health Expectancies between the first and the last period for the three different health states per health indicator into the contributions of 5-year age groups using the method introduced by Arriaga and extended by Nusselder and Looman [28]. Shkolnikov and Andreev pointed out that this method can lead to misspecified age contributions [29]. However, this can be avoided by applying the calculation steps as described by van Raalte and Nepomuceno [8]. We applied all steps of the calculation accordingly which led to correct calculations of the age contributions. We distinguished between contributions to the change in Health Expectancies over time caused by changes in mortality and caused by changes in prevalence proportions. However, the effect of decreasing mortality on trends in HLE within working age between the first and the last period is minor [partial LE at age 18 to age 64 increased by only 0.1 years in women (46.1–46.2) and 0.4 years in men (45.1–45.5)]. All analyses were carried out separately for women and men using Stata 14 and R 4.4.3.

Results

In total, 232 393 individuals aged 18–64 years across all three periods were included in the analysis. In general, the proportion of (very) good SRH is higher than those of above average physical and mental HRQoL. This holds for both genders (Table S1).

Overall, the development of life expectancy in good health of the working age population differed by health indicator (Fig. 1). For SRH, there is also a clear difference in time trends between men and women. While life years in (very) good SRH increased in men due to a shift from the life years in satisfactory SRH, years in poor SRH increased in women (again due to a shift from the middle category). The pattern of shifts between health states in Health Expectancies in terms of pHRQoL among women is very similar to that in SRH: life years in below-average pHRQoL increased while they decreased in average pHRQoL. No changes were found for men. For mHRQoL, we did not find any clear trend towards an increase or decrease in Life Expectancy in the three health states. Life years spent in poor mental health remained stable on a persistently high level (Fig. 1).

Focusing on more general patterns, it becomes apparent that the shift from satisfactory to (very) good SRH in men (Fig. 1) was driven by clear age patterns (Fig. 2). While LE in (very) good SRH increased strongest between age 40 and 59 (+0.2–0.3 years within each of the 5 years’ age group) but deteriorated somewhat below age 30, decreases in satisfactory SRH were also driven by changes in middle and higher working age (age 30–54). For age groups below age 40, we also found increases in years in poor SRH. The major part of these trends resulted from shifts in prevalence proportions over time, while the contribution of changes in mortality rates remained minor. For women, increases in life years in poor SRH (Fig. 1) were driven by increasing years in poor health among the younger: life years in poor health increased by more than 0.5 years due to deterioration in SRH in young working age between age 18 and 30 alone (Fig. 2). We found a similar age pattern for women in terms of pHRQoL (Fig. 3). Here too, changes in poor health (below-average pHRQoL) were driven by deteriorating health in the younger working-age women. The deterioration up to the age of 34 alone adds 0.5 years of life with below-average pHRQoL. For men, contributions towards increasing life years in below-average pHRQoL were also strongest at younger and middle working age (Fig. 3). For mHRQoL, no clear age patterns were found (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This is one of the few studies investigating how age-specific health trends contribute to changes in Health Expectancies over time and, to the best of our knowledge, so far the only one with a specific focus on the working-age population comparing trends in different health indicators. There are two important findings of our study: First, we found clear evidence for convergent trends in Health Expectancies by age group as health deteriorated at younger working age and fostered increases years in poor health, while health at older working age improved and led to increases in years in good health. This holds for women as well as for men in terms of SRH and pHRQOL. Second, based on three health indicator specific analyses, we found that trends in the working-age population differed depending on how health is defined. For men, ‘healthy’ years during working age in terms of SRH increased over time, while they stagnated in terms of pHRQoL and mHRQoL. Among women, ‘unhealthy’ years based on poor SRH and below average pHRQoL declined, while we found again no clear trend in mHRQoL.

From a public health perspective, the results appear concerning, as the number of years lived in poor SRH and below-average pHRQoL are rising clearly among younger people. This trend is stronger in working-age women than in men. In the past, numerous studies have found a compression of morbidity among the older population, i.e. an absolute or relative decrease in the number of years lived in poor health. Many of the studies used to build this evidence focused on less specific health impairments most prevalent among the older population such as functional limitations and disabilities [2, 5, 7], while some also showed increasing life years free of serious specific diseases such as cancer, stroke, dementia, or myocardial infarction [30–34]. However, these studies investigated changes in healthy years among the older population or across the full age range without differentiation between trends among the younger and the older.

There is still too little evidence on how trends in health in different age groups shape the development of HLE over time. This holds especially for the working age, which makes comparisons with our study difficult. One study investigated the development of HLE over time in India covering a broader age range [35]. Although the cultural and economic contexts differ greatly between the two studies, this paper is consistent with our findings as it found a decline in health in younger working-age adults, contributing to an increase in years lived with disability. This trend towards poorer Health Expectancy was largely offset by improvements in health at older age, so that the number of years with disability in India remained fairly constant over time [35]. Focusing on trends in Europe, another study found morbidity expansion or constant proportions of poor life years in most countries based on the EU-SILC study and the Global Activity Limitation Indicator (GALI) [36]. For countries with decreasing HLE at birth, the strongest contributions to this trend were made by increasing prevalence of activity limitations in younger ages. However, the results are affected by a change in the GALI question in Germany resulting in a massive increase in HLE in 2015 [6, 36] so that a useful within-country comparison with our study cannot be made. One other study investigated changes in HLE at age 60 in various European countries based on the GALI indicator [3]. The results show that age groups contributed differently in strength to the general trend of morbidity compression among the elderly, but there is no evidence that there are clear patterns in the contributions of the age groups [3]. Another study investigated trends in HLE at age 65 in the Swedish population and found similar trends of morbidity compression in terms of SRH and GALI [37]. These two studies might suggest that health develops more consistently over time beyond working age. Further research is needed to test this hypothesis.

Concerning health trends in Germany, our results are in line with two previous studies that found that health in terms of SRH and pHRQoL developed less favourable among the younger age groups than in the older ones, with improvements being strongest beyond working age [12, 13]. Furthermore, there are other studies that also found deteriorations in health based on specific diseases in the younger and middle-aged population in Germany. These include diabetes [38], myocardial infarctions [39], and musculoskeletal disorders [4]. It therefore remains to be seen whether health among the younger working-age population will start to improve again, or whether the poor health trend will progress to older age groups over time and lead to a clear expansion of morbidity driven by the younger cohorts. In any case, our study shows that younger people today have poorer health than older generations at the same age. Since poor SRH and pHRQoL are associated with reduced work capacity and premature retirement [19–22], the increase in years of poor health found in younger working age points to growing challenges for policies aiming at increasing the retirement age and for the labour market, which is facing a growing labour shortage. These findings underscore the importance of effective prevention measures that focus on improving general and physical health in young and middle working age.

The results of the study underline the multifaceted nature of health. The study clearly shows that a trend identified in one health indicator does not necessarily have to be found in another indicator, which is in line with a previous study comparing the effect of the choice of the health indicator on trends in HLE [6]. This seems obvious at first glance, but it is central to the question of how to define ‘healthy’ when calculating HLE. While the European Commission proposes the use of the GALI indicator to calculate HLE [40], there are many other health indicators used for the calculation e.g. [2, 5]. The use of a variety of indicators greatly enriches research, precisely because different indicators reflect different aspects of health and using them can help to provide a more differentiated picture of the development of ‘Healthy’ Life Expectancy. Using different and more specific indicators can also help to identify specific prevention needs in the population, which is much more difficult to depict with more global health indicators. However, the study shows the importance of acknowledging the multifaceted nature of health when interpreting the results and putting them into the current research context, and that the indicators should not be used interchangeably.

Strengths and limitations

The data used for this study contain information on several health indicators and provide high case numbers. This enabled us to calculate Health Expectancies using three indicators and to analyse not only changes in years spent in a ‘healthy’ and ‘unhealthy’ state, but also in the state in between, thereby taking into account the complexity of health. We applied a decomposition method that made it possible to break down changes in Health Expectancies over time into age contributions and to distinguish between the effect of changing mortality and morbidity based on the Sullivan method.

The Sullivan method had the limitation that it is based on prevalence proportions and that therefore health-mortality dynamics cannot be mapped (e.g. transition rates from healthy -> dead, and sick -> dead). This implies that the endogeneity between morbidity and mortality cannot be accounted for. Therefore, a decomposition based on the Sullivan method usually results in an underestimation of morbidity contributions and an overestimation of mortality contributions as the same mortality risks are assumed for each health state. Regarding endogeneity, we assume at most a slight overestimation of the mortality effect, as the contribution of mortality in the age group of 18- to 64-year-olds considered in our analyses is very low anyway. We weighed this limitation against the advantages of the method concerning the dataset characteristics: There are implausibly (very) small number of recorded deaths in 2016–22 and no information on the vital status in 2022 (the respective sub-data set is no longer updated in the current SOEP version). Therefore, we decided against an incidence-based multistate method that requires information on deaths by the three analysed health states of the respondents and for the Sullivan method that relies on the official mortality data and not on the reported deaths in the survey. Another point in favour of using the Sullivan method is that physical and mental HRQoL is only recorded every 2 years, which means that the time of incidence can only be determined imprecisely.

Although sample weights were used in the analysis, it cannot be ruled out that the structure of the survey participants does not fully correspond to that of the total German working-age population. For example, the data only include people living in private households. However, this should not affect our analyses, as the proportion of people living in institutions is very low among the working-age population. Furthermore, it is likely that people in good health are overrepresented in the data due to selective survey participation. Therefore, it is likely that Healthy Life Expectancy is somewhat overestimated in our study. However, time trends remain unaffected by this sort of bias as long as this overestimation does not change over time, for which we have found no indication so far.

The season at the time of the survey could have an impact on the subjectively measured health indicators used and thus could influence the results. We therefore performed sensitivity analyses on the influence of the season on prevalence. This was done by controlling for the influence of the season on the odds for the respective health outcome in logistic regression models and using post-regression commands (‘predict’ in Stata) to output the estimated prevalence by age. These adjusted age-specific prevalences were then used as input for the life table analysis. This had only a minor impact on our results, suggesting that possible shifts in seasons between survey waves did not affect our findings (Figs. S1–S4).

The cultural composition of the population can change over time and may influence the average perceptions of one’s health. Similarly, perceptions of one’s own health can also change across generations. When using subjective health indicators, it cannot be ruled out that these changes in the population structure may have an impact on trends in health over time. This could apply in particular to SRH, which, in contrast to physical and mental quality of life, is based on a very general single question. In the case of physical and mental HRQoL, we assume this effect to be minor due to the specific questions asked which leave less space for individual interpretation. However, against the backdrop of other studies focusing on specific diseases that also found worsening health among the younger and middle-aged population [4, 38, 39], it can be concluded that the health of the younger population in Germany has indeed deteriorated over time and that this is consequently also reflected in subjective health indicators as reported by this study.

Conclusion

We found clear patterns regarding the contributions of age groups to the general trend in Health Expectancies among working age for two out of three analysed health indicators. These patterns show that deteriorating health in younger working age increased life years in poor SRH and below-average pHRQoL, while trends in older working age increased healthy years. If these developments continue in the future, this would lead to a paradigm shift from morbidity compression currently found among the older population to morbidity expansion. This would pose challenges in many areas of society, including raising the retirement age, combating labour shortages, and public health efforts. However, no such development was found for mHRQoL, which underscores that trends differ between health indicators and the results should be interpreted against the backdrop of the multifaceted nature of health.
