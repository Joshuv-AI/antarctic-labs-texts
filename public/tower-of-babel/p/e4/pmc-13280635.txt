Managing patients with mental health conditions in family medicine: a qualitative study exploring the experiences of general practitioners' experiences in Switzerland
Authors: Juliane Messer, Konstantinos Tzartzas, Nguyen-Toan Tran, Christine Cohidon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral practitioners (GPs) frequently serve as the first or only point of contact for patients with mental health disorders, playing crucial roles in detection, prevention, and management. Despite this, many GPs feel poorly prepared to address mental health issues, often citing the barriers of limited time, heavy workloads, and insufficient training.ObjectivesThe present study's main objective was to make an in-depth exploration of the potential difficulties encountered by GPs as they manage and follow up with patients with mental health problems.MethodsOur qualitative approach involved semi-structured individual interviews with 17 GPs from French-speaking Switzerland. Interviews were transcribed and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis within an inductive theoretical framework. Once the most important themes and subthemes had emerged, we externally and internally validated them.ResultsFour principal themes emerged. GPs reported that: (i) they enjoyed caring for patients with mental health issues, valuing their unique position in providing comprehensive, long-term support; (ii) they faced significant systemic barriers, including frustration with limitations related to Switzerland's health assurance scheme; (iii) collaboration with psychiatrists was lacking and resources were insufficient; and (iv) they required help and new ideas to improve the quality of their care.ConclusionGPs' experiences revealed a mixed reality: their dedication to patient care existed alongside their frustration with systemic constraints, highlighting the complexity of mental health management in primary care. Sustainable improvements would require addressing gaps in caregivers' training, greater interprofessional collaboration, and more public health initiatives to mainstream mental health care into society.Keywords: mental health, primary health care, general practitioners, mental health disorders, qualitative research, health services accessibility

Introduction

General practitioners (GPs) regularly have to deal with patients suffering from mental health disorders [1-3]. A prevalence of 25%–60% of mental disorders in primary care medicine patients has been reported worldwide [4-6]. GPs are often the first or even the only health-care professionals in contact with these patients [7, 8]. Indeed, they are among the principal actors in the detection, prevention, and adapted therapeutic management of mental health disorders [1, 2, 8-12]. For example, in 2022, 7.8% of Switzerland's population had received care for a mental health issue in the past 12 months, and 17.2% of this group reported having been treated, at least partially, by their GP [13].

Whether the initial reason for a consultation is psychological or somatic in nature, some GPs can perceive those involving mental health issues to be more difficult [14-16] because they often feel that they lack the skills to deal with them [16, 17]. Indeed, studies have shown that patients' psychiatric comorbidities play a major role in physicians' perceptions of how difficult a consultation is [18-20.] These difficulties have only been partially explored and seem related to a variety of factors, such as the consultation's limited length, not always giving GPs enough time to manage mental health issues properly [15, 21].

The lack of theoretical knowledge passed on during their medical education and the narrow specific training in mental health also seem to limit GPs' therapeutic capabilities [14, 22, 23]. In many countries, including Switzerland, clinical experience in a psychiatric setting is not a mandatory part of family practice residency programmes. GPs' difficulties collaborating with dedicated mental health professionals are also sometimes barriers to effective treatment, particularly in terms of communication and availability [1, 15, 24, 25].

In Switzerland, a basic health assurance policy is compulsory for all residents. Psychiatric consultations are part of basic care, but psychiatrists' availability is limited. Since 1 July 2022, psychologists who have completed post-graduate training as psychotherapists can be reimbursed through basic health assurance schemes provided that a GP has issued a prescription for psychotherapy of up to 30 sessions. Further prescriptions must be handled by a psychiatrist. The evolving regulatory context is reshaping psychiatric treatment options in Switzerland [26]. Nevertheless, GPs agree that there is considerable room for improvement in this area [16].

The present study's main objective was to conduct an in-depth investigation of the difficulties GPs encounter in the management and follow-up of patients with mental health problems, including their real-life experiences, the context, and the challenges related to this type of care. A secondary objective was to study GPs' needs in this domain to identify potential solutions for improving the mental health management of patients in family medicine. Our results could help guide future recommendations for overcoming barriers to mental health treatment in general practice and identify solutions that meet GPs' particular needs for improving the management of patients with psychiatric comorbidities.

Methods

With these objectives in mind, we chose a qualitative methodology involving individual semi-structured interviews that would enable a better understanding of GPs' lived experiences [27]. We followed the COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) and SRQR (Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines throughout this work [28].

Participants

We purposively sampled GPs from the network of over 500 French-speaking GPs in Switzerland with whom we regularly work and whom we solicited for this study. We also included three GPs who collaborate directly with a psychiatrist who is present once a week in their primary care practices [29, 30]. The GPs were recruited and participated voluntarily, signing an informed consent form before we recorded their interview; audio data were processed anonymously. Participants were free to withdraw from the study at any time. As compensation for their time, each participant was given a CHF 50 gift voucher valid in a bookshop (see Table 1).

Our sampling considered the variable criteria of a rural versus an urban location, individual versus group practices, sex, age, and years of experience.

Data

Interviews lasted from 35 to 60 minutes and mostly took place in GPs' offices; four GPs came to our research office. They were conducted in French by this manuscript's main author between January and May 2023. After 17 interviews, we estimated that data saturation had been reached [31, 32]. The interview guide was based on the results of previous studies investigating the particular difficulties of managing mental health issues in family medicine contexts, including GPs' experiences regarding referrals to specialists and their collaboration with psychiatrists and other health professionals [29, 30, 33]. The interview guide was developed jointly by the whole research team. During the interviews, the term” mental health” was used in a very broad sense to include all patients with psychological symptoms, whether they had a formal psychiatric diagnosis or not.

Analysis

Once the interviews had been transcribed, we used a reflexive thematic analysis as this approach enabled us to analyse the data both flexibly and thoroughly when forming codes and developing themes [34-36]. The study's underlying theoretical framework and methodological orientation were inductive and aimed to gain a deeper understanding of the multiple experiences of the GPs interviewed [27, 34].

JM began by randomly selecting one interview and then attentively reading and precoding each statement within it. KT did the same with another randomly picked transcription. They shared their precoding and started to create a first version of the codebook, based on these precodes. They used this initial codebook to independently code two further interview transcriptions, and both made propositions to add, merge, or delete codes. JM tested the resulting second version of the codebook on a fifth transcription and adapted it further. The goal was to be able to code most of the significant statements, which went some way to answering the research question. The codebook's third version was tested by asking CC (who had not yet participated in the codebook's development) to code a sixth transcription with it. She made comments about what she could not code using the codebook, and, consequently, some minor readjustments were made. The final codebook was based on an analysis of six interviews, or 35% of all the data (see Supplementary Annex).

We then used ATLAS.ti software to analyse all the interview transcriptions, which consisted of coding every interview using the codebook, slowly making themes emerge, and considering their frequency and the context in which they appeared. Once the most important themes and subthemes had emerged, we proceeded to their external validation by two GPs, one of whom participated in the study and the other did not, and their internal validation by the research team, illustrating each subtheme with a quote from the interviews. Triangulation between the researchers took place throughout the process.

Caring for patients: the GP's primary mission

Caring for patients: the GP's primary mission

1.1 Emotional duality: between ease and a sense of injustice: The GPs surveyed stated that a large proportion of their patients “only” required mental health support rather than specialist psychiatric care. They added that they felt quite comfortable fulfilling this role, believing that the whole point of general practice is to have an overall vision of a patient's health. They also reported that their long-term follow-up of their patients put them in a privileged position to provide this support.

“[…] I don't have any specific skills in psychiatry or anything like that, but I can still give people with these difficulties a bit of support.” (GP10)

“So, um, I feel pretty comfortable with that [taking care of patients' mental health] because it's part of the overall care of a… of a patient.” (GP13)

At the same time, some GPs felt frustrated by the barriers imposed by Switzerland's health-care system and the lack of recognition shown for their skills. They also reported feelings of inequity and injustice regarding their workloads.

“[…] it's that… care isn’t as good as… isn’t as good as we'd like. We can't manage and that's difficult.” (GP1)

“And I think that the on-call system is unfair, because it's up to the family doctor to do all the work […].” (GP5)

1.2 Caring for the victims of a dysfunctional society: Several GPs mentioned that they felt Switzerland's labour market itself was “sick” and, more generally, they spoke of a “dysfunctional” society, reporting that a large part of their clinical work involved supporting people who had developed mental health problems due to their stressful professional environment.

“They are in perfect psychological condition, but society declares them to be psychologically ill. Why? Because they come to me in distress; because they are victims of mobbing and harassment at work.” (GP4)

1.3 More complex somatic treatment: Another difficulty reported by the GPs interviewed was that psychiatric comorbidities could interfere with somatic treatments, increasing the complexity of their usual care.

“A patient known to have bipolar disorder, who also has insulin-dependent diabetes, is very complicated […] so we end up having very low threshold requirements [for his treatment] when this is a patient who […] deserves to have optimal control of his diabetes […].” (GP9)

1.4 Helping patients to accept their problem: The study's participants emphasized the difficulty of getting patients to accept the psychological dimensions of their symptoms.

“It's “symptomatic reassignment”. In the patient's mind, the symptom might be cancer, and in the doctor's mind it's, “No, this person really has too much stress, or is exhausted, or can't take it anymore, or is anxious, or I don't know what”, and so this is the difficult part of general practice.” (GP6)

1.5 Adolescents' mental health needs: Finally, the interviewed GPs said that they were poorly trained to deal with young adults and adolescents, a population that is significantly affected by mental health problems.

“Yes, so adolescent patients are complicated because we have… I don't have, I don't have the necessary resources to be able to look after them […].” (GP3)

2. Undervalued and underpaid

2. Undervalued and underpaid

2.1 Undervalued expertise: GPs underlined that their expertise in mental health—including the evaluation of sickness, invalidity, and loss of earnings—is hardly recognized by Switzerland's health assurance companies. Despite GPs' longstanding relationships with their patients and their holistic and comprehensive understanding of their medical histories, assurers increasingly ask them for psychiatric reports, even though GPs feel they are not always the best placed to complete them.

“It's just that, it's true that as soon as there's any time taken off work or a problem with the invalidity assurance or at least related to assurance, unemployment or so on, as a general practitioner, we are not recognised [as being competent].” (GP1)

2.2 Undervalued services: The GPs interviewed reported feelings of injustice concerning the tariffs at which their work on their patients' mental health was reimbursed by the health assurance companies, considering that they were not allowed to fairly invoice all the mental health services they provided, especially as the billable time for patient-related work outside of consultations is very limited.

“[…] this method of financing care […], it is not adequate to care for patients suffering from psychological disorders in particular.” (GP13)

2.3 Pressure from health assurance companies: GPs reported pressure from health assurance companies not to exceed the average consultation times for usual care. However, according to the interviewed GPs, this created a barrier to the proper management of patients with psychiatric comorbidities.

“If you're a GP who takes a lot of time—an hour per patient, for example—then your average cost per case will light up at [the health assurance umbrella organisations], and you'll get letters telling you that you're spending too much time with patients and that you have to give the money back.” (GP6)

3. Accessing psychiatric expertise

3.1 Psychiatrists are difficult to contact: The GPs in our study reported that communication with most psychiatrists was difficult, not only in terms of reaching them by telephone but also in terms of getting feedback on the care of their joint patient, which prevents good continuity of care.

“[…] in psychiatry, there's a real taboo on exchanging data, so in fact, you get no reports, no contact, you don't know what's going on.” (GP16)

3.2 Considerable waiting times for specialist treatment: One repeatedly cited obstacle was that waiting times for psychiatric treatments were very long. Several GPs said that they were getting longer and longer and that it was rare to find a psychiatrist who could see a patient before several months had elapsed.

“[…] sometimes it takes a long time, usually several months.” (GP12)

3.3 Underserved rural areas: The time needed for a referral to occur also depended on the region in which the GP practiced, with rural areas appearing to be much less well-served than cities.

“[…] well, we don't really have a psychiatrist in this region […].” (GP10)

3.4 Limited non-hospital emergency assistance: The GPs interviewed also identified the problem of “intermediate” situations, when patients required more intensive care but without needing to be admitted to a psychiatric hospital. In such situations, it was difficult for GPs to free up consultation slots or provide or find the appropriate help within their existing networks.

“[…] well, often, if we have a patient who is in a state of extreme distress, we'll have a really hard time quickly putting in place associated psychiatric or psychological care to be able to offload part of the treatment […].” (GP9)

4. GPs’ Needs

4. GPs’ Needs

4.1 Support from relevant authorities: More public health support appears critical for GPs, and they identified multiple systemic needs requiring government investment: comprehensive mental health prevention strategies targeting workplace and educational environments, and the establishment of structured referral networks of mental health specialists able to receive new patients.

“[…] maybe help us by actually doing a lot of prevention, […] maybe inform the public what the symptoms of burn-out are, what the symptoms of depression are, when to seek help, um… where the resources are […].” (GP11)

“What's missing is a type of classification of psychologists and even psychiatrists. Who does what, who we can refer to […].” (GP7)

4.2 Need for accessible basic training: The study participants expressed a need for mandatory continuing professional education on mental health for all GPs, something that goes beyond their current individual motivations to learn more about this field. Additionally, they advocated for psychiatric supervision and case management mechanisms involving a specialist physician, as well as for some formal recognition that time under supervision was part of their continuing professional education and development time.

“[…] to have some training, perhaps training that is a little more accessible to all general practitioners, including mandatory continuing education, not just for people who have a very strong interest in this speciality. Mental health in general practice is a bit like a “poor relation”, um… .” (GP2)

4.3 Collaboration and sharing responsibilities with psychiatrists: The GPs interviewed stated that they could be greatly helped by a model that shared on-call responsibilities for frontline mental health crisis management with psychiatrists, thus enhancing capacities for immediate psychiatric intervention. Overall, they wanted to have more links with psychiatrists and psychologists, for example, by having them work directly within the same practice.

“[…] where, in fact, on the front line, there are a huge number of consultations for psychiatric reasons, […] that are sometimes very serious and very complex […].” (GP9)

“[…] I'd like to have someone from [the field of] psychology in the practice; [someone] with whom we can talk through a situation.” (GP5)

4.4 Taking care of one's own mental health: Finally, the GPs described their need to take care of their own mental health, to be particularly attentive to what resonated within them when psychiatric patients talked to them about their difficulties, and to know how to protect their own psyches.

“[…] I still get the impression that I've been overloaded and that when you have [to deal with] too many mental health problems, you affect your own mental health.” (GP5)

Discussion

Our study explored the experiences of GPs managing patients with mental health problems. The findings revealed the opportunities and significant challenges of managing mental health care, highlighting systemic gaps in the professional support, training, and psychological resilience of primary care providers. Our results underlined that GPs consistently emphasized their interests in and their capacities for dealing with mental health problems in their practice. Despite numerous obstacles, they demonstrated a strong commitment to the provision of mental health care, thereby reflecting a notable duality in their professional experience. They were frequently exposed to profound patient suffering, and our results indicated that this exposure often led to frustration regarding the lack of resources available to support these patients effectively. Nevertheless, GPs' very capacity to welcome and acknowledge such suffering was in itself a valuable resource, one that merits greater institutional recognition [37, 38].

Numerous studies have demonstrated that GPs are often the first point of contact for patients in psychological distress, a situation attributable not only to the limited availability of psychiatric resources but also to the fundamental role that mental health occupies within the biopsychosocial model of family medicine [11]. Our findings underscore the need for greater institutional recognition of GPs, not only as pillars of mental health care but also as astute observers of the social and structural dysfunctions that reflect the challenges of modern work environments.

The phenomenon of the “psychiatrization” of workplace harassment issues, or more broadly, the medicalization of societal problems, clearly emerged from our findings. This trend illustrates the systemic shortcomings in simply protecting employees, as evidenced by the absence of robust occupational health policies in certain countries [39]. Although GPs are expected to address these issues, their ability to do so is frequently restricted by externally imposed limitations [40]. For example, in France, the government has implemented measures to strengthen verifications of the legitimacy of any sick leaves prescribed by GPs, whereas in Switzerland, they are reproached for spending too much time with patients. Such criticisms raise questions about whether companies should be given greater legal responsibilities, which should ensure healthier working environments for their employees.

Considering these challenges, our interviewed GPs' demands for enhanced access to psychiatric expertise and more interaction with psychiatrists appear particularly pertinent. Indeed, studies have already shown that introducing liaison psychiatrists into GPs' practices can improve patient care [29, 30]. Such an approach could be part of broader health-care system reforms, integrating innovative care models like multidisciplinary health centres. Such structures often include a psychiatric nurse or a psychologist, thus promoting interdisciplinary collaboration. Switzerland could draw inspiration from these models to optimize coordination between different professionals, supporting and expanding existing models [30]. Another area of reflection is the reciprocal integration of professionals. One question that emerges is whether mental health professionals should mainly be incorporated into general medicine practices or primary-care structures, or whether GPs should be integrated into mental health facilities. The second strategy has already been implemented in certain countries, such as the behavioural health homes in North America, which have shown promising results in terms of efficiency and continuity of care [41, 42].

In addition to addressing systemic and structural issues, our study highlighted the critical “needs of GPs' with respect to training and self-care. There is a clear need for comprehensive training in mental health, which should begin during medical studies and extend through structured work placements in psychiatric settings during residency training. Furthermore, ongoing professional development through mechanisms such as quality circles, intervisions, regular supervision, and Balint groups has been shown to be effective in enhancing clinical competence and building the psychological resilience required to manage the demands of modern practice [43].

Beyond the practical difficulties reported, our findings reveal important areas of overlap and discontinuity between primary care and specialist psychiatry. GPs manage a substantial proportion of psychological distress that does not reach specialist services, engaging in forms of implicit mental health care grounded in continuity and a biopsychosocial understanding of patient [11, 44, 45]. This suggests blurred professional boundaries and a significant overlap of competencies.

However, structural discontinuities persist, including limited communication, delayed referrals, and insufficient institutional recognition of GPs' mental health expertise. These tensions reflect not only organizational barriers but also differences in professional positioning and care models. Addressing these structural and conceptual gaps may be essential for developing more coherent and integrated mental health pathways.

Beyond individual support to patients by GPs, our findings suggested the necessity for broader, systemic-level interventions that could enhance the early detection of mental health issues, improve patient guidance, and optimize the allocation of mental health resources. For instance, developing public awareness campaigns about mental health symptoms can empower patients to seek help earlier, while the creation of standardized diagnostic and referral pathways can increase care efficiency. Clearly delineating boundaries between the roles of psychology and psychiatry specialists would further clarify care responsibilities. In addition, implementing targeted prevention initiatives across organizational settings could help address mental health issues before they escalate.

Finally, it is crucial to underscore the importance of collaboration and interdisciplinarity in managing often complex and emotionally demanding cases—siloed and single-specialty practices prove counterproductive [18, 46]. The continuity of care is essential in mental health, and our study reflected the persistent difficulties in moving beyond a binary, somatic or psychiatric view of medicine, despite the stated ideal of a biopsychosocial model and integrated care [44, 45]. While psychiatrists and GPs play central roles, the contributions of other health and social professionals must not be overlooked. In some cases, comprehensive care may require the involvement of external actors and resources outside traditional health-care's boundaries.

Strengths and limitations

A key study strength lies in our semi-structured interview approach, which enabled GPs to express their experiences freely. This design facilitated the emergence of unanticipated information and viewpoints that we had not found in prior literature.

Voluntary participation in the study may have introduced a selection bias, as our interviewees were all GPs with a pre-existing interest in mental health. As such, our findings should not be generalized to represent the experiences of all GPs in French-speaking Switzerland.

Further research exploring patient experiences with mental health care provided by GPs is warranted, mirroring studies done elsewhere (e.g. England [47]).

We defined “mental health disorders' very broadly, specifying to our study participants that this could include patients with or without a formal psychiatric diagnosis. The definition of what constitutes a mental health disorder lacks clarity, especially as a psychiatric diagnosis sometimes has to be made for legal reasons. Even assuming a certain level of uniformity in our interviewees' understandings of mental health disorders overall, how they interpret their patients and their disorders may vary. Interpreting our findings, therefore, should be done carefully.

Conclusion

General practitioners in French-speaking Switzerland occupy a central yet structurally ambivalent position in the management of mental health problems. They assume substantial responsibility for the detection, support, and longitudinal follow-up of psychological distress, but their work remains only partially integrated into specialist psychiatric structures.

Our findings suggest that primary care functions not merely as a gateway to psychiatry, but as a distinct site of mental health expertise grounded in continuity and a biopsychosocial approach. However, persistent discontinuities—such as limited collaboration mechanisms, communication gaps and institutional constraints—hinder the development of coherent care pathways.

Strengthening primary mental health care, therefore, requires clearer articulation of professional roles, improved structural interfaces between primary care and psychiatry, and greater institutional recognition of the mental health work already carried out in general practice.
