Informing the Equitable Implementation of Hepatitis C Interventions: Cost-Effectiveness Modeling in Rural Populations of People Who Inject Drugs
Authors: Sheri A. Tubach, François M. Castonguay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study evaluates the cost effectiveness of implementing evidence-based interventions among persons who inject drugs (PWID) in rural settings, where structural barriers and limited resources contribute to disproportionate hepatitis C virus (HCV) burden. Findings are intended to guide equitable implementation planning and resource allocation.MethodsWe developed an economic–epidemiological model to describe HCV transmission among PWID to assess the cost effectiveness of testing, treatment, and harm reduction interventions over a 20-year time horizon. Outcomes included healthcare costs, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs).ResultsExpanding testing and treatment was highly cost effective (US$1139 per DALY averted), achieved a 90% reduction in HCV incidence by 2035 (within 10 years of the modeled horizon), and averted 45,126 HCV infections equivalent to protecting one in four people at risk of HCV infection over 20 years. Adding syringe services programs (SSPs, 25% coverage) accelerated incidence reduction and remained cost effective (US$2116 per DALY averted). Adding medication-assisted treatment (MAT, 25% coverage) was more costly (US$12,545 per DALY averted) and prevented fewer infections than when adding SSPs. Combining expanded testing and treatment with both MAT and SSPs averted 53,503 HCV infections, protecting one in three people at risk and achieved a 90% incidence reduction in 6 years for US$14,350 per DALY averted.ConclusionsScaling up testing and treatment is a highly cost-effective strategy for rural communities disproportionately affected by HCV. Adding SSPs enhances value and offers an approach to guide policy decisions to reduce disparities where harm reduction infrastructure is limited. Investment in comprehensive, community-centered interventions is essential to advance health equity in HCV elimination efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40273-026-01622-3.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Point-of-care testing for hepatitis C virus (HCV) seroprevalence and infection provides new tools to test vulnerable populations at high risk for infection. We evaluated the cost effectiveness of meeting the World Health Organization (WHO) testing and treatment targets with low and moderate levels of harm reduction coverage using a point-of-care RNA test to provide a more equitable testing option for persons who inject drugs (PWID).Expanding testing and treatment to WHO testing and treatment levels was highly cost effective and reduced hepatitis C incidence within 10 years. Adding low levels of syringe services program coverage accelerates incidence reductions, remains cost effective, and enhances value compared with testing and treatment alone. These findings highlight that PWID in rural and underserved communities will benefit the most from the introduction of low-barrier interventions; these strategies not only improve efficiency but also reduce disparities in HCV outcomes. Our results provide actionable evidence to support local planning efforts, optimize resource allocation, and support policy development aimed at expanding equitable access to HCV prevention, testing, and treatment services for PWID in rural and underserved settings.

Introduction

In the United States, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection remains a significant public health concern, with an estimated 2.4–5.6 million people currently living with infection [1]. HCV is the most common bloodborne virus and is a leading cause of chronic liver disease, including cirrhosis and liver cancer, and death. Unequal access to care and treatment have continued to persist among vulnerable populations, particularly persons who inject drugs (PWID), who continue to bear a disproportionate burden of disease. PWID face the highest risk of infection, as the virus is most often transmitted through sharing needles and other drug-injection equipment [2–4]. In many areas of the United States, particularly in rural communities, the rising use of illicit opioids and methamphetamines is driving large increases in HCV infections among PWID [5, 6]. Yet, HCV testing and treatment rates in this population remain low, reinfection risk is high, and barriers such as stigma, criminalization, transportation, and fragmented care often prevent sustained engagement with care [7].

A health equity–driven approach requires meeting PWID where they are and removing the structural barriers to care. Evidence shows that medication-assisted treatment (MAT) and syringe service programs (SSPs) can significantly reduce the risk of HCV transmission among PWID [8]. Recent advances in testing and treatment—including the availability of point-of-care HCV RNA testing and pan-genotypic direct-acting antivirals with simplified treatment recommendations—make it possible to diagnose and initiate treatment in a single location on the same day [9, 10]. When paired with harm reduction, simplified test and treat strategies offer a pathway to achieving the World Health Organization (WHO) elimination targets for 2030, defined as a 90% reduction in new HCV cases (≤ 2 new cases/100 PWID) relative to a 2015 baseline and significant improvements across the HCV care cascade, where 90% of infected persons are diagnosed and 80% are treated [11]. However, achieving these goals remains challenging, particularly in rural areas, where access to harm reduction services, including MAT and SSPs, is often limited or non-existent, and where HCV testing and treatment among PWID remains low [12, 13].

Previous studies have demonstrated that combining high-coverage harm reduction interventions, including MAT and SSPs, along with increasing treatment, reduces HCV incidence and prevalence among PWID [14–18]. Cost-effectiveness analyses reinforce that more frequent testing, simplified treatment delivery, and scaling up both treatment and harm reduction are not only impactful but also cost effective [19–22].

While prior research has established the individual and combined benefits of these interventions, most have focused on urban settings or high-prevalence outbreaks, leaving a critical gap in understanding implementation feasibility and cost effectiveness in rural settings with dispersed populations and limited infrastructure. Using the US state of Kansas as a case study, exemplifying rural states facing geographic and service-delivery challenges, this study evaluates the cost effectiveness of expanding and integrating a combination of evidence-based interventions among PWID to generate HCV prevention policy recommendations and inform local implementation planning efforts [23, 24]. Specifically, we modeled the integrated effects of scaling MAT and SSPs at low and moderate levels with increasing point-of-care HCV RNA testing to 90% coverage and improving access to direct-acting antivirals to achieve 80% treatment uptake. By incorporating single-site, same-day test-and-treat approaches tailored to PWID, our model emphasizes removing access barriers as essential to achieving HCV elimination. Using a dynamic, Kansas-calibrated transmission model linked to economic evaluation, we estimate incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) under multiple scenarios. Our findings provide policy-relevant evidence to guide implementation planning in rural jurisdictions facing similar barriers to HCV elimination and to advance equitable access to HCV prevention, testing, and treatment services for populations most affected facing similar barriers to HCV elimination.

Methods

We developed an economic–epidemiological model to describe the disease dynamics of HCV transmission among PWID (see Fig. 1) and estimate the associated healthcare costs. The model represents HCV transmission among PWID through needle sharing and tracks disease progression across different HCV disease stages, each with specific health consequences and related healthcare expenses. Adopting a healthcare sector perspective, the analysis takes place over a 20-year time horizon from 2025 to 2045 to capture the full benefits of reduced disease progression and complications [21, 25, 26]. Using ICERs, we compare the cost effectiveness of a range of policy alternatives—varying levels of different intervention types—to the baseline status quo scenario characterized by low levels of testing and treatment and no harm reduction [27]. Model parameters were derived from published literature and Kansas Department of Health and Environment surveillance data when available (see Table S2 in the electronic supplementary material [ESM]). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the model outputs to uncertainty in model parameters, ensuring the validity and reliability of the results. Modelling Different Interventions

This paper models four interventions: HCV testing, HCV treatment, MAT and SSPs. Testing occurs annually and is randomly administered among the PWID population and serves solely to identify whether an individual is infectious, thereby enabling the possibility of treatment [19, 28]. Only a subset of PWID who test positive for HCV receive direct-acting antivirals treatment because of a mix of social and systemic barriers including unstable housing, homelessness, mental illness, lack of social support, stigma and mistrust of the healthcare system, negative experiences with providers, and limited access [29–32]. Treatment is assumed to be with an 8-week course of glecaprevir and pibrentasvir (Mavyret), a pan-genotypic direct-acting antiviral medication used to treat HCV infection [10, 33]. Only a portion of treated individuals achieve sustained virologic response [34]. Those that fail treatment can be retreated with a 12-week course of sofosbuvir, velpatasvir, and voxilaprevir (Vosevi) [35]. Harm reduction programs, including MAT and SSP, are available to all PWID regardless of HCV infection or ability to pay, diagnosis, or direct-acting antiviral treatment status or success. MAT decreases opioid cravings [36] while SSP provides access to clean, sterile syringes and other injection equipment [37]. Although their mechanisms differ, both interventions reduce the risk of HCV transmission by lowering the average number of contaminated needles used by PWID over time.

Model of Disease Transmission and Progression

A deterministic ordinary differential equations-based compartmental model was developed to simulate HCV transmission among current PWID in Kansas [3, 38, 39]. This model simulates HCV transmission dynamics by classifying PWID into five compartments based on HCV infection progression. Persons enter the model at the initiation of injection drug use and leave through permanent cessation of injecting or mortality (drug-related and other causes). As is common in this type of model, the susceptible population of PWID becomes infected based on the prevalence of HCV in the modeled PWID population, the risk of HCV transmission while sharing needles and other drug paraphernalia, and the number of shared events per year regardless of diagnosis or treatment [38]. Participation in MAT or SSP reduces the risk of acquiring HCV infection by reducing the force of infection proportionally scaled by harm reduction coverage (see Fraser et al. [16], Martin et al. [15], Ward et al. [40], for integration of MAT and SSP in compartmental models; see additional details in the ESM). HCV-infected persons are divided into four infectious compartments based on disease stage: (1) individuals in fibrosis disease stages F0–F4, where treatment with direct-acting antivirals is routinely recommended; (2) those that fail initial treatment and undergo retreatment, (3) those with decompensated cirrhosis, and (4) those with hepatocellular carcinoma. For persons in compartments 3 and 4, treatment is still recommended but is unlikely to stop disease progression due to severe disease complications. Persons who spontaneously clear HCV infection or achieve sustained virologic response move back to the susceptible compartment and can be reinfected at the same rate as those that have never been infected [38]. Persons who are not diagnosed or diagnosed and not treated can progress to decompensated cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and death. Persons that are diagnosed, treated, and fail to achieve sustained virologic response can be retreated. The four infectious compartments are modeled to account for the increased cost due to HCV complications and the progression to liver transplantation (Fig. 1). See ESM for more information on disease dynamics, testing, treatment, and harm reduction.

Model of Economic Costs

We modeled economic costs related to HCV infection as healthcare expenses varying across infectious compartments. These expenses included HCV testing, treatment with direct-acting antivirals, retreatment with direct-acting antivirals, and healthcare costs for decompensated cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver transplant.

Harm reduction interventions, MAT and SSP, were modeled as reducing the force of infection among susceptible PWID based on intervention coverage and the risk reduction associated with each intervention. Economic costs associated with MAT and SSP were applied to all active PWIDs, regardless of HCV infection status. Specifically, intervention costs were calculated by multiplying unit costs by the total number of individuals in active PWID compartments at each time step. Thus, while the epidemiologic effect of MAT and SSP operates through reductions in the force of infection, program costs reflect real-world implementation in which services are available to all PWID. Input costs are from Kansas Medicaid when available, and from published literature adjusted to 2025 US dollars (USD) [41]. Testing costs included in this model were for a point-of-care HCV RNA test and a 30-min health encounter [41]. Treatment costs include one 60-min health encounter to initiate treatment, pretreatment laboratory tests, three 30-min health encounters to monitor treatment adherence, an 8-week course of glecaprevir and pibrentasvir, and post-treatment tests [41, 42]. Costs for retreatment include one 60-min health encounter to initiate retreatment and four 30-min health encounters, a 12-week course of sofosbuvir, velpatasvir, and voxilaprevir, and post-treatment tests [35, 42]. Costs for treating complicated HCV infection included estimated medical costs for decompensated cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma and for a liver transplant [43, 44]. MAT costs include an intake visit, monthly visits, and oral buprenorphine—a drug used to treat opioid use disorders [42, 45]. Since there are currently no SSPs in Kansas, SSP costs included implementation expenses and per-person annual cost estimates based on a medium SSP serving 1250 clients per year in a rural setting [46]. More details on costs are in Table S3 in the ESM.

The main health outcomes were the decrease in the number of new HCV infections from 2025 to 2045 (20-year time horizon) and DALYs averted. Health state disutility weights for HCV infection, decompensated cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma were derived from the Global Burden of Disease study [47]. All costs and disutilities were discounted 3% annually [21, 22].

Model Parameterization

Model parameters were obtained from published literature or from Kansas Department of Health and Environment data when available.

The population of PWID in Kansas is based on a published estimate of PWID for the Midwest region in the United States [48]. Adjusting for the population in Kansas, this produced a PWID estimate of 26,139 persons (95% confidence interval [CI] 13,296–51,824). Comparing 2 years of National Survey on Drug Use and Health data for Kansas and adjusting these estimates for injection drug use produced an estimated increase of 1560 (95% CI 1040–1820) new PWID per year [49, 50]. Estimates of HCV infection, decompensated cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma are based on the prevalence of HCV in PWID in the United States and on the rate of progression from chronic uncomplicated HCV to decompensated cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma and on the rate of liver transplant [51–53]. The force of infection was calculated based on HCV prevalence in the modeled PWID population, average number of shared injections per year, and risk of HCV transmission per shared injection. For this model, we assume that MAT and SSP reduce HCV transmission risk by 50% and 56%, respectively, and by 72% if on both [54] (see ESM for details on the derivation of these estimates). The sustained virologic response probability after treatment with HCV direct-acting antivirals was 93% [55].

The remaining parameters for this model are in Table S2 in the ESM.

We assessed the cost effectiveness of each policy combination using ICERs [27], defined as the average yearly incremental cost to avert one DALY [47, 57]. Results were plotted on a cost-effectiveness plane, which plots incremental costs on the vertical axis and incremental health benefits (DALYs averted) on the horizontal axis [21]. This visual representation allows comparison of each scenario against willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds, which indicate whether an intervention is considered cost effective. Although there is no consensus on decision makers’ WTP threshold, benchmarks such as per capita healthcare expenditures and per capita GDP have been used in previous studies [58]. In this analysis, both these benchmarks are adjusted to reflect Kansas-specific values [59, 60].

Scenarios were evaluated against their ability to achieve the goal of a 90% reduction of new HCV cases, defined as ≤ 2 new cases/100 PWID (relative to a 2015 baseline). Annual incident infections were calculated as the force of infection multiplied by the susceptible population of PWID and incidence per 100 PWID was calculated. For the modeled population, this corresponds to < 235 new HCV infections annually. We assessed whether scenarios achieved this threshold by 2035 and within the 20-year modeled horizon (through 2045) [11]. Although the WHO elimination target is defined for 2030, evaluating outcomes through 2045 allows an assessment of both the near-term progress (2035) and sustained long-term impact to inform public health interventions. Of the policy scenarios that met this threshold by 2035, we calculated the relative ICER by comparing scenarios within the same intervention type to determine if any scenarios were dominated [27]. Specifically, we compared (i) 25% SSP vs 50% SSP; (ii) 25% MAT vs 50% MAT; and (iii) all potential combinations of 25% SSP + 25% MAT, 25% SSP + 50% MAT, 50% SSP + 25% MAT, and 50% SSP + 50% MAT. The dominated strategies were not analyzed further.

A probabilistic sensitivity analysis was conducted to quantify the impact of parameter uncertainty on model results [61]. Parameters were assigned probability distributions based on literature and other data sources [62, 63]. We then used a Monte Carlo simulation approach in which the model was run 5000 times with replacement sampled from the parameter uncertainty distributions shown in Table S2 in the ESM [64, 65]. This generated distribution of costs and health outcomes from which incremental cost and incremental DALYs averted were calculated and plotted on cost-effectiveness planes along with 95% CI ellipses [27]. ICERs were estimated and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves were created and compared to the WTP thresholds [27].

To account for uncertainty and heterogeneity in parametrization of the model, we conducted nine one-way sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of these results to variations in several key parameters [63], namely PWID/HCV prevalence, number of shared events per year, MAT and SSP dropout rate, the number of years until drug use cessation, the duration of the time horizon, PWID recruitment, and treatment costs. See the Sensitivity Analyses section in the ESM for more details.

All data processing and statistical analyses were performed with the R statistical computing software (version 4.4.0) using tidyverse and deSolve packages [66–68]. The 95% CI ellipses were calculated using the ConfidenceEllipse library in R [69].

The remaining 14 scenarios achieved the 90% HCV incidence reduction target within the modeled time horizon. Nine of the 14 remaining scenarios reached the target by 2035 within 5 years of the WHO’s 2030 elimination benchmark: one by 2030 (model year five), three by 2031 (model year six), two by 2032 (year 7), two by 2033 (year 8), and one by 2035 (year 10) (see Table 1). All nine policy scenarios involve the concurrent scale-up of testing and treatment, either with or without MAT and/or SSP. Increased testing and treatment alone achieves 90% reduction in HCV incidence by 2035, averting 45,126 infections at an annual incremental cost of US$1139 per DALY averted—the lowest cost option. Adding harm reduction measures leads to earlier reductions in HCV incidence but comes with higher costs. Specifically, adding 25% or 50% SSP coverage to increased testing and treatment accelerates the reduction in HCV incidence by 2 and 3 years, respectively. While 50% SSP coverage prevents an additional 1973 infections compared with 25% coverage, it has a relative ICER of US$74,734 (US$ per DALY averted), exceeding the GDP per capita WTP threshold of US$63,096. Similarly, pairing increased testing and treatment with 25% or 50% MAT coverage achieves the 90% incidence reduction also by 2032 and 2033 (in 7 and 8 modeled years). The higher MAT coverage prevents 1811 additional HCV infections but has a relative ICER of US$598,372 (US$ per DALY averted) compared with 25% coverage. Combined coverage scenarios—25% MAT with 50% SSP and 50% MAT with 25% SSP—versus the 25% MAT and 25% SSP scenario avert 1335 and 1188 additional infections, respectively, but have higher relative ICERs of US$166,710 and US$983,790 per DALY averted, respectively, also exceeding the higher WTP threshold, but achieve the 90% reduction in HCV infections all by 2031. The most expensive scenario, combining increased testing and treatment with 50% coverage of both SSP and MAT, achieves a 90% reduction by 2030, meeting the WHO elimination target (versus 2031 with 25% coverage), averts 2298 additional infections, and has a relative ICER of US$618,440 per DALY averted compared with the 25% coverage strategy. Based on these findings, the four strategies highlighted include increased testing and treatment with and without 25% SSP coverage (both below the WTP threshold of US$11,732), increased testing and treatment with 25% MAT coverage and with both 25% MAT and SSP coverage (both below the higher WTP threshold) (see Table 1 for a summary of the nine policy scenarios).

Discussion

Main Findings

We evaluated the cost effectiveness of scaling up hepatitis C virus (HCV) testing, treatment, and harm reduction—such as medication assisted treatment (MAT) and syringe service programs (SSPs)—among persons who inject drugs (PWID). We parametrized our model to the US state of Kansas to provide a case study that represents a rural jurisdiction with dispersed populations and limited harm reduction infrastructure. Our findings show that multiple strategies can substantially reduce new HCV infections and are cost effective under different willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds. Among all evaluated strategies, scaling up testing and treatment without harm reduction was generally less costly but took 10 years to reach the World Health Organization (WHO) target of a 90% reduction in new HCV infections. In contrast, incorporating one or both harm reduction strategies alongside expanded testing and treatment accelerated progress towards this target. Although these combined strategies incurred higher costs, they remained highly cost effective, with incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) values well below the upper-bound WTP threshold (based on Kansas’ GDP per capita).

Lower coverage levels of harm reduction were associated with more favorable ICERs than higher coverage levels, indicating potential diminishing returns as coverage increases. Among harm reduction strategies, implementing SSP alone yielded more favorable ICERs than MAT alone or the combination of MAT and SSP. The latter two showed potential to not be cost effective at the lower-bound WTP threshold (based on Kansas’ per-capita health expenditures), reflecting the higher costs associated with MAT provision and its relatively lower direct impact on HCV transmission compared with SSPs. Nonetheless, including MAT may offer broader benefits—such as reducing overdose risk [70], improved retention in care [71], and decreased involvement with the criminal justice system [72]—that were not captured in our model. The only strategy that met the WHO elimination target of a 90% reduction in new HCV infections was also the highest cost alternative, increased testing and treatment with 50% coverage of SSP and MAT. Despite the higher ICER, this strategy may still be relevant for policymakers prioritizing achievement of HCV elimination targets. The additional investment required to expand harm reduction coverage could be justified by the accelerated reductions in transmission and progress toward meeting WHO elimination benchmarks.

We found that the most influential parameter for all scenarios was treatment cost and for scenarios with MAT, duration on MAT and the time horizon substantially affected the cost effectiveness. Other key drivers included the duration of injecting and initial modeled HCV prevalence, highlighting the importance of accurate local surveillance data to support future modeling and decision-making efforts. To our knowledge, no other economic studies have varied duration of time on MAT, or the estimated number of shared needles in sensitivity analyses. In our study, a shorter time horizon yielded more favorable cost-effectiveness results, whereas a longer time horizon was less favorable—opposite to prior findings [21, 22]. This difference is likely because we modeled harm reduction throughout the entire time horizon, in contrast to those studies that modeled harm reduction for the first 10 years. HCV prevalence and duration of injecting have been varied in other sensitivity analyses, supporting our findings; while duration of injecting was not among the most influential parameters in our analysis, it had a modest effect across all four scenarios [19, 20, 70]. Finally, potential reductions in direct-acting antiviral costs following patent expirations in 2036 [71] are likely to further improve the cost effectiveness of all strategies, as also reported in a similar study [22].

Although our results strongly support scaling up testing and treatment, they also highlight the potential value of combining this approach with even modest levels of harm reduction. Combining low SSP coverage with testing and treatment led to improved outcomes, incurred relatively modest additional costs, and enhanced the surrounding ICER estimate. Beyond efficiency, these results underscore important health equity implications. Testing and treatment expansion provides the foundation for reducing incidence, but SSPs are uniquely positioned to extend prevention and care to PWID who face the steepest barriers to healthcare engagement [7]. However, the absence of existing SSPs in certain jurisdictions, as exemplified in Kansas, suggests the need to address policy barriers before such interventions can be implemented, which will also advance equity concerns as PWID in rural and underserved areas remain least likely to access existing prevention services.

In this context, our findings provide timely, evidence-based support for planning efforts, stakeholder engagement, and advancing policy reform.

Strengths and Limitations

While this model captures core transmission dynamics and intervention effects, it has several limitations and simplifying assumptions. First, we only included direct medical costs from a healthcare sector perspective and our model does not capture broader healthcare or other societal costs and benefits, including productivity losses, criminal justice involvement, overdose, or other injection-related infections such as HIV [37, 72–75]. Similarly to our study, many HCV cost-effectiveness analyses are from a healthcare payor perspective [21, 22, 28, 70, 76]; however, few are evaluated from a societal perspective [77, 78]. Hence, our approach, which utilizes only healthcare costs, should be seen as a more conservative approach; if we had included broader societal costs, more scenarios would have been cost effective and maybe even cost saving. Additionally, in this model we assume linear cost functions for HCV treatment, MAT, and SSP that scale costs proportionally with the number of treatments provided or services delivered. In practice, costs may not increase proportionally with program expansion; instead, economies of scale, fixed infrastructure costs spread across more clients, or diseconomies of scale, higher marginal costs in rural or resource-limited settings, may occur. However, most models adopt linear cost functions to simplify analysis, and this approach is often a reasonable approximation when detailed cost data across different scales of implementation are unavailable [22, 79].

Second, our model did not account for the disability associated with being a PWID [47]. We focused solely on disability associated with HCV infection and its complications. Although disability among PWID is generally high, and it could have reduced the apparent magnitude of the interventions’ effectiveness, modeled interventions may have also indirectly reduced the PWID disability burden.

Third, we modeled HCV disease progression using four infectious compartments. The first included persons with chronic HCV and included fibrosis stages from F0 to F4, consistent with a previous study [21]. Recommendations for HCV treatment, costs associated with chronic HCV (F0–F4), and disability weights for these disease stages remain unchanged. As a result, representing the F0–F4 HCV disease states within a single compartment should not have affected our results and greatly simplified our modeling effort.

Fourth, this model assumes full treatment compliance. Although recent studies have shown that PWID can achieve high cure rates with HCV direct-acting antivirals despite suboptimal adherence, loss to follow-up or extended treatment interruptions can significantly reduce effectiveness [34, 80, 81]. Additionally, we assumed that individuals who cleared HCV infection—either spontaneously or after treatment—can be reinfected at the same rate as those never infected. While reinfection is common among PWID who continue injecting, potential changes in risky behavior after HCV treatment was not incorporated into the model [82, 83].

We also assumed that the rate of treatment initiation following diagnosis is uniform across diagnosed individuals and is not stratified by fibrosis stage. Evidence on treatment initiation patterns by fibrosis stage among persons with HCV remains limited, particularly among PWID. Nguyen et al. reported that insured individuals with compensated cirrhosis (F4) were more likely to initiate treatment than those without cirrhosis (F0–F3), whereas individuals with decompensated cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma were least likely to receive treatment [84]. However, these results were not stratified by PWID status and because PWID are more likely to be uninsured, these findings may not be generalizable to our modeled population, and therefore cannot be used to inform stage-specific treatment initiation probabilities in our modeled population [85, 86]. Accordingly, in the absence of such data, we applied a uniform treatment initiation rate across all disease stages in the model.

Lastly, the model’s accuracy is limited by data availability. Through our Kansas case study, we obtained accurate state-specific surveillance data from the Kansas Department of Health and Environment, however, detailed information on the PWID population size and behavioral risk within Kansas was unavailable, requiring us to rely on national estimates [48, 51, 56]. There is considerable uncertainty regarding the impact of harm reduction on HCV transmission. We based our estimates on a global Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis [54]; however, the generalizability of these findings to the United States remains unclear. Additionally, there is substantial uncertainty around the duration of participation in MAT or SSP programs, as well as the time until permanent cessation of injection drug use [40, 87, 88]. These behavioral factors vary widely across individuals and settings. Although our sensitivity analyses explored a range of values, real-world variability could substantially influence the long-term cost effectiveness of harm reduction strategies. Improved access to local data would enhance the precision and policy relevance of future modeling efforts.

This study is the first economic evaluation of HCV prevention strategies from the perspective of a rural state with no legally operating SSPs, supporting resource planning and providing locally relevant evidence to guide public health decision making.

Our model uniquely highlights low-barrier care delivery strategies to improve HCV prevention and treatment among PWID, a population frequently facing structural and geographic barriers to care. Specifically, the model incorporates point-of-care HCV RNA testing, simulating a same-day test-and-treat approach—an emerging best practice that facilitates rapid diagnosis and immediate treatment in a single visit, particularly among PWID populations with limited engagement in traditional healthcare systems [89]. This health equity model of HCV care meets PWID where they are and removes significant structural barriers to care. In addition, the study models MAT using buprenorphine [45], which can be prescribed in non-specialty and community-based settings such as primary care clinics, mobile units, and harm reduction programs [90]. This approach enhances the generalizability and feasibility of the modeled MAT interventions, particularly in rural and underserved areas with limited access to specialty care.

Finally, this study evaluated a comprehensive range of intervention combinations—including increased testing, treatment, MAT, and SSP—enabling extensive comparisons of cost, effectiveness, and time to achieve HCV elimination goals. These comparisons provide actionable insights into the trade-offs between efficiency, speed of impact, and implementation feasibility.

Future research should consider expanding modeling frameworks to incorporate comorbidities frequently associated with PWID and HCV infection including HIV, drug overdose, and bacterial infections such as endocarditis and wound infections [91]. Accounting for these conditions would provide a more comprehensive estimate of the health and economic impact of interventions, as they contribute substantially to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs in this population. Additionally, further work is needed to understand the recommended length of time on MAT and the factors influencing retention on treatment, as well as the length of time PWID utilize SSPs. Incorporating these behavioral and programmatic dynamics into future models could improve the accuracy of long-term projections and intervention impact estimates.

Conclusions

Our model suggests that expanding HCV testing and treatment among PWID in Kansas could be a highly cost-effective and equity-promoting strategy that has the potential to achieve the WHO HCV incidence goal within a decade, even without harm reduction services. Combining this approach with moderate coverage of MAT or SSP may further improve health outcomes and accelerate reductions in HCV incidence. Notably, interventions incorporating SSPs demonstrate favorable cost effectiveness, indicating their potential value as components of a comprehensive HCV response strategy. These findings highlight that PWID in rural and underserved communities stand to benefit the most from the introduction of low-barrier interventions such as SSPs, MAT, and point-of-care testing. By reducing structural and geographic barriers, these strategies not only improve efficiency but also reduce disparities in HCV outcomes. Together, our results provide actionable evidence to support local planning efforts, optimize resource allocation, and support policy development aimed at expanding equitable access to HCV prevention, testing, and treatment services for PWID in rural and underserved settings.
