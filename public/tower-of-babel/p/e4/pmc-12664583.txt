Healthcare costs and resource utilization for acute respiratory syncytial virus pediatric hospitalizations in Canada: a population-based study

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major cause of bronchiolitis and pneumonia in pediatric populations, especially in infancy. This study aims to assess overall and age-specific incidence of RSV-associated hospitalization and healthcare resource use throughout childhood in Canada.MethodsData were retrieved from a national administrative dataset, which captured hospitalizations with International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, tenth revision from participating Canadian hospitals (Canadian Institute for Health Information, CIHI), and from an active surveillance program in tertiary care pediatric hospitals (the Canadian Immunization Program ACTive, IMPACT). Children aged 0–16 years with RSV in November 2017 through April 2023 were eligible. We estimated overall and age-specific RSV hospitalization incidence, healthcare resource use (length of stay, mechanical ventilation use), and costs by age group (0–5, 6–11, and 12–23 months, and 2–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–16 years). Costs were adjusted to 2022 Canadian dollars (CAD). The population denominator for age-specific RSV incidence estimates was retrieved from Statistics Canada.FindingsAn estimated 29,277 RSV hospitalizations occurred, with an average of 5831 cases/year in pre-pandemic years (November 2017–June 2020). Infants aged <6 months old accounted for 13,055 (44.6%) of cases for study duration (November 2017–April 2023). RSV incidence among infants aged <6 months increased from 1250 per 100,000 in November 2017–June 2018 to 2393 per 100,000 in July 2022–April 2023. The average annual cost of RSV was $66,267,950 CAD. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed with the search terms “(RSV) AND (incidence OR economic) AND Canada AND pediatrics” for articles published in any language between Jan 1, 2022, and March 28, 2025, that estimated the incidence and economic burden of Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) in Canadian pediatric population. After excluding irrelevant studies, clinical trials of efficacy, effectiveness of interventions and modelling studies that did not estimate incidence and economic burden, we identified seven relevant studies looking at incidence and economic burden of RSV in Canadian pediatric populations. Most studies (6/7) focused on the incidence or economic burden in a single province or focused on first year of life (i.e, Ontario, Alberta, British Columbia, Quebec), whereas one pan-Canadian study focused on capturing health burden from pediatric tertiary care hospitals across 0–16 years of age.

Added value of this study

The Canadian Immunization Monitoring Program, ACTive (IMPACT), conducts active surveillance of RSV-related hospitalizations, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, mechanical ventilation use, and in-hospital deaths in children through a network of pediatric tertiary care centers across Canada. This hospital-based surveillance system provides a robust, in-depth dataset with rigorous quality control processes, which serves as an integral component of Canada's disease reporting and surveillance infrastructure. In parallel, we analyzed data from the Canadian Institute of Health Information (CIHI) to determine the national incidence of RSV hospitalizations. Utilizing CIHI and IMPACT datasets, we assessed the healthcare resources associated with RSV-related hospitalizations in children.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings demonstrate that half of the annual average healthcare costs associated with RSV hospitalizations occur in infants under six months of age. Therefore, public health programs should prioritize all infants aged <6 months for RSV prevention.

Introduction

The global respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) disease burden in 2019 was estimated to be 33 million RSV infection episodes, four million hospitalizations, and 30,000 in-patient deaths among children under five years of age.1 RSV season typically begins in the fall and peaks in the winter.2 RSV affects 90% of all children by two years of age.2 The most serious RSV illness occurs in prematurely born children or those with other risk factors (e.g., congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, immunodeficiency). However, over 80% of children under two years of age hospitalized with RSV have no known risk factors.3 Children ill enough to be hospitalized may also need respiratory support in an intensive care unit. Targeted high-risk programs to prevent RSV hospitalizations with the short-acting monoclonal antibody palivizumab have been in place since 2003 in Canada; unfortunately, these have not been shown to alter the substantial burden on healthcare systems in Canada and globally.1,4,5

The approval in 2023 by the European Medicines Agency, the United States Food and Drug Administration, and Health Canada of both a long-acting monoclonal antibody (nirsevimab) and a maternal RSV prefusion vaccine (RSVpreF) to protect infants against RSV6, 7, 8, 9, 10 has changed the RSV prevention landscape. Several immunization technical advisory groups globally recommend universal infant RSV prevention programs, either using long-acting monoclonal antibodies or maternal RSV vaccines.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Previous studies have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of nirsevimab and maternal RSVpreF vaccine in the first year of life in Canada, but did not capture the disease and economic burden for all children in their analyses.16,17 Consequently, to aid policy decision-making, we assessed pediatric RSV hospitalizations among individuals aged 0–16 years in Canada and provide an in-depth evaluation of the associated healthcare resource use in this analysis.

Methods

We used data both from the Canadian Institute of Health Information (CIHI), to determine the incidence of RSV hospitalizations in Canada, and from an active RSV hospitalization surveillance program, the Canadian Immunization Monitoring Program, ACTive (IMPACT), to quantify resource use associated with all RSV-associated hospitalizations. Statistics Canada population estimate data by age, province and territory for the years 2017–2022 were used as the denominator for incidence rates.18

Data sources

CIHI dataset

De-identified hospitalization records of children aged 0–16 years during RSV seasons from November 1, 2017 to April 1, 2023 for approximately 5 million children were extracted by CIHI.18 All provinces and territories, except the province of Quebec, submit these records to CIHI. The CIHI discharge abstract database was used to identify RSV hospitalizations from November 1, 2017 to April 1, 2023, the latest date for which data were available at the time of our analysis. RSV-associated hospitalization in pediatric population was identified using a previously validated RSV case-finding algorithm, which included had a primary or a secondary diagnosis of RSV infection using the following International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, tenth revision: RSV as a cause of disease (B97.4); acute bronchitis due to RSV (J20.5); acute bronchiolitis due to RSV (J21.0); and RSV pneumonia (J12.1).3 Re-admissions or hospital transfers within 30 days were considered part of the same incident hospitalization case or episode.19 The CIHI discharge abstract database captured demographic variables, admission and discharge dates, re-admission, diagnostic codes, urban/rural residency, discharge disposition, and hospitalization costs. Data from the northern territories of Canada (Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Nunavut) were pooled to maintain patient confidentiality because of the small number of observed hospitalizations. Patients with missing unique patient identifier number were excluded.

The CIHI dataset included costs per patient care episode based on the cost of a hospital stay for the illness and the specific services the patient received. The cost of a hospital stay includes labor costs for nursing and allied health professionals, pharmacy (drugs) costs, supplies, medical imaging, laboratory expenses, and indirect (overhead) costs, but excludes salary costs for physicians and transportation expenses. As such, CIHI methodology averages costs across provinces, accounts for a wide range of hospitalization types, and resources used thus providing standardized estimates of costs based on national data by age group and level of care provided for the pediatric population.20 While the IMPACT and CIHI datasets were not merged at the patient level, the proportion of ICU admissions was determined from IMPACT and applied to the CIHI dataset for tertiary care cases.21

Active surveillance of RSV in pediatric hospitals (IMPACT dataset)

The IMPACT network, which during the study period included 13 academic and acute-care regional hospitals across Canada and accounted for more than 90% of the pediatric tertiary care beds in Canada, conducted an active surveillance program for RSV hospitalizations, as previously reported.5,22 IMPACT centers collected all cases aged 0–16 years admitted with laboratory-confirmed RSV, including hospital-acquired cases, using previously validated case-identification for the following RSV seasons: November 2017–June 2018, November 2018–June 2019, October 2019–June 2020, July 2020–June 2021, and July 2021–June 2022.5,23 Individual-level case data were extracted from the hospital chart using a standardized electronic case report form.5 The data collected included admission and discharge dates, age, sex, ICU admission status and outcome at discharge. Every third to fifth case was selected each RSV season for more detailed data collection, including referral hospital stay, ICU length of stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation. Cases residing in areas with fewer than 400 people per square kilometer were categorized as having a rural residence,24,25 determined using the second digit of the case's postal code.24,25

Statistical analyses

This study reports descriptive statistics including proportions, means, standard deviation (SD), medians, and interquartile ranges as applicable. The crude incidence of RSV-associated hospitalized pediatric cases are reported per 100,000 children by epidemiological year (2017–2018, 2018–2019, 2019–2020, 2020–2021, 2021–2022, 2023) and age group. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) for crude incidence rates were calculated using the exact method for the Poisson distribution.26 We conducted stratified analyses to study health resource utilization and associated costs by age group (0–5 months, 6–11 months, 12–23 months, 2–4 years, 5–9 years, and 10–16 years old). We conducted statistical analyses using SAS v9.4 and R v 4.1.3.

The cost of RSV associated hospitalization was computed using the cost of hospitalization per day multiplied by the duration of stay in the general pediatric ward and incorporating the cost of ICU support per day.27, 28, 29 Details on the resource use, such as a pediatric general ward, ICU, and mechanical ventilation, and associated costs can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Annual and age-group specific hospitalization costs were compiled by using the mean costs for general pediatric wards at non-tertiary and tertiary care centers from the ICD10-coded CIHI dataset. Mean costs for ICU stay and mechanical ventilation at tertiary care centers were compiled from IMPACT. Costs were adjusted to 2022 Canadian dollars, using the healthcare component of the consumer price index.30

Ethical considerations

All participating IMPACT centers obtained Research Ethics Board (REB) or hospital approval to collect data with a waiver of consent. Analysis of the CIHI data received REB approval from the University of British Columbia (UBC). We did not obtain individual patient consent for use of the CIHI data because this is a secondary analysis of administrative databases. The study team adhered to the CIHI and IMPACT data privacy and confidentiality rules which required cell sizes containing fewer than five individuals and subsequent proportions to be masked.

Role of funder/sponsor

Public Health Agency of Canada provided input into the IMPACT RSV surveillance activity. PHAC, Sanofi and AstraZeneca did not provide any input into the economic burden study design, analysis or interpretation of results.

The overall length of hospital stay averaged 3.5 days (SD: 2.7 days) in general pediatric wards; 6.5 days (SD: 4.5 days) for those requiring ICU support without mechanical ventilation, and 20.8 days (SD: 36.9 days) among children requiring mechanical ventilation. Healthcare burden

The estimated annual average cost of RSV associated pediatric hospitalizations in Canada was $66,267,950 CAD. Infants under six months of age accounted for almost half of the annual average healthcare costs (49.0%, $32,471,296 CAD) associated with RSV infections in Canada, representing the largest share compared with other age groups (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

In this study of RSV associated hospitalizations in Canadian children from infancy to adolescence over six years, we confirmed the substantial burden of hospitalization (and associated costs) in infancy but also found that there is considerable morbidity in older children. These cost estimates enable a detailed understanding of the burden of RSV hospitalizations in children in Canada and the costs to the healthcare system generally. RSV disproportionately affects infants under 12 months of age, with the majority of cases occurring in infants aged under 6 months. Although an outlier in our six-year study, in the 2022–2023 season, two per 100 infants under six months of age were hospitalized with RSV in Canada. RSV consistently causes higher rates of hospitalization compared with influenza in children under 5 years of age.31 During the study period, infants aged <6 months accounted for almost half of the annual average RSV associated hospitalization costs. These findings underscore the need for prevention strategies directed to reduce illness both in the first year of life and in early childhood.

Depending on the case definition and study population, previous studies have reported a wide range of clinical severity, health resource utilization, and healthcare expenditure costs for RSV.23,29,32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37 Some Canadian studies estimate 1100–3300 annual hospitalizations across pediatric age groups 0–16 years,5,19,34 with just under one-quarter of the hospitalized patients requiring ICU support.5 Our study's finding aligns with the existing literature while providing additional insights, particularly for young infants. In terms of ICU support, 2756 (9.4%) of cases were admitted to the ICU, which is consistent with previous studies, indicating that 8–24% of hospitalized patients require ICU support.23,35,37 Furthermore, our study offers a detailed age-specific analysis, revealing that among infants aged <6 months admitted to hospitals, 1768 (13.5%) required ICU support. Regarding mechanical ventilation, it has been reported that 4%–11% of pediatric populations at tertiary care centers require mechanical ventilation support,23,38 similar to our findings of 578 (4.4%) in children under 6 months of age.

Our study adds novel insights to the existing literature on the economic burden from RSV in Canada. While previous studies have demonstrated cost variations across hospital types, care levels, and reporting periods (month or year),19,29,32,33,35 our study presents annual, average healthcare system direct costs associated with RSV hospitalizations in Canada along with an age-group analysis. Rafferty et al. estimated the 30-day cost for acute lower respiratory tract illness among infants to be approximately $14,000 CAD from the healthcare system perspective.19 Mitchell et al. estimated parents incur an average of $700 CAD in out-of-pocket costs for each hospitalization episode.39 We report $66 million CAD in annual, average healthcare costs associated with RSV hospitalizations in pediatric populations in Canada, with over half of these costs occurring in children under six months of age. As such, hospitalization costs constitute a major portion of healthcare expenditure related to respiratory tract infections in Canada. The health and economic burden of RSV in children from hospitalization may account for most of the health system costs, but it is an underestimate as it does not include emergency room or clinic visits, physician reimbursement, and parental absenteeism.39,40 The new preventive tools are likely to reduce the disease burden, and direct and indirect costs associated with RSV in Canada.

We present several hypotheses to explain the observed increase in RSV-associated hospitalizations in our study. The implementation of public health control measures during the COVID-19 pandemic may have resulted in a decrease in the circulation of RSV and a subsequent decrease in immunity and an increase in the naïve population in children.34 This may have resulted in a larger pool of susceptible individuals available for infection when public health control measures were lifted and may partially explain the surge in cases in 2022. In addition, the widespread implementation of combined COVID-19, influenza, and RSV testing panels introduced to meet the increased demand for COVID-19 testing may have also contributed to the increase in incidence.34,41 Importantly, the increase in RSV hospitalizations across all age groups may not be attributable solely to a pandemic-related rise in healthcare-seeking behavior or testing for respiratory symptoms.42 A US study of 4373 children hospitalization for COVID-19 during March 2020–February 2022 found 21% had viral co-infections including RSV and rhinovirus.43 Children under 2 years of age with RSV and COVID-19 co-detections had 2-fold higher risk of severe illness (aOR1.9 [95% CI 1.3–2.9]).43 Additional studies are needed to confirm whether COVID-19 and RSV co-infection increases the risk of severe illness.

Our data should be considered in light of some limitations. Foremost, the IMPACT and CIHI data cover different study periods. We may have missed cases. In the IMPACT dataset data collection was not year-round except for the last 2 years (2020–2022). For 2017 and 2018 data were only collected for 8 months and for 2019 for 9 months. In the CIHI dataset data were only abstracted through April 2023. Nevertheless, we would not expect these missing months to greatly affect our results as outside of the winter respiratory season, these months tend to have a relatively low number of RSV infections.5 The years July 2020–June 2021 and July 2022–April 2023 represent the lowest and the highest rates, as they were influenced by the lack of exposure during the first year of COVID-19 pandemic and the rebound increase was noted after previously unexposed children returned to out-of-home activities. As such we excluded July 2020–June 2021 and July 2022–April 2023 from RSV annualized case burden. However, for average annual RSV economic burden we excluded only July 2020–June 2021. With the absence of data from the province of Quebec in the CIHI dataset, we are underestimating the overall disease and economic burden of RSV in Canada. Furthermore, we did not differentiate community acquired versus hospital acquired RSV cases in the CIHI dataset. Out of hospital costs and physician reimbursement also are not included, which would lead to underestimation of the cost. Furthermore, while we have incorporated transportation costs for individuals with a referral hospital stay in the IMPACT dataset, we could not capture the transportation costs for patients transported directly to the tertiary care facility. The cost estimates may be skewed to those requiring hospital transfers and re-admission to intensive care units within 30-days. Future studies should consider reporting on hospital re-admission as an important endpoint, especially in studies evaluating the impact of preventive tools. Moreover, there is notable heterogeneity in disease burden and healthcare costs across provinces and territories,5,44 as such our findings may not be fully generalizable to each jurisdiction. While, we were unable to explore impact of ethnicity, sex/gender and birth outcomes (i.e. small for gestational age, pre-term birth) on the costs in this study, these outcomes have been explored by others.29

In spite of these limitations, our findings underscore the urgent need to implement RSV prevention measures in infants under six months of age, who experience the highest rates of RSV-related hospitalizations and place a significant strain on an already overburdened healthcare system. Prevention strategies such as monoclonal antibody and maternal and infant vaccines have the potential to significantly reduce hospitalizations and healthcare costs, and improve outcomes for the most vulnerable. This study's detailed analysis offers valuable insights for shaping effective RSV prevention strategies at the policy level by providing comprehensive data on healthcare resource use and hospitalization costs among pediatric populations in Canada, particularly those treated in tertiary care centers. The findings also can be used to develop cost-effectiveness models for RSV prevention programs.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that half of the annual average healthcare costs associated with RSV hospitalizations occur in infants under six months of age. Therefore, public health programs should prioritize all infants aged <6 months for RSV prevention. This study highlights the urgency of preventive programs, such as long-acting monoclonal antibodies or prenatal vaccination for infants against RSV, to reduce severe RSV cases and alleviate the burden and cost on the healthcare system.
