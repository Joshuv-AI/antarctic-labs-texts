Efficient Extracellular Production of Insulin Glargine in Trichoderma reesei by an Integrative Secretion‐Based Strategy
Authors: Aiqin Yan, Xihai Li, Di Zhang, Lunbo Li, Jinfeng Liu, Xuejian Si, Kewen Li, Qingmin Luan, Hong Liu, Yaohua Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInsulin glargine, a long‐acting insulin analog, is essential for diabetes treatment. However, its industrial production remains challenging due to limitations in conventional expression systems. Here, we employed the industrial filamentous fungus Trichoderma reesei as an alternative expression host for insulin glargine production, leveraging its superior protein secretion capacity. Initially, expression constructs containing the constitutive Pcdna1 promoter and CBH1 signal peptide (SP1) showed successful transcription but failed to achieve extracellular secretion, presumably due to induced endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Consequently, we implemented a fusion protein strategy utilising three distinct carrier proteins (CBH1, CBH2, and LA‐20) to enhance glargine secretion. Notably, the CBH1 fusion not only enabled detectable glargine secretion but also significantly alleviated the ER stress. Furthermore, replacement of SP1 with the Aspergillus niger β‐glucoamylase signal peptide achieved a fourfold enhancement in glargine secretion and further reduced cellular stress responses. Following these systematic optimizations, a final yield of 58.95 mIU/L glargine was achieved in shake‐flask cultures. Thus, the combined strategy described here could achieve extracellular production of glargine in T. reesei, suggesting that it is a promising host for secretory production of therapeutic recombinant proteins, particularly complex analogs like glargine.

Introduction

Since insulin's first clinical application in 1922, it has been prescribed as a glucose‐lowering agent for people with various forms of diabetes mellitus (Mathieu et al. 2021). As the incidence of diabetes is increasing rapidly, there is a mounting pharmaceutical requirement for insulin. However, due to the limited ability to adjust the normal glycaemic control with insulin, there is a clear demand for new insulin formulations that have a faster onset of action as well as a longer half‐life (Mathieu et al. 2017). Insulin glargine (also referred to as glargine) is one of the long‐acting insulin analogues with reduced hypoglycemic risks and enables patients with diabetes to properly manage their blood glucose levels with a low frequency of injections (McKeage and Goa 2001; Gu et al. 2002). Compared with traditional insulin, glargine offers distinct advantages in clinical treatment. Structurally, glargine closely resembles human insulin but incorporates two key modifications: the substitution of asparagine for glycine at position A21 of the A‐chain, and the addition of two arginine residues (RR) to the C‐terminus of the B‐chain. These changes shift the isoelectric point of insulin from 5.4 to 6.7, which significantly enhances its chemical stability relative to conventional insulin (Hwang et al. 2016; Baeshen et al. 2014).

Currently, industrial production of insulin primarily relies on Escherichia coli and mammalian cells, both of which present significant limitations (Zimmerman et al. 2012). The E. coli system involves complex and potentially denaturing steps, such as inclusion body refolding and multi‐step enzymatic cleavage, which can compromise protein integrity (Clark 1998; Marston 1986; Sørensen and Mortensen 2005). The mammalian cell system, meanwhile, is limited by its high cultivation costs and lengthy fermentation cycle (Brady and Love 2021). These challenges highlight the urgent need to develop eukaryotic expression systems with high secretion efficiency and high‐precision processing capacity. Consequently, there is a growing interest in exploring and engineering non‐conventional microbial hosts that offer robust growth, metabolic versatility, and superior capabilities for industrial bioproduction (Bozkurt et al. 2024; Timmis et al. 2025; Calero and Nikel 2019; Volke et al. 2020). The filamentous fungus Trichoderma reesei, generally recognized as safe (GRAS), exhibits exceptional protein secretion capacity (yields exceeding 100 g/L) and well‐established industrial fermentation characteristics (Rantasalo et al. 2019; Sewalt et al. 2016). Its eukaryotic post‐translational modification system enables the synthesis of mammalian‐like glycosylation precursors (GlcNAc2Man5) and has been successfully used to produce various complex therapeutic proteins (Salovuori et al. 1987; Stals et al. 2004). Combined with its inherent ability to utilise low‐cost lignocellulosic hydrolysates, T. reesei possesses a more advanced post‐translational modification system than yeast and demonstrates multiple unique advantages for recombinant protein production (Table 1) (Wang et al. 2022). This capability positions it as a promising microbial host for the cost‐effective production of high‐value compounds, potentially offering significant economic advantages over traditional expression hosts that rely on refined sugars (Wang et al. 2022).

This study aims to establish a secretory production platform for glargine in T. reesei. We rationally designed a fusion protein, screened optimal signal peptides, and incorporated a Kex2 protease (Kex2p) cleavage site to achieve precise C‐terminal processing of the B‐chain RR residues, thereby circumventing the impurity issues associated with conventional trypsin digestion. Concurrently, the unfolded protein response in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) was analysed to elucidate the host's adaptive mechanisms to heterologous protein expression. By optimising the secretory pathway and alleviating the ER stress, this study not only significantly enhanced the yield and quality of glargine but also provided powerful strategies for developing T. reesei as an efficient and reliable production host for therapeutic proteins.

Materials and Methods

Strains, Medium, and Culture Conditions

T. reesei strain QP4, a pyrG‐negative mutant strain derived from T. reesei strain QM9414, was used as both the parental and control strain in this study. The conidia were harvested from the potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates containing peeled potato extract 200 g/L, glucose (2%, w/v), agar (2%, w/v, Dingguo Corp., Beijing, China; GA010‐500 g) after the fungal strains were cultivated for 7 days at 30°C. For target protein production, two cultivation methods were employed: shake‐flask culture and bioreactor fermentation. For shake‐flask culture, conidia were inoculated into 50 mL of liquid minimal medium (MM) containing 1% (w/v) glucose at a final concentration of 106 conidia/mL. The culture was incubated at 30°C and 200 rpm for 36 h to prepare the seed culture. After that, 10 mL of the inoculum was transferred into 100 mL of fermentation medium (FM) and cultivated in shake flasks for 7 days at 30°C. The FM was composed as follows: (g/L): 20.0 glucose, 1.0 CaCl2, 0.6 MgSO4·7H2O, 5.0 KH2PO4, 5.0 (NH4)2SO4, 20.0 corn plasm and 100 μL trace element solution (0.005 mg/L FeSO4·7H2O, 0.0014 mg/L ZnSO4·2H2O, 0.002 mg/CoCl2·6H2O, 0.0017 mg/L MnSO4·H2O).

Bioreactor cultivations were performed in a 1.4 L parallel bioreactor system (Multifors 2, Switzerland) with a working volume of 800 mL. The fermentation was conducted under the following controlled conditions: temperature, 30°C; agitation speed, 400 rpm; aeration rate, 1.0 vvm (volume of air per volume of medium per minute). The pH was initially set to 4.0 and automatically maintained at this setpoint by the addition of ammonia water (25%, w/v) or sulfuric acid (2 M) as needed. To sustain cell growth and protein production, a fed‐batch strategy was implemented whereby a concentrated glucose solution (600 g/L) was added whenever the residual glucose concentration decreased to approximately 5 g/L. The strains used in this study were listed in Table S1.

Construction of Glargine Expression Cassettes and Transformation of T. reesei

The fusion of fragments used the Double‐joint PCR technique in this study, as described previously (Yu et al. 2004). Phanta Super‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd., Nanjing, China) was used for PCR amplification. Primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

The fragment of Pcdna1 with SP1 of 0.94‐kb was amplified using primer pair Pcdna1‐F/Pcdna1 (SP1)‐R with the QP4 genome as the template. Glargine (with or without mini C‐peptide) nucleic acid sequences were codon optimised for T. reesei expression (Table S3). The synthetic glargine coding genes were obtained from BGI, Beijing, China, and the fragments were amplified with the primer pair glargine‐F/glargine‐R. The 0.8‐kb TtrpC was amplified from plasmid pAN7‐1 (GenBank accession number Z32698.1) with primer pair TtrpC‐1F/TtrpC‐762R. Subsequently, the three DNA fragments were isolated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using Gel Extraction Kit (Omega, USA), and then fused together as the template to amplify glargine expression cassettes with the primer pair Pcdna1‐1F/TtrpC‐762R.

The genes encoding two intrinsic carrier proteins were amplified from the genomic DNA of T. reesei strain QP4 using the primer pairs CBH1 carrier‐UF (SP1)/CBH1 carrier‐DR (KR), CBH2 carrier‐UF (SP1)/CBH2 carrier‐DR (KR), respectively. The synthetic leader LA‐20 fragment was obtained by chemical synthesis and the 0.12‐kb fragment was amplified with the primer pair LA20‐F/LA20‐R (KR). Then, fragments of Pcdna1 (with SP1), three carrier proteins, glargine, and TtrpC were fused to obtain three expression cassettes that drive glargine expression.

The Signal peptides, AnG, AnA, and SP2 were respectively fused to the N‐terminus of the CBH1 carrier and PCR amplified using oligonucleotide Primers. Consequently, the fragments of the CBH1 carrier with corresponding signal peptide was obtained. Then, the fragments of Pcdna1, CBH1 carrier with different signal peptides, glargine and TtrpC fused into three glargine expression cassettes.

The pyrG fragment was amplified by plasmid PAB4‐1 as a template, and was co‐transferred into T. reesei strain QP4 protoplasts together with the above glargine expression cassettes using the method described previously, respectively (Penttilä et al. 1987). After that, the transformants were screened on MM agar plates without uracil and further verified by PCR. The plasmids used in this study were listed in Table S4.

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR Analysis

Equal amounts of mycelia were inoculated into 100 mL fermentation medium with 2% (w/v) glucose as the sole carbon source and cultivated at 30°C and 200 rpm in flasks. To ensure transcriptional analysis was conducted at a consistent physiological state corresponding to the late exponential growth phase, mycelial samples for each strain were harvested specifically at 72 h post‐inoculation for subsequent RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer's protocol of TRIzol Reagen (Invitrogen, USA) and then reversely transcribed into the complementary DNA (cDNA) using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Subsequently, the LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) was used to calculate Ct value, and the data were analysed using the relative quantitation/comparative Ct (ΔCt) method with actin gene for normalisation (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). The primers used in this study were listed in Table S2, and the growth curve (dry cell weight vs. time) for each strain under this condition was provided in Figure S1, with the specific sampling time point (72 h) clearly indicated.

Trichloroacetic Acid (TCA) Precipitation

The equal volume of fermentation supernatants was collected after centrifugation. Then, 100 μL of 100% TCA was added to 1 mL supernatants, and the sample was vortexed and allowed to precipitate on ice for 30 min. Aspirating the supernatants and carefully retaining the precipitate, after centrifuging the samples at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The precipitate was washed several times in 100 μL cold acetone to remove residual TCA, followed by centrifugation as above. The protein precipitate was air‐dried for a short duration and resuspended in a moderate amount of distilled water.

Tricin‐SDS‐PAGE

Culture supernatants from transformants were separated by Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE to evaluate efficiency of glargine secretion. Prior to Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE, the protein samples concentrated by TCA were mixed with an appropriate volume of 4× SDS sample buffer and boiled for 10 min at 100°C for protein denaturation. Proteins were separated by 16.5% under reducing conditions according to the manufacturer's protocol of Tris‐Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE Gel Kit (Solarbio) and then visualised using Coomassie Blue G‐250 (Sangon) staining.

Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)

To remove the mycelia, fermentation supernatants were passed through a 0.22 μm PES membrane (Millipore). After that, the fermentation supernatants were subjected to LC–MS/MS using a Linear Ion Trap (LTQ)‐orbitrap Hybrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Electron, San Jose, CA). Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry was used for the identification of target proteins in fermentation according to the method previously described with some modifications (Ribeiro et al. 2012).

Proteins in supernatant were precipitated by TCA and cold acetone, and then the suspended was dissolved in ultrapure water. Next, 50 μL denaturation buffer [0.5 M Tris–HCl, 2.75 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 6 M guanidine‐HCl, pH 8.1] was added to 100 μg of proteins (measured by the Bradford method). Subsequently, 50 μL of 1 M dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to the protein samples at 37°C for 2 h as a reducing agent. The proteins were then alkylated with 50 μL 1 M iodoacetamide (IAA) in dark conditions for 1 h and were filtered and collected by Microcon YM‐100 centrifugal filter (Millipore, USA). After centrifugation, the samples were digested by trypsin (Sigma, USA) at 37°C overnight. Protein samples were desalted using a ZipTip C18 column (Millipore Corporation, United States) and dissolved in 10 μL Elution buffer [50% (v/v) acetonitrile (ACN) and 0.1% (v/v)] and then were subjected to nanoelectrospray ionisation, followed by analysis in LTQ Orbitrap Velos Pro (Termo Scientifc, USA) coupled with HPLC system.

Detection of Glargine Concentration via ELISA Kit

To determine glargine concentration in fermentation supernatants, we employed an ultra sensitive Human insulin glargine ELISA kit (Ruixin, China). The assay was performed according to the instructions by the fabricant, and the absorbance of the enzymatic reaction was measured at 450 nm in an ELISA reader. All determinations were performed in triplicate.

Results

Glargine Production by T. reesei With the Strong Promoter Pcdna1

Here, T. reesei was selected as the host for heterologous expression of glargine. This study explored two forms of glargine expression in T. reesei: (i) glargine was expressed as a secreted single‐chain consisting of A‐ and B‐chains with no connecting peptide. (ii) A mini C‐peptide (AAK) was inserted between the A‐ and B‐chains of glargine. The genes of two forms of glargines (with and without mini C‐peptide) were codon optimised for expression in T. reesei (Table S3). To minimise co‐production of undesired endogenous proteins, the strong constitutive promoter Pcdna1 was implemented, which facilitates sustained high level glargine expression across diverse cultivation conditions without requiring exogenous induction (Wang et al. 2022). Concurrently, to enable glargine secretion, the endogenous CBH1 signal peptide (SP1) was utilised to facilitate efficient secretory pathway engagement. The promoter of Pcdna1, SP1, glargine‐encoding gene (with or without mini C‐peptide) and the terminator TtrpC were fused to construct the two different glargine expression cassettes (Figure 1b). Moreover, the pyrG cassette was obtained from the plasmid pAB4‐1 and used as the selectable marker for transformation of T. reesei. Then glargine cassettes were co‐transformed with the pyrG cassette into the T. reesei strain QP4, respectively. In the preliminary screening, transformants were selected based on their ability to grow in the minimal medium (MM) plates lacking uracil. The colonies which survived on MM plates were confirmed by PCR for the presence of glargine cassettes integrated into the genome (Figure 1c).

Subsequently, the transformants were cultured in the fermentation medium with glucose as the sole carbon source. The transcription level of glargine was detected using reverse transcription quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR). For each cassette, the transformant with the highest transcription level of glargine was selected and respectively named as S1r (not containing the mini C‐peptide) and S1HCr (containing the mini C‐peptide). It was found that the glargine gene could be successfully transcribed in both strains, and the S1r strain exhibited a higher transcription level (Figure 1d). Then, the transformants selected were screened for glargine productivity in shake flasks with the untransformed T. reesei strain QP4 as a negative control. The fermentation supernatants were subjected to Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE. However, no target bands of glargine double‐strands were detected in Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE, indicating that it was not feasible to directly express glargine without the addition of carrier proteins in T. reesei.

The ER Stress Evoked in T. reesei by Glargine Expressing Strains

In T. reesei, the newly synthesised proteins enter the ER secretory pathway to gain activity and traffic to their final destinations (Sun and Brodsky 2019). However, aberrant folding properties of the target proteins or the high level production of proteins can lead to accumulation of misfolded or even aggregated proteins in the ER, which can cause the ER stress and trigger the unfolded protein response (UPR) and the ER‐associated degradation (ERAD) pathway, resulting in the failure of the desirable proteins to be efficiently secreted (Yao et al. 2023; Kautto et al. 2013). To determine whether the ER stress was evoked during glargine production, the transcription levels of genes encoding key components involved in the UPR (pdi1 (Trire2: 122415) and bip1 (Trire2: 122920)) and the ERAD pathway (hrd1 (Trire2: 50647) and der1 (Trire2: 119903)) were determined in S1r and S1CHr (Figure 2). The functional annotation of these genes in T. reesei was determined based on sequence alignment (using the BLASTP tool) and conserved domain analysis (via the InterProScan database) against confirmed homologues in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and other filamentous fungi. It was found that the transcription levels of bip1 and pdi1 were significantly upregulated in S1r, while the same phenomenon was not detected in the S1CHr strain (Figure 2a). These results indicated that the expression of glargine aroused significant UPR in S1r strain to facilitate the folding of heterologous protein, whereas in the S1CHr strain, the UPR was not induced. Moreover, in both recombinant strains, the transcription levels of hrd1 and der1 were increased, suggesting that the ERAD pathway was markedly activated (Figure 2b). These transcriptional results suggest that the heterologous expression of glargine likely elicited the ER stress, thereby activating the ERAD pathway for precursor degradation. This further implies that glargine may have been misfolded or otherwise impaired in its folding within the ER of T. reesei.

Application Carrier Protein Fusion Strategy to Promote Glargine Secretion

As described above, expressing glargine directly in T. reesei could cause intense ER stress, indicating that T. reesei is unable to provide an optimal environment for its proper folding. In T. reesei protein production systems, carrier protein fusions enhance the efficient secretion of target proteins by attaching them to a secreted fungal protein (Nyyssönen et al. 1993). Two native secretory enzymes exhibiting high expression under inductive conditions, together with the synthetic pre‐pro‐leader (LA‐20), were selected as glargine carrier proteins. These included the CBH1 catalytic domain with its linker region (CBH1 carrier) and the CBH2 cellulose‐binding domain containing its linker (CBH2 carrier). These carrier proteins have been shown to promote the secretion of heterologous proteins in T. reesei (Paloheimo et al. 2003). The synthetic leader (LA‐20) was proved to be more effective in facilitating the secretion of the insulin precursor from Saccharomyces cerevisiae compared to the α‐factor leader, resulting in a higher yield of correctly folded insulin precursor in the culture supernatant (Kjeldsen et al. 1997).

To guide the fusion protein through the T. reesei secretory pathway and achieve efficient cleavage, glargine was linked to the carrier protein in the format “carrier protein‐KR‐glargine” with the KR motif (NVISKR) serving as a potential Kex2p processing site (Figure 2a). The constructed expression cassettes were co‐transferred with pyrG cassette into T. reesei strain QP4, and the selection of transformants was performed as previously described. The strains carrying the CBH1 carrier, CBH2 carrier, and LA‐20 were named S1C1r, S1C2r, and S1CLr, respectively. The transcription levels of glargine and carrier proteins in each strain were quantified using RT‐qPCR (Figure 3b,c). These results showed that both of them were constitutively transcribed in the recombinant strains.

Following that, Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE was used to analyse the fermentation cultures of S1C1r, S1C2r, and S1CLr strains. A polypeptide of approximately 6.0 kDa, corresponding to the predicted molecular weight of glargine (6.06 kDa), was detected in the culture supernatants of S1C1r. No bands of the expected size appeared in the culture supernatants of the S1C2r, S1CLr, and QP4 strains. This suggests that it was the proinsulin glargine including B‐ and A‐chains. To verify the result, Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE was further performed on the culture supernatants of the S1C1r strain fermented for different days. As shown in Figure 4a, the target band was visible from days 2 to 5 of fermentation (indicated with dashed triangle). Moreover, LC–MS/MS identification further confirmed that the band of 6.0 kDa in size was glargine (Table S5). Meanwhile, a clear band of about 49.0 kDa, presumed to be the CBH1 carrier, was also detected. Additionally, the concentration of glargine in the fermentation cultures on day 3 of S1C1r was measured using the Human G‐INS ELISA Kit, revealing a concentration of approximately 14.43 mIU/L (Figure 4b). These results indicated that the use of the CBH1 carrier and Kex2 linker along with the SP1 could promote the secretory production of glargine.

The ER Stress Response in T. reesei by Glargine Expressing Strains

To investigate the impact of selected carrier proteins on the ER stress activation and their role in glargine secretion, transcription levels of UPR‐ and ERAD‐related genes were quantified using RT‐qPCR. It was found that the strain S1C1r displayed an increase in transcription levels of the bip1 and pdi1, whereas the transcription levels of the ERAD‐related genes (der1 and hrd1) were not elevated (Figure 5a,b). These results indicated that the secretory expression of glargine fused with CBH1 carrier in S1C1r triggered the UPR but did not activate the ERAD pathway for protein degradation, allowing for glargine secretion without intracellular degradation. Additionally, transcription levels of UPR‐related genes were significantly higher in the S1C2r and S1CLr strains than in the parental strain (Figure 5a). The transcription levels of ERAD‐related genes were increased in different degrees in the strains S1C2r and S1CLr compared to the control (Figure 5b). The observed transcriptional up‐regulation of UPR‐ and ERAD‐related genes is consistent with the activation of these pathways during glargine production in the strains S1C2r and S1CLr. This activation might be consistent with the degradation observed in these strains. Taken together, the data suggest that the CBH1 carrier may attenuate the ER stress more effectively than both the CBH2 carrier and LA‐20, neither of which showed a substantial alleviating impact.

Optimised Secretory Production of Glargine Using Alternative Signal Peptide Designs

Considering the variability in the efficiency of signal peptides (SPs) to secrete recombinant proteins, selecting a suitable signal peptide for a given protein is crucial (Kottmeier et al. 2011). To obtain an efficient signal peptide for Glagrine secretion, we tested three different fungal signal peptides by replacing the initial SP1: AnG (the signal peptide of β‐glucoamylase from A. niger ), AnA (the signal peptide of α‐amylase from A. awamori), and SP2 (the signal peptide of CBH2 from T. reesei). Expression cassettes containing the three different signal peptides were constructed and co‐transformed with the pyrG cassette into the T. reesei strain QP4 (Figure 6a). Transformants were screened as described above. Subsequently, the candidate transformants were fermented in FM containing 2% glucose as the carbon source to determine their capacity to produce glargine. The transcription levels of glargine were detected by RT‐qPCR. For each expression cassette, the transformant with the highest transcript level of glargine was selected for subsequent studies and referred to as SGC1r (AnG), SAC1r (AnA), and S2C1r (SP2), respectively (Figure 6b). Meanwhile, the transcription levels of CBH1 carrier of each strain were detected (Figure 6c). It was found that there exhibited high transcription levels of glargine and CBH1 carrier in the SGC1r and S2C1r strains, while the transcription levels of both were low in SAC1r. The secreted proteins from these strains were analysed by Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE. As shown in Figure 6d, a band of approximately 6.0 kDa was observed in SGC1r, which was subsequently identified as glargine by LC–MS/MS analysis (Table S6). In contrast, no corresponding bands were observed in the other two strains. Moreover, the Human G‐INS ELISA Kit was used to measure the concentration of glargine in the culture supernatants. Glargine content in the culture supernatants of SGC1r was approximately 58.95 mIU/L, nearly four times higher than that in the S1C1r strain (Figure 6e). When scaled up to a 1‐L bioreactor under controlled parameters (30°C, 400 rpm, 1.0 vvm, pH 3–5), the SGC1r strain achieved a glargine yield of 144.75 mIU/L, confirming its enhanced production performance under industrially relevant conditions (Figure S2).

Discussion

In this study, we established an efficient secretory production system for glargine in T. reesei through an integrated strategy focusing on fusion partners, signal peptides, and the ER homeostasis. The choice of fusion partner proved critical for successful secretion, with the CBH1 carrier yielding the highest glargine titre among the tested candidates (Figure 4a,b). Further engineering of CBH1 by deleting its C‐terminal PGP sequence was performed to reduce potential structural rigidity near the Kex2 cleavage site, thereby potentially enhancing cleavage efficiency (Nyyssönen et al. 1993). Notably, expression of the CBH1‐glargine fusion protein in the S1C1r strain activated the UPR, as indicated by the upregulation of bip1 and pdi1 genes, but did not elicit the ERAD pathway (Figure 5a,b). This pattern suggests that the CBH1 carrier facilitated correct folding to a degree that UPR activation sufficed to restore ER homeostasis, thereby obviating the need for robust ERAD‐mediated degradation. In contrast, the strains expressing fusions with the CBH2 carrier or LA‐20 exhibited upregulation of both the UPR and ERAD genes (Figure 5a,b), suggesting the induction of ER stress, which may have resulted in impaired protein folding. This would be consistent with their poor secretory performance.

Beyond the fusion partner, the secretory signal peptide also significantly influenced production levels. Replacing the native signal with the AnG signal peptide (the strain SGC1r) enhanced glargine secretion by 4.09‐fold compared to the baseline construct (Figure 6d,e). This increase was accompanied by the UPR activation (Figure 7a), implying that the higher protein flux into the ER necessitated enhanced folding capacity. Importantly, none of the signal peptide variants triggered the ERAD pathway (Figure 7b), affirming that the CBH1 carrier effectively supported glargine folding across different secretion contexts. The failure of the SAC1r strain to activate the UPR might be attributable to insufficient transcriptional abundance of the fusion protein to exceed the threshold for ER stress signalling. A key challenge encountered was the proteolytic degradation of secreted glargine. The addition of Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) to culture supernatants stabilised the product (Figure S2), confirming that endogenous proteases secreted by T. reesei pose a significant obstacle (Adav et al. 2011). Future efforts would benefit from employing protease‐deficient strains (Landowski et al. 2016; Goodrick et al. 2001; Sun et al. 2022). Finally, these results revealed that the primarily secreted product was the single‐chain glargine, not the mature double‐chain form, as confirmed by Tricine‐SDS‐PAGE (Figures 4a, 6d). While the endogenous Kex2p is an efficient protease to cleave the fusion protein at the cleavage site in eukaryotes, its activity proved insufficient for complete processing at the engineered Kex2p cleavage site of glargine in T. reesei, which may arise from the restricted protease specificity of Kex2p or the steric constraints at the substrate interface is a recognised limitation in fungal expression systems (Kurokawa et al. 2002; Seeboth and Heim 1991; Caballero‐Pérez et al. 2021). Undoubtedly, enhancing the proteolytic processing capacity through co‐expression of the Kex2p proteases or engineering of the cleavage site remains essential (Takahashi et al. 2000). Although the limitation of incomplete processing indicated a challenge for therapeutic applicability and industrial scalability, the secreted glargine precursor retained structural integrity through correct formation of three disulfide bonds, a critical and often error‐prone step essential for physiological activity of pharmaceutical proteins. This demonstrates the T. reesei's inherent capacity to provide an oxidative environment and chaperone machinery sufficient for disulfide bridge formation to support protein folding and maturation. Consequently, the stable secretion of a folded, intact glargine represents a significant advance in harnessing T. reesei for complex biopharmaceutical protein production. Future strategies to achieve complete processing of the bioactive two‐chain form may include: (i) The co‐expression of Kex2p with glargine precursor leverages the endogenous secretory pathway for in vivo cleavage, which bypasses the requirement for exogenous trypsin, minimises product‐related impurities, and ultimately enhances the processing efficiency and yield of correctly matured insulin (Sreenivas et al. 2015); (ii) The modulation of cultivation parameters, such as lower temperature and/or adjusted pH, creates an oxidative environment that mimics the ER folding environment in mammalian cells, thereby enhancing the fidelity and activity of key proteases through optimised folding conditions or structural stabilisation (Sevier et al. 2007); (iii) CRISPR‐mediated knockout of genes encoding extracellular proteases prevents product degradation and avoids interference with specific processing reactions, ultimately improving product yield and quality (Wang et al. 2020).

However, it should be noted that the glargine yield reported in this study (58.95 mIU/L) is substantially lower than current industrial platforms based on E. coli or mammalian cells (typically the yield level of IU/mL). This is primarily because the current strain represents a foundational engineered chassis, designed primarily to validate the feasibility of heterologous expressing the complex protein drug glargine in T. reesei. Current glargine high‐yield industrial strains such as E. coli or CHO are the result of decades of long‐term directed evolution and systematic engineering optimization. Therefore, the significance of this study lies in providing proof of concept to demonstrate that T. reesei possesses the capacity to express and secret the complex insulin analogs, which facilitates expanding the sources of industrial strains for the production of protein drugs. Future enhancement of glargine production in T. reesei can be achieved through several integrated strategies: (i) The co‐expression of chaperones, such as Bip1 and PDI, directly enhances protein folding efficiency and alleviates ER stress, thereby increasing the secretion yield of functional proteins (Yu et al. 2015); (ii) Systematic metabolic engineering optimises the supply of key precursors (e.g., acetyl‐CoA, amino acids) and cofactors, ensuring adequate resources and energy for efficient biosynthesis (Sha et al. 2025); (iii) Advanced fermentation optimisation involves fed‐batch strategies and precise control of critical parameters (e.g., DO, pH), thereby maximising production scalability and overall titre in bioreactors (Darabzadeh et al. 2018). Collectively, these approaches present a comprehensive roadmap for improving the manufacturing efficiency of complex protein therapeutics in microbial systems. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the rational integration of a strong constitutive promoter, the CBH1 carrier protein, and the AnG signal peptide creates a synergistic effect for glargine production in T. reesei, primarily by modulating the ER stress to circumvent degradation pathways. This integrated secretion strategy provides a valuable framework for producing other complex therapeutic proteins in this fungal host.
