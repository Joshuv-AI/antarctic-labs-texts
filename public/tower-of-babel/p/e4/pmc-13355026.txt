From episodic imaging to real-time sensor monitoring: translational advances in assessing fracture healing dynamics
Authors: Fawwaz Al-Smadi, Ze Lin, Na Li, Jiewen Liao, Sajeda Al-Smadi, Xiayidan Abudourusuli, Xudong Xie, Chenyan Yu, Yiming Li, Mengfei Liu, Bobin Mi, Guohui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFracture healing culminates in restoration of mechanical competence, yet clinical monitoring remains largely dependent on episodic imaging and subjective assessment, which provide delayed structural information and limited insight into the evolving stability of the fracture construct, contributing to uncertainty in clinical decision-making and delayed recognition of impaired healing. Advances in bioelectronics, implantable sensors, and wearable systems are enabling longitudinal assessment of fracture recovery by capturing quantitative signals related to mechanical load transfer, local tissue state, and functional activity in real-world settings. In this review, we synthesize recent developments in sensor-based fracture monitoring and propose a conceptual framework that integrates three complementary dimensions of healing assessment: mechanical competence, biological progression, and functional recovery. Among these, load-path sensing of implant–bone load transfer provides the most direct proxy for fracture stiffness and currently represents the most translationally mature approach, supported by emerging preclinical and early clinical studies. In contrast, biological sensing strategies, including impedance- and dielectric-based approaches, aim to detect earlier changes in callus composition but remain at lower levels of translational readiness, while wearable monitoring technologies offer scalable insights into rehabilitation trajectories but provide indirect measures of fracture stability. Collectively, these approaches support a transition from episodic structural imaging toward continuous, data-driven characterization of healing dynamics. Achieving clinical implementation will require workflow-integrated sensing systems, interpretable analytical frameworks linking sensor outputs to clinically actionable endpoints, and multicentre validation establishing standardized thresholds across fracture types and treatment strategies.The translational potential of this articleCurrent literature on fracture healing monitoring is largely technology-centric, with limited integration of sensing outputs into clinically actionable frameworks. This review addresses that gap by providing a unified, decision-oriented synthesis that links mechanical, biological, and functional sensing paradigms to the core clinical endpoint of fracture healing, restoration of mechanical competence, and to key management decisions such as weight-bearing progression, follow-up intensity, and early detection of delayed union. By contextualizing existing technologies within a translational maturity (TRL) framework and evaluating evidence from benchtop, preclinical, and early human studies, this work identifies which sensing strategies are closest to clinical implementation and what barriers remain, including the need for standardized protocols, validated decision thresholds, workflow integration, and scalable data interpretation. The translational value of this review lies in defining how continuous, quantitative monitoring can complement or partially replace episodic imaging, enabling earlier, more objective, and individualized fracture care, while providing a roadmap for the development, validation, and clinical adoption of sensor-enabled, data-driven orthopaedic management systems.Keywords: Electrical impedance sensing, Fracture healing monitoring, Implant-integrated sensors, Load transfer mechanics, Smart orthopaedic devices, Wearable sensing

Introduction

Traumatic injury represents a major cause of global disability, substantially restricting patient activity and resulting in reduced quality of life and work productivity [1]. Among traumatic injuries, bone fractures constitute a particularly significant public health burden, contributing to long-term disability, impaired quality of life, health loss, and substantial economic costs, especially in aging populations and individuals with osteoporosis [[2], [3], [4]]. Recent epidemiological analyses estimate that, in 2019 alone, approximately 178 million new fractures occurred worldwide, with 455 million individuals living with acute or chronic fracture-related symptoms and nearly 26 million years lived with disability (YLDs) attributable to fractures [2]. Despite advances in surgical techniques and rehabilitation, the overall global risk of fracture non-union remains as high as 5% [5], underscoring the persistent clinical challenge of monitoring and guiding fracture healing.

Fracture healing is a multistep biological process governed by the interplay between biomechanical stability, biological signalling, and the local tissue environment [5,6]. Depending on fixation strategy and mechanical conditions at the fracture site, healing may proceed through primary healing under rigid fixation or through secondary bone healing characterized by callus formation and overlapping inflammatory, reparative, and remodelling phases [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Importantly, the progression of healing is highly dynamic and patient-specific, influenced by interfragmentary motion, anatomical location, and systemic factors. Mechanical competence of the fracture construct evolves continuously over time, yet remains largely inaccessible to direct clinical assessment [[7], [8], [9]].

Despite this dynamic biology, fracture aftercare has changed little since the introduction of X-ray imaging more than a century ago [11]. Clinical follow-up continues to rely predominantly on episodic physical examination and serial imaging, including conventional radiography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasound [[12], [13], [14]]. These assessments provide intermittent snapshots of a three-dimensional, time-dependent process and are subject to substantial interobserver variability [15,16]. Advanced imaging modalities are costly, expose patients to cumulative radiation, and are impractical for frequent monitoring in routine clinical care [12,13,17]. Moreover, radiographic evidence of union reflects mineralized tissue formation and therefore lags behind the underlying biological and mechanical processes of healing, limiting the early detection of delayed union or non-union [18].

This mismatch between dynamic healing and episodic assessment directly affects clinical decisions. Progression of weight-bearing, rehabilitation intensity, implant removal or dynamization, and the need for reintervention are commonly determined under uncertainty, guided by symptoms and delayed radiographic findings [19]. Pain and tenderness are nonspecific and may not reliably reflect fracture stability, while functional assessments performed in the clinic provide limited insight into real-world loading [20]. Although experimental and clinical studies demonstrate a strong relationship between fracture stiffness and safe weight-bearing capacity [21,22], clinicians currently lack tools to assess these parameters longitudinally and objectively in vivo. As a result, fracture follow-up is characterized by variability in care, conservative rehabilitation strategies in some patients, and increasing demands on healthcare systems already facing a growing fracture burden.

Sensor-based monitoring offers a route to address this gap by providing repeated or continuous quantitative information on the mechanical, structural, and functional dimensions of healing [14,23]. Such approaches could move fracture aftercare beyond episodic imaging toward longitudinal assessment of how mechanical competence and function evolve in daily life, allowing earlier recognition of stalled recovery and more individualized guidance on loading and rehabilitation. At present, clinicians manage a dynamic biological process using tools that reflect only delayed structural change; the clinical value of sensing technologies will depend on their ability to convert these new streams of data into metrics that can meaningfully inform decisions about weight-bearing, follow-up, and reintervention. Sensor-enabled monitoring may also support remote fracture follow-up in home environments, allowing clinicians to observe recovery trajectories during routine daily activities between clinic visits.

Several recent reviews have summarized emerging sensing technologies for fracture healing and smart orthopaedic implants. However, most existing reviews primarily catalog individual sensing platforms or focus on specific technological modalities, with less attention to how different sensing strategies correspond to distinct dimensions of fracture healing or how these signals may support concrete clinical decisions during fracture aftercare. In this review, we synthesize evidence on wearable and implant-adjacent sensing technologies that extend fracture monitoring beyond conventional imaging by organizing them according to the dimension of healing they interrogate, mechanical competence, local biological progression, or functional recovery. We further evaluate their translational maturity using a Technology Readiness Level (TRL) framework and examine evidence spanning simulation, benchtop validation, animal models, and early human investigations. The review focuses primarily on long-bone fractures treated with internal or external fixation and on wearable monitoring during rehabilitation, highlighting how these sensing paradigms may inform clinical decisions such as progression of weight-bearing, implant management, follow-up intensity, and early investigation of delayed union.

Literature search strategy

Search terms combined fracture healing terminology with keywords related to sensing systems and biomechanical monitoring. Representative search terms included fracture healing monitoring, implantable sensors, instrumented fixation, bone strain sensing, electrical impedance fracture healing, smart orthopaedic implants, wearable monitoring, gait analysis, and biomechanical load monitoring. The representative PubMed search strategy was: (“fracture healing” OR “bone healing” OR fracture) AND (“implantable sensor” OR “instrumented fixation” OR “bone strain sensing” OR “electrical impedance” OR “smart implant” OR “wearable sensor” OR “biomechanical monitoring”) AND (monitoring OR sensing OR telemetry).

Peer-reviewed English-language articles describing sensing technologies, technical developments, validation studies, and relevant narrative or systematic reviews were considered for inclusion. Studies focusing specifically on orthopaedic fracture monitoring were prioritized; however, selected studies from related fields of biomedical sensing or digital health were also included when they provided important methodological or technological insights applicable to fracture healing monitoring. Studies focusing exclusively on imaging modalities or therapeutic stimulation without a monitoring component were excluded unless directly relevant to sensor integration concepts. Reference lists of key articles were additionally screened to identify seminal studies and recently published work that may not have been captured in the database search.

Article screening and thematic synthesis were conducted independently by two authors, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consultation with a senior author to reach consensus. Because this work was designed as a narrative review, formal PRISMA-based screening procedures, record counts, and risk-of-bias assessments were not performed. Nevertheless, the search and selection process was conducted in a structured manner to ensure transparency and comprehensive coverage of the relevant literature on sensor-based fracture healing monitoring technologies.

Challenges in current fracture healing assessment and follow-up

Despite fracture healing being conceptually defined as the restoration of biomechanical integrity at the site of injury, there remains no consensus among orthopaedic surgeons regarding how and when this state is achieved in clinical practice [13,24]. Assessment of fracture healing continues to rely on a combination of radiographic findings, clinical examination, and patient-reported symptoms; however, these domains frequently diverge, and none provides a direct or reliable measure of evolving mechanical competence at the fracture site [20]. This lack of consensus contributes to uncertainty in clinical decision-making and substantial variability in fracture aftercare.

Radiographic limitations

Plain radiographs remain the most widely used modality for fracture diagnosis and follow-up owing to their low cost, wide availability, and familiarity [13]. However, radiographs have repeatedly been shown to be neither reliable nor sufficiently accurate for determining fracture union or staging healing progression [[25], [26], [27]]. Radiographic assessment relies on indirect features such as callus visibility and cortical bridging, which are qualitative and subject to substantial interobserver variability [5].

CT improves visualization of fracture lines and assessment of cortical continuity and has demonstrated high sensitivity for detecting non-union [28,29]. Nevertheless, its limited specificity, cost, and radiation exposure restrict routine use in longitudinal follow-up. MRI offers valuable insight into early biological processes, particularly in conservatively managed fractures, with resolution of bone marrow edema correlating with progression toward union [30]. However, MRI is resource-intensive and impractical for repeated assessment. Ultrasound has shown promise in detecting callus formation earlier than conventional radiography [31,32], but its clinical utility is constrained by operator dependence and limited penetration through cortical bone [27].

Even when imaging modalities are selected, there is no consensus regarding the timing or frequency of follow-up imaging. Substantial variability exists in clinical practice, as demonstrated by a multi-institutional survey of hand surgeons that reported wide variation in the number and timing of radiographic series obtained during distal radius fracture follow-up [33]. This lack of standardized imaging protocols further limits the comparability and interpretability of radiographic findings across patients and institutions.

Several scoring systems have been developed to improve the objectivity of radiographic assessment. The Radiographic Union Scale for Tibial Fractures (RUST) and its modified variants attempt to quantify healing by grading callus formation and fracture line visibility across cortices [34,35]. Similar approaches have been proposed for other anatomical regions, such as the Radiographic Union Score for HUmeral fractures (RUSHU) [36]. While these scales improve reproducibility in research settings, their adoption in routine clinical practice remains limited, and they continue to rely on static, morphology-based features that correlate inconsistently with mechanical stability and weight-bearing capacity [5].

Clinical and functional assessment gaps

Clinical assessment of fracture healing typically incorporates physical examination findings, functional capacity, and patient-reported symptoms. Commonly used clinical criteria include absence of pain or tenderness at the fracture site, absence of pain during weight-bearing, and perceived functional recovery [19,37,38]. Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) have increasingly been integrated into fracture care, reflecting a shift toward patient-centered assessment [19,39]. However, clinical, radiographic, and patient-rated measures often show poor concordance, complicating the interpretation of healing status [20]. Because these clinical indicators are weakly coupled to mechanical stability, they provide limited guidance for key decisions such as advancing weight-bearing, escalating imaging, or planning revision surgery.

Weight-bearing ability is frequently used as a surrogate marker of healing, particularly in lower-extremity fractures [38]. However, systematic reviews demonstrate that clinical judgments based on pain, tenderness, and weight-bearing capacity vary widely and lack standardization [19]. Importantly, clinicians’ subjective assessment of fracture stiffness has been shown to correlate poorly with objective measures, regardless of training level or clinical experience [34,35,40,41]. Pain on palpation is particularly unreliable, as pain perception is influenced by individual, cultural, and psychosocial factors [42].

Biomechanical demands further complicate clinical assessment. Load-bearing bones such as the femur and tibia require substantially greater mechanical competence than non–weight-bearing bones, and healing timelines vary according to fracture pattern, fixation method, and anatomical site [20,43]. As a result, defining a universal time-based or symptom-based threshold for union remains impractical across fracture types and fixation constructs.

These limitations are reflected in marked variability in clinical practice. Surveys and systematic reviews demonstrate substantial disagreement among surgeons regarding both clinical and radiographic criteria for healing, as well as inconsistent definitions of delayed union and malunion [24,44]. The addition of clinical notes to radiographic adjudication can further alter healing determinations, underscoring the subjective nature of current assessment paradigms [45].

Collectively, these limitations illustrate a fundamental gap between the biological endpoint of healing, restoration of mechanical competence, and the tools used to judge it in practice. Current assessments provide intermittent and largely qualitative surrogates that are weakly linked to load-bearing capacity and real-world function. This gap creates uncertainty in decisions about weight-bearing, rehabilitation, and reintervention, and underpins the rationale for sensing approaches capable of delivering objective, longitudinal measures of the healing process.

Conceptual paradigms for sensor-based monitoring of fracture healing

In fracture care, monitoring refers to the repeated or continuous acquisition of quantitative data reflecting the evolving mechanical competence, local tissue state, or functional recovery of the healing bone over time. This differs from standard follow-up, which is typically anchored to intermittent clinic visits and episodic imaging, creating additional costs and logistical burdens for patients and health systems while offering only sparse snapshots of a dynamic process [46]. Within this broader shift toward longitudinal data capture, connected sensor platforms, often framed within the Internet of Things (IoT), enable automated sensing, trend detection, and remote access to measurements via wireless communication and miniaturized electronics [47,48].

In orthopaedics, this has motivated the development of “smart” implants and adjunct devices (plates, nails, external fixators, casts/braces) designed to generate objective signals that track healing progression [13]. Importantly, the clinical value of these technologies is determined less by hardware novelty than by which aspect of healing they interrogate and how directly that signal supports clinical decisions. Conceptually, existing approaches can be grouped into three paradigms: (1) load-path–based mechanical monitoring, (2) local structural/biological monitoring, and (3) functional/behavioural monitoring. Because the current evidence base and clinical interpretability differ across these domains, load-path mechanical monitoring is discussed in greatest depth, while biological and functional monitoring are framed as essential companion dimensions whose translational value will likely depend on integration with mechanical endpoints and longitudinal clinical validation.

Load-path–based mechanical monitoring

Mechanical integrity is the clinical endpoint of fracture healing, and multiple experimental studies support the use of stiffness (or related measures of load transfer) as a surrogate for strength during bone callus formation [49,50]. Load-path monitoring measures how mechanical load is distributed between the healing bone and a supporting construct (external fixator, plate, or intramedullary nail). The central principle is consistent across all fixation platforms: as the fracture stiffens, the bone carries more load and the fixation construct carries less, producing predictable changes in measured strain, deflection, or compliance [[51], [52], [53]]. This paradigm is most directly connected to decisions such as weight-bearing progression, dynamization strategies, and timing of implant removal (Fig. 1).

External fixation and externalized constructs

Because load is shared between bone and external fixation devices, multiple sensing strategies have been developed for fractures treated with external fixators [58]. These include instrumented struts/rods using strain gauges and systems using vibration or modal analysis. A representative example is the instrumented fixator strut described by Ernst et al., which developed an instrumented fixator-strut with strain gauges, electronics, battery power, and Bluetooth telemetry for remote monitoring of tibial fracture healing up to implant removal, including home-care data collection with smartphones. They monitored implant loading progression via rod strain changes and reported characteristic time courses including initial increases followed by declines (to ∼45% of max) during healing, with measurement durations reported as 139 ± 89 days (mean ± standard deviation, SD) [55], consistent with progressive load transfer trends illustrated in clinical monitoring examples (Fig. 1B).

Beyond strain monitoring, vibrometric/modal approaches with external excitation have been evaluated. Mattei et al. reported in vitro vibrometric assessment of callus stiffness using an instrumented micro-hammer for low-magnitude mechanical excitation and accelerometers to capture vibration response, with increasing resonant frequencies as simulated callus stiffness increased [59]. They subsequently reported in vivo monitoring in humans for femoral lengthening and tibial fracture healing using similar micro-hammer excitation and accelerometers, reporting increasing resonant frequency trends (∼2-4% per week) during callus evolution and emphasizing greatest utility in early stages, with translation challenges related to workflow simplification and robustness to changes in fixator assembly [[60], [61], [62]]. Chiu et al. similarly performed experimental modal analyses in phantoms fixed with plate-screw systems or intramedullary nails, reporting resonant frequency increases (<600 Hz) as simulated callus stiffness increased, with sensitivity primarily in early healing windows (the first 8–10 weeks after implant insertion) [63,64].

Regarding mechanical displacement inference via capacitive sensing, Sorriento et al. proposed a network of parallel capacitive sensors to detect relative translations and rotations of external fixator pins and used in silico models to relate pin displacements to callus stiffness changes under compressive loading, reporting predicted displacement resolution and emphasizing real-setting challenges such as soft tissue, swelling, temperature, and humidity [17]. For sensor placement and optimisation, Di Puccio et al. performed in silico analyses and in vitro validation to identify optimal strain gauge locations on fixator components across callus geometries and loading conditions, reporting measurable strain changes particularly in early healing phases (6 weeks after fixation) [65]. Kalinowski et al. explored fibre Bragg grating (FBG) sensors glued to Schanz pins of a circular external fixator, using synthetic models and preliminary in vivo testing to differentiate simulated callus stiffness and to identify resonant frequency differences (approximately 4 Hz differences between simulated callus states), while noting the need for larger studies [50].

Kumpuniemi et al. proposed flex (bend) sensors placed on skin under a cast at the fracture location to monitor potential angle changes associated with abnormal healing, describing a testbed and initial accuracy investigations as a candidate for further studies [46].

Implant-adjacent and fixation-integrated mechanical monitoring

Implant-adjacent strain sensing aims to improve fidelity by measuring deformation directly within plates, nails, or adjacent bone. This class has progressed rapidly with advances in miniaturization, packaging, and wireless data transfer.

A notable recent direction is battery-free and wirelessly powered implant ecosystems. Kasper et al. reported a technological framework enabling wireless, battery-free implant operation in freely moving subjects using NFC-enabled implants powered and communicated with via wearable devices. In 11-month long-duration large-animal fracture healing studies these devices demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, osteointegration, and successfully tracked decreasing bone strain adjacent to a healing fracture, identifying a specific strain minimum as a biomarker for union, alongside imaging and stiffness assessments [54], illustrating the feasibility of continuous implant-adjacent monitoring (Fig. 1A).

The AO Fracture Monitor (AOFM) project is described as an implantable biofeedback sensor system that can be attached to a standard bone implant to monitor implant deflection under physiological loading and transfer data via Bluetooth to a smartphone app and cloud infrastructure. This platform represents an important translational milestone in implant-adjacent load-path monitoring. Preclinical in vivo evaluation in the Smart Plate Project demonstrated the feasibility of long-duration implant load monitoring during fracture healing, supporting progression from experimental smart-implant development toward clinical translation [66]. Building on this work, the first-in-human Fracture Monitor T1 study was designed for patients with femoral fractures and femoral instabilities, including post-traumatic non-unions, with safety endpoints and secondary clinical, radiographic, and technical performance measures [67]. Although validated clinical decision thresholds remain to be established, these studies mark a key transition from preclinical validation toward human safety and feasibility evaluation.

Earlier clinical translation of load-path principles is also represented by instrumented internal fixators/telemetry modules used to compute “elasticity” (load–deformation relationships) under standardized loading maneuvers. Kienast et al. monitored femoral fracture healing using an instrumented internal fixator to quantify elasticity trends over time and reported the ability to distinguish fast vs slow healing and stable healing vs unstable non-healing patterns across prolonged follow-up [56]. Seide et al. similarly used strain-gauged implants with inductive coupling and standardized loading cycles during visits; while not autonomous (requiring extracorporeal powering), their long-term human monitoring demonstrated longitudinal elasticity decline consistent with healing and supported feasibility using readily available electronic components [57]. Both studies are illustrated in Fig. 1C. Together, these studies remain among the clearest clinical demonstrations that implant-adjacent mechanical sensing can yield clinically interpretable longitudinal trajectories [56,57].

Additional strategies include self-powered or minimally powered sensing that logs mechanical exposure. Borchani et al. evaluated piezo-floating-gate sensors integrated with fixation structures, harvesting energy from micro-strain variations and storing evolving loading statistics in memory; the concept is attractive for long-duration logging but requires further in vivo validation and careful interpretation given dependence on loading behaviour and fracture context [68]. Wolynski et al. described ex vivo use of multiple flexible substrate wireless microelectromechanical sensors adhered to intramedullary nails to quantify the biomechanical environment along fixation hardware during simulated healing, reporting differences by sensor location and fracture state, supporting the plausibility of multi-site mechanical environment mapping [69]. Najafzadeh et al. used fibre Bragg grating sensors instrumented on composite femur and implant surfaces under cyclic loading with epoxy “healing” stages, reporting strain patterns consistent with union states and proposing potential application to long bones [70]. He et al. reported a strain sensor monitor system applied in a rabbit fracture model to record motion status and monitor bone healing, with bench validation against simulations and in vivo feasibility reporting [71]. Feist et al. validated a mechanoregulatory fracture healing simulation algorithm against continuous in vivo sensor data from an ovine study using plate strain sensors, applying clustering to distinguish healing outcome categories and reporting differences in predicted trajectories [72].

Rajamanthrilage et al. described a fluidic X-ray visualized strain indicator (X-VISUAL) designed to quantify bending strain in orthopaedic plates under physiological loading using standard radiographs [73]. The device converts plate deformation into displacement of a radiodense fluid column via a mechanical lever mechanism, enabling indirect strain estimation without embedded electronics or active telemetry. In Sawbones composite tibia models and human cadaveric specimens, the system demonstrated reversible and repeatable behaviour and discriminated unstable osteotomy constructs from allograft-repaired, stiffer configurations. Based on prior fixation fatigue data, the authors proposed that the achieved measurement resolution (<2% body weight) was sufficient to track progression from unstable fixation toward safe weight-bearing thresholds during fracture healing [73].

Non-contact mechanical proxies of stability

A distinct subset infers mechanical competence without instrumenting the implant itself. Direct electromagnetic coupling (DEC) measures changes in electromagnetic near-field behaviour caused by implant deformation under applied load. Labus et al. first demonstrated the feasibility of DEC-based sensing in experimental models, showing that implant deflection and fracture stiffness states could be detected through shifts in electromagnetic coupling in simulated healing environments (Fig. 1D) [18]. Building on this concept, a subsequent observational study evaluated DEC monitoring in patients with diaphyseal tibial fractures, including home-based measurements with repeatability criteria met in most participants; bending compliance decreased over time in fractures that progressed to union and increased in a non-union case, suggesting potential for early trajectory identification [74]. Wolynski et al. further explored multi-antenna DEC approaches to detect spatial variation in healing along the implant and reported early differentiation of delayed-healing trends in experimental models, while noting that prospective (not post hoc) early classification remains a key translational requirement [75].

Load-path technologies are conceptually well aligned with the mechanical endpoint of fracture healing because they directly capture changes in load transfer between fixation constructs and the healing bone. Consequently, load-path mechanical monitoring represents one of the most mature sensing paradigms for fracture healing assessment. Clinical and translational studies have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach across multiple fixation platforms. Implant-integrated strain monitoring reported by Kienast et al. and Seide et al. demonstrated longitudinal reductions in implant elasticity that correlated with fracture union in human cohorts, providing strong evidence that implant load transfer can serve as a quantitative proxy for healing progression [56,57]. Similarly, telemetry-enabled external fixation systems have demonstrated the feasibility of wireless communication and long-term implant-load monitoring during fracture healing [54,55]. Despite these advances, interpretation of mechanical monitoring signals remains context dependent. Measured strain or resonant-frequency shifts can be influenced not only by callus stiffness but also by muscle tone, soft-tissue damping, fixation geometry, and patient loading behaviour, resulting in substantial patient-specific variability. In addition, many systems rely on extracorporeal excitation, such as mechanical impacts or externally applied electrical signals, to generate measurable responses, introducing workflow complexity and operator dependence, while limited spatial resolution may obscure heterogeneous callus development along the fracture site. Addressing these challenges will require standardized excitation protocols, context-aware signal-processing algorithms, and improved multi-region sensing strategies. Local structural and biological monitoring

Electrical impedance and dielectric sensing of callus composition

Local tissue-state monitoring seeks to probe biological progression (hematoma → cartilage → bone) that may precede radiographic union. Electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is a leading approach because impedance components reflect tissue composition and fluid/cellular architecture. These approaches are summarized in Fig. 2.

In murine fracture models stabilized with external fixators or plates, Lin et al. showed that EIS measured across microelectrodes within the fracture gap differentiated good vs poor healing and correlated with quantitative μCT metrics, supported by equivalent circuit models that classified repair states [77] (Fig. 2B). Translationally, Fukase et al. developed a rabbit tibial defect model using a standard plate modified to house wireless EIS sensors, with measurements transmitted serially over weeks; impedance magnitude/phase increased during the transition from hematoma to cartilage to bone and then plateaued with consolidation, correlating with radiography, μCT, and histology and distinguishing union versus not-healed cases [76] (Fig. 2A). These studies collectively support EIS as a sensitive indicator of early callus state and suggest potential pathways for implant-compatible implementation [76,77].

Capacitive and dielectric approaches represent a related family of sensing strategies. Conceição et al. proposed an ultrasensitive capacitive sensing system designed for incorporation into osteosynthesis plates, supported by numerical modelling and initial experimental validation showing phase-dependent capacitance patterns and multi-region discrimination across healing phases, while emphasizing practical design trade-offs (resolution vs sensitivity, hardware complexity vs power consumption) relevant to eventual implant integration [78]. A similar direction is represented by Pires et al., who developed a bioelectronic osteosynthesis plate concept with a network-architectured capacitive system and demonstrated that normalized capacitance patterns change across healing stages, though real-world variability in bone geometry and dielectric inhomogeneity remains a key source of measurement error [79].

Electromagnetic and RF-based tissue sensing proposals

Electromagnetic and radiofrequency (RF)-based sensing approaches provide a non-ionizing strategy for probing fracture morphology, implant deformation, and local tissue-state evolution during healing. Rather than representing a single sensing modality, this field spans several related mechanisms, including antenna-based electromagnetic interrogation, dielectric or capacitive tissue sensing, and resonant LCR/RLC-based electromechanical transduction. Antenna-based approaches have mainly been explored for detecting structural or dielectric contrast. For example, Moqadam et al. presented a dual-polarized transceiver sensor system based on patch antennas and a reactive impedance surface layer to detect and characterize narrow cracks in phantoms using transmission parameters and imaging reconstruction, with analyses including specific absorption rate (SAR) considerations [80]. Aldelemy et al. reported a simulated RF sensing framework with machine-learning classification of fracture stages across modeled anatomical variations and multiple sensor configurations, reporting up to 99.2% accuracy in classifying different stages of simulated femoral fracture healing, highlighting the need for clinical validation [81]. These studies highlight the potential of RF-based assessment, but their current evidence remains largely computational or phantom-based, and therefore requires validation under realistic biological, mechanical, and anatomical variability.

A more implant-oriented branch of RF sensing is passive resonant LCR/RLC monitoring, in which mechanical deformation or changes in the local electromechanical environment are encoded as shifts in resonance frequency, impedance response, or quality factor. This architecture is particularly relevant for orthopaedic implants because it can combine sensing and wireless readout in compact passive or minimally powered circuits, reducing the need for bulky active electronics and potentially limiting mechanical mismatch at the implant–tissue interface. In a fracture-healing context, McGilvray et al. demonstrated that near-field resonant sensors mounted on fixation hardware could track changes in the implant–bone mechanical environment and distinguish normal from delayed healing in an ovine model, with micro-computed tomography and histological findings supporting the divergent healing outcomes [82]. Related advances in passive LCR sensing and wireless electronic suture systems further illustrate the broader engineering potential of resonant and wireless musculoskeletal monitoring architectures, although these approaches require direct validation in fracture-fixation settings before clinical relevance can be inferred [83,84]. Overall, passive resonant monitoring remains a promising but early-stage approach, with unresolved challenges including coupling stability, encapsulation reliability, readout distance, and clinically meaningful links between resonant features and fracture stiffness or union progression.

Taken together, RF-based orthopaedic sensing should be viewed as a spectrum that includes antenna-based structural/dielectric interrogation, capacitive or dielectric tissue-state sensing, and passive LCR/RLC resonant implant monitoring. Among these, LCR-type approaches are especially attractive for smart orthopaedic implants because they combine strain-sensitive transduction, passive wireless readout, and reduced circuit complexity. Translation will require validation of coupling stability, encapsulation reliability, and clinically meaningful links between resonant features and endpoints such as construct stiffness, union progression, and safe weight-bearing.

Tissue-state sensing may enable earlier detection of impaired biological progression, particularly with electrical impedance–based approaches, by directly probing changes in the evolving fracture environment before radiographic union becomes apparent. Experimental studies by Lin et al. demonstrated strong correlations between impedance measurements and micro-computed tomography (μCT)-derived bone metrics in murine fracture models [77], while work by Fukase et al. extended these observations to wireless monitoring in rabbit fracture models [76], highlighting the potential of impedance-based sensing to capture early biological changes within the fracture callus. Capacitive and dielectric sensing concepts proposed by Conceição et al. and Pires et al. further suggest that implant-integrated electronics could enable multi-region monitoring of callus development, although validation to date has largely been limited to simulation or controlled experimental settings [78,79].

Circulating biochemical markers of bone turnover provide a useful comparator for defining the distinct information gain of local tissue-state sensing. Formation markers such as procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase reflect osteoblastic matrix formation and mineralization activity, whereas C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX) reflects type I collagen degradation and osteoclastic bone resorption [85]. In fracture populations, CTX and P1NP have been reported to peak at six weeks after tibial or femoral shaft fracture fixation and to associate with subsequent radiological healing, although not with functional healing [86]. However, systematic review evidence in long-bone non-union highlights substantial heterogeneity in patient characteristics, fracture sites, sampling schedules, and marker interpretation, limiting the use of circulating markers as standalone predictors [87]. Thus, biochemical markers provide systemic and intermittent information on bone remodelling activity, whereas sensor-derived measurements provide localized or functional longitudinal information related to implant–bone load transfer, fracture-site tissue-state evolution, or real-world limb use. These approaches should therefore be considered complementary rather than interchangeable.

Despite these promising signals, interpretation of tissue-state measurements remains challenging, particularly during long-term in vivo monitoring. Impedance and dielectric signals may be influenced by electrode placement, fracture geometry, surrounding tissue composition, temperature, hydration, proximity to metallic hardware, and sensor–tissue interface changes caused by biofouling, protein adsorption, or fibrous encapsulation. In capacitive systems, parasitic capacitance from adjacent plates, screws, leads, and encapsulation layers, together with time-dependent dielectric drift from tissue remodelling or encapsulant aging, may further distort longitudinal signal trajectories. These effects can generate impedance or capacitance changes that resemble healing-related signal evolution, creating a risk that sensor-interface drift or environmental variation is misinterpreted as callus maturation. This concern is especially relevant during early healing, when hematoma organization, inflammatory edema, edema resolution, and early callus formation overlap and may produce nonspecific impedance changes. Consequently, tissue-state sensors should currently be interpreted as local biological risk-stratification tools rather than standalone indicators of delayed union, fracture stability, or weight-bearing readiness. Addressing these limitations will require robust disturbance-rejection strategies, stable calibration, reference or ratiometric measurement designs, and prospective validation in heterogeneous large-animal and human cohorts. Functional and behavioural monitoring

Functional monitoring does not interrogate the fracture site directly; instead, it quantifies the consequences of healing through movement and loading patterns in daily life. This paradigm is attractive for scalability and patient-centered relevance, but it must be interpreted as downstream and potentially confounded by pain, fear-avoidance, rehabilitation adherence, and comorbidities.

A representative example is plantar pressure monitoring combined with data-driven modelling. Chen et al. developed a quantitative model to estimate modified Radiographic Union Score for Tibial fractures (mRUST) scores using continuous insole pressure data in patients with tibial shaft fractures treated with intramedullary nails. Their approach achieved high estimation performance (a mean absolute error of 0.641 and an R2 of 0.902) and optimized a reduced sensor layout while using interpretability analyses such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to show clinically plausible shifts in sensor contribution during healing. These results illustrate how functional sensing can be translated into longitudinal recovery metrics that support follow-up decisions, particularly by identifying patients whose loading or gait patterns deviate from expected recovery [88]. Wearable and behavioural sensing approaches are well suited for monitoring real-world recovery, rehabilitation adherence, and functional milestones. Their primary clinical role is to support triage by identifying patients whose recovery trajectories deviate from expected progression and who may require earlier clinical review, rather than to replace diagnostic assessment of fracture union. Their greatest value likely lies in complementing mechanical or tissue-state monitoring, particularly in high-volume fracture pathways. Continuous assessment of gait symmetry, loading distribution, and activity levels can reveal recovery deviations that are not apparent during episodic clinic visits. However, functional signals are influenced by factors such as pain perception, fear-avoidance behaviour, rehabilitation adherence, and comorbidities, and therefore cannot be interpreted as direct surrogates of structural union. Instead, their principal clinical role lies in trajectory surveillance, identifying patients whose recovery patterns diverge from expected progression and who may benefit from earlier imaging, mechanical assessment, or therapeutic adjustment.

Functional monitoring approaches using wearable sensors represent one of the most clinically scalable strategies for longitudinal assessment of fracture recovery. Because these systems can be deployed non-invasively and integrated into routine rehabilitation workflows without altering surgical procedures, they offer strong translational potential. Studies such as those reported by Chen et al. demonstrate that plantar-pressure measurements combined with machine-learning models can approximate radiographic healing scores and capture recovery trajectories during rehabilitation [88]. Nevertheless, currently available datasets remain relatively limited and are often restricted to specific fracture populations or controlled rehabilitation environments. Consequently, while wearable sensing is well positioned to support recovery surveillance and early identification of atypical rehabilitation trajectories, its ability to directly infer fracture stability or biological union remains indirect and will likely require integration with complementary mechanical or biological sensing approaches.

Practical synthesis across paradigms

Across paradigms, the central translational challenge is not sensing in isolation but generating clinically interpretable metrics that map to decisions: timing of weight-bearing progression, need for intensified follow-up, or earlier investigation for impaired healing. Load-path systems are most directly linked to mechanical competence and have the most mature human feasibility evidence [[55], [56], [57]], including emerging remote and non-contact implementations [74,75]. Tissue-state systems, particularly EIS, offer sensitivity to early biological progression with increasingly convincing translational models [76,77], but require validated thresholds and robust long-term implant integration. Functional monitoring offers scalable, patient-centered longitudinal data and can support recovery surveillance at population scale [88], though it remains indirect and confounded.

To synthesize the evidence presented across monitoring paradigms, the maturity of representative fracture healing monitoring technologies was evaluated using the TRL framework adapted from the NASA and European Commission models ranging from TRL 1 (conceptual research) to TRL 9 (fully deployed clinical technology). TRL provides a structured metric for comparing development stages across sensing paradigms and identifying the critical steps required for clinical translation. In this review, TRL ranges were inferred from the reported validation environments and study designs in the literature. Conceptual demonstrations were categorized as early-stage technologies (TRL 2–3), benchtop or ex vivo validation as intermediate development (TRL 3–4), preclinical animal studies as translational prototypes (TRL 4–5), and technologies evaluated in early human feasibility studies as higher translational maturity (TRL 6–7). From a translational standpoint, systems that integrate seamlessly into existing surgical and follow-up workflows are more likely to achieve clinical adoption than those requiring repeated extracorporeal excitation or specialized operator input. Intracorporeal sensing platforms that avoid additional procedural complexity, minimize infection risk, and maintain electrical safety are generally better suited for routine practice. Equally important considerations include cost, hardware miniaturization, accessibility, and automated data interpretation. Technologies that depend on complex post-processing or expert handling may remain confined to specialized centres. Standardized hardware/software ecosystems and automated trend analysis will therefore be essential for enabling broader use, including potential home-based monitoring.

A realistic near-term clinical trajectory is complementary deployment rather than replacement of existing assessment pathways. Mechanical or tissue-state monitoring may inform union-related decisions in selected cases, while wearable functional monitoring may guide rehabilitation and identify atypical recovery patterns requiring earlier clinical review. The rationale for sensor fusion follows from the different information provided by each modality: load-path signals estimate implant–bone load transfer and construct stiffness, impedance or dielectric signals probe local callus composition and tissue-state evolution, and wearable functional signals capture real-world limb use and rehabilitation behaviour. Interpreted together, these signals could help distinguish whether delayed recovery primarily reflects impaired mechanical load transfer, delayed biological progression, or altered patient loading behaviour. AI and machine-learning methods may provide the analytic framework for this integration by modelling individualized recovery trajectories, detecting deviations from expected progression, and generating decision-support outputs for weight-bearing progression, follow-up intensity, or rehabilitation adjustment [89]. However, direct evidence that multimodal sensing improves outcomes compared with single-modality monitoring remains limited. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether sensor fusion improves delayed-union prediction, clinical decision-making, rehabilitation guidance, or follow-up efficiency, with transparent algorithm validation and clinician oversight required before routine implementation.

Challenges to clinical translation and implementation

Sensor-based monitoring has clear conceptual appeal, but translation into routine fracture care has been constrained by limitations spanning biology, engineering, clinical workflow, and health-system adoption. Many proposed systems demonstrate feasibility under controlled laboratory or preclinical conditions, yet fail to generalize across fracture types, fixation constructs, and real-world patient behaviour. A translationally credible monitoring strategy must therefore address not only signal acquisition but also interpretability, safety, usability, and regulatory–economic viability (Fig. 4).

Barriers to clinical translation of sensor-based fracture monitoring. Multiple factors limit the integration of sensing technologies into routine fracture care, including biological variability and limited human validation, engineering constraints in implantable systems, challenges in interpreting large sensor datasets, clinical workflow integration, regulatory and economic requirements, and patient acceptance. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated translational validation frameworks and multidisciplinary collaboration.

Biological and translational limitations

A central limitation of the current evidence base is its heavy reliance on simplified models of healing and preclinical validation. Most evidence derives from animal models (e.g., sheep, rabbits, and mice) or simplified in vitro phantoms [54,76,78]. Even when sensors capture physiologically meaningful signals, the relationship between a sensor trajectory and “healing” is context dependent. Healing in vivo is characterized by heterogeneous and spatially variable callus formation, overlapping biological stages, and strong dependence on mechanical loading history, features that are difficult to reproduce in benchtop experiments that model tissues as homogeneous materials and impose discrete stage boundaries [72,78]. Translation therefore requires demonstrating that sensors can detect clinically relevant transitions under realistic conditions, including overlapping stages and heterogeneous callus structure, ideally in anatomically faithful geometries derived from μCT and under representative loading scenarios [78].

Patient heterogeneity further complicates generalization. Differences in fracture morphology, bone quality, fixation stiffness, and patient activity patterns produce different load-transfer pathways and different biological timelines, such that thresholds derived in one construct or anatomical site may not transfer to another. Early human work illustrates this challenge: studies restricted to a specific fracture location or short early time windows provide important feasibility signals but leave uncertainty about performance across broader clinical populations, later-stage remodelling, and diverse injury patterns [74,88]. It remains largely unknown how sensing technologies, particularly those measuring local biophysical properties (e.g., capacitance, impedance), will perform with complex fracture geometries (oblique, comminuted) or in different anatomical locations [79,90]. The healing environment is further complicated by patient-specific factors like age, bone quality, vascularity, and comorbidities, which are difficult to model preclinically [68,78]. These limitations underscore that clinical translation depends not only on improved sensor performance, but on validation frameworks capable of capturing biological heterogeneity across fracture types, fixation constructs, and patient populations. Without such frameworks, even technically robust systems may fail when exposed to real-world variability.

Technical and data interpretation challenges

Power supply and operational lifetime remain major engineering constraints for implant-adjacent monitoring. Wireless, battery-free (WBF) implants enabled by inductive or ultrasonic wireless power transfer (WPT) offer a pathway to long-duration sensing without battery depletion [91,92], and improvements in thin-film encapsulation have extended in vivo survival to months or years [93,94]. These architectures may support both sensing and actuation when paired with wearable hubs for power delivery and data relay [95]. However, the practical burden often shifts to external hardware, which may require tethering or large wearable batteries, creating trade-offs between autonomy, comfort, and adherence [91,92,96]. In comparison, battery-powered implants can support more autonomous sampling, onboard data storage, and scheduled telemetry but are constrained by implant volume, finite lifetime, and hermetic sealing requirements. Conversely, battery-free systems may be more dependent on external reader alignment, hub wear time, and patient compliance. These factors can influence sampling frequency, data completeness, and transmission reliability, particularly during home-based monitoring. Beyond wireless power transfer, energy harvesting strategies from physiological motion (piezoelectric, triboelectric) remain promising but currently provide limited power for sustained sensing [68,97,98].

Signal integrity and interpretability are equally limiting. Mechanical and bioelectrical signals are highly sensitive to confounding from activity level, fixation configuration, soft tissue changes, temperature, and humidity. Even technologies that perform well in proof-of-concept studies frequently rely on simplified models that do not reproduce the nonlinearities introduced by progressive loading, changing sensor–tissue distances, and evolving tissue composition [78,99]. These realities motivate the need for disturbance rejection and robust signal conditioning, particularly for impedance or capacitive sensing, where parasitic capacitances, proximity to metallic implants, and physiologic environmental variation can alter measurement performance [78,79,100]. Key challenges include signal drift from biofouling or sensor degradation [101], interference from physiological changes in temperature and humidity, and the impact of varying distances between sensor and tissue during loading [78].

Across sensor classes, the most consequential translational gap is the lack of validated thresholds that map sensor trajectories to clinically actionable states. For functional monitoring, for example, it remains unclear which gait parameters reliably distinguish normal from impaired healing, and whether models built in one fracture population generalize across fixation constructs and behaviour patterns [88,102]. Similarly, electromagnetic coupling methods can generate repeatable metrics of early healing rate, but conclusions are constrained by limited time windows, small samples, and uncertain applicability beyond the studied anatomical site [69]. Without validated decision thresholds and uncertainty estimates, continuous monitoring risks producing data streams that are difficult to operationalize.

The absence of universally validated thresholds has motivated the use of AI to model patient- and construct-specific healing trajectories from multidimensional time-series data rather than relying on fixed cut-offs. Early clinical studies suggest that machine-learning approaches can approximate radiographic healing scores (mRUST) or distinguish divergent mechanical trajectories [72,88], although many results remain dependent on limited cohorts or simulated environments [81]. More broadly, continuous monitoring generates data volumes that exceed manual interpretation capacity and require automated analytics, integration with electronic health records, and uncertainty quantification [[103], [104], [105]]. At present, AI is best positioned to provide probabilistic trend interpretation and anomaly detection; definitive judgments regarding union status and weight-bearing decisions must remain clinician-led until prospective validation demonstrates safety and reliability.

Finally, approaches that extend beyond mechanics into chemical or biochemical analytics face additional instability challenges, including biofouling, calibration drift, and specificity of analytes to healing rather than inflammation or infection, requiring careful claims discipline until robust long-term performance is demonstrated in vivo [101,106].

Clinical and surgical barriers

Beyond biological and technical feasibility, successful translation ultimately depends on whether monitoring systems can be incorporated into routine surgical and follow-up pathways without introducing disproportionate risk or burden. Implant-adjacent devices may introduce additional steps (mounting, calibration, sealing), increase implant design complexity, and raise concerns about infection, biocompatibility, long-term encapsulation integrity, and mechanical reliability under repetitive loading. Even when devices are designed as “minimally customized” versions of clinically used fixation constructs, meaningful translational work remains, including miniaturization of electronics, robust packaging, hermetic sealing, and mechanical validation under relevant standards [79]. For capacitive and impedance-based systems, proximity to metallic osteosynthesis components and the need to prevent unintended field lines or interference add additional design constraints [78]. Any implanted electronic component introduces additional risk of surgical site infection, hardware failure, and the need for potential removal. Ensuring biocompatibility and hermetic sealing of implants for long-term dwell time is a non-trivial engineering challenge [79,107]. For impedance and capacitive sensors, these same interface and encapsulation challenges also affect signal interpretation: progressive tissue encapsulation, changes in the effective sensor–tissue distance, and drift in impedance or capacitance may be difficult to distinguish from true callus maturation. Mitigation will require stable encapsulation, low-drift sensor interfaces, reference or ratiometric measurement designs, environmental compensation, and prospective reporting of drift, signal loss, and sensor failure modes during long-term in vivo validation.

Non-implantable strategies may lower surgical barriers but face practical challenges of deployment and adherence. Under-cast sensors, for instance, may be feasible in controlled settings, yet real anatomical conditions raise questions about sensor adhesion, stability, and whether skin-level angle measurements remain informative as internal alignment changes [46]. For wearable gait monitoring, the translational bottleneck is often workflow: selecting parameters that matter clinically, enabling rapid acquisition, and deploying software that can produce interpretable summaries at the point-of-care rather than offline analyses [102].

Even when acquisition is feasible, implementation requires a defined clinical pathway for data review and response. Continuous monitoring generates responsibility: who triages alerts, how results enter the clinical record, and how clinicians are protected from signal ambiguity. Expert adoption frameworks emphasize reliability, interoperability, ease of access, and the availability of technical and data support, as well as the importance of training for clinical staff [108]. Without workflow integration, sensors risk becoming “add-on” technologies that disrupt care rather than streamline it.

Regulatory and economic barriers

Smart implants and remote monitoring systems are combination products that integrate hardware, software, connectivity, and cloud infrastructure, requiring evidence for implant safety, electromagnetic compatibility, cybersecurity, data integrity, and clinical performance. Regulatory approval becomes particularly complex when sensor outputs are intended to influence clinical decisions such as weight-bearing progression, imaging frequency, or reintervention, because the evidentiary threshold is higher for diagnostic or decision-guiding claims than for purely observational monitoring. Such systems may therefore require coordinated evaluation under FDA or CE-marking pathways, including assessment of implanted hardware, wireless power, software as a medical device, and cloud-connected analytics. This is especially challenging when clinical endpoints, healing thresholds, and intended-use claims are not yet standardized, creating a high barrier to entry for innovators [79].

In parallel, reimbursement remains uncertain: technologies must demonstrate measurable clinical or economic value, such as reducing imaging frequency, optimizing follow-up scheduling, preventing delayed-union complications, or shortening time to functional recovery, rather than merely proving technical feasibility. Expert evaluations of adoption highlight that acquisition and maintenance costs are key financial subcriteria influencing implementation decisions, alongside technical support and training requirements [108].

Economic viability is particularly challenging for sensor systems that require specialized external infrastructure, such as power casting, wearable hubs, or long-term service models for data handling and analytics. From a value-based healthcare perspective, the economic case for sensor-enabled monitoring should be judged by total episode-of-care value rather than implant cost alone. Although smart implants are likely to increase upfront device and infrastructure costs, these costs may be offset if monitoring reduces unnecessary follow-up visits or imaging, enables earlier detection of delayed union, supports safer weight-bearing progression, prevents avoidable revision surgery, or shortens time to functional recovery and return to work [109]. Future studies should therefore include health-economic endpoints, including follow-up utilization, imaging frequency, complication-related costs, reoperation rates, time to return to work, patient travel burden, and quality-adjusted life-years. Until reimbursement pathways for remote orthopaedic monitoring are clearer, adoption is likely to remain concentrated in specialized centres or in high-risk subgroups where the cost-benefit proposition is strongest.

Patient-centered and ethical concerns

Patient acceptability is central to any system that extends monitoring into daily life. Wearable and mobile health approaches have demonstrated general acceptability in postoperative monitoring across specialties, but published studies often involve small cohorts, limited patient co-design, and passive use of patient-generated data [23]. Equity concerns are prominent: requirements for smartphone ownership, connectivity, or sustained engagement can systematically exclude older adults and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, potentially widening disparities [23]. These concerns are not peripheral; they directly influence which patient populations can benefit from monitoring-enabled care and therefore must be addressed during technology design and implementation. Wearable devices must be comfortable, low-burden, and easy to use to ensure patient adherence over months of recovery [23]. Patients may find continuous monitoring intrusive, and socioeconomic factors can limit access to required smartphones or wireless data plans, potentially exacerbating health inequities [23,108].

Continuous or near-continuous monitoring may be perceived as surveillance, particularly when activity, gait, adherence, or location-linked data are collected outside the clinic. Consent processes should therefore clearly define what data are measured, who can access them, how long they are retained, and whether they may be used for secondary research or algorithm development. The continuous generation of sensitive health data raises critical questions about data security, storage, and patient privacy [23,110]. Clear protocols and potentially new frameworks (e.g., blockchain) are needed to govern data access, sharing with healthcare teams, and protection against breaches [106,110]. The clinical role of patient-generated data and its integration into electronic health records (EHRs) remains an open challenge [103]. Without credible safeguards, transparent governance, and clearly defined clinical responsibility, patient trust and sustained adoption will remain fragile, regardless of technological performance.

Taken together, these biological, technical, clinical, regulatory, and patient-centered constraints illustrate that the principal barrier to clinical adoption of sensor-based fracture monitoring is not the absence of sensing technologies, but the integration of these technologies into coherent and validated care pathways. Progress toward routine implementation will require coordinated advances across multiple domains: robust validation in heterogeneous patient populations, engineering solutions for long-term implant stability and signal reliability, interpretable analytical frameworks capable of translating continuous sensor streams into clinically actionable metrics, and regulatory pathways that recognize the hybrid nature of connected implant–software systems. Equally important are workflow integration, reimbursement mechanisms, and patient trust in the management of continuously generated health data. Ultimately, successful translation will depend on multidisciplinary collaboration and multicentre validation frameworks capable of establishing standardized healing metrics, calibrated sensor outputs, and clinically meaningful thresholds across fracture types and treatment strategies.

Future directions toward clinically viable monitoring

Progress toward clinically viable fracture healing monitoring will depend on coordinated advances across three interdependent domains: engineering design, data interpretation, and clinical validation. As illustrated in Fig. 5, these domains form a translational pipeline in which reliable human-ready sensing systems must generate interpretable signals that can be linked to clinically meaningful endpoints and validated across heterogeneous patient populations. Advances in any single domain are unlikely to be sufficient in isolation; rather, successful translation will require simultaneous progress in device reliability, computational frameworks for interpreting sensor trajectories, and multicentre clinical studies capable of establishing standardized healing metrics and decision thresholds.

Translation of sensor-based fracture monitoring requires coordinated advances in system design, data interpretation, and clinical validation. Key priorities include development of human-ready sensing systems integrated with surgical workflows, computational frameworks that translate continuous sensor signals into clinically interpretable metrics, and staged human validation through first-in-human, observational, and outcome-based clinical trials to enable regulatory approval and integration into routine fracture care.

Design principles for human-ready monitoring systems

Future monitoring systems must be designed with clinical integration (not technical novelty) as the primary objective. Implant-adjacent platforms should align with established fixation constructs and surgical workflows, emphasizing mechanical reliability, long-term encapsulation stability, and minimal added operative complexity [79]. For bioelectronic plates and capacitive/impedance systems, miniaturization of circuitry, reduction of parasitic effects, and increased sensitivity through electrode optimisation are key engineering priorities [79,100]. Advances in flexible, biocompatible materials (e.g., liquid crystal polymer, Parylene) and encapsulation techniques are essential for chronic implants [107]. For wearable and under-cast approaches, designs must anticipate real anatomical variability, adhesion challenges, and realistic usage conditions outside the clinic [46,102]. Across modalities, systems must be engineered to function reliably under physiological variability rather than idealized laboratory conditions.

Robustness requires explicit engineering for confounding factors. Signal conditioning that compensates for temperature and humidity variation, changes in sensor–tissue distance under progressive loading, and variability in patient activity is essential [78,99]. Power strategies should be matched to the expected clinical monitoring duration: long-term implants may justify wireless power transfer and battery-free architectures, whereas short-term monitoring may favor low-power or intermittent acquisition strategies to reduce burden [46,91,92,96,106]. Interoperability, rapid setup, and intuitive data visualization should be treated as core design requirements rather than optional enhancements [108].

The ideal system operates with minimal patient burden, providing automated trend analysis and seamless data transmission for clinician review without requiring specialized technical handling. For wearables, energy-harvesting capabilities and sustainable, recyclable materials will be key for long-term use [106,111].

From sensor signals to clinically meaningful decisions

The central translational shift is from generating continuous signals to producing longitudinal sensor-derived metrics that can be linked to predefined clinical decisions. Regardless of sensing modality, mechanical, electrical, electromagnetic, or functional, the key question is whether longitudinal patterns indicate expected progression or deviation that warrants altered follow-up, imaging, or rehabilitation strategy. Studies to date repeatedly highlight that feasibility alone is insufficient; interpretation frameworks must account for fracture type, fixation strategy, and patient loading behaviour, and must provide uncertainty estimates rather than fixed thresholds [68,74,88,102].

This challenge is particularly important for load-path monitoring, where raw mechanical signals must be normalized before they can be interpreted as proxies of fracture stiffness. Implant strain, deflection, compliance, resonant-frequency shifts, or electromagnetic-coupling changes are influenced not only by callus stiffness, but also by body weight, gait speed, limb position, muscle activation, walking-aid use, fixation geometry, and loading direction. Vibration, modal, and electromagnetic approaches are additionally affected by soft-tissue damping, sensor–implant coupling, and boundary-condition changes, which can alter measured frequency or coupling features independently of healing. Therefore, data-processing pipelines should include artefact rejection, filtering, baseline-drift correction, loading-event identification, and normalization to patient- and task-specific loading conditions. Standardized loading tasks during clinic visits, or wearable-derived context during home monitoring, may help distinguish true load transfer to the healing bone from reduced implant strain caused by lower patient activity. Accordingly, load-path outputs should be reported as calibrated load-transfer or stiffness-related proxies with uncertainty ranges rather than as direct measurements of fracture stiffness.

AI offers a mechanism to convert multidimensional sensor streams into decision-relevant metrics aligned with clinical actions such as weight-bearing progression or intensified follow-up. In wearable monitoring, early studies demonstrate that machine-learning models can approximate radiographic scores or identify divergent mechanical patterns [72,88], but broader validation across fracture types and clinical contexts remains necessary. These approaches reflect a broader vision of pervasive health systems in which sensor data are fused with electronic health records and contextual information to support personalized care and clinician alerts [103,105]. For fracture management, AI should therefore be deployed to augment (not replace) clinical judgment, generating individualized recovery baselines, uncertainty estimates, and prompts for review when trajectories deviate from expected ranges. The most realistic early use is decision support for scheduling and rehabilitation guidance, with diagnostic labeling reserved for validated multimodal frameworks.

In the near term, the most realistic role for sensors is likely triage and follow-up optimisation rather than definitive diagnosis. For example, functional monitoring may identify patients whose recovery trajectory deviates from expected patterns [88,102], while implant-adjacent systems may provide mechanistic context for whether deviation reflects insufficient stability or behavioural under-/over-loading. Wearable and implant ecosystems that enable continuous data streams could support early detection of problematic trajectories and reduce unnecessary imaging or visits in low-risk cases, if paired with validated clinical pathways [23,74,106]. Sensor data should feed into intelligent systems that integrate with EHRs and other patient data (e.g., genomics and comorbidities) to generate patient-specific estimates of healing progression and risk of delayed union. This could alert clinicians to patients trending toward delayed union or recommend personalized rehabilitation protocols [103,105,112]. Importantly, sensor outputs should be framed as decision support that augments clinician judgment, not as autonomous arbiters of union.

Sensor-based monitoring also creates opportunities for remote fracture follow-up in home settings. Wireless telemetry and smartphone-connected platforms may allow clinicians to track recovery trajectories and detect deviations suggestive of delayed healing without requiring frequent in-person visits. Such approaches could support hybrid care pathways that combine periodic clinical evaluation with continuous home-based monitoring.

Pathways for human validation

Clinical translation should proceed through staged validation. First-in-human studies (e.g., the ongoing AOFM trial [67]) should establish safety, usability, and measurement stability under realistic conditions, including durability of implants and wearables, data completeness, and patient adherence [74,88,93,94]. Subsequent observational studies should correlate sensor trajectories with established clinical endpoints, radiographic union, mechanical stability, and patient-centered outcomes, while explicitly accounting for heterogeneity in fracture site, fixation construct, and comorbidity burden [74,88]. Where possible, studies should incorporate mechanistic anchoring, e.g., relating strain or compliance measures to objective stiffness proxies, or validate tissue-state sensors against histologic or imaging ground truth in suitable models [76,78].

Only after robust observational validity is demonstrated should outcome-based trials evaluate whether sensor-informed pathways improve clinical decision-making, reduce unnecessary imaging or visits, and enhance functional recovery (time to functional recovery, complication rates). Across stages, equity and data governance should be built into trial design, including strategies to avoid excluding patients without smartphones or stable connectivity and transparent reporting of privacy, security, and cost-effectiveness to determine whether monitoring meaningfully improves value of care rather than simply increasing data generation [23,106,110].

Taken together, these considerations suggest several concrete priorities for the next phase of research. First, sensor systems must be designed for integration with existing fixation constructs and clinical workflows, emphasizing reliability, long-term stability, and minimal procedural burden. Second, computational frameworks must translate continuous sensor streams into clinically interpretable trajectories that quantify healing progression and uncertainty. Third, multicentre validation studies are required to establish standardized healing metrics and clinically meaningful thresholds across fracture types and treatment strategies. Finally, successful deployment will require interoperable digital infrastructures that integrate sensor data with electronic health records while ensuring regulatory compliance, economic viability, and patient trust. Addressing these priorities collectively will be essential to move fracture healing monitoring from experimental sensing technologies toward clinically deployable decision-support systems.

Conclusion

Fracture healing is a dynamic biomechanical and biological process, yet clinical monitoring still relies largely on episodic imaging and subjective clinical assessment. This mismatch limits the ability to detect impaired healing early and complicates decisions regarding weight-bearing, rehabilitation progression, and reintervention. Sensor-enabled monitoring technologies offer a promising pathway toward continuous and objective assessment by capturing longitudinal information on mechanical load transfer, local tissue state, and functional recovery. Collectively, the evidence reviewed here suggests that load-path mechanical sensing currently shows the strongest translational maturity, while biological sensing and wearable functional monitoring provide complementary insights that may enhance early detection of healing deviations.

Despite these advances, significant barriers remain before these technologies can be integrated into routine fracture care. Biological variability, engineering constraints in implantable systems, and the lack of validated thresholds linking sensor signals to clinically actionable decisions continue to limit translation. In addition, regulatory requirements, workflow integration, and data governance must be addressed to ensure safe and scalable implementation. Moving forward, research should prioritize the development of human-ready sensing systems compatible with existing fixation constructs, computational frameworks capable of translating sensor trajectories into clinically interpretable indicators of healing, and multicentre validation studies that establish standardized metrics across fracture types and patient populations.

Ultimately, the most realistic near-term pathway lies in complementary monitoring strategies that combine implant-adjacent or tissue-state sensing with wearable functional monitoring. Such integrated systems could enable earlier identification of delayed healing, guide individualized rehabilitation strategies, and optimize follow-up pathways. These sensing systems could also support remote fracture follow-up in home environments, reducing reliance on frequent clinic visits while allowing clinicians to observe healing progression between evaluations. If supported by robust clinical validation and interoperable digital infrastructure, sensor-enabled monitoring has the potential to transform fracture aftercare from episodic evaluation toward continuous, data-informed management of bone healing.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the presented synthesis. First, the literature assessment was conducted as a structured narrative review rather than a fully systematic meta-analysis; consequently, although representative studies were identified through targeted database searches and reference screening, some relevant reports may not have been captured. Second, the TRL assignments used to contextualize translational maturity were inferred from the reported validation environments and study designs in the available literature and therefore represent approximate categorizations rather than formal regulatory assessments. Third, the evidence base across sensing paradigms remains heterogeneous, with many technologies evaluated primarily in benchtop experiments, computational models, or preclinical studies, and only a limited number of early human feasibility investigations. This variability restricts direct quantitative comparison across monitoring approaches and limits the ability to derive standardized thresholds linking sensor signals to clinically actionable endpoints such as union progression or weight-bearing advancement. Finally, the rapidly evolving nature of implantable sensing, wearable monitoring, and AI-enabled analytics means that emerging technologies or unpublished translational developments may not yet be reflected in the literature. These considerations highlight the need for standardized validation frameworks, longitudinal human studies, and prospective clinical trials to establish the clinical utility and decision impact of fracture healing monitoring systems.
