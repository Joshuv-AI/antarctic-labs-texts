Decentralizing dementia research to the US–Mexico border: the Rio Grande Valley AD‐RCMAR as a model for translational equity
Authors: Gladys E Maestre, John Blangero, Eron G Manusov, Gabriel A de Erausquin, Francisco Fernández, Silvia Mejía‐Arango, Vincent Diego, Juan Carlos López‐Alvarenga, Claudia Alaniz, Neyra García, Rosa V Pirela, Andrew Tsin, Michael C Mahaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranslational equity means ensuring that diagnostics, treatments, care models, and prevention strategies are developed and implemented so they work for populations with the greatest burden and least access to care. In Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD), where most evidence derives from highly educated, non‐Hispanic White participants at large academic centers, translational equity is critical to prevent misclassification, limited access, and widening disparities and to ensure that underrepresented communities shape how new knowledge moves into practice. Hispanic and other populations in the United States are disproportionately affected by ADRD yet remain underrepresented in research, with gaps especially pronounced in high‐poverty border regions such as the Rio Grande Valley (RGV) of South Texas. We describe the design and early outcomes of the Rio Grande Valley Alzheimer's Disease Resource Center for Minority Aging Research (RGV AD‐RCMAR), a National Institute on Aging‐funded P30 center located at a new, R2, Hispanic‐Serving Institution. Drawing on program documents, center evaluation data, and alignment with national RCMAR statistics, we examine how the RGV AD‐RCMAR's three cores, the Leadership and Administrative Core, Analysis Core, and Community Liaison and Recruitment Core (CLRC), and the Research Education Component (REC) were structured to decentralize where dementia research questions are generated, who leads that work, and how benefits flow back to the community. During the 2018 to 2023 cycle, the RGV center supported 14 early‐career investigators, representing ∼5% of all scientists in the national RCMAR/AD‐RCMAR portfolio and ∼12% of all AD‐RCMAR scientists. The REC maintained 50% female representation, retained 70% of scholars at the institution after pilot funding, and saw scholars secure National Institutes of Health (NIH) K‐ and R‐level awards. The CLRC has built a registry of over 600 residents interested in participation and documented hundreds of engagement activities and community changes, positioning the RGV AD‐RCMAR as a border‐based model for translational equity in AD/ADRD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, community‐engaged research, dementia, health disparities, Hispanic Americans, Latino, minority aging, translational research

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) equity imperative in Latino communities

In the United States, approximately 7.2 million adults aged 65 and older live with Alzheimer's dementia, with numbers projected to rise sharply in the coming decades. 1 Risk is not evenly distributed: Hispanic Americans are about 1.5 times more likely than non‐Hispanic White adults to develop Alzheimer's or another dementia. 2 Yet Latino communities remain underrepresented across the continuum of research – from observational cohorts and neuroimaging studies to clinical trials and implementation science. 3

The Rio Grande Valley (RGV) in South Texas is a majority‐Latino (90%) (Figure 1), largely rural border region that can be understood as a border humanitarian setting, marked by persistent poverty, restricted access to specialty care, and layered effects of migration, humanitarian crises, and environmental stressors. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The region also has a long history of political and environmental activism 8 , 9 , 10 and cultural resistance 11 that shapes civic trust, 12 institutional relationships, and research participation. Historically, the RGV has been a high‐volume site for recruitment and biospecimen collection (e.g., the Starr County Health Studies since 1981 13 ), yet the intellectual leadership and data governance remained centralized outside the region. The establishment of the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley (UTRGV) and the subsequent funding of the RGV Alzheimer's Disease Resource Center for Minority Aging Research (RGV AD‐RCMAR) provided the infrastructure necessary to anchor research authority and leadership within the community being studied and the cultivation of a regional training pipeline. Medicare data and state reports indicate that dementia prevalence in the poorest Texas border counties can approach double the national average among older adults. 14 These realities make the RGV a critical site for developing translational models that genuinely center communities with the greatest disease burdenS1.

Centralized research and its limits

Over the last several decades, ADRD science has been propelled by a highly centralized research model. Large, well‐resourced academic medical centers serve as hubs for national cohorts, biobanks, clinical trial networks, neuroimaging, and multi‐omic platforms. This structure offers major advantages:

Standardization and rigor: Coordinating centers and harmonized protocols enable reliable biomarker classification and multisite trials.

Standardization and rigor: Coordinating centers and harmonized protocols enable reliable biomarker classification and multisite trials.

Scale: Centralized infrastructure allows the management of large, complex datasets and long‐term longitudinal studies.

Scale: Centralized infrastructure allows the management of large, complex datasets and long‐term longitudinal studies.

Efficient resource allocation: Concentrated funding can be directed toward high‐cost endeavors like drug development and advanced imaging.

Efficient resource allocation: Concentrated funding can be directed toward high‐cost endeavors like drug development and advanced imaging.

However, centralization also has unintended consequences that are increasingly visible in AD/ADRD disparities research:

Data homogeneity: When most infrastructure is located in a small set of urban, tertiary centers, study samples tend to overrepresent people who live near those centers, speak English, and have the resources to participate. This constrains generalizability and, in practice, means that diagnostic thresholds, biomarker cut points, and treatment evidence are disproportionately calibrated to non‐Hispanic White participants. 15 , 16 , 17

Intellectual lock‐in: Dominant hypotheses and methods – especially those aligned with high‐cost laboratory science – become the default definition of “rigor” and “impact.” Social and behavioral interventions, qualitative methods, and community‐driven questions are more easily labeled as peripheral. 18 Studies of faculty of color describe this as a pattern of scholarly devaluation in which particular topics, methods, and lived experiences are systematically discounted. 19

Combined with language barriers, restrictive eligibility criteria, and a history of ethical abuses, this fuels mistrust and low trial participation. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30

To meet the mandate to accelerate translational research that improves care and outcomes in ADRD research, new models must address not only what questions we ask but also who frames those questions, where research power sits, and how benefits are shared.

RCMAR mechanism and the opportunity at the border region

The National Institute on Aging's Resource Centers for Minority Aging Research (RCMAR) Program is the flagship behavioral and social science infrastructure for expanding the pool of the aging research workforce and producing knowledge relevant to older adults in populations facing health inequities. The current cycle (RCMAR VI, 2023 to 2028) funds 18 centers, including several explicitly focused on ADRD. 31

RCMAR centers share three core aims:

Expand the pool of early‐career investigators in aging research.

Provide high‐quality mentorship and methods support for behavioral and social science focused on priority populations.

Provide high‐quality mentorship and methods support for behavioral and social science focused on priority populations.

Support rigorous behavioral and social research in pilot projects.

Support rigorous behavioral and social research in pilot projects.

In the 2018 to 2023 cycle, the national RCMAR/AD‐RCMAR network supported nearly 300 early‐career scientists across 18 centers, generating dozens of National Institutes of Health (NIH) grants and hundreds of publications. National evaluations highlight the program as an especially effective mechanism for building supportive learning communities and promoting holistic career development. 32 , 33

Within this network, the RGV AD‐RCMAR occupies a singular position: It is the only ADRD‐focused RCMAR located at a new, R2, Hispanic‐Serving Institution (HIS) in a high‐poverty humanitarian border region. This article describes how the RGV AD‐RCMAR was intentionally designed as a “counter‐architecture” to conventional centralization and reports early outcomes relevant to translational equity.

RGV AD‐RCMAR: SETTING AND DESIGN

Institutional and regional context

Established in 2018, the RGV AD‐RCMAR is hosted by the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley (UTRGV). A HSI within the University of Texas System, UTRGV was formed in 2013 through the consolidation of legacy campuses, officially opening in 2015 and launching its school of medicine the following year. The center is embedded in a tri‐campus system (Brownsville, Harlingen, Edinburg) along the US–Mexico border.

Two features of this context are central to its design:

Border humanitarian setting: The region is affected by high rates of poverty, immigration enforcement, mixed‐status families, and periodic humanitarian crises. These conditions shape stress exposures across the life course 34 and complicate access to dementia care. 5 , 35

Emerging research enterprise: The RGV AD‐RCMAR launched in 2018 as UTRGV's first NIH‐funded center. What distinguishes the RGV AD‐RCMAR is how these cores are used – not simply to “extend” a centralized model into a new location but to shift the center of gravity of dementia research toward a historically neglected community. In what follows, we outline the structural strategies used to do this and present early outcomes.

STRUCTURAL STRATEGIES FOR DECENTRALIZING RESEARCH POWER

LAC: Anchoring the center in the institutional strategy

In many R1 universities, RCMAR centers coexist with numerous large‐scale research entities. Even when successful, they can remain islands of excellence whose goals compete with other institutional priorities. In contrast, at UTRGV, the AD‐RCMAR quickly became a central element of the university's trajectory toward R1 status.

The LAC works explicitly with senior leadership to:

Frame RCMAR investments as foundational to the university's research identity.

Frame RCMAR investments as foundational to the university's research identity.

Align faculty hiring and promotion with aging and dementia equity priorities.

Align faculty hiring and promotion with aging and dementia equity priorities.

Ensure that bridge funding, space allocation, and institutional resources support the center's growth.

Ensure that bridge funding, space allocation, and institutional resources support the center's growth.

This alignment changes the gravitational pull usually observed at R1 institutions, where scholars interested in health equity and community‐engaged methods often feel pressure to narrow their work to fit dominant biomedical norms. Here, career advancement is increasingly linked to advancing dementia equity in the local community rather than moving away from it.

Building a humanitarian research mandate

While all RCMARs address health disparities, the RGV center explicitly defines the RGV as a humanitarian research setting. This framing recognizes that chronic stressors related to migration, documentation status, poverty, and environmental exposures are not merely background variables but potential causal forces in ADRD risk.

This mandate shapes:

Topic selection: prioritizing questions that link migration history, social exposome measures, and neighborhood conditions to cognitive outcomes and plasma biomarkers.

Topic selection: prioritizing questions that link migration history, social exposome measures, and neighborhood conditions to cognitive outcomes and plasma biomarkers.

Ethical commitments: designing studies that provide immediate, tangible benefits (e.g., health screenings, referrals) alongside data collection.

Ethical commitments: designing studies that provide immediate, tangible benefits (e.g., health screenings, referrals) alongside data collection.

Partnerships: collaborating across the border and with local agencies that serve migrants, mixed‐status families, and older adults left behind when younger relatives migrate.

Partnerships: collaborating across the border and with local agencies that serve migrants, mixed‐status families, and older adults left behind when younger relatives migrate.

By embedding humanitarian concerns into its scientific agenda, the center resists the tendency to treat social context as a mere confounder or background characteristic.

Economic and programmatic permanence

To address the precarity of grant‐funded centers, the LAC instituted mandatory Economic Impact Analyses for pilot projects and major initiatives. These analyses estimate potential regional cost savings from improved dementia prevention, diagnosis, and care, for example, projecting substantial reductions in local health system costs if specific interventions succeed.

Framing the center as a public health infrastructure asset, rather than a time‐limited research expense, has strengthened relationships with local government and health systems. Economic analyses have been used to support:

This approach helps ensure that, even if federal funding ebbs, local stakeholders have concrete reasons to sustain key elements of the center's infrastructure.

The REC is designed to attract and retain investigators whose backgrounds and commitments align with the realities of the border region. Scholars are recruited from across disciplines (e.g., neurology, psychology, public health, social work, engineering) but share a focus on ADRD and older adults in priority populations.

Demographic and mission alignment. The REC has maintained ∼50% representation of both minority and female scholars, reflecting the local community and building a workforce that can authentically engage with participants and families.

AnC: Methods tuned to context

The AnC is designed to counter the idea that rigor requires narrow, reductionist methods. Instead, it treats complexity and context as central to scientific validity. Its distinctive contributions include:

Genetically informed modeling. Leveraging large Mexican American family cohorts (e.g., the Genetics of Brain Structure and Function Study 36 ) and the long‐running Maracaibo Aging Study, 37 the AnC uses kinship‐based approaches (such as Best Linear Unbiased Predictors 38 ) to control for genetic background while enhancing detection of environmental and social effects on ADRD‐related phenotypes.

CLRC: From extraction to mutual benefit

The CLRC aims to transform the relationship between academic research and the RGV community. Rather than treating community members primarily as data sources, the CLRC builds mutually beneficial relationships in which both knowledge and tangible benefits circulate locally.

Key features include:

Integration with service infrastructure. The CLRC is tightly linked to UTRGV's Valley Interprofessional Dedicated Access and Service (VIDAS) program, Area Health Education Centers, and the UniMovil mobile health unit. Dementia research is embedded within events that provide screenings, vaccinations, mental health support, and referrals.

DISCUSSION

Addressing three entrenched constraints in ADRD research

The RGV AD‐RCMAR model speaks directly to three structural constraints that have limited progress toward dementia equity:

Biomedical reductionism. By positioning social determinants and humanitarian conditions as central causal factors and by investing in advanced methods to quantify their biological imprint, the center moves beyond “add SDoH and stir” approaches. Social interventions and policy‐relevant exposures become legitimate targets for mechanistic investigation and, eventually, intervention trials.

Underrepresentation and misclassification. Locating analytic and decision‐making power within a majority‐Latino border institution increases the likelihood that measurement tools, recruitment strategies, and analytic decisions will reflect the realities of Mexican American families. This can reduce misclassification, challenge biased norms, and generate evidence better suited to inform diagnosis and care for underrepresented populations. 48 , 49

The registry, ODSS/CCB evaluation system, and Scholar Immersion experiences create tangible structures for shared accountability and co‐created knowledge.

Shifting who defines “rigor” and “impact”

Qualitative and theoretical work on scholarly devaluation has highlighted how certain topics, methods, and identities are systematically undervalued in academic settings, especially at large, elite institutions. 19 , 50 The RGV AD‐RCMAR can be understood as a practical response: It reshapes the local environment so that community‐engaged, mixed‐methods, equity‐focused work is treated as central rather than peripheral.

By aligning institutional incentives (e.g., promotion criteria, resource allocation) with dementia equity goals and by sponsoring scholars into national networks without requiring geographic relocation, the center reduces the pressure on investigators to abandon community‐anchored topics to be seen as “serious” scientists. This is not merely a matter of representation; it changes the types of questions that can plausibly reach high‐impact journals, major funders, and trial platforms.

Interventions must be designed where the burden is highest. Locating intervention development and testing in high‐disparity regions like the RGV ensures that behavioral and pharmacologic strategies will be feasible, acceptable, and effective in settings where comorbidities, structural barriers, and social stressors are most intense.

Limitations

This analysis is descriptive and based on program documentation, early evaluation data, and selected examples of scientific output. It does not provide a controlled comparison to other RCMAR centers or a formal test of causal pathways between organizational design and outcomes. Some outcome measures (e.g., grant counts, publication impact, long‐term retention) are still evolving, and several pilot findings remain unpublished.

Additionally, the RGV context is unique: Not all regions have comparable humanitarian conditions, institutional histories, or policy environments. Adaptation will require tailoring principles, not wholesale copying of structures.

CONCLUSION

The RGV AD‐RCMAR demonstrates that it is possible to decentralize dementia research infrastructure and authority to a high‐disparity, underresourced border region while maintaining scientific rigor and productivity. For ADRD translational research, the lesson is clear: Equity cannot be achieved solely by adding diverse recruitment targets to trials conducted in distant metropolitan centers. It requires shifting decision‐making power, analytical capacity, and long‐term investment to the communities that bear the heaviest burden of disease and designing research relationships in which knowledge and benefits flow in both directions.

As the broader field looks toward the next generation of prevention and treatment strategies, models like the RGV AD‐RCMAR offer a blueprint for how to link scientific excellence with justice – by treating the places and people most affected by dementia not as afterthoughts, but as primary sites of innovation and leadership.
