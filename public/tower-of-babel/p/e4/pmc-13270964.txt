Introduction

Transcutaneous tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) is 1 of the expanding procedures in the field of structural interventional cardiology to treat severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and frequently used when edge-to-edge repair is not deemed feasible.1 Roughly one-third of patients referred for TTVR have preexisting cardiac implantable electrical devices (CIEDs), which may or may not be causally linked to TR itself, the management of which includes transvenous lead extraction ahead of TTVR or jailing of the current leads.2,3 The short- and long-term effects of lead entrapment on lead function are not well understood owing to limited studies with low enrollment; however, early publications did not report a substantial risk of lead malfunction.4 In patients without CIEDs, the risk of high-degree atrioventricular (AV) block requiring permanent pacing was 11.9% in the Global Multicenter Registry on Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement and as high as 24.7% in the randomized TRISCEND II trial.5,6 The timeframe for the development of both AV block and lead malfunction is unpredictable; this creates a potentially dangerous situation in which permanent pacing is urgently required and a newly implanted valve prevents preexisting CIEDs from delivering therapy. We present a case of subacute complete heart block with concomitant lead malfunction requiring urgent device reprogramming to treat asystolic arrest.

Case report

A 78-year-old man with a history of persistent atrial fibrillation, sick sinus syndrome with a dual-chamber Medtronic pacemaker (Minneapolis, MN) implanted 21 years earlier, severe aortic stenosis status post transcutaneous aortic valve replacement, and severe TR thought to be secondary to lead impingement was referred for TTVR. Preprocedure ventricular pacing burden was 45% when in sinus rhythm and in excess of 80% while in atrial arrhythmia. The preprocedure capture threshold, impedance, and sensing of the ventricular lead were 0.75 V at 0.4 ms, 497 Ω, and 16 mV, respectively. These were stable since the generator exchange several months earlier (Figure 1). Lead extraction was discussed at a multidisciplinary meeting and was not pursued. The lead was positioned at the septal right ventricular outflow tract (Figure 2).

The presenting rhythm was atrial fibrillation with slow ventricular response and a programmed lower rate limit of 60 beats/min. TTVR implantation using an EVOQUE bioprosthetic valve (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA) was uncomplicated, with transesophageal imaging showing a reduction of torrential TR to mild paravalvular regurgitation without stenosis (Figure 3). The ventricular lead was jailed with deployment (Video 1). The patient was transferred to the general ward for recovery, and roughly 5 hours after the procedure was noted to be bradycardic at exactly half of the programmed lower rate limit on telemetry; shortly thereafter, the patient experienced asystolic arrest (Figure 4). The advanced cardiac life support protocol resulted in a wide ventricular escape rhythm at roughly 20 beats/min, thought to be caused by epinephrine injection. Emergent device interrogation showed appropriately sensed ventricular electrograms with failure to capture at previous chronic output; the pace threshold was increased to 8 V at 1 ms, and this resulted in immediate resolution of bradycardia with normalization of hemodynamics. The patient was taken for insertion of a transvenous temporary pacing catheter and transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit for further monitoring.

Ventricular pacing at half the programmed LRL (60 beats/min) before asystolic arrest, indicating dynamic capture threshold increase. The QRS morphology during 2:1 capture was identical to 1:1 ventricular capture, arguing against an escape rhythm coincidentally occurring at half the LRL. The programmed LRL could be marched backward and matched the timing of QRS complexes during normal device function. LRL = lower rate limit.

Device interrogation the next morning showed a ventricular threshold of 0.5 V at 0.4 ms, impedance of 456 Ω, and no underlying ventricular escape rhythm. It was not clear when the capture threshold normalized; however, the bradycardic arrest with resuscitation was >30 minutes long, confirming a lengthy period of compromise. Echocardiography and cardiac biomarkers showed unchanged ventricular function and no significant elevation, respectively.

Over the subsequent days, the capture threshold sporadically increased and decreased, with eventual reliance on the temporary transvenous pacemaker (Table 1). The patient underwent successful Micra (Medtronic) implantation (Figure 5). Follow-up interrogation of the transvenous pacemaker 1 week later demonstrated impedance of 475 Ω with an R-wave sensing of 6.4 mV generated via the Micra; capture threshold testing was not performed.

Discussion

Our case highlights the unpredictable nature of conduction disturbances after TTVR and that routine measures of lead integrity can be completely normal despite impending life-threatening device malfunction. The exact cause of lead malfunction is uncertain in this case; however, microdislodgement is a possibility that could explain the variable capture thresholds with normal impedance and sensing (Figure 5). Lead fracture is also a possibility; however, we would have expected a change in impedance. We considered transient ischemia as a cause of elevated capture threshold; however, the persistence of the abnormality despite normal postresuscitation hemodynamics, unchanged ventricular function, and normal cardiac biomarker levels argued against this. Our patient was fortunate to experience lead compromise while in the hospital; most patients will be discharged with remote monitoring the day after TTVR. Same-day discharge has also been proposed for this procedure, which could have been fatal in the case of our patient.7

Previous reports have shown that as many as one-quarter of patients without a preexisting CIED will require permanent pacing within 30 days of valve replacement.6 The presence of left bundle branch block or left anterior/posterior fascicular block imparts a 3.6-fold risk of high-grade AV block after TTVR, indicating a high-risk subgroup.5 The Valve-in-Valve International Data registry showed that 11% of patients with entrapped leads had dislodgement or dysfunction after TTVR, but these patients had lead impingement against a preexisting surgical valve or annuloplasty ring as opposed to a native valve.8 A single-center study found that 3 of 14 patients with jailed leads experienced complications, with 2 of 3 resulting in death.9 Our patient seemed to have tethering of the lead against the free wall of the native valve, which could have pulled the distal tip away from the septum, resulting in unreliable ventricular capture.

Therefore, the risk of lead malfunction must be weighed in conjunction with the frequent occurrence of AV block. The absence of apparent lead malfunction within the first 24 hours of valve implantation may be falsely reassuring for the risk of life-threatening lead compromise at discharge. Although an arrhythmic cause of death was not identified in the TRISCEND II trial, our case shows that this is a real possibility even in the presence of a CIED implanted 21 years earlier. Until more data are available to risk stratify patients for lead malfunction, a conservative approach with multidisciplinary discussion for leadless pacemaker or coronary sinus lead implantation ahead of TTVR is in the best interest of patients. Decisions regarding the selection of a pacing modality must also consider the risks of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy, which could have the inadvertent effect of negating the benefits of TTVR itself. If lead extraction is thought to be too high risk, the argument can also be made for prophylactic leadless or coronary sinus pacing. There are ample data supporting the use of coronary sinus leads across the spectrum of ejection fractions.10

Conclusion

High-grade AV block necessitating permanent pacing occurs in 11%–25% of patients undergoing TTVR who do not have preexisting CIEDs. Given the limited experience with postimplantation assessment of lead integrity, caution must be taken in patients with preexisting CIEDs because they may experience AV block and lead malfunction simultaneously. There were no detectable abnormalities in impedance or sensing that suggested lead compromise before the loss of capture in our patient. A conservative approach with emphasis on prophylactic leadless or coronary sinus pacing should be considered for all patients undergoing TTVR.
