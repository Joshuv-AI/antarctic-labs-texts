A multicenter predictive model for inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia management in elderly patients in Southwest China
Authors: Dayuan Wei, Yi Tao, Su Min, Ke Wei, Jun Hu, Feng Lv, Chunyuan Liu, Hao Li, Chaoyang Zeng, Ailing Wu, Hui Zhong, Guihua Huang, Jianxiong Wu, Yongfeng Liu, Deyuan Li, Daishun Xiao, Feng Xu, Jian Xian, Yunming Yu, Shuiping Xu, Yue Li, Zeliang Huang, Qingyan Lin, Jin Hua, Zhonghui Wang, Feng Chen, Chaoyu Li, Tiande Yang, Xianfeng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInadvertent intraoperative hypothermia (IOH) significantly increases the risk of complications in elderly patients undergoing general anesthesia. This study aimed to develop and validate a predictive model for IOH specifically for elderly patients in Southwest China. A total of 443 patients aged ≥ 60 years from 24 hospitals were enrolled and randomly assigned to a derivation cohort (n = 310) and an internal validation cohort (n = 133). Logistic regression, LASSO regression, and random forest models were developed, with internal validation used to select the optimal approach. External validation was performed on 334 patients from 4 participating and 4 non-participating hospitals. In the internal validation, the Logistic Regression model outperformed machine learning algorithms, demonstrating an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.841 (95% CI: 0.772–0.910), with a specificity of 81.48%, sensitivity of 70.21%, and optimal risk threshold of 48%. In the external validation cohort (n = 334), the model maintained robust discriminative ability (AUC: 0.760; 95% CI: 0.703–0.817). Crucially, in a subgroup analysis of patients with normal temperature (≥ 36.5℃) at 10 min before anesthesia induction, the model successfully identified 34 high-risk individuals who would likely be overlooked by standard clinical assessment. Introduction

With the exception of surgeries necessitating therapeutic hypothermia (e.g., cardiac or transplant surgery), maintaining core body temperature above 36 °C is standard practice during general anesthesia1. However, the incidence of ﻿inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia (IOH) remains persistently high, ranging from 20 to 70%2–4. IOH is associated with severe adverse outcomes, including increased blood loss, delayed drug metabolism, and surgical site infections5–7.

Although intraoperative temperature monitoring is mandatory, monitoring alone is insufficient for prevention. Notably, the incidence of IOH is significantly elevated during general anesthesia compared to other anesthetic techniques8. Without prophylactic warming measures initiated prior to surgery, anesthesiologists are often limited to observing progressive heat loss; once IOH is established, rewarming the patient intraoperatively becomes challenging and often ineffective. Consequently, accurate predictive models are essential for preoperative risk stratification. While Huang et al.9 developed prediction models for adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years), the applicability and performance of these models specifically for the geriatric population remain uncertain.

Elderly patients, aged 60 and over, are particularly susceptible to IOH due to heightened sensitivity to anesthetics and impaired thermoregulatory mechanisms10–12. According to the Seventh National Population Census of China, this demographic accounts for 18.70% (264.02 million) of the total population. Population aging is particularly pronounced in Southwest China, notably in Sichuan (21.71%) and Chongqing (21.87%)13. Given that regional variations in climate, patient characteristics, and awareness of temperature protection may influence outcomes, region-specific studies are necessary to formulate targeted preventive strategies.

This study aims to develop and validate a predictive model for IOH specifically for elderly patients in Southwest China to guide preoperative decision-making. We employed traditional logistic regression alongside machine learning algorithms (LASSO regression and random forest) to construct candidate models, followed by internal validation to select the optimal approach. Subsequently, the generalizability of the selected model was assessed using an external validation cohort. Finally, we sought to identify an optimal risk threshold to facilitate graded clinical management and the precise application of thermal protection measures.

Methods

Study overview, patient selection, and study design

This study was conducted as a secondary analysis of a multicenter, randomized controlled trial (RCT; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05430997) investigating intraoperative temperature management in elderly patients across Southwest China. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (ZZ2022-24-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The parent RCT was conducted from June 2022 to July 2023 across 24 hospitals in Chongqing Municipality, Sichuan, Yunnan and Guizhou provinces, and strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant Chinese clinical research regulations. Elderly patients were randomly assigned to either a conventional warming group (CWG) or an enhanced warming group (EWG). Patients in the CWG received standard thermal care, including intravenous fluid warming, irrigation fluid warming, and cotton blankets. Conversely, the EWG received additional active warming via forced-air warming. IOH was defined as an axillary temperature below 36 °C during surgery, measured continuously using an axillary thermometer. Previous studies have demonstrated that normative axillary temperature serves as a reliable approximation of core temperature14–16. Standard operating procedures for temperature monitoring are provided in eFigure 1 (Supplement 1).

Inclusion criteria of NCT05430997 were as follows: (1) aged 60 years and older; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status classification (ASA-PS) I–IV; (3) surgical duration exceeding 30 min; (4) normothermia upon operating room entry; (5) normal preoperative coagulation function. Exclusion criteria included (1) mental illness; (2) metabolic diseases; (3) thermoregulatory abnormalities; (4) extensive skin impairment; (5) infectious fever; (6) body temperature > 38.5 °C within three days prior to surgery; (7) any condition deemed unsuitable by the investigator; (8) refusal to sign the informed consent form.

For the development of the predictive model in this sub-study, data were restricted to the CWG cohort. Data from the EWG were excluded because the use of active warming would introduce significant intervention bias, rendering the data unsuitable for baseline risk modeling. Furthermore, to avoid confounding factors associated with pre-existing thermal deficits, patients in the CWG who presented with hypothermia upon entry to the operating room were excluded from the final analysis.

Variables selection and handling

A total of 42 potential independent variables associated with IOH were identified based on clinical practice and relevant literature. Details regarding variable selection and the handling of missing data are provided in eMethods 1 (Supplement 1).

Statistical analysis

We randomly divided the data into a derivation cohort and an internal validation cohort with a 7:3 ratio. To optimize predictive accuracy and interpretability, three distinct modeling techniques were applied to the training set: (1) logistic regression model; (2) LASSO regression model; (3) random forest model9,17,18. To identify independent predictors and adjust for potential confounders, variable selection strategies differed by model type. The LASSO regression and random forest models incorporated variables selected via algorithmic screening mechanisms. Variables demonstrating statistical significance in the univariate analysis and factors considered clinically relevant to IOH based on medical experience were included in the forward stepwise multivariate logistic regression model.

Model performance in the internal validation cohort was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, calibration curves, and clinical decision curves. External validation was performed using data prospectively collected from participating and non-participating hospitals (listed in Supplement 2). In the external cohort, ROC curve were plotted, and sensitivity and specificity were calculated to estimate the model’s generalizability and reliability.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 and SPSS version 26.0. Detailed statistical methodologies are provided in eMethods 2 (Supplement 1). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were analyzed using the t-test and presented as mean (SD), while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test and presented as median (IQR). Categorical data were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared test and presented as frequency (percentage). To evaluate the clinical superiority of temperature 10 min before anesthesia induction (T2) over temperature upon entering the operating room(T1), a subgroup analysis was performed on patients clinically perceived as low-risk (T2 ≥ 36.5℃). The sensitivity and specificity of the model within this specific subgroup were calculated for both the CWG and external validation cohort. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Sample size calculation

Based on previous literature and preliminary data, we estimated that the incidence of IOH would decrease from 60% to 45% in elderly patients receiving active warming19,20. The study was initially designed with a 1:1 allocation ratio between the CWG and EWG, with a target sample size of 1500. However, due to ethical considerations regarding beneficence and the higher observed incidence of IOH in the CWG, the allocation ratio was adjusted to 1:2. This modification was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. 20222402). Accounting for a reduced dropout rate of 10% (eAppendix 1, Supplement 1), the revised total sample size requirement was at least 1304 participants. A final sample of 435 patients in the CWG and 869 in the EWG provided 99% power to detect a difference in proportions of 0.15 at an α value of 0.01 (2-sided). Power analyses were conducted using PASS software, version 21.0.3.

Results

A total of 462 patients were initially screened, of whom 443 met eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. The overall incidence of IOH was 46.7% (207/443). The data was randomized into a derivation cohort (n = 310) and an internal validation cohort (n = 133). In the derivation cohort, the median age was 69 years, mean T1 was 36.43 °C, mean T2 was 36.42 °C, and 45.8% (142/310) of patients developed IOH. In the internal validation cohort, the median age was 70 years, mean T1 was 36.41 °C, mean T2 was 36.40 °C, and 48.9% (65/133) of patients developed IOH. Baseline, anesthetic and surgical characteristics for the derivation cohort are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Comparisons between the derivation and internal validation cohorts revealed no significant differences (eTable 1 and eTable 2 in Supplement 1), ensuring data homogeneity and mitigating potential selection bias. Internal validation

In the internal validation cohort, the logistic regression model demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to the other machine learning approaches. It achieved an AUC of 0.841 (95% CI: 0.772–0.910), surpassing both the LASSO regression (AUC = 0.807; 95% CI: 0.732–0.882) and random forest (AUC = 0.720; 95% CI: 0.634–0.817) models (Fig. 3A). Calibration curves indicated that the logistic regression model exhibited the highest agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, whereas the Random Forest model showed larger calibration errors (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, decision curve analysis confirmed that the logistic regression model provided the highest net clinical benefit across the threshold probability range of 20% to 70% (Fig. 3C). Based on the Youden index in the derivation cohort, the optimal probability cutoff for predicting IOH was determined to be 48% (Table 4). Consequently, the logistic regression model was selected for nomogram construction (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1) and external validation.

External validation

The logistic regression model was further evaluated in an external validation cohort comprising 334 patients (168 from participating hospitals and 166 from non-participating hospitals). The overall IOH incidence in this cohort was 15.27% (51/334). The model yielded an AUC of 0.760 (95% CI: 0.703–0.817) (Fig. 4). To ensure clinical consistency, the optimal cutoff of 48% was applied to the external dataset. Using this unified threshold, the model achieved a sensitivity of 84.31% and a specificity of 59.72% (Table 5). The confusion matrices for the external data are provided in eTable 3 (Supplement 1). To facilitate clinical application, an online risk calculator was developed (available at https://atpcr7.github.io/Dayuan-Wei), with detailed instructions provided in Fig. 5.

Subgroup analysis

To evaluate the model’s performance in identifying risk among patients who appeared clinically safe before surgery, a subgroup analysis was performed on patients with T2 ≥ 36.5℃. This subgroup included 178 patients from the CWG and 154 patients from the external validation cohort. Despite presenting with normal baseline temperatures, 27.0% of patients in the CWG and 9.7% in the external cohort subsequently developed IOH (Table 5). In this subgroup, the logistic regression model maintained discriminative ability, achieving a sensitivity of 52.1% (25/48) and a specificity of 82.3% (107/130) in the CWG, and 60.0% (9/15) and 77.7% (108/139) in the external validation cohort, respectively.

Discussion

Despite the implementation of fluid warming, our study revealed a persistently high incidence of IOH (46.7%) in elderly patients. This highlights an urgent need for a predictive model of IOH specifically tailored to elderly patients to enable stratified temperature management. To address this, we developed and validated three predictive models in elderly patients in Southwest China. The optimal model was first identified through internal validation, followed by external validation to enhance its generalizability and robustness.

The Logistic regression model demonstrated optimal performance in internal validation and maintained reliable predictive power in external validation, outperforming both the Lasso regression and Random Forest models. This may be attributed to the moderate sample size and the predominantly linear relationship between the predictive features and the outcome21,22. With a training set of 310 patients, the data sparsity likely prevented the Random Forest model from establishing robust decision boundaries, leading it to capture noise rather than true biological signals. Unlike logistic regression, which captures these patterns with high fidelity, complex algorithms like random forest may even compromise accuracy by attempting to force non-linear interpretations.

Although preoperative baseline temperature is a known risk factor, its specific definition remains ambiguous and is most commonly operationalized as T123,24. However, relying solely on T1 overlooks significant heat loss induced by the cold ambient environment during patient transfer, preoperative preparation, and skin disinfection25. Our data reveal a risk shift phenomenon, with core temperature declining significantly from T1 to T2, particularly in patients who subsequently developed hypothermia. Consequently, T2 better captures cumulative heat loss and ensures accurate identification of patients who transition from a preoperative normothermic state to a high-risk state prior to induction. To validate the model’s robustness beyond simple dependence on temperature, we performed subgroup analysis in patients with normal T2 (≥ 36.5 °C). In this warm subgroup—where clinical intuition alone might fail to identify risk—the logistic regression model maintained good discriminative ability, successfully identifying 25 and 9 high-risk patients in the CWG and external validation cohorts, respectively. This indicates that, even in patients with a safe initial temperature, the model effectively integrates multidimensional variables (including projected surgical duration and anesthesia method) to predict the likelihood of intraoperative hypothermia. These findings provide scientific guidance during the induction phase, representing the last critical window for anesthesiologists to implement active warming measures before sterile draping limits access.

In our study, dynamic intraoperative variables, such as volumes of intravenous and irrigation fluids, were excluded from the model. This approach differs from several previous prediction models, which incorporated fluid infusion volumes as key predictors to improve accuracy12,26. Although it is acknowledged that large volumes of cold fluids are widely recognized contributors to hypothermia27, these parameters cannot be precisely quantified preoperatively and often vary unpredictably intraoperatively. Exclusion of these variables prioritizes preoperative applicability of the model, allowing clinicians to assess risk and initiate warming strategies prior to anesthesia induction. Conversely, surgical duration serves as a robust and evaluable preoperative proxy. Prolonged surgical duration typically implies a greater cumulative fluid load, extended skin exposure, and a larger total dosage of anesthetics, all of which independently exacerbate heat loss28.

Age was not identified as a significant predictor in our final model, despite its well-established association with impaired thermoregulation. This finding likely reflects the specific characteristics of our study population, which consisted exclusively of elderly patients (≥ 60 years). Unlike studies encompassing broader adult age ranges, our cohort exhibited high physiological homogeneity in thermoregulation, limiting the discriminatory power of age as a variable29,30. From a statistical perspective, Brydges31 notes that effect sizes in gerontology research are often smaller than traditional guidelines anticipate. This suggests that within a restricted geriatric age range, the signal of age differences (e.g., 70 vs. 80 years) is likely too weak to outweigh the dominant influence of surgical factors.

Consistent with previous studies, we identified BMI and general anesthesia combined with epidural anesthesia as independent risk factors for IOH30,32,33. In addition, nerve block anesthesia was explicitly incorporated as a critical risk factor in the present model. Following neuraxial or peripheral nerve blocks, transient increases in distal limb skin temperature (e.g., fingers and toes) occur due to sympathectomy-induced vasodilation and enhanced cutaneous blood flow34,35. However, this peripheral heat gain stems from internal redistribution of thermal energy from the core to the periphery, thereby precipitating core hypothermia. For example, significant decreases in core temperature—despite perceived warmth—have been documented in volunteers receiving epidural anesthesia36,37. Accordingly, the combination of general anesthesia with epidural or nerve blocks exacerbates this redistribution effect and elevates the risk of IOH. With respect to surgical site, the incidence of IOH was markedly higher in limb, thoracic, and abdominal procedures than in spinal surgery38–41. By contrast, hypothermia during spinal surgery is primarily documented in pediatric scoliosis cases, characterized by substantial hemorrhage and extended duration—factors that are less prevalent in routine geriatric spinal procedures42.

Our study has two limitations. First, the data were derived exclusively from a geriatric population in Southwest China. Given the population differences, the model’s generalizability in different regions requires further external validation. Second, the sample size, while sufficient for logistic regression, was relatively moderate for machine learning applications. This data constraint likely restricted the performance of the Random Forest model, potentially underestimating the predictive value of non-linear interactions that might be detectable in larger datasets.

In summary, the present model, which incorporates patient, anesthetic, and surgical characteristics, offers a reliable method for predicting the risk of IOH in elderly patients. Patients with a predicted risk exceeding 48% may warrant enhanced active warming strategies. For those with lower predicted risk (≤ 48%), implementation of basic thermal protective measures—such as fluid warming—is advisable, rather than dependence on monitoring alone. This facilitates accurate assessment of the requirement for intensified warming by clinicians, with potential to reduce IOH incidence and advance stratified temperature management in perioperative care.
