A novel integrated framework for damage detection in steel structures using magnetic piezoelectric sensors and hybrid damage index
Authors: Dattar Singh Aulakh, Divya Rawat, Suresh Bhalla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectro-mechanical impedance (EMI) and the vibration-based monitoring techniques deploy piezoelectric sensors that are permanently bonded to the structural surface. This increases the risk of sensor degradation over time and raises monitoring costs due to the need for multiple sensors. This study explores the development and performance of reusable single magnet-bonded piezoelectric sensor (MBPS) for damage monitoring in steel structures to address these limitations. In this study, MBPS is evaluated for capturing bolt looseness by measuring root mean square deviation (RMSD) using the EMI technique and curvature change during modal testing. The MBPS configuration demonstrates high repeatability (Correlation Coefficient > 99.5%), ensuring reliability for SHM applications. Piezo disc buzzer MBPS, noted for having the highest sensor-to-magnetic base area ratio, displays the highest RMSD index in the miniature specimen. It also effectively identifies damage in a thin-walled steel plate using the RMSD damage index (RDI) and the curvature damage index (CDI) across multiple damage states. The damage indices are found to be highest at the actual damage locations but are at the same time significantly spread over the adjacent undamaged panels, exhibiting an umbrella-like formation. A novel hybrid damage index (HDI) is proposed that fuses the local and the global monitoring techniques, integrating higher-order RDI and CDI, thereby effectively reducing noise over the undamaged panels adjacent to the damaged ones. MBPS paired with HDI offers a highly effective and robust monitoring solution as an alternative to bonded piezoelectric sensors, providing a blend of high sensitivity, flexibility, and resilient damage monitoring for various SHM applications in steel structures.Keywords: Magnetic bonded piezoelectric sensor, Electro-mechanical impedance, Modal analysis, Structural health monitoring, Data fusion in SHMSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) provides real-time or near-real-time data to detect potential issues early, thus facilitating timely interventions and reducing the risk of catastrophic failure. As infrastructure ages and budget constraints tighten, there is an increasing need for cost-effective SHM solutions that deliver accurate, real-time insights while minimizing operational expenses and resource allocation. Traditional SHM systems often depend on expensive, labour-intensive equipment and periodic manual inspections, which can limit their feasibility for large-scale or financially constrained projects. In recent years, various local and global sensing technologies have been employed to identify structural damage, including optical fiber sensors1–3, acoustic emission methods4,5, electro-mechanical impedance6–8, and global vibration analysis9–11. Among the different SHM methods, electro-mechanical impedance (EMI) has garnered considerable attention due to its sensitivity and efficacy in detecting local damage, being a high-frequency vibration technique. The EMI technique utilizes piezoelectric transducers, which serve as sensors and actuators when bonded to a structure. By measuring changes in the impedance signature, EMI-based monitoring can detect micro-level damages, such as cracks at their incipient stage. This makes it especially valuable in monitoring critical infrastructure components prone to localized damage. It has been extensively applied for damage monitoring in structures across various applications12–16. Additionally, it has also been proven effective for vibration-based monitoring of structures on a global scale17–20.

EMI technique has advanced significantly in SHM for concrete structures, as evidenced by recent studies. Park et al.21 developed a sensor diagnostics process to monitor the operational status of PZT sensors in situ, identifying degradation and bonding defects that significantly alter high-frequency SHM responses like Lamb wave propagation and impedance spectra, underscoring the necessity of sensor validation for reliable EMI applications. Lu et al.22 introduced an EMI-Miniature Prism technique to track strength development and dynamic modulus in early-age concrete, offering a non-destructive method sensitive to microstructural changes during hydration, though its focus was limited to early-stage monitoring. Ai and Zhang23 enhanced EMI with a probability-weighted imaging algorithm to localize damage in reinforced concrete slabs, achieving high accuracy in detecting crack locations but noting limitations in sensor placement optimization. Ai and Mo24 proposed a 3DCNN-enhanced EMI approach for predicting unknown compressive stress and damage without pre-labelled data, outperforming traditional networks with rapid, accurate stress predictions across a 16 MPa range, paving the way for automated SHM in real-world scenarios.

Na et al.25 proposed a double magnet-based setup, featuring a magnet on the opposite side for attaching the sensor to composite structures to monitor the adhesive joint. Silveria et al.26 developed and explored extended double magnetic piezo sensor configurations adapted to a foil-based setup for EMI-based monitoring. This extended configuration involved a piezoelectric sensor bonded at one end of the metal foil, while the other end was bonded to the structure using epoxy. Na and Lee27 designed a coupling configuration by extending the piezoelectric sensor with a steel wire instead of using a metal foil, which was attached to the structure with epoxy. Naskar and Bhalla28 presented a metal foil-based orthogonal twin-array configuration of piezo sensors for the EMI technique in remote locations in structural health monitoring (SHM). Moharana and Bhalla29 investigated a thin metal foil setup with a piezoelectric patch attached at one end and embedded in concrete at the other, along with a piezo patch embedded in cement mortar as reusable sensors. Raju et al.30 proposed metal foil-based reusable, non-bonded piezo sensors for monitoring corrosion damage in pipeline segments through accelerated corrosion tests conducted in a laboratory setting. Baral et al.31 introduced the reusable bolted piezo sensor (RBPS) for damage detection in steel structures. Aulakh and Bhalla32 offered a novel magnetic clamp-based reusable piezo configuration for global vibration-based monitoring. Gupta et al.33 presented preliminary investigations into reusable miniature magnet-based piezo sensors mounted on a miniature steel plate for EMI-based damage monitoring as proof of concept.

However, these studies have primarily focused on miniature specimens, such as adhesive joints and small steel components, typically only a few times larger than the piezo sensor itself. This limitation raises concerns about scalability and applicability to larger, real-world structures. Existing magnet-based configurations depend on additional magnets for attachment, but they often suffer from limited holding strength under dynamic loads and require exposed surfaces, which limits their practical use. Extended configurations utilizing metal strips or wires introduce mechanical instability, misalignment, and inconsistent contact pressure, negatively impacting measurement accuracy. While bolted, non-bonded setups are reusable, they involve complex installation and may create local stress concentrations that alter structural behaviour. Additionally, many configurations do not provide sufficient sensitivity and durability under environmental stresses such as vibration, temperature fluctuations, and long-term monitoring.

These limitations underscore the urgent need for practical, robust, and sensitive reusable piezoelectric sensor configurations paired with enhanced damage detection algorithms to improve EMI and vibration-based monitoring. This study further investigates the proof-of-concept MBPS33 for local and global SHM applications and develops an enhanced damage index to enhance accuracy. Three single-magnet configurations, varying in sensor and magnet dimensions, were tested on a miniature steel specimen to assess sensitivity to bolt looseness. The most effective configuration was then evaluated on a 2D large thin-walled steel plate (1200 × 970 mm) using EMI and modal testing across multiple damage states. While this work demonstrates improved reusability and sensitivity in controlled laboratory conditions, it does not fully overcome challenges related to environmental stresses such as vibration, temperature fluctuations, and long-term monitoring, which require future in-situ validation. Electromagnetic impedance technique

The EMI technique was first developed by Liang et al.34. It is a non-destructive technique that uses piezo sensors to monitor the condition of a host structure. In the EMI technique, piezoelectric patches bonded to structures are electrically excited at high frequencies, typically in the range of 30–500 kHz, by an impedance analyser. At these frequencies, the piezo patches actuate the structure and then sense the response, which is reflected from the structure in the form of admittance signatures. This signature consists of a real part, known as conductance (G), and an imaginary part, known as susceptance (B). Any damage to the structure results in a deviation in the admittance signature, which is recorded in the frequency domain. Since the EMI technique operates at high frequencies, it can detect damage in its incipient stages and is immune to low-frequency structural vibrations. Vibration-based monitoring

Modal analysis is a global, vibration-based dynamic testing technique used to extract the modal parameters of structures, namely, natural frequencies, damping ratios, and mode shapes, which reflect their mass, stiffness, material properties, and boundary conditions. Changes in these parameters indicate structural alterations, enabling damage detection, severity assessment, and life prediction. Experimental details for preliminary investigations

Reusable sensor configuration development

The research objectives in this study involved the development of MBPS with different sizes of sensor materials and magnetic bases to study the effect of sensor and magnet size on the sensitivity of MBPS for the preliminary investigations. In the proposed configuration, three different types of sizes were positioned at the centre of a circular magnet, which served as the base for the instrumentation, as shown in Fig. 2. The piezo sensors were attached to the magnet surface with an adhesive layer (epoxy) and coated with epoxy for protection against operational damage. Sensor-I consists of a P-876 DuraAct piezo patch with PIC 255 piezo material, measuring 16 × 13 × 0.5 mm, mounted on a circular magnet with an 18 mm diameter and a 5 mm thickness. A magnetic base was selected in accordance with the objectives of the study to investigate the influence of the magnet-to-sensor size ratio on the sensitivity of the magnetic bonded piezoelectric sensor (MBPS). Prior to sensor attachment, the magnetic base surface was cleaned and lightly abraded to enhance adhesion. A uniform layer of epoxy adhesive was then applied to the prepared surface, and the piezoelectric sensor was carefully positioned to avoid misalignment or entrapped air bubbles. The magnet–sensor assembly was left to cure under ambient room temperature conditions for 24 h to ensure complete polymerization of the adhesive and stable bonding. After the curing, an additional protective layer of epoxy was applied over the sensor to shield the assembly from mechanical impacts, moisture ingress, and other operational hazards during experimental testing.

(a) PIC DuraAct Patch (b) PIC PZT patch (c) Piezo disc buzzer.

Sensor-II includes a piezo ceramic-based 151 A PZT (Lead-zirconate-titanate) piezo patch measuring 5 × 5 × 0.3 mm, also on an 18 mm diameter magnet with a 5 mm thickness. Sensor-III, as shown in Fig. 2, consists of a piezoelectric disc buzzer of diameter 28 mm bonded on a 30 mm diameter circular magnet with a 5 mm thickness, similar to the other two configurations. The magnet thickness was kept constant across all three specimens to maintain consistent damping in the response measurement. Sensor I and II consist of piezo sensors of two different sizes bonded on identical-sized magnets with sensor-to-magnet area ratios of 87.69% and 9.8%, respectively. Sensor-III is a larger sensor with a larger magnet, with a sensor-to-magnet base ratio of 87.11%. Sensors I and II will be compared to study the effect of sensor dimensions on bolt loosening monitoring with constant magnetic base dimensions. Sensor I and III will be compared to study the effect of the size of the magnetic base with an identical sensor-to-magnet area ratio on damage monitoring.

The thickness of the adhesive layer plays a critical role in EMI measurements, as it directly influences the efficiency of stress transfer between the piezoelectric patch and the host structure. A thicker adhesive layer tends to attenuate high-frequency responses, thereby lowering the conductance amplitude and reducing the sensitivity of the sensor system. In this study, the adhesive thickness was kept as small as practically possible, without undergoing a separate calibration procedure, to streamline the fabrication process. Since damage detection is performed relative to a baseline signature, and the adhesive thickness remains identical in both the baseline and subsequent measurements, its influence essentially introduces a constant bias in the absolute admittance. This constant bias cancels out when comparative indices such as RMSD are employed. As a result, the consistent bonding conditions ensured that the observed variations within the 100–500 kHz frequency range were primarily due to structural damage rather than adhesive effects, thereby validating the reliability of the MBPS-based monitoring results.

In sensor-II (PZT), the amplitude of conductance signatures is observed to be decreased in all the damage states compared to the amplitude in the pristine state, indicating a downward shift in the peaks in the damage state. The decrement is visibly observed in all the damage states in Figs. 6(b), 7(b) and 8(b). The amplitude decrement is dominant in the 300–500 kHz range, similar to the pattern exhibited by sensor I MBPS. The damage sensitivity across different damage states is identical to that observed in sensor I. RMSD indices for damage states 1, 2 and 3 are 2.31%, 2.0% and 3.10%, respectively. The RMSD indices are less than the corresponding values by sensor-I, which indicates that the piezo sensor area is proportional to the sensitivity of the MBPS, in agreement with the pattern observed in Fig. 4. Thus, sensor-I, with a higher piezo sensor area than sensor-II, with identical magnetic base area, exhibits a higher sensitivity towards bolt loosening in plate specimens.

The sensor-III (piezo disc buzzer) demonstrates the highest sensitivity to damage states 1, 2 and 3. This is visibly observable in the conductance plots in Figs. 6(c), 7(c) and 8(c). This high sensitivity of the MBPS could be attributed to the larger sensor and magnetic base size, compared to sensor II, which has a smaller sensor and magnetic base size with an identical sensor-to-magnetic base ratio. This increases the area for response measurement, and hence, the quality of information about the structural behaviour in resonance affects the efficiency of the damage monitoring. The RMSD index reaches its highest value with the piezo disc buzzer at 7.9%, 5.88%, and 11.750% for damage states 1, 2, and 3, respectively. These findings establish that the sensor-III MBPS is effective and has better sensitivity than sensor I and II MBPS for damage monitoring. Overall, the piezo disc buzzer MBPS proves to be the most efficient sensor for damage detection in MBPS applications using the EMI technique. The sensor size and magnetic base size are related to the sensitivity of MBPS towards bolt loosening with higher sensitivity with higher sensor and magnetic base dimensions. Combined with the hassle-free magnetic attachment and high sensitivity, MBPS proves to be an effective and improved alternative to bonded piezo configurations for EMI-based damage monitoring.

The dimensions of the magnets, particularly their size and thickness, play a decisive role in both attachment strength and sensing performance. While smaller magnets improve portability, they provide weaker anchorage under dynamic loading, thereby raising the possibility of detachment. In contrast, larger magnets (such as the 30 mm magnet used with the piezo disc buzzer) ensure stronger stability and improved coupling efficiency, albeit with reduced portability. The thickness of the magnet further influences the magnetic flux density; thinner magnets tend to exhibit weaker adhesion, especially when placed on curved or uneven surfaces. Other challenges include potential misalignment on non-planar structures and reduced performance in high-vibration environments due to micro-slippage. Additionally, the magnetic interface may introduce extra damping at the sensor–structure contact, suppressing high-frequency responses and lowering sensitivity compared with rigidly bonded alternatives. In this work, magnet dimensions were selected based on the sensor-to-magnet area ratio to strike an optimal balance between coupling strength and sensitivity. Among the sensors tested, the piezo disc buzzer was found to demonstrated the highest RMSD values due to largest magnet with highest magnet-sensor ratio.

Bolt loosening monitoring in 2D large thin-walled steel structure by the EMI and modal analysis

After preliminary investigations in miniature steel specimens and establishing the piezo disc buzzer MBPS effective for bolt loosening monitoring, it is necessary to investigate the efficacy of MBPS on a large steel structure. Piezoelectric sensors have a unique dual application in structural identification-based SHM. This study also investigates the efficacy of the developed MBPS for bolt loosening using the modal testing approach.

Experimental details for EMI-based monitoring

A 2D large, thin-walled structural steel plate of size 1200 × 970 mm, supported on a square hollow pipe of cross-Sect. 38 × 38 mm2 and 3 mm thickness, was adopted for in-depth investigation on the efficacy of MBPS for damage monitoring, as shown in Fig. 9. The piezo disc buzzer MBPS, identified as the most effective of the three types of MBPS sensors, was utilized for monitoring bolt loosening in the large plate structure, as depicted in Fig. 9. The plate was divided into a grid comprising 30 panels, with six panels along the longitudinal axis and five panels across the lateral dimension of the plate. The MBPS was attached at every node of the grid on the plate to capture admittance signatures in a pristine state. The plate specimen featured a reversible damage configuration related to the bolt loosening of a cover plate that covers a hole with a diameter of 50 mm. The cover plate closes the hole in the plate structure and is secured over the hole when in a pristine state. In the pristine condition, the cover plate was affixed to the main plate structure with four bolts of 10 mm diameter tightened to 35 Nm torque. The damage was inflicted through bolt loosening and removing the cover plate. The cover plates, fastened with four 10 mm diameter bolts tightened to 35 Nm torque in the pristine state, serve to restore local stiffness and continuity to the plate structure by covering the hole. The removal of a cover plate simulates a localized loss of stiffness and mass, mimicking real-world scenarios such as bolt loosening, material loss, or structural disconnections commonly encountered in steel structures. The mechanical changes (stiffness loss, mass reduction, and boundary alteration) produce measurable effects in both local (EMI-based) and global (vibration-based) responses, making it an ideal test case for evaluating MBPS performance. The EMI technique detects these mechanical changes by measuring the admittance signature (comprising conductance, G, and susceptance, B) of the MBPS structure system at high frequencies (100–500 kHz) as explained in Sect. Piezoelectric sensor. The technique leverages the electro-mechanical coupling of the piezoelectric sensor, where changes in the structural impedance (Z) due to damage are reflected in the admittance signature as presented in Eq. 4. The large thin-walled plate and grid-based measurement approach (30 panels) enhance scalability, addressing limitations of prior studies on miniature specimens and ensuring applicability to practical SHM scenarios.

Experimental details for vibration-based monitoring

A 2D thin-walled steel structure plate identical to EMI-based experiments was adopted for the study, which requires vibration-based monitoring using modal testing, as shown in Fig. 9. Unlike EMI-based monitoring, which requires piezo sensors to be bonded at every measurement node, vibration-based monitoring can be performed using a single piezoelectric sensor for response measurement and applying excitation sequentially at all measurement nodes. Accordingly, MBPS and bonded piezo sensors (BPS) were instrumented at node 24 to compare the efficacy of the developed MBPS with the established BPS. However, BPS were not adopted to evaluate them against the MBPS for EMI-based monitoring plate as it is not practically feasible to instrument them at all nodes in the grid. This is one of the primary objectives of this study to counter this drawback of bonded sensors. The strain response was acquired simultaneously from both sensors for comparison of the damage monitoring efficacy. The impact hammer with a sensitivity of 22.25 mV/N was rowed consecutively at all the grid nodes to actuate the plate with impulse excitations. The force and strain responses were captured from BPS and MBPS by an M + P data acquisition system in the pristine and all the damage states. Three excitations were acquired at each node and averaged out to filter out any noise in the measurement of response. Results and discussion for monitoring of the thin-walled steel plate

This section details the sensitivity and damage monitoring efficacy of MBPS in both local and global monitoring and compares the sensitivity of both RMSD and curvature damage indices.

EMI-based damage monitoring

Figure 10 illustrates the bubble plots of the RDI for damage States 1, 2, and 3, respectively. In these plots, the bubble size and colour correspond to the normalized RDI value at each panel location, enabling simultaneous visualization of the magnitude and spatial spread of structural response deviations across the monitored area. This representation directly compares damaged and undamaged panels under different damage scenarios. The EMI technique is sensitive to changes in mechanical impedance caused by stiffness loss and mass reduction. The removal of the cover plate increases local flexibility, enhancing the strain response in the piezoelectric sensor and altering the electrical admittance. The high-frequency range ensures sensitivity to incipient damage, as low-frequency structural vibrations (e.g., global modes) are filtered out. The RDI quantifies these deviations by comparing the conductance signatures between pristine and damaged states, with higher RDI values indicating greater impedance changes at the damage location. In damage State 1, localized damage at panel 15, located at the centre, results in an RMSD of approximately 10% at the damaged panel and a corresponding RDI of 1. Adjacent panels 9 and 21 exhibit RDI values of 0.6 and 0.7, respectively, progressively decreasing to baseline levels (0.1–0.2) in panels farther from the damage site. The spatial decay of RDI indicates that the damage influence is confined to a few neighbourhood panels, with minimal signal contamination in undamaged regions. This sharp gradient demonstrates the RDI’s ability to localize damage with high spatial resolution, effectively differentiating between damaged and undamaged zones in a relatively unbounded structural region. In contrast, damage State 2 involves bolt loosening near the steel plate boundary. Here, the peak RMSD increases to 17% at the damaged panel with RDI of 1, with neighbouring panels 20 and 14 recording RDI values of 0.5 and 0.3, respectively. Hybrid damage index

RMSD is highly sensitive to local stiffness changes reflected in dynamic response and can detect early-stage anomalies and subtle structural degradation/damage. Curvature is directly related to global structural stiffness and offers localized spatial resolution for identifying abrupt stiffness loss at specific panels. The damage monitoring results presented in the previous section display that while curvature effectively identifies damage locations through prominent peaks at damaged panels, both RMSD and curvature can exhibit moderate yet non-negligible damage indices at panels (21, 22, 23) adjacent to the actual damage location due to stress redistribution and modal coupling. This distributed response in undamaged areas, especially in damage state 3, where two distant damage locations exist, can increase false positives if either metric is used independently. Furthermore, RMSD values are often affected by boundary conditions, sensor coupling quality, and excitation variability, while curvature-based indices are more resilient to such variations but sensitive to spatial sensor density and noise in mode shape differentiation. To eliminate this drawback and enhance the accuracy and robustness of structural damage localization, a hybrid damage index (HDI) is proposed by integrating RDI and CDI. This approach is motivated by RMSD and curvature, distinct yet complementary damage-sensing capabilities by implementing the piezoelectric sensors in local and global monitoring.

By unifying them, the limitations of each are compensated by the strengths of the other, leading to a more stable and noise-robust index, especially over the non-damaged locations. This integrated metric is particularly advantageous for the MBPS system, where indirect sensor coupling via magnetic bases may introduce minor signal inconsistencies and decreased sensitivities. The integrated approach by combining both indices ensures that damage detection remains reliable regardless of such variabilities. Ultimately, the unified approach yields sharper and more accurate localization, minimizes the damage index at non-damaged panels, and enhances damage specificity across all damage states. HDI achieves this by combining the high localization accuracy of curvature with the damage sensitivity of RMSD, using a weighted formulation that amplifies consistent signals and suppresses spurious responses. It acts as a filter that reinforces peaks where both indices concur and attenuates scattered signals at undamaged locations, thus increasing the signal-to-noise ratio of the damage index.

HDI is based on a variance-weighted approach that effectively fuses multiple structural damage features into a single damage indicator using an optimal variance-based weighting scheme. It offers a reliable method to predict and monitor structural integrity across different damage states while accounting for feature variability and uncertainty. In this framework, the HDI is computed as a weighted fusion of damage-sensitive features and their higher-order features, where the weights are determined by the inverse of the variance (i.e., the inverse of the squared standard deviation) of each feature. This design inherently assigns more importance to features exhibiting lower variability, based on the assumption that lower variability indicates higher reliability. The variance-based HDI approach carries a firm statistical foundation, aiming to fuse multi-modal damage-sensitive features to minimize the overall uncertainty, considering the correlation between the damage-sensitive features. The HDI minimizes its own variance by assigning higher weights to features with greater precision and lower variance, producing a more stable and reliable damage indicator than any individual feature (RDI or CDI) alone. This balance between global sensitivity (captured by CDI) and local precision (captured by RDI) enhances the robustness of damage detection, significantly improving the system’s resilience against false alarms caused by measurement noise.

Justification for inverse-variance weighing approach

To ensure scientific rigor and reproducibility, the HDI employs a statistically optimized, inverse-variance weighting scheme to fuse these indices. This data-driven approach prioritizes features with lower variability by assigning higher weights, thereby enhancing the reliability of the fused index by reducing the influence of measurement noise and sensor coupling variability, which is particularly critical for MBPS due to their indirect coupling via magnetic bases. These weights are normalized to sum to 1, ensuring that the HDI remains interpretable and maintains a consistent scale across different fusion strategies. In the context of this study, RDI, derived from local EMI measurements, tends to exhibit higher variance due to its sensitivity to microstructural noise and localized perturbations. In contrast, CDI, obtained from global modal analysis, typically demonstrates lower variance but may overlook subtle local changes. The inverse-variance method automatically balances these characteristics by assigning higher weights to CDI when its variability is lower, ensuring that the HDI leverages the global robustness of CDI while retaining the local sensitivity of RDI. This eliminates the need for arbitrary or empirically determined weights, which could introduce subjectivity and compromise generalizability.

Alternative weighting approaches, such as equal weighting or empirical tuning, deemed less suitable. Equal weighting disregards the inherent variability of each index, potentially amplifying noisy signals and increasing false positives, particularly in scenarios with significant measurement noise. Empirical weighting, based on manual tuning or expert judgment, introduces subjectivity and reduces reproducibility, as it depends on the specific knowledge or assumptions of the researcher. In contrast, the inverse-variance approach is fully deterministic and data-dependent, relying solely on the statistical properties of the RDI and CDI data. By adapting to the inherent noise characteristics of the data, the inverse-variance weighting scheme minimizes the impact of measurement uncertainties, such as those arising from the indirect coupling of MBPS, and enhances the reliability of damage detection in practical structural health monitoring applications.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive investigation into the application of MBPS for SHM of thin-walled steel structures. The sensitivity of the proposed MBPS was observed to increase proportionally with the dimensions and the sensors and magnets, with the piezo disc buzzer configuration featuring the largest magnet and sensor dimensions yielding superior sensitivity and damage detection capability among the evaluated prototypes. The MBPS demonstrated notable efficacy in EMI-based monitoring of bolt-loosening damage in multiple damage states using RDI. In vibration-based monitoring, the MBPS successfully captured strain mode shapes and identified damage through discernible peaks in CDI across all damage states. Although BPSs produced slightly sharper resolution, MBPS provided comparable accuracy in localizing damage locations using the CDI. However, the RDI and CDI were observed to be spread across the grid with moderate values for the undamaged panels, which might result in false positives. To enhance the damage detection sensitivity and noise resilience of MBPS-based damage monitoring, HDI is proposed through the fusion of RDI and CDI in linear and higher-order strategies to bolster diagnostic efficacy. Overall, MBPS paired with HDI offers a practical, cost-effective, and robust monitoring approach, providing significant advantages in reusability, ease of deployment, and reduced setup time, with only minimal compromise in signal fidelity compared to BPS based monitoring. These characteristics underscore the suitability of MBPS for large-scale SHM applications, particularly in scenarios requiring rapid sensor relocation and cost-effective monitoring strategies.

However, a few limitations must be acknowledged and investigated for adoption of the proposed sensor and damage index for real-life structures. The experiments were conducted in controlled laboratory conditions, limiting insights into MBPS performance under real-world environmental variations, such as temperature and humidity, which may affect magnetic coupling and sensor sensitivity. The long-term durability of MBPS, particularly the stability of the magnetic attachment and potential epoxy degradation under prolonged dynamic loads or environmental stressors, remains untested. MBPS paired with HDI offers a cost-effective, reusable SHM solution, enhancing damage localization with minimal setup complexity. Future research should validate MBPS in-situ, test durability and epoxy stability, assess scalability to complex structures, explore diverse damage types, and integrate wireless systems for real-time SHM in critical infrastructure like bridges and buildings.
