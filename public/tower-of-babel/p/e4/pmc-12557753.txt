Impact of antithrombotic therapy on colonoscopy outcomes in FIT-positive patients: implications for polyp detection, diverticulosis and normal findings
Authors: Naim Abu-Freha, Mifleh Tatour, Fadi Abu Baker, Tarek Saadi, Rawi Hazzan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFaecal immunochemical test (FIT) is widely used in colorectal cancer (CRC) screening, but the impact of antithrombotic therapy on colonoscopy outcomes remains unclear. This study aimed to compare polyp detection rate (PDR) and prevalence of non-neoplastic findings in FIT-positive patients undergoing colonoscopy, stratified by antithrombotic therapy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, multicentre cohort study of 26 280 FIT-positive colonoscopies performed between 2016 and 2023 across seven gastroenterology centres in Israel. The FIT positivity threshold was 75 ng/mL. Patients were grouped by therapy: antiplatelets (n=1904), anticoagulants (n=262) or no antithrombotic treatment (n=24 079). Using crude and matched analyses, we assessed PDR, adenoma detection rate (ADR), CRC detection and diverticulosis prevalence.ResultsPatients on antithrombotics were older (mean 65.9±7 vs 61.0±8 years; p<0.001), more often men (65.2% vs 50.1%; p<0.001) and had higher comorbidity rates. Crude PDR was higher in antithrombotic users (55.5% vs 51.9%; p=0.001), but ADR was similar (28.2% vs 28.3%; p=0.950). Diverticulosis was more prevalent in the antithrombotic group (24.0% vs 17.4%; p<0.001). After matching for age and sex (n=2201 pairs), all previously significant associations were eliminated: PDR were no longer different (55.5% vs 53.1%; p=0.112), diverticulosis prevalence became identical (24.0% vs 24.3%; p=0.799) and normal colonoscopy rates were similar (27.5% vs 29.6%; p=0.123). ADRs remained comparable (28.2% vs 26.7%; p=0.269), and CRC detection rates were identical (2.0% vs 2.0%; p=0.998).ConclusionDemographic confounding, rather than antithrombotic therapy per se, explains the crude associations in colonoscopy outcomes. The matched analysis demonstrates that antithrombotics do not independently affect polyp detection, diverticulosis prevalence or adenoma detection when age and sex are controlled for. These findings support the continued use of antithrombotics during CRC screening, shifting clinical interpretation from medication-based to demographic-based risk stratification.Keywords: COLORECTAL CANCER, POLYP, COLONOSCOPY

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This large, multicentre study demonstrates, through matched case–control analysis, that after adjusting for age and sex, polyp detection, adenoma detection, colorectal cancer (CRC) rates and diverticular disease prevalence are all equivalent between users and non-users of antithrombotics. This provides the most substantial evidence to date that antithrombotics increase FIT positivity without independently affecting colonoscopy outcomes when demographic factors are adequately controlled.

Introduction

The adenoma detection rate (ADR) is one of the most widely recognised quality indicators of colonoscopy.1 Research has demonstrated an inverse association between ADR and the risk of interval colorectal cancer (CRC).2 Calculating ADR can be difficult and time-consuming, as it often involves manually entering pathology findings.3 Polyp detection rate (PDR) is a more accessible metric that can be easily extracted from endoscopy reports and correlates with ADR. PDR rates of 40% in men and 30% in women, corresponding to 30% and 20% ADRs, respectively.4 According to European guidelines, the recommended targets in colonoscopy are 40% PDR and 25% ADR for quality assurance.5 Given the challenges in calculating ADR, PDR has been suggested as a simpler surrogate.6

Concurrently, the use of antithrombotic medications—including aspirin, clopidogrel (Plavix) and oral anticoagulants such as warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants has increased due to cardiovascular comorbidities in ageing populations.7 Aspirin has been associated with increased detection of serrated and right-sided lesions, possibly due to mucosal bleeding enhancing endoscopic visibility.8 Similarly, limited data suggest that anticoagulants may contribute to higher positive faecal immunochemical test (FIT) rates; however, it remains unclear whether this reflects improved neoplasia detection or increased false positives.9

Few studies have directly compared the impact of aspirin, clopidogrel and anticoagulants on PDR and ADR in patients undergoing colonoscopy for a positive FIT.7 Furthermore, the influence of these medications on polyp characteristics (size, location, histology) and the rate of normal colonoscopies remains underexplored.10 This study specifically addresses the impact of antithrombotic and anticoagulation therapy on colonoscopy findings in patients with a positive FIT result, using a defined FIT cut-off, and excludes patients with guaiac-based Fecal Occult Blood Test (FOBT). A large, real-world study can help clarify these relationships and inform clinical decision-making regarding CRC screening with antithrombotic therapy.11 Sawhney et al reported that patients taking low-dose aspirin, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or warfarin had lower positive predictive values (PPVs) for advanced neoplasia on FIT. They concluded that antithrombotic medications might lower the specificity of the test. They recommend discontinuing these agents before guaiac-based faecal occult blood testing if clinically feasible (ie, if the thrombotic risk is acceptable).12 In contrast, recent investigations have found that continuing antithrombotic therapy does not significantly compromise FIT performance or yield in colonoscopies. For example, Rasmussen et al.13 conducted a large Danish national cross-sectional study, demonstrating that antithrombotic treatment, especially non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs), is associated with increased FIT positivity but significantly decreased positive predictive value (PPV) for colorectal cancer and high-risk adenomas in screening. Their findings highlight that while antithrombotic therapy influences FIT performance metrics, CRC detection rates remain only slightly lower in treated patients compared to those untreated. In contrast, Sakamoto et al reported that antithrombotic agents did not adversely affect the sensitivity or PPV of FIT for colorectal neoplasia.14

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis by Nieuwenburg et al. examined the effect of anticoagulants and NSAIDs on FIT accuracy in colorectal cancer (CRC) screening: across eight studies (2,022 patients), these medications had minimal impact on FIT accuracy, with similar PPVs for advanced neoplasia in users versus non-users (38.2% vs. 39.4%).15 In line with these findings, Wauters et al. (2021) reported from a multicenter Belgian cohort of 510 patients that antithrombotic use did not increase false-positive FIT results or alter detection rates of advanced polyps or CRC after adjustment for confounders.16 This body of evidence suggests that FIT accuracy is not influenced by oral anticoagulants or aspirin/NSAIDs, indicating no need to discontinue these medications before FIT screening. Similarly, Kershenbaum et al. found that while the FIT positivity rate doubled in warfarin users compared to non-users (7.7% vs. 3.6%, p<0.0001), there was no significant difference in the positive predictive value for carcinoma; notably, detection rates of clinically significant adenomas were higher in warfarin users (8.9/1000 vs. 4.0/1000, p=0.02).17 A meta-analysis by Ashraf et al. also confirmed no statistically significant difference in neoplasia detection between FIT-positive patients on or off anticoagulants (odds ratio ≈ 0.92; 95% CI, 0.67–1.26).18

Few studies have directly compared the effects of aspirin, clopidogrel and anticoagulants on PDR and ADR in patients undergoing colonoscopy for a positive FIT. Additionally, the influence of these medications on polyp features (size, location, histology) and the rate of normal colonoscopies remains unclear. Large-scale, real-world studies examining the impact of antithrombotic and anticoagulation therapy on colonoscopy results in patients with a positive FIT, using a specific FIT cut-off, are lacking. Such studies could help clarify these relationships and guide clinical decisions for CRC screening among patients on antithrombotic therapy.

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational studies (https://www.strobe-statement.org/); the completed checklist is provided as an online supplemental file 1.

Study design and patients

We conducted a retrospective, multicentre cohort study of colonoscopies performed between 2016 and 2023 in seven endoscopy centres across Israel. Only patients who underwent the FIT were included in the study. A positive FIT was defined as a faecal haemoglobin concentration≥75 ng/mL, according to national screening guidelines. All patients included in the analysis were referred to colonoscopy after a positive FIT for screening; however, a very small number of patients (~1%) had additional symptoms or indications for colonoscopy. Patients with inflammatory bowel disease, prior colonic surgery or poor bowel preparation quality were excluded from analysis. All consecutive FIT-positive colonoscopies meeting these criteria between 2016 and 2023 were included (n=26 280).

Data collection

Our data collection process was comprehensive and meticulous. Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the integrated electronic medical record system. Colonoscopy reports were reviewed for key outcomes, including polyp findings (number, size, location and histology when available), presence of CRC, diverticulosis, angiodysplasia and other relevant findings. Minor polyps were defined as non-adenomatous polyps (eg, hyperplastic polyps<5 mm) that were not included in the ADR calculation.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarised as mean±SD for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test if normally distributed or the Mann-Whitney U test if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test.

A case–control (ratio 1:1) analysis was performed. Patients treated with antiplatelets or anticoagulation were matched 1:1 by sex and age (±3 years) to controls, and outcomes were compared between matched pairs.

Results

During the study period, a total of 338 524 colonoscopies were performed across the participating centres. Among these, 26 280 colonoscopies met the inclusion criteria (performed due to a positive FIT result). Group 1 (antiplatelet therapy) comprised 1904 patients, group 2 (anticoagulation therapy) 262 patients and group 3 (no antithrombotic therapy) 24 079 patients. Baseline characteristics of the groups are summarised in table 1. Patients in the antithrombotic therapy groups (groups 1 and 2) were significantly older on average than those in the control group (group 3), and a higher proportion was men. Specifically, the mean age was 65.5±7 years in group 1 and 68.6±7 years in group 2, compared with 60.9±8 years in the controls (p<0.001 for both comparisons). Men constituted 65.4% of group 1 and 61.1% of group 2, compared with 50.1% in controls (p<0.001 for both).

Patients on antithrombotic therapy also had a higher burden of comorbidities. The prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) was 25.2% in group 1 and 19.1% in group 2, compared with 9.0% in the controls (p<0.001). Similarly, rates of hypertension were 39.7% and 39.3% in groups 1 and 2, compared with 20.5% in controls (p<0.001). Chronic ischaemic heart disease (CIHD) was more common in the antithrombotic groups (7.7% and 6.1%) than in controls (0.5%, p<0.001). These differences reflect patients’ expected higher cardiovascular risk profiles when taking antithrombotic medications.

Key colonoscopic outcomes for the three groups are summarised in table 2. Patients receiving antiplatelet therapy (group 1) and those on anticoagulation (group 2) had a higher incidence of diverticulosis of the colon (24.1% and 23.7%, respectively) compared with controls (17.4%; p<0.001 for group 1 vs 3 and p=0.008 for group 2 vs 3). There were no significant differences between the groups in the rates of diagnosed CRC on colonoscopy (overall CRC detection rates were around 1.7–2.2% across groups; p=0.077 for group 1 vs 3, and p=0.569 for group 2 vs 3). We observed significant differences in polyp detection between the groups. The crude PDR (proportion of colonoscopies with ≥1 polyp found) was higher in the anticoagulation group (group 2: 59.5%) compared with controls (51.9%, p=0.014). Group 1 (antiplatelets) had a crude PDR of 54.6%, which was slightly higher than that of the controls (51.9%) and reached statistical significance in the univariate comparison (p=0.021). Despite these differences in PDR, the ADR (proportion with ≥1 adenoma) was similar among all groups: ADR was 27.6% in group 1, 30.9% in group 2 and 28.3% in group 3 (p=0.511 and p=0.346 for comparisons of groups 1 vs 3 and 2 vs 3, respectively). In addition, there were no significant differences in the anatomical distribution of colorectal neoplasia: the frequency of right-sided versus left-sided CRC among those diagnosed with cancer was not significantly different between groups (p=0.126 for group 1 vs 3; p=0.188 for group 2 vs 3).

Notably, the rate of ‘normal colonoscopy’ (with no findings) was significantly lower in the antithrombotic therapy groups. Only ~28.3% of group 1 and 23.8% of group 2 had a normal colonoscopy, compared with 34.2% of controls (p<0.001 for both comparisons). This suggests that patients on antithrombotic medications were less likely to have no findings, potentially because minor lesions (such as small polyps or bleeding stigmata) were more often detected in the context of antithrombotic-associated bleeding. As mentioned, diverticulosis was more frequently observed in groups 1 and 2, consistent with the older age and higher comorbidity. Importantly, when comparing the two treatment groups directly (group 1 versus group 2), no significant differences were observed in colonoscopy outcomes; antiplatelet therapy and anticoagulation therapy yielded generally similar findings regarding polyp, adenoma and CRC detection rates.

In a secondary analysis, we combined groups 1 and 2 (any antithrombotic therapy, n=2201) and compared them with group 3 (control, n=24 079) to increase statistical power (see table 3 for this combined analysis). As expected, the combined antithrombotic group was older on average than the controls (65.9±7 vs 61.0±8 years, p<0.001) and had a higher proportion of men (65.2% vs 50.1%, p<0.001). Comorbidities such as DM (24.4% vs 9.0%), hypertension (39.6% vs 20.5%) and CIHD (7.5% vs 0.5%) were markedly more prevalent among patients on antithrombotic therapy than controls (all p<0.001). The combined antithrombotic group showed a significantly higher overall PDR (55.5% had ≥1 polyp) compared with the controls (51.9%, p=0.001). However, the overall ADR did not differ between these groups (28.2% vs 28.3%, p=0.950). There was also a higher incidence of diverticulosis in the antithrombotic-treated patients (24% vs 17.4%, p<0.001). No significant difference was observed in CRC detection rates between the combined therapy group and controls (2.0% vs 1.7%, p=0.182), and the distribution of CRC by colon subsite (right vs left colon) was not significantly different (p=0.073).

To control for potential confounding by age and sex, we performed a matched case–control analysis, pairing 2201 antithrombotic users with 2200 controls matched for age (within ±3 years) and sex (table 4). After matching, the mean age was similar between groups (65.9±7 vs 66.4±7.9 years; p=0.069), and gender distribution was identical (65.2% men in both groups; p=0.984). Importantly, the colonoscopy outcomes that were significantly different in the crude analysis became non-significant after matching. PDR were no longer significantly different (55.5% vs 53.1%; p=0.112), and the previously significant difference in diverticular disease prevalence was eliminated (24.0% vs 24.3%; p=0.799). Normal colonoscopy rates also became similar between the groups (27.5% vs 29.6%; p=0.123). ADRs remained comparable (28.2% vs 26.7%; p=0.269), and CRC detection rates were identical (2.0% vs 2.0%; p=0.998). However, comorbidities remained significantly more prevalent in the antithrombotic group even after matching, including DM (24.4% vs 11.1%; p<0.001), hypertension (39.6% vs 23.8%; p<0.001) and CIHD (7.5% vs 0.4%; p<0.001).

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort study, we examined the impact of chronic antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy on PDR and other colonoscopic findings in patients undergoing colonoscopy for a positive FIT. Our results demonstrate significant baseline differences between patients on antithrombotic therapy and those not on such therapy, particularly in age and comorbidity profiles. Consistent with these differences, crude (unadjusted) PDR were higher in patients on antithrombotic agents than in controls. Notably, patients on anticoagulation therapy had a higher overall PDR (59.5%) than controls (51.9%, p=0.014). This finding supports the hypothesis that a bleeding tendency in antithrombotic users may ‘unmask’ underlying lesions, causing them to test positive on FIT and be detected on subsequent colonoscopy. Importantly, our matched case–control analysis (table 4) provides compelling evidence that the observed crude differences in colonoscopy outcomes were primarily due to confounding by age and sex rather than a direct effect of antithrombotic therapy. After matching 2201 antithrombotic users with 2200 controls for age and sex, all previously significant associations with colonoscopy findings became non-significant. Specifically, PDR were no longer different (55.5% vs 53.1%; p=0.112), and the substantial difference in diverticulosis prevalence observed in the crude analysis (24.0% vs 17.4%; p<0.001) was eliminated after matching (24.0% vs 24.3%; p=0.799). This finding is particularly significant as it demonstrates that the higher diverticulosis rates in antithrombotic users are attributable to their older age profile rather than medication effects. This is a critical point: despite differences in crude polyp detection, the detection of adenomas (the primary targets of screening, as precursors to CRC) was equivalent between patients on antithrombotics and those not on these medications. Similarly, CRC detection rates did not differ significantly, nor did the distribution of identified cancers between the right and left colon vary by the use of antithrombotics. The matched analysis fundamentally changes the clinical interpretation of our findings. While crude analysis suggested that antithrombotic therapy was associated with higher polyp detection and diverticulosis rates, the matched analysis reveals that patient demographics entirely explain these associations. This has important implications for FIT interpretation: the lower PPV of FIT in antithrombotic users appears to be related to patient characteristics (older age and male predominance) rather than the effects of the medication per se. Clinicians should therefore focus on patient age and sex when interpreting FIT results, rather than assuming that antithrombotic therapy itself significantly alters colonoscopy yields.

Our results align with a growing body of evidence on this topic, but importantly, provide new insights through our matched case–control analysis. A recent nationwide Danish study by Rasmussen et al examined over 550 000 individuals and found that antithrombotic therapy was associated with a higher FIT positivity rate (11.8% in users vs 6.0% in non-users).13 Notably, non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants were linked to the most significant increase in FIT positivity (adjusted relative risk ~2.4). Despite the rise in positive tests, the same study reported only a slightly lower PPV of FIT for CRC in antithrombotic users (6.0% vs 6.4% in non-users). However, our matched analysis goes beyond these findings by demonstrating that even among FIT-positive patients, the apparent differences in colonoscopy outcomes are primarily due to demographic confounding rather than medication effects. After adjusting for age and sex, our study revealed that the PDR were no longer significantly different between antithrombotic users and controls (55.5% vs 53.1%; p=0.112), thereby fundamentally challenging the assumption that antithrombotics directly increase polyp detection.

Most significantly, our matched analysis revealed that the substantial difference in diverticulosis prevalence observed in the crude analysis (24.0% vs 17.4%; p<0.001) was eliminated after controlling for age and sex (24.0% vs 24.3%; p=0.799). This finding is significant as it demonstrates that the higher diverticulosis rates in antithrombotic users—a key contributor to false-positive FIT results—are entirely attributable to patient age rather than medication effects. These findings from our matched analysis provide a more nuanced understanding than previous studies. While antithrombotics may increase FIT positivity rates, as shown by Rasmussen et al, the colonoscopic findings in FIT-positive patients are primarily determined by patient demographics rather than the effects of medication.

Furthermore, our study’s conclusions are supported by other recent investigations, though our matched analysis provides more substantial evidence for demographic confounding. Cohen et al reported broadly similar outcomes among patients with positive FITs, finding that those on antithrombotic therapy did not have significantly different rates of detecting colorectal neoplasia compared with those not on treatment. Nakamura et al likewise found that antithrombotic agents did not substantially alter FIT sensitivity or specificity for colorectal neoplasia. Our study, which includes over 26 000 FIT-positive patients and features a robust matched case–control analysis of 2201 pairs, provides the most substantial evidence to date that demographic factors, rather than medication effects, drive the observed differences in colonoscopy outcomes. The evidence indicates that while antithrombotic medications may increase the likelihood of a positive stool test due to minor bleeding, the matched analysis demonstrates that they do not independently affect the prevalence of colonoscopic findings once age and sex are controlled for.

It is worth noting that earlier studies, such as the one by Sawhney et al, suggested a reduction in FIT’s PPV in patients taking these medications and recommended temporary cessation of antithrombotics to improve test specificity. However, given the more recent evidence to the contrary, the consensus has shifted towards the view that the benefits of continued antithrombotic therapy (avoiding thromboembolic events) outweigh the downsides of a slightly higher false-positive rate on FIT. Our findings strongly support this contemporary view by demonstrating through matched analysis that antithrombotic therapy per se does not compromise adenoma or cancer detection and that the apparent associations in crude analysis are entirely explained by patient age and sex characteristics.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, there is potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Patients on antithrombotic therapy were older and had more comorbidities, which might influence colonoscopy outcomes. We attempted to control for this by using matched analyses, but unmeasured factors may still exist. Second, the study population is from Israel and may not be fully generalisable to other regions or screening programmes. Third, minor polyps and inflammatory markers were defined by report review, which may be subject to reporting bias. Finally, we did not have information on medication adherence or over-the-counter antithrombotic use that was not captured in the medical record. Despite these limitations, the large sample size and multicentre design enhance the validity of the findings.

Interpretation

Our findings indicate that the crude differences in colonoscopy outcomes between antithrombotic users and non-users are primarily explained by demographic differences (age and sex) rather than medication effects. After matching, PDR, ADRs and CRC detection did not differ between groups. This is consistent with recent reports (eg, Cohen et al and Nakamura et al13). We therefore conclude that continuing antithrombotic therapy during FIT-based screening does not compromise the yield of clinically significant lesions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, demographic confounding, not antithrombotic therapy per se, explains the initial associations between antithrombotic use and colonoscopy outcomes. Our matched analysis shows no independent effect of antithrombotics on PDR, ADR, CRC detection or normal colonoscopy rates. These results support current practices of not withholding antithrombotic therapy for FIT screening and suggest that risk stratification should focus on age and sex rather than medication status.
