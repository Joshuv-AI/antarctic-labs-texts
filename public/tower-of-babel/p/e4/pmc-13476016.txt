Intraoperative Pulmonary Embolism During Right Atrial Mass Excision: A Case Report on Intraoperative Rescue TEE
Authors: Praveen K. Neema, Barsha Sen, Nagarjuna Panidapu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the case report, Intraoperative Pulmonary Embolism During Right Atrial Mass Excision: A Case Report on Intraoperative Rescue TEE’ published in the recent issue of Annals of Cardiac Anaesthesia.[1] We congratulate the authors on their successful management of intraoperative hemodynamic collapse due to tumor embolization. The report highlights the importance and utility of transthoracic (TTE) and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). We would like to present our perspective on managing patients with a right atrial mass.

The preoperative TTE clearly demonstrated a large, mobile right atrial mass prolapsing across the tricuspid valve, which carries a high risk for life-threatening embolization.[2] This underscores the necessity for meticulous preoperative planning to prevent such a catastrophe until the patient is put on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and to have measures in place to promptly resuscitate the patient should tumor embolization occur before CPB. Although the maneuvers that cause tumor embolization are not well defined, it is conceivable that central venous and pulmonary arterial (PA) catheterization can fragment and embolize the tumor; hence, the central venous catheter should be positioned above the SVC-RA junction, ideally, under ultrasound guidance, and the PA catheter should be avoided.[34] Alternatively, a sheath may be placed in the internal jugular vein, and the central venous catheter can be inserted after tumor excision and before weaning from CPB.

It is well established that forceful myocardial contractility (hyperdynamic circulation) generates strong negative pressure in the ventricles during early diastole.[5] A strong negative pressure or suction can suck the tumor across the tricuspid valve, cause repeated tricuspid valve obstruction, and potentially, precipitate hemodynamic collapse. Therefore, hypovolemia, tachycardia, and increased myocardial contractility, which are known to cause hyperdynamic circulation, should be carefully avoided. By the same logic, vasodilators should also be avoided. In case of hypotension, volume loading and a peripheral vasoconstrictor should be preferred for resuscitation. Inotropic agents should be reserved for extreme hemodynamic instability and should be used only after ensuring adequate fluid resuscitation. Further, precautions should be taken to counter anesthetic agents-induced vasodilation with volume loading and attenuating sympathetic surge with β-blockade or fentanyl prior to stressful procedures, such as laryngoscopy, endotracheal intubation, skin incision, and sternotomy.

If the tumor is large and highly mobile, tricuspid valve obstruction and hemodynamic collapse can be a strong possibility. In such conditions, similar to redo cardiac surgery, securing the femoral vessels to initiate fem-fem CPB in case of urgency should be considered. The authors of the present case report have not described the technique of anesthesia, the precautions taken while inserting the central venous line, or the reasons for hemodynamic collapse. Description of these details will help elucidate the mechanism of hemodynamic collapse and identify the measures that should be taken in case of hemodynamic collapse.

The authors have stated, “In our case, the primary function of TTE was not to definitively diagnose PE but to visualize the embolic mass within the pulmonary artery, confirming its presence and location. This allowed for the timely adjustment of the surgical plan, facilitating rapid intervention. While TTE is not sufficient for diagnosing PE on its own, its ability to visualize embolic events and guide immediate surgical decisions played a pivotal role in the successful outcome of the patient.” Both statements, describe the use of TTE, but the title describes rescue TEE; is it TTE or is it TEE? Further, it was the embolized tumor that was removed from the pulmonary artery; logically, it is not a thrombectomy. Incidentally, the Figures 2 and 3 shown in the case report are labeled intraoperative transthoracic echocardiography; they should be labeled as intraoperative (TEE), midesophageal bicaval view shows a mass at the SVC-RA junction [Figure 2], and the intraoperative (TEE), midesophageal short-axis view shows a mass in the right pulmonary artery.
