Alcohol consumption and oral human papillomavirus infection among men living with HIV: a cross-sectional study from the ULACNet 201 trial

ABSTRACT
Alcohol consumption is recognized as a risk factor for oropharyngeal cancer. However, its role in oral human papillomavirus (HPV) infection among people living with HIV (PLWH) remains unclear. This cross-sectional study analyzed baseline data from 699 men living with HIV (MLWH) enrolled in the multicenter ULACNet-201 trial (NCT04255849) to assess oral HPV infection by drinking behavior, examine the association between alcohol consumption and HPV infection, exploring smoking as a potential effect modifier. Oral gargle specimens were tested for high-risk (HR-HPV) and low-risk (LR-HPV) HPV genotypes using SPF10 PCR-DEIA-LiPA25. Logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratios (ORs, 95% CIs) controlling for key covariates and explored heterogeneity by smoking status. Alcohol use in the past month was reported by 76.4% of participants, with 6% classified as heavy drinkers and 27% as binge drinkers. Oral HPV infection was similar between drinkers and nondrinkers (27.7% vs. 28.5%), with comparable rates for HR-HPV (11% vs. 10.9%) and LR-HPV (18.7% vs. 19.7%). Alcohol consumption was not associated with HR-HPV overall, and exploratory analysis found no evidence of effect modification by smoking status. A possible interaction between alcohol consumption and smoking status was observed for LR-HPV, but these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Introduction

Alcohol consumption is a major global public health concern and a well-established risk factor for seven types of cancer, including oropharyngeal cancer (OPC)1,2. In 2020, alcohol use was associated with approximately 741,300 cancer cases worldwide1,2. Although the risk of developing alcohol-attributable cancers increases proportionally with the amount of alcohol consumed, even consumption of up to two drinks per day can contribute to cancer deaths1,2. Other well-established risk factors for OPC include smoking and high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV), which is responsible for about 46% of OPC cases worldwide3. According to the recent IARC Handbooks on oral cancer prevention, combined exposure to alcohol, tobacco, and HPV-16 may confer at least an additive risk of OPC4. While this evidence relates to cancer risk, it raises the possibility that alcohol may also influence earlier stages in the disease pathway, including oral HR-HPV infection. In a cohort of 3,121 U.S. men (HPV Infection in Men [HIM] study), consuming 16 or more drinks/month was independently associated with oral HR-HPV (adjusted odd ratio [aOR] 1.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.07–2.40)5. The association appeared stronger among non-smokers (aOR 2.57, 95% CI 1.37–4.96) than among current/former smokers (aOR 1.12, 95% CI 0.67–1.88). Whether these relationships are similar in populations with a high prevalence of oral HR-HPV infection, such as people living with HIV (PLWH), remains unclear.

PLWH, and particularly men living with HIV (MLWH), have higher rates of alcohol consumption and of alcohol use disorders compared to the general population6–10. Among PLWH, heavy alcohol consumption, compared with non-drinking, has been associated with lower adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART), a higher risk of adverse HIV-related outcomes, and increased all-cause mortality9,11–14. Concurrent alcohol and tobacco use may further worsen these effects15. Moreover, heavy alcohol use is associated with high-risk sexual behaviors as well as higher prevalence of sexually transmitted infections, which may partly explain the elevated prevalence and incidence of oral HPV infection among PLWH16–26. Compared with the general population, PLWH also have a significantly higher risk of developing OPC (risk ratio [RR] 2.7; 95% CI: 2.10–3.58)27–29. However, the relationship between alcohol consumption and oral HR-HPV infection among PLWH has been poorly studied, and existing findings are inconsistent, with few studies accounting for confounders or potential effect modifiers16–18,21–26.

This study aimed to address this gap by pursuing three objectives. First, we evaluated the detection of oral HPV infection according to alcohol use among MLWH enrolled in the US-Latin American-Caribbean HIV/HPV-Cancer Prevention Clinical Trials Network (ULACNet)−201 trial30. Second, we examined the independent association between alcohol consumption and oral HR-HPV infection. Third, as an exploratory aim, we assessed whether smoking status modified the eventual association. We hypothesized that drinkers would have a higher oral HR-HPV detection compared with non-drinkers and that increasing levels of alcohol consumption would be independently associated with higher odds of oral HR-HPV infection, and that smoking status would further modify this relationship.

No significant differences in the distribution of drinkers and nondrinkers were observed across sociodemographic, HIV-related, and sexual behavioral characteristics (Table 1). In contrast, the distribution of drinkers and nondrinkers differed significantly across smoking-related categories. Use of marijuana in the past year was reported by 37% of participants who reported alcohol consumption in the past month, compared to 19% of those who did not report alcohol consumption (p = 0.002). Similarly, participants who reported alcohol consumption in the past month were more likely to be current smokers (28%) and former (21%) smokers than those who did not report that consumption (18% and 16%, respectively) (p = 0.002). There were no significant differences in alcohol consumption in the median pack-years, cumulative smoking years, or number of cigarettes smoked per day. In the past month, MLWH reported a median of two drinking days (IQR 1, 4), five total drinks (IQR 1, 18), and two drinks per occasion (IQR 1, 5); 6% of the participants were classified as heavy drinkers, and 27% met criteria for binge drinking. Alcohol consumption metrics varied significantly by country.

The highest median number of drinking days was reported among participants from Brazil (4; IQR 1–7), followed by Puerto Rico (2; IQR 0.5–5) and Mexico (1; IQR 0–3) (p < 0.001). Similarly, participants from Brazil had the highest median number of drinks consumed in the past month (9; IQR 1–30), whereas similar values were observed among those from Puerto Rico (4; IQR 1–18) and Mexico (4; IQR 0–12) (p < 0.001). The highest proportion of heavy drinkers (> 60 drinks/month) was also reported in Brazil (11%), followed by Puerto Rico (6%) and Mexico (4%) (p < 0.001). In contrast, the median number of drinks per occasion was similar across countries (2; p = 0.425), and the detection of binge drinking (> 5 drinks/occasion) was similar among participants from Brazil (29%) and Mexico (27%), compared to Puerto Rico (16%) (p = 0.282). Supplementary Figure S1 shows the types of alcohol consumed in the past month by country. Beer was the most commonly consumed beverage across all countries, followed by wine, liquor, cocktails, and wine coolers, with some significant differences by country.

HIV-related clinical variables were examined according to the number of drinks consumed in the past month and smoking status, and no major differences were observed between groups (Supplementary Table S2).

Oral HPV infection by alcohol consumption status

The detection of HPV oral infection was 27.7% among men reporting alcohol use and 28.5% among non-users (p = 0.926) (Fig. 1). Specifically, HR-HPV was identified in 11% of drinkers and 10.9% of nondrinkers (p = 1), and low-risk HPV (LR-HPV) in 18.7% and 19.7%, respectively (p = 0.883). None of the alcohol-related variables were significantly associated with oral HR-HPV or LR-HPV infection (Supplementary Table S3). By contrast, the detection of LR-HPV infection increased across smoking categories, from 14% among never smokers to 21% among former smokers and 28% among current smokers (p = 0.001) (Supplementary Table S3). Table 3 presents the results of logistic regression analyses examining the association between the number of drinks consumed in the past month and oral HR-HPV and LR-HPV among MLWH, overall, and stratified by smoking status, among 677 participants with complete information on alcohol consumption in the past month and smoking status.

Overall, higher alcohol intake showed no clear association with either LR- or HR-HPV outcome (Table 3). For HR-HPV, the crude OR was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.52–1.56) for 3–15 drinks and 0.66 (95% CI: 0.35–1.22) for ≥ 16 drinks; for LR-HPV, the crude OR was 1.30 (95% CI: 0.81–2.09) and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.54–1.54), respectively. After adjustment for age, country, lifetime number of female partners, and smoking status, estimates were similar and remained non-significant: for HR-HPV, the adjusted OR (aOR) was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.52–1.60) for 3–15 drinks and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.37–1.38) for ≥ 16 drinks; for LR-HPV, aOR was 1.11 (95% CI: 0.68–1.84) and 0.95 (95% CI: 0.53–1.68), respectively (Table 3). Supplementary Tables S4 and S5 present univariate logistic regression analyses for each variable included in the adjusted models, relating to oral HR-HPV and LR-HPV infection. Further adjustment for AIDS-defining conditions did not significantly change these estimates (Supplementary Table S6).

Comparisons between the 677 included and the 22 excluded participants from the model showed some differences in age, country, and smoking-related variables, but not in alcohol exposure in the HR-HPV (Supplementary Tables S7) and LR-HPV models (Supplementary Table S8). Estimates were similar between the complete-case and imputed models, suggesting that missing data were unlikely to have substantially altered the main conclusions presented in Table 3 (Supplementary Tables S9 and S10).

In the explorative stratified analyses (Table 3), consuming ≥ 16 drinks was not significantly associated with HR-HPV among never smokers (aOR = 1.14; 95% CI: 0.46–2.65) or current/former smokers (aOR = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.14–1.04), and the interaction term was not significant (RERI = − 0.61; 95% CI: − 2.32 to 0.41; p = 0.381). In contrast, for LR-HPV consumption of ≥ 16 drinks was associated with lower odds among never smokers (aOR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.09–0.90) and higher odds among current/former smokers (aOR = 2.02; 95% CI: 0.91–4.69). In exploratory interaction analysis, the positive RERI (1.22; 95% CI: 0.11–2.55; p = 0.050) suggested a possible additive interaction, indicating that the joint effect of smoking and alcohol consumption on oral LR-HPV infection may exceed the sum of their individual effects.

Discussion

The main findings of this study correspond to its three objectives. First, we observed a high prevalence of alcohol consumption in the past month among MLWH enrolled in the ULACNet-201 trial. Second, after adjustment for confounders, alcohol consumption, irrespective of quantity consumed, was not associated with oral HR-HPV infection. Third, smoking status did not significantly modify the association between alcohol consumption and oral HR-HPV infection. These findings are particularly relevant because HR-HPV is the type of greatest clinical importance, as persistent infection is the pathway most closely related to cancer development31. Interpreting these findings in the context of previous studies and the limitations of the present study may help clarify their significance.

Unlike previous studies conducted among PLWH in the United States, which reported alcohol consumption in the past month at approximately 53–54%, a higher proportion was observed in our study population7,8. MLWH who reported alcohol consumption in the past month were significantly more likely to report also marijuana use, tobacco smoking, and having more than 100 lifetime male sexual partners. This finding aligns with previous evidence showing that men who have sex with men tend to have higher use of alcohol and tobacco compared with heterosexual men and the general population32. However, only 6% of participants were classified as heavy drinkers, defined as consuming 60 or more drinks per month, with a median of five drinks and two drinking days reported in the past month. This finding is consistent with results from a study conducted among PLWH in the United States in 2006, which reported a proportion of heavy drinkers of 8%8. Additionally, 27% of participants reported consuming five or more drinks on a single occasion (binge drinking), a proportion not markedly different from that reported among PLWH (32%), and in the general population (23%) of the United States10. MLWH in our study reported alcohol use patterns similar to those observed among men without HIV enrolled in the HIM Study in Brazil, Mexico, and the United States, among whom 10% were heavy drinkers, and 21% reported binge drinking5. These behavioral patterns may reflect the trial’s inclusion criteria, which were generally less compatible with heavy alcohol use, as MLWH were eligible only if they had been on ART for at least six months and were willing to adhere to the vaccination schedule and follow-up visits30.

Overall, self-reported alcohol consumption in the past month, used as a measure of recent drinking behavior, was not associated with oral HR-HPV infection among MLWH in either unadjusted or adjusted multivariable models. HR-HPV infection is strongly influenced by HIV-related immunologic factors33,34. Adjustment for AIDS-defining conditions did not alter the estimates, suggesting that these factors are unlikely to explain the lack of association observed. Moreover, some variables, including sexual behaviors, may lie on the causal pathway between alcohol consumption and oral HPV infection; adjusting for them could result in overadjustment and reduction of the associations. Previous studies conducted in PLWH have reported inconsistent results, showing both higher and lower odds of oral HPV infection among drinkers compared with nondrinkers, but most of these differences were not statistically significant17,24–26. Also, authors rarely specified whether differences were observed by HPV genotype. This aspect is particularly important, as persistent oral HR-HPV infection represents the necessary precursor for the development of OPC, and viral-immunologic factors may be more relevant for oncogenic than non-oncogenic types33–35. Vianna et al. observed genotype-specific patterns, reporting higher odds of HR-HPV (OR = 1.82; 95% CI: 0.35–9.54) and lower odds of LR-HPV (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.07–1.73) among drinkers compared with nondrinkers; however, neither association reached statistical significance26. Interpretation of these findings is further limited by the use of an assay with known sensitivity and specificity issues, making it less comparable to PCR-based detection method36.

Most of the cited studies relied on univariate analyses, and only a few adjusted their models for potential confounders. Riddell et al. reported that, among PLWH, current alcohol consumption was associated with a lower detection of any oral HPV infection after adjusting for sex (aPR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.32–0.86)18. In contrast, Gaester et al. reported that any oral HPV infections were not significantly associated with current alcohol use (aOR = 0.99; 95% CI: 0.94–1.03), but were independently associated with current smoking (aOR = 10.04; 95% CI: 1.98–5.92)23. However, neither study stratified analyses by HPV genotype nor evaluated smoking as a potential effect modifier of the relationship between alcohol use and oral HPV infection, aspects addressed in the present analysis18,23. Current smoking and intensity have been independently associated with oral HPV infection and persistence in the general population37 and in PLWH23,38. Epidemiologic studies of HPV-related OPC have shown that the combined exposure to smoking and drinking increases cancer risk synergistically, far more than either exposure alone39,40. In this context, smoking may influence the pathways through which alcohol is associated with susceptibility to oral HPV infection. Previous studies have suggested that alcohol and tobacco may share biological effects, such as mucosal irritation, increased epithelial permeability, and impairment of local immune responses, which may facilitate HPV acquisition or persistence1,40,41. However, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study. Therefore, exploring smoking as an effect modifier provides insight into the potential joint effects of these exposures on oral HPV infection odds. In the HIM study of 3,121 men without HIV, 16 or more drinks in the past month was associated with higher odds of oral HR-HPV (aOR 1.60, 95% CI 1.07–2.40), with a stronger association among non-smokers (aOR 2.57, 95% CI 1.37–4.96) than among current or former smokers (aOR 1.12, 95% CI 0.67–1.88); no clear differences were observed for LR-HPV in smoking-stratified analyses5. By contrast, in the current study, no significant associations or interactions were observed between the same level of alcohol consumption and HR-HPV. For LR-HPV, the additive interaction was positive but borderline statistically significant (RERI 1.22; 95% CI: 0.11–2.55; p = 0.050), suggesting a possible pattern rather than a definitive synergistic effect. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the small number of participants in our analysis, particularly among drinkers who were current or former smokers, which may have resulted in imprecise estimates. Therefore, the observed pattern may reflect chance or residual confounding rather than a true protective effect. Furthermore, HR-HPV was the primary outcome of the study, and the stratified analyses should be considered exploratory.

Although our findings did not support a clear association between alcohol consumption and oral HR-HPV, any observed subgroup patterns in stratified analyses were exploratory. Nonetheless, integrated alcohol and tobacco counseling may still be useful within the HIV care setting42. Given the established association between alcohol consumption and increased cancer risk, counseling to promote moderation in alcohol use should still be considered as part of comprehensive HIV care. Similarly, all patients should be routinely screened for tobacco use at each healthcare encounter, and those who smoke should be strongly encouraged to quit and offered evidence-based smoking cessation support42.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the inclusion criteria for the parent trial likely selected participants of older age who were stable on antiretroviral therapy and engaged in regular clinical follow-up, reflecting a group with overall healthier behaviors. This may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader PLWH population, particularly younger individuals or those with less consistent access to care. Second, a cross-sectional study design prevents inference of temporal or causal relationships between alcohol consumption and oral HPV infection, as exposure and outcome were assessed at the same time. Third, alcohol consumption and other behavioral variables were self-reported, which may have introduced recall, misclassification, or social desirability bias. Fourth, because participants coinfected with both HR and LR-HPV types are classified exclusively in the HR-HPV group, the LR-HPV–only category may underestimate the true burden of LR infections, while the HR-HPV category becomes more heterogeneous by including infections partly driven by LR types. This misclassification is expected to be non-differential with respect to smoking and would likely bias associations for both outcomes toward the null, potentially attenuating true effect estimates. Fifth, because the number of HR-HPV–positive cases was limited, particularly within alcohol and smoking subgroups, the stratified and interaction analyses lacked sufficient power, and the resulting estimates should be interpreted with caution. Finally, despite adjustment for multiple potential confounders, unmeasured or residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

While this study examined the association between alcohol consumption and the detection of oral HPV genotypes, future investigations should evaluate its impact on incidence and persistent infection to further elucidate the natural history of oral HPV, particularly HR-HPV. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings, particularly for incident infection. For example, Beachler et al. reported that, among MLWH, heavy alcohol consumption (≥ 2 drinks per day) was independently associated with a higher risk of incident oral HPV infection after adjusting for several confounders (aHR = 3.3; 95% CI: 2.0–5.6)16. Conversely, D’Souza et al. found that incident oral HPV infection, regardless of HIV status, was independently associated with current smoking (aHR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.05–1.56), but not with current alcohol use (HR = 1.11; 95% CI: 0.89–1.38)22. In this context, our findings, showing no strong association between alcohol consumption and oral HPV detection, further highlight the need for larger longitudinal studies in PLWH that focus on prevalence and incidence of oral HR-HPV and incorporate multivariable and interaction analyses to clarify the role of alcohol across different stages of infection.

In conclusion, although most MLWH reported alcohol consumption in the past month, alcohol use, independently of quantity consumed, was not significantly associated with oral HR-HPV infection in this population. Overall, the exploratory stratified findings by smoking status were inconsistent across subgroups and may reflect limited statistical power or chance, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future larger longitudinal studies should examine how alcohol and smoking behaviors, both modifiable risk factors, influence the acquisition and persistence of oral HR-HPV infection in PLWH. Such evidence could inform targeted prevention strategies to reduce the risk of persistent HR-HPV infection and, ultimately, the development of OPC.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study using baseline data from the randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled ULACNet-201 trial (NCT04255849), the protocol of which has been previously described30.

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

MLWH from Brazil, Mexico, and Puerto Rico were enrolled in the ULACNet-201 trial in the age group 20–50 years, had been on antiretroviral treatment for at least six months, were sexually active within the past six months, and were willing to adhere to the vaccine schedule and attend all follow-up visits30. Participants were excluded if they had previously received HPV vaccination, had a history of HPV-related diseases or cancer, experienced an AIDS-defining condition within the last six months, had a known allergy to any vaccine component, were receiving immunosuppressant therapy, or had received blood products. The ULACNet-201 trial includes 700 participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was obtained by the human subject committees of the University of São Paulo in Brazil, the Instituto Nacional de Salud Publica in Mexico, the University of Puerto Rico, and the School of Medicine of Puerto Rico.

Data and oral sample collection

For this cross-sectional analysis, only specific characteristics collected through a self-administered questionnaire were considered. These included socio-demographic factors (age, country of residence); HIV history and viral-immunologic measures (previous AIDS-defining condition, nadir CD4 count, baseline CD4 count, baseline HIV viral load); sexual behavior (age at sexual debut, lifetime number of female and male sexual partners, lifetime number of people kissed); smoking-related variables (marijuana use in the past year, tobacco smoking status, pack-years, cumulative years of smoking, cigarettes per day); and alcohol consumption. Participants were informed that a standard alcoholic drink was defined as one beer, one glass of wine, one wine cooler, one cocktail, or one shot of liquor. Participants were asked whether they had consumed alcohol in the past month, the number of drinking days, and the average number of drinks per occasion. The type of alcohol (beer, wine, wine cooler, cocktail, liquor, or other) was also recorded. From these responses, four main alcohol consumption measures were derived: (1) alcohol consumption (binary), (2) number of drinking days (continuous), (3) number of drinks per occasion (continuous), and (4) total drinks per month (continuous), calculated as drinking days × average drinks per occasion. Oral gargle specimens were obtained through a 30-second rinse and gargle, as previously described30. CD4 cell count and plasma viral load were obtained from blood samples analyzed at local clinical laboratories with CLIA certification or equivalent accreditation, as described previously30.

HPV detection and genotyping

Oral gargle specimens were analyzed using the SPF10 PCR-DEIA-LiPA25 system (DDL Diagnostic Laboratory, Rijswijk, Netherlands)30. High-risk HPV (HR-HPV) genotypes included 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, and 68. Low-risk HPV (LR-HPV) genotypes included 6, 11, 34, 40, 42, 43, 44, 53, 54, 66, 70, 73, and 74. Samples that showed positive DNA amplification without type-specific identification were classified as unclassified HPV and included within the LR-HPV category.

Statistical analysis

Consistent with the study’s aim and objectives, among 700 participants enrolled in the ULACNet-201 trial, only those with available data on both alcohol consumption and smoking status were included, resulting in an analytic sample of 699 individuals. Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics according to their alcohol consumption status, as previously described for the overall population30. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with first and third quartiles (Q1, Q3) representing the interquartile range (IQR). Alcohol-related variables were categorized using the same definitions applied in the HIM Study conducted among men without HIV, to allow direct comparison between studies: 0–1, 2–5, ≥ 6 days for the number of days alcohol consumed per month, 0–2, 3–15, ≥ 16 drinks per month for the number of drinks consumed per month, and 0–1, 2–3, ≥ 4 drinks for the number of drinks consumed on each occasion5. Following CDC parameters, binary variables were created for heavy drinking (≥ 60 drinks/month, equivalent to 15 drinks/week) and binge drinking (≥ 5 drinks/occasion)43. Group differences by alcohol consumption status, overall and by country, were assessed with Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher tests for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables.

The primary outcome was HR-HPV infection, defined as positivity for one or more HR-HPV types. When both HR-HPV and LR-HPV were positive, we counted them as HR-HPV positive, and they were not included in LR-HPV analyses. LR-HPV infection was defined as positivity for LR-HPV types in the absence of HR-HPV. Thus, the HR-HPV-positive and LR-HPV-positive groups were mutually exclusive. Associations between the categorized alcohol-related variables and primary outcomes were examined using Pearson’s chi-squared, Wilcoxon rank sum tests, and Mantel-Haenszel test for trend, as appropriate. Logistic regression models were used to assess the independent association between the number of drinks consumed in the past month (categorical exposure variable) and oral HPV infection outcomes, with HR-HPV and LR-HPV modelled as binary outcomes compared with HPV-negative individuals. For the HR-HPV analysis, 9 participants (1%) did not report the number of drinks consumed in the past month, while for the LR-HPV analysis, 8 participants (1%) did not provide this information. All participants provided data on tobacco smoking. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs), controlling for potential demographic, socio-economic, and behavioural variables as potential confounders, were estimated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), using the lowest category of alcohol consumption (0–2 drinks per month) as the reference category. Backward elimination (p < 0.157) was applied, keeping age and country in all models, with Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) used to select the most parsimonious model. Both the lifetime number of female and male sexual partners were initially considered; AIC-based selection retained female partners as providing the best model fit. The final models included age (continuous), country (categorical), lifetime number of female sexual partners (categorical), and smoking status (categorical) and were based on 677 participants with complete data on all covariates. We compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants for the HR-HPV and LR-HPV analyses and performed a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation for missing covariates, comparing these results with the primary complete-case analysis.

Effect modification by smoking status was evaluated in an exploratory analysis using two approaches: stratified logistic regression for never vs. current/former smokers, and joint exposure analysis combining smoking status with monthly alcohol intake (0–15 vs. ≥16 drinks). Effect modification was assessed on the additive scale using the Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI), which was calculated along with 95% CI. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. R version 4.4.0 was used for the analyses.
