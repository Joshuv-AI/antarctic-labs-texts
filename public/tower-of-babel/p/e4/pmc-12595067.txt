An innovative approach to welding robot operator reliability analysis using extended TESEO and HEART methods
Authors: Michał Pałęga, Mariusz Salwin, Tomasz M Chmielewski, Piotr Przybyłowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paper presents a comprehensive identification and assessment of occupational hazards related to the operation of robotic welding systems. New technology fundamentally improves quality and accuracy of welds, especially precise narrow seams connecting thin sheets or profiles. The paper draws attention to typical occupational hazards occurring in the workplace, as well as those resulting from the use of robotic welding devices. Risk assessment was performed using the commonly used RISK SCORE method. Moreover, as part of our own research, the employee’s reliability levels were determined by determining the probability of making a mistake. The human factor reliability study was carried out using HRA (Human Reliability Analysis) methods. The classic TESEO and HEART approaches have been expanded to include additional factors, such as human-machine interface (HMI), cognitive load, quality of documentation and procedures, and task ambiguity. This addition addresses a significant methodological gap in classic HRA analyses, which have so far overlooked these key aspects that influence the effectiveness and safety of human-robot interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26357-7.Keywords: Robotic welding, Welding robot operator, Occupational safety management, Human reliability, Occupational risk, Innovative HRA in a robotic environment, Extended TESEO and HEART techniquesSubject terms: Engineering, Health occupations, Mathematics and computing, Risk factors

Introduction

Good operation management and maintaining a high level of reliability of devices and machines at work stations are essential to ensure safety and good hygiene at work, which is the basis for effective risk management and health protection of employees1–3. Machines and devices kept in good technical condition significantly reduce the risk of accidents and failures, which contributes to creating a safe work space4–6. Effective use of workstations is also crucial to streamlining operational processes, which directly reduces employees’ exposure to potential hazards6–8. The need for regular inspection, careful monitoring of technical condition and provision of appropriate training in the use of equipment are necessary to ensure continued safety and reliability at work stations9–11. As a consequence, adequate management of the functioning of workstations and ensuring their reliability have a direct impact on improving the working environment, which translates into increasing the efficiency of the entire enterprise and shaping a healthy, safe workplace12–14.

Automation of production processes has been observed for many decades15,16. The presence of robots on production lines replacing activities previously performed by humans is becoming more and more common17,18. Initially, this automation was limited to simple and monotonous tasks, but technological progress now allows robots to perform highly complex operations, such as welding19–21.

Welding is a field in which the use of robots was initiated relatively early, which contributed to improving the quality of manufactured products and compliance with applicable standards2,12,22. Robotization of welding processes allows you to achieve the necessary precision and uniformity of results23–25. Modern welding stations often use multi-robot systems, where precise connection of components plays a key role26,27. The scale and scope of use of welding robots depend on the specific nature and production goals. For example, the assembly of large-sized structures requires advanced, multi-robot welding systems28,29.

Welding robots are selected taking into account a number of parameters, such as the number of programmable axes, operational range, accuracy of movement repetition and the possibility of programming additional functions28,30. The robot is integrated with a power source and a single- or double-station welding Tables12,31,32. It is important that the power source allows accurate measurements and corrections in real time, which is crucial when using welding techniques such as MIG, MAG, or TIG, where process control is implemented through digital communication interfaces of robots33,34.

This approach to automation in the welding industry not only increases the efficiency and quality of production, but also affects work safety by reducing the risk of errors and product non-compliance35–37.

In the context of automated welding stations, the configuration of the number of welding tables plays a key role in the efficiency of the production process. Two-station tables, allowing simultaneous welding of elements on one table and collection of finished parts on the other, are commonly used to ensure uninterrupted work and optimize process flow. It is crucial that the design of such stations is preceded by a detailed analysis, taking into account the specific requirements of production systems38,39.

Robotization of the welding process contributes to a significant increase in work safety by minimizing the operator’s direct contact with harmful factors, such as high temperature, bright light, and emitted toxic substances40,41. Long-term exposure to these factors in traditional welding can lead to health problems42,43.

Automation of the welding process not only improves safety, but also significantly affects work efficiency44,45. Welding robots, characterized by high precision and speed, can work without interruption, which translates into an increase in production46,47. High precision of robot movements guarantees the repeatability of the process, which ensures uniformity and high quality of welds, while reducing the need for further processing and the associated costs23,28.

To sum up, the use of welding robots in industry not only optimizes the production process and ensures continuity of work, but also increases safety and production quality standards35,48. However, the introduction of robotization requires an in-depth analysis and adaptation to the individual needs of the production process, which is key to maximizing the benefits of automation49–51.

In the industrial sector, welding robots are among the most technologically advanced and complex machines, requiring a high level of precision and accuracy to ensure optimal performance. Despite being designed for autonomous welding tasks, these robots require extensive programming and maintenance knowledge and skills to ensure proper operation52–54.

A significant challenge in using welding robots is their calibration and programming. Precisely adjusting welding positions and angles is essential to achieving the desired results, which requires a deep understanding of advanced technologies and tools, including welding software and optical sensors. Furthermore, constant exposure to high temperatures and dust highlights the importance of regular maintenance and maintenance of welding robots to ensure safe and efficient operation. When components fail or become damaged, quick and effective repairs are crucial to prevent additional costs and production delays.

Welding robots contain several hazardous elements, such as electric arcs and hot surfaces, requiring operators to be aware of potential hazards and follow safety measures, including the use of appropriate protective clothing and guards.

Taking into account the above considerations, there is a justified need for scientific research in the field of identification and assessment of occupational hazards related to the work of welding robots and the reliability of operators. This study used the Risk Score method to assess the occupational risk faced by welding robot operators. Additionally, two methodologies were used to analyze human reliability: TESEO and HEART.

It should be emphasized that the standard TESEO and HEART methodologies only consider the fundamental factors that determine the probability of human error. Given the increasing automation of production and industrial processes, positions such as those of an automatic robot welder are inadequate and require consideration of specific working conditions. According to the authors, the previously used TESEO and HEART methods for assessing human reliability do not fully account for the ergonomic and cognitive factors characteristic of automated workstations. In response to the identified research gap, the authors propose an innovative extension of these methods to include four additional factors: user interface (HMI), cognitive load, quality of technical documentation, and task ambiguity.

The proposed extension of the TESEO and HEART methods goes beyond previous HRA adaptations for automated systems by incorporating factors that are specific to modern, robotic workstations. While classic TESEO and HEART methods mainly address general determinants of human error, such as operator experience or time pressure, the extension considers real-world work environment conditions. It accounts for the way human–machine interaction occurs, primarily through visualization systems, system messages, and digital procedures, which are not fully addressed in earlier HRA approaches. This allows for a more accurate assessment of operator reliability in highly automated contexts.

The four additional parameters—user interface (HMI) quality, cognitive load, technical documentation quality, and task ambiguity—significantly complement existing models of cognitive ergonomics and human–automation interaction. Their novelty lies in precisely capturing the cognitive processes that occur during work at automated workstations. Unlike prior models, which largely focus on general human error determinants, these parameters allow the assessment of operator reliability in conditions that reflect actual workstation challenges. This approach addresses contemporary needs in automated work environments, where humans are primarily responsible for monitoring system operation, interpreting transmitted information, and responding to critical situations, thereby providing a meaningful scientific contribution to both HRA methodology and practical industrial applications.

The human-machine interface (HMI) is a fundamental element of communication between the operator and the robot and directly influences the correctness of the performed operations. If improperly designed, it can significantly increase the risk of operator error. In terms of technical system reliability, it is a fundamental element supporting decision-making. This factor is crucial for automated workstations, as it can shorten the operator’s reaction time, reduce cognitive load, and minimize the risk of human error.

Cognitive load complements traditional stress and experience factors and indicates how mental effort (e.g., caution, memory) can influence the probability of error. It is particularly important in automated positions, which are characterized by limited physical effort and an increasing role of cognitive processes. Considering human reliability, it should be noted that excessive cognitive load can lead to human error, especially in emergency and non-standard situations.

The quality of documentation and procedures is another important factor that influences the accuracy of an operator’s work, and thus the risk of error. Proper documentation allows the operator to adhere to established standards and minimizes the risk associated with their own interpretation of how to perform a task. When an operator follows established procedures and procedures, the risk of error is reduced.

Task ambiguity considers the operator’s decision-making in problematic situations (e.g., during a breakdown), which may be non-routine, unclear, and require non-standard solutions. In such situations, operator error is often possible. From a human reliability perspective, clearly defined procedures and ensuring task repeatability are key to minimizing the risk of operator error.

The research conducted is crucial for improving industrial safety, particularly in Industry 4.0 environments characterized by a dynamic pace of work and high levels of system integration and automation. These factors determine the risk of errors resulting from improper human-machine interaction. Incorporating the proposed factors into reliability assessment allows for better identification of critical areas, such as operator information overload, unclear procedures, or suboptimal control panel design. In practice, this could translate into the design of more intuitive HMI systems, optimized staff training and preparation, and improved operational procedures. In a broader perspective, the proposed extension of the TESEO and HEART methods could also contribute to better organization of production lines and minimize the risk of human error. Consequently, this will increase efficiency, production continuity, and employee safety.

Extending the classic approach of the TESEO and HEART methods with the proposed innovative factors enables a more precise representation of operator working conditions in automated work environments and is crucial for assessing the risk of human error and system reliability. Furthermore, they provide a basis for making more informed decisions regarding the design and organization of automated workstations, as well as implementing preventive measures, which can translate into increased safety and efficiency. The proposed methodology represents both scientific and applied value for this publication.

Research methodology

Occupational risk assessment using the risk score method

Occupational risk assessment is the probability of occurrence of undesirable events related to the work performed for each profession55–57. Occupational hazards occurring in the environment or related to the way work is performed may result in losses, including adverse health effects58,59.

Collecting such information is therefore mandatory because its task is to check and assess what in the workplace may harm or cause harm to employees60,61. On its basis, it is verified whether all necessary measures to reduce or eliminate threats have been implemented. Every employer is obliged to prepare this type of data. It is also intended to inform employees about the occupational risks associated with their work and the principles of protection against threats.The Risk Score method developed in the United States meets all international standards for occupational risk assessment. Although the implementation of this method requires the use of four different tables, its main advantages are the transparency of results and minimizing the impact of subjective assessments on the final risk estimates62,63. In Poland, this method is relatively widely used. Many different methods of assessing occupational risk are described in the literature, and the Risk Score method is among the most commonly use63–65.

The Risk Score method expands the concept of occupational risk, defining it as the probability of occurrence of undesirable events related to work performed, which may lead to losses, especially in the health area of employees, as a result of exposure to occupational hazards present in the work environment or related to the methods of performing work. As part of the Risk Score method, occupational risk is classified into two acceptable categories: 1 and 2. Exceeding these categories and entering the scope of category 3 means exceeding the risk acceptability threshold, which implies the need to implement reduction measures aimed at reducing the risk to a level considered acceptable.

The Risk Score method also enables dynamic determination of risk acceptability, which is crucial for strategic planning of preventive actions and improvement of occupational safety standards. Risk acceptance is determined by management decisions, taking into account the company’s economic potential and available resources. The accepted risk level is expressed by a numerical value [R] resulting from the risk assessment process. According to the recommendations of the Risk Score method, the suggested maximum level of risk acceptance is R = 70. As safety conditions improve, organizations are encouraged to continuously and measurably reduce the level of occupational risk below the value of 70 within the acceptable risk category64.

Experimental method for assessing TESEO operator errors

The TESEO (Technica Empirica Stima Errori Operatori) method, or Experimental Method for the Evaluation of Operator Errors, designed by Bello and Colombari in 1980, is an approach based on empirical evaluation. It was developed to estimate the probability of errors made by system operators66,67. The operator’s main task is to perform a specific activity, and the model takes into account five key factors, marked K1 to K5, that may affect the operator’s risk of making an error68,69.

The values of the coefficients for individual factors, predicted by the creators of the method, are taken from tables prepared by them in detail66,68. This model takes into account both internal and external factors affecting the operator, which highlights the complexity of the work environment and the variety of influences that can contribute to errors70,71.

K1—type of task performed,

K2—time available to complete the task (here tentatively called the stress factor),

K3—characteristics of a person acting as a traffic dispatcher, in particular his preparation to perform the function entrusted to him, taking into account education and habits,

K4—emotional state of the operator (here called the anxiety factor),

K5—environmental features, including MMI (here called ergonomic factor).

Supplementing the classic TESEO method with these factors addresses the need to adapt this method to the assessment of automated workstations, which should take into account the interactions between the operator and the technical system72–74. It should be emphasized that these factors result from the characteristics of the automated work environment, where monitoring, programming, and supervision of machine operation are dominant. Correction values for the new factors were determined based on their importance in assessing human reliability and in proportion to the values of the classic factors used in the TESEO method. Additionally, they were verified using expert knowledge through consultations with operators and engineers experienced in working on automated stations, which allowed them to be adapted to real industrial conditions75–77.

The proposed correction values were verified using an expert method, which included consultations with industry representatives and the authors’ knowledge derived from years of observation of automated workstations. Based on the feedback obtained, the correction values were optimized to reflect actual conditions. This approach ensures that the indicated values reflect both theoretical knowledge and empirical evidence (industrial experience).

According to the authors, the classic method fails to account for the specific cognitive, environmental, and decision-making factors inherent in automated work environments. Therefore, to fill the identified research (methodological) gap and adapt the traditional method to contemporary industrial conditions, they proposed extending this method to include four new modifying factors: human-machine interface (HMI) quality, cognitive load, documentation and procedure quality, and task ambiguity85,86. The selection of these factors takes into account their significant impact on safety, work efficiency, and operator reliability. While the proposed factors may appear similar to some classic indicators, their extension with additional, more precisely formulated indicators is fully justified methodologically and from an application perspective, particularly in the context of automated work environments87,88.

Research object and data sources

The analysis involved a production facility that manufactures pressure vessels and steel components for overhead cranes, cranes, and jib cranes. The company has been operating in the market for 15 years and boasts significant welding experience and a stable position in the steel construction industry. Its technological processes utilize standard-quality low-alloy steel and high-strength unalloyed steel as structural materials. The company utilizes the following welding methods: MAG, TIG, MIG, and MMA.

The company’s infrastructure includes 10 pieces of equipment, including six welding machines, a welding robot, thermal cutting equipment (plasma and oxygen), and a shot-blasting facility for surface treatment. The annual production volume is approximately 240 tons, using approximately 300 kg of consumables. Production is conducted in accordance with the requirements of EN ISO 3834 for lifting and pressure structures, ensuring high quality and compliance with industry standards. Welding processes account for approximately 40% of total production, 20% of which is automated. The company employs five welders, four welding supervisors, and six non-destructive testing specialists, ensuring both the correct operation of the processes and systematic quality control.

The average age of the equipment is approximately 10 years, and the machinery is gradually modernized by purchasing new equipment approximately every two years from internal funds. In 2023, the company achieved a turnover of €800,000, reflecting its stable growth and ability to invest in innovative solutions. For example, the purchase of a €50,000 welding robot contributed to increased productivity and laid the foundation for analyzing operator reliability in industrial environments.

It should be emphasized that the company under review collaborates with the Department of Welding at Warsaw University of Technology, which facilitates the exchange of knowledge and experience between industrial practice and academia. This collaboration facilitates the evaluation of applied technological processes and the joint implementation of research on the reliability of welding processes, with particular emphasis on the reliability of welding robot operators.Data sources for the study were multidimensional. The authors primarily utilized internal company materials, including process documentation, work station instructions, and event logs related to the operation of welding systems. Furthermore, a significant element of data acquisition was the authors’ own research, conducted through on-site observations, which allowed them to identify actual operator behavior and identify differences between procedures and practice. This approach was complemented by an analysis of workstation ergonomics, encompassing both physical factors and cognitive factors. The study was conducted based on observations of 60 operators working under typical production conditions. Data was collected over a four-week period, covering various work shifts. The authors aimed to select the sample size and observation time to account for the diversity of operator experience and typical stresses resulting from human-system interactions. This ensured repeatability of the results.

Furthermore, the assessment of human reliability using the HEART and TESEO methods is based on the authors’ specialized knowledge and experience in the field of occupational safety and human reliability. This allowed for the proper assignment of coefficients and parameters in the HEART and TESEO methods. Consequently, it was ensured that the analysis results reflect real-world industrial conditions.

Selecting the appropriate production facility (research facility) collaborating with a research institution, analyzing internal documentation, and observing the workstation allowed us to obtain reliable data necessary to analyze the reliability of welding robot operators. Thanks to this approach, research conducted using the extended HEART and TESEO methods provides reliable results and significantly contributes to the development of methods for assessing human reliability in robotic welding processes.

All procedures used in the research were conducted in accordance with applicable guidelines and legal regulations, ensuring full compliance with ethical standards. The research protocols, in the form of observation sheets for the welding robot operator’s workstation, received prior approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee at the Warsaw University of Technology, confirming their compliance with applicable institutional and regulatory standards. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, confirming their voluntary participation in the research study. Furthermore, they were fully aware of the purpose and conduct of the research study.

Welding robot operator: characteristics of the profession

A person acting as a welding robot operator is responsible for the operation, maintenance and potential programming of welding devices. It is a key element in ensuring the correct course of the welding process using automation, in accordance with applicable technical specifications. Cooperation with the person programming the robot is important, although these duties can also be performed by the operator himself, which is increasingly required by employers, as indicated in job advertisements43,83.

The operator’s responsibilities include preparing materials, managing a welding line consisting of many robots and daily inspection of the technical condition of equipment before and after work. This control covers both visual and auditory aspects. In addition, the operator is obliged to carry out regular maintenance of the robot in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines and to initially assess the quality of the welds21,83.

Labor market analysis also indicates that operators are expected to have the ability to perform welding corrections, which requires additional competences in welding using methods specified by the employer. This requires the operator to have both manual skills and the ability to effectively use available welding tools and equipment.

Therefore, this profession requires not only the ability to operate advanced equipment, but also knowledge of safe work principles and competence in quality control of welds made.

As part of their duties, welding robot operators with appropriate qualifications for various welding methods are required to use specialized protective clothing, including aprons, welding masks or protective glasses, special gloves and shoes. This work is usually performed autonomously, which carries significant responsibility. In the context of operating welding robots included in welding or production lines, the operator may cooperate with other specialists responsible for pre- and post-welding processes. Cooperation with the quality control department is also key.

In large enterprises, the operator’s work takes place in shifts to ensure continuity of production, while in smaller craft and service plants, the single-shift system predominates.

While working, operators are exposed to a number of harmful factors, such as exposure to electric current, magnetic field, ultraviolet radiation, visible radiation and thermal radiation of the electric arc, as well as to dust, fumes and welding gases. Additionally, there is a risk of contact with molten metal spatters and exposure to noise. The effects of these threats may include skin and eye diseases, burns, poisoning, mechanical injuries, headaches, shortness of breath, respiratory diseases, damage to hearing, musculoskeletal organs, and even fractures or spine injuries. Therefore, compliance with occupational health and safety and fire protection regulations is extremely important in the work of a welding robot operator.

Testing the reliability of a welding robot operator using the classic and extended TESEO and HEART methods

Human reliability analysis, also known as human error analysis, plays an important role in identifying and assessing the causes of events caused by human actions. It mainly focuses on assessing the impact of operators on the functioning of processes, systems and machines. In addition, it enables the detection of factors influencing employee behavior and facility safety, taking into account various environmental factors and working conditions. Human reliability analysis is important in assessing the risk resulting from potential human errors and aims to minimize the susceptibility of systems to failure. Human reliability analysis methods assume that identifying and assessing human errors can significantly reduce the risk of failure32–34. This study focuses on the reliability of welding robot operators using the TESCO and HEART methods. The research assumptions and obtained results for both the classic and innovative (proposed by the authors) approaches to assessing human reliability are presented below.

Human reliability analysis was conducted using the classic TESEO method (factors K1–K5) and its extended version (factors K6–K9 proposed by the authors). The aim of comparing the P(E) and P1(E) values was to demonstrate how taking into account additional factors affects the assessment of the risk of error by the welding robot operator.

Based on the comparative analysis of the values of the probability of making an error by the operator P(E) and P1(E), it can be concluded that taking into account additional factors (K6–K9) reduces the risk of human error by the operator. The largest difference in risk values was observed for the following four tasks: preparing components for welding (task 1), configuring the welding robot (task 2), correcting its settings (task 5), and assessing weld quality (task 6). The P(E) values for these tasks were 0.6. However, after considering additional factors, the risk level for these tasks decreased to 0.15 or 0.3, which in some cases represents a risk reduction of up to 75%. It should be emphasized that reducing risk after considering additional factors should not mean ignoring them. On the contrary, if, for example, the interface is unreadable and the documentation is of inadequate quality, these factors will increase the risk of error. Therefore, the proposed factors have a significant impact on the risk associated with human reliability, and therefore on the efficiency and safety of work in automated positions (such as welding robot operators). Furthermore, the obtained research results demonstrate that the proposed innovative approach to the TESEO methodology enables reliable and credible risk analysis and allows for more effective occupational safety management.

The conducted assessment of the welding robot operator’s reliability allows for a comparison of the results obtained using the classic and extended HEART methods. According to the classic HEART method, most tasks performed by the operator are characterized by a moderate level of error risk, with the error probability P(E) ranging from 13% (task 7) to 23% (tasks 1, 2, and 5). The introduction of four new factors, including a poorly designed interface, high cognitive load, insufficient or difficult-to-use documentation, and high task ambiguity, increases the risk of error. A two-fold increase in error risk was observed for tasks related to welding robot setup (tasks 2 and 5). This suggests that non-intuitive interface operation, or cognitive overload, plays a crucial role in assessing operator reliability and the risk of human error, something the classic HEART method did not consider. It should also be noted that not all factors had the same impact on the risk of operator error. Examples include tasks such as removing weld defects – making post-weld corrections (task 7) and the ongoing inspection, cleaning, and maintenance of welding robots and other tools, machines, and devices used during welding (task 8). These tasks are repetitive and less demanding in terms of cognitive load. Based on the research results, it can be concluded that simpler, more standardized tasks have a moderate impact on increasing operator reliability risk. However, this does not mean that these factors should be ignored.

The results of the P1(E) error probability assessment indicate that each activity performed by a welding robot operator carries a high level of risk. This means that traditional preventative measures, such as employee training or procedure communication, are insufficient. In the context of the presented results, elements such as intuitively designed interfaces, automation of critical robot settings, and the implementation of systems that support operator decision-making are crucial. Furthermore, technical documentation should be properly developed, unambiguous, and understandable to the operator. Furthermore, employee training should address not only the operation of the device itself but also issues related to the interpretation of information and proper conduct under conditions of cognitive overload.

This proprietary extension of the classic HEART method provides a reliable and flexible tool that enables a more accurate assessment of human reliability and can also serve as a basis for determining actions to improve automated workstations. Furthermore, it should be noted that the proposed additional factors, such as digital interface quality, cognitive overload, and task ambiguity, reflect the actual working conditions of a welding robot operator. Furthermore, it should be noted that the proposed innovative approach to the HEART method addresses a defined methodological gap and can be implemented in all workstations where employees interact with technical systems, which is particularly relevant given the rapidly evolving automation and digitization of production processes.

Conclusion

The subject of this publication was the identification and quantitative assessment of human reliability in the welding robot operator position, which is characterized by a high level of process automation and digitization. The aim of the research was to develop an innovative, extended methodological approach to the TESEO and HEART methods, which incorporate cognitive and systemic factors that reflect real-world working conditions in an automated work environment.

The research involved a three-stage assessment of occupational safety and operator reliability in industrial settings. This included assessing occupational hazards using the Risk Score method, estimating the probability of human error using the TESEO method, and conducting a detailed error analysis using the HEART method. It should be emphasized that the research on the probability of human error and operator reliability assessment was conducted based on both a classic and innovative methodological approach to the TESEO and HEART techniques.

A preliminary risk assessment using the Risk Score method identified and classified typical occupational hazards encountered in the welding robot operator’s workplace. The risk assessment included 21 physical and environmental factors that could cause mechanical injuries, musculoskeletal strain, and occupational accidents. Most of these factors were assessed as low or acceptable occupational risk, demonstrating the effectiveness of the implemented preventive measures for occupational safety.

However, it should be emphasized that the Risk Score method does not address cognitive load, human interaction with technical systems, or information ergonomics, which is crucial from the perspective of a welding robot operator.

In response to identified methodological shortcomings, the authors proposed extending the classic TESEO and HEART methods to include factors related to the human-machine interface (HMI), cognitive load, quality of documentation and procedures, and task ambiguity. The measurable results of the research are the results of assessing operator reliability using both the classic and extended versions of the TESEO and HEART methods.

Analyzing the results of the TESEO error probability assessment, it can be concluded that additional parameters, such as the human-machine interface, cognitive load, quality of documentation and procedures, and sentence ambiguity, significantly reduced this risk. However, this does not mean that these factors have a neutral impact on human reliability. It should be emphasized that an unreadable interface, significant cognitive load, or inadequate documentation can increase reliability risk. Therefore, these factors are bidirectional; they can reduce or increase risk, while their omission may lead to underestimation. Therefore, the use of the extended version of the TESCO method in industrial practice is justified, as it more closely reflects real-world workflows and enables more precise prediction of operator errors.

The assessment of welding robot operator reliability using the classic HEART method revealed that most occupational activities were characterized by a moderate level of risk, with the P(E) index estimated at 13–23%. The introduction of four additional parameters into the assessment: poorly designed interface, high cognitive load, insufficient or difficult-to-use documentation, and high task ambiguity significantly increased the estimated risk level. This was particularly true for activities requiring the setup or configuration of welding robot settings. In contrast, for more standardized and repetitive tasks, a smaller, though still significant, impact was observed. This demonstrates the crucial importance of assessing operator reliability in automated work environments.

The results of the conducted research clearly indicate that traditional preventive measures, such as employee training and procedure training, may be insufficient in the case of automated workstations. Based on the findings, it can be concluded that there is a need to implement solutions that will improve the level of occupational safety and operator reliability. Key actions include: designing intuitive and ergonomic user interfaces, automating critical robot settings, implementing systems that support operator decision-making, and developing clear, understandable technical documentation. Furthermore, employee training should encompass more than just device operation. In particular, training programs should address how to interpret system information and how to properly handle high cognitive load conditions.

In summary, the research results clearly indicate that the classic TESEO and HERAT methods are inadequate in terms of human reliability and work efficiency in automated workstations and should be adapted to the modern demands of the work environment, where errors may result not only from behavior or stress but also from interface design, cognitive load, or information quality. The innovative, proprietary approach to the TESEO and HEART methodologies is fully justified in terms of application and fills the identified methodological gap. The methodology presented in this paper is characterized by universality and the potential for broader application across various industries. The methodology proposed in this paper is universal and can be successfully applied to assessing human reliability during the operation of other robotic devices. It should be emphasized that factors such as HMI quality, cognitive load, documentation clarity, and task ambiguity influence the risk of error in many automated environments, regardless of the type of operation performed, for example, during assembly or packaging robots. Therefore, it is important to emphasize that the developed methodology has significant potential for use in various Industry 4.0 sectors. At the same time, it is important to note certain limitations of the current approach. First, the study was conducted for a welding robot station at a selected manufacturing facility and involved a limited number of operators. This may make it difficult to apply the results to other production environments. Second, part of the evaluation (evaluating correction factors) was based on expert knowledge and practical observations, which is inherently subjective and may limit the ability to precisely determine the impact of individual parameters. Future research plans to expand the methodology to include additional automated stations, a larger sample of operators, and the use of quantitative techniques, which will increase the repeatability of the results and confirm the universality of the developed method.
