Graduating medical students’ perception of preparedness for practice: experiential learning is key
Authors: Caitlin Ying Ming Louey, Antonia Zeng, Simone Roberts, Julia Harrison, Claire Palermo, Michelle Leech, Arunaz Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreparedness for practice (P4P) encompasses experience, knowledge, confidence, self-awareness, accountability, resilience, and competency, and is crucial to improved patient outcomes, healthcare team dynamics and doctor wellbeing. This study aimed to investigate graduating medical students’ perceptions of P4P.MethodsGraduating medical students from the class of 2022 at an Australian University were invited to voluntarily participate in the study. The cross-sectional exploratory study was developed using co-design (co-creation with faculty and medical students) and employed through a 69-question survey. The survey measured eight key competency areas using 5-point Likert confidence scales and 34 open-ended questions. Quantitative data was analysed using descriptive statistics. Text responses were analysed with qualitative description which drew upon thematic analysis and coding.ResultsA total of 365 graduating Doctor of Medicine students completed the survey (83% response rate). Overall, 87% of students reported that they felt adequately to very well prepared for practice. Results showed a median Likert rating of 4 (out of 5) across six of the eight competency areas. Students reporting feeling most prepared in core clinical competencies, teamwork, and professionalism and least confident in procedural skills. Themes identified as key influences in confidence and perceptions of P4P were; adequacy of opportunities to practise, experiences relating to clinical placement, supportive or unsupportive learning environments, personal attributes and life experience, curricula design, assessment with feedback and perceived easiness of skill/task and risk to patients.ConclusionsThe findings of the study indicate that overall students do feel prepared for practice, but factors to further improve work-readiness and transition to practice could focus on repeated skills practice, supportive real-life clinical learning environments and quality feedback.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07801-8.

Introduction

Junior doctors are an integral part of the Australian healthcare system. In 2023, 3805 new graduates entered the Australian workforce for their first year of internship [1]. It is well documented that the transition between medical school and internship can be an intense period of adjustment [2]. Preparedness for practice (P4P) is crucial to improved patient outcomes, healthcare teams and junior doctor wellbeing. While regulators attempt to measure graduates P4P, little peer reviewed evidence exists about why Australian junior doctors feel or do not feel prepared with regards to entry-level competencies. This limits the data available to inform curriculum improvements. Moreover, a lack of preparedness for the heavy workload on entering the workforce and self-perceptions of competence in knowledge and skills may be linked to increased stress in new graduates [3]. Emotional exhaustion, anxiety, depression, distress and burnout have been identified in both medical students and junior doctors [3–5]. Therefore, it is imperative that medical schools do as much as possible to prepare students for the realities of the workplace and complexity of the profession that they are entering.

The process of ‘becoming prepared’ occurs gradually across all years of medical school as students are exposed to learning experiences that facilitate growth in their knowledge, skills and confidence to become doctors [6]. This process of transformation is facilitated both by the formal curriculum and informal learning or ‘hidden curriculum’ such as influence from peers and role models, workplace hierarchies and immersion in different learning environments [3, 7]. Evaluation of student and new graduate P4P can assist medical educators to identify potential gaps in student learning experiences, undertake quality improvement of curriculum delivery and provide better structures to support student wellbeing and resilience. The term ‘preparedness for practice’ is challenging to define and has had varying interpretations in the literature [3, 4, 8, 9]. As an abstract concept, P4P broadly refers to the state of being ready and relies on subjective interpretation. While there is no consistent definition, we defined it as encompassing work readiness, competence or fitness for practice of graduates transitioning into their new role as a clinician in the workplace [3, 4, 8, 9]. We also used P4P conceptualisations previously identified in the literature including P4P as knowledge, confidence, skills, experience, self-awareness, competence, long-term and short-term vision, employability, initiative, self-efficacy, independence, responsibility/accountability, resilience, professional behaviours, ability to manage stress, psychological and emotional readiness and safe practice [4, 8].

In Australia, the Australian Medical Council (AMC) sets the entry level competency outcomes that new graduates must meet to be able to transition into their internship or first years of working. Responding to a lack of data on P4P across these competency areas, the AMC and Medical Board of Australia conducted a Preparedness for Internship survey from 2017 to 2019 [10]. This survey which was completed by graduates from across Australia found that 74% of respondents thought that their medical education was sufficient for entering the workforce. Whilst these survey results identified some areas where students felt more confident (including taking a history, performing a physical examination, and communicating with patients) or lack thereof (including prescribing drugs and understanding of data informatics) it did not elaborate on the reasons why students felt this way. Currently there is limited literature [4, 11] exploring what is meant by P4P with varying definitions [4, 6, 8, 12] and relatively less exploration of why graduates feel prepared or not. In addition, the majority of the data is greater than 5 years old [13], and only focuses on a specific set of narrow factors impacting P4P, such as being in postgraduate versus undergraduate cohorts [14], or specific problem based tutorial types or simply focusing on either quantitative or qualitative outcomes [15–17]. Moreover, there is a distinct lack of P4P evidence in the Australian context and limited data exploring graduates’ perceptions against entry-level competencies. The overwhelming majority of prior literature is based on United Kingdom (UK) cohorts [14–17]. To our knowledge there has been only a single prior study based in Australia published over 5 years ago [16] and few - if not any - designed with a co-creation between medical professionals/academic staff and medical students. Co-creation – a relatively new concept – is defined as close collaboration between learners and teachers and emphasises the value of active learning involvement in the design and development of educational initiatives [18]. Through multi-stakeholder engagement within the research team, co-creation aims to improve teaching and learning outcomes by the effects on the quality of educational design [18]. Understanding student perceptions of what underpins P4P will therefore help inform the development of curriculum and guide what specific competencies, assessments and support are needed to provide students with an optimal transition to the workplace.

To this end we sought to investigate and answer two research questions (RQs):

Methods

Our study was positioned within social constructivism and Lev Vygotsky’s [19] sociocultural theory of development in which medical students learn collaboratively through social interactions as well as the different contexts and cultures of the systems that they are immersed in throughout their training. Our study was co-created and led by medical students who are now junior doctors (CL, AZ), medical school staff (AK, ML, JH) and health professions education researchers (CP, SR). Team reflexivity and the position of the researchers in relation to the study was considered regularly throughout data analysis.

At the end of 2022, medical students from the graduating class of 2022 – those entering the 2023 medical workforce - at Monash University (Australia) were invited to voluntarily participate in the study. Ethics approval was obtained through the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (project and approval number 35386) prior to commencement and research adhered to the declaration of Helsinki (clinical trial number: not applicable). All participants were provided with an explanatory statement and provided informed electronic consent prior to commencement of the survey. No inducement was offered to participants. The survey collected both quantitative and qualitative data to explore participants’ perception of P4P at the very end of their medical education. This study was based at Monash University, a large research-intensive university offering the Doctor of Medicine (MD) through both undergraduate (students enrolled in their first level of tertiary education - MD 5-year course) and postgraduate (students who have already completed an undergraduate degree MD 4-year course) pathways. The Monash University MD course is designed as an integrated curriculum providing core units/themes (personal and professional development; population, society, health, and illness; scientific basis of clinical practice; and clinical skills) taught in an interdisciplinary fashion across a diverse range of learning environments [20]. The pre-clinical years are largely campus based, and the clinical years are conducted in hospitals and other clinical settings across metropolitan Melbourne and rural/regional Victoria, Australia.

A convenience sample of final year medical students were recruited during the final week of their 5-year undergraduate or 4-year postgraduate medical education. At this time, students had completed the entirety of the curriculum and were invited to voluntarily participate in the survey (in the form of an online questionnaire) through email sent by administrative staff. The survey was developed and data collected using the secure web-based platform Qualtrics [21] hosted at Monash University (Australia). The survey which was developed exclusively for this study by the research team (Supplementary File 1) consisted of 69 questions and drew inspiration from the Preparedness for Internship Survey [10] (Supplementary Table 1). The survey was co-created with faculty and medical students where it was piloted with 5 students and refined prior to whole cohort recruitment in 2022. In the context of this survey, P4P refers to students developing their readiness and the necessary knowledge and skills to be able to apply it to their clinical practice. Students were provided our definition of P4P which was derived from the broad and varied interpretations of P4P in the current literature [4, 6, 8, 12]. The definition provided to students was; P4P can be both short term and long term and conceptualisations of P4P include experience, knowledge, confidence, self-awareness, accountability, resilience, and competency in skills. Questions captured students’ demographic data and Likert scales were used to measure the eight key competency areas including professional behaviour [10]; core clinical competencies, features of working within a hospital system, skills involving patient management, documentation, procedures, self-management, teamwork, and professionalism. Only the first and last point of the majority of Likert scales were defined with middle points 2,3 and 4 presumed to be interpreted as low, neutral and moderate. In addition, questions were followed up with open text boxes inviting students to describe their perspectives of P4P and provided them with a formal platform to express their highlights, concerns, and suggestions for MD course improvement. A combination of closed and open response items were used in the survey in line with our constructivist approach to gain a perception of participant preparedness but also to understand why these perceptions were held by participants.

Data analysis

Comparable quantitative data was tested for normality and identified as non-parametric therefore reported as median/IQR. SPSS software (IBM, version 29.0, ©2022) was used for all statistical analysis.

Qualitative description drew upon thematic analysis [22] whereby sections of the text were coded. This process was performed by CL, AZ and SR. Initially all qualitative responses were read multiple times to ensure familiarity with the data. Qualitative questions were equally divided amongst each team member to independently review and open code. The identified codes were then cross-checked, discussed, and debated by the team until they reached a consensus on a final code list. This final code list was then re-applied to the data. Based on the frequency of codes, and in consideration of the significance of patterns across the data in relation to the research questions and in the context of existing literature, common concepts were then grouped into themes. Themes and subordinate themes were refined through constant comparison which were reported. Team reflexivity was considered throughout whereby each team member challenged interpretations of others based on their different perspectives and this was applied throughout the analysis process.

Results

Demographics

A total of 365 graduating Monash University Doctor of Medicine (MD) students completed the survey with a total 83% response rate (365 of 442). The majority of students were undergraduate students (77%), and most students primarily completed their training in metropolitan based placement sites (81%). Our definition of P4P was provided to students and defined as P4P refers to students developing their readiness and the necessary knowledge and skills to be able to apply it to their clinical practice. 99% of students (363 of 365) agreed with this definition with only 1 student providing an alternate definition. Their definition stated, “I think the definition should include having the necessary knowledge to be a safe practitioner.” Overall, 87% of students reported that they felt adequately to very well prepared for practice (Table 1).

Procedural skills

Overall, this competency was the area in which students were least confident with a median Likert rating of 2 out of 5 (with 1 = no confidence, 5 = very confident). Out of all listed procedural skills, none received a Likert score of 4 or 5. Intravenous cannulation had the highest confidence with 75% (272 of 365) rating it a Likert score of 3. Taking part in advanced life support (60%, 218 of 365), suturing and wound care (48%, 176 of 365), back slab application (59%, 214 of 365) and indwelling urinary catheter (50%, 181 of 365) all received a predominant Likert score of 2. Ultrasound scanning (for ultrasound guided intravenous cannulation) received the lowest confidence with 44% (162 or 365) of students giving a Likert score of 1 and 41% (151 of 365) giving a Likert score of 2. When asked whether students had adequate opportunity to practise procedural skills, 65% (235 of 365) felt they had adequate opportunity to practise procedural skills on placement. 21% (78 of 365) felt they did not have adequate opportunity to practise and 14% (52 of 365) were unsure.

Self-management skills

Although students reported a high sense of confidence with self-management skills, they were least confident in coping with uncertainty (median Likert score 3, 34%, 125 of 365) in this domain. 69% of students responded “yes” to being aware of the support systems available to should they need to seek support regarding these self-management skills.

Impact of COVID-19 on preparedness for practice

Regarding the COVID-19 pandemic, 72% (261 of 365) students felt it negatively impacted their preparedness for practice, 17% (63 of 365) felt it did not negatively impact their preparedness of practice and 11% (41 of 365) were unsure about the impact of it on their preparedness for practice.

Factors affecting preparedness for practice on placement

94% of students (344 of 365) believed they had good clinical role models during their time in medical school. 90% of students (328 of 365) reported having the opportunity to do parallel consulting during placement.

Discussion

We sought to investigate how prepared across key areas of practice do graduating medical students perceive they are. We explored the reasons and the experiences which have contributed to or hindered these feelings of P4P. In the Australian context, our study found that the majority (87%) of graduates reported that they felt adequately to very well prepared for practice. This finding aligns with the external national survey conducted by Medical Deans Australia and New Zealand administered at graduation. In 2022 – conducted in the same year as our survey - students showed a high level (over 90%) agreement (response rate 89% of cohort) with the statement ‘my basic medical degree has prepared me to practice as an intern’ [1]. In our study, the reasons underpinning these perceptions included: adequacy of opportunities to practise, experiences relating to clinical placement, supportive or unsupportive learning environments, personal attributes and life experience, curricula design, assessment with feedback and perceived ‘easiness’ of skill/task and risk to patients.

In our study we aimed to explore confidence and competencies as elements of preparedness for practice but recognise that confidence is not a static concept but may be contextual. Confidence plays a crucial role in how students approach clinical tasks and challenges. It encapsulates a student’s belief in their own abilities and therefore their willingness to engage in new learning tasks, take initiative and seek opportunities to improve their learning and skills [8, 23]. Confidence impacts a student’s perceived sense of readiness whereby increased confidence may foster greater feelings of preparedness while transitioning to real-world practice, even if their objective capabilities are still developing. This perception can influence how they approach patient care, workplace challenges, dealing with uncertainty and the demands of clinical practice [24]. Equally, it is important to recognise that over-confidence or lack of self-awareness including that of human fallibility and the limits of human performance when operating in a complex system may lead to underestimating gaps in knowledge or skills [25, 26]. Therefore, analysing confidence and competencies regarding student perception of P4P at this stage of their learning is essentially to understanding the factors influencing students P4P as they confront the complexities of practice.

Our findings report higher preparedness for practice than that of the average medical graduate in Australia [1, 10] and even greater when compared to international literature. A systematic review by Cameron et al. [11] analysed literature concerning the transition from student to junior doctor published from 2004 to 2014 which measured or explored one or more factors affecting preparedness. Out of twenty-six international articles, with most based in the UK, the review found new doctors feel inadequately prepared for practice [11]. Another qualitative UK study found only 29% of their new graduates (newly qualified doctors undertaking the first year) reported feeling well prepared in their preparedness to practice and 64% reported mixed preparedness [27]. We acknowledge that medical education has experienced substantial changes with time and the lack of existing literature makes direct comparisons hard, however, it highlights that students from our institution feel comparably more prepared for practise in both the Australian and International context. This is also in spite of the fact that 72% of students reported the COVID-19 pandemic negatively impacting their preparedness for practice. While this study has highlighted some potential curriculum elements that clearly contribute to perception of P4P, these are likely to be common across medical training in the country [9, 16]. The reasons our graduates report higher than average levels of preparedness remain largely unknown. It is known that confidence has an impact on student performance with studies describing student experiences and opportunity to practise improving confidence [8]. In our study, clinical placement especially those in rural and regional areas, time spent in hospitals and exposure to certain conditions, patient groups and applying learned skills were key to building confidence. Students also reported factors of feeling supported, having balance outside of medical school and time – referring to the time needed to practise and consolidate knowledge and skills – as important elements to developing their overall preparedness for practice. Further work needs to be undertaken to understand what builds confidence and readiness more deeply for practice.

We also found student perception of the ‘easiness’ of a certain skill or task and self-evaluated risk to patients had an impact on confidence and hence P4P. Similarly seen in the AMC and other study findings, higher preparedness ratings were given for skills such as performing physical exams, history taking and patient communication. Whereas low preparedness ratings included procedural skills and prescribing [10, 12, 28]. Formal curriculum and assessment around prescribing have been augmented year on year in our institution’s course as well as almost all medical schools in Australia. Moreover, expressed ‘confidence’ in prescribing in a system which cannot legally support supervised prescribing by student doctors would be a cause for concern. These observations could indicate the need for a re-framing or shifting some prescribing competencies into the internship and pre-vocational curriculum where prescribing context can be legally provided in Australia. That is not to say that the identification by students at graduation that they are not feeling as well prepared in some skills may actually reflect a healthy, balanced and appropriate assessment of their lack of opportunity to practice a complex and risky task even in supervised settings. In fact, a reported lack of confidence may be a positive or protective finding as students demonstrate self-awareness of their own limitations in their skillset at this stage of their career. Nonetheless, a lack of understanding of human fallibility and the limits of human performance when operating in a complex system coupled with one’s own feelings of uncertainty underpin the importance of providing supportive learning environments, assessment with feedback and increased opportunities to practise to help support students in their feelings of P4P.

The Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander curriculum framework seeks to develop many skills such as reflection and advocacy but chief among them is intellectual and cultural humility. In the context of this medical school, experience in caring for Indigenous Australians occurs in many of the tertiary and quaternary health services as well as community practices. The student responses in this survey indicates students felt underprepared to work with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. The curriculum-framework for Indigenous Health at Monash University developed using National Framework has a strong focus on humility and life-long learning and cautions against confidence especially given the vast and diverse cultural heritage of Aboriginal and Torres strait Islander peoples. Seeking to establish confidence in working cross culturally may be at odds with cultural humility. A systematic review exploring the impact of Indigenous curriculum on health professional learners (including medical students) found that Indigenous story-telling, practical patient care classes, activity based learning and pre-clinical learning were among possible learning activities that could be considered [29]. The need for continued efforts to build cultural safety and responsiveness of our medical graduates remains a key priority.

Increased confidence and P4P was also found to be positively impacted by mentors, feeling involved in teams and learning in supportive environments aligning with research from other studies [8, 30–32]. The social nature of learning through real life experiences on clinical placement and observation and learning from more knowledgeable others aligns with social constructivist theory of development where medical students will learn in a collaborative way that is influenced by context, culture and interactions within their learning environment [33]. Students reported increased confidence across multiple domains when they had an experience on placement with a supportive team and opportunities for mentoring, supervision and feedback. We found 94% of students believed they had good clinical role models during their time in medical school and 90% of students had the opportunity to do parallel consulting. Those who experienced negative team environments or experiences - specifically, teams with hierarchical structures, feelings of intimidation, receiving harsh/unfair criticism or being in teams who were “too busy” to engage with their learning – and/or a lack of support reported their preparedness as being diminished. This highlights the importance of being given sufficient opportunities throughout medical school to practise and learn under the guidance of more experienced doctors who are effective supervisors [34]. It also points to the complexity and individual nature of the medical student journey, each who will be exposed to different opportunities to practise and develop their skills as a junior doctor. Moreover, this observation highlights that P4P is not static and may be in a constant state of flux influence by intrinsic and extrinsic situational factors. A deeper exploration of the multifactorial nature of P4P considering the different stakeholders and environments of influence would therefore help medical educators understand how to optimise learning experiences and environments and should be considered during course evaluations to provide a more holistic view of contribution to P4P.

Feedback quality was identified as an enabler of P4P as recurrent high quality feedback experiences promoted improvement in skill performance and confidence in those skills. Conversely, we found that students lacked confidence in teamwork and professional skills where they have had less experience or feedback. Feedback is a critical component of experiential learning and growth [35]. Although students did highlight that assessment and feedback within the medical curriculum was associated with temporary feelings of stress, they understood and valued the benefit for their long-term learning. These insights and reflections on practice are valuable to highlight and augment continued work required to develop quality supervisor feedback capability and to build students’ feedback literacy. Supporting students to gain experience and confidence should be envisioned as a critical requirement to build P4P.

Congruent with Ottrey et al.’s previous study, students highlighted how real-life experiences such as those gained through work-integrated learning, volunteering or shadowing provided unmatched opportunities to increase confidence and feelings of preparedness through consolidating knowledge [8]. This concept of life experience relates to preparing students to be ‘work ready doctors’ who are self-efficacious lifelong learners with the necessary skills to build upon their own professional development and adaptive capabilities [16]. However, uncertainty is inevitably associated with real-life experience and the nature of medical practice itself. Similar to other studies, students felt least confident and prepared in dealing with uncertainty, where poor uncertainty tolerance has been linked with negative outcomes including reduced psychological well-being [16, 31]. There is evidence to suggest that medical education interventions can improve student uncertainty tolerance whereby 69% of students reported being aware of the support systems available if needed to aid their self-management skills. This highlights the importance for medical institutions to improve building skills and supporting students in competently managing unexpected and complex scenarios that arise in healthcare to avoid psychological outcomes such as burnout [16, 31, 36]. Moreover, although P4P generally increases with time, it is important to acknowledge that there may be temporal fluctuations and variations with each individual due to personal attributes [23].

The strengths of our study include the high response rate in a large cohort, intentional recruitment during the final week of medical school at the end of course completion and prior to commencement of work as clinicians, the presence of postgraduate and undergraduate students and the measurement of both perceptions of P4P and the reasons behind this. Combining both quantitative and qualitative results aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of P4P with the potential for other researchers or medical education institutions to use these results to improve new graduate P4P. Capturing students’ reflections and insights at this significant point of transition provides critical insights into the effectiveness of medical school training, areas of confidence, and perceived gaps that may influence their early clinical experiences. Another strength is the co-creation with medical students and academics. CL and AZ who are key members of the research team, have been involved in the co-creation of the study from their time as medical students to now junior doctors. This involvement included from study concept creation and survey creation as medical students through to analysis and publication as junior doctors. The impact of co-creation has been demonstrated to influence three levels including the psychosocial learning environment, motivation and metacognition and quality of the educational design [18]. The study’s co-creation acts as a strength as the active engagement and close collaboration between learners (medical students) and teachers (academic staff) provided meaningful insight, feedback and perspectives of different stakeholders to support the formulation of plans for educational change [18]. However, our study was not without limitations. As participants came from one Australian university, findings may be less transferable to other graduating cohorts. Nevertheless, the challenge of helping medical students transition to doctors is universal. Throughout the data analysis process, the research team engaged in ongoing reflexivity through regular team discussions and with the supervision of the wider research team. Despite efforts and employed strategies to increase transparency and challenge assumptions, we recognise that these measures do not fully eliminate alternate interpretation of results. We acknowledge the limitations of the survey design in only defining the first and last point of the Likert scale for majority of these questions. We also recognise that surveys rely on subjective self-reporting and consequently there is an inherent risk of inconsistencies in responses particularly when interpreting questions and measuring nuanced constructs such as confidence. Several survey items were also directly taken from the AMC survey (supplementary Table 1) which prevented adequate exploration of multiple concepts which were conceptualised together. Future surveys should consider separating these concepts. Our initial focus was on presenting an overall picture of P4P across the cohort, which aligned with our primary research aims. However, due to scope constraints and focus on qualitative findings, further stratified analysis such as examining differences based on training location or other demographic characteristics was not pursued in this phase of the study. This may be an area for potential exploration in future research or in a larger sample size.

Conclusion

Our study shows that among a large sample of medical graduates from a single Australian institution, the majority felt prepared for practice. Despite this, key skills such as procedural skills, features of working within the hospital system, coping with uncertainty and delivering care to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples suggest additional efforts are required to support students in these areas. Repeated and structured skills practise is essential to building confidence and therefore competence. Ensuring students have adequate opportunities for skills practice and exposure to realistic clinical scenarios in safe contexts can help students bridge the gap between theoretic knowledge and practical application. It is also important to acknowledge the impact of dealing with uncertainty on self-perception of P4P. Creating supportive learning environments which encourage reflection, collaborative learning and quality supervisor feedback can help combat this and help students prepare in their transition to junior doctors. These insights may help inform strategies to foster more balanced confidence with the aim of helping students develop a realistic understanding of their capabilities while continuing to build upon the necessary skills to become safe practitioners. This emphasises the importance of continuing to obtain and understand medical students’ perspectives to identify areas of uncertainty or perceived gaps in preparedness where student voices are core to curriculum development and improvement. These findings having the potential to help medical educators and institutions inform the development and improvement of medical curricula. Collectively these findings highlight the importance for medical curricula to provide sufficient skill practice opportunities and incorporate supportive learning environments that mirror real-life clinical settings to help facilitate students’ transition to the workplace. P4P is dynamic and may shift significantly with real-world experience. Ongoing research is required to understand how students’ initial perceptions of preparedness align with the demands of clinical practice.
