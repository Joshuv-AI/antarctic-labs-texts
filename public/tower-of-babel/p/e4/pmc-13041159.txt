Real world evaluation of a distance-supervised ultrasound training programme in a physician-staffed emergency medical service: a prospective, descriptive implementation study
Authors: María José Luque-Hernández, Adriana Gil-Rodrigo, Carmen Gutiérrez-García, Carmen Martínez-Buendía, Virginia Mestanza-Ávila, Juan Antonio Péculo-Carrasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntroducing point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) into prehospital emergencies is challenging. This study evaluated a blended POCUS training programme with remote supervision on clinical use and competence in a physician-staffed Emergency Medical Service (EMS).MethodsA prospective observational study considering the RE-AIM framework was conducted over six months in a regional physician-staffed EMS. Board-certified emergency physicians received training in focused protocols for respiratory failure, shock, cardiac arrest, and trauma, combining simulation with real-patient scanning under asynchronous expert supervision. Cases with and without POCUS were compared. Stored clips were reviewed by blinded experts for technical adequacy and interpretation accuracy. Interobserver agreement was measured using Cohen's κ and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).ResultsAmong 476 calls, ultrasound-trained physicians attended 375 (78.8%). POCUS was performed in 70 cases (18.7% with a trained physician; 38.3% when the primary indication was present). POCUS use was mainly for respiratory distress (47%) and shock (31%), with low trauma use (~ 6%). Use was reduced during overnight shifts (p = 0.0049). Scene time did not differ between POCUS and non-POCUS cases (21.5 vs 20.2 min, p = 0.64). Approximately 40% of scans met all technical quality criteria. Interpretation accuracy was high—experts agreed with trainees in ~ 80% of cases (κ = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.65–0.92); ultrasound diagnoses were confirmed in 85.7% (ICC = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.75–0.96). POCUS patients had higher clinical severity scores (34% vs 17% with p < 0.005). In some of these cases, episodes of cardiac arrest were documented.ConclusionsBlended training with remote mentorship enabled safe POCUS integration into high-acuity prehospital care without delaying scene time. Ongoing practice and mentorship are recommended to improve image acquisition and sustain skills.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-026-01582-9.

Background

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is now a core diagnostic adjunct during out-of-hospital emergencies, and the current consensus emphasises management for its safe use. The 2023 European Society for Emergency Medicine European Federation of Societies for Ultrasound in Medicine and Biology (EuSEM/EFSUMB) position paper recommends structured credentialing, image archiving, and ongoing feedback to ensure quality and safety in the field [1]. The Royal College of Emergency Medicine’s 2021 POCUS syllabus shifts from fixed scan counts to an entrustment model emphasising deliberate practice, formative assessment, and local supervision [2]. These shifts mirror broader trends in competency-based medical education and create an imperative for assessment tools that operate outside tertiary centres.

POCUS integration into prehospital care is feasible without delays, yielding quality images and faster interventions. Effective training for prehospital staff and trainee prehospital providers, using strategies like remote mentoring and high-fidelity simulation, can achieve competency levels comparable to traditional teaching despite logistical hurdles [2–8].

In medical education, it is common to report satisfaction and knowledge acquired (levels 1–2), but the impact on patient care (Kirkpatrick level 4) is rarely evaluated [9]. To date, no prehospital POCUS curriculum has demonstrated improvements in clinical or system outcomes, representing a significant evidence gap that this study aims to address [10–13]. It is unclear if competency-based curricula improve real-world proficiency for experienced emergency physicians working autonomously in demanding environments.

To date, several studies have demonstrated the potential benefits of POCUS in prehospital setting, mainly focusing on patients with non‑traumatic dyspnea, major trauma, and shock [14, 15]. However, the most efficient prehospital POCUS curriculum to be implemented has not yet been explored, considering both its dynamic nature and the specific characteristics of care delivery in this environment, as well as the potential improvements in clinical outcomes that it may generate. Indeed, it remains unclear whether competency‑based curriculum improves real‑world skills among experienced emergency physicians who work autonomously in demanding settings. This represents an important gap in the evidence that the present study aims to address, which, in our view, should be analysed even prior to assessing the impact on patients’ clinical outcomes.

The current evidence gap is rooted in the lack of evaluation frameworks that assess the true impact of POCUS training in prehospital settings. To address this, we considered the RE-AIM framework (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) [16] which we will develop further below. However, the five dimensions of the RE‑AIM framework were not systematically considered in this study, as its primary objective was to evaluate a more specific learning tool. This study evaluated the real-world POCUS competence of board-certified prehospital emergency physicians post-blended learning with remote mentoring, and its 6-month impact on patient care and operational outcomes, comparing patients managed with and without POCUS.

Methods

Objective, study design and setting

A prospective observational implementation study that evaluated a distance training and remote mentoring model in prehospital POCUS, partially considering the use of the RE‑AIM framework. RE-AIM framework provides a multidimensional structure essential for implementation research, moving beyond simple individual effectiveness (Kirkpatrick level 4) to systematically assess the external validity and sustainability of the intervention. This was conducted at two physician-staffed ground ALS bases (urban Base A704, peripheral Base A706) of the Andalusian Pre-hospital Emergency Service (CES061) in Málaga, Spain (covering an area of approximately 7,308 km2 and serving a population of 1.70 million inhabitants in 2021) [17].Two bases were included to increase the representativeness of the study population. However, there are no relevant differences between urban and peripheral units in terms of equipment, staff, or the types of conditions managed, and both utilized identical handheld Philips Lumify® ultrasound devices. The ALS teams in Málaga respond to an average of 11,617 missions annually (Base A704: 2,502; Base A706: 1,157), with transport rates of 61% and 72%. Referral centres include Virgen de la Victoria University Hospital, Málaga Regional Hospital, and Costa del Sol Hospital.

Patient recruitment commenced, following the completion of the in-person training, in June 2021 and continued for the subsequent six months.

Characteristics of the participants and description of the material

Fifteen ALS physicians holding permanent positions (each with more than 10 years of pre-hospital experience) undertook a three-phase training program; nurses attached to the same bases attended the face-to-face sessions as observers. Physicians who worked temporarily at these bases during the study period (to cover vacations, sick leaves, and/or isolated shifts) were not included and these were classified as physicians not ultrasound trained. Patients were eligible when they were ≥ 18 years old, attended by one of the two equipped ALS ambulances, and presented with an a-priori first-line indication for point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS): major trauma, dyspnoea, undifferentiated shock, or out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. These indications were selected according to current evidence‑based protocols for each critical scenario (EFAST, BLUE, RUSH, and FEEL, respectively), all of which were supported by cognitive aids (see supplementary material). Missions handled by physicians who had not completed the ultrasound curriculum or in which no accessible ultrasound device was in place were excluded. Exclusions included missions managed by physicians who had not finished the ultrasound curriculum or those where no functioning ultrasound device was available. We employed a consecutive sampling approach that included all emergency calls attended by study physicians during the 6-month period, without applying additional exclusions beyond the predefined eligibility criteria. This consecutive sampling approach minimizes selection bias inherent to volunteer or convenience samples. No refusals to participate were recorded among trained physicians, and all eligible patients were included according to standard emergency service protocols.

Procedures, interventions and comparisons

The educational curriculum is summarized in Table 1; it combined e-learning, four full-day workshops, and six months of remote mentorship, the latter delivered through the encrypted Medxat® channel, which allowed trainees 24/7 access to mentors for case discussion and feedback. Participants were considered to have successfully completed the remote training and mentorship program once they had documented at least 10 cases. A response time from instructors to trainees of less than six hours was established. To achieve this promptness, one of the researchers provided external support by monitoring pending consultations. Training ultrasound programm

After training, physicians could perform ultrasound at their discretion on any patient. They were required to save a labelled video clip and complete the protocol sheet (eFAST, Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma; BLUE, Bedside Lung Ultrasound in Emergency; RUSH, Rapid Ultrasound in Hypotension or FEEL, (Focused Echocardiographic Evaluation in Life Support), documenting every sonographic finding (Supplementary Material 2). Investigators prospectively collected, for every emergency call attended by the two equipped ambulances, a case-report form that merged data from the electronic patient-care record and, when POCUS was used, the trainee’s worksheet.

Variables recorded included patient age and sex; date and time of attendance (08:00–15:00, 15:00–22:00, or 22:00–08:00); and the originating base (urban or peripheral). Data were collected on the presence of a physician trained in ultrasonography, the primary clinical category, and severity prediction scores, including the NEWS2 (National Early Warning Score 2) [18] and the NACA scale (National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics) [19]. Additional variables included endotracheal intubation, on-scene time, transport destination and level, as well as subsequent imaging in the emergency department (plain radiography or computed tomography), final hospital diagnosis, and admission status. All data was entered into a secure database. Stored clips and worksheets were independently reviewed by two blinded emergency-ultrasound educators (> 5 years’ teaching experience) and emergency physicians with ongoing clinical activity in both prehospital and in-hospital emergency care). For every examination, each evaluator rated image-acquisition quality in four domains—appropriate window, gain, depth, and overall quality—on a three-point scale (2 = adequate, 1 = inadequate, 0 = not recorded). Evaluators also recorded agreement or disagreement with the trainee’s interpretation of each exam and judged whether the ultrasound-based diagnosis matched the final hospital diagnosis.

The direct costs of the training program included: Education and training: €6,500; acquisition of two Philips Lumify® portable ultrasound devices with phased array probe (approximate cost: €10,000 per unit); subscription to the Medxat® platform for remote mentorship over 6 months (sponsored).

Primary study outcomes were the POCUS utilization rate in eligible missions and the accuracy of field scan interpretations, as determined by blinded expert reviewers. Secondary outcomes included technical adequacy of saved images, image interpretation, diagnosis correlation, and inter-observer agreement between expert evaluators. Additional analyses compared patients managed with and without POCUS, examining differences in illness severity, clinical indications, critical interventions, scene time, hospital disposition, and subsequent in-hospital diagnostic pathways to explore the potential impact of POCUS on patient care and operational outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed with R (version 4.3.3). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] where appropriate; categorical variables as counts and percentages with 95% confidence intervals. As a prospective observational implementation study, a formal a priori sample size calculation for hypothesis testing was not performed. The sample size was dictated by operational feasibility within the defined EMS system (two bases, 6-month period), encompassing all 476 eligible calls. This pragmatic approach, necessary for real-world implementation, yielded 70 POCUS examinations. For inter-rater agreement analysis, this sample size provides > 99% statistical power to detect the observed substantial agreement (κ = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.65–0.92) when compared against a minimum acceptable threshold of moderate agreement (κ = 0.40), far exceeding the conventional 80% power standard and the minimum n > 50 recommendation for reliable kappa estimation. [18]. Furthermore, the patient comparison n = 375 cases managed by trained physicians was exploratory. Future studies are warranted to achieve sufficient statistical power for definitive patient-centered outcomes [19]. Ultrasound-performed versus ultrasound-not-performed groups were compared with the χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as dictated by distribution. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For the competency assessment, inter-observer reliability in each categorical domain was quantified with Cohen’s κ, interpreted as poor (≤ 0.20), moderate (0.21–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), or almost perfect (> 0.80); overall diagnostic-accuracy agreement (treated as a continuous score) was assessed with the intraclass correlation coefficient using a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement.

Analyses stratified by protocol type (eFAST, BLUE, RUSH, FEEL), indication status, and temporal factors (shift) were exploratory and conducted post hoc to identify utilization patterns and potential implementation barriers. These analyses should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating for future confirmatory studies.

No multivariable modeling or adjustment for confounders was performed due to the exploratory nature of the study as an initial implementation evaluation and the limited sample size of the POCUS group (n = 70), insufficient for stable adjusted models with multiple covariates; and recognition that confounding by indication (physician selection of higher-severity cases for POCUS) limits causal interpretation regardless of statistical adjustment. Bivariate comparisons are presented to characterize case-mix differences, not to infer causal effects.

Ethical and regulatory considerations

Approved by the institutional ethics committee as an internal quality-improvement and educational project with a waiver of informed consent. Trainees and mentors provided written consent for the use of de-identified performance data. Investigators accessed electronic health records via a research-only, identifier-masking credential, with all accesses logged. No conflicts of interest or external funding influenced the study's design, data, analysis, or manuscript preparation.

This manuscript adheres to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) [20] statement for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies and to the StaRI (Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies) [21] statement for implementation research. The educational intervention was described according to the TIDieR (Template for Intervention Description and Replication) [22] checklist to ensure replicability. The completed checklists are provided as supplementary material. (Supplementary file 1).

Patient demographics

There was no statistically significant difference in patient sex distribution between POCUS-used and non-POCUS cases. In contrast, POCUS was used more often in older patients (p < 0.05).

Operational factors

Ultrasound use demonstrated marked temporal variation. Only 15.4% of POCUS cases occurred during night shifts (22:00–08:00), while night shifts accounted for 32.1% of all calls (p < 0.005). POCUS utilization was similar between urban and peripheral EMS bases.

Call type and clinical indication

Ultrasound was used selectively by clinical indication. Within the POCUS group, the most frequent indications were respiratory distress (36.8%), cardiac arrest (14.7%), and shock (10.3%), markedly higher than in non-POCUS cases (15.1%, 3.0%, and 1.6%, respectively). Trauma represented only 5.9% of POCUS cases versus 8.5% without POCUS, whereas "Other" complaints dominated the non-POCUS group (71.8% vs 32.4%, p < 0.001). POCUS was performed in 38% of indicated cases (49/128) but only 8% of non-indicated cases (19/245), demonstrating appropriate clinical judgment in ultrasound selection.

Clinical severity

Patients undergoing ultrasound had higher acuity. NACA score [23] ≥ V (moderate-critical illness) occurred in 34% of POCUS patients versus 17% without POCUS (p < 0.005); critically, 12.5% of POCUS patients had cardiac arrest (NACA VI) compared to 3% without POCUS. NEWS2 score [24] ≥ 7 (high-risk) was present in 27.9% of POCUS patients versus 10.2% without ultrasound (p < 0.001). Critical interventions reflected this severity difference: endotracheal intubation was performed in 11.8% of POCUS cases compared to 5.2% of non-POCUS cases, but not statistical significance (p = 0.06).

On-scene time and interventions

Ultrasound examination did not increase scene time. Average on-scene duration was 21.5 ± 8.3 min with POCUS versus 20.2 ± 7.4 min without POCUS (p = 0.64).

Disposition and hospital findings

Secondary outcomes showed trends without statistical significance. POCUS patients had higher admission rates to hospital wards or ICUs (27.94% vs 17.49%, p = 0.06) and observation units (41.18% vs 31.48%, p = 0.08). Chest radiography was obtained more frequently in the POCUS group (61.76% vs 40.98%, p < 0.05), whereas CT scanning was less frequent (8.82% vs 15.41%, p < 0.05). In cases of positive prehospital eFAST for free fluid, (n = 2), early surgical team activation was documented, suggesting potential POCUS impact on in-hospital management.

Trainee ultrasound performance and competency

A total of 70 ultrasound examinations were reviewed by two blinded expert evaluators for technical quality and interpretive accuracy.

Regard to technical image quality, evaluators assessed sonographic window, gain and depth adjustment, and overall image clarity. Inter-observer agreement was substantial (κ = 0.70–0.74). Approximately 40% of examinations met adequacy criteria across all technical parameters, with individual evaluator ratings ranging from 32.9% to 42.9%. The reported percentage reflects the overall agreement between both evaluators across all these parameters. Most suboptimal cases involved image quality issues (blurred, off-center) rather than complete acquisition failure. However, 31% of examinations (n = 22) lacked recorded video clips for technical assessment, though trainees completed standardized interpretation worksheets for all 70 cases.

Through the MedxatⓇ platform, a total of 1,235 interactions were recorded among trainees and between trainees and instructors. An absolute number of 154 cases (47.9%) were directly consulted with the mentor, corresponding to ultrasound- and/or clinically related queries. A response time from instructors to trainees of less than six hours was established and achieved in 100% of consultations.

Evaluators demonstrated substantial agreement regarding interpretation appropriateness (κ = 0.79). Of the examined cases: 29% showed agreement that the interpretation was correct and clinically appropriate; approximately 15% showed agreement that the interpretation was incorrect or a finding was missed; the remaining ~ 50% involved indeterminate or absent images precluding definitive interpretation. Regarding diagnostic accuracy, trainees' ultrasound-based diagnoses were confirmed as correct in 85.7% of cases (60/70), while expert evaluators judged the final ultrasound diagnosis as clinically appropriate in 80% (56/70) of cases. We have data on the number of ultrasound examinations performed over the subsequent three years, which demonstrate a growing trend toward their integration and sustained adherence, despite the challenges in quantifying this information using the same metrics as in the described study. Specifically, considering the same time interval one year later (June 2022 to December 2022) at the same bases, a total of 110 ultrasound examinations were recorded. Overall, ultrasound utilization has exhibited exponential growth: ultrasound devices have been equipped to an additional 35 advanced life support units (both ground and air services), with registry data across all units evidencing a 600% increase (463, 1,743, and 2,971 ultrasound examinations were recorded in 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively). Although image quality could not be formally evaluated, ultrasound use has remained consistently robust over time. Furthermore, many of the initial trainees have transitioned into instructors within the same emergency service, having completed specialized master's programs focused on ultrasound, underscoring a highly favorable and ongoing trend.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the impact ofultrasound training and remote mentorship applied to prehospital emergency care scenarios. It has allowed us to determine that a remotely supervised training program in prehospital ultrasound led to a selective yet targeted integration of POCUS into emergency care. In our physician-staffed EMS system, the overall utilisation rate was 18.7% of all missions attended by a trained provider (70/375) but increased to 38% when considering only cases with a clear primary indication for ultrasound (49/128). This indicates that POCUS was applied in more than one-third of eligible patients and was preferentially used in older and higher-acuity cases, such as shock, respiratory failure, or cardiac arrest. Such selective use mirrors real-world patterns in other systems, where adoption is highest in scenarios likely to influence management. Reported utilization rates in the literature range widely—from around 30% in mature physician-based HEMS programs to well under 5% in systems without formal ultrasound integration [25, 26]. A Belgian HEMS service reported a 29.6% utilization rate with POCUS influencing treatment in ~ 31% of cases and hospital destination in ~ 9% [25], whereas a German ground EMS without a structured program documented only ~ 1% utilization [26]. Our 6-month results therefore represent moderate adoption in the early phase of implementation. Importantly, incorporating ultrasound did not impede time-sensitive care: scene times were similar in POCUS and non-POCUS cases (mean 21.5 vs 20.2 min, p = 0.64), in line with previous studies showing that most prehospital scans can be completed in 2–5 min without delaying transport [7, 26].

After training, the use of POCUS in our service shifted predominantly to medical indications rather than trauma. Nearly half of all scans were lung ultrasounds for acute dyspnea, and about one-third were part of a shock protocol, while trauma accounted for only ~ 6% of cases. This contrasts with earlier reports in which trauma was the leading indication for prehospital ultrasound, representing up to 45% of cases in pooled reviews, with respiratory and cardiac causes being less common [27]. The reduced trauma proportion in our cohort likely reflects both our ground EMS case mix, where major trauma is less prevalent than in Helicopter Emergency Medical Service (HEMS), and pragmatic prioritization of rapid transport in severely injured patients. Notably, all scans performed were within the focused applications taught during training (lung, shock, cardiac arrest, eFAST), indicating high adherence to course guidance. This targeted application may suggest that providers were judicious in selecting cases where ultrasound could add meaningful diagnostic value, as has been reported in other physician-led EMS systems [25, 27]. However, it may also reflect constraints imposed by the healthcare system on prehospital teams, which warrants more in‑depth analysis in future research.

Operator performance demonstrated a marked disparity between technical and interpretive skills. Only 35–43% of field scans met all adequacy criteria for window, depth/gain, and overall image quality, and about 30% lacked any recorded diagnostic image—figures lower than the > 80% adequacy reported in mature HEMS systems with continuous QA feedback [25], likely due to the short follow-up period and the inherent challenges of prehospital scanning. Many suboptimal cases involved incomplete or blurred recordings rather than a total failure to perform the scan, indicating that trainees made attempts but frequently struggled to acquire optimal images within the constraints of time and space. This aligns with prior findings emphasizing the necessity of frequent practice to sustain technical proficiency [7]. In contrast, interpretive agreement with expert review was strong: independent evaluators concurred that the trainees’ interpretation was clinically appropriate in approximately 80–86% of cases (ICC 0.87, κ = 0.79), comparable to other physician-led EMS cohorts [25, 26]. This suggests that, despite imperfect image acquisition, providers were able to incorporate sonographic data into sound clinical decision-making. The blended training model, which included remote case review, likely facilitated cognitive integration of ultrasound findings during the ongoing development of psychomotor scanning skills. This underscores the importance of targeted feedback and continued practice to enhance image acquisition, while interpretive proficiency can be attained early through structured mentorship.

Regarding clinical and operational impact, our comparison of cases with and without POCUS—as predefined in the study outcomes—showed that introducing ultrasound did not hinder time-critical EMS operations: mean scene times were similar (21.5 vs. 20.2 min, p = 0.64) and critical interventions were not delayed. Patients in the POCUS group had higher clinical severity at presentation, which likely explains their higher hospital admission rates (27.9% vs. 17.5%, p≈0.06) and greater use of targeted diagnostic tests such as chest X-ray (61.8% vs. 41.0%, p = 0.03). However, the absence of statistically significant differences in these figures may be related to the extensive integration of conventional chest X-rays in the initial assessment of critically ill patients. In selected trauma cases, positive eFAST findings triggered early surgical activation and expedited operating room transfer, illustrating potential process-of-care benefits. These findings are consistent with previous physician-led EMS studies reporting that POCUS influences treatment and triage decisions in a substantial proportion of cases [25, 28], even if definitive patient-centered outcome gains remain unproven in most series.

Despite the generally successful integration of POCUS within our system, we identified barriers that may limit its consistent use. Notably, ultrasound use during night shifts was substantially lower, with only ~ 15% of ultrasounds performed between 22:00–08:00, despite these hours accounting for roughly one-third of all EMS calls. This pattern may reflect both case-mix differences and operational or human factors such as provider fatigue, limited team support, or reduced confidence operating in low-light conditions. Another finding was that the presence of a second ultrasound observer (nurse) on scene did not significantly increase usage, indicating that the decision to scan rested primarily with the lead physician. These observations align with previous reports that sustained POCUS adoption requires more than individual training: it demands a supportive system culture, defined indications within protocols, administrative endorsement, and continuous quality assurance [25, 28]. Addressing these factors could help normalize POCUS use across shifts and teams, ensuring equitable access to its potential benefits.

Sustaining and improving POCUS use will require ongoing training, structured support, and robust quality assurance. Our six-month blended program, combining initial high-fidelity simulation with asynchronous remote review of field scans, likely contributed to the interpretive accuracy observed. However, the modest image quality rates and the low proportion of “correct image interpretations” in stored cases highlight the need for continued skills reinforcement.

Regular refresher courses, mandatory image archiving, and prompt expert feedback have been shown to raise adequacy rates in other physician-led EMS systems to > 80% [25]. Tele-ultrasound supervision offers a promising adjunct: real-time remote guidance has successfully enabled non-expert providers to complete prehospital scans in most attempted cases [29], while tele-mentored simulation has achieved skill gains comparable to on-site teaching [5]. Although we did not employ live tele-guidance, our post-mission review process functioned as an asynchronous tele-mentoring model and may have bolstered diagnostic decision-making despite acquisition challenges. Any strategy to scale POCUS within EMS must also address operational factors, such as equipment availability, environmental constraints, and provider situational awareness. Simulation studies have shown that ultrasound tasks can divert attention from critical monitoring if performed without team coordination [30], underscoring the importance of human-factors training. Embedding POCUS into standardized protocols for targeted high-yield clinical scenarios, ensuring universal access to ultrasound devices, and fostering a culture that prioritizes its appropriate use will be critical. This will establish ultrasound as a routine, safe, and impactful component of prehospital care. The RE-AIM framework enables the evaluation of system-level factors, which are critical for informing policy and subsequent scaling of the program in physician-staffed EMS systems. However, the integration of POCUS into prehospital settings still requires standardisation across all relevant dimensions. This study has several limitations. First, its observational design precludes causal inference regarding the impact of POCUS on patient clinical outcomes (e.g. mortality, morbidity, length of stay). Although trends toward higher admission rates and targeted diagnostic workup were observed in the POCUS group, these patients were generally more critically ill at baseline, which likely influenced both the decision to scan and subsequent care pathways. Second, the study was conducted within a single physician-staffed emergency medical services (EMS) system with only two participating bases, resulting in a sample size that was too small to achieve adequate statistical power. Both factors limit the generalizability of the findings to other services, including paramedic-led or mixed-model services. Third, follow-up relied on hospital records without comprehensive outcome measures such as mortality, morbidity, or length of stay, limiting assessment of definitive patient benefits. Fourth, the technical adequacy of saved images was modest, partly due to the challenging prehospital environment and incomplete image archiving; therefore, our competency assessment may underestimate actual in-field performance in cases where a scan was performed but not recorded. Fifth, the low scan volume per provider over six months restricted opportunities for skill consolidation, suggesting image quality might improve with increased case exposure. Sixth, the 6-month follow-up period provides data on the initial implementation phase but does not allow evaluation of long-term sustainability. Consequently, the Maintenance dimension (M) of the RE‑AIM framework has not been analysed or applied in depth in this study. However, even without the depth of the original study, a remarkable adherence and exponential growth of the technique have been demonstrated among advanced life support emergency teams, accompanied by enhanced equipment availability and a 600% increase in recorded examinations, underscoring the program's potential impact among professionals. Further studies are needed to analyse the concordance between trained personnel and instructors, as well as to conduct longer-term follow-up (12–24 months) of competencies. It is also necessary to assess the evaluation process, the maintenance of skills, the utilization rates of the technique, and whether the program remains operationally feasible without the active support of mentors. Finally, the investigators, who also acted as instructors and mentors, prospectively recorded each ultrasound attempt, reviewed the stored images, and provided case-specific feedback. This could introduce performance bias, which we sought to mitigate by including two experts and blinding their assessment of the images and cases to each other. Future multicentre controlled studies with larger sample sizes and standardized patient outcome measures are needed to confirm the impact of continuous remote mentorship and even real-time tele-guidance during missions, which could enhance image quality and operator confidence.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that a blended POCUS training programme with remote mentoring enabled board-certified prehospital emergency physicians to selectively and appropriately integrate ultrasound into high-acuity out-of-hospital cases, achieving high interpretive accuracy despite variable image quality.

POCUS use did not extend scene times and often influenced hospital diagnostic pathways, expediting critical interventions in selected cases. However, higher hospital admission rates among POCUS patients likely reflected greater illness severity. Barriers such as reduced nighttime utilization and technical acquisition challenges highlight the need for ongoing skills development, quality assurance, and system-level integration to maximize the uptake of POCUS in prehospital care.
