LECT2 deficiency contributes to bile acid metabolic reprogramming and cholestatic liver injury
Authors: Wenbo Shi, Luze Cen, Jing Huang, Xinyi Lei, Ying Wang, Shan Wang, Jiayi Ying, Yuanyuan Li, Yuxia Ma, Yimeng Fang, Aiming Liu, Caide Lu, Manyun Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cholestatic liver injury involves impaired bile acid (BA) formation or flow, leading to toxic hepatic BA accumulation, yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Leukocyte cell-derived chemotaxin 2 (LECT2) has been implicated in liver metabolic disorders; however, its specific role in cholestasis remains incompletely understood.Methods:ANIT was administered to wild-type (WT) and LECT2 knockout (KO) mice to establish an intrahepatic cholestasis model. Metabolomics and metagenomics were performed to discover the role of BA metabolism and the gut–liver axis in cholestatic liver injury. Clinical samples were analyzed to assess the relationship between LECT2 and cholestatic liver injury.Results:LECT2 deletion was associated with altered BA synthesis, characterized by a shift toward the classical pathway with upregulation of CYP7A1 and CYP8B1. Under cholestatic conditions, LECT2 deficiency was associated with aggravated liver injury, accompanied by alterations in gut microbiota composition, changes in intestinal FXR–FGF15 signaling, and increased hepatic JNK activation. In KO mice, HDCA supplementation restored the alternative synthesis pathway, FMT reshaped gut microbiota, and antibiotic cocktail treatment suppressed intestinal FXR signaling, each of which was associated with improved cholestatic liver injury. In clinical samples, LECT2 levels were negatively correlated with markers of cholestasis, supporting its potential relevance to disease severity.Conclusion:LECT2 deficiency is associated with aggravated cholestatic liver injury, which may involve altered BA synthesis, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and modulation of intestinal FXR–hepatic JNK signaling. These findings offer new insights into the role of LECT2 in regulating metabolism and identify potential therapeutic targets for managing cholestatic liver injury.

INTRODUCTION

Cholestasis is a medical condition characterized by abnormal accumulation of bile acids (BAs) within the liver. Elevated intrahepatic concentrations trigger the release of proinflammatory mediators and signaling factors, subsequently inducing liver inflammation. This inflammatory response damages hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, leading to cholestatic liver injury. 1 Clinically, cholestasis typically manifests as jaundice, intractable pruritus, and fat malabsorption. 2 In the absence of effective treatment, ongoing liver damage can progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and ultimately liver failure. 3 Yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, which limits further drug discovery and therapeutic development.

Disruption of BA synthesis homeostasis is a key pathological factor in cholestasis. BAs are synthesized in the liver predominantly via the classical pathway, with a lesser contribution from the alternative pathway. The classical 7α-hydroxylation of cholesterol by cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1)/sterol 12α-hydroxylase (CYP8B1) pathway mainly generates cholic acid (CA), whereas the alternative sterol-27-hydroxylase (CYP27A1)/oxysterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7B1) pathway produces chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), which is further converted to muricholic acids in rodents. 4 CYP8B1 plays a critical role in CA biosynthesis by regulating the CA to CDCA ratio. In non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and diabetic models, downregulation of CYP7B1 suppresses the alternative BA synthesis pathway, leading to a relative increase in 12-hydroxylated BAs, such as CA, which contributes to disease progression.5–7 Conversely, CYP8B1 deficiency reduces 12-hydroxylated BA production in the classical pathway, shifting the BA pool toward non-12-hydroxylated species, predominantly CDCA, and thereby improving metabolic outcomes.8,9 These findings indicate that altering the expression ratio of CYP8B1 to CYP7B1 could restore BA homeostasis; however, the therapeutic significance of this approach in the management of cholestatic conditions remains unexplored.

Gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the circulation of BAs. Under physiological conditions, primary BAs are metabolized into secondary BAs by the gut microbiota, with ~95% of the total BA pool actively reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and transported back to the liver to maintain BA homeostasis. 10 Conversely, under pathological conditions, gut microbiota dysbiosis disrupts this balance by altering the BA pool composition, inhibiting intestinal reabsorption, and subsequently aggravating the accumulation of BA in the liver. 11 In spontaneous cholestasis models, resulting from impaired farnesoid X receptor (FXR) signaling, pronounced gut microbiota dysbiosis and distinct alterations in serum metabolomic profiles have been observed. 12 In a mouse model of primary sclerosing cholangitis, gut microbiota depletion significantly exacerbated hepatobiliary injury and pathological progression. 13 These studies indicate that the gut microbiota plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of cholestatic liver injury.

Leukocyte cell-derived chemotaxin 2 (LECT2) represents a multifunctional 16-kDa protein predominantly secreted by the liver. Notably, earlier clinical studies have revealed that serum LECT2 levels in patients with acute liver failure are significantly negatively correlated with hepatic injury biomarkers, including AST and ALT, suggesting that LECT2 may provide hepatoprotective effects. 14 This finding was further supported by a concanavalin A-induced liver injury mouse model, where LECT2 deletion mice were found to exhibit a more severe hepatic injury. 15 Accumulating evidence further established that LECT2 regulates inflammatory responses, angiogenesis, and metabolic processes by binding to receptors CD209a, VEGFR2, or Tie1.16–18 Despite its involvement in the pathogenesis of hepatic diseases, its specific role and underlying mechanisms in cholestasis remain unclear.

This study aimed to elucidate the role of LECT2 and underlying mechanisms in cholestatic liver injury. The investigation was conducted using a combination of clinical sample analysis and α-naphthylisothiocyanate (ANIT)-induced models in wild-type (WT) and LECT2 knockout (KO) mice. Metabolomics and metagenomics were employed to profile BAs and gut microbiota, while the impact of LECT2 deletion on intestinal FXR and hepatic JNK signaling was assessed to uncover the mechanisms linking BA metabolism, gut–liver axis dysfunction, and cholestatic liver injury. In KO mice, our findings highlight a potential role of the gut–liver axis in LECT2 deficiency–associated cholestatic liver injury, as evidenced by concurrent alterations in BA synthesis, gut microbiota composition, and intestinal FXR–FGF15 signaling. These results provide new insights into the role of LECT2 in BA metabolism and suggest potential avenues for therapeutic intervention in cholestatic liver disease.

METHODS

Animals and treatments

Male 6–8 weeks old C57BL/6N wild type (WT) and LECT2 knockout (KO) background mice, weighing 24±2 g, were housed at the Laboratory Animal Center of Ningbo University for 7 days to acclimatize them. The animals were maintained at a controlled temperature of 21–25 °C, humidity of 60%–70%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Animals had free access to a standard rodent diet and sterilized drinking water during the study. Animal procedures were ethically approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Review Board of the Medical School of Ningbo University, China (Approval No. NBU20220258). LECT2 knockout mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. 19

Animal experiment 1 was designed to investigate the effects of ANIT-induced cholestasis in both the WT and KO mice. The WT and KO mice were assigned to 4 groups (n=5): WT-CON, WT-ANIT, KO-CON, and KO-ANIT. Mice in the WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups received ANIT (75 mg/kg) through oral gavage, while those in the WT-CON and KO-CON groups were administered an equivalent dose of corn oil vehicle.

In experiment 2, daily oral gavage of hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA) was administered to elucidate the role of dysregulated hepatic classic and alternative BA biosynthetic pathways in the pathogenesis of cholestatic liver injury. Notably, the WT and KO mice were categorized into 8 groups (n=5): WT-CON, WT-ANIT, WT-HDCA, WT-HDCA-ANIT, KO-CON, KO-ANIT, KO-HDCA, and KO-HDCA-ANIT groups. The WT-HDCA, WT-HDCA-ANIT, KO-HDCA, and KO-HDCA-ANIT groups were administered HDCA via oral gavage at a dose of 100 mg/kg/day for 7 days, while the remianing groups received a vehicle comprising 6% NaHCO3 solution only. 6 On day 5, ANIT (75 mg/kg) was administered to the designated groups, while the WT-CON and KO-CON groups received a vehicle comprising corn oil vehicle.

To assess the role of Parabacteroides and Alistipes in the pathogenesis of cholestatic liver injury, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) was conducted in experiment 3. We performed the FMT according to the procedures established in a previous study. 20 The WT and KO mice were classified into 4 groups (n=5): WT-ANIT, WT-ANIT-FMT, KO-ANIT, and KO-ANIT-FMT groups. The experiment lasted 14 days, with all 4 groups intragastrically administered with vancomycin (100 mg/kg), neomycin sulfate (200 mg/kg), metronidazole (200 mg/kg), and ampicillin sodium (200 mg/kg) once a day for the first 7 consecutive days to deplete the gut microbiota. For the subsequent 7 days, the WT-FMT and KO-FMT groups were administered 0.2 mL of fecal suspension via oral gavage daily, while the control groups received an equal volume of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Notably, all mice in the 4 groups were administered 75 mg/kg of ANIT via oral gavage 48 hours before the conclusion of the experiment. Feces from donor mice (WT-CON or KO-CON) were sampled, resuspended in PBS at 0.05 g/mL, followed by centrifugation and collection of the supernatant.

To confirm the involvement of the intestinal FXR pathway in cholestatic liver injury, an antibiotic cocktail intervention was performed in experiment 4. The WT and KO mice were divided into 4 groups (n=5): WT-ANIT, WT-ABX-ANIT, KO-ANIT, and KO-ABX-ANIT groups. The WT-ABX-ANIT and KO-ABX-ANIT groups were administered a mixture of antibiotics once a day for 7 consecutive days to deplete the gut microbiota. The antibiotic mixture was similar to that utilized in experiment 3. A single dose of ANIT (75 mg/kg) was administered to the designated groups on day 5.

In vivo experiments involved weighing and subsequent euthanization of the mice through carbon dioxide asphyxiation. Gallbladder tissues were harvested, and the gallbladder index was calculated using the proportional formula. In addition, the collected whole blood samples were centrifuged to obtain plasma. Half of the left lobe of the liver was harvested and immediately fixed using 10% formalin, while the remaining tissues were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved at −80 °C for further experiments. Furthermore, the ileum and cecum were harvested and stored at −80 °C for further experiments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism version 10.2. Data were presented as mean±SEM. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student t test, while one-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey post hoc test was applied for multi-group comparisons. Correlation analysis was performed using the Pearson correlation test. Linear regression analysis was used to assess the relationship between variables. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) and p values were calculated. Significance levels were set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001; “ns” implies “not significant.”

Additional methods

Further details of the methodology are described in the supplemental materials, including the baseline characteristics of the clinical participants (Supplemental Table 1) and the primer sequences used for quantitative real-time PCR (Supplemental Table 2), which are available at http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416.

RESULTS

LECT2 deletion exacerbated cholestatic liver injury

Morphologically, the gallbladders were similar in the WT-CON and KO-CON groups but were enlarged in both ANIT-treated groups. Gallbladder weight and coefficient were consistent with the observed results (Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416). Compared with the WT-ANIT group, gallbladder enlargement was more pronounced in the KO-ANIT group, with the bile exhibiting dark-green coloration (Figure 1A). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the liver showed normal histology in both control groups. Conversely, ANIT-treated groups exhibited a loss of cellular boundaries and significant necrosis. Notably, the KO-ANIT group showed more severe hepatocellular degeneration and necrosis compared with the WT-ANIT group (Figure 1B).

Cholestatic markers were markedly elevated. ALP and TBA levels increased by 4-fold and 61-fold, respectively, in the WT-ANIT group, and by 3-fold and 93-fold in the KO-ANIT group (Figure 1C). Levels of TBIL and DBIL increased by 35-fold and 41-fold in the WT-ANIT group, and the corresponding increases were 49-fold and 44-fold in the KO-ANIT group (Figure 1D). With the exception of DBIL, all measured indicators were elevated in the KO-ANIT group compared with the WT-ANIT group. Serum ALT and AST levels are key biochemical indicators for evaluating the degree of hepatic injury. Compared with the corresponding control groups, the WT-ANIT group showed 15-fold and 10-fold increases in serum ALT and AST, respectively. These increases were further elevated in the KO-ANIT group to 30-fold and 12-fold, respectively (Figure 1E). In the animal model induced by 1% DDC diet, similar trends were observed for these biomarkers (Supplemental Figure S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416).

The KO-ANIT group exhibited elevated levels of Il-6, Il-1b, and Ccl2 mRNA levels compared with the WT-ANIT group (Figure 1F). The mRNA levels of c-Fos and c-Jun, which are target genes for the JNK pathway, were significantly elevated in both ANIT-treated groups, with a greater increase observed in the KO-ANIT group. The mRNA levels of the STAT3 target genes, Socs3, Fgg, Fga, and Fgb, were not upregulated in the WT groups. However, Socs3 mRNA expression was increased by 8-fold, while Fgg mRNA expression was increased by 3-fold in the KO-ANIT group compared with the KO-CON group (Supplemental Figure S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416). These results indicate differential activation of the STAT3 signaling pathways between the WT and KO mice. Western blot analysis showed significantly increased phosphorylation of JNK (p-JNK), with bands corresponding to the p46 and p54 isoforms, in the KO-ANIT group compared with the WT-ANIT group. Regarding the STAT3 pathway, phosphorylated STAT3 was downregulated in KO-CON mice compared with WT-CON mice. Both phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) and phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-p65) were upregulated in both ANIT-treated groups; p-p65 activation was significantly stronger in the KO-ANIT group (Figure 1G). Taken together with the mRNA expression data, these results suggest that the JNK/NF-κB/STAT3 signaling pathway likely mediates a stronger inflammatory response to cholestatic liver injury in the KO mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LECT2 deletion exacerbates cholestatic liver injury.

BA synthesis in the classical pathway was strengthened by LECT2 deletion

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the liver tissues revealed significant separation between ANIT and CON groups (Figure 2A). Discriminatory variables from the S-plot of the ANIT and CON groups are presented in Figure 2B. A total of 16 associated ions were identified as CA, TCA, DCA, TDCA, HDCA, UDCA, LCA, ωMCA, βMCA, αMCA, THDCA, TUDCA, TCDCA, TωMCA, TαMCA, and TβMCA in comparison to authentic standards. 21 Further analysis revealed that among the liver non-12-OH BAs, ωMCA, βMCA, TωMCA, TαMCA, and TβMCA were all elevated in both the WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups, while THDCA and TUDCA were reduced (Figure 2C). For 12-OH BAs, CA in the WT-ANIT group was increased by ~27-fold compared wth the WT-CON group. The KO-ANIT group exhibited a ~90-fold increase in CA compared with the KO-CON group. The KO-ANIT group exhibited ~3-fold elevated levels of CA compared with the WT-ANIT group (Figure 2D).

In both the WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups, the activation of the liver FXR–SHP signaling pathway suppressed the expression of genes associated with BA biosynthesis, including Cyp7a1, Cyp8b1, Cyp27a1, and Cyp7b1 (Figure 2E). Notably, the mRNA expression levels of Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1 in the KO-CON group were significantly higher compared with those in the WT-CON group. The WB analysis findings aligned with the mRNA data, further validating this phenomenon (Figure 2F). In the KO-CON group, the mRNA levels of Cyp27a1 and Cyp7b1 were consistent with those in the WT-CON group, but the protein expression was reduced (Figure 2G). Interestingly, CYP8B1 protein levels remained relatively high in both WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups and did not show a corresponding decrease. We performed multiple qPCR analyses with different primer sets,22,23 which consistently showed reduced CYP8B1 mRNA levels (Supplemental Figure S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416). The discrepancy between mRNA and protein levels suggests the involvement of post-transcriptional and/or post-translational regulatory mechanisms, which may be partly attributable to reduced protein degradation or BA accumulation.

We have also examined the expression of key BA transporters in our samples, and the results are as follows (Supplemental Figure S5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416). The expression of transporters in the liver of LECT2-KO mice showed no significant changes compared to WT mice. After ANIT treatment, mRNA expression levels of Bsep, Ostb, and Mrp4 were upregulated compared with the control group, facilitating BA efflux. In contrast, the mRNA expression levels of Ntcp and Oatp2 were downregulated, which reduces the reuptake of BAs from the portal vein. These changes in transporter expression are consistent with previous findings. 21 These findings—specifically that BA synthesis in the classical pathway was strengthened by LECT2 deletion—indicated that LECT2 may play a crucial role in modulating BA homeostasis by influencing the key enzymes involved in its biosynthesis.

Activation of the nonclassical BA pathway improved cholestatic liver injury

Experimental findings demonstrated that LECT2 deletion facilitates comprehensive reprogramming of BA metabolism, involving activation of classical pathways and suppression of alternative pathways. Existing evidence has shown that HDCA can alter hepatic BA synthesis pathways by activating the CYP7B1-associated alternative pathway. 6 Therefore, we conducted a 7-day HDCA intervention in the ANIT-induced cholestatic mice to evaluate its regulatory effects on BA metabolism and potential therapeutic effects.

HDCA alone did not exert any effect in both mouse strains. In WT mice, serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, and TBA were significantly decreased after HDCA administration compared with the ANIT group (Figures 3A, B). In the KO mice, HDCA treatment decreased serum ALT and TBA levels by ~50% compared with the KO-ANIT group, while AST and ALP levels remained unchanged, indicating dysregulated BA homeostasis due to LECT2 deletion (Figures 3C, D). In addition, in both mouse strains, HDCA treatment significantly reduced mRNA levels of Il-6, Il-1b, and Ccl2 compared with those in the ANIT group (Figures 3E, F). The H&E staining of the liver revealed that both the control and HDCA groups exhibited normal liver tissue. The ANIT-treated groups displayed more extensive hepatocellular degeneration and larger necrotic areas. HDCA administration markedly reduced the extent of necrosis (Figure 3G). These results indicate that HDCA intervention effectively alleviates ANIT-induced cholestatic liver injury, and its protective effect may be associated with the activation of the alternative BA synthesis pathway.

LECT2 deficiency exacerbates intestinal BA dysregulation during cholestasis and is accompanied by FXR–Fgf15 activation

PCA of ileum content showed that the WT-CON and KO-CON groups clustered together, while the WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups were distinctly separated (Figure 4A). Discriminatory variables from the S-plot and permutation test results for overfitting are shown in Figures 4B, C. Analysis of ileal 12-OH BAs revealed that CA levels were elevated 2-fold in the WT-ANIT and 4-fold in the KO-ANIT groups, whereas TCA levels increased 1.8-fold in the WT-ANIT but decreased 3.2-fold in the KO-ANIT groups (Figure 4D). Non-12-OH BAs, including HDCA, UDCA, LCA, ωMCA, βMCA, αMCA, TαMCA, and TβMCA, were significantly downregulated in both the WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups compared with the control groups (Figure 4E). Notably, the levels of TαMCA and TβMCA in the KO-ANIT group were decreased by 9.5-fold and 5.3-fold, respectively, compared with those in the WT-ANIT group. TαMCA, TβMCA, and HDCA have been identified as endogenous FXR antagonists. This coordinated decrease in FXR inhibitors suggests that LECT2 deficiency may be associated with excessive activation of intestinal FXR signaling during cholestasis.

Further analysis revealed that Fgf15 and Shp mRNA expression levels were significantly elevated in the KO-ANIT group, but remained unchanged in the WT-ANIT group (Figure 4F). Consistently, serum FGF15 levels were elevated in both the ANIT-treated groups compared with the controls, with the KO-ANIT group exhibiting a 3-fold increase compared with the WT-ANIT group (Figure 4G). Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated FGF15 levels can activate hepatic JNK signaling, thereby aggravating liver injury in mice. 24 Combined with the observed JNK hyperactivation and aggravated liver injury in KO-ANIT mice, these results suggest that the FGF15/JNK axis may be involved in the progression of liver injury mediated by LECT2 deficiency.

LECT2 deletion elevated bile salt-hydrolyzing enzymes and diminished beneficial probiotics

PCA of ileum content revealed distinct clustering between the WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups, suggesting genotype-specific alterations in the gut microbiota composition under cholestatic conditions. To further characterize gut microbiota composition, metagenomic sequencing was performed on fecal samples from the 4 experimental mouse groups. Alpha diversity indices (Shannon and Simpson) showed a significant reduction in the diversity of the microbial community in both the WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups. While no significant alterations were observed in both the Chao and Ace indices in the WT-ANIT group compared with controls, these richness estimators exhibited a significant decline in the KO-ANIT group relative to the KO-CON group (Supplemental Figures S6A, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and PCA revealed clear separation among the 4 clusters, which was further confirmed by ANOSIM analysis (Supplemental Figures S6C, D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416). Subsequently, we evaluated the microbiome composition of the 4 groups at different taxonomic levels. Bacteroidota and Bacillota dominated the control group, while Pseudomonadota was the dominant phylum in the WT-ANIT group, and Verrucomicrobia and Pseudomonadota were the dominant phyla in the KO-ANIT group (Supplemental Figure S6E, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416). At the genus level, Bacteroides was notably diminished in the ANIT groups, while Escherichia and Akkermansia were relatively abundant (Supplemental Figure S6F, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C416).

To identify key bacterial genera potentially involved in ANIT-induced cholestatic liver injury, a high-dimensional taxonomic comparison was conducted using linear discriminant analysis (LDA > 3.5, p < 0.05), and effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify specific gut microbiota and explore dissimilarities in the fecal microbiota. We identified 20 significantly different microbial genera, with 10 enriched in the WT-ANIT group and 10 in the KO-ANIT group (Figures 5A, B). At the species level, Parabacteroides and Alistipes were predominant in the WT-ANIT group compared with the KO-ANIT group (Figure 5C). Existing evidence suggests that the genera Parabacteroides and Alistipes may exhibit hepatoprotective effects. Parabacteroides has been shown to significantly inhibit the progression of liver fibrosis, whereas sustained abundance of Alistipes is closely associated with the amelioration of hepatic metabolic disorders. 25 The abundance of bile salt hydrolase-producing bacterial taxa, particularly Enterococcus and members of the order, was significantly elevated in the KO-ANIT group compared with the WT-ANIT group (Figures 5D, E). This increase in BSH-producing bacterial taxa was strongly associated with reduced TβMCA levels. These findings suggest that increased bile salt hydrolase activity lowers ileal TβMCA levels, thereby alleviating inhibition of FXR signaling in the intestine.

FMT rescued LECT2 deletion–induced liver injury

To assess whether the gut microbiota mediates LECT2-dependent progression of cholestatic liver injury, we designed an FMT experiment as outlined in Figure 6A. The WT recipients received microbiota from KO-CON donors, while KO recipients were transplanted with microbiota derived from the WT-CON mice. In the WT mice, the FMT-treated group exhibited no significant differences in serum ALT, AST, ALP, or TBA levels, nor in the extent of hepatocellular necrosis, compared with the ANIT-treated group (Figures 6B–D), indicating that KO-derived microbiota alone was insufficient to ameliorate liver injury in WT mice. In contrast, the FMT-treated group of KO mice exhibited significantly downregulated serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, and TBA, along with reduced hepatocyte necrotic area compared with the KO-ANIT group (Figures 6E–G). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that the severity of cholestatic liver injury depended on gut microbiota composition, and FMT supplemented with Parabacteroides and Alistipes ameliorated LECT2 deletion-associated liver injury.

Antibiotic treatment improves cholestatic liver injury in LECT2-deficient mice, accompanied by reduced intestinal FGF15 expression

Evidence from mouse models shows that FXR knockout can alleviate liver injury induced by biliary obstruction. 26 Based on this finding, we treated mice with a cocktail of antibiotics (ABX) for 7 days to deplete the gut microbiota. This treatment effectively recapitulates intestinal FXR deficiency. Compared with the ANIT group, the levels of ALT and AST were significantly decreased in the WT-ABX-ANIT and KO-ABX-ANIT groups, accompanied by suppressed hepatocellular degeneration and reduced necrosis area after 1 week of antibiotic pre-treatment (Figures 7A, B). Compared with the WT-ANIT group and KO-ANIT group, the WT-ABX-ANIT group and KO-ABX-ANIT group had markedly lower mRNA levels of Il-6, Il-1b, and Ccl2 (Figures 7C, D). The WT-ABX-ANIT group showed a 30% reduction in FGF15 levels compared with the WT-ANIT group, whereas the KO-ABX-ANIT group exhibited a 50% decrease in FGF15 levels relative to the KO-ANIT group (Figure 7E). These findings indicate that antibiotic-mediated depletion of the gut microbiota alleviates cholestatic liver injury in Lect2-KO mice, potentially by downregulating the intestinal FXR–FGF15 axis.

LECT2 negatively correlated with clinical cholestasis biomarkers

Serum samples from 17 healthy controls, 19 patients with gallstones (non-cholestatic), and 9 patients with cholestasis were used to investigate the potential association between LECT2 and cholestasis. The results showed no significant difference in LECT2 levels between the healthy control and gallstone groups; however, levels were ~50% lower in the cholestasis group compared with healthy controls (Figure 8A). In addition, LECT2 levels were negatively correlated with cholestasis-associated biomarkers—including ALP, GGT, TBA, TBIL, and DBIL (Figures 8B–F). OPLS-DA and PLS-DA plots revealed a clear separation in metabolic profiles between the healthy control group and both the gallstone and cholestasis groups (Figures 8G, H). The S-plot analysis results between healthy controls and the cholestasis group are shown in Figure 8I, with the significantly discriminatory variables appropriately labeled. Further analysis revealed that the levels of 12-OH BAs, including GCA and TCA, were elevated by 6.5-fold and 17.5-fold, respectively, relative to healthy controls. In contrast, the levels of non-12-OH BAs, including GHCA and TCDCA, were increased by 10-fold and 5-fold, respectively, relative to the healthy controls (Figures 8J, K). Overall, the raising fold changes in 12-OH BAs were greater compared with those observed in non-12-OH BAs. These findings suggest a potential association between reduced circulating LECT2 and cholestatic features in patients, providing translational support for the observations from animal models.

DISCUSSION

LECT2 is a multifunctional protein, acting not only as a chemokine but also playing roles in liver injury, fibrosis, cancer, and metabolic disorders. 17 Using murine models of cholestatic liver injury, we found that the levels of liver function parameters (ALT, AST, ALP, TBA, and TBIL) as well as the severity of histopathological damage were elevated in LECT2-KO mice compared with WT mice. In addition, analysis of clinical sample showed that LECT2 levels were negatively correlated with cholestatic markers. These findings suggest that LECT2 is associated with the progression of cholestatic liver injury.

BAs synthesized via the classical and alternative pathways in the liver can be classified into 2 categories based on their chemical structures: 12-OH BAs and non-12-OH BAs. In this study, LECT2 deletion resulted in a marked increase in the expression of CYP7A1 and CYP8B1. In contrast, CYP7B1 expression was significantly downregulated. Notably, the upregulation of CYP7A1 and CYP8B1 in KO mice was consistent with the BA profile observed in cholestasis, characterized by increased production of 12-OH BAs. Activation of the alternative BA synthesis pathway is generally associated with a higher proportion of non-12-OH BAs, which are considered less hydrophobic and have been linked to improved metabolic outcomes.4,27–29 Consistently, pharmacological activation of the alternative pathway using HDCA was associated with attenuation of cholestatic liver injury in both WT and LECT2-deficient mice. These findings suggest that LECT2 deficiency is associated with altered regulation of BA synthesis pathways and BA composition.

Under physiological conditions, hepatic inflammatory signaling and BA synthesis are tightly regulated, with inflammatory pathways such as STAT3 signaling reported to suppress CYP7A1 expression. 30 In this study, LECT2-KO mice showed reduced hepatic p-STAT3 levels, accompanied by increased expression of Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1. Given that STAT3 activation has been shown to inhibit BA synthesis gene expression, these findings suggest that reduced STAT3 signaling may be associated with derepression of BA synthesis in LECT2-deficient mice. 31 Previous studies have indicated that LECT2 can modulate hepatic inflammatory responses, including cytokine expression such as IL-6, which is an upstream regulator of STAT3 activation. 32 Therefore, it is plausible that LECT2 deficiency may influence STAT3 signaling indirectly through inflammatory mediators; however, this regulatory axis remains to be further investigated.

The gut microbiota and BA synthetic pathways are engaged in a bidirectional regulatory network that contributes to their dynamic equilibrium. 33 Here, the results revealed that LECT2 deletion induced significant alterations in intestinal BA composition and specific changes in gut microbiota structure. Probiotic bacteria, such as Parabacteroides distasonis and Alistipes, were significantly enriched in WT-ANIT but were strongly depleted in KO-ANIT. This depletion is functionally significant, as Parabacteroides distasonis has been shown to alleviate liver fibrosis, and Alistipes has been shown to improve metabolic profiles in murine models.34,35 The functional importance of these microbial shifts was confirmed by FMT experiments. Transplantation of fecal suspensions from WT mice rich in Alistipes and Parabacteroides distasonis caused a significant improvement of cholestatic liver injury in KO mice. In contrast, transplantation of fecal suspensions from KO mice lacking these 2 bacterial species into ANIT-treated WT mice failed to ameliorate liver injury. The data suggest that reduced abundance of these 2 bacterial genera may contribute to the aggravated ANIT-induced cholestatic liver injury in LECT2-KO mice.

Gut microbiota composition can influence bile salt hydrolase (BSH) activity, particularly within Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes), including genera such as Lactobacillus and Enterococcus, which have been reported to deconjugate amino acid–conjugated BAs such as TβMCA. 36 This process may reduce intestinal FXR antagonism and thereby influence FXR signaling activity. In this study, KO-ANIT mice showed a higher relative abundance of Enterococcus and Lactobacillales compared with WT-ANIT mice, accompanied by reduced levels of the FXR-antagonistic BA TβMCA and increased expression of the intestinal FXR target gene Fgf15. The intestinal FXR–FGF15 signaling pathway is a key regulator of BA homeostasis and enterohepatic circulation, and its dysregulation has been associated with hepatic inflammatory responses, including JNK activation, in cholestatic liver injury.37,38 Consistently, hepatic JNK phosphorylation was increased in KO mice during cholestatic injury. Previous studies have shown that antibiotic treatment can modulate BA pools and is associated with suppression of intestinal FXR–FGF15 signaling, partly through increasing TβMCA levels.39,40 In line with these reports, ABX treatment reduced Fgf15 expression in both WT and KO mice, with a more pronounced effect in KO mice, and was associated with improvement of liver injury. Taken together, these findings suggest that LECT2 deficiency is associated with alterations in gut microbiota, BA composition, and intestinal FXR–FGF15 signaling, which may relate to aggravated cholestatic liver injury.

In this study, under baseline (untreated) conditions, LECT2-KO mice exhibited significantly higher hepatic expression of Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1 compared with WT mice, whereas serum FGF15 levels remained comparable between genotypes, suggesting that compensatory mechanisms may maintain BA–FGF15 homeostasis under physiological conditions.41,42 Following ANIT-induced cholestasis, serum FGF15 levels were markedly increased in LECT2-KO mice relative to WT controls, accompanied by suppression of hepatic Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1 transcription in both genotypes. This suppression may reflect the integrated effects of multiple regulatory pathways, including intestinal FXR–FGF15 signaling via hepatic FGFR4/β-Klotho, hepatic FXR–SHP–mediated transcriptional repression, and additional nuclear receptor–related and inflammation-related pathways.43–45 Notably, CYP8B1 protein levels remained relatively high in both WT-ANIT and KO-ANIT groups, with higher levels observed in KO mice, which is consistent with altered BA composition. The discrepancy between mRNA and protein levels suggests post-transcriptional and/or post-translational regulation. Overall, these findings indicate that BA synthesis is differentially regulated under physiological and cholestatic conditions, and that LECT2 deficiency is associated with context-dependent alterations in BA homeostasis and gut–liver axis signaling.

It is also important to note substantial species differences in BA metabolism between mice and humans. In humans, CDCA remains a major primary BA, whereas in mice it is largely converted into MCAs. 46 Therefore, caution is warranted when comparing BA composition across species, particularly the ratio of 12-OH to non-12-OH BAs. CYP8B1-associated BA remodeling observed in mice may not directly reflect human physiology. To validate our findings, we further analyzed clinical samples, which should be regarded as preliminary and provid translational support rather than direct evidence of identical mechanisms in humans. Further studies in larger, better-characterized cohorts and more physiologically relevant models are needed to confirm clinical relevance.

CONCLUSION

LECT2 deficiency is likely to increase susceptibility to cholestatic liver injury. This phenotype is associated with disrupted gut–liver crosstalk, including reprogrammed BA synthesis (upregulated CYP7A1/CYP8B1 and downregulated CYP7B1), alterations in gut microbiota ( depletion of Alistipes and Parabacteroides), dysregulation of intestinal FXR–FGF15 signaling, and increased hepatic JNK phosphorylation. Collectively, the present results indicate that therapeutic strategies targeting LECT2 and restoring the balance between classical and alternative BA synthesis pathways may be effective in treating cholestatic liver injury.
