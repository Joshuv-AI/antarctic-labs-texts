Deciphering the genomic landscape of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae in South African tertiary hospitals
Authors: Ayavuya T Papiyana, Jolly Musoke, Likhona Dingiswayo, Olusesan A Adelabu, Zothile T Skosana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) is an emerging pathogen causing severe infections globally, yet genomic data from inland South African regions remain limited, hindering understanding of local hvKp diversity and virulence.MethodsElevenhypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates collected in 2021 and 2024 were subjected to WGS. Background

In early 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) issued a global alert following the detection of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) in 16 of 43 reporting countries [1]. Several of these countries also identified isolates that were both hypervirulent and carbapenem-resistant, raising concerns about the emergence and global spread of carbapenem-resistant hvKp.

Unlike classical K. pneumoniae (cKp), which predominantly affects hospitalised or immunocompromised individuals [2]. Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae has the capacity to cause severe, rapidly progressive and often fatal infections in otherwise healthy hosts. Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae has gained global attention due to its ability to cause community-acquired invasive infections, including liver abscesses, meningitis, and endophthalmitis, often affecting young and otherwise healthy individuals. Its hypervirulent phenotype is driven by multiple factors, including the presence of large virulence plasmids encoding regulators of the mucoid phenotype (rmpA, rmpA2, and rmpD), which are correlated with higher virulence both in vivo and in clinical studies, as well as siderophore systems such as aerobactin (iuc), salmochelin (iro), and yersiniabactin (ybt), and enhanced capsule production [3–5].

A distinctive feature of hvKp was initially described as its hypermucoviscous phenotype, based on the string test (> 5 mm viscous string formation) [6]. Hypermucoviscosity has historically been associated with hypervirulence; however, recent evidence indicates that not all hvKP strains exhibit a hypermucoviscous phenotype. Conversely, some cKP strains may be hypermucoviscous without possessing the genetic hallmarks of hypervirulence [7–8]. This phenotypic overlap underscores the limitations of relying solely on the string test for classification and highlights the need for molecular biomarkers to differentiate hvKP from cKP accurately. Genes such as iucA, rmpA, rmpA2, and peg-344 have been identified as key genetic determinants associated with hypervirulence and are increasingly used as reliable markers for hvKP identification [6, 9]. To standardize the evaluation of these markers, the Kleborate tool assigns a virulence score (0 to 5) based on the presence of specific loci. In this framework, a score of 0 indicates the absence of major siderophores, while higher scores denote the acquisition of yersiniabactin (ybt, score 1), colibactin (clb, score 2), or the critical hypervirulence marker aerobactin (iuc, score 3). The highest scores, 4 and 5, represent the convergence of multiple loci, such as the combination of ybt, iuc, and clb [10].

Initially described in East and Southeast Asia, hvKp has now disseminated globally, with increasing reports across Europe, North America, South America, Oceania, and Africa. Although ST23 (typically associated with K1) remains the prototypical hypervirulent lineage, other globally distributed hvKp sequence types include ST65, ST86, ST375, and ST25. In parallel, historically multidrug-resistant epidemic clones such as ST11, ST258, and ST512 have dominated nosocomial outbreaks worldwide [11]. Of particular concern is the increasing convergence between hypervirulence and carbapenem resistance, including reports of ST23 and ST11 acquiring carbapenemase genes in multiple regions, underscoring the evolving global public health threat [1].

Genomic analyses have demonstrated that hvKp strains harbour a characteristic repertoire of virulence loci, including siderophore biosynthesis systems such as iuc and iro, regulators of hypermucoviscosity including rmpA and rmpA2, and metabolic genes such as peg-344 [12]. These determinants are typically located on large mosaic virulence plasmids, often exceeding 200 kb, which may also encode genes involved in metabolic adaptation and enhanced survival within the host [13]. Although the presence of the siderophore-encoding genes is associated with systemic infections, their presence alone is insufficient to define an isolate as hvKp. Similarly, while K1 and K2 capsule types are commonly associated with hypervirulence, they primarily confer resistance to phagocytosis and serum-mediated killing [11]. Recent whole-genome sequencing (WGS) studies have demonstrated increasing genomic convergence between hvKp and cKp driven by horizontal gene transfer [14]. This convergence has resulted in the emergence of carbapenem-resistant hvKp (CR-hvKp), a phenotype that combines hypervirulence with multidrug resistance and represents a significant global health threat [5, 11].

In South Africa, WGS-based surveillance has identified carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae lineages, including NDM-1- and GES-5-producing ST307 and ST101, that also carry hvKp-associated genes such as rmpA and iucA [15]. However, genomic data from low-income countries in Sub-Saharan Africa remain limited, restricting insights into hvKp diversity, clonal distribution, and circulating virulence gene repertoires [16, 17]. Therefore, in this study, isolates are classified as hvKp if they carried the complete aerobactin locus (iucA–D) with or without iutA, corresponding to a Kleborate virulence score ≥ 3.

Methods

Design and setting of the study

In this study, we analysed whole-genome sequencing data from eleven hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates. We analyzed virulence gene profiles, sequence types, antimicrobial resistance determinants, and genomic relationships to global hvKp lineages to contextualise hvKp circulating in South Africa. The study was conducted at the University of the Free State, Department of Medical Microbiology, affiliated with the service laboratory of the province, National Health Laboratory Services (NHLS), Universitas Academic, South Africa. Samples originated from patients admitted to Botshabelo Hospital and Pelonomi Tertiary Hospital obtained from different clinical specimens, including blood cultures, tracheal aspirate, urine and sputum where nine of these were reported in a study published by Dingiswayo et al. 2024 [16].

In that earlier work, genomic characterisation was limited to targeted sequencing approaches. In contrast, the present study employs whole-genome sequencing to enable comprehensive comparative genomic analyses. Specifically, this study expands upon the prior work by including additional isolates and extended analyses of the genetic repertoire, including multilocus sequence typing (MLST), capsule (K) and lipopolysaccharide (O) locus typing, detailed profiling of virulence determinants and antimicrobial resistance genes, and phylogenomic comparison with publicly available global hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) genomes. These analyses substantially extend the scope and resolution of the previously reported findings.

Clinical Isolates

A total of eleven Klebsiella pneumoniae clinical isolates were included in this study. Of which nine isolates were previously confirmed, collected between March and September 2021 and identified using the VITEK 2® system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) at the NHLS, Department of Medical Microbiology, as described. Two additional isolates collected in September 2024 were processed and identified using the same procedures [16].

Hypermucoviscous phenotype

The isolates were screened for hypermucoviscosity using the string test. Briefly, K. pneumoniae isolates were subcultured overnight at 37 °C on 5% sheep blood agar (DMP, Port Elizabeth). A bacterial loop was used to stretch a “string” of mucoid material from the bacterial colony. A mucoid string longer than 5 mm was indicative of hypermucoviscosity [18]. Only isolates demonstrating a positive string test were included in subsequent analyses.

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted on all eleven isolates using the VITEK 2® 2 AST cards (AST-N) (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France), in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The antimicrobial panel included the following antimicrobials: Ampicillin, Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, Cefuroxime, Cefuroxime axetil, Cefotaxime, Gentamicin, Ciprofloxacin, Nitrofurantoin, Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, Ertapenem, Imipenem and Meropenem. The breakpoints and antimicrobial classifications were determined by the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) to interpret results as susceptible, intermediate or resistant.

DNA purification and whole genome sequencing (WGS)

Crude DNA previously extracted from pure colonies of string test–positive bacterial isolates was further purified using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and quality of the extracted genomic DNA (gDNA) were assessed using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA). Sequencing was carried out at the Agricultural Research Council Biotechnology Platform (Pretoria, South Africa) using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA). The DNA was first fragmented, followed by the addition of dual barcodes to the fragments. Barcoded libraries were purified using Agencourt AMPure magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, USA), which enabled size selection with an average insert size of approximately 300 bp (range, 200–400 bp). Library validation and quantification were performed prior to sequencing using the 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) and the Qubit™ 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), respectively. The validated and quantified libraries were then pooled, and WGS was carried out on an Illumina® MiSeq platform using a v3 standard sequencing kit (Illumina®, San Diego, CA, USA) for 500 cycles (2 × 250 bp paired-end reads), with a 5% PhiX control spike-in.

Sequence editing, genome assembly and analysis

Quality control of the raw sequencing data from the 11 Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates was performed using FastQC v0.12.1 [19] and summarized with MultiQC v1.14 [20]. To eliminate ambiguous bases and low-quality regions, read trimming was carried out using Trimmomatic v0.39 [21].

Genome assembly was conducted using SPAdes v4.1.0 [22] to generate high-quality contigs and scaffolds. Assembly polishing, including correction of misassemblies, gaps, small indels, and single-nucleotide errors, was performed using Pilon v1.24 [23]. The quality of the assembled draft genomes was assessed using QUAST v5.3.0 [24], which evaluated metrics such as N50, L50, and GC contents. Genome completeness was further assessed using BUSCO v5.4.7 [25]. Final genome annotation was performed with Prokka v1.14.6 [26]. Species identity was confirmed by BLASTn analysis of assembled contigs against the NCBI nt database, which showed ≥ 99% identity to Klebsiella pneumoniae.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes and virulence factor genes were predicted from the Prokka-Generated GFF files using multiple tools such as ResFinder (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/Klebsiella/) [accessed on October 14, 2025], Kleborate v3.2.4. Only the high-confidence AMR gene hits with ≥ 90% sequence identity were incorporated into this study.

Furthermore, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and serotyping (K and O loci) were determined in silico on the K. pneumoniae WGS data using the BIGSdb-Klebsiella database and Kleborate v3.2.4. In addition, virulence genes were additionally screened using the BIGSdb-Klebsiella Pasteur database.

Core and pan-genome analysis

Pangenome analysis was conducted using annotated genome assemblies generated by Prokka v1.14.6 [26]. The GFF files from all eleven Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were used as input for Roary v3.13.0, a high-speed pangenome pipeline for prokaryotes [27]. Default parameters were used, with a 95% BLASTp identity threshold for defining orthologous gene clusters. Core, accessory, and unique gene contents were identified and quantified. The resulting presence/absence matrix was used to categorize genes into four groups based on their frequency across isolates. Core genes (present in ≥ 99% of isolates), Soft-core genes (present in 95–99% of isolates), Shell genes (present in 15–95% of isolates), and Cloud genes (present in < 15% of isolates).

The distribution of genes across these categories was visualized as a pie chart. A presence/absence gene matrix was used to generate a pangenome profile and to construct a core genome phylogeny using FastTree v2.1.11 [28]. We used Snippy v.4.6.0 [29] with default parameters to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) among the Klebsiella genomes, using the Klebsiella pneumoniae reference genome (NCBI accession number: NZ_CP019047.1). SNPs were called relative to the reference genome, and the total number of high-quality SNPs per isolate was examined to assess genomic variation. To distinguish true point mutations from those resulting from recombination events, we applied Gubbins v2.4.1 [30] with default settings.

Read mapping and variant calling

Read mapping and variant calling were performed using the trimmed-paired sequencing reads and the Klebsiella pneumoniae reference genome retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (RefSeq accession number NZ_CP019047.1). The reference genome was first indexed using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM v0.7.17) algorithm [31], and high-quality sequencing reads generated after quality trimming using Trimmomatic v0.39, were aligned to the reference genome to produce Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files. The resulting BAM files were processed using Picard tools to mark duplicate reads, thereby minimising biases introduced by PCR amplification. Snippy v.4.6.0 tool was then used to call high-confidence single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and generate a core SNP alignment. Recombination events were detected and filtered using Gubbins to produce a recombination-free alignment, which was subsequently used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree with IQ-TREE using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The analysis included a reference genome from NCBI with accession number NZ_CP019047.1. In addition, publicly available Klebsiella pneumoniae reference genomes were retrieved from the NCBI RefSeq and GenBank databases and included in the phylogenomic analysis to provide global context for the study isolates. The reference dataset comprised hypervirulent and high-risk clonal lineages from multiple geographical regions, including NTUH-K2044 from Taiwan (GCF_000009885), hvKP 287 and hvKP 366 from China (GCF_041281675 and GCF_041222775), GCKp15 and GCKp1 from Bangladesh (GCF_016762355 and GCF_016762615), ST101 and ST147 strains from Egypt (GCF_004803095 and GCF_004802955), and a European clinical strain from France (GCF_000968155). Detailed information on accession numbers, strain names, countries of isolation, and sources for all reference genomes is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Hypermucoviscous phenotype

All eleven isolates were positive on the string test, confirming the hypermucoviscous phenotype.

Genome assembly statistics

Whole-genome assemblies were obtained for 11 Klebsiella isolates with high sequencing depth (55×–186× coverage). Genome sizes were highly consistent across isolates, ranging from 5,348,134 bp to 5,348,764 bp. The number of predicted coding sequences varied slightly (4,886-4,963 CDS), reflecting minor genomic variation among isolates.

Assembly contiguity differed between samples, with contig numbers (≥ 1,000 bp) ranging from 72 to 977. Isolates 61 and 64 demonstrated the highest assembly contiguity (N50 values of 128,061 bp and 148,196 bp, respectively), while isolates 24 and 62 exhibited more fragmented assemblies. GC content ranged from 52.88% to 57.21%, consistent with reported values for Klebsiella species. Genome completeness was high across all isolates, with BUSCO completeness values between 90.0% and 99.2%. Duplicated BUSCOs were minimal (0.0–3.1%), indicating low redundancy and generally high assembly quality, with varied contiguity among isolates. (Table 1). Therefore, while contiguity metrics varied among isolates, these differences did not materially influence downstream comparative genomic analyses or alter the overall interpretation of the study findings.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants, virulence profiles, capsule (K) loci, and O-antigen types

We identified ten AMR-associated genes (Table 2) across the isolates, with mdtL and emrD detected in two isolates each, mdtH in three isolates, and bmr3 in one isolate. Heavy-metal resistance genes (copA, copB, pcoC, copD) were common, occurring in 4–6 isolates. The mercury-resistance regulator merR1 appeared in two isolates. Genomic virulence profiling of the 11 Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates revealed substantial heterogeneity in siderophore and hypermucoviscosity-associated determinants. Aerobactin genes (iucA–D ± iutA), which are considered key markers of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKp), were detected in five isolates (45.5%): ST23 (Sample 61), ST86 (Sample 63), ST714 (Sample 64), ST2039 (Sample 62), and ST29 (Sample 51). Among these, three isolates (Samples 61, 63, and 64) additionally carried the hypermucoviscosity regulator rmpA (with Sample 64 also harbouring rmpA2), representing classical hvKp genotypes. Notably, Sample 61 belonged to ST23 with K1 capsular type, a lineage strongly associated with invasive hypervirulent infections. Sample 63 demonstrated an expanded virulence repertoire including aerobactin, salmochelin (iroBCDN), yersiniabactin, rmpA, and the colibactin genotoxin locus (clbA–Q), indicating a highly virulent genetic profile.

Pangenome structure of the hypermucoviscous K. pneumoniae isolate

The pan-genome analysis revealed a diverse distribution of gene clusters across the genomes (Fig. 2). A total of ~ 17,989 gene clusters were identified across all K. pneumoniae genomes analysed. Among these, 1,104 genes (≥ 99%) were classified as core genes, representing functions conserved across almost all isolates. No genes were assigned to the soft-core category (95–99%). The majority of the pangenome consisted of 10,531 shell genes (15–95%), shared by subsets of isolates and contributing substantially to genomic diversity. Additionally, 6,354 cloud genes (< 15%) were detected, indicating a large pool of rare or strain-specific genes within the population. The overall distribution highlights the predominance of accessory genes (shell + cloud), emphasising the highly variable nature of the K. pneumoniae genome.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive genomic and phenotypic characterisation of hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae clinical isolates from a low- to middle-income setting in sub-Saharan Africa. By integrating phenotypic testing, virulome and resistome profiling, pangenome analysis, SNP-based phylogenetics, and capsule and O-antigen typing, we demonstrate substantial genomic diversity alongside the presence of well-recognised hypervirulent lineages. Collectively, these findings highlight the increasing clinical and public health relevance of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKp) in this region. Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae is reportedly spreading across the globe and is of great concern as it is shifting the clinical paradigm of an already problematic K. pneumoniae due to its elevated pathogenicity including the ability to cause invasive infections in immunocompetent, and its strong association with community-acquired infections [4].

All eleven isolates exhibited a positive string test, confirming the hypermucoviscous phenotype, a hallmark commonly associated with hvKp. Genomic analysis corroborated this phenotype, particularly among ST23 (K1) and ST86/ST2039/ST14 (K2) isolates, which harboured multiple hypervirulence-associated loci, including rmpA/rmpA2, iro, iuc, kfu, and, in one isolate, the colibactin (clb) gene cluster. These virulence determinants are well established as contributors to enhanced iron acquisition, immune evasion, and tissue invasiveness, and their co-occurrence is strongly associated with severe and invasive infections [4, 13, 39]. The predominance of K1 and K2 capsular types among the most virulent cluster is consistent with global reports identifying these serotypes as central to hvKp pathogenesis. Additionally, the detection of K54 and K58 capsules in isolates with extensive siderophore repertoires further supports emerging evidence that hypervirulence is not restricted to classical K1/K2 backgrounds. O-antigen typing revealed a dominance of O1/O2 variants, which are frequently linked to serum resistance and successful bloodstream dissemination, reinforcing the invasive potential of these isolates [13, 39]. Hierarchical clustering of virulence genes clearly separated the isolates into low- and high-virulence groups. The low-virulence cluster (Fig. 1: comprising Klebsiella01, Klebsiella05, and Klebsiella54 according to Kleborate), largely comprising ST3430-K17 isolates, possessed limited siderophore systems and lacked key hypermucoviscosity regulators, whereas the high-virulence cluster (Fig. 1: including Klebsiella61, Klebsiella62, Klebsiella63, Klebsiella64 and Klebsiella65 as defined by Kleborate) contained multiple iron-acquisition systems and regulatory genes that synergistically enhance pathogenicity. This dichotomy underscores the heterogeneity within hypermucoviscous K. pneumoniae and highlights that hypermucoviscosity alone does not equate to maximal virulence [39–41]. Eight capsular serotypes K1, K2, K5, K16, K20, K54, K57, and KNI are recognised as hypervirulent variants of K. pneumoniae, amongst which our cohort had 3/11 (27%) (Table 3) isolates that had a capsular type of K2 [42–45]. Despite their hypervirulent profiles, all isolates were phenotypically susceptible to the antimicrobial classes tested by the VITEK® 2 system. This observation was supported by genomic analyses, which revealed a limited repertoire of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, predominantly comprising multidrug efflux pumps (mdtL, mdtH, emrD, bmr3) and stress-response or heavy-metal resistance systems (copA, copB, pcoC, copD, merR1) (Table 2). Importantly, no major carbapenemase or extended-spectrum β-lactamase genes, such as bla_KPC,bla_NDM, bla_OXA-48-like, or bla_CTX−M, which are commonly reported in carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae in South Africa and globally, were detected [4, 46].

The resistome profile suggests that virulence, rather than antimicrobial resistance, is currently the dominant adaptive strategy among the study cohort [47]. However, the presence of multiple efflux systems indicates a latent capacity for tolerance to diverse antimicrobial compounds and environmental stressors, which may facilitate survival under selective pressure and predispose these strains to future acquisition of clinically significant antimicrobial-resistant genes. The convergence of hypervirulence and multidrug resistance, although not observed in this dataset, remains a major concern given the documented emergence of such “dual-risk” clones elsewhere [4, 47] .

The pangenome analysis (Fig. 2) revealed an open and highly diverse pangenome, with accessory genes (shell and cloud) comprising over 90% of the total gene clusters. This extensive accessory genome reflects the remarkable genomic plasticity of K. pneumoniae and its capacity for horizontal gene transfer, niche adaptation, and rapid evolution [48]. This variability suggests that hypervirulence traits are largely strain-specific, which may contribute to the emergence of highly pathogenic clones within the population. The relatively small core genome highlights the limited set of conserved functions essential for survival, while the absence of soft-core genes suggests a sharp demarcation between universally conserved and highly variable genomic regions [48]. However, the absence of soft-core genes in the pangenome likely reflects a threshold effect rather than true biological absence, as frequency-based categories, given the limited sample size in the study, such soft-core becomes difficult to resolve in small datasets.

The dominance of shell genes indicates that many genes are shared among subsets of isolates, likely reflecting lineage-specific adaptations, including virulence traits, metabolic functions, and environmental survival mechanisms [48]. The large pool of cloud genes further suggests the presence of rare or isolate-specific elements that may contribute to phenotypic variability, including differential virulence potential and host adaptation. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing K. pneumoniae as a species with an exceptionally flexible genome and high epidemic potential [48].

Core genome phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 3) and SNP-based detailed analysis (Table 4) were concordant with accessory gene content and virulence profiles. Closely related isolates shared highly similar gene presence-absence patterns (Fig. 3), while more distantly related isolates displayed greater genomic divergence, underscoring the role of accessory genome (Fig. 2) variation in shaping lineage-specific traits. However, homologous recombination was not included in this study. The analysis focused on the core genome SNPs to infer clonal relationships, as recombinant SNPs are limited in the hypervirulent K. pneumoniae core genome and are unlikely to significantly influence phylogenetic inference within this cohort.

The identification of conserved SNPs within a hypervirulence-associated genomic region across most isolates suggests either a shared ancestral origin or horizontal acquisition of a common virulence locus. In contrast, isolate-specific SNPs observed in samples 62 and 64 (Fig. 4) indicate ongoing microevolution, potentially driven by host adaptation, ecological pressures, or independent introduction events. The wide range of total SNP counts across isolates further reflects varying degrees of divergence relative to the reference genome, supporting the presence of multiple evolutionary lineages within the dataset.

Phylogenomic analysis (Fig. 5) demonstrated substantial genetic diversity among the study isolates, with no single monophyletic cluster observed. Several isolates (Fig. 5 sample51, sample65 and sample64) clustered closely with the publicly available genome (GCF016762615), suggesting shared evolutionary backgrounds with globally circulating Klebsiella pneumoniae lineages. Isolates belonging to international high-risk sequence types, including ST23 (K1), ST14 and ST86 (K2), were grouped proximal to genomes representing hypervirulent lineages described across Asia and Europe, consistent with phylogenomic analysis [40] (Lam et al., [13]). In contrast, ST3430 (K17) isolates were more dispersed across the tree, reflecting greater genomic divergence. Short branch lengths between selected study isolates and reference genomes suggest recent common ancestry, whereas longer branch lengths among other isolates suggest more independent evolutionary relationships and independent diversification [49]. The presence of these lineages in sub-Saharan Africa underscores the urgent need for enhanced genomic surveillance to monitor their spread, evolution, and potential convergence with antimicrobial resistance [13]. Although the isolates in this study were largely antimicrobial susceptible, their extensive virulence repertoires and genomic flexibility suggest a substantial capacity for future adaptation. Continuous monitoring is therefore essential to detect early signals of virulence resistance convergence, which would pose a serious threat to clinical management and public health.

Conclusion

While this study provides valuable insights, it is limited by the relatively small sample size and the lack of detailed clinical outcome data, which would allow for stronger correlations between genomic features and disease severity. Future studies incorporating larger, longitudinal datasets, combined with phenotypic virulence assays and clinical metadata, will be critical to fully elucidate the epidemiology and impact of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae in this region.
