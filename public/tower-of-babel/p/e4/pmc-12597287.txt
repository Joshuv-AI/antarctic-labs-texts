Characterization of alpha-1 antitrypsin in a Pi∗ZZ patient with emphysema: a case report
Authors: Arturo Olivares Rivera, Julia Held, Julia Beimdiek, Falk F.R. Buettner, Claus P. Heußel, Sabina Janciauskiene, Franziska Trudzinski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung disease severity and progression vary widely among individuals with severe alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD; Pi∗ZZ, Glu342Lys). In addition to low levels of polymerized Z mutant AAT (Z-AAT), this variability may result from other genetic and environmental factors and individual differences in Z-AAT's biochemical properties.Patient & methodsWe examined a 55-year-old female with Pi∗ZZ genotype, a history of smoking (8 pack-years), and exertional dyspnea, who is not receiving augmentation therapy. She was diagnosed with COPD at the age of 40, and also has asthma, psoriasis, and a history of non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction. Plasma Z-AAT was isolated twice (6 months apart) and analyzed via Western blotting, anti-elastase activity, and N-glycan profiling, alongside comparisons to Z-AAT from other non-augmented donors.ResultsThe patient had an FEV1 of 1.96 L (56 % of predicted) and a DLCO of 4.56 mmol/(min∗kPa) (50 % of predicted). HRCT revealed basal panlobular emphysema with a global emphysema index of 23 % and early-stage bronchiectasis. Compared to other Pi∗ZZ cases, she exhibited lower plasma levels of Z-AAT polymers and HA but higher levels of PAI-1, MPO, and NGAL. Her isolated Z-AAT protein demonstrated significantly reduced anti-elastase activity and lower levels of complex-type bi-antennary N-glycans compared to Z-AAT from non-augmented Pi∗ZZ donors.ConclusionThe severe dysfunction of Z-AAT in this Pi∗ZZ case most likely contributed to early-onset psoriasis and the progression of pulmonary disease, highlighting its systemic impact. Our findings emphasize the importance of studying individual Z-AAT properties to improve diagnosis and personalized treatment for AATD patients.

Introduction

Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency (AATD) is an inherited disorder caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene, leading to low levels and/or dysfunctional alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) protein, the major inhibitor of neutrophil serine proteases, especially elastase [1]. The most common mutation is Pi∗ZZ (Glu342Lys), which results in severe AATD (about 10 % of normal levels, which are 1–2 g/L), reducing protection against proteases [2]. This severe AATD, especially in smokers, strongly increases the risk of early-onset pulmonary diseases, including emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). It also contributes to liver diseases such as fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer due to the accumulation of misfolded Z-AAT protein in hepatocytes [3].

The severity and progression of AATD-related lung and liver diseases vary widely between individuals. While factors like smoking and environmental pollution significantly influence pulmonary disease development, patients with the same Pi∗ZZ genotype and exposure to the similar environment can have different outcomes [4]. Recent studies from patient registries and clinical cohorts suggest that disease susceptibility and clinical variability among Pi∗ZZ individuals are not solely determined by low AAT protein levels. Instead, other genetic factors, as well as variations in the biochemical properties and functional activity of the Z-AAT protein, may also play a significant role.

Here, we present a case of a Pi∗ZZ individual with a detailed medical history, comprehensive lung and liver assessments, routine laboratory testing, and a quantitative CT imaging analysis. In addition, we provide a biochemical characterization of the patient's plasma Z-AAT protein, at two time points, six months apart, and compare it to Z-AAT from age matched Pi∗ZZ donors. This translational approach highlights the importance of analyzing individual Z-AAT properties to help guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

All Pi∗ZZ donors included in the comparison had no clinically significant lung or liver disease. Isolation of Z-and M-AAT protein from plasma

Plasma AAT protein was isolated by affinity chromatography using the AAT-specific Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Select matrix (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Cytiva, Sheffield, UK). Briefly, 2 ml of plasma were diluted in a ratio of 1:4 using binding buffer (20 mM TRIS, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). After binding to the matrix and performing several wash steps, AAT was eluted using an elution buffer (20 mM TRIS, 2M MgCl2, pH 7.4). The buffer of the purified protein was then exchanged to phosphate buffered saline (PBS, ThermoScientific, Rockford, IL, USA) + 0.02 % NaN3 using Vivaspin centrifugal concentrators with a 30,000 molecular weight cut-off (Vivaproducts, Littleton, MA, USA). Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoScientific). The purified AAT was stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Western blotting analysis of the molecular forms of AAT protein

Samples of whole plasma or purified AAT were separated by 7.5 % native polyacrylamide electrophoresis (PAGE) followed by western blotting. Total AAT was visualized using polyclonal rabbit anti-human AAT antibody (1:800, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark) followed by secondary polyclonal swine anti-rabbit immunoglobulins/HRP (1:10000, DAKO). Polymeric AAT was detected with monoclonal mouse anti-AAT polymer antibody (LG96, 1:1000) followed by the secondary polyclonal rabbit anti-mouse immunoglobulins/HRP (1:10000, DAKO). The immune complexes were visualized using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA), and images were captured with the Chemidoc Touch imaging system. Z-AAT polymer levels were quantified using Image Lab 6.1 software (BioRad) by measuring signal intensities. Polymer concentrations in the loaded samples (μg/ml) were calculated based on the intensity of a known 1.5 μg Z-AAT protein standard.

Spectrophotometric anti-elastase activity assay

AAT protein (working concentration 1.45 μg/ml) or buffer alone (as control) was incubated with porcine pancreatic elastase (Sigma Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Steinheim, Germany, Cat.No.E7885, specific activity ≥4 units/mg protein, working concentration 1.82 μg/ml) for 5 min at 37 °C before the addition of the chromogenic elastase substrate, succinyl-(Ala)3-p-nitroanilide (Sigma Aldrich). Elastase activity was measured over 3 min by recording optical density (OD) at 405 nm every 8 sec using the Infinite M200 microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Elastase activity without AAT was set to 100 %. Anti-elastase activity was determined by subtracting the blank OD at 405 nm and calculating the reaction slope. The following equation was used to calculate the percentage of elastase inhibition:%elastaseinhibition=Slope(uninhibitedreaction)−slope(testsample)x100%Slopeinhibitedreaction.

Electrophoretic analysis of AAT/Elastase complex formation, in vitro

Isolated AAT protein was mixed with porcine pancreas elastase at a molar ratio of 2:1 and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped by adding equal volumes of 2X SDS sample buffer and heating for 3 min at 95 °C. Following, samples were separated by 7.5 % Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (BioRad). Images were taken using the Chemidoc Touch imaging system (BioRad).

Analysis of AAT N-glycosylation by multiplexed capillary gel electrophoresis coupled to laser-induced fluorescence detection (xCGE-LIF)

Purified AAT proteins were separated using 7.5 % SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250. Afterwards, protein bands were cut from the gel and subjected to in-gel digestion with peptide-N-glucosidase F (PNGaseF from Elizabethkingia meningoseptica (BioReagent grade, Sigma-Aldrich) to release protein-bound N-glycans. The samples were then prepared for xCGE-LIF analysis as previously described with slight modifications [5,6]. The released N-glycans were fluorescently labeled with 8-aminopyrene-1, 3, 6-trisulfonic acid (APTS; Sigma-Aldrich), incubated for 16.5 h at 37 °C in the dark. Following HILIC-SPE purification, N-glycan profiles were analyzed by xCGE-LIF using a remodeled ABI PRISM 3130-Avant Genetic Analyzer (advanced biolab services GmbH, Munich, Germany) and Run 3100-Avant Data Collection Software v.2.0. N-glycans were injected for 15 s at 1.6 kV, followed by a 50 s data delay, after which data were collected for 45 min at 15 kV and 60 °C. The resulting data were analyzed using GeneMapperTM Software v.3.7. To facilitate comparison of N-glycan levels across different samples, relative signal intensities of peaks from xCGE-LIF electropherograms were calculated as a percentage of the total peak heights within each sample. N-glycan annotation was conducted using an in-house developed latest N-glycan database.

Results

Clinical presentation of the Pi∗ZZ case

A 55-year-old female presented to the Thoraxklinik at the University of Heidelberg in 2023 with a diagnosis of COPD II (GOLD classification) and AATD (Pi∗ZZ genotype) without prior augmentation therapy. Her medical history includes neonatal jaundice and childhood-onset psoriasis, but no documented history of recurrent respiratory infections. At age 20, she was diagnosed with asthma in the absence of identifiable allergies. She has two children, and during her first pregnancy in 2009, she experienced a non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), a complication considered a variant of preeclampsia. She reported no relevant occupational exposure to hazardous substances. The patient had a 15-year smoking history, averaging eight pack-years, but quit smoking 25 years ago. Regular liver follow-ups at the University Hospital Aachen indicated normal liver stiffness. Biochemical analyses

Comparative plasma analysis between the Pi∗ZZ case and non-augmented Pi∗ZZ donors

Certain plasma markers can provide insight into lung and liver status, helping to assess disease severity in Pi∗ZZ patients. For example, elevated levels of HA, PAI-1, MPO, NGAL, and complement factor D may indicate ongoing inflammation, neutrophil activation, and liver dysfunction, while decreased fetuin-A may reflect impaired hepatic function and chronic inflammation [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. As shown in Table 5, the Pi∗ZZ case had lower HA levels compared to age-matched Pi∗ZZ donors, which aligns with the clinical findings indicating no signs of progressive fibrosis or significant liver impairment. In contrast, levels of PAI-1, MPO, and NGAL levels were elevated in the Pi∗ZZ case, particularly in the second plasma sample taken six months later, suggesting ongoing neutrophilic inflammation. N-glycan analysis performed on M- and Z-AAT proteins isolated from plasma

To further characterize Z-AAT from our Pi∗ZZ case, we analyzed its glycosylation status and compared it to purified M-AAT from healthy donors (n = 6) and purified Z-AAT from Pi∗ZZ patients (n = 7; 6 non-augmented patients, 1 augmented patient). For this analysis, purified proteins were separated using 7.5 % SDS-PAGE, and the gel containing protein bands of about 60 kDa were subjected to N-glycan profiling by capillary gel electrophoresis with laser-induced fluorescence detection (xCGE-LIF). Our analysis identified diverse N-glycan species within a migration time window of 127–321 MTU, consistent with those found in plasma-derived glycoproteins. Only significantly different structures are shown in Fig. 5. Based on our in-house glycan database, several but not all glycan signals could be assigned to specific N-glycan structures. We noted that several complex-type bi-antennary N-glycans were significantly decreased on Z-AAT from Pi∗ZZ case in comparison to M-AAT from healthy Pi∗MM donors and Z-AAT from a group of Pi∗ZZ patients. These structures comprised complex-type bi-antennary N-glycans with two (A2G2S2), one (A2G2S) or zero sialic acids (A2G2) as we as well as its less matured derivative lacking galactosylation, but harboring core-fucosylation (FA2). Discussion

Observational studies suggest that lung disease is the primary clinical manifestation of AATD. Wahlin et al., using Swedish National Patient Registry data (2002–2020), found that at diagnosis, 44 % of AATD patients had pulmonary disease, whereas only 5 % had liver disease. [11]. The EARCO study, an international, observational, and prospective study of AATD patients, also identified emphysema, COPD and bronchiectasis as the most common disease in Pi∗ZZ individuals [12]. Similar findings were reported in a retrospective analysis of longitudinal follow-up data from the Spanish AATD Registry (REDAAT) [13]. Lung diseases are often present at the time of AATD diagnosis, with studies reporting a median diagnosis age of approximately 50+ years [14]. In contrast, AATD-related liver diseases tend to develop later, with studies indicating a median diagnosis age of 61 years [15]. The variability in disease onset and organ involvement among Pi∗ZZ individuals remains largely unexplained. This suggests that additional factors, such as environmental exposures (e.g., smoking, pollution), genetic modifiers, and/or individual characteristics of the Z-AAT protein, may influence the progression and manifestation of AATD-related diseases.

Here, we present a 55-year-old female diagnosed with Pi∗ZZ AATD in 2010 at the age of 41. Although there are no clinical signs of liver impairment, her primary pulmonary diagnosis includes COPD with emphysematous changes and incipient bronchiectasis. To gain further insight into patient's disease phenotype, we conducted a detailed biochemical characterization of her plasma Z-AAT protein.

It is well known that Z-AAT protein forms extracellular and intracellular polymers of varying sizes. Since AAT is primarily produced in the liver, Z-AAT polymer accumulation in hepatocytes leads to liver damage [16]. Z-AAT polymer levels have been shown to correlate with liver-related diseases, including hepatic fibrosis [16], liver stiffness [17] and cirrhosis [18]. The Z-AAT polymer profile in both plasma and isolated Z-AAT from this Pi∗ZZ case resembled that of other Pi∗ZZ donors. However, electrophoretic analysis showed lower overall Z-AAT levels in patient's plasma, suggesting possible intrahepatic polymer accumulation. Despite this, the patient exhibits no clinical signs of liver impairment, and her plasma HA levels, a fibrosis marker, were lower than in age-matched Pi∗ZZ donors. In contrast, levels of PAI-1, MPO, and NGAL were elevated in the Pi∗ZZ case, with a notable increase observed in the second plasma sample collected six months later. These biomarkers are closely linked to neutrophil activation and sustained inflammatory responses [19]. Thus, their elevation suggests ongoing neutrophilic inflammation, which is consistent with the patient's radiological findings of emphysema and incipient bronchiectasis. The progressive increase over time may reflect chronic, low-grade inflammation contributing to structural lung damage and disease progression.

AAT is a glycoprotein with three N-glycosylation sites (Asn70, Asn107, Asn271). The N-glycans of native AAT are predominantly complex-type bi-, tri-, or tetra-antennary structures that are fully sialylated [20]. Kinetic analyses have shown that glycosylation plays a crucial role in stabilizing the native state of the AAT protein. For example, non-glycosylated recombinant AAT degrades much faster than glycosylated plasma or yeast-derived AAT, with yeast-derived AAT, in turn, degrading more rapidly than plasma AAT [21]. These observations suggest that not only glycosylation itself but also the specific type of N-glycans determine the stability and function of AAT [21]. Previous studies have shown that Z-AAT contains 20–30 % less carbohydrate than M-AAT [22,23]. Additionally, the Z-AAT monomer is less stable than M-AAT [24] and less effective as an inhibitor of neutrophil elastase [25], and compared to M-AAT, Z-AAT has been found to exhibit higher fucosylation on N-glycans [20], a modification associated with ongoing inflammation in AATD individuals.

Using elastase inhibition assays and N-glycan profiling analysis, we confirmed that Z-AAT isolated from Pi∗ZZ donors exhibited significantly lower anti-elastase activity and decreased levels of certain N-glycans compared to M-AAT. Notably, Z-AAT isolated from our Pi∗ZZ case showed an even greater functional impairment, with markedly lower anti-elastase activity and a more pronounced reduction across all detected N-glycan species compared to Z-AAT from the other Pi∗ZZ donors. These findings suggest a particularly dysfunctional Z-AAT phenotype in this patient, which may contribute to her pulmonary disease severity.

A proper glycosylation pattern is essential for the stability, anti-protease function, correct folding, and efficient secretion of AAT [21,26]. For example, a study by Gross et al., demonstrated in a human monocyte model that inhibition of de novo glycosylation significantly impaired the secretion of both M- and Z-AAT. In monocytes from Pi∗ZZ individuals, only about 35 % of newly synthesized Z-AAT was secreted under basal conditions, whereas blocking de novo glycosylation completely abolished Z-AAT secretion [26]. Additional studies have shown that non-glycosylated or poorly glycosylated AAT is more prone to misfolding and rapid clearance. In this context, the marked reduction in N-glycans observed in Z-AAT from our Pi∗ZZ case may potentially reduce the risk of intracellular polymer accumulation and associated liver injury. However, this altered glycosylation may also contribute to decreased circulating levels of functionally active Z-AAT, thereby diminishing its anti-elastase capacity and increasing susceptibility to lung tissue damage.

Finally, it is important to note that the Pi∗ZZ patient was diagnosed with psoriasis in early childhood. Historical reports suggested an increased frequency of Pi∗ZZ among patients with psoriasis [27]. Psoriatic plaques are characterized by dense infiltration of neutrophils, dendritic cells, macrophages, and T cells, creating a highly inflammatory environment. If neutrophil proteases are not effectively counteracted, they can amplify and sustain chronic inflammation, exacerbating disease severity [28]. This may explain why some Pi∗ZZ patients, with significantly reduced levels and impaired function of Z-AAT, like our Pi∗ZZ patient, are more susceptible to developing psoriasis and other skin diseases.

One of the key features of psoriasis is altered keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation, along with abnormal expression of inflammatory cytokines [29]. While vitamin D does not play a central role in psoriasis pathogenesis, it is a regulator of keratinocyte proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [30] and therefore its levels should be monitored and corrected if necessary [29]. Our Pi∗ZZ case had very low vitamin D levels (by 89 % lower from the required levels), which were successfully corrected. However, despite this intervention, her psoriasis remained active, suggesting that other underlying inflammatory mechanisms contribute to disease persistence. Current data indicate that elevated interleukin-23 (IL-23) expression is present in psoriasis-affected skin. IL-23 is a heterodimeric pro-inflammatory cytokine from the IL-12 family, secreted primarily by dendritic cells and macrophages [31], which plays a role in the differentiation and function of T helper 17 (Th17) cells [32]. Today a Risankizumab (Skyrizi), a humanized monoclonal antibody that specifically inhibits interleukin 23A, is approved for psoriasis treatment [33]. When our Pi∗ZZ patient-initiated treatment with Skyrizi, her psoriasis completely resolved. Hence, the elevation in neutrophil-related markers in patient plasma and eventual response to IL-23-targeted therapy with risankizumab suggest that chronic neutrophilic inflammation and dysregulation of the IL-23/Th17 axis are central to her dermatologic symptoms. In addition to pulmonary involvement, the patient's early-onset and treatment-resistant psoriasis points to an extrahepatic and extrapulmonary consequence of Z-AAT dysfunction.

Conclusion

This case highlights the clinical complexity and systemic impact of Pi∗ZZ AATD. Although the patient showed no signs of progressive liver damage, detailed plasma analysis revealed low levels of circulating Z-AAT polymers, impaired anti-elastase activity, and significantly altered N-glycosylation patterns. These biochemical abnormalities may reduce the risk of hepatic polymer accumulation but simultaneously impair the protective function of AAT in the lungs, contributing to emphysema and bronchiectasis. Altogether, this case underscores the importance of personalized biochemical and immunological profiling in Pi∗ZZ patients to better understand disease heterogeneity, identify at-risk individuals, and guide tailored therapeutic interventions.
