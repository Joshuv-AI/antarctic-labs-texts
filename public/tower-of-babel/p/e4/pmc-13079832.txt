COVID-19 pandemic perceived impacts on the Australian general population, a national survey exploring the role of socio-demographic and psychological factors
Authors: Rachel Campbell, Dion Candelaria, Dayna J Fullerton, Rebecca Mercieca-Bebber, Margaret-Ann Tait, Richard Norman, Sabina Kleitman, Madeleine T King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic and associated public health orders did not impact all population groups equally. We examined perceived pandemic impacts on daily life by socio-demographic and psychological factors. Participants, quota sampled by region, age and sex, completed a cross-sectional online survey between Oct 21st and Nov 10th 2020. COVID-19 impact categories (negative/same/positive) were compared by age and sex with chi-square tests. Associations between COVID-19 impacts and socio-demographic and psychological characteristics were examined with hierarchical regressions. COVID-19 impacts differed by sex for 7/19 aspects: females reported negative impacts on mental health, loneliness, and sleep quality more frequently than males, whereas more males reported negative impacts on substance use, alcohol consumption, and time availability. Males reported both more negative and positive impacts on job security than women. Young adults reported more negative impacts across all daily life aspects except social connections, where those ≥ 60 years reported worse impacts. Negative impacts were also associated with poor health status, reduced work hours, lower satisfaction with government communication, higher intolerance of uncertainty, and some coping strategies, while several other coping strategies correlated with positive impacts. Overall, COVID-19 impacts differed by age, sex, and several other variables. These findings could guide targeted public health messaging and interventions to mitigate the impact of future pandemics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41428-z.Keywords: COVID-19, Coronavirus disease, Pandemic responses, Socio-demographics, Coping, Intolerance of uncertaintySubject terms: Psychology, Public health, Quality of life

Introduction

On 30th January 2020 the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the coronavirus disease of 2019 (COVID-19) outbreak a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC), then a worldwide pandemic on 11th March 20201. The first case of COVID-19 was confirmed in Australia on January 25th 20202. Australia pursued an aggressive suppression strategy, keeping case numbers low with border closures, travel bans, widespread social distancing measures, contact-tracing, stay-at-home orders, and mandatory quarantine3. By the end of March 2020, non-essential overseas travel was banned, non-essential services were closed, and all Australian States and Territories were under stay-at-home orders or restrictions of varying extent2. These public health and social measures (PHSMs) had a pervasive impact on the daily lives of people living in Australia. By May 2020 restrictions began to ease in most states2, but a second outbreak in July 2020, mainly affecting the state of Victoria, resulted in renewed restrictions and a second lengthy regional lockdown. We now live with COVID-19, but lessons learnt from these initial suppression efforts can inform responses to future national and global health crises.

The COVID-19 pandemic and associated PHSMs did not impact all population groups equally. In Australia government reports and research from the early phase of the pandemic demonstrated younger people and those identifying as female reported poorer mental health and increased psychological distress compared to males or older adults4,5. Lockdowns and school/day-care closures meant parental caring responsibilities increased dramatically6. Mothers were disproportionately affected, with many reducing work hours or ceasing employment to accommodate increased family and household responsibilities6. Young people in Australia also experienced higher rates of psychological distress, unemployment, and housing stress than older people4. Similar findings emerged internationally7. A systematic review and meta-analysis including 255 studies from 50 countries found that during the COVID-19 pandemic females were at higher risk of anxiety, depression, and psychological distress, and younger adults were at higher risk of psychological distress and other acute stress disorders7. In contrast, a rapid review synthesizing the impact of the pandemic on older adults, found that decreased social life and fewer in-person social interactions were occasionally associated with reduced quality of life and increased depression in those of older age8. These discrepant findings with regard to age may perhaps be explained by the Strength and Vulnerability Integration (SAVI) model9. According to this model as people get older they develop greater skills and strengths in regulating everyday emotional experiences leading to higher overall affective well-being9. However, the model further posits that in situations where people are exposed to chronic stressors (e.g. during a pandemic) these age-related improvements are attenuated and may even disappear9, possibly accounting for contradictory age-related results. Of particular note, the majority of previous studies focussed exclusively on the mental and/or physical health impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic. Thus, further exploration of the disparities in self-reported COVID-19 impacts by sex and age on a more diverse range of aspects of the daily lives of general populations (e.g. time availability, household & extended family responsibilities, productivity and daily routine) is warranted to inform how best to identify and support at-risk population groups in the event of future health crises.

Aside from socio-demographic characteristics, individual differences in psychological characteristics such as coping strategies and intolerance of uncertainty may account for some variations in perceived impacts of the pandemic. Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) refers to an individual’s tendency to respond negatively (e.g. with excessive worry) to uncertain situations and events10. A study conducted in the United Kingdom, United States, and Australia from May 2020 to April 2021 found that people with moderate to high IU reported more depression and anxiety compared to those with low IU11. However, 91% of the sample was female limiting generalisability of the results more broadly11. Another study conducted in China in February 2020 during the COVID-19 outbreak found a positive association between media exposure to COVID-19 and acute stress which was partially mediated by IU12. In this study the association between IU and acute stress was stronger in individuals with low perceived social support12. A meta-analysis of 35 studies from 36 geographical regions during the COVID-19 pandemic also found that neuroticism was positively associated with anxiety13. However, neuroticism which reflects a general tendency to experience distress and negative emotions across broad life domains13 is distinct from IU which relates specifically to a negative response to uncertain or unpredictable situations such as a pandemic.

Coping strategies refer to the cognitions and behaviours people use to manage stressful or adverse events and may also influence perceived pandemic impacts14. A large meta-analysis including 151 independent samples from 44 countries examining coping styles during the initial wave of the COVID-19 pandemic found that problem-focused coping styles (e.g. planning, problem solving) were associated with lower symptoms of anxiety and depression whereas avoidant coping styles (e.g. behavioural disengagement, denial) were associated with higher symptoms of anxiety and depression15. A cross-sectional study of adults living in Australia during the initial COVID-19 lock-downs (April-May 2020) similarly found that problem-focused and adaptive emotion-focused coping styles such as positive reframing, acceptance, and humour were associated with better mental health, whereas maladaptive emotion-focused coping and avoidant coping styles such as self-blame, venting, behavioural disengagement, and self-distraction were associated with poorer mental health16. However, 81.5% of the sample was female limiting the generalisability of these results16. Together, these previous findings suggest that adaptive coping strategies may buffer against negative impacts of the pandemic more broadly, whereas intolerance of uncertainty and the use of maladaptive coping strategies may exacerbate adverse COVID-19 impacts. However, similar to previous research examining differences in COVID-19 impacts by age and sex, previous studies examining the role of intolerance of uncertainty and coping strategies mainly focused on their association with mental health outcomes, and neglected to examine their association with a more diverse range of aspects of daily life (e.g. productivity and time availability) in a representative sample of the general population. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge no previous studies examined the incremental, unique role of socio-demographics, intolerance of uncertainty, and coping styles in accounting for variance in diverse COVID-19 impacts.

This study had two aims: (1) to describe differences in self-reported impacts of Australia’s COVID-19 suppression strategy on a range of aspects of daily life as a function of sex and age, and (2) to examine the role of socio-demographic characteristics, intolerance of uncertainty, and coping strategies in predicting perceived impacts of COVID-19 on diverse aspects of daily life, controlling for people’s attitudes to PHSMs and their beliefs about their efficacy as additional explanatory variables to account for their possible association with people’s behavioural and lifestyle changes amidst PHSMs. In line with past research, we hypothesized that females would report worse impacts of COVID-19 across a range of aspects of daily life. However, we had no specific hypotheses for age given contradictory findings in past research. Additionally, we hypothesized that socio-demographic characteristics, intolerance of uncertainty, and coping strategies would relate to a range of COVID-19 impacts, with each set of variables accounting for unique variance in self-reported impacts.

Methods

This secondary analysis is part of a broader study approved by the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (Protocol 2020 − 451) and conducted in accordance with the National Health and Medical Research Council National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research 2018. It was registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12621001240831). The objective of the broader study was to examine how the COVID-19 pandemic and related public health orders affected the daily lives and self-reported quality of life of the Australian general population. The aim of the primary paper published from this data was to compare the health-related quality of life (HRQL) of the Australian general population during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020) with pre-pandemic data (2015–2016) and identify pandemic-related and demographic factors associated with poorer HRQL17. As such, the aim of this paper and data analysed are distinct. Although this study was not a clinical trial, it was pre-registered on the ANZCTR to increase transparency and reduce possibility of publication bias.

Study context

This study was designed and conducted in 2020, during Australia’s initial COVID aggressive suppression period. As shown in Supplementary Figure A1.1, a series of national lockdown measures implemented in March and April 2020 succeeded in limiting the spread of Australia’s first COVID wave and were eased from May to June 2020. Thereafter, PHSMs were the remit of each state and territory; these are summarised in Supplementary Tables A1.1 and A1.2. The intensity and timing of PHSMs varied between states, proportionate to the number of daily cases. Australia’s second COVID wave (June-September 2020) largely affected Melbourne and regional Victoria; Figure A1 shows measures introduced in Victoria during that period. By the time of our survey (October-November 2020), daily case numbers per state were generally low (Table A1.3).

Participants and sampling

Adults (aged 18 and older) living in Australia who were members of an online panel managed by the survey company Toluna (https://au.toluna.com) were invited to complete our study survey. Participant sampling was managed by the host survey company, Survey Engine. Responses to each item were mandatory. Participants who successfully completed the survey were awarded ‘panel points’ by Toluna, which are used to redeem vouchers or consumer goods, with an approximate value of less than 1 AUD.

To achieve a range of exposures to COVID case numbers and restrictiveness of PHSMs in 2020, participants were quota sampled by four regions: (1) Melbourne, the capital of Victoria: with the greatest number of cases and most restrictive PHSMs, including city-wide lockdown; (2) Regional Victoria: the second highest number of cases with the second most restrictive PHSMs; (3) New South Wales and Queensland: some localised COVID hot-spots with state border closures; (4) Western Australia, South Australia, Tasmania, the Australian Capital Territory, and the Northern Territory: very few cases with the least restrictive PHSMs. To achieve a representative sample by age and sex, participants were quota sampled by age and sex proportional to the Australian general population within each region. All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to completing the survey.

Data collection

Participants self-completed an online survey. This included 13 sociodemographic and health status variables (Supplementary Table S1). The study outcome variable, COVID impact, was measured with the COVID Impact Index.17 which assesses 19 specific impacts of COVID-19 on daily life; the questionnaire items and scoring are provided in Supplementary Appendix 2. The recall period for these impacts was from the start of Australia’s national lockdown period (March 2020) to the survey period (October-November 2020), with impact rated on a scale from 1 (much worse) to 5 (much better) than before the pandemic.

For Aim 2, two questionnaires were included to provide key explanatory variables:

Intolerance of uncertainty (12 items)10: captured reactions to uncertainty, ambiguous situations and the future (e.g. “When I am uncertain I can’t function very well”). Items were rated on a scale from 1 (not at all characteristic of me) to 5 (entirely characteristic of me). Items were summed to a total score, higher scores indicate greater intolerance of uncertainty.

Data quality checks

Seven data quality and logic checks were performed to ensure the final dataset included high-quality, genuine responses. Quality checks included survey completion in less than 30% of the sample’s median completion time, and six inconsistent response checks (Supplementary Appendix 3 provides details, including Figure A3.1, Table A3.1, Table A3.2 & Table A3.3). Any participants who failed two or more of these checks were excluded from analysis.

Analyses

Aim 1. Our first aim was descriptive. To examine COVID-19 impacts by age and sex, for each of the 19 impact items we calculated proportions based on item response options: “Negative impact” (1–2), “The same” (3), “Positive Impact” (4–5) relative to pre-COVID. For each item, we examined the frequency (%) of these impacts across age groups (18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, ≥70 years) and sex (female, male). Chi-square tests were used to examine differences in the proportion of respondents reporting “negative”, “the same” or “positive” for each COVID-19 impact across these groups. This approach of categorizing items was chosen to aid clear communication of findings and to present data in an easily digestible format.

Aim 2. Hierarchical linear regressions were used to examine the independent associations of 37 explanatory or control variables with the COVID-impact outcome variables. To determine optimal COVID-impact variables for these regressions, exploratory factor analysis was used to examine the underlying dimensionality of the COVID-19 Impact Index items and reduce the number of outcomes without losing specificity (Supplementary Appendix 2 provides details; Table A2.1). This led to 11 impact outcome variables: 6 composites: (1) Activity, physical health, diet; (2) Family relationships, living situation, social connections; (3) financial situation, job security; (4) Household and extended family responsibilities; (5) Productivity, daily routine; and (6) Alcohol and substance use; and 5 single-item outcomes: mental health, sleep quality, loneliness, hobbies/leisure activities, and time availability. Scores on COVID-impact variables were reverse scored such that higher scores (above 3) indicate more negative impact, and lower scores (below 3) indicate more positive impact. All 37 explanatory/control variables were included in each regression model (i.e. per outcome variable; 11 models in total). These were entered in blocks: socio-demographic/health characteristics (Block 1); COVID-related control variables (attitudes towards the COVID-19 PHSMs, beliefs about the efficacy of COVID-related beliefs, satisfaction with government communication, Block 2); intolerance of uncertainty (Block 3); and coping strategies (13 in total, Block 4). R2 changed was examined to indicate the additional proportion of variance in each COVID-impact outcome variable accounted for by each block.

All models were checked against assumptions: Normality of residuals was checked by inspecting the predicted probability plots; homoscedasticity and linearity were examined through scatterplots of the predicted and residual values; multicollinearity was checked by Tolerance and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values, with Tolerance values of lower than 0∙25 and VIF higher than 4 generally indicative of possible multicollinearity issues. Significance levels were adjusted to control for level of Type 1 error using Hochberg’s 1988 method within each model20. This approach was chosen as many correlations (Spearman’s) between dependent variables ranged between 0.20 and 0.40, the range determined by Blakesly et al.21 as well-served by Hochberg’s 1988 method (see supplementary Table S4). All analyses were performed using SPSS v27.

Sample size

This was a secondary analysis of an existing dataset. A sample size calculation was provided in the primary manuscript17. In summary the primary study required a minimum sample size of n = 1456 to detect a pre-specified difference in the primary endpoint with 80% power. This recruitment target was increased to n = 2000 to allow n = 500 per covid exposure sampling region and allowed tight confidence intervals around estimates for descriptive analyses and regression coefficients, and robust sub-group analyses and downward adjustment of critical p-values for multiple comparisons.

Results

A total of 2007 survey responses were collected between October 21st and November 10th, 2020. Of these, 1898 (95%) passed data quality checks and were included in the final analyses (see Appendix 3 for details). The sample was representative of the Australian general population for age, sex, number of children, and proportion who had had a stroke; differences for the remaining socio-demographic and health characteristics were generally small but statistically significant due to the large sample size (Supplementary Table S1). Reliability estimates (Cronbach’s alpha) for all study variables are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

COVID-19 impacts by sex and age groups

The proportion of males and females reporting positive/negative impacts (Fig. 1) differed significantly for 7 of the 19 COVID impacts: a larger proportion of females reported negative impacts on mental health (p<∙001), loneliness (p<∙001), and sleep quality (p=∙008), whereas males reported more negative impacts on substance use (p=∙004), alcohol consumption (p=∙024), and time availability (p<∙001). Males reported both more negative and positive impacts on job security than females (p<∙001).

Self-reported COVID-19 impacts by sex.

Younger age groups (particularly 18–29-year olds) consistently reported more negative impact across all aspects of daily life except social connections (p value range: <∙001 to ∙004) (Fig. 2). Conversely, older age groups (60 plus) reported more negative impact on social connections relative to all younger age groups (59 and younger) (Fig. 2).

h. Decrease in work hours scored as number of work hours in a typical week in the 6 months before COVID-19 (September 2019 to February 2020) minus work hours in a typical week at time of survey completion.

i. Measured with the Attitude and Motivation Towards Compliance scale.

j. Measured with the COVID-19 Beliefs scale, Response efficacy beliefs subscale and Perceived barriers subscale, respectively.

k. Measured with the Satisfaction with Government item.

l. Measured with the Intolerance of Uncertainty Short-form scale.

m. Measured with the Brief-COPE inventory

Socio-demographic/health characteristic variables (Block 1) accounted for 2% to 14% of the variance (R² change) in COVID-19 impacts (14% of variance in household and extended family responsibilities). Older respondents reported worse family relationships, living situation, and social connections (p<∙001), whereas younger respondents reported worse impact on household and extended family responsibilities (p<∙001). Females reported worse mental health (p<∙001) and more loneliness (p<∙001), whereas males reported less time availability (p<∙001). Healthcare workers were less likely than other workers to report negative impact to their financial situation and job security (p<∙001); 40% of healthcare workers reported no change, 22% positive impact, and 38% negative impact. People who cared for children who would usually have been at school/daycare whilst working reported more household and extended family responsibilities (p<∙001). Those with more health risk factors and comorbidities reported more loneliness and household and extended family responsibilities (both p<∙001), while a reduction in work hours was associated with worse mental health, financial situation and job security, and productivity and daily routine (all p<∙001).

COVID-related attitudes and beliefs (Block 2) accounted for an additional 0% to 4% of the variance (R² change) in the COVID-19 impacts. Lower satisfaction with government communication was associated with negative impacts on mental health, sleep quality, activity, physical health and diet, family relationships, living situation, and social connections, financial situation and job security, and productivity and daily routine (all p<∙001), and household and extended family responsibilities (p=∙001).

Intolerance of uncertainty (Block 3) accounted for an additional 0% to 3% of the variance (R² change) in COVID-19 impacts across models. The only significant association was more negative impact on mental health (p<∙001).

Coping strategies (Block 4) accounted for an additional 2% to 8% of the variance (R² change) in COVID-19 impacts. Several coping strategies were associated with more negative impacts: self-distraction was associated with more negative impact on mental health (p<∙001) and sleep quality (p<∙001), behavioural disengagement with more negative impact on mental health (p<∙001) and hobbies (p<∙001), self-blame with worse family relationships, living situation, and social connections (p=∙001), and venting with more negative impact on mental health (p<∙001) and family relationships, living situation, and social connections (p=∙001). Some coping strategies related to more positive impacts. Support seeking was associated with better family relationships, living situation, and social connections (p=∙001). Positive reframing was associated with better mental health (p<∙001), sleep quality (p=∙001), family relationships, living situation, and social connections (p<∙001), and productivity/daily routine (p<∙001). Religion was associated with better family relationships, living situation, and social connections (p=∙001) and less alcohol and substance abuse (p<∙001). Two coping strategies had mixed effects : Denial was associated with better mental health (p<∙001) but more negative impact on household and extended family responsibilities (p<∙001), and Humour was associated with better household and extended family responsibilities (p<∙001) but more alcohol and substance abuse (p<∙001).

Discussion

This study revealed that during Australia’s aggressive suppression of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the impacts of PHSMs such as border closures, travel limitations, social distancing measures and stay-at-home orders varied by sex and age. Females reported negative impacts on mental health, loneliness, and sleep quality more frequently than males, whereas males more frequently reported worse impacts on substance use, alcohol consumption, and time availability. The youngest age group (18–29 years) reported more negative impact across all aspects of daily life except social connections, which was more negatively impacted for those 60 years and older. Negative COVID-19 impacts were independently associated with sex, age, more health risk factors, more comorbidities, reduction in work hours, lower satisfaction with government communication, and six coping strategies (self-distraction, behavioural disengagement, venting, self-blame, denial and humour) and higher intolerance of uncertainty (for mental health only). Positive COVID-19 impacts were associated with four coping strategies (positive reframing, support seeking, denial and humour). Denial and humour were the only coping strategies associated with both negative and positive impacts.

Supporting previous findings both in Australia45, and internationally7, our findings add further evidence that COVID-19 amplified existing gender disparities in both mental and physical health outcomes, with its impacts felt more acutely by females23. There are several contributing factors. Females tend to take on more household responsibilities than males and comprise up to 81% of informal family caregivers24. During COVID-19 restriction periods, these responsibilities likely increased due to school closures, reduced access to childcare services, and increased care needs of older family members23. The added burden, compounded by managing working from home and home-schooling, likely contributed to increased stress, anxiety, and lack of sleep for females25,consistent with our findings. Females tend to maintain more close relationships than males, and pandemic-related physical distancing measures, including lockdowns, disrupted many social connections25. Females may have found this more challenging than males, leading to loneliness and other negative mental health impacts. Incidences of domestic violence increased during COVID-19, with females disproportionately affected26. Notably, by examining a broader range of COVID-19 impacts this study also found that males were disproportionately affected by negative impacts on substance and alcohol use and time availability. Understanding these factors can guide targeted solutions and support to both males and females to minimise impacts on diverse aspects of daily life during public health emergencies.

Extending previous findings on the age-related impact of the COVID-19 pandemic4,7,8, our study revealed that young people experienced the greatest negative impacts across almost all aspects of daily life. This may be related to educational disruption, employment and financial insecurity, and mental health challenges caused by the lockdowns of 20204. The COVID-19 pandemic led to job losses and financial uncertainty, with young adults more vulnerable27because they are more likely to have casual jobs with limited security and benefits. National Australian pandemic data (2020–2022) revealed higher prevalence of mental health issues and substance abuse in young adults than in older people for both males and females28. Pandemic-related disruptions added stress and anxiety, triggering the development of, or exacerbating, mental health conditions29. Data from the primary study associated with this data reported elsewhere showed that the 18–29 year age group reported negative impacts in overall health-related quality of life and emotional functioning compared to pre-COVID data17. Interestingly, in this paper, we show that social connections were not negatively impacted for young people; this is likely due to the mediating effect of technology, with younger people maintaining personal connectedness via social media and other forms of virtual communications430. In contrast, older people (60 or older) reported worse impacts on social connections. This aligns with previous studies which found discrepant findings with regard to age-related differences7,8, suggesting differential impacts for age depending on the specific outcome under investigation.

Targeted support, effective communication and relief from certain duties may help to reduce the long-term effects of the pandemic in groups that are vulnerable to greater negative impacts. Socio-demographic and other risk-factor findings are important in terms of who to target with public messaging and in identifying vulnerable groups requiring additional and individualised support. The mode or medium of communicating public health messages should suit the preferences and needs of its intended audiences. For instance, younger age groups may be more effectively reached via social media campaigns31,while traditional media such as television and newspapers may be more appealing or accessible to older age groups32. Culturally and linguistically diverse subgroups are also at-risk due to both socio-demographic and health disparities and require tailored communication strategies33,34–35.

Our findings with regard to intolerance of uncertainty and coping strategies provide potential targets for psychological interventions and support strategies. In line with past research11,12, higher intolerance of uncertainty was found to be associated with poorer mental health in a large representative sample of the general population. However, intolerance of uncertainty was not associated with any other impact on daily life, suggesting targeted intervention efforts to improve tolerance to uncertainty is likely to be especially protective for mental health, but unlikely to have direct impacts on other aspects of daily life.

Similar to a previous study conducted in Australia which found a positive association between adaptive emotion-focused coping and mental health36, our study similarly found that adaptive emotion-focused coping, specifically positive reframing, was associated with better mental health. Our study extended these findings in a more nationally representative sample by demonstrating positive reframing related to less negative COVID-19 impacts across various other life domains, including sleep quality, family relationships, living situation, and social connections. Importantly, it was significant above and beyond socio-demographic factors, indicating positive reframing may play a protective role against negative impacts the pandemic had on people due to circumstances over which they have less control. This indicates that in the midst of a health crisis, to the extent that people are able to find positive aspects to focus on, they are likely to be protected from negative impact. Also similar to previous findings both in Australia36 and Internationally15, this study found that avoidant coping styles (i.e. behavioural disengagement & self-distraction) and maladaptive emotion focused strategies (i.e. venting) were associated with worse COVID-19 mental health impacts. Our study extended previous findings by demonstrating these maladaptive coping styles also related to worse impacts of COVID-19 more broadly on sleep quality, hobbies, family relationships, living situation and social connections. However, somewhat surprisingly, and in contrast to past research15, we found that denial, which is typically labelled a maladaptive or avoidant strategy, was associated with less negative impact on mental health. Extending previous findings, conversely denial15 also related to more negative impact on household and extended family responsibilities. In previous studies examining coping styles participant samples tended to be female dominated15, possibly accounting for these differences in findings. Overall, these findings further highlight the need to support people to find more appropriate and healthier ways of coping with stress during a public health crisis to prevent long-term negative effects.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this survey was its large sample size, nationally representative by age and sex. Although our sample was not representative across all sociodemographic characteristics, our multivariate regressions showed that other sociodemographic factors had little explanatory power, so our findings of prevalence of impact by sex and age are likely robust. Our cross-sectional survey design means causality cannot not be implied, and our data provide only a snapshot of short-term self-reported impacts. A longitudinal design may have provided valuable insights into longer term impacts, and whether the impacts we observed were sustained or improved over time. However, in the complex context of the rapidly changing nature of the pandemic and consequently policies and restrictions, the interpretation of longitudinal studies may be fraught. Our study was conducted during the aggressive suppression period that preceded vaccine roll-out in Australia, providing a context of intense restrictions on personal freedoms. This survey was also conducted when restrictions had eased in states other than Victoria. Thus, negative impacts of the pandemic on daily life may have been more pronounced had the survey been conducted at the peak of national restrictions. Of note, the reliability of some of the composite scores of COVID impacts (i.e. Family relationships, living situations, social connections; Household & extended family responsibilities; and, Alcohol and substance use) was moderate (Cronbach’s Alphas = ~ 0.60). As a result, findings in relation to these impacts should be interpreted with caution and warrant replication in future studies. Finally, although our sample size is large, multiple testing was accounted for, and no multicollinearity detected, overfitting may still have played a role, potentially reducing parsimony of the models and generalizability of conclusions. Further, although we controlled for level of Type 1 error using Hochberg’s 1988 method, our findings should be interpreted with caution given a proportion of correlations were below the 0.2 threshold proposed by Blakesley et al.21 in their recommendations for multiple-testing adjustment methods.

Conclusion

These findings emphasize the importance of considering age, sex, and psychological factors such as coping strategies when considering the impacts of a pandemic on peoples’ lives. Our data provides valuable insights into some specific areas where certain subgroups may be more affected, highlighting potential areas for intervention and support. The factors we have identified as associated with negative impacts provide forewarning to healthcare professionals and policymakers about at-risk groups and suggest directions for targeted strategies to mitigate adverse effects and guide public health messaging and policy in the event of future pandemics and public health crises.
