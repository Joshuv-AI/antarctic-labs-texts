A novel method of Francisella infection of epithelial cells using HeLa cells expressing fc gamma receptor
Authors: Takemasa Nakamura, Takashi Shimizu, Naho Nishinakama, Reika Takahashi, Kohei Arasaki, Akihiko Uda, Kenta Watanabe, Masahisa Watarai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrancisella tularensis, the causative agent of tularemia, is a facultative intracellular bacterium. Although the life cycle of this bacterium inside phagocytic cells (e.g., macrophages, neutrophils) has been well analyzed, the difficulty of gene silencing and editing genes in phagocytic cells makes it difficult to analyze host factors important for the infection. On the other hand, epithelial cell lines, such as HeLa, have been established as cell lines that are easy to perform gene editing. However, the infection efficiency of Francisella into these epithelial cells is extremely low.MethodsIn order to facilitate the molecular biological analysis of Francisella infection using epithelial cells, we constructed an efficient infection model of F. tularensis subsp. novicida (F. novicida) in HeLa cells expressing mouse FcγRII (HeLa-FcγRII), and the system was applied to evaluate the role of host GLS1 on Francisella infection.ResultsAs a result of colony forming unit count, HeLa-FcγRII cells uptake F. novicida in a serum-dependent manner and demonstrated an approximately 100-fold increase in intracellular bacterial infection compared to parental HeLa cells. Furthermore, taking advantage of the gene silencing capability of HeLa-FcγRII cells, we developed GLS1, a gene encoding glutaminase, knockdown cells using lentiviral sh RNA vector and assessed the impact of GLS1 on F. novicida infection. LDH assay revealed that GLS1-knockdown HeLa-FcγRII cells exhibited increased cytotoxicity during infection with F. novicida compared with control HeLa-FcγRII cells. Furthermore, the cell death was inhibited by the addition of ammonia, the metabolite produced through glutaminase activity. These results suggest that ammonia plays an important role in the proliferation of F. novicida.ConclusionsIn this report, we proposed a new cell-based infection system for Francisella infection using HeLa-FcγRII cells and demonstrated its effectiveness. This system has the potential to accelerate cell-based infection assays, such as large-scale genetic screening, and to provide new insights into Francisella infection in epithelial cells, which has been difficult to analyze in phagocytic cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10083-y.Keywords: Francisella, Tularemia, HeLa cells, FcγRII, Infection system, Host factors, GLS1, Glutaminase, Ammonia

Background

Francisella tularensis, a facultative intracellular bacterium, is the causative agent of tularemia of the zoonotic disease tularemia [1]. Tularemia exhibits severe symptoms in humans, such as high fever, swelling of the axillary lymph nodes, and arthralgia, and has an untreated mortality rate of 60% [2]. Because F. tularensis can be easily cultured, aerosolized, and made resistant to antibiotics, it was developed into a biological weapon in the US and Soviet Union until the 1960s [3]. F. tularensis is considered a potential biological weapon and is currently classified as a category A bioterrorism agent by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [4]. F. tularensis can be classified into four subspecies. Among the different subspecies of F. tularensis, only F. tularensis subsp. tularensis (Type A) and F. tularensis subsp. holarctica (Type B) is highly pathogenic to humans [5]. F. tularensis subsp. novicida (hereafter referred to as F. novicida) causes disease in mice but does not exhibit pathogenicity in humans except in immunocompetent patients, although it shares ∼ 97% nucleotide identity with F. tularensis subsp. tularensis. Therefore, F. novicida was commonly used as a surrogate organism for experimental studies [6].

Francisella research has been conducted mainly in the context of its high pathogenicity, and to date, considerable facts related to its pathogenicity have been uncovered. In particular, knowledge regarding bacterial aspects, such as essential factors for intracellular growth, has been well established. In contrast, intracellular pathogens must possess strategies to compete with host metabolic processes to replicate using limited host-derived resources in the cell while avoiding cellular immune defenses [7]. Host cells also need to alter their metabolic state in response to infection. Therefore, the analysis of host–pathogen interactions, with a particular focus on the interaction with the host’s nutritional and immune systems, is the key theme of the current Francisella research. However, there is insufficient knowledge of the host factors involved in Francisella infection.

Cell-based experiments are associated with gene silencing or gene editing are ideal strategies for analyzing host factor-dependent mechanisms of Francisella infection. Since Francisella can multiply within macrophages, in vitro phagocytic cell-based infection models have been developed [2]. In general, bone marrow-derived macrophage cells, phorbor myristate acetate (PMA) differentiated U937 (human), THP-1 (humans) cells, and J774 (mice) cells are widely used for the pathogenicity analysis of Francisella [8–11]. However, editing and silencing genes in these macrophage-like cells are challenging because transfection of DNA plasmids is only effective in proliferating cells and, not in nonproliferating ones [12]. Additionally, gene editing during the construction of stable cell lines can cause failure of normal differentiation due to PMA differentiation that alters the expression of diverse gene expression [13]. These features could pose obstacles to analyses involving gene editing. In contrast, there have been relatively few analyses of using epithelial cell lines compared to macrophage cell lines. However, Francisella infection is rarely performed using epithelial cells, despite their ease of gene editing, because the efficiency of the infection is largely reduced compared with macrophage-like cells. Francisella infection of epithelial cell lines such as HeLa, A549, and HEK293 has been performed at a relatively high multiplicity of infection (MOI) (100–10,000) and for a long time (8–24 h) [14–16]. This is 100–10,000 times greater than the rate of infection in macrophage-like cells.

The efficiency of Francisella uptake by macrophages depends on whether the bacteria are opsonized or not. It is known that serum- or antibody-opsonized bacteria are taken up by macrophages at 10-fold higher levels than unopsonized bacteria [17]. Bone marrow-derived macrophages isolated from Fc-gamma receptors (FcγR)-knockout mice exhibit a 90% reduction in the uptake of immunoglobulin G (IgG)-opsonized bacteria. Therefore, FcγR-mediated uptake may be a potent strategy for infecting epithelial cells with Francisella.

GLS1 is a glutaminase that generates glutamate from glutamine. GLS1 is regarded to be a key enzyme in the glutamine metabolic pathway. GLS1 plays an important role in various aspects, including neurotransmission in the central nervous system and tumorigenesis [18, 19]. GLS1 has also recently been reported to be implicated in the regulation of lysosomal function and autophagy [20, 21]. Here, we develop a new epithelial cell infection model of F. novicida using HeLa cells expressing FcγRII [22, 23]. Subsequently, using this new infection model, we demonstrated that the human protein GLS1 is an important host factor for Francisella infection. Furthermore, the detailed mechanisms by which GLS1 is involved in Francisella infection were investigated. These results suggest that our new Francisella infection model represents a powerful tool for identifying host factors important for Francisella infection.

Methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

F. novicida U112 was obtained from the Pathogenic Microorganism Genetic Resource Stock Center (Gifu University, Gifu, Japan). F. novicida was cultured aerobically at 37 °C in a rain heart infusion broth (Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) supplemented with 0.1% cysteine (BHIc) [24]. The construction of a wild-type strain expressing GFP F. novicida U112 wild-type strain was described previously [25]. The bacterial concentration was adjusted by optical density (OD595) in the culture medium. All experiments were conducted according to institutional biosecurity guidelines and approved by Yamaguchi University.

Cell culture

HeLa cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HEK293T cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with GlutaMAX™ supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 10% FBS at 37ºC in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Human monocytic U937 cells were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37ºC in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Plasmid construction

Additional file Table 1 lists the primer sets and templates used to construct the plasmids. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using KOD-Plus-Neo polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) and ligation was performed using an In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan).

Stable cell lines

The establishment methods of HeLa cells stably expressing FcγRII (HeLa-FcγRII cells) were described previously [26]. Briefly, the cDNA of mouse beta Fc receptor type II gene (Fcgr2b) mRNA (GenBank accession no. M31312.1) was amplified by PCR and inserted into pcDNA 3.1/Hygro (Takara Bio). Plasmid DNA was transfected into HeLa cells, and cells expressing FcγRII (HeLa-FcγRII cells) were selected by culture in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS in the presence of 200 µg/ml hygromycin (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan). Immunoblotting assays were conducted to verify the expression of the FcγRIIb protein (Additional file Fig. 3). HeLa-FcγRII cells were grown in DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 200 µg ml− 1 hygromycin at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. In order to construct the GLS1 knockdown cells of HeLa-FcγRII, we constructed the lentiviral vector for short hairpin (sh)RNA-based knockdown. Sequences of the GLS1 targeting sequences were selected from the Genetic Perturbation Platform: TRCN0000051135 for GLS1 sh1 and TRCN0000051136 for GLS1 sh2. The forward and reverse sequences were ordered as primer oligonucleotides and annealed as the insertion sequence. Both oligonucleotides (final concentration: 2.5 µM) were mixed with annealing buffer (10 mM Tris [pH 7.5], 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) and incubated at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by at room temperature for 45 min. Annealed sequences were purified and inserted into a linearized pLKO.1-puro plasmid cut by EcoRI and AgeI using a DNA ligation kit (Takara Bio). The plasmid DNA was transfected with packaging plasmids (psPAX2, pCMV-VSV-G) (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) in HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 48 h. The supernatants containing lentivirus were collected, and debris was removed by passing through a 0.45 pm pore-size filter. The pLKO.1-TRC control plasmid (Addgene, #10879) was used as the sh control vector. Lentiviral vectors were infected with HeLa-FcγRII by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 2 h at 30 °C (MOI = 0.30) and selected with 1.0 µg/ml puromycin starting 24 h after infection. The constructed cell lines were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS in the presence of 200 µg/mL hygromycin and 1.0 µg/ml puromycin.

Immunoblotting

For western blot analysis, a 1x sample buffer was prepared by mixing 4× NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen, MA, USA) with 100 µM dithiothreitol (DTT) in distilled water (DW). Cells were lysed with 1× sample buffer and boiled for 5 min. The samples were electrophoresed on 4–12% Bis–Tris gels for 40 min at 200 mV in MOPS SDS running buffer (Invitrogen) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were incubated with blocking buffer (5% skim milk in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20; TBS-T) at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with the first antibody in 1% skim milk in TBS-T at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h and was developed using an Amersham™ ImageQuant™ 800 (Cytiba, Little Chalfont, UK).

Cytotoxicity assay

HeLa-FcγRII cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were preincubated in a 48-well tissue culture plate overnight. F. novicida strains in the exponential phase were measured for OD595 and the bacterial concentrations were adjusted to OD595 = 0.01. The adjusted bacterial suspension was mixed with mouse serum containing F. novicida antibodies at the indicated concentrations and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. The F. novicida strains were added to the cells at a MOI of 1.0 or 5.0. The plates were then centrifuged for 10 min at 300 × g and incubated for the indicated times. lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release into supernatants was measured using the LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Takara Bio). U937 cells (4 × 105 cells/well) were preincubated in a 48-well tissue culture plate with 200 nM of PMA for 48 h. The F. novicida strain was added at an MOI of 1.0. The plates were then centrifuged for 10 min at 300 × g and incubated for the indicated times. The release into supernatants was measured using the LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Takara Bio).

Intracellular growth assay

HeLa-FcγRII cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were incubated in a 48-well tissue culture and incubated overnight. F. novicida strains in the exponential phase were measured for OD595 and the bacterial concentrations were adjusted. The adjusted bacterial suspension was mixed with mouse serum containing F. novicida antibodies at the indicated concentrations and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Then, F. novicida strains were added to the cells at the indicated MOI. Next, the plates were centrifuged for 10 min at 300 × g and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The cells were then washed three times with DMEM medium and extracellular bacteria were killed with gentamicin at 50 µg/ml for 1 h. The cells were then incubated in a fresh medium at 37 °C for the time durations in figure legends. In order to measure intracellular growth, cells were washed with PBS and then lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100 in CDM. colony-forming unit (CFU) was determined on BHIc agar plates by plating serial dilutions of cultures.

Fluorescence microscopy

HeLa-FcγRII cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were incubated overnight on 12-mm glass coverslips in 24-well tissue culture plates. GFP-expressing F. novicida strains were incubated with mouse serum and infected at an MOI of 1.0. The plates were then centrifuged for 10 min at 300 × g and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were washed three times with DMEM and extracellular bacteria were eliminated using gentamicin at 50 µg/ml for 1 h. The cells were then incubated in a fresh medium at 37 °C for the time durations indicated in the figure legends. After treatment with LysoTracker™ Red DND-99 (Invitrogen) according to the instructions, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. A FluoView FV100 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to obtain images of the cells.

Lysosomal pH measurements

The Lysosomal Acidic pH Detection Kit (DOJINDO LABORATORIES, Kumamoto, Japan) was used for the measurement of lysosomal pH. Briefly, HeLa-FcγRII cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were incubated overnight in 48-well tissue culture plates. The supernatants were removed, and the cells were washed with Live Cell Imaging Buffer (LCIM, Invitrogen), then incubated with LysoPrime Green solution in LCIM (1:1000 dilution of stock solution) at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed with LCIM and replaced with pHLys Red solution in LCIM (1:2000 dilution of stock solution) containing either Bafilomycin A1 (1:2000 dilution of stock solution) or 5 mM ammonia. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed with LCIM, and images were obtained using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus). Since ammonia diffuses easily from lysosomes, ammonia was constantly added through all procedures in microscopic observation.

Chemicals and antibodies

The chemicals were obtained from the following sources: BPTES (S7753, Selleck Biotech, Tokyo Japan), GSH-MEE (G1404, Sigma-Aldrich), α-ketoglutaric acid (α-KG, 75890, Sigma-Aldrich) and ammonia solution (900–01835, Kishida Chemical, Osaka, Japan). Antibodies were obtained from the following sources: anti-Glutaminase rabbit monoclonal antibody (ab156876, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:1,000 dilution), FcγRIIb rabbit monoclonal antibody (96397T, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA; 1:1,000 dilution), LC3 A/B rabbit monoclonal antibody (12741 S, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1,000 dilution), SQSTM1/p62 rabbit polyclonal antibody (5114 S, Cell Signaling Technology; 1: 1,000 dilution), beta-actin rabbit monoclonal antibody (5125 S, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:10,000 dilution), and goat antirabbit IgG antibody HRP conjugated (12–348, Sigma-Aldrich; 20,000 dilutions).

Preparation of anti-F. novicida mouse serum

F. novicida was disrupted by sonication (Taitech, Saitama, Japan). The resulting insoluble fraction was collected by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min and then suspended in PBS. Protein concentration was determined using a Coomassie Brilliant Blue protein assay kit (Nacalai tesque, Kyoto, Japan) after dissolving in 10% SDS solution. Immunization of mice was performed by Cosmo Bio (Tokyo, Japan) as contract research. Briefly, mice were immunized with 20 µg of protein four times every 2 weeks and serum was collected.

Statistical analysis

Student’s t-test or multiple comparisons using the Dunnett test were used to evaluate the significance of differences compared to the control condition; P < 0.05 indicates a significant difference.

Results

F. novicida internalizes into HeLa-FcγRII cells depending on the anti-F. novicda serum

HeLa-FcγRII cells are a cell line that produces the FcγRII protein, which enables them to efficiently internalize bacteria opsonized with IgG (Fig. 1A). In order to determine whether F. novicida uptake into HeLa-FcγRII cells is enhanced in the presence of anti-F. novicida serum, we quantified the number of intracellular bacteria by CFU count. The wild-type strain of F. novicida was incubated with serum derived from mice immunized with membrane components of F. novicida and then added to the culture of HeLa-FcγRII cells. As treating the F. novicida with various serum concentrations, the number of intracellular bacteria at 2 h after infection increased depending on the serum concentration (Fig. 1B). At the same serum concentration, the number of intracellular bacteria in HeLa-FcγRII cells increased significantly at both time points compared to the parental strain of HeLa cells. In HeLa-FcγRII cells, intracellular growth up to 24 h after infection was also significantly increased compared to the numbers of parental strains, and the intracellular bacteria were approximately 100-fold. Next, we check the efficiency of infection with various concentrations of bacteria. Although there was a tendency for the number of intracellular bacteria to increase with increased MOI of infection, there were no significant differences between each MOI (Fig. 1C). Because some intracellular F. novicida taken up by macrophages are fused with lysosomes, we investigated whether internalized F. novicida in HeLa-FcγRII cells were also fused with lysosomes using confocal laser scanning microscopy. Four hours postinfection, the GFP-expressing F. novicida strain was observed in HeLa-FcγRII cells, and some intracellular bacteria were colocalized with the lysosomes (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that HeLa-FcγRII cells serve as a model for intracellular infection of epithelial cells, and their uptake efficiency depends on the concentration of antiserum against F. novicida.

Glutaminase knockdown enhances F. novicida infection-induced cytotoxicity in HeLa-FcγRII cells

In order to investigate the utility of HeLa-FcγRII cells, the gene of host cell was knocked down and its effect on the infection by F. novicida was investigated. Since glutaminase has been reported to be an important factor for lysosomal function and autophagy, we focused on GLS1, the gene encoding glutaminase, and stably knockdown HeLa-FcγRII cells were created using sh RNA (Fig. 2A). First, we examine the cytotoxicity of F. novicida infection using a LDH cytotoxicity assay. GLS1 knockdown cells showed a significantly increased LDH release compared to control cells upon F. novicida infection at MOI = 5.0 (Fig. 2B). However, there were no significant differences in intracellular growth of F. novicida between GLS1 knockdown cells and the control cells (Fig. 2C), when the number of intracellular bacteria was calculated using the CFU count. Furthermore, we investigated the effect of BPTES, a selective GLS1 inhibitor, on cytotoxicity during F. novicida infection. The LDH assay revealed that the release of LDH from HeLa-FcγRII cells upon F. novicida was significantly increased in the presence of BPTES (Fig. 2D). These results suggest that GLS1 plays a protective role against cytotoxicity in Francisella-infected HeLa-FcγRII cells.

Ammonia prevents intracellular growth of F. novicida in HeLa-FcγRII cells

Glutaminase is an enzyme important for producing glutathione (GSH), α-KG, and ammonia downstream of the glutaminolysis pathway [19]. In order to determine the important factor for F. novicida infection, we attempted to rescue the increase in the cytotoxicity of GLS1 knockdown HeLa-FcγRII cells upon infection by F. novicida by supplying these metabolites to the medium. Neither the α-KG nor GSH supplementation prevented enhanced cytotoxicity enhanced by GLS1 knockdown (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast, the ammonia treatment significantly decreased the cytotoxicity of HeLa-FcγRII cells after F. novicida infection compared to nontreatment conditions (Fig. 3C). Additionally, the supply of ammonia to the medium significantly decreased the number of intracellular bacteria of F. novicida in HeLa-FcγRII cells (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that ammonia treatment protects HeLa-FcγRII cells from F. novicida infection by inhibiting intracellular bacterial growth.

Ammonia inhibits autophagy in HeLa-FcγRII cells

In order to determine whether ammonia activates or inhibits autophagy in HeLa-FcγRII cells, we measured autophagy flux under ammonia treatment using p62 (SQSTM1) and LC3 protein levels as indicators. Consequently, ammonia treatment increased the level of LC3 II in both uninfected and infected HeLa-FcγRII cells of F. novicida (Fig. 4A), indicating an increase in autophagosomes by ammonium treatment. In contrast, the expression level of p62 also increased in uninfected and infected HeLa-FcγRII cells, suggesting the inhibition of autophagy flux despite the increased autophagosomes (Fig. 4A). To check the condition of lysosome in ammonia-treated HeLa-FcγRII cells, the pH of lysosomes was measured using Lysosomal Acidic pH Detection Kit. As the results, lysosomes were neutralized by ammonia and pH of lysosomes was increased (Additional file Fig. 4 These results indicate that ammonia treatment may protect cells from F. novicida infection by inhibiting autophagy flux through dysfunction of lysosomes. Additionally, the effect of ammonia treatment on the cytotoxicity of F. novicida infection was checked using U937, a human macrophage cell line commonly used as a Francisella infection model. Similar to the result in HeLa-FcγRII cells, ammonia treatment significantly decreased the cytotoxicity of U937 cells during F. novicida infection compared to no-treatment conditions (Fig. 4B). These results indicate that these phenomena seen in HeLa-FcγRII cells are also observed in cells of macrophage lineage.

Discussion

Macrophage-based cell lines have been used as models of Francisella infection. However, they are less amenable to gene silencing and editing than epithelial cells, making them inappropriate for the search for host-side factors. In this study, we demonstrated HeLa-FcγRII cells as a new model of Francisella infection in epithelial cells. In the infection model using HeLa-FcγRII cells, opsonized bacteria are internalized into mouse FcγRIIb receptors that express HeLa cells by phagocytosis. The mouse FcγRIIb is conserved between humans and mice including the dileucine motif, which is essential for endocytosis [27, 28]. To date, several studies with similar infection systems have shown that internalization dependent on mouse FcγRIIb could be used for various pathogens, including Legionella, Toxoplasma, and human immunodeficiency virus [26, 29, 30]. In the intracellular growth assay, F. novicida internalized into HeLa-FcγRII cells and replicated in cells in a serum concentration-dependent manner. Under the same serum concentration conditions, HeLa-FcγRII cells showed approximately two orders of magnitude increase in intracellular bacterial number compared to parental HeLa cells that were absent FcγRII. Additionally, MOI and the incubation time to infection remain low. Specifically, an MOI of 0.10 showed the same infection efficiency as an MOI of 10, and only 2 h of infection was necessary to detect intracellular bacteria. These facts support that the infection model we established works as an efficient Francisella infection model of epithelial cells. Previous reports have confirmed that the IgG complex internalized by overexpressed FcγRIIb was recruited into lysosomes [31]. Francisella trapped in phagosomes rapidly escapes into the cytosol within minutes of phagosome formation [32]. In contrast, serum-opsonized Francisella has been reported to result in acidification of a modest 20–30% of phagosomes [33]. In our data, almost all bacteria escaped into the cytosol at 4 h postinfection, but a few had colocalized with lysosomes. These results suggest that this infection model mimics the Francisella life cycle from internalization to intracellular replication in previous reports.

The ease of gene silencing and editing is one of the advantages of this experimental system using epithelial cells compared to macrophage-like cells. Human monocyte cell lines such as U937 and THP-1 are commonly used in Francisella research for infection after differentiation into macrophage-like cells with PMA stimulation [9, 34, 35]. However, these cells, which have been differentiated into macrophage-like cells, are difficult to gene silencing or editing because DNA plasmid transfection into cells is effective in proliferating cells but not in nonproliferating cells [12]. Furthermore, in the construction of stable cell lines, gene editing can lead to the failure of normal differentiation because PMA differentiation alters diverse gene expression [13]. These features could be obstacles to an analysis that involves frequent gene editing. We demonstrated the versatility of HeLa-FcγRII cells by constructing GLS1 stably knockdown cells of HeLa-FcγRII. GLS1 is an enzyme that hydrolyzes glutamine into glutamate; it is a critical protein for mediating and glutaminolysis [36]. Glutaminolysis plays a crucial role in various cell functions and is also required for M1-like polarization and Th17 cell differentiation in immune cells [37, 38]. In fact, reducing glutamine concentrations causes differentiation of U937 cells along the monocytic pathway [39]. Also, we attempted to construct the GLS1 stable knockdown of U937 cells, but most cells failed to differentiate into macrophages when the cells were treated with PMA (data not shown). In this research, we constructed the GLS1 stably knockdown HeLa-FcγRII cells and analyzed its importance in the context of Francisella infection for the first time. The GLS1 knockdown HeLa-FcγRII cells showed a high cytotoxicity in F. novicida infection compared with the control cells, despite the constant intracellular number of bacteria. Recently, it was suggested that GLS1 mediates the production of peripheral blood mononuclear cells cytokines during Mycobacterium tuberculosis and the activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway during Staphylococcus aureus infection [40, 41]. Glutamine is also an important factor in F. tularensis infection; Francisella has been shown to manipulate the expression of host glutamine transporter expression during infection [42]. These facts support the hypothesis that GLS1 may be a promising new target for the analysis of Francisella infection mechanisms. Altogether, the infection model using HeLa-FcγRII cells which can be easily genetically modified, could provide new insights, particularly into the host factors involved in Francisella infection.

GLS1 is an important enzyme that produces GSH, α-KG, and ammonium in the glutaminolysis pathway. Among those glutaminolysis metabolites, in our data, only ammonia supplementation rescued the GLS1 knockdown HeLa-FcγRII cells from increased cytotoxicity caused by F. novicida infection. Furthermore, ammonia treatment suppressed the intracellular growth of F. novicida in HeLa-FcγRII cells. In addition, ammonia supplementation increased lysosomal pH in HeLa-FcγRII cells (Additional file Fig. 4). Increasing evidence has revealed that glutaminolysis-produced ammonia neutralizes lysosomal pH and prevents excess acidification [21, 43]. For many intracellular bacteria, lysosomes pose a significant threat and they employ various strategies to evade them [44]. In Francisella, rapid escape from the phagosome is considered a strategy of lysosomal evasion [32]. It is contentious whether a host intracellular acidification side with a replication of Francisella. However, previous research has shown that a certain number of bacteria are entrapped inside lysosomes and that alkalizing the pH of the intracellular environment inhibits the intracellular proliferation of Francisella [45–47]. Additionally, it was demonstrated that the acidification of Francisella-containing phagosomes is not required for escape and replication of unopsonized or serum-opsonized Francisella but is required for rapid escape of antibody-opsonized Francisella. Our results using anti-F. novicida serum and ammonia that resulted in the reduction of cytotoxicity and proliferation of intracellular bacteria are consistent with these previous reports. These findings suggest that ammonia-mediated intracellular pH regulation can affect the intracellular fate of Francisella. The high susceptibility of GLS1 knockdown cells to F. novicida infection may reflect an excessively high lysosomal pH.

The utilization of host autophagy to acquire nutrients is a particularly interesting feature of the Francisella life cycle within host cells [48]. Autophagy inhibitors such as 3-methyladenine, bafilomycin A1, and ammonium chloride can effectively restrict the pathogenicity of Francisella [48–51]. However, it is controversial whether ammonia is an inhibitor or promoter of autophagy. The hypothesis that autophagy flux inhibition interferes with autophagosome degradation due to lysosomal dysfunction induced by neutralization is widely accepted [52, 53]. In contrast, low concentrations of ammonia are believed to activate autophagy flux through several pathways, including the mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase complex 1 (mTORC1) and the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [54]. The accumulation of LC3 II and p62 proteins observed in our data implied that ammonia treatment activated autophagy induction but suppressed autophagy flux even at low concentrations (2 mM) in HeLa-FcγRII cells. Previous research has reported that ammonium chloride inhibits endosomal acidification and abrogates endosomal escape and intracellular growth of Francisella [47, 51]. Our data in this study follow these previous reports that lysosomal neutralization reduces autophagy flux and highlights the importance of autophagy regulation supported by lysosomal stabilization in Francisella infection. In addition, the recent study showed that glutamine suppression caused by inactivation of GLS1 during Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection results in autophagy suppression [55], emphasizing the link between GLS1 and autophagy upon intracellular bacterial infection. In this study, we also demonstrated that ammonia treatment reduced the cytotoxicity induced by F. novicida in U937 cells. These results indicate that this new infection model with HeLa-FcγRII cells mimics the responses against F. novicida infection in macrophage cell lines and emphasizes the usefulness of the HeLa-FcγRII cells infection model for the pathogenicity analysis of Francisella infection.

In general, we demonstrated a novel Francisella infection model using HeLa-FcγRII cells and validated its usefulness in this research. The newly constructed infection model using HeLa-FcγRII cells improved the efficiency of F. novicida infection of epithelial cells and facilitated the analysis of the intracellular dynamics of Francisella. Next, using this new infection model, we also presented novel evidence that GLS1 may play an important role in the control of F. novicida infection. Although further studies are needed to reveal the detailed functions of GLS1 under infection, data acquisition with HeLa-FcγRII cells can provide new information about host factors for Francisella infection. However, the use of this infection model requires caution on the following two points. Firstly, we have to carefully consider the applicability of the data, as they were acquired under limited conditions using F. novicida, a non-pathogenic strain of Francisella in humans. Secondly, the infection mechanism through Fc receptor-mediated forced uptake is an extraordinary condition. Therefore, further verifications using macrophage cell lines or highly pathogenic Francisella strains are still needed to prove the exact function of the GLS1 in host cells. By addressing these issues properly, data acquisition with HeLa-FcγRII cells can provide new insights from alternative prospectives about host factors for Francisella infection.

Conclusion

In this study we developed a new Francisella infection system using epithelial cells, which are easy to genetically manipulate. By infecting HeLa cells expressing FcγRII with antibody-treated bacteria, we achieved an infection efficiency about 100 times higher than that of normal HeLa cells. Using this infection system, we clarified that GLS1 and ammonia produced downstream GLS1 signaling in F. novicida infection are important for controlling the infection. This demonstrates that the novel infection system using Hela-FcγRII cells is useful for the identification of host factors important for Francisella infection.
