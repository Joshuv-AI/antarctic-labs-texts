Second‐Look Ultrasonography for Evaluating BIRADS 3 and 4 Non Mass‐Enhancement: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Elaheh Yaghoubvand, Saeed Mohammadzadeh, Fahimeh Zeinalkhani, Nasrin Ahmadinejad, Mahshid Hamidi, Peyman Kamali Hakim, Hadise Zeinalkhani, Hamid Rajabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsBreast MRI is a highly sensitive imaging modality for assessing suspected breast lesions; nevertheless, its specificity is poor, ranging from 40% to 80% in various studies. Second‐look ultrasound (SLUS) is a tool for the validation of MRI‐identified breast abnormalities. This study aimed to assess the role of SLUS as a complementary method for evaluating suspicious non mass‐enhancement (NME) on breast MRI.MethodsFifty patients with 52 non‐mass enhancing lesions, classified as BIRADS 3 or 4 on MRI by an expert breast radiologist, were rechecked by SLUS. The relationship between age, breast cancer history, MRI indication, imaging features, and BIRADS classification of the lesions with the sonographic detection rate and pathology findings was analyzed.ResultsOut of 52 lesions, 23 were identified on SLUS, 21 patients referred to biopsy. Seven out of 21 biopsies had malignant pathologies. Age less than 40 years, pathological breast discharge, and BIRADS‐MRI 4 had a positive relationship with the identification on SLUS. Five of 29 lesions that had no sonographic findings were referred to stereotactic biopsy due to suspicious findings on mammography, and four were malignant on pathology. The only variable that showed a significant relationship with the lesion malignancy was the BIRADS category on MRI.ConclusionNMEs with BIRADS 4 on MRI were more detectable on SLUS than BIRADS‐MRI 3. If detected, ultrasound could not downgrade the BIRADS category of the lesions or exempt them from biopsy.

Introduction

Breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is one of the useful imaging methods that is applied with high sensitivity in evaluating suspicious breast lesions. Still, the specificity of this method is low, and in various studies, its specificity for malignant breast lesions has varied between 40% and 80% [1, 2].

Imaging and reading methods for breast MRI were not standardized before the introduction of BIRADS. The first edition of BIRADS‐MRI was published in 2003, initiating the diagnosis using the common lexicon [3]. One of the important lesions seen on breast MRI is the non‐mass enhancement or NME. Although MRI is considered the most sensitive method for detecting malignant breast lesions [4, 5], its role in evaluating the malignancy of NMEs is limited [6]. Lexicons are defined by distribution (focal, linear, segmental, regional, multiple regions, and diffuse) and internal enhancement patterns (homogeneous, heterogeneous, clumped, and clustered ring) for NME. Among these, linear, segmental, clumped, clustered ring, and heterogeneous are the terms that suggest malignancy. In particular, these NMEs are intended for ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) [7, 8]. The malignancy frequency of NME is widely distributed, ranging from 25% to 83.6% [6]. Segmental lesions are very likely to be malignant, while linear lesions are less likely to be malignant. Focal lesions seem to be less worrisome but have a certain malignancy frequency [7, 9]. Showing a certain trend was impossible for regional, multiple regional, and diffuse, where the number of cases is small [6, 7, 10].

Second‐look ultrasound (SLUS) is a complementary method for rechecking and confirming the diagnosis of suspicious breast lesions that are detected on MRI [11]. Different studies have demonstrated the efficiency of the SLUS in the evaluation of suspicious lesions seen on MRI [12, 13].

Some lesions that are identified on MRI are hidden in mammography and ultrasound. However, if a lesion can be detected by ultrasound, ultrasound‐guided biopsy (US) is preferred because US‐guided biopsy is faster and more convenient, as well as more accurate and less expensive than MRI‐guided biopsy because the ability to biopsy in real‐time is higher in ultrasound [14].

The most important limitations of MRI in investigating suspicious breast lesions include the high number of false positives for malignant lesions and the management of the accidental lesions in case of diagnosis on MRI [15].

The importance of non‐mass enhancement in MRI is not well known. On the other hand, MRI‐guided biopsy, in addition to being expensive and time‐consuming, is not easily available anywhere. Therefore, ultrasound, as a safe available method, can play a significant role in the additional evaluation of non‐mass enhancement and prevention of unnecessary interventions [16].

Materials and Methods

This was a cross‐sectional study conducted in Imam Khomeini Hospital, Tehran, Iran, from March 2022 to March 2023 on patients with non‐mass enhancement lesions in their MRI. After the proposal was approved by the ethics committee of Tehran University of Medical Science (IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1401.043), 52 patients with NME on breast MRI were included in the study. After explaining the importance of the study and obtaining informed consent, the patients underwent a second‐look sonography. Patients with poor compliance or incomplete past medical history were excluded from our study. As for reporting we adhered to Guidelines for reporting of statistics for clinical research in urology to enhance the methodological rigor of our investigation [17].

The variables of patient's age, history of breast cancer (personal or in first‐degree relative), indication of MRI (mass palpation, pathological nipple discharge, screening, and as a supplementary imaging), as well as the imaging characteristics of the lesion on MRI (distribution, internal enhancement pattern, and BIRADS‐MRI) from the data in the patient's clinical file and MR imaging were extracted. Breast MRIs were conducted using a GE 3 T Discovery 750 Gem with a phased array surface coil. Our imaging methodology comprised axial T1‐weighted sequences (repetition time [TR]: 400 ms; echo time [TE]: 10 ms; bandwidth: 31.25 Hz/pixel; slice thickness: 5.0 mm; matrix size: 384 × 256; field of view [FOV]: 320 mm; number of excitations [NEX]: 1). We obtained axial short tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences with a TR/TE of 4500/63 ms, a bandwidth of 62.5 Hz/pixel, a slice thickness of 5.0 mm, a matrix size of 320 × 256, a FOV of 320 mm, and a NEX of 1. MRI examination was assessed by an attending radiologist with 10 years of experience in breast imaging, interpreting an average of 700 cases annually. The NME lesions and their BIRADS assessment were determined by the interpretation of the attending radiologist.

SLUS examination was conducted utilizing a SuperSonic Imagine Aixplorer MACH 30 ultrasound system, fitted with a high‐frequency linear array transducer (5–15 MHz) by a radiologist with more than 5 years of experience in breast sonography. Based on the BIRADS lexicon, ultrasound findings were described and registered. Also, all patients underwent mammographic evaluation as part of standard breast management prior to MRI examination, with all imaging interpretations performed by a radiologist with 7 years of experience. The decision regarding the necessity of a biopsy for the patients was made based on the clinical assessment, mammography, MRI, and SLUS finding. Finally, 23 lesions based on ultrasound review and 5 lesions based on the mammography findings of the patients (which were already available) were candidates for biopsy under imaging guidance. Biopsies of 26 lesions were performed in our center, and lesions were classified as either benign or malignant.

Our cohort of 52 lesions exceeded this threshold, ensuring adequate power for the primary analysis. Data analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). We employed the Chi‐square test to assess associations between categorical variables. When expected cell counts were less than 5, Fisher's exact test was utilized. A power analysis, assuming a malignancy incidence of 5% for BI‐RADS 3 and 40% for BI‐RADS 4 lesions (α = 0.05, power = 80%), determined that at least 40 lesions (20 per group) are necessary to identify lesion pathology (benign/malignant) based on sample size formula of Chi‐square test (N = (Zα/2 + Zβ)2 × (p1(1 − p1) + p2(1 − p2))/(p1 − p2)2).

We compared diverse imaging variables and patient characteristics with the definitive pathology (benign vs. malignant) and the visibility of lesions on SLUS. We specifically evaluated the correlation between the BIRADS‐MRI category and malignancy, along with the relationships between NME‐MRI distribution and internal enhancement patterns with malignancy. Furthermore, we examined the relationship between a positive breast cancer history, NME‐MRI distribution, and lesion detection on SLUS. We also investigated the correlation between the lesion pathology (benign/malignant) and their detection rate via SLUS. Ultimately, we assessed whether parameters, including age under 40 years, pathological nipple discharge, BIRADS 4 on MRI, and particular internal enhancement patterns (homogeneous, clumped, and cluster ring vs. heterogeneous) correlated with an increased probability of lesion identification with sonography. A two‐sided p‐value of < 0.05 was considered for statistical significance. Quantitative data were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed variables and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non‐normally distributed variables. A significance level of 0.05 was considered as a significance scale.

Results

In this study, 50 patients with 52 NME‐MRI (NMEs detected on MRI) underwent an SLUS. Forty‐one patients (82%) were over 40 years old, and the rest were under 40 years old. Seven patients (14%) had a positive personal or family history (in a first‐degree relative) of breast cancer. Thirty‐four cases (68%) due to mass palpation, five (10%) cases for pathological nipple discharge, and 11 (22%) cases for screening/supplementary imaging were referred to do contrast‐enhanced breast MRI.

Heterogeneous internal enhancement was the most frequent enhancement pattern and was seen in 35 lesions (67%). Linear NME‐MRI distribution was the most frequent distribution that was found in 18 lesions (34%), and multiple regional distribution was found in none of them. Finally, using MRI findings, 34 lesions were classified into BIRADS 4 and 18 lesions into BIRADS 3. More details are shown in Table 1.

Out of 23 lesions detected on SLUS, 21 cases were classified as BIRADS‐US 4 and two cases as BIRADS‐US 3. More precisely, all the BIRADS‐MRI 4 lesions found on sonography remained in the BIRADS 4 category, and SLUS could not downgrade the BIRADS class of these lesions. In the case of BIRADS‐MRI 3 lesions, sonography assigned a higher BIRADS to the lesion in one case while maintaining the same classification in another case.

As mentioned above, 23 NME‐MRIs were found on SLUS, and 29 lesions had no ultrasound finding. On SLUS evaluation, eight of 23 lesions presented as NME‐US, while 15 manifested as mass lesions. Also, five cases out of 29 lesions without sonographic findings had suspicious findings on mammography and thus were referred for stereotactic biopsy. All of these 5 lesions were BIRADS‐MRI 4. The other 24 patients without findings on SLUS were recommended for short‐term follow‐up with MRI 6‐month time intervals for 2 years.

Finally, 26 lesions were biopsied in our center under the guidance of sonography or mammography, and the results of their histological examination are recorded separately in Table 3. Eleven of 26 biopsies (42.3%) had malignant results, and invasive ductal carcinoma was more frequent (26.9%). Among the variables of this study, the only variable that had a significant relationship with the final pathology in terms of benignity and malignancy was the BIRADS category of the lesion on MRI (p‐value = 0.01), as all 11 malignant lesions identified in this study were BIRADS‐MRI 4 and none of the BIRADS‐MRI 3 lesions had malignant pathology. We found no statistically significant association between the malignancy probability and the distribution (p‐value = 0.28) or the internal enhancement pattern (p‐value = 0.15) of the lesions.

Among the studied variables, a positive history of breast cancer and NME‐MRI distribution had no significant relationship with the detection of the lesion on SLUS (p‐value = 0.121). Most NME‐MRIs with focal distribution were not visible on SLUS. In contrast, NME‐MRIs with segmental and linear distribution were more frequently correlated. However, these findings were not statistically significant with a small difference (p‐value = 0.08). Also, there was no significant relationship between benign and malignant pathology of the lesions and their detection rate on sonography (p‐value = 0.18). On the other hand, age less than 40 years, pathological nipple discharge, and BIRADS 4 on MRI were associated with a higher frequency of detection of the lesions on sonography (p‐values, respectively, 0.04, 0.008, and < 0.001). Also, lesions with homogeneous, clumped, and cluster ring internal enhancement patterns were more likely to be discovered on sonography than lesions with a heterogeneous enhancement pattern with pairwise p‐values of 0.007, < 0.001, and 0.012, respectively.

Discussion

This study was conducted on 52 NME lesions found on beast MRI, which included 18 lesions of BIRADS‐MRI 3 and 34 lesions of BIRADS‐MRI 4. BIRADS 3 assessment on MRI remains a challenging issue among breast radiologists. Given the absence of well‐established criteria for BIRADS 3 NME lesions [18], we conducted BIRADS classification based on expert opinion. In this manner, the lesions that didn't have definite suspicious features to consider as BIRADS 4 by an expert beast radiologist, were classified as BIRADS 3, such as NME lesions that were difficult to differentiate from background parenchymal enhancement.

The detection rate of the lesions on SLUS was 44%, comparable to similar studies [11] or significantly different from them [1, 14, 19]. In general, in various studies, the detection rate of SLUS for lesions seen on MRI, both mass and non‐mass, has varied from 22% to 82%, and in most studies, the detection rate of mass lesions and malignant lesions was higher than NMEs and benign lesions [19]. Also, the sonographic detection rate of NME lesions has been very variable in different studies (from 12% to 92%) [14], and some of these differences can be caused by differences in the inherent characteristics of the studied population, method, and sonographer experience. In the present study, all included lesions were non‐enhancement types. The examined variables, including a positive breast cancer history and NME distribution, exhibited no significant correlation with the lesion detection rate on SLUS. Additionally, an age under 40 years, pathological nipple discharge, and BIRADS‐MRI category 4 were statistically correlated with an increased detection rate of the lesions. Moreover, lesions exhibiting a heterogeneous, clumped, and clustered ring internal enhancement pattern demonstrated a superior detection rate compared to homogeneous lesions. In previous studies, lesions with rim and clumped enhancement patterns were reported to be more detectable on SLUS [20].

Our study revealed that focal NME‐MRIs were mostly not visible on SLUS. In contrast, we found a more frequent detection of lesions on SLUS for segmental and linear NME‐MRIs. While these findings weren't statistically significant possibly due to the small sample size for each NME distribution type. These initial findings necessitate further investigation into the type of NME morphologies that might have more detectability on SLUS.

In this study, there was no significant relationship between benign and malignant pathology of the lesions and their detection rate on sonography. Also, four out of 11 malignant lesions were not detected on SLUS, which were identified on mammography and underwent stereotactic biopsy. These lesions included two DCIS cases (presenting as isolated pleomorphic microcalcifications) and two IDCs (small spiculated masses). This corresponds with established modality constraints: DCIS frequently exhibits a deficiency in sonographic correlates, although minor IDCs may be concealed by dense parenchyma or isoechogenicity [21]. Some previous investigations have shown that mammography outperforms ultrasonography, especially in the identification of microcalcifications [15, 22]; However, it was not consistent with the results of other studies that reported more sensitivity of SLUS in detecting malignant breast lesions [1, 11].

The negative predictive value of SLUS in the current study was about 87%, which is similar to the value reported in the meta‐analysis conducted by Spick and Baltzer in 2014 [23]. This significant number of false negatives reminds us of the need to pay more attention to the MRI characteristics of lesions.

Among the variables analyzed, the BIRADS‐MRI category was the only significant predictor of a lesion's final pathology. Specifically, all 11 malignant lesions identified were classified as BIRADS‐MRI 4, and notably, none of the BIRADS‐MRI 3 lesions exhibited malignant pathology. This strong association indicate the role of BIRADS‐MRI in detection of malignant breast lesions within our cohort, a finding that consistently aligns with findings reported extensively in the literature [24, 25].

The findings of this study did not show a significant relationship between age, history of breast cancer, MRI indication, NME distribution, internal enhancement pattern, sonographic finding type (mass and non‐mass), and BIRADS category of the lesions on sonography with the final pathology result.

In this study, the lowest malignancy rate was seen in the lesions with focal distribution (12%) and homogeneous internal enhancement pattern (0%). The highest frequency of malignancy was also observed in linear distribution (28%) and clumped internal enhancement pattern (43%). Although in previous studies, NME characteristics, including distribution and internal enhancement pattern were associated with a higher probability of malignancy [5, 6], in the present study, these results were not statistically confirmed.

All 23 lesions detected on SLUS were recommended for biopsy, and sonography did not reduce the need for biopsy in this group of lesions. Also, all BIRADS‐MRI 4 lesions detected on sonography remained BIRADS 4, and the SLUS could not downgrade BIRADS category of these lesions. These findings were in contrast to the results of some previous studies, such as the study of Kolta and Colleagues. In the mentioned study, 23 BIRADS‐MRI 4 lesions after SLUS were downgraded, and the number of unnecessary biopsies was reduced by 30%–60% [26].

This study shows that SLUS cannot rule out the possibility of malignancy in suspicious lesions seen on MRI, considering that 36% of cases of malignancy were not detected on SLUS. In such cases, it is more reasonable to use MRI findings as the basis for deciding on a biopsy, which has been mentioned in some studies before [27, 28]. In other words, for a lesion with a risk of malignancy of more than 2%, the absence of a suspicious finding on SLUS does not eliminate the need for biopsy, and in such cases, biopsy under MRI guidance is recommended [28].

Our study had several limitations: (1) The relatively small number of patients reduces statistical power for detection of small effect size difference between groups and may impact the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, our sample size was adequate for evaluating clinically meaningful differences, which was the primary objective of our investigation. (2) Our study utilized p‐values alongside descriptive percentages to evaluate the statistical significance of associations between variables; nevertheless, we acknowledge that p‐values alone do not indicate effect size. The very small sample size constrained our capacity to present more robust precision metrics (e.g., narrow confidence intervals) or conduct multivariable analyses accounting for potential confounders. Future studies with bigger cohorts should emphasize effect size estimation and predictive values to enhance the characterization of diagnostic performance.

Conclusion

Overall, in this study, we demonstrated that non‐mass‐enhancing lesions with BIRADS‐MRI 4 were more detectable on SLUS, while SLUS had no added findings in BIRADS‐MRI 3 lesions. Also, considering the significant rate of false negative results on sonography, the absence of an SLUS correlate cannot eliminate the need for biopsy in cases of NME with significant suspicion of malignancy.
