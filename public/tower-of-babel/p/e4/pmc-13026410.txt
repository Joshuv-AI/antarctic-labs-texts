Addressing the gaps in heart failure treatment for frail older adults: challenges, evidence, and future directions
Authors: Marius Myrstad, Elisabeth Skaar, Peder Langeland Myhre
Published: 2026

This viewpoint examines gaps in evidence and practical challenges in treating heart failure (HF) among people living with frailty. Our aim is not to argue that guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) should be universally applied or withheld. Rather, we (i) highlight limitations in current randomized trial evidence for frail and multimorbid patients, (ii) offer person-centred approaches for decision-making when trial data are incomplete, and (iii) propose realistic research and implementation priorities to improve safety and outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Frailty is highly prevalent in older patients with HF, yet individuals living with frailty—especially those living with severe frailty, cognitive impairment, or substantial multimorbidity—are underrepresented in HF trials. While recent pharmacological advances [e.g. neprilysin inhibition and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibition) have reduced HF-related events and improved symptoms, the balance between benefits and harms remains uncertain when extrapolated to patients living with severe frailty. Frailty magnifies vulnerability to adverse effects, polypharmacy harms, and functional decline, and conversely, many patients with frailty derive large symptom and function benefits from appropriate HF therapy. Moreover, treatment goals for frail patients may place relatively greater emphasis on symptom control and quality of life than on mortality benefits.

Frailty assessment tools in HF: strengths and limits

Global demographic shifts have increased the population aged 80+ dramatically with a tripling from 1990, contributing to changing HF epidemiology, in which HF with preserved ejection fraction is increasingly common. Although often associated with age-related deficits, studies indicate that frailty is not uncommon even among younger HF patients, and frailty is a stronger predictor of outcomes than age itself.1 Frailty is a state of increased risk, due to reduced physiological reserve and diminished resistance to stressors, with reduced physical or cognitive capacity, dependency in activities of daily living, unintentional weight loss, and the presence of comorbidities being central components of frailty. Frailty assessment methods differ in intent and utility. Electronic frailty indices (e.g. the UK electronic frailty index) and Rockwood cumulative deficit frailty indices (FI) are well suited for population risk stratification,2 while the Clinical Frailty Scale and bedside performance measures are more practical for screening and clinical decision-making. The Fried phenotype remains a commonly used research standard for syndromic frailty, and comprehensive geriatric assessment is the clinical gold standard for individualized evaluation.3 ESC recommends an HF-specific frailty tool encompassing four domains: clinical, psycho-cognitive, functional, and social.4 A properly constructed FI has shown consistency in detecting frailty even when the specific deficits vary, provided it covers a range of physiological systems.5 However, several HF studies have derived FIs that include multiple HF symptom measures (e.g. the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire), which risks producing a disease-specific severity index rather than a true frailty index. Because FIs are assembled from selected deficits, they can vary by dataset and developer. Validation studies often use cut-points such as >0.3 for moderate frailty and >0.4 for severe frailty,2 but reporting and thresholds are inconsistent in HF trials, where a FI ≥0.31 is often classified as ‘most frail’.6

Most frailty analyses in HF trials are post-hoc, rendering their findings exploratory. The DELIVER trial, is an exception, as treatment effect by frailty status was a prespecified analysis.6 The main finding was that the SGLT2 inhibitor dapagliflozin reduced HF events or cardiovascular death compared with placebo across frailty strata and yielded the largest health-related quality-of-life gains in the most frail subgroup. Post-hoc analyses from other pivotal HF trials (including EMPEROR-Preserved, DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced and PARADIGM-HF) generally report consistent treatment effects across the range of FI and age (Figure 1). PARAGON-HF reported a significant interaction by FI on the primary outcome (P = .002), suggesting a greater benefit from neprilysin inhibition in the most frail.7 These signals are encouraging but must be tempered by the limited representation of people with severe frailty or marked cognitive impairment in these datasets. Gaps in trial populations: cognition, kidney disease, hypotension, and sarcopenia

Cognitive impairment and dementia are rarely reported; when cognitive testing has been done [e.g. in PARAGON-HF where Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was included], the ‘most frail’ patients still had a mean MMSE score of 27, indicating well preserved cognitive function.7,8 This contrasts with the high prevalence of cognitive impairment and dementia among older people living with frailty. Furthermore, common trial exclusions, such as reduced kidney function, and short life expectancy systematically exclude many real-world frail patients. Symptomatic hypotension is an important adverse effect and a frequent reason for the non-prescription of pharmacological HF treatment, yet systolic hypotension is commonly an exclusion criterion in HF trials. While weight loss and sarcopenia are key indicators of frailty, the relatively high average body mass index (∼32 kg/m2 in the most frail patients in DELIVER6) should be considered when interpreting the results. HF patients living with frailty are more vulnerable to polypharmacy and rapid up-titration of pharmacological treatment. Trial participants typically receive closer follow-up and more intensive monitoring than many patients receive in routine care, which may reduce adverse events and enable rapid up-titration that is less feasible in standard practice. In the STRONG-HF trial, which informed the Class 1 recommendation on rapid GDMT up-titration, only 16% of the included patients were aged 75 years or older, and data on frailty has not been reported.9 The intensive treatment group received close follow-up, with four visits within the first 6 weeks after discharge. By contrast, the less frequent follow-up of real-world HF patients may increase the risk of adverse events and reduce the net benefit of aggressive titration in frail patients—a reality that helps explain why frailty often impedes initiation and titration of HF treatment.10

SGLT2 inhibitors, now recommended for HF across the spectrum of ejection fraction, have favourable risk-benefit profiles in trials that included older adults, but some adverse effects (euglycaemic ketoacidosis, genitourinary fungal infections, volume depletion) can be difficult to recognize in frail or cognitively impaired patients. This aspect may shift the net benefit for some patients. The recent FRAIL-AF trial, which enrolled patients with atrial fibrillation aged ≥75 years living with frailty, provides a useful illustration.11 This study demonstrated an increased bleeding risk without reduction in thromboembolic complications in patients who switched from vitamin K antagonist to non-vitamin K oral anticoagulant (NOAC). These findings contrast with the results of patients with older age and frailty in the large pivotal NOAC trials, suggesting that consequences of the diversities between real-life HF patients living with frailty and trial patients may be challenging to overview.

Role of comprehensive geriatric assessment and multidisciplinary care

Current guidelines recommend assessing frailty in HF patients to identify those at increased risk of adverse outcomes, reduced access to or tolerance of therapies, and impaired self-care.4,12 Performing a multidomain geriatric assessment (medical, cognitive, functional, and social domains) when frailty is suspected can help tailor management and treatment decisions. Frailty is dynamic: many patients decline over time, but status can also improve (e.g. after transcatheter aortic valve implantation).13 Evidence that nutritional support and exercise improve frailty in HF remains limited; the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines explicitly identify the efficacy and safety of nutritional supplementation in HF patients with frailty or malnutrition as a gap in knowledge.12 NT-proBNP concentrations rise with age, renal impairment and atrial fibrillation, so thresholds used for diagnosis or risk stratification may have reduced specificity in multimorbid, frail older people—results must be cautiously interpreted in clinical context, consider serial trends rather than single cut-points, and integrate with geriatric assessment. Current HF guidelines also recommend early integration of palliative care, advance care planning, and treatment aligned with patients’ goals across all stages of HF.14 The British Geriatrics Society advocates pragmatic prescribing to minimize harm in older people with moderate to severe frailty (frailty assessed by the Clinical Frailty Scale).15 Clinicians should not discontinue HF therapies on the basis of frailty alone; in well-compensated patients without unacceptable adverse effects, continuing guideline-directed medical therapy often remains appropriate and may provide symptom and prognostic benefit.

Recommendations for clinical decision-making when evidence is limited

Frailty exists along a continuum from mild to severe, with the balance of treatment risks and benefits shifting across that spectrum. A person with advanced cancer, severe dementia and cachexia may appropriately receive a palliative approach rather than aggressive GDMT, whereas a patient whose frailty largely reflects potentially reversible HF-related cachexia may gain substantial benefit from optimized therapy. When high-quality trial evidence is lacking for a given frailty phenotype, clinicians should:

use the best available evidence (trial subgroup data, rigorous observational, and registry analyses) and acknowledge its limitations;apply comprehensive geriatric assessment to identify reversible contributors to frailty (nutrition, mobility, medication-induced problems);elicit goals of care and patient values and involve caregivers using shared-decision aids where available;initiate therapy stepwise with closer monitoring (early follow-up, lower starting doses, slower titration, and predefined thresholds for deprescribing); andinvolve multidisciplinary teams (geriatricians, HF nurses, pharmacists, and rehabilitation) early.

Research priorities: trial design, frailty measurement, outcomes, and safety monitoring

While clinicians should expect the unexpected when extrapolating trial results to older, frail patients, the FRAIL-AF demonstrates that enrolling such patients into clinical trials is feasible. Observational studies of truly frail populations, like AGING-HF, suggest that HF therapies can be beneficial and safe in this group; however, they remain vulnerable to confounding by indication.16 We advocate for the following future research priorities: include individuals with moderate to severe frailty in randomized trials with outcomes that matter to frail people (function, quality of life, symptom burden, safety); use standardized frailty assessment—if constructing a frailty index, ensure it samples across multiple physiological systems; prespecify frailty analyses and report cognition, comorbidity, polypharmacy and monitoring intensity; embed enhanced safety monitoring (early visits, telemonitoring, geriatric input) and adaptive stopping rules in trial designs; and conduct implementation studies of safe up-titration, deprescribing and shared-decision tools to support translation into routine care.

Ideally, clinical decisions are guided by randomized trial data, but it is essential for clinicians to acknowledge evidence gaps for patients with severe frailty, and individualize therapy, considering frailty phenotype, comorbidities, cognition, life expectancy, and patient-centred goals.
