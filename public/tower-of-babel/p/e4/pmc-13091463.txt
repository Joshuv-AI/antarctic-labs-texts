Stress-driven design of intervertebral disc implants and optimization of mechanical and biological performance
Authors: Meng Zhang, Shuyun Pang, Yanqin Wang, Yanru Xue, Xiaogang Wu, Weiyi Chen, Yi-Xian Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe degeneration of the intervertebral disc (IVD) is a leading cause of low back pain, causing severe pain and spinal motion disorder. While spinal fusion remains the gold-standard treatment of IVD degeneration, it restricts spinal motion and accelerates adjacent segment degeneration. Artificial total disc replacement has emerged as an alternative to preserve mobility, but conventional implants often fail to replicate natural disc mechanics, resulting in high reoperation rates. This study aimed to propose a stress-driven design approach for graded IVD implants that have mechanical properties similar to those of natural discs, with good shock absorption and biocompatibility.MethodsA detailed finite element model of the L1-L2 functional spinal unit (FSU) was developed. Applying axial force of 500 N or 7.5 N⋅m moment, stress distributions of IVD under seven physiological motions (upright standing, flexion, extension, right/left lateral bending, and right/left axial rotation) were obtained. IVD implants were designed using gyroid unit cells (5 × 5 × 5 mm3), with three porosity levels, i.e., 55%, 65%, and 75%. The wall thicknesses were determined based on the weighted stress distribution to design three graded IVD implants (G55N, G65N, and G75N), and three uniform implants (G55Y, G65Y, and G75Y) were developed for comparison. All implants were fabricated using selective laser sintering. Mechanical testing, finite element analysis, in vitro experiments and computational fluid dynamics simulations were conducted to evaluate the mechanical and biological performance of the designed implants and to identify the optimal IVD design.ResultsThe G65N and G65Y exhibited mechanical performance closest to those of natural IVDs, with G65N showing reduced sensitivity to loading rate, lower irreversible energy dissipation, and enhanced cyclic stability. The L1-L2 FSU implanted with G65N demonstrated stress ranges of 0.00009-26.600 MPa under compression and 0.00067-54.090 MPa under flexion, and it also showed lower endplate contact pressure (2.59 MPa) and axial displacement (1.86 mm), indicating a reduced risk of implant subsidence. In vitro, cells proliferated steadily on both the G65N and G65Y, aligning along the implant struts, with slightly higher proliferation observed on G65N. Furthermore, both implants exhibited high permeability (4.43 × 10−7 m2 for G65N and 4.33 × 10−7 m2 for G65Y) and uniform wall shear stress, supporting nutrient transport and cell growth.ConclusionThe stress-driven design of G65N reproduced the biomechanical behavior of natural IVDs and improved biocompatibility, offering a promising design strategy to minimize implant failure and promote tissue regeneration.The translational potential of this articleThis study presents a stress-driven and patient-specific framework for graded IVD implant design. The optimal implants replicate the biomechanics of natural discs, improve load sharing, and support segmental stability, while demonstrating favorable biological performance. This design approach has significant potential to promote postoperative recovery and reduce the risk of reoperation, thereby supporting precision and patient-specific care in orthopedics. Beyond IVDs, the proposed stress-driven and biomimetic design strategy could be applied to other mechanically adaptive implants, such as femoral stems, knee and hip prostheses, shoulder prostheses, and dental implants, offering a versatile tool for the development of personalized orthopedic devices for various clinical applications.

Introduction

Low back pain has become a significant public health issue because it is a leading cause of disability worldwide, affecting about 40% of the global population [1]. The degeneration of the intervertebral disc (IVD) is thought to be the primary contribution to low back pain [2], causing severe pain and spinal motion disorder. The prevalence of IVD degeneration in people over 50 years old has been reported to be more than 80% [3]. To alleviate low back pain due to degenerative IVD, spinal fusion is considered the gold-standard treatment. However, this surgery severely restricts the range of motion (ROM) of the functional spinal unit (FSU) and may accelerate degeneration at adjacent levels [4]. Artificial total disc replacement (A-TDR) is another alternative surgical intervention to treat IVD degeneration, and this procedure involves the excision of the degenerated disc and the implantation of an artificial disc. Compared to spinal fusion, A-TDR not only preserves intervertebral height and the segmental ROM but also slows the adjacent segment degeneration [1]. Therefore, A-TDR has gained increasing attention and been widely used in clinics recently [5].

Conventional A-TDR devices are generally “joint” structures, such as ball and socket joints. It was shown that the geometry of these artificial discs significantly affects the ROM and facet contact force [6]. The above non-individual IVD replacement products cannot perfectly match the shape of the host bone, resulting in an increased risk of revision following A-TDR. The reoperation rate has been reported to be as high as 19.5% at 10 years [7]. Inspired by the structure and material of natural lumbar IVD, previous researchers proposed a biomimetic disc by adjusting the hardness, diameter, and arrangement of fibers, achieving 90% similarity to the natural IVD [8]. In an earlier study, an artificial IVD implant with modified auxetic structures was designed and fabricated using three-dimensional (3D) printing technology. This novel prosthesis expanded laterally under compression and exhibited excellent energy absorption and stability, which could effectively treat lumbar disc herniation [9]. However, current attempts have not been able to achieve mechanical performance comparable to that of natural IVDs while simultaneously demonstrating adequate biocompatibility and shock stress absorption, which may restrict spinal physiological motion/functions and exacerbate facet joints degeneration [10].

In vitro and finite element (FE) analysis studies have shown that stresses are not uniformly distributed in the IVD. Under all loading modes, the central region of IVD (nucleus pulposus) had low stress levels [11]. In contrast, stresses in the anterior and posterior regions exhibited high levels under compression, flexion, and extension motions [9]. Besides, the main characteristics of IVD degeneration are abnormal mechanical behaviors of stress and strain [11]. Therefore, it is necessary to replicate the stress distribution of natural discs in the design of prostheses to minimize the risk of failure. However, research on stress-driven autonomous design methods for IVD implants remains limited.

With the rapid development of computer-aided design and additive manufacturing, an increasing number of porous structures are being utilized in the design of prostheses. Specifically, the triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) has garnered increasing attention, owing to its zero mean curvature and precise expressiveness through mathematical functions [12]. As a commonly used TPMS structure, gyroid scaffolds have been utilized in various applications, including tissue scaffolds, total hip replacements, and vascular stents, among others, offering high permeability, superior bioactivity, and excellent energy absorption performance [13].

Therefore, this study aimed to design specific IVD implants with gyroid structures based on the stress distributions of natural IVDs under practical loading conditions, which could achieve biomimetic mechanical behavior while maintaining good biocompatibility. In this study, the mechanical and biological properties of stress-driven designed IVD implants were assessed, and the optimal IVD implant was determined. The stress-driven design framework proposed in our study may inspire the development of new generations of clinical prostheses.

Materials and methods

FE modeling of the L1-L2 FSU

In this study, the L1-L2 segment was selected as the representative lumbar segment due to its reduced sensitivity to the anatomical and loading characteristics specific to the lumbosacral junction. A detailed 3D FE model of the L1-L2 FSU was developed using computer tomography (CT) data from a healthy 66-year-old male subject weighing 69.70 kg and measuring 169.20 cm in height (Fig. 1(a)). The subject was obtained from the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men (MrOS) cohort in Hong Kong part. All participants in the MrOS study provided written informed consent [14]. Digital CT data with 1.25 mm slice thickness were imported into Mimics 10.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). The vertebral geometric model was extracted using thresholding, region growing, and editing masks. Moreover, the facet joints were constructed by a Boolean operation. Meshing and creating the IVD and ligaments were performed using Hypermesh 13.0 (Altair Engineering, MI, USA). The endplate, nucleus pulposus, and annulus fibrosus together formed the IVD (Fig. 1(a)). Annulus fibrosus was divided into eight annular layers accounting for 56% of the total IVD volume [15], which were reinforced with collagen fibers oriented at ±24° to ±46° with crosswise arrangements [11]. Seven major ligaments were defined as truss elements, including the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL), the posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL), the ligamentum flavum (LF), the interspinous ligament (ISL), the supraspinous ligament (SSL), the intertransverse ligament (ITL), and the capsular ligament (CL).Fig. 1FE model of L1-L2 FSU. FE modeling of IVD implants based on stress distributions

The material of IVD implants was assigned as thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU). This high-performance elastomer exhibits outstanding biocompatibility and tunable mechanical properties, rendering it a widely used alternative material for tissue implants [9]. A third-order Ogden hyperelastic constitutive model, derived from a previous study [9], was employed to characterize the mechanical behavior of TPU in our simulations, demonstrating the best fit in terms of accuracy and stability when compared to experimental data. The IVD implants were designed using gyroid cellular structures, which provide optimal biocompatibility and energy dissipation capacity, while minimizing stress shielding and promoting bone tissue regeneration.

The gyroid unit cell was developed using nTopology 5.0.4 (nTopology Inc., NY, USA) with dimensions of 5 × 5 × 5 mm3. The cell-wall thickness was graded according to the weighted stress profile of the natural IVD under seven motions (standing, flexion, extension, right/left lateral bending, and right/left axial rotation). The stress-driven design method involved: (1) normalizing the stress distribution of natural IVD under each loading condition; (2) multiplying by specific weightage factors and adding together to obtain the weighted stress distribution; (3) implementing thickness-stress correspondence (increased wall thickness in high-stress regions and decreased wall thickness in low-stress regions) to achieve biomechanically optimized non-uniform topology. Given that standing and flexion are the principal postures associated with the disc failure and account for the predominant loading patterns during daily activities [9], the weightage factors for compression, flexion, extension, right lateral bending, left lateral bending, right axial rotation, and left axial rotation were 0.55, 0.20, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05, and 0.05, respectively. The porosities of IVD implants were set 55%, 65%, and 75%, respectively. Graded IVD implants (G55N, G65N, and G75N) with three porosity levels (55%, 65%, and 75%) were developed. Corresponding uniform IVD implants (G55Y, G65Y, and G75Y) with matched porosities were constructed for comparative evaluation (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Structural design of graded and uniform IVD implants. G55N, G65N, and G75N represent graded IVD implants with porosities of 55%, 65%, and 75%, respectively, and G55Y, G65Y, and G75Y represent uniform IVD implants with corresponding porosities.Fig. 2

Fabrication and morphological characterization of IVD implants

Six types of IVD implants (i.e., G55N, G55Y, G65N, G65Y, G75N, and G75Y) were fabricated using selective laser sintering (SLS; P360, TPM 3D, China) with TPU powder. The SLS process parameters were set as follows: laser power of 60 W, laser scanning speed of 8600 mm/s, layer thickness of 0.12 mm, and a powder bed temperature of 108.5 °C. Prior to morphological characterization, the specimens were gold-sputtered under vacuum. The surface morphologies of the fabricated implants were then examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM-7100F, JEOL, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV.

Uniaxial compression tests

Uniaxial compression tests were performed using a universal testing machine (Instron 3343, Instron, USA). To characterize the rate-dependent mechanical behavior, compression tests were conducted at loading rates of 1 mm/min, 10 mm/min, and 100 mm/min, respectively. These rates were selected to capture the rate-dependent mechanical response of the IVD under physiologically relevant compression conditions [9]. The cyclic compressive behavior of the IVD implants was further investigated by applying cyclic loading between 0 and 500 N at a loading rate of 100 mm/min for five consecutive cycles [9,21]. For each experimental condition, three specimens were tested for each implant design.

Stress distributions of IVD implants following A-TDR

Six IVD implants were implanted into the FE model of L1-L2 FSU, replacing the intact disc structure to establish the FE model following A-TDR. A concentrated axial force of 500 N and a moment of 7.5 N⋅m were applied to the implanted FSU model to simulate compression and flexion motions, respectively. The von Mises stress distributions of IVD implants were obtained and compared with those of the intact IVD under corresponding loading conditions.

Following a comparison of stress distributions among the six IVD implants, the structures exhibiting mechanical behavior most similar to that of the natural IVD were identified as superior implants. Subsequently, the von Mises stress distributions of the FSU implanted with these superior IVD implants were compared with those of the intact FSU under compression and flexion loading conditions to determine the optimal IVD implant.

Biomechanical performance of FSU implanted with IVD implants

To comprehensively evaluate the functional and mechanical performance of the FSU implanted with the optimal IVD design, a combined loading condition consisting of a 7.5 N⋅m flexion moment and 1175 N axial compression was applied. This loading condition represents trunk flexion with the arms held forward while carrying weights, which has been identified as a high-risk scenario associated with vertebral fracture [22]. Under this compression-flexion condition, four biomechanical outcome measures were evaluated, including segmental ROM, facet joint contact force, endplate contact pressure, and implant-endplate axial displacement. All results were compared with the corresponding values of the intact FSU.

In vitro cell experiments

Cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 15 mm were extracted from the posterolateral region of the 3D-printed optimal IVD implants for in vitro evaluation, as this region exhibited a pronounced gradient in gyroid wall thickness. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Every Green, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, China) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. A total of 5 × 105 cells were seeded onto the surface of each sterilized specimen. Cell seeding efficiency and proliferation were assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China) assay. After 1, 3, and 5 days of culture, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Varioskan Lux, Thermo Scientific, USA) following 2 h of incubation. Cell proliferation was expressed as the mean optical density (OD) values obtained from three independent specimens.

In addition, BMSCs were then seeded on the sterilized specimens in well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/well. After 5 days of culture, cell viability was assessed using a calcein/PI cell viability/cytotoxicity assay kit (Beyotime, China). Live cells (green) and dead cells (red) were visualized using a Stellaris 5 laser confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Computational fluid dynamics simulations

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis was performed on the optimal IVD implants to evaluate the mass-transport performance, including permeability, flow velocity, and wall shear stress (WSS). The inlet velocity of the fluid was set to 1 mm/s, and the pressure at the outlet was zero. A no-slip condition was used on the walls. The fluid was considered incompressible, and the Navier–Stokes equation was used as Eq. Morphological characterization of 3D-printed IVD implants

IVD implants with well-defined geometries were successfully fabricated using the SLS process. SEM observations confirmed that the overall morphology of the fabricated implants was in good agreement with the designed models (Fig. 4). For graded structures (e.g., G55N), the peripheral regions exhibited thicker struts and smaller pore sizes, whereas the central region was characterized by thinner struts and larger pores. In contrast, uniform structures (e.g., G55Y) showed consistent wall thickness and pore size throughout the entire implant. Mechanical properties of 3D-printed IVD implants

As shown in Fig. 5(a), force-displacement curves of six types of IVD implants exhibited pronounced nonlinear mechanical behavior. At a compressive force of 500 N, the graded structures demonstrated larger displacements than the corresponding uniform structures for implants with porosities of 55% and 65%, indicating a superior energy absorption capability of the graded designs. In contrast, an opposite trend was observed for implants with a porosity of 75%, where the uniform structure exhibited greater displacement. With increasing porosity, the maximum displacement of the IVD implants increased progressively, suggesting enhanced energy absorption capacity. Based on cadaveric experimental data reported in previous studies, the mechanical responses of the 55% and 65% porosity implants fell within the reported physiological range of natural IVDs [8,32]. Considering that implants with a porosity of 65% provided improved energy absorption performance compared with those with 55% porosity, subsequent analyses focused on the mechanical behavior of the 65% porosity designs.Fig. 5Mechanical properties of 3D-printed IVD implants. Comparisons of the force-displacement curves of G65N and G65Y under different loading rates revealed that G65N exhibited lower sensitivity to loading rate (Fig. 5(b)). When compressed to 500 N at loading rates ranging from 1 mm/min to 100 mm/min, the maximum displacement of G65N varied within a narrow range of 1.18-1.22 mm, indicating more stable mechanical behavior compared with G65Y. Furthermore, cyclic loading-unloading tests were performed on G65N and G65Y, and force-displacement hysteresis loops were obtained (Fig. 5(c)). The energy dissipation per cycle was quantified as the enclosed area of the force-displacement loop. The first loading-unloading cycle was excluded from the hysteresis analysis because the loop was not fully closed due to initial structural conditioning and irreversible deformation (Fig. 5(d)). With increasing cycle number, the energy dissipation of G65Y gradually increased, reaching 11.75 ± 0.97 N⋅mm at the fifth cycle. In contrast, the energy dissipation of G65N decreased with successive cycles, with a value of 2.15 ± 1.13 N⋅mm at the fifth cycle. These results indicated that G65N exhibited reduced irreversible energy dissipation and progressive mechanical stabilization under cyclic loading, reflecting improved recoverability, cyclic durability, and a reduced tendency toward structural degradation.

It should be noted that the mechanical evaluation was limited to the first five loading cycles and did not fully capture the long-term physiological fatigue behavior of the IVD implants. However, the adopted protocol followed established methods in the literature [9,21], providing a reasonable preliminary assessment of the implants' cyclic behavior and mechanical stability. Further comprehensive fatigue testing will be conducted in future studies to evaluate their long-term mechanical durability and functional performance of the implants.

Among all IVD implants with three porosity levels (55%, 65%, and 75%), the 65% porosity implants (G65N and G65Y) demonstrated stress ranges that closely matched those of natural IVD under compression and flexion conditions. Under compression loading, the stress range of natural discs was 0.0018 to 7.992 MPa, while the G65N and G65Y showed comparable ranges of 0.0133 to 9.248 MPa and 0.0015 to 9.759 MPa, respectively (Fig. 6(a)). Under flexion loading, the stress ranges of G65N and G65Y were 0.0095 to 10.490 MPa and 0.0003 to 11.110 MPa, respectively, which were well approximated by the natural disc stress range of 0.0034 to 13.290 MPa (Fig. 6(b)).

The stress distributions of L1-L2 FSU following A-TDR

Given that the G65N and G65Y were superior among six designed implants in maintaining stress distribution patterns similar to natural disc, then the stress distributions of L1-L2 FSU with G65N and G65Y were assessed. Under compression loading, the stress range of the intact L1-L2 FSU was 0.00004 to 19.320 MPa, while that of the L1-L2 FSU implanted with G65N and G65Y were 0.00009 to 26.600 MPa and 0.00009 to 30.520 MPa, respectively (Fig. 6(c)). The intact L1-L2 FSU exhibited stress ranges of 0.00084 to 46.900 MPa during flexion, while the stress range of the L1-L2 FSU implanted with G65N and G65Y were 0.00067 to 54.090 MPa and 0.00035 to 56.220 MPa, respectively (Fig. 6(d)). These results showed that G65N-implanted L1-L2 FSU demonstrated corresponding stress ranges close to the intact model.

In addition to stress distribution, the maximum von Mises stress of the implanted G65N model was located at the interface between the L2 vertebral body and the lower right region of posterior elements under both compression and flexion motions, which agreed with the maximum stress location of intact L1-L2 FSU. By contrast, the maximum von Mises stress of L1-L2 FSU implanted with G65Y was located at the left transverse process of L1.

Functional and mechanical performance of L1-L2 FSU following A-TDR

The functional and mechanical performance of the FSUs implanted with G65N and G65Y were evaluated under the compression-flexion loading condition. The segmental ROM of the FSU implanted with the G65N was 3.12°, while that of the G65Y-implanted FSU was 3.21°, both of which were lower than the ROM of the intact L1-L2 segment (5.10°). Compared with the intact FSU, which exhibited a facet joint contact force of 46.64 N, implantation of the proposed IVD implants resulted in a marked reduction in facet joint loading, with values of 34.28 N for the G65N model and 30.01 N for the G65Y model. The risk of implant subsidence was further assessed using the maximum endplate contact pressure and the maximum implant-endplate axial displacement. Implantation of the artificial IVD increased the maximum endplate contact pressure compared with the natural disc (G65N: 2.59 MPa; G65Y: 2.94 MPa vs. natural IVD: 2.33 MPa); however, the pressure distribution became more uniform, as shown in Fig. 7(a). In addition, implantation of the IVD resulted in a slight increase in the maximum axial displacement of the endplates (G65N: 1.86 mm; G65Y: 1.88 mm vs. natural IVD: 1.47 mm) (Fig. 7(b)). Compared with G65Y, the G65N-implanted FSU exhibited lower endplate contact pressure and reduced maximum axial displacement, indicating a lower risk of implant subsidence.Fig. 7Endplate contact pressure and axial displacement distributions under the compression-flexion loading. The mass-transport performance of IVD implants

The permeability, flow velocity, and WSS of G65N and G65Y were evaluated. The permeability values of G65N and G65Y were 4.43 × 10−7 m2 and 4.33 × 10−7 m2, respectively, both of which fall within a relatively high range and are favorable for nutrient transport and cell proliferation. As shown in Fig. 8(c), G65N exhibited a more pronounced velocity gradient than G65Y, characterized by higher flow velocities in the central region and lower velocities in the peripheral regions. This flow pattern can be attributed to the graded thickness of the gyroid unit cells in G65N, with thinner struts in the central zones and thicker struts toward the periphery. Moreover, the WSS distributions of both designs were relatively uniform (Fig. 8(d)). The average WSS values were 2.54 mPa for G65N and 2.72 mPa for G65Y, with WSS predominantly distributed in the range of 1-4 mPa, accounting for 67.83% of the surface area in G65N and 65.74% in G65Y.

Discussion

In this study, a FE model of the L1-L2 FSU was developed to obtain the weighted stress distribution of natural IVD under seven motions (standing, flexion, extension, right/left lateral bending, and right/left axial rotation). Three graded IVD implants (G55N, G65N, and G75N) with different porosity levels (55%, 65%, and 75%) were designed through stress-driven gradient gyroid unit cells. Moreover, three uniform IVD implants (G55Y, G65Y, and G75Y) with matched porosities were constructed. Mechanical testing, FE analysis, in vitro experiments, and CFD simulations were conducted to evaluate the mechanical and biological performance of the designed implants. The G65N and G65Y closely replicated the biomechanics of natural IVDs, with G65N showing reduced sensitivity to loading rate, lower irreversible energy dissipation, and enhanced cyclic stability. The L1-L2 FSU implanted with G65N also showed uniform stress distribution, as well as lower endplate contact pressure and axial displacement, indicating a reduced risk of implant subsidence. In vitro, cells proliferated steadily on G65N and G65Y, with slightly higher proliferation observed on G65N. Additionally, both implants demonstrated high permeability and uniform WSS, supporting nutrient transport and cell growth.

FE analyses demonstrated that under compression and flexion loading conditions, the graded IVD implants exhibited lower stresses and more uniform stress distributions compared to uniform IVD implants, regardless of porosity (Fig. 6). This finding is consistent with a previous study [33]. These results revealed that graded structures could effectively avoid stress shielding and stress concentration effects. In addition, the advantages of this gradient design were reflected in three aspects: (1) The non-uniform distribution of cell wall thickness leads to heterogeneous porosity distribution. For G65N in this study, the porosities of central region (nucleus pulposus) and peripheral region (annulus fibrosus) were 72.87% and 62.30%, respectively. Notably, this distribution pattern of porosity closely resembles that of natural IVD [34], contributing to long-term stability and adaptability. (2) Thicker unit cell walls in the peripheral regions can provide excellent mechanical support, reduce fracture risk, and increase the implant's service life. (3) Thinner unit cell walls in the central regions can improve material utilization efficiency, enlarge available areas for cell adhesion, and promote recovery after implantation.

It was accepted that disc failure is prone to occur under upright standing and flexion motions [9]; therefore, compression and flexion loading conditions were chosen to evaluate the mechanical performance of the designed IVD implants. Among all graded implants (G55N, G65N, and G75N), G65N exhibited stress ranges closest to those of natural discs under these two loading modes (Fig. 6). Specifically, the central region sustained minimal stresses, while higher stresses concentrated in the anterior and posterior regions, which is consistent with previous studies on physiological disc stress distribution patterns [11]. Many cadaveric and animal studies have confirmed that the posterior region of the IVD consistently experiences elevated stresses during various motions, making it more susceptible to damage and subsequent pathological conditions, such as disc herniation and annular delamination. Consequently, daily activities such as prolonged standing and extension postures that exacerbate posterior loading should be avoided for elderly patients [11]. Correspondingly, the posterior region of graded implants in this study was designed with thickened cell walls, which could provide excellent mechanical support to minimize the risk of posterior failure. Under compression-flexion loading, the G65N implant achieved a segmental ROM comparable to that of G65Y, while exhibiting lower endplate contact pressure and reduced axial displacement (Fig. 7). These results indicated a more favorable load distribution at the implant-endplate interface and suggested that the graded porous architecture of G65N may reduce the risk of implant subsidence while maintaining functional mobility.

The biomechanical performance of the proposed G65N implant was compared with reported data of commercial A-TDR devices, including ProDisc-L and SB-Charité, under comparable loading conditions [35]. Under compression-flexion loading, the segmental ROM of the G65N-implanted FSU (3.12°) was substantially higher than that reported for ProDisc-L and SB-Charité, both of which exhibited ROM values below 1.50°, indicating improved preservation of segmental mobility without excessive motion. Meanwhile, the maximum von Mises stress in the G65N-implanted segment (73.13 MPa) was markedly lower than the values reported for ProDisc-L (290.78 MPa) and SB-Charité (115.69 MPa), and was comparable to those reported for second-generation TDR designs (70.24 MPa) [35]. These differences can be primarily attributed to the distinct structural concepts of the implants. Conventional ball-and-socket A-TDR devices rely on rigid articulation, which tends to concentrate stresses and increase posterior load transfer. In contrast, the monolithic graded porous architecture of G65N enables distributed load sharing and intrinsic shock absorption. The graded implant exhibited a moderated ROM together with reduced von Mises stress under compression-flexion loading, suggesting a biomechanical advantage over conventional rigid-articulation disc prostheses.

To evaluate the biocompatibility of the optimal implants, cell proliferation and viability as well as mass-transport performance (permeability, flow velocity, and WSS) were assessed. Cell proliferation on G65N was slightly higher than on G65Y (Fig. 8(a)), which may be attributed to its higher specific surface area (2.70 mm−1 for G65N and 2.57 mm−1 for G65Y). The calculated permeabilities of G65N and G65Y were 4.43 × 10−7 m2 and 4.33 × 10−7 m2, obviously higher than the physiological range of human bone permeability (2.7 × 10−11-2.0 × 10−8 m2) [36], indicating the capability to meet host tissue mass-transfer requirements. The fluid velocity distribution is a critical parameter to reflect mass-transport performance. Due to the thicker cell walls in the peripheral region and thinner cell walls in the central region, the fluid velocity gradient in G65N is more pronounced than in G65Y, with higher velocities in the medial area and lower velocities in the outer area (Fig. 8(c)). Moreover, within the individual flow channel, the velocity of the pore center was higher than that of the walls. This velocity gradient indicated that fluid swirling and twining were generated in G65N, which is beneficial for nutrient transport and waste discharge [37]. WSS is an essential regulator of cell growth and proliferation. Previous studies have suggested that WSS within the range of 0.5 to 25 mPa can promote cell growth, whereas cell growth is prohibited when WSS exceeds 25 mPa [38]. As shown in Fig. 8(d), WSS values were mainly concentrated in the range of 1 to 4 mPa (accounting for 67.83% for G65N and 65.74% for G65Y), which falls within the optimal range for promoting cellular activities. Collectively, G65N exhibited slightly better biological performance than G65Y.

This study had several limitations. First, this study evaluated the proposed implant design using an L1-L2 FSU model, which may not fully capture the distinct anatomical features and loading conditions of the lower lumbar spine. However, the proposed stress-driven design framework is generic and transferable, and can be applied to other lumbar levels. Future work will extend the analysis to clinically prevalent segments such as L4-L5 and L5-S1. Second, a dedicated clinical fixation mechanism for anchoring the implant to the vertebral endplates was not explicitly designed. However, the proposed porous and graded architecture is compatible with established fixation concepts and can be easily integrated with anchoring features in future designs. The current study focuses on evaluating the implant's mechanical and biological properties, with fixation mechanism design and validation to be addressed in future work. Third, a comprehensive parametric sensitivity analysis was not performed in this study. Although standardized modeling assumptions were applied consistently across all designs to ensure internal comparability, the influence of factors such as weighting factors, material properties, and contact definitions was not quantitatively assessed. This represents a limitation of the current study, and future work will focus on quantifying these influences to further validate the robustness of the design framework.

Conclusions

This study proposed an optimized design method to balance the mechanical properties and biocompatibilities of IVD implants through stress-driven TPMS structural modeling. Three graded IVD implants (G55N, G65N, and G75N) were developed by employing gyroid cellular structures based on stress distributions of natural IVD under seven loading conditions. Among these designs, G65N demonstrated superior biomechanical compatibility with host tissues, combining matched mechanical performance with good biological compatibility, which could enhance segmental stability, facilitate functional restoration, and reduce the risk of reoperation. The stress-driven design framework presented in our study can provide technical support for the optimization of IVD implants and accelerate the clinical translation of customized spinal implants. Moreover, this stress-driven and biomimetic design paradigm could be extended to other mechanically adaptive implants, such as femoral stems, knee and hip prostheses, shoulder prostheses, and dental implants, offering a versatile tool for the development of personalized orthopedic devices for various clinical applications.
