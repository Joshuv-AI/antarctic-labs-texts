The effect of kinesiophobia and successful aging on quality of life in older adults: machine learning approach
Authors: Muhammet Ali Aydin, Nilufer Yildirim, Veysel Kizilarslan, Muruvvet Emrem, Şevin Polat, Metin YILDIZ
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKinesiophobia and successful aging are key factors affecting quality of life in older adults; kinesiophobia, the fear of movement, can lead to reduced physical activity, while successful aging promotes overall well-being.AimThis study was conducted to examine the effect of kinesiophobia and successful aging on quality of life in the older adults.MethodThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted with 451 older adults living in Erzurum between July and November 2024. R programming language 4.1.3, G*Power 3.1 and SPSS-22 program were used in the analysis of the study.ResultsIn the study, the level of kinesiophobia explained 8.4% of the total variance of the quality of life level (R2 = 0.084). It was determined that an increase in the level of kinesiophobia (t=-6.406, p < 0.001) caused a statistically significant decrease in the level of “quality of life”. Kinesiophobia level and successful aging levels together explain 52.4% (R2 = 0.524) of the total variance in quality of life level. It was determined that the increase in the level of successful aging of the participants (t=-2.854, p < 0.001) caused a statistical increase in the level of “quality of life”. The SHAP values of the variables in the best-performing model were examined. According to the graph, successful aging is identified as the most important variable in predicting the quality of life variable. It was found that the successful aging variable has an effect that is 4.917 units higher compared to other variables.ConclusionIn this study, it was found that quality of life decreased with increasing kinesiophobia, whereas quality of life increased with increasing level of successful aging. Longitudinal studies on quality of life in the elderly are recommended.

Background

As the global population of older adults continues to grow, concerns related to aging and old age are becoming increasingly significant [1]. According to the World Health Organization, there are 1.4 billion older people in the world and this number is estimated to exceed 2.1 billion in 2050 [2]. It has been determined that the proportion of the older adults population in our country is 10.2% of the total population with 8 million 722 thousand 806 people [3].

Aging is a complex process with biological, psychological and social dimensions [4]. With the advancement of age, various effects in physiological, psychological, cognitive and social areas may occur in individuals and lead to a decrease in the general functional capacity of the individual [5]. Avoidance of physical activity and adoption of a sedentary lifestyle by older adults can lead to many negative health effects [6, 7]. Physical inactivity and kinesiophobia (fear of movement) often co-occur and can be mutually reinforcing. Kinesiophobia can be caused by fear of falling, joint pain, muscle weakness, lack of confidence, bad experiences and psychological reasons [8–10]. According to one of the studies, kinesiophobia decreases physical fitness capacities by decreasing physical activity and may lead to cardiovascular diseases, lung diseases and depression [11–14]. Kinesiophobia is believed to significantly affect patients’ physical and mental health, as well as their quality of life, due to the complex and diverse nature of its risk factors and neurophysiological mechanisms [15]. In many studies examining the relationship between kinesiophobia and quality of life, it has been reported that it negatively affects quality of life. Studies have shown that individuals with kinesiophobia struggle to carry out daily activities due to the fear cycle, often leading to partial or complete dependence and a decline in their quality of life [16–18].According to studies, being physically active positively affects quality of life in old age. Physically active older adults report improved energy and sleep quality and feel emotionally and cognitively well. Physical health is linked to quality of life in older adults and being physically active is important for maintaining good health [19–22].

It is seen as a human right that the older adults can live as active individuals with appropriate conditions and health, and that their quality of life is not reduced [23]. Although healthy and successful aging are considered as similar concepts in the literature, successful aging is increasingly being recognized as a broader and more holistic measure of well-being in older adulthood. The related concepts aim to age in a healthy way by minimizing the negative effects that come with aging, which is a natural part of the aging process [24]. Successful aging is a multidimensional concept that encompasses the maintenance of physical, cognitive, and psychological functioning, active engagement in life, and the ability to adapt positively to age-related changes. Successful aging has emerged as a key concept in defining the quality of the aging process [25]. This concept includes the adoption of healthy living habits such as healthy eating, regular exercise, stress management, and hobbies to avoid diseases in old age. At the same time, physical and mental functioning is considered as the ability to maintain independence and continue daily activities in old age [26–28]. Self-development by learning from life experiences, maintaining social relationships, self-control, optimism, achieving personal meaning and goals in life, and valuing oneself are the foundations of successful aging. In this way, the older adults can remain physically and mentally active and enjoy life [2, 28]. Studies have shown that successful aging positively affects quality of life [29–34]. Moreover, successful aging may act as a protective factor against the negative effects of fear-based conditions such as kinesiophobia by promoting resilience, autonomy, and life satisfaction. This interaction remains understudied in current literature.

In the literature, there is no similar study examining the relationship between kinesiophobia and successful aging and quality of life. Based on this essence, this study, which is planned to examine the relationship between kinesiophobia and successful aging and quality of life in older adults, is thought to contribute to the literature.

Research questions

RQ1: What is the relationship between kinesiophobia and quality of life among older adults?

RQ2: What is the relationship between successful aging and quality of life among older adults?

Methods

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted between July and November 2024. The population of the study consisted of elderly individuals aged 65 and over living in Erzurum province. The convenience sampling method was used in the study. The sample size was determined using the formula for estimating a population proportion with an unknown universe size. Assuming a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a 5% margin of error (d = 0.05), and a population proportion (p) of 0.5 to ensure maximum variability, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 384 individuals. The study was completed with 451 elderly individuals. After the study, a post hoc power analysis was performed using data from 451 participants, indicating a study power of 99% at a medium effect size with a 95% confidence level [35]. The STROBE guideline was used in the reporting of this research paper [36].

Inclusion criteria

The study included elderly people who were 65 years of age or older, who did not have speech and hearing problems, who were conscious, perceptive and responsive, who did not have neurological and psychiatric disorders that prevented the understanding of the questions in the scales, who were not in the terminal period and who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Participants under 65 years of age, who were not willing to participate in the study, and who had any obstacles to evaluation or communication were not included in the study.

Data collection

Data were collected through an online form.

Data collection tools

In this study, the dependent variable was quality of life, which was assessed using the “Quality of Life Scale for the Elderly”. The independent variables were kinesiophobia and successful aging, measured using the “Tampa Kinesiophobia Scale” and the “Successful Aging Scale”, respectively. Higher scores on the Quality of Life Scale and the Successful Aging Scale indicate higher levels of perceived well-being and aging success, while higher scores on the Kinesiophobia Scale indicate increased fear of movement or physical activity. The details and psychometric properties of these scales are described below.

Introductory information form

It was created by the researchers and consisted of questions including descriptive characteristics of the individuals.

Successful aging scale

The Turkish validity and reliability of the Successful Aging Scale (SAS) developed by Reker (2009) was conducted by Hazer and Özsungur in 2017 [37, 38]. It is a short 7-point Likert-type scale (strongly agree = 7, agree = 6, partially agree = 5, undecided = 4, partially disagree = 3, disagree = 2, strongly disagree = 1) that psychometrically measures older adults’ healthy lifestyle related to successful aging and their efforts to overcome a problem. The HRAS is a scale consisting of 10 statements. The Successful Aging Scale consists of two sub-dimensions: Healthy Lifestyle ([5, 6] and [10]) and Dealing with Problems ([1–4, 7, 8] and [9]). The minimum score that can be obtained from the scale is 7 and the maximum score is 70. An increase in the score obtained from the scale indicates an increase in successful aging. In the study conducted by Özsungur and Hazer, the Cronbach alpha internal consistency coefficient of the Turkish adaptation of the scale was 0.85 [37, 38]. In our study, Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.92.

Tampa kinesiophobia scale

The Tampa Kinesiophobia Scale (TKS) was used to determine the kinesiophobia levels of the participants. The TQS was first developed by Miller et al. in 1991 but was not published. It was later republished by Vlaeyen et al. in 1995 with the permission of the researchers [39]. The scale includes parameters of injury/re-injury and fear-avoidance in work-related activities. It consists of a list of 17 questions. The scale has a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Agree, 4 = Strongly agree). A high score on the scale indicates a high level of kinesiophobia. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Yılmaz et al. in 2011. Test-retest reliability was found to be 0.806 (CI = 0.720–0.867) [40]. In our study, Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.88.

Quality of life scale for the elderly

The Elderly People’s Quality of Life Scale was created by Çalışkan et al. by adapting Bowling et al.‘s “Older People’s Quality of Life Short Scale (OPQOLbrief)” into Turkish [41]. The scale has a single-item preliminary question and 13 items. This preliminary question is not scored together with the other items of the scale. The preliminary question is coded from “Very good (five)” to “Very bad (one)”. The five-point Likert-type scale form is coded from “Strongly disagree (five)” to “Strongly agree (one)”. However, the scoring of the score was made from “Strongly disagree (one)” to “Strongly agree (five)” in the original adaptation, in the opposite way to the coding of the scale to express an increase in quality of life. Thus, it was ensured that the scoring of the items representing high quality of life was high and a meaningful relationship with other scales was established. The internal consistency of the data was tested with Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient and the scale showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) [42]. In our study, Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.90.

Ethical considerations

Approval from the Bingöl University Health Sciences Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (Document Date and Number: 18.07.2024-E.167357) was obtained for the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were informed about the study by the researcher. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Since data were collected through an online form, the ethical principles outlined by the Association of Internet Researchers (AoIR) Ethics Working Committee were also taken into consideration. Participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study’s purpose, data confidentiality, and voluntary nature of participation. Informed consent was obtained digitally. Data anonymity, privacy protection, and secure digital storage were ensured in line with AoIR ethical guidelines.

Data evaluation

The analysis of the study data was performed by using SPSS 22.0 and G*Power 3.1 Statistical package software. Percentage, arithmetic mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values were calculated using SPSS 22.0. Necessary normality tests were performed in the process of analyzing the data and it was understood that the data showed normal distribution (kurtosis and skewness − 1.5 to + 1.5) [43]. P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition to classical statistical analysis, machine learning models were developed to evaluate the predictive power of the independent variables on quality of life. The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. The models were constructed using the caret package in R programming language (version 4.3.1), and the algorithms applied included k-nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machines (svmRadialSigma), artificial neural networks (avNNet, monmlp, pcaNNet), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost with xgbLinear).

Preprocessing procedures such as centering, scaling, and removal of multicollinearity were conducted before model training. Ten-fold cross-validation was used for model validation during the training phase. The performance of the models was evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE), R-squared (R²), and mean absolute error (MAE). To determine the relative contribution of the independent variables to the prediction of quality of life, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values were used.

Discussıon

In this chapter, the effects of kinesiophobia and successful aging on quality of life in the older adults are discussed in the light of the literature.

In our study, it was determined that quality of life decreased as kinesiophobia increased. When the literature is reviewed, there is a lot of evidence that kinesiophobia is associated with quality of life [44]. In a study conducted by Uçurum et al. in 2023, it was shown that quality of life decreased with increasing kinesiophobia level [45]. In a longitudinal study, high levels of kinesiophobia at baseline predicted negative changes in quality of life [46]. Askary-Ashtiani et al. reported that there was a significant and negative relationship between kinesiophobia and quality of life (physical function), but this relationship was not significant in terms of mental health [47]. In addition. Kinesiophobia has a stronger mediating role between catastrophizing and disability in individuals aged 55–82 years. evidence of a stronger mediating role between catastrophizing and disability in individuals aged 55–82 years than in individuals aged 41–54 years [48]. In addition, Larsson et al. reported that there was a significant and negative relationship between kinesiophobia and quality of life in individuals with poor health status, while this relationship was not significant in individuals with very good, good and moderate health status [49]. Our findings are consistent with these results and add further evidence from a Turkish elderly population. However, unlike some of the studies focusing only on the presence of a correlation, our results underline the need to consider the role of other psychosocial variables, such as successful aging, when interpreting the impact of kinesiophobia on quality of life. This suggests that interventions aimed solely at reducing kinesiophobia may be insufficient unless complemented with strategies that promote successful aging.

Notably, kinesiophobia was a significant negative predictor of quality of life in Model 1 but became statistically non-significant (p = 0.068) once successful aging was introduced in Model 2. This pattern may reflect a suppression or mediation effect, where successful aging absorbs or overlaps with the variance in quality of life that was initially attributed to kinesiophobia. According to Baron and Kenny’s conceptual framework [50], this type of statistical behavior may emerge when a mediating variable explains part of the relationship between an independent and a dependent variable. In this context, successful aging may act as a psychosocial buffer, mitigating the impact of fear-based behaviors like kinesiophobia on well-being. Baltes and Baltes describe successful aging as involving resilience, personal control, and adaptive strategies, which could help older adults maintain quality of life even in the presence of physical or psychological limitations [51]. Our finding suggests that individuals with higher levels of successful aging may be less affected by the fear-avoidance mechanisms associated with kinesiophobia. This underscores the importance of strengthening adaptive aging capacities in public health interventions, rather than targeting isolated symptoms alone.

In our study, it was determined that quality of life increased as successful aging increased. Maintaining the social life of older adults increases their life satisfaction and is directly associated with successful aging [52]. Research evidence suggests that leisure activities, such as physical, cognitive, intellectual, and social engagement, are linked to improved well-being and various positive health outcomes in older adults [53–56]. For example, Jeong and Park examined the relationship between participation in leisure activities and quality of life and depression among community-dwelling older adults in Korea. Their findings indicated that active, emotional, social, and productive activities were positively correlated with improved quality of life and lower levels of depression [57]. In another study examining the associations between physical activity, quality of life, and successful aging among community-dwelling older adults in South Korea, researchers found that both physical activity and quality of life were significantly linked to successful aging. Their findings suggest that these factors play a crucial role in promoting successful aging among older adults in Korea [58, 59]. In 2023, Zhao et al. demonstrated a correlation between the frequency of participation in specific leisure activities and successful aging among the Chinese older adults. Their findings indicated that the likelihood of successful aging increased proportionally with the number of leisure activities engaged in [60]. In addition, it is stated that maintaining a good night’s sleep and daytime alertness in elderly individuals is important in the process of successful aging [61]. Our findings strongly support the perspective that successful aging is not only a protective factor but a primary determinant of perceived life quality in old age. Unlike previous studies which examined these variables separately or descriptively, our study provides empirical support through multivariate analysis for the dominant role of successful aging when considered alongside kinesiophobia. These results highlight the need for public health strategies that go beyond physical symptom management and emphasize aging-related strengths such as resilience, autonomy, and active engagement.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the ability to draw causal inferences from the observed relationships. While associations between kinesiophobia, successful aging, and quality of life were identified, no conclusions can be made regarding the directionality or causality of these associations. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to better understand causal pathways. Second, the data were collected from a single province in Turkey, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Regional cultural norms, health system characteristics, and lifestyle patterns may influence perceptions of aging and movement-related fear, thereby affecting the outcomes. Third, potential cultural and social factors that influence both aging experiences and fear of physical activity were not examined. Differences in family structure, social support systems, religious practices, and community engagement may moderate the relationships explored in this study. Finally, although multiple validated scales were used, self-reported data are subject to biases such as social desirability and recall error. These biases may affect the reliability of the responses, particularly in elderly populations.

Recommendations for future research

Future research should employ longitudinal or mixed-methods designs to assess causal mechanisms and track changes in kinesiophobia, successful aging, and quality of life over time.Expanding the geographic and cultural scope of the research will improve generalizability and help identify how sociocultural norms shape aging experiences and movement-related fears. Comparative studies across regions or countries may yield deeper insights. Further studies might also explore mediating or moderating variables, such as resilience, social support, physical health status, or psychological well-being, that could influence the observed relationships. Additionally, qualitative approaches can enrich understanding by capturing the lived experiences of older adults and how they perceive successful aging in the context of physical limitations or fear of movement. Finally, intervention studies that test the effectiveness of programs aimed at both reducing kinesiophobia and enhancing successful aging indicators (e.g., autonomy, purpose, and social connectedness) are needed to support evidence-based public health strategies.

Conclusion

This study revealed that quality of life decreased with increasing kinesiophobia and increased with higher levels of successful aging. SHAP analysis further identified successful aging as the most influential predictor of quality of life among the elderly participants. Despite limitations such as cross-sectional design, limited geographic sampling, and potential response bias, the findings provide meaningful insights. Public health professionals and policymakers should focus on enhancing the adaptive capacities of older adults, such as resilience, engagement in meaningful activities, and psychosocial well-being, rather than targeting isolated problems like fear of movement. Future research should employ longitudinal and culturally diverse approaches to further clarify the relationships between aging, fear, and quality of life and develop interventions tailored to promote holistic aging in diverse communities.
