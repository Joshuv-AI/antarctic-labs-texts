Research on the application of refined operating room turnover efficiency in cross-hospital operating room efficiency evaluation
Authors: Chaoyu Zhang, Ronghua Gong, Fang Yan, Yi Liu, Ling Zang, Wenxia Pang, Yusheng Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a refined evaluation system for operating room (OR) turnover efficiency that addresses challenges in inter-hospital comparisons arising from variations in surgical volume and specialty distribution.MethodsOperational data (including date, operating room, table sequence, surgical name, entry time, start time, end time, and exit time) from July to December 2023 were retrieved from the surgical information management system. A specialty-specific turnover efficiency metric was constructed by excluding the upper and lower 5% of outliers. Comparisons were performed among overall OR averages, specialty-specific averages, and the specialized turnover efficiencies using independent samples Student’s t-test (P < 0.05).ResultsAmong the nine specialties analyzed, six exhibited significantly higher average turnover times than the overall OR average, with no overlap in most cases. Statistically significant differences were observed between the specialized turnover efficiencies and the OR-wide averages (for example, breast surgery: t = − 10.315, P < 0.00001).ConclusionSpecialty-specific turnover efficiency provides a more accurate reflection of operating room performance than unadjusted overall averages and shows promise for cross-hospital benchmarking, pending multi-center validation. Future studies should investigate the root causes of delays and develop targeted improvement strategies.

Introduction

Operating room (OR) turnover efficiency refers to the time needed to prepare the room for the next surgery after one ends. Enhancing this efficiency reduces patient waiting times, improves hospital throughput, boosts economic benefits, alleviates staff fatigue, and minimizes risks like patient anxiety or electrolyte imbalances [1–5]. Prolonged turnover can increase medical errors and dissatisfaction [6–9].

Literature shows variability in turnover times: 78 min [10], reduced from 71 to 42 min via quality control [11], 53.89 min in a tertiary hospital [12], and 52.51 ± 23.15 min [13]. However, existing studies focus on single-hospital management, with inconsistent indicators limiting cross-hospital comparability [4, 10, 12]. Without standardized metrics accounting for specialty differences and surgical volumes, experience sharing remains challenging.

This study introduces a refined, specialty-specific turnover efficiency metric to enable more objective performance evaluations, providing a foundation for cross-hospital benchmarking as well as targeted improvements through root-cause analysis (e.g., identification of workflow bottlenecks) and implementation of strategies such as staffing optimization or technology integration.

Materials and methods

General information

Our hospital is one of the first nine tertiary comprehensive hospitals in Jiangsu Province. At present, there are 46 operating rooms with a total of 132 nurses, divided into 11 surgical specialties: dentistry, sports medicine, gynecology, facial features, neurosurgery, thoracic surgery, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular surgery, general surgery, urology, orthopedics, and ophthalmology. The annual surgical volume exceeds 60,000 cases.

Methods

General information

This study was conducted in a large public tertiary hospital in Jiangsu Province, China. As part of the country’s public healthcare system, this hospital operates under a centralized model of perioperative management, with dedicated surgical specialties and a standardized surgical information management platform integrated across departments. The hospital has 46 operating rooms and performs over 60,000 surgeries annually, encompassing a wide range of specialties such as neurosurgery, general surgery, orthopedics, and urology.

Data collection and processing

To ensure methodological rigor and reproducibility, all personnel involved in data processing received standardized training in extraction, validation, and handling protocols. This training included workshops focused on identifying incomplete records and applying uniform inclusion/exclusion criteria, thereby minimizing inter-operator variability.

Retrospective operational data from July to December 2023 were extracted from the hospital’s surgical information management system. The dataset included the following key variables for each procedure: date, operating room identifier, department/specialty, patient hospitalization number, surgical name, anesthesia type, instrument nurse assignment, estimated and actual surgical durations, patient entry time into the OR, surgical start time, surgical end time, and patient exit time.

Turnover intervals were calculated as the time between the end of one procedure (end time) and the start of the next consecutive procedure (start time) in the same operating room. Only elective, consecutive cases were included to isolate true turnover processes and exclude the effects of scheduling gaps or non-consecutive procedures.

Data were first grouped by surgical specialty to account for inherent differences in procedure complexity, equipment preparation, and staffing requirements (e.g., orthopedic procedures frequently require more extensive setup due to implants and intraoperative imaging). Incomplete records (e.g., those with missing timestamps) were systematically identified and removed using automated scripts designed to detect anomalies, thereby ensuring data integrity.

Calculation of specialized turnover efficiency

To add novelty beyond traditional mean calculations, we developed a refined metric—specialized turnover efficiency—that incorporates outlier trimming to address the skewed distributions common in healthcare operational data, where rare events (e.g., emergencies or equipment failures) can disproportionately inflate averages.

For each specialty, a sample of 100 turnover intervals was selected using a random number table method to ensure representativeness and reduce selection bias. These intervals were sorted in ascending order by duration. To mitigate the impact of extreme values, the top and bottom 5% (i.e., the 1st to 5th and 96th to 100th percentiles) were excluded, as these often represent atypical delays not reflective of standard processes. The remaining central 90% (data points 6–95) were then used to compute the mean turnover time (µ) and standard deviation (σ), expressed as µ ± σ minutes.

While this study focuses on descriptive metrics, future iterations could integrate advanced techniques such as regression modeling (e.g., multivariate analysis to identify predictors of delays like anesthesia type or staff experience) or lean methodology elements (e.g., value stream mapping) for root cause analysis. For instance, stratifying data by subprocesses (cleaning, setup, patient transfer) could pinpoint bottlenecks, though this requires additional timestamp granularity not available in the current dataset.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between OR-wide averages, specialty-specific averages, and specialized turnover efficiencies were conducted using independent samples Student’s t-tests, with a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of differences, providing additional depth beyond P-values. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied, as the analyses were exploratory; however, Bonferroni correction could be considered in confirmatory multi-center studies to enhance rigor. Power analysis, based on pilot data, confirmed the sample size (n = 90 per specialty) provided > 80% power to detect differences of 10 min or more at α = 0.05.

Specialized turnover efficiency vs. OR average

Specialized turnover efficiencies (calculated after outlier exclusion) were compared to the OR average from July to December 2023 (Table 2). Independent samples t-tests revealed statistically significant differences for all specialties (Fig. 1). For example, in breast surgery, t = -10.3151, P < 0.00001, indicating lower turnover efficiency compared to the OR average. Similar differences were observed across other specialties, highlighting the limitations of using unrefined OR averages for performance evaluation. Additionally, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated, ranging from 0.8 to 1.5 across specialties, suggesting large practical differences. These findings underscore the metric’s sensitivity to specialty-specific variations, though multi-center data are needed to validate cross-hospital applicability.

While existing research on operating room efficiency typically focuses on punctuality rates or management tools for the first surgery, cross-hospital comparisons often lack specificity in indicators, making it difficult to exchange valuable insights. The specialized turnover efficiency evaluation system proposed in this study addresses these gaps by optimizing the calculation method (e.g., removing extreme values) and refining sample size limitations. First, this system enhances horizontal comparability, eliminating the impact of hospital size and specialty composition on overall efficiency, providing standardized metrics for cross-hospital benchmarking. Second, it offers specialized specificity, allowing for the identification of each specialty’s unique needs (e.g., breast surgery or orthopedics) in process optimization. Finally, through refined management, the system can pinpoint bottlenecks, such as anesthesia preparation and equipment turnover, avoiding a one-size-fits-all approach to improvements. This methodological foundation lays the groundwork for building a multi-center quality improvement alliance for operating rooms, particularly suited for collaborative improvements among highly specialized medical centers (Table 4).

Limitations

This study prioritizes metric refinement over direct process interventions, yielding a foundational rather than transformative contribution compared to applied management studies. Its single-center design restricts generalizability, and the basic statistical approach, while practical, lacks advanced modeling (e.g., regression for delay predictors). We advocate for expert consensus in multi-center collaborations to standardize methodologies and facilitate knowledge exchange.

Conclusion

The refined operating room turnover efficiency evaluation system, based on specialty-specific metrics, overcomes the limitations of traditional methods and facilitates more meaningful cross-hospital comparisons. This innovative approach aligns with global trends in operating room efficiency evaluation, enabling fair and objective inter-hospital performance assessments. Furthermore, the study’s cost-benefit analysis of single-disease surgeries and surgery pricing offers significant reference value. Ultimately, this study provides valuable theoretical and practical insights for enhancing operating room management in Chinese medical institutions.
