Neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities of children and adolescents with specific learning disorders: associations with gender and educational level
Authors: Giulia Lazzaro, Andrea Battisti, Cristiana Varuzza, Francesca Giumello, Fabio Quarin, Federica Somma, Pietro De Rossi, Stefano Vicari, Deny Menghini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpecific Learning Disorders (SLDs) are among the most common neurodevelopmental conditions, affecting not only academic performance but also a child’s daily functioning and overall well-being. Alongside their learning difficulties, many young people with SLDs show a greater vulnerability to emotional and behavioural problems. Although these issues are increasingly recognised, much of the available research still relies on screening questionnaires rather than structured diagnostic assessments, focuses mainly on SLDs with impairment in reading, or is based on small samples. In addition, more than a decade has passed since the last epidemiological study, resulting in a lack of updated evidence on neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities in SLDs. As SLDs diagnoses increase and mental health needs intensify, robust updated data are necessary. This study offers clinically based estimates of comorbid neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders in a large SLDs cohort and examines the association between gender and educational level.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 952 children and adolescents (7–19 years) diagnosed with SLDs and evaluated at the Child and Adolescent Neuropsychiatry Unit of the Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital. Neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities were assessed through multidisciplinary clinical evaluation, including developmental history, clinical observation, and a semi-structured diagnostic interview (K-SADS-PL DSM-5), and were diagnosed according to DSM-5/DSM-5-TR criteria.ResultsOverall, 53.9% of participants presented at least one neurodevelopmental or psychiatric comorbidity. ADHD was the most frequent diagnosis (32.1%), followed by anxiety disorders (15.9%), mood disorders (3.3%), and oppositional defiant disorder (ODD, 1.9%). Gender and educational level were associated with comorbidity patterns. Specifically, males showed higher rates of ADHD (M/F: 65.7% vs 49.5%) and ODD (M/F: 9.4% vs 4.2%), whereas females exhibited higher rates of anxiety (M/F: 30.8% vs 50.5%). Moreover, primary school students were more likely to receive an ADHD diagnosis (Primary/Secondary: 69.1% vs 47.6%), while secondary school students showed higher rates of anxiety and mood disorders (respectively, Primary/Secondary: 28.1% vs 51.1%; Primary/Secondary: 4.9% vs 11.6%). Finally, stratified descriptive analyses suggested increase prevalence of mood disorders among female participants attending secondary school.ConclusionsSLDs frequently co-occur with clinically significant neurodevelopmental and psychiatric diagnosis. ADHD and anxiety disorders are the most common comorbidities, displaying associations with gender and age. Our findings may be interpreted within the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework, which conceptualizes neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidity as the expression of partially overlapping symptom dimensions arising from shared risk factors and, potentially, common underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Moreover, these findings support the need for comprehensive assessments and early identification to guide targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13034-026-01097-2.Keywords: Behavioural and emotional problems, Psychiatric disorders, Neurodevelopmental disorders, Research domain criteria, Gender, Educational level

Introduction

Specific Learning Disorders (SLDs) are neurodevelopmental conditions characterized by persistent difficulties in acquiring reading (e.g., accurately and fluently reading words, understanding written text), written expression (e.g., correctly spelling and forming words), and mathematical abilities (e.g., arithmetic calculation and numerical reasoning) [1]. SLDs may occur in one domain—reading, writing, or mathematics—or in a combination of these, with an estimated prevalence of about 5% among school-aged children [2]. Beyond the core academic impairments, youths with SLDs frequently experience detrimental consequences across the lifespan, including higher rates of school dropout, unemployment, and reduced overall achievements [3]. These outcomes are often accompanied by psychological distress and poorer quality of life compared with their peers [4].

Children and adolescents with SLDs are at heightened risk for developing neuropsychiatric disorders across development [5–10]. Converging evidence indicates that youths with SLDs exhibit elevated levels of both externalizing and internalizing symptoms compared to typically developing peers (for a meta-analysis, see [8]). Among externalizing conditions, Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) represents the most frequently reported comorbidity [6, 11–16]. In addition, disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders have also been observed in this population [11–19]. Regarding internalizing difficulties, anxiety emerges as particularly prominent among children and adolescents with SLDs [11, 13–15, 19–23] and mood symptoms have also been reported in association with SLDs [11, 13–15, 17, 19, 20, 22, 23].

Although the association between learning difficulties and emotional and behavioural symptoms has been consistently reported [5, 7, 8, 10], current findings still present limitations.

First, comorbidity profiles have often been inferred from symptoms reports rather than clinically ascertained diagnoses. Indeed, to date, the most recent epidemiological study dates back to 2013, whereby the authors [24] examined 240 children and adolescents aged 7–16 years with a diagnosis of SLDs and documented high rates of clinically ascertained neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities: ADHD in 33%, anxiety disorders in 28.8%, and mood disorders in 9.4%. Given the lack of recent data, a more up-to-date epidemiological study on the neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities of SLDs is warranted to fill the 12-year gap.

Second, most investigations have relied on non-clinical samples of children and adolescents with learning difficulties, typically identified through screening procedures based on self-, parent-, or teacher-reports. When studies have included youths with a diagnosis of SLDs, sample sizes have generally been small, limiting representativeness.

Third, research has predominantly focused on SLDs with impairment in reading, thereby restricting the generalisability of findings to other forms of SLDs.

Finally, little attention has been paid to potential associated factors, including gender and educational level. Indeed, gender [25, 26] and educational level [27] are central dimensions in developmental psychopathology, and have been associated with differences in the onset, expression, and course of symptoms. To date, evidence on how these factors (i.e., gender and educational level) are associated with psychiatric comorbidities remains particularly limited, as most studies have focused primarily on emotional and behavioural symptoms. Consistent with previous studies in SLDs [23], educational level was selected as the primary grouping variable because the transition from primary to secondary school coincides with adolescence (from 10 years old according to the World Health Organization), and involves substantial contextual and environmental changes within the school learning environment. In secondary school, academic demands increase, instructional expectations become more stringent, and social relationships with both adults and peers become more complex [23].

To address the aforementioned gaps, we investigated the prevalence and distribution of comorbid neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders in 952 children and adolescents with SLDs, testing associations with gender and educational level. For clarity, in the current study, “neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities” refers to internalizing and externalising emotional and behavioural disorders, such as ADHD, oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder, anxiety disorders, mood disorders, stress-related disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorders. These findings update the last available prevalence data, published in 2013 [24], thereby contributing essential evidence for mental health services to refine assessment practices and optimise clinical management strategies.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The study cohort consisted of 952 youths with SLDs aged 7–19 years (Males, M/Females, F: 576/376; Primary School/Secondary School: 585/367) evaluated at the Child and Adolescent Neuropsychiatry Unit of the Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital between 2019 and 2024. The hospital operates as a tertiary-level paediatric referral hospital within the Italian healthcare system. The Unit serves as a regional and national referral centre, receiving patients primarily referred by primary care paediatricians, local child neuropsychiatry services, and other hospital specialists.

This is a retrospective, cross-sectional study. Data were obtained through a review of hospital records and clinical charts of children and adolescents referred for clinical evaluation. Due to the retrospective design, only de-identified data were analysed. All children and adolescents underwent comprehensive clinical evaluations in accordance with good clinical practice, as recommended by international guidelines for neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders. The assessments considered current and past symptomatology, as well as neurodevelopmental history, and were carried out by a multidisciplinary team consisting of child neuropsychiatrists and developmental psychologists.

All children and adolescents had a diagnosis of SLDs (with impairment in reading and/or with impairment in written expression and/or with impairment in mathematics) according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, DSM-5 [28] and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision, DSM-5-TR [1]. Inclusion criteria were: (1) non-verbal intelligence quotient (nvIQ, Raven Matrices [29, 30]; Perceptual Reasoning Index in Wechsler Scale: WISC-IV [31] or WAIS-IV [32]) in the normal range (> 80); (2) normal or corrected-to-normal vision; (3) no personal history of neurological conditions, hearing impairments, or/and medical diseases; (4) no current pharmacological treatment. Participants with autism spectrum disorder or intellectual disabilities [1, 28] were excluded.

The anonymity of the participants and the confidentiality of the data were guaranteed. All procedures performed in the study involving human participants were in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards (approved by the Local Ethical Committee; protocol 1547_OPBG_2018).

SLDs assessment

The diagnosis of SLDs was based on assessments of reading, writing, and mathematical skills, or through previous clinical and psychometric evaluations. Specifically, SLDs diagnosis (i.e., reading disorder, and/or writing disorder, and/or math disorder) was made in accordance with either DSM-5 [28] or DSM-5-TR [1] criteria. Children and adolescents met the diagnostic criteria for reading, writing, and/or math disorder if performance, in terms of accuracy and/or speed, was at least 1.5 SDs below the school-age norm-referenced (in accordance with the Consensus Conference [33] and the Istituto Superiore di Sanità [34]) on Italian standardized assessments of reading [35–37], writing [36–38], and/or math [36, 39], and if such difficulties significantly interfered with daily functioning.

Reading. Text reading errors, speed, and comprehension were assessed using the Prove MT-3 (Prove MT-3 Clinica for primary and middle school students [35]; Prove MT-3 Clinica Avanzate for first- and second-year secondary school students [36]; Prove di lettura e scrittura MT-16-19 for third-, fourth-, and fifth-year secondary school student [37]). In the text reading accuracy and speed, children and adolescents required to read a text aloud as accurately and rapidly as possible. In the text comprehension task, children and adolescents were asked to read the text in silence at their own pace, then answer a few multiple-choice questions, choosing one of four possible answers. Raw scores for errors (i.e., the total number of errors), speed (i.e., the number of syllables per second), and the total number of correct responses in the text comprehension task were classified as clinically-relevant, borderline, or within the average range, based on grade-specific normative data.

Maths. Mathematical abilities were evaluated using the BDE 2 - Batteria test diagnosi discalculia evolutiva (for primary and middle school students [39]) or Prove MT-3 Clinica Avanzate (for first- and second-year secondary school students [35]). Namely, mental calculation and arithmetic fact retrieval tasks were considered. Mental calculation tasks included additions and subtractions of increasing difficulty, each administered with a fixed maximum response time of 30 s per item. Arithmetic fact retrieval was assessed through timed single-digit multiplication problems (e.g., 3 × 3), with a maximum response time of 3 s per item. Raw scores for accuracy (i.e., the total number of correct responses provided within the time limits) were converted into percentiles based on grade-specific normative data.

Writing. Writing skills were assessed using standardized dictation or spontaneous writing tasks from the BVSCO-2 Valutazione della Scrittura e della Competenza Ortografica (for primary and middle school students [38]), Prove MT-3 Clinica Avanzate (for first- and second-year secondary school students [35]), Prove di lettura e scrittura MT-16-19 (for third-, fourth-, and fifth-year secondary school students [37]). Raw scores for errors (i.e., the total number of incorrect words) were classified as clinically-relevant, borderline, or within the average range, based on grade-specific normative data.

Clinical assessment

In the current study, we focused on the neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities clinically ascertained. Clinical assessment was conducted by a child neuropsychiatrist and developmental psychologists, bringing specialized expertise to the evaluation of psychopathological disorders in children and adolescents. Neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities were assessed according to the developmental history, extensive clinical observation and the semi-structured interview (Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School Aged Children Present and Lifetime Version DSM-5, K-SADS-PL DSM-5 [40]). Clinicians were extensively trained in utilizing the semi-structured interview [40].

K-SADS-PL DSM-5 is a semi-structure interview that serves as a valuable tool for gathering information about the clinical history and developmental trajectories of primary neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders according to DSM-5. This instrument not only relies on the input from the children and adolescents but also incorporates insights from their parents, enhancing the breadth and depth of information gathered. The K-SADS-PL DSM-5 has a 3-point scale, whereby absent symptoms are coded as 0, subclinical symptoms are coded as 1, and clinical symptoms (e.g., symptoms severe and frequent enough to impair individuals’ functioning in multiple contexts) are coded as 2.

Diagnostic formulations were based on the integration of all available clinical information and were discussed daily within the multidisciplinary clinical team. The use of established diagnostic procedures—integrating developmental history, extensive clinical observation, and semi-structured interviews—together with regular team-based discussions, was intended to enhance diagnostic consistency across clinicians.

Statistical analysis

Prevalence rates of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities were calculated and expressed as frequencies and percentages. Associations between neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities and gender (male vs. female) as well as educational level (primary vs. secondary school) were examined using Chi-square (χ²) tests of independence. Effect sizes were quantified using Odds Ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.0.1, x64), using the epitools package (v. 0.5.10.1) and SPSS IBM 23.

Discussion

The primary aim of the study was to provide an updated estimate prevalence and distribution of comorbid neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders in a large cohort of children and adolescents with SLDs, using a categorical, DSM-based approach. Consistent with previous findings [24], 50% of the sample with SLDs met criteria for at least one comorbid neurodevelopmental or psychiatric disorder. Thus, approximately one in two youths with SLDs were affected by an additional neurodevelopmental or psychiatric condition. Although with considerable caution, our findings may be tentatively compared with prevalence estimates of mental health disorders from the general paediatric population to provide contextual reference [41]. Our findings indicate that, in our population of children and adolescents with SLDs, neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities are observed in about half of the group, whereas in the general population of children and adolescents worldwide, the prevalence of mental health disorders is estimated at 13.4% [41].

Within the externalising disorders, ADHD was the most prevalent, affecting approximately 30% of our sample – higher than the prevalence reported in the general population of children and adolescents worldwide (3.4%; [41]). These results corroborate previous findings, whereby ADHD diagnosis was reported in 20 to 40% of youths with SLDs [6, 24]. The strong association between ADHD and SLDs is not unexpected, as evidence from the Colorado Longitudinal Twin Study on learning difficulties and ADHD indicates that genetic influences on learning difficulties are substantial and highly stable [42].

Of note, among participants with co-occurring ADHD and SLDs, anxiety disorders were also observed as an additional psychiatric comorbidity, affecting 14% of our sample. This pattern is clinically relevant, as the co-occurrence of multiple conditions may be associated with greater chronicity [43]. In this regard, the presence of both ADHD and anxiety further underscores the cumulative burden of comorbidity, which may increase clinical complexity and make both diagnostic assessment and treatment planning more challenging.

In addition, in line with previous evidence [6, 24], anxiety was the most prevalent internalizing disorder in our sample, both when it co-occurred with SLDs and ADHD and when it represented the isolated comorbidity associated with SLDs. Specifically, anxiety was observed in approximately 15% of participants with SLDs, a rate that appears higher than that reported in the general population of children and adolescents worldwide (6.5%; [41]). This finding is particularly relevant in light of the literature suggesting that anxiety in SLDs may emerge in domain-specific forms, such as math anxiety [44] or, more recently, reading-related anxiety [6]. In our sample, however, anxiety was identified in a broader form, extending beyond specific academic domains. This may suggest that difficulties initially linked to school-related tasks can also be associated with more generalized anxiety symptoms. In more severe cases, this may have important clinical implications, as anxiety related to academic functioning can contribute to school avoidance and, potentially, school refusal [45]. Such patterns highlight the functional impact of SLDs beyond academic performance, emphasizing the need for early identification and interventions that address both learning difficulties and associated emotional distress. Anxiety, in this context, may stem from low self-esteem, which arises from repeated exposure to challenging academic situations that elicit discomfort [46]. These experiences can foster dysfunctional beliefs and negative self-perceptions, which may generalize to other domains of life [46].

The association between SLDs and anxiety disorders may be framed within the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework [47]. Rather than conceptualizing SLDs and anxiety as entirely separate categorical conditions, RDoC describes psychopathology across dimensional and transdiagnostic domains of functioning that cut across conventional diagnostic boundaries. Within this framework, anxiety in individuals with SLDs may be considered in relation to alterations involving interacting domains, particularly negative valence systems, cognitive systems, and social processes. From this perspective, the elevated rates of anxiety observed in children and adolescents with SLDs fit with a model in which emotional and cognitive difficulties are examined as partially overlapping dimensions, rather than exclusively as distinct co-occurring disorders. This view supports a broader characterization of anxiety in SLDs, consistent with the RDoC emphasis on cross-domain symptom dimensions and functional processes. Moreover, the elevated rates of anxiety in children and adolescents with SLDs may reflect shared familial risk factors, as suggested by the higher prevalence of generalized anxiety disorder among siblings of children with SLDs with impairment in reading [12]. Twin studies further indicate a genetic contribution to the co-occurrence of learning difficulties and anxiety, in addition to environmental influences [12].

Disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders (e.g., ODD) were diagnosed in our group of SLDs in association with ADHD with a prevalence of 6.5%, slightly higher than the 5.7% reported in the general population of children and adolescents worldwide [41]. This pattern aligns with evidence suggesting that such associations are typically mediated by ADHD symptoms rather than being directly attributable to SLDs [11]. Indeed, ODD as isolated disorder was diagnosed only in 1.9% out of participants. Finally, mood disorders were diagnosed in association with ADHD in 9 cases and alone in 31 cases, with an overall prevalence of 4%, slightly higher than that reported in the general population of children and adolescents worldwide (2.6%; [41]).

Our findings in SLDs indicated differences in the distribution of comorbid ADHD, ODD, and anxiety disorders across gender and educational groups. With respect to gender, our results showed that boys with SLDs are almost twice as likely as girls with SLDs to meet criteria for ADHD. A similar pattern emerged for ODD, with prevalence rates of 9.4% in boys and 4.2% in girls, corresponding to more than a twofold increased risk of ODD among males. It is well documented in the literature that ADHD and ODD are more frequently diagnosed in males, likely because they tend to display more externalizing behaviors [48, 49]. In contrast, females are often under-identified, as they are more likely to present with inattentive and less disruptive symptoms, making their difficulties less noticeable [50]. On the other hand, our findings revealed that 50.5% of girls with SLDs met diagnostic criteria for an anxiety disorder, compared with 30.8% of boys. This pattern aligns with broader evidence of greater vulnerability to internalizing disorders in females [51]. Early recognition of anxiety in girls with SLDs may be therefore critical, not only to provide timely interventions but also to prevent chronicity of comorbid psychopathology.

With respect to educational level, our results documented that 70% of students with SLDs in primary school met criteria for ADHD compared to 47% in secondary school, corresponding to more than two-fold higher odds in the primary group. On the other hand, 51% of students with SLDs in secondary school met criteria for an anxiety disorder compared with 47% in primary school. Similarly, 11% of students with SLDs in secondary school met criteria for a mood disorder, compared with 5% in primary school. Both results reflect a lower prevalence of internalizing disorder (i.e., anxiety and mood disorders) in primary than in secondary school. These patterns are consistent with previous literature, which suggests that neurodevelopmental disorders such as ADHD are more commonly diagnosed in younger children [52], whereas anxiety and mood disorders are more prevalent in preadolescents and adolescents [53]. A possible, albeit speculative, explanation for this pattern is the progressive accumulation of academic challenges and the increasing cognitive and social demands faced by students during secondary school. As academic tasks become more complex and social pressures intensify, children with SLDs may be more vulnerable to anxiety and mood symptoms, potentially reflecting the influence of environmental stressors on psychological functioning over time.

Last, exploratory analysis showed a higher prevalence of females with mood disorders among participants in secondary school. This finding appears broadly consistent with previous literature reporting greater rates of depression in adolescent girls, possibly reflecting the combined influence of biological factors, such as hormonal and pubertal changes, and psychosocial processes, including stress exposure and interpersonal stress reactivity, which may be more pronounced in females during adolescence [54]. However, this result should be interpreted with caution, given the small number of participants with SLDs and mood disorders, a limitation that becomes even more relevant when the sample is further stratified by gender and educational level (primary vs. secondary school). Larger studies are therefore needed to clarify this pattern and to prove the interaction between age and gender in the expression of comorbidities in SLDs.

Our study could be further refined in several aspects. First, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the relationships observed between SLDs and neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities. Longitudinal research would be valuable to better understand the developmental trajectories and potential bidirectional influences among these conditions. Second, since our sample was recruited from a tertiary referral neuropsychiatry unit within a specific Italian clinical context, the findings may not be fully generalizable to broader paediatric populations. Future studies involving more diverse and representative samples across different levels of healthcare system, educational as well as cultural settings would help to enhance the external validity of these results. Third, in the absence of a non-SLDs clinical comparison group evaluated within the same setting and time period, neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidity prevalence observed in our clinical SLDs sample was compared with estimates derived from population-based studies [41]. Such comparisons should be interpreted with caution, as differences in case ascertainment, diagnostic procedures, and referral patterns may substantially influence prevalence rates. Future studies would benefit from including non-SLDs clinical comparison groups assessed in the same clinical context.

Overall, our findings suggest that SLDs rarely occur in isolation and are frequently accompanied by ADHD and anxiety, and that the distribution of comorbid neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions differs across gender and educational level. Overall, the high prevalence and heterogeneity of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities observed in SLDs support the value of moving beyond a strictly categorical reading of these conditions. Rather than considering each comorbidity as an entirely separate clinical entity, our findings suggest the importance of examining SLDs within a broader multidimensional framework that takes into account the frequent overlap between learning, attentional, emotional, and behavioral difficulties. In this regard, dimensional and transdiagnostic approaches may offer a useful framework for capturing the complexity of these clinical presentations and for better characterizing the broad psychopathological burden associated with SLDs. Future studies should further explore these patterns using longitudinal and multivariable designs.

Within this framework, our findings also suggest that the diagnostic assessment of youths with SLDs should systematically include screening for both neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders, with particular attention to differences across gender and educational levels, in order to facilitate timely recognition, referral, and intervention. In the same vein, these results further underscore the importance of multidisciplinary clinical management, in which educational support is integrated into a broader care model that also includes the monitoring and treatment of emotional and behavioural difficulties.
