Admixture of anesthetic drugs in the era of precision monitoring: a dangerous convenience
Authors: Susana Vacas, Michele Introna, Francisco Lobo
Published: 2026

In contemporary anesthetic practice, balanced multimodal general anesthesia relies on the precise titration of hypnotic and analgesic agents that act through distinct mechanisms across central and peripheral neural pathways. This approach aims to achieve unconsciousness, amnesia, antinociception, and akinesia while minimizing hemodynamic instability, drug toxicity, delayed emergence, and postoperative complications [1]. Accordingly, modern anesthesia increasingly depends on multimodal monitoring. Electroencephalogram (EEG) provides a real-time assessment of consciousness and hypnotic level, [2, 3] while emerging technologies seek to quantify the nociception–antinociception balance [4]. Together, these tools support individualized, physiology-guided titration of anesthetic drugs to maintain an appropriate balance between unconsciousness, unresponsiveness, and analgesia [5–7].

Pharmacologic complexity and the fallacy of drug mixing

Despite these advances, the practice of mixing anesthetic drugs within a single syringe persists. Common combinations include propofol with ketamine, dexmedetomidine, or opioids, as well as more complex multi-drug admixtures [8–10]. These mixtures may be delivered via continuous infusion or manual boluses, often justified by convenience. However, this practice is increasingly difficult to reconcile with contemporary pharmacologic principles. Multiple authors have cautioned against drug admixture, [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16] emphasizing that it fundamentally compromises the clinician’s ability to interpret pharmacodynamic responses. Hypnotic agents such as propofol primarily modulate cortical activity and level of consciousness, whereas analgesics, particularly opioids and adjuncts like ketamine, act on nociceptive pathways and autonomic responses. When administered as a mixture, their effects become inseparably intertwined. Overlapping side-effect profiles, including respiratory depression, hypotension, and bradycardia, further obscure attribution of clinical responses to a specific agent. Critically, pharmacodynamic interactions between these drugs are not simply additive but often synergistic and non-linear. As a result, the clinician loses the ability to predict, interpret, or adjust drug effects with precision.

Loss of independent titration

Mixing hypnotic and analgesic agents into a single syringe or infusion inevitably results in fixed ratios of drugs, representing a departure from the core principle of balanced anesthesia: the independent and physiologically guided titration of distinct drug classes. These fixed proportions cannot adapt to dynamic changes in surgical stimulus, patient sensitivity, or pharmacokinetic variability. In practice, this creates a scenario in which adjusting one pharmacologic domain inevitably alters another. Increasing infusion rates to deepen hypnosis simultaneously increases opioid exposure, potentially leading to opioid-related side effects unrelated to analgesic need. Conversely, treating nociceptive responses by escalating a mixed infusion risks excessive hypnotic dosing, increasing the likelihood of EEG burst suppression, delayed emergence, and hemodynamic instability.

Mechanistic illustration: propofol and ketamine

The interaction between propofol and ketamine illustrates this problem at a systems neuroscience level. Both drugs alter the cortico-thalamic neuronal pathways through distinct molecular actions. Propofol enhances GABAergic inhibition, generating synchronized slow and alpha oscillations within thalamocortical circuits and suppressing cortical connectivity. In contrast, ketamine blocks NMDA receptors, disinhibits cortical pyramidal neurons, and produces high-frequency beta and gamma activity while disrupting thalamic spindle generation. When co-administered, these agents interact with overlapping thalamocortical inter-neuronal networks. Ketamine may attenuate or disrupt propofol-induced oscillatory patterns, as reflected by a decrease in hyper-synchronous alpha oscillations (Fig. 1). Consequently, EEG signatures become difficult to interpret, undermining one of the most reliable tools for monitoring hypnotic level. Importantly, identifying a low, anti-nociceptive dose of ketamine, such as one targeting the dorsal horn of the spinal cord [17], requires isolating its effects from those of the hypnotic agent. This is only possible when drugs are administered separately, and their individual EEG signatures can be distinguished.

A similar rationale applies to mixing propofol and dexmedetomidine in the same syringe, as it makes it impossible to adjust the dose of each drug based on the shape of the alpha spindles [2,18].

Toward drug-specific, feedback-driven monitoring

If the goal of modern anesthesia is individualized, physiology-based care, then monitoring and drug delivery strategies must be aligned. Pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK-PD) models provide estimates of effect-site concentrations but require continuous clinical or surrogate feedback to remain accurate in the face of interpatient variability. Real-time physiologic monitoring systems integrating EEG for hypnotic level and validated measures of nociception are essential to disentangle drug effects and guide titration. Such systems enable clinicians to adjust hypnotic and analgesic components independently, preserving mechanistic clarity and optimizing patient outcomes – a fundamental necessity that is obscured by single-syringe drug mixtures. A major objective for the anesthesiology community will be to determine the sustainability, feasibility, and clinical utility of large-scale adoption of monitoring systems capable of selectively capturing distinct physiological endpoints, thereby enabling separate and physiologically informed titration of individual anesthetic agents. As the field moves toward precision anesthesia, embracing selective, drug-specific monitoring while simultaneously adopting non-selective drug delivery strategies represents a conceptual inconsistency that is increasingly difficult to justify.

Conclusion and editorial position

Mixing hypnotic and analgesic agents in a single syringe for anesthesia induction and maintenance represents a trade-off between convenience and pharmacologic precision. Continued progress in clinical monitoring, particularly systems capable of providing reliable, real-time assessment of both hypnotic level and nociceptive balance, must be accompanied by equally precise approaches to drug administration. In this context, studies employing the admixture of anesthetic agents within a single syringe face inherent limitations in pharmacologic interpretability and mechanistic clarity, and represent a conceptual inconsistency with contemporary advances in perioperative monitoring and computational modelling. We therefore encourage approaches that preserve drug titration and align with current monitoring paradigms, supporting greater physiologic precision, interpretability, and scientific rigor. Accordingly, we will not consider for publication submissions that involve the admixture of anesthetic drugs within a single syringe.
