Magnetoencephalography Profile of Patients with Drug-Resistant Focal Epilepsy and Normal MRI
Authors: Ajay Asranna, Asheeb Abdulhak, Lakshminarayanapuram Gopal Viswanathan, Ravindranandh Chowdary Mundlamuri, Raghavendra Kenchaiah, Mariyappa Narayanan, Bhargava Gautham, Velumurugan Jayabal, Rose Dawn Bharath, Jitender Saini, Chandana Nagaraj, Sandhya Mangalore, Karthik Kulanthaivelu, Nishanth Sadashiva, A Mahadevan, Jamuna Rajeswaran, Arivazhagan Arimappamagan, Bhaskara Rao Malla, Sanjib Sinha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Magnetoencephalography (MEG) could be a valuable tool in the presurgical evaluation of drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), especially when the initial evaluation is inconclusive. In this retrospective study, we describe the profile of MEG in patients with DRE and normal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).Methods:We included patients with focal epilepsy and normal MRI who underwent presurgical evaluation for DRE. MEG profiles of these patients, including the frequency of spikes, density of clusters, number of clusters, and concordance with video electroencephalography (VEEG), were analyzed.Results:Of the 73 patients included, magnetic source imaging (MSI) provided localizing information in 51 (69.9%) patients. Among patients with localizing MEG findings, localizing information on VEEG too was noted in 42 (57.5% of the whole cohort). Thirty-one (42.5%) patients had concordant findings with region-specific localization, six (8.2%) patients had partial concordance, and five (6.8%) subjects showed discordant findings. There was a moderate agreement for the presumed epileptogenic zone in comparing findings derived from MEG and VEEG (kappa value of 0.451, P < 0.001). The agreement was lower when MEG localized to the frontal lobe (kappa value of 0.379, P = 0.001) than the temporal lobe (kappa value 0.442, P = 0.002).Conclusions:MEG can provide localizing information in most patients with a normal MRI. A moderate degree of agreement between localization by MEG and VEEG was noted. These findings highlight the usefulness of MSI in the presurgical evaluation of MRI-negative DRE.

Introduction

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) is a valuable tool in the presurgical evaluation of drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). It has distinct advantages, combining the superior spatial resolution of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with the better temporal resolution of electroencephalography (EEG) and provides a direct measure of neuronal activation.[1] DRE affects almost one-third of patients with epilepsy and warrants a formal presurgical evaluation focused on delineating the epileptogenic zone and further consideration of epilepsy surgery. MRI of the brain and video EEG (VEEG) findings are key steps in the initial presurgical evaluation of DRE.[2] The presence of an epileptogenic lesion on MRI increases the chances of a patient undergoing epilepsy surgery and is also a key predictor of favorable surgical outcomes.[3] Approximately 20%–40% of patients with DRE have no lesions on MRI.[4] Although technological advancements, including 7 T MRI, may increase sensitivity, their clinical utility is yet to be established.[56] MEG can offer key insights and aid localization of the epileptogenic zone, especially when the initial presurgical evaluation is inconclusive.[7] Studies have shown how MEG contributes to the decision-making and planning of epilepsy surgery, including pediatric epilepsy surgery.[89] MEG can also help recognize patients unsuitable for epilepsy surgery and where invasive monitoring may not be feasible for further localization. In addition to its role in the localization of the epileptogenic zone, MEG also offers insights into the organization of the epileptic network and helps in mapping the eloquent cortex.[1011] Though other modalities like positron emission tomography (PET) and electrical source imaging techniques have found utility in the presurgical evaluation of MRI-negative DRE, we need to increase our understanding of these noninvasive modalities and their relative advantages.[12]

In this study, we share our experience on the profiles of MEG in patients with DRE, where MRI showed no epileptogenic lesions. We aimed to analyze the characteristics of MEG dipoles in patients with DRE and normal MRI and the role that MEG played in presurgical evaluation.

Methods

This is a retrospective study conducted at the MEG Center, Department of Neurology at a tertiary care center and University Hospital. The institute’s ethics committee approved the study. Inclusion criteria for the study were consecutive patients with focal epilepsy who underwent MEG after 2013 as a part of presurgical evaluation for DRE and whose 3 T MRI done at the institute showed no lesions associated with epilepsy. The MRI was consistent with Harmonized Neuroimaging of Epilepsy Structural Sequences-MRI protocol with millimetric T1 and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images and high-resolution sub-millimetric T2 images. The images were reported by trained neuro-radiologists (KK, JS, SM, RDB). Patients with lesions on MRI and those who did not undergo MEG were excluded. MEG data was acquired with an Elekta Neuromag Triux MEG system with 306 channels (204 gradiometers and 102 magnetometers). The average study duration was 45–120 min at a sampling frequency of 2 kHz. Structural MRI was acquired in a 1.5/3.0 T MRI system (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany).

Source localization was performed with standard equivalent current dipole modeling. The dipole selection criteria involved choosing dipoles that met specific thresholds for dipole moment (100–500 nAm) and goodness of fit (>75%). Dipoles were fitted at the peak of the interictal discharges. Localized discharges were then overlaid on the subjects’ MRI. Spike load was defined as frequent: >60 spikes/60 min and infrequent: <60 spikes/60 min.[13] Standardized definitions for clusters were used – a minimum of six dipoles seen within 1 cm3 qualified for a cluster.[14] The dosage of antiseizure medications (ASMs) was adjusted based on the frequency of seizures. For most patients, the evening dose of ASMs was reduced to half the usual dose on the night before they underwent MEG, and for some, the morning dose was also reduced or skipped entirely. For patients suffering multiple episodes per day, we did not alter the ASM dose. We used the Elekta proprietary software, that is, DANA software, for magnetic source imaging.

Analysis and review of VEEG were done by trained and certified epileptologists (SS, RM, RK, AA, and LGV). The following parameters were considered for analyzing VEEG: 1) seizure semiology, 2) interictal discharges, and 3) ictal discharges. This information was used for localization and lateralization of the presumed epileptogenic zone. Any differences in the interpretation of seizure semiology and electrographic data were resolved by mutual discussion among investigators.

Concordance between VEEG localization and MEG was classified as follows: 1) concordant: MEG localization overlapped VEEG localization to the lobar/sub-lobar level; 2) partially concordant: MEG localization overlapped VEEG localization, but with additional sources or concordant lateralization: the hemisphere of MEG localization and VEEG localization was the same, but not at the lobar level; and 3) discordant: MEG localization and VEEG localization were in different hemispheres.

Interpretation of PET-MRI was done by trained neuro-radiologists (KK, JS, SM, RDB). Concordance between MEG and PET was classified as follows: 1) concordant: MEG localization overlapped the area of PET hypometabolism to the lobar/sub-lobar level; 2) partially concordant: MEG localization overlapped PET hypometabolism, but with additional areas of involvement or concordant lateralization: PET hypometabolism and MEG localization in the same hemisphere, but not at the lobar level; and 3) discordant: MEG localization and PET hypometabolism were in different hemispheres.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics in frequency distribution and proportions for categorical variables and mean, range, and standard deviation were used for continuous variables. Agreement and disagreement between MEG and VEEG were statistically assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Kappa values of 0 were taken to indicate no agreement and 0–0.20 as slight, 0.21–0.40 as fair, 0.41–0.60 as moderate, 0.61–0.80 as substantial, and 0.81–1 as an almost perfect agreement between the reference region and MEG localization.

Results

Six hundred and twenty-eight patients underwent MEG during the study period. Of them, 531 patients had a lesion compatible with focal epilepsy, whereas 24 had inconclusive findings or did not undergo 3 T MRIs and were excluded. Seventy-three patients had drug-refractory focal epilepsy and were MRI negative and were included in the analysis.

Concordance between VEEG and MEG localization

Among patients with localized MEG findings, localizing information on VEEG was available in 42 (57.5% of the whole cohort) patients. This was based on assessing clinical semiology, irritative zone, and ictal onset zone gleaned from the interpretation of VEEG (SS, RC, RK, AA, and LGV).

On analyzing the degree of concordance between MEG and VEEG findings, we found that 31 (42.5%) patients had concordant findings with region-specific localization, six (8.2%) patients had partial concordance, and five (6.8%) had discordant findings [Figure 1]. In nine (12. 3%) patients, MEG provided additional localizing information, where VEEG was inconclusive. An agreement was calculated for the presumed epileptogenic zone comparing MEG and VEEG, which showed a moderate agreement (kappa value of 0.451, P < 0.001). The agreement was lower when MEG localized to the frontal lobe (kappa value of 0.379, P = 0.001) than temporal lobe localization (kappa value 0.442, P = 0.002).

Ictal MEG: Two patients had ictus during MEG. Ictal onset was from the right temporal region in one patient, which was concordant to interictal MEG and VEEG localization. The ictal MEG localized to the right posterior cingulate in another patient. This correlated with localization by interictal MEG and seizure semiology. The ictal onset on VEEG was also concordant, characterized by right frontocentral ictal onset (F4 and C4 channels).

Fludeoxyglucose-18 (FDG)-PET: MRI-PET was available in 18 (24. 6%) patients and showed areas of abnormality in 16 (21.9%) patients. PET revealed more than one area of hypometabolism in nine (50%) patients. The areas of PET metabolism were concordant to MEG localization in two (11.1%) patients and partially concordant in seven (38.8%) patients, whereas discordant changes were noted in eight (44.4%) patients.

Epilepsy surgery: Three patients in this cohort underwent epilepsy surgery. Patient 1 is a 22-year-old man who had an irritative and ictal onset zone over the left temporal lobe on VEEG. MEG showed dipole clusters over the left lateral temporal lobe, and MRI-PET showed suspicious left medial temporal hypometabolism. He underwent left anterior temporal lobectomy with amygdalohippocampectomy and, at 8 years of follow-up, had Engel IB outcome as per the Engel Epilepsy Surgery Outcome Scale. Postoperative MEG was also done and showed no dipoles. Patient 2 is a 13-year-old boy with electroclinical features consistent with Lennox–Gastaut syndrome. MEG showed diffuse and scattered dipoles. These findings supported a multifocal epilepsy, ruling out a resective surgery for seizure freedom. He underwent a corpus callosotomy for control of atonic head drops. Though his head drops have been controlled, he continues to have focal motor-unaware seizures. Patient 3, a 42-year-old man, had DRE with clinical features suggesting Temporal Lobe Epilepsy (TLE) and a normal MRI. VEEG showed interictal discharges arising from the left temporal and frontotemporal regions. Ictal recording revealed a temporal neocortical rhythm. MEG showed prominent and dense clusters in the left temporal lobe, specifically in the superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyri, as well as in the fusiform gyrus. In addition, there were clusters in the peri-opercular areas. He underwent stereo-EEG with electrodes also placed in the areas that showed clusters on MEG. The ictal onset was recorded from the left fusiform gyrus, left inferior temporal gyrus, and planum temporale. However, the peri-opercular areas were not involved in Stereo-EEG (SEEG). He subsequently underwent temporal lobe resection with Electro-Corticography (ECOG)-based resection of the superior temporal gyrus. The resection involved areas with dense MEG clusters in the temporal lobe, as mentioned above. He was seizure free at 1-year follow-up [Figure 3].

Discussion

We analyzed the MEG profile of 73 patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy and with no detectable lesions on MRI. In our study, MEG provided localizing information in 51/73 (69.9%) patients. MEG and VEEG findings were concordant in 31/73 (42.5%) patients, and more so when MEG was localized to the temporal lobe.

Our study’s MEG yield was slightly lower than the reported yield of about 80% in published literature.[1] One reason could be that we excluded patients with MRI abnormalities in our study. MRI-negative patients with DRE probably have diverse etiologies and poor localization of the epileptogenic zone, leading to scattered and diffuse dipoles, which could contribute to inconclusive MEG findings.

Spike clusters were seen in 82.2% of the patients, most of whom had a single cluster [Table 2]. However, no spike clusters were found in 13/73 (17.8%) patients, whereas diffuse or sparse clusters were noted in 28/73 (38.4%) patients. Previous studies have established the localizing value of MEG spike dipoles clustered around MRI lesions.[1315] Recent studies in MRI-negative patients have shown that clustered dipoles, rather than scattered dipoles, have a higher correlation with the ictal onset zone.[14] Single clusters also coincide more often with the ictal onset zone and are associated with better seizure-free outcomes.[16] Thus, a monofocal cluster points more accurately to the ictal onset zone, whereas the presence of multifocal clusters may suggest a wider epileptic network.[17]

The localizing value of MEG has been confirmed by studies that have demonstrated good concordance with intracranial EEG localization and a high positive predictive value.[18] Concordance between EEG and MEG has been associated with good outcomes after epilepsy surgery.[19] There was good concordance between VEEG and MEG findings in 42.5% of patients in our study. This concordance was lower when MEG localized to the frontal lobe than the temporal lobe. A relatively lower sensitivity of MEG to sources in the mesial temporal structures has been previously reported.[20] Previous studies also indicate that MEG is superior to EEG in neocortical and frontal lobe epilepsy, although most patients in this study had radiologic abnormalities.[2122] A combination of EEG and MEG may be ideally suited to guide localization.[22]

Knowlton[18] compared various modalities – MEG, fluorodeoxyglucose-PET, and ictal single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT). The author reported that concordance with intracranial EEG findings was greatest with MEG. MEG and PET could provide complementary information, particularly when the other investigations are nonlocalizing.[23]

The current study found poor concordance between MEG and PET findings, while no patients underwent SPECT. Variations in the etiology and duration of epilepsy may account for these findings.

Ictal MEG can provide better localizing information in a noninvasive manner.[24] Two patients (2.7%) had ictus during MEG, and while the ictal onset was concordant with VEEG localization in one patient, it was partially concordant in another. Ictal MEG has been reported in 7%–24% of MEG recordings.[25] In a study by Alkawadri et al.,[26] ictal MEG dipoles correlated with the lobe of onset in 88% of patients compared to intracranial EEG evaluations. Some patients (7%) also showed clear ictal MEG patterns, but no EEG changes were noted. We previously reported the concordance of ictal high-frequency oscillations on MEG to the presumed epileptogenic zone.[27]

Thus, a detailed analysis of these ictal changes could lead to more accurate localization.

There have been many instances where dense clusters on MEG and localized ictal MEG findings have prompted a re-evaluation of MRIs that were previously reported as normal and led to the detection of radiologic lesions.[2428] However, we could not formally evaluate this aspect because of the study’s retrospective nature.

MEG has shown promise in the planning of intracranial EEG. In a prospective study, MEG showed nonredundant information in about 33% of patients who underwent intracranial EEG.[8] Furthermore, it changed decision-making in 9% of patients. One patient in our study underwent stereo-EEG. Though a formal prospective analysis was not done, MEG clusters influenced the placement of invasive electrodes in our patient and subsequently showed concordant changes leading to successful epilepsy surgery. Similarly, concordant VEEG and MEG findings guided temporal lobectomy in another patient with a good seizure outcome at follow-up.

Therefore, MEG can provide useful information in localizing the epileptic network when MRI is noncontributory. Along with VEEG, especially where concordant findings are seen, they can help in generating a hypothesis regarding the epileptic networks that are involved, which intracranial EEG can further explore. However, there are limitations to this approach of looking at findings from VEEG and MEG solely in terms of concordant/discordant findings. In some cases, the lack of concordance between VEEG and MEG could be explained by the differences in sensitivity of VEEG and MEG to electrical sources in various brain regions. For instance, previous studies have shown that MEG is more sensitive than EEG in frontal lobe epilepsy.[22] A nuanced approach is, therefore, advisable, keeping in mind the strengths and limitations of each of these modalities.

Limitations

The study’s retrospective nature precluded a more precise evaluation of the role of MEG in presurgical evaluation. As most patients did not undergo epilepsy surgery, the epileptogenic zone could not be confirmed; hence, a comparison with the gold standard is lacking. Thus, findings from the current study need to be confirmed by larger studies in which patients have undergone invasive monitoring and epilepsy surgery. The number of patients undergoing invasive EEG was also low, chiefly due to financial constraints faced by the patient. The role of MEG in detecting radiologic abnormalities could not be made. Given the diverse etiologies of epilepsy in the region, the results cannot be generalized. We did not use voltage maps for interpreting EEGs, which could have helped in better visualization of tangential dipoles.[29]

Despite these limitations, the study provides a good overview of the MEG profile in patients with MRI-negative DRE and highlights the role of MEG in this context.

Conclusion

MEG can provide some localizing information in about two-thirds of DRE patients with a normal MRI. A moderate degree of agreement between localization by MEG and VEEG was noted in this study. The degree of agreement was higher when MEG localized to the temporal lobe than the frontal lobe. These findings could potentially be utilized effectively to inform planning of invasive EEG and epilepsy surgery.
