The art of precision in unveiling hydrogen solubility in bines through data-driven modeling
Authors: Raouf Hassan, Mohammad Reza Kazemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting hydrogen solubility in aqueous environments requires sophisticated modeling to decipher complex parameter interactions. By employing diverse machine learning algorithms, researchers developed advanced predictive frameworks capable of identifying intricate relationships among critical input parameters and hydrogen solubility. The methodology involved multiple algorithmic techniques, including supervised learning algorithms like artificial neural networks, regression approaches, support vector regression, ensemble methods, and gradient boosting algorithms, each designed to capture nuanced interactions between pressure, salinity, temperature, and hydrogen solubility. Data integrity was ensured using the Monte Carlo outlier detection algorithm to rigorously screen experimental datasets, guaranteeing reliable model training. Performance evaluation utilized comprehensive metrics and visualization techniques to assess predictive accuracy. Empirical results identified CatBoost, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Support Vector Regression as top performers, with CatBoost achieving the greatest forecasting precision, evidenced by an R-squared value of 0.9756 for testing, together with mean squared error of 0.0012 (testing), and mean relative deviation percentages of 57.35% (testing). Sensitivity analyses revealed key insights into hydrogen solubility dynamics, showing positive correlations with temperature and pressure, and a nverse relationship with salinity. SHAP analysis further identified pressure and salinity as the most influential factors. These findings validate the efficacy of sophisticated machine learning techniques and enhance the scientific understanding of hydrogen solubility mechanisms in aqueous systems.Keywords: Hydrogen solubility, Machine learning algorithms, Aqueous systems, SHAP analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the global shift toward sustainability, driven by the need to reduce CO₂ emissions and harness cleaner energy, hydrogen (H₂) has become a key candidate for energy utilization and storage, thanks to its tremendous energy density as well as the possibility of production from renewable resources1. The advent of a hydrogen (H₂)-based economy faces a critical obstacle: the need for safe and efficient H₂ storage solutions2. Both European Commission and International Energy Agency (IEA) have identified development of safe and economical hydrogen (H₂) storage systems as a priority. Report of IEA in 2019 called “Future of Hydrogen: Seizing Today’s Opportunities” offers a detailed assessment of hydrogen’s role as a fuel, emphasizing the need for robust H₂ storage methods to support its widespread integration into energy systems3. In parallel, European Commission’s 2020 Hydrogen Strategy for a Climate-Neutral Europe underscores the importance of significant investments in hydrogen (H₂) storage technologies as a vital part of transition to a climate-neutral future4. As a result, it is crucial to formulate and implement effective strategies to address hydrogen (H₂) storage challenges worldwide5.

Underground hydrogen storage (UHS), employing exhausted sedimentary formations from gas and oil industry, can be viewed as a practical method for achieving large-scale storage of energy globally6 analogous to techniques used in carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS), and carbon capture field7. Salt caverns, especially those formerly utilized for storage of hydrocarbon can be considered major candidates for UHS owing to their substantial storage capacity, quick operational efficiency, and reduced contamination risks. However, their use is restricted by geographical and economic considerations7. Depleted oil and gas reservoirs offer the benefits of mature infrastructure and reliable containment for UHS. Despite this, there are doubts about hydrogen (H₂) interacting with remaining hydrocarbons or reacting with subterranean minerals, potentially impacting storage safety and effectiveness8. Caverns found within hard rock, like granite, are viable candidates for UHS owing to their high capacity and stability. However, they are associated with greater developmental costs and potential leakage risks if not sufficiently sealed9.

Evaluating the feasibility of a underground reservoir for hydrogen storage (UHS) involves analyzing key trapping mechanisms, such as structural, solubility, residual, and mineral trapping. This procedure aligns with the practices used in geological carbon storage (GCS) initiatives10,11. Solubility trapping is regarded as highly efficient, as it involves lower maintenance and monitoring efforts relative to other mechanisms including capillary trapping in which ongoing surveillance is typically essential12. Dissolving hydrogen (H₂) in water offers a promising approach for UHS, as hydrogen solubility in water is virtually 0.179 g/L under conditions of 10 atm pressure and 25 °C (298.15 K) temperature13. This method provides notable benefits over alternative storage solutions, such as superior energy density and reduced costs, underscoring its viability for hydrogen storage14, Precisely foreseeing the dissolvability of hydrogen (H₂) in aqueous solutions is difficult because it depends on several variables, including pressure, temperature, and the presence of dissolved substances15.

The hydrogen solubility in aqueous solutions has been predicted using traditional thermodynamic models, as evidenced by research such as16,17. Classical thermodynamic methods along with laboratory methodologies face significant limitations, including high costs and resource demands for data processing and gathering, which have impeded further research. Machine learning (ML) presents a promising solution for predicting solubility of hydrogen (H₂) in aqueous solutions for UHS. By utilizing prior information, ML models can identify patterns and render estimations based on input variables, enabling a more streamlined and cost-effective methodology18. ML offers a distinct advantage in predicting hydrogen (H₂) solubility by accommodating a wide array of input variables. In contrast, conventional models are often constrained to simplistic equations that focus on a narrow set of parameters, such as temperature and pressure, while ML leverages comprehensive datasets to deliver more accurate and nuanced predictions19, On the contrary, ML methods can integrate additional variables, including the presence and characteristics of other solutes, enhancing the precision and robustness of computations. Recent advancements have used ML to estimate solubility value of gases such as CH₄, CO₂, and H₂ in different mediums, showcasing its potential for diverse applications20–26.

This work aims to construct effective ML algorithms for forecasting solubility of hydrogen in brine solutions under different salinity, pressure, and temperature conditions. A varied collection of algorithms is applied, including linear regression, convolutional and artificial neural networks, lasso regression, ridge regression, support vector regression, elastic net, random forests, gradient boosting, k-nearest neighbors, decision tree, light gradient boosting, extreme boosting, categorical boosting, and Gaussian processes. The Monte Carlo outlier detection (MCOD) algorithm is utilized to verify the databank’s appropriateness for model training. Various performance metrics and visualization methods are used to assess the models, and SHAP values are analyzed to uncover the impact of critical features on solubility predictions. Backgrounds in machine learning

This section proposals brief overviews of the ML methods employed in research.

Convolutional neural network

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) represent a category of deep learning algorithms tailored for handling structured information like pictures. These networks utilize convolutional layers to autonomously and adaptively acquire hierarchical spatial features from input data. These layers detect regional trends such as textures, edges, and shapes by utilizing filters (or kernels) to the input. By employing several successive convolutional layers, CNNs can acquire more intricate and abstract features, allowing them to effectively identify objects, faces, and other visual patterns. Furthermore, pooling layers are commonly used to decrease dimensionality of feature maps, aiding in minimizing computational complexity and mitigating overfitting27,28.

K-Nearest neighbors (KNN)

The K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm is an easy but powerful supervised learning technique applied to regression and classification problems. It functions on the similarity basis, identifying value or class of a point by looking at the most common class or the average value of its (k) nearest neighbors within the feature space29. The parameter (k) in KNN denotes account of nearest neighbors, chosen by user. The method measures distance from target point to every other point in dataset, usually employing metrics like Manhattan, Euclidean, or Minkowski distance. KNN is a non-parametric technique, signifying that it does not make any assumptions about underlying data distribution, allowing it to be flexible and suitable for various data types30.

Artificial neural network

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) represent a robust method in ML and artificial intelligence, drawing inspiration from organization and human brain operation. These networks are made up of tiny processing units known as artificial neurons, which are arranged in several layers. Every a neuron receives inputs, combines them with particular weights, uses an activation function, and generates an output31. The elements of an ANN consist of an input layer, several hidden layers, and an output layer that collaborate to identify intricate patterns in data. ANNs are frequently employed to address different issues like image identification, language processing, and forecasting time series32.

Gaussian processes

Gaussian Processes (GP) represent a robust and adaptable approach in ML for data modeling. These techniques function according to the theory of stochastic functions and, instead of forecasting a precise value, they present a probability distribution across potential functions. In GP, it is believed that the data conform to a multivariate Gaussian distribution, allowing for non-parametric modeling33. An essential aspect of this method is its capacity to offer uncertainty alongside predictions, which makes it perfect for uses like optimization and modeling data that is noisy34.

Extreme gradient boosting

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), commonly referred to as XGBoost, is a sophisticated and well-known ML algorithm that utilizes gradient boosting and decision tree techniques. It integrates several weak models (usually small decision tree) and progressively enhances predictions to produce a robust and precise model. XGBoost utilizes the gradient boosting method, whereby each subsequent model is developed to reduce the residual errors of earlier models35. Tailored for structured data and tasks involving both classification and regression, XGBoost is extremely favored in data mining contests thanks to its exceptional speed and impressive accuracy36.

Categorical boosting

Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), commonly known as CatBoost, is a sophisticated ML algorithm uniquely crafted to manage categorical data. This approach relies on the gradient boosting technique and employs decision trees as its foundational models. A distinctive aspect of CatBoost is its capability to handle categorical data automatically, eliminating the requirement for intricate preparation like one-hot encoding37. By employing methods such as substituting categorical values with target-oriented statistics, CatBoost greatly enhances model precision. It is widely employed in regression and classification activities and is very favored in data science competitions because of its outstanding performance and speed38.

Light gradient boosting machine

Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) is a sophisticated and effective ML method that relies on the gradient boosting method and decision tree. Tailored for managing extensive and intricate datasets, LightGBM utilizes algorithms like Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB) and Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) to improve speed and precision39. By selectively sampling data and decreasing feature dimensions, LightGBM frequently exceeds the performance of other boosting techniques such as XGBoost and CatBoost in numerous situations. It is extensively utilized for classification, regression, and ranking purposes, and is very favored in data science contests because of its rapid speed and considerable precision40.

Gradient boosting machine

GBM is an efficient supervised and widely used ML method based on the boosting technique. This method combines several weak models and gradually improves estimations to achieve an accurate model. In GBM, each new model is trained to decrease residual error of the previous models41. The method uses gradient descent to optimize the error function and is extensively used in regression and classification problems. GBM is known for its capability to model nonlinear data, making it suitable for practical applications such as fraud detection, recommendation systems, and predictive analytics42.

Elastic net

Elastic Net is a merged regression algorithm that merges the advantages of both Lasso and Ridge methods. This approach is especially appropriate for modeling data with multicollinearity (strong correlation) among independent variables. By leveraging both L1 and L2 penalties, Elastic Net conducts feature selection and coefficient shrinkage at the same time43. This characteristic enables Elastic Net to excel over Lasso and Ridge in situations with numerous independent variables, particularly when some of them are closely related44.

Ridge regression

Ridge Regression is a algorithm utilized in regression to solve issue of multicollinearity present among independent variables. By incorporating an L2 penalty into the cost function, this approach reduces the model coefficients and helps avoid overfitting45. The L2 penalty consists of the total of the squared coefficients, which pulls large coefficients nearer to zero without entirely eliminating them. This characteristic enables Ridge Regression to surpass ordinary linear regression, especially when the data shows multicollinearity46.

Support vector regression

Support Vector Regression (SVR) is an effective and commonly-utilized ML algorithm for both regression and classification purposes. It operates by locating a perfect hyperplane that separates datapoint into separate categories47. The aim of SVM is to maximize margin separating the classes, defined as the distance to nearest data points of every class and the hyperplane. This characteristic allows SVM to exceed the performance of various other classification techniques, especially when the data can be separated linearly48.

A remarkable attribute of SVM is its ability to manage non-linear data. Using kernel trick, SVM transforms data into a space with more dimensions where it is possible to separate it linearly. Various kernels, including polynomial, linear, and radial basis function (RBF), can be used for this aim. SVM additionally employs parameter tuning, including modifying the C parameter and using cross-validation to determine the optimal parameters49.

Lasso regression

Lasso Regression is a algorithm utilized in regression to tackle multicollinearity issues between independent variables and to conduct feature selection. This technique incorporates an L1 penalty into the cost function, which diminishes model coefficients and reduces a few of them to zero. This capability allows Lasso Regression to remove less important variables, streamlining the final model and enhancing its interpretability50. Lasso Regression is extensively utilized in regression tasks and is especially appreciated for its robust feature selection abilities in real-world applications like genetics, economics, and predictive analytics51.

Linear regression

Linear Regression is among the most basic utilized techniques in ML. It is utilized to represent the connection among a reliant variable (target) and one or several variables that are independent (features). This approach functions by identifying a straight line that most accurately represents the data, intending to illustrate the linear connection between the variables. The central aim of Linear Regression is to decrease the total of the squared prediction errors (the disparities between actual and estimated values)52. It is widely utilized in regression issues and, because of its straightforwardness and great interpretability, has applications in real-world areas like economics, finance, and social sciences53.

Decision tree

Decision Tree (DT) are commonly employed techniques in ML, suitable for addressing both regression and classification issues. This technique creates a tree-like framework that divides the data into smaller parts, with each node indicating a choice based on one of the features of the data. Every branch of the tree signifies a condition, while each leaf denotes the ultimate result (class or predicted value)54. Because of their straightforwardness and strong interpretability, DT is widely employed in real-world scenarios like medical diagnosis, recommendation systems, and predictive analytics55.

Random forest

Random Forest (RF) is a robust and commonly applied ML method based on DT. By integrating various DTs and utilizing methods like bagging and random feature selection, it establishes a strong and precise model. In RF, every tree is trained separately on a random sample of datapoint (with replacement) and on a random selection of features56. The forecasts from every tree are subsequently combined to generate the ultimate outcome. This approach is widely utilized in regression and classification jobs because of its outstanding performance and ability to prevent overfitting. It is utilized across multiple domains, such as medical diagnosis, recommendation systems, and predictive analytics57.

Database explanation and analysis

The present work focused on estimating solubility of hydrogen (H₂) in aqueous solutions using pressure, salinity, and temperature as key variables. To construct accurate ML models, 255 datapoints were gathered from earlier laboratory studies documented in the literature16,58–65 gathered by seeking ACS, Elsevier, Mendeley and Wiley research repositories with keywords like “H₂ solubility”, “H₂ storage” and “aqueous solution”. This process ensured the compilation of a dataset containing the necessary variables for developing and validating the ML models in this study. The final dataset included H₂ solubility measurements under diverse pressure, temperature, and salinity conditions.

The lab databank was split randomly into three sections: 178 datapoints for training, 38 for testing, and 39 for validation. Key input parameters pressure, temperature, and salinity were selected based on their known influence on hydrogen solubility. The study’s primary output parameter was hydrogen solubility, measuring experimentally obtained values under varying environmental conditions. In this study, the MCOD algorithm was employed to identify and manage outliers in the hydrogen solubility dataset. MCOD uses random sampling and density-based detection to efficiently identify data points that significantly deviate from their surrounding points. Unlike traditional methods, MCOD evaluates a subset of data points, reducing computational complexity while maintaining accuracy in outlier detection. By analyzing local data density, the algorithm effectively ensures data reliability before ML model development.

MCOD’s key advantage is its scalability for high-dimensional databank, enabling efficient outlier detection in large-scale applications. The method uses arbitrary selection to minimize processing demands, balancing efficiency with tolerable precision. While the sampling approach introduces compromises between precision and computing resources, MCOD remains a valuable method for preliminary data examination and outlier identification. Its effectiveness in identifying outliers makes it particularly useful for complex databank like hydrogen solubility measurements.

The advanced ML models CatBoost, SVR, Gradient Boosting, and RF demonstrated exceptional performance in predicting hydrogen solubility. These algorithms exhibited elevated precision, minimal estimation errors, and robust execution throughout various assessment standards. In opposition, Linear Regression, Lasso Regression, and DT models proved less effective, showing poor forecasting abilities and substantial deviations between anticipated and real values. The intricate, non-linear nature of hydrogen solubility appears to be better captured by more sophisticated ensemble and ML techniques.

Conclusion

This research aims to create a varied array of data-driven models utilizing numerous ML techniques, like RF, linear regression, KNN, ridge regression, SVR, CNNs, lasso regression, elastic net, ANNs, LightGBM, XGBoost, GP, GBM, DT, and CatBoost. These models were purposefully developed to forecast hydrogen solubility in water-based systems, using essential input factors like pressure, salinity, and temperature. To evaluate the effectiveness of these models, different assessment metrics and visualization tools were utilized. The MCOD technique was utilized to verify the dataset’s reliability, affirming that most of the data was appropriate for training and validation. The findings indicated that CatBoost, Gradient Boosting, SVR, and RF surpassed the other models, attaining the highest R-squared values and the lowest error metrics, thus confirming their status as the most precise and dependable for estimating hydrogen solubility. Additional examination showed that pressure and temperature positively affected solubility, whereas salinity displayed a negative correlation. These results highlight the capability of the chosen models to accurately represent intricate relationships in the dataset, validating their appropriateness for predictive uses in this domain. Moreover, SHAP values were used to elucidate models, pinpointing pressure and salinity as the key factors influencing hydrogen solubility. This all-encompassing method not only confirms the models’ effectiveness but also delivers enhanced understanding of the critical factors affecting solubility, setting the stage for more precise and interpretable predictions in upcoming studies.
