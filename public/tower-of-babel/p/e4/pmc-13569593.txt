IgG4-Related Hepatic Disease Unmasking Hepatic Actinomycosis
Authors: Anissa Desmoulin, Jasmine Muyard, Laure-Marie Dardaud, Anne-Gaëlle Leroy, Sabine Revuz, Domitille Droz-Bartholet, Domitille Droz-Bartholet, Emeline Leroux, Fanny Andry, Maud Lekieffre, Kévin Diallo, Paul-Elie Gosset
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeHepatic actinomycosis is a rare granulomatous disease caused by an opportunistic Gram-positive bacillus of the genus Actinomyces. We report an unusual association between hepatic actinomycosis and IgG-4 related disease.Case DescriptionA 70-year-old man was admitted to the University Hospital of Reunion Island for asthenia and abdominal pain. Laboratory tests showed hepatic cholestasis and inflammatory reaction without cytolysis. The computer tomography scanner (CT scan) revealed a large hepatic mass in segment IV. IgG4 related disease (IgG4-RD) was diagnosed following liver biopsy. Despite the use of corticosteroids, the lesions progressed. A new biopsy was performed and a PCR test returned positive for Actinomyces spp. We observed patient improvement after 4 months of treatment with amoxicillin and discontinuation of corticosteroids.ConclusionsClinical presentation, imaging characteristics, and diagnostic challenges are strikingly similar between hepatic actinomycosis and IgG4-related disease. However, distinguishing between these two disorders is essential – as the treatment of one may worsen the course of the other. Knowing and understanding the interactions between these two disorders can only be achieved through an integrated and collaborative approach involving various disciplines.

Introduction

Hepatic actinomycosis (HA) is a rare liver disorder caused by Gram-positive bacilli belonging to the genus Actinomyces. Actinomyces israelii, a commensal of the oropharynx, digestive tract and female genital tract, is one of the most commonly implicated species. Several cases of HA have been reported; the majority occur in non-immunocompromised patients [1]. Abdominal involvement accounts for 20% of all cases of actinomycosis, of which 5% are hepatic HA. Its insidious presentation makes it a diagnostic challenge. We report here a new case of association between HA and IgG4 related disease (IgG4-RD).

Case Presentation

A 70-year-old man was referred to the gastroenterology department of the University Hospital of Reunion Island in October 2022 for general deterioration, weight loss (15 kg in 3 months) and abdominal pain. His medical history included gastric adenocarcinoma treated with total gastrectomy, in remission since 1997; ulcerative fungal esophagitis in 1998; a Streptococcus anginosus liver abscess in 2008, managed with percutaneous drainage and a 6-week course of antibiotic therapy, with complete resolution; and right inguinal hernia repair before 2008. Laboratory tests revealed an inflammatory reaction with leukocytes at 9.19 X 109/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) at 123 mg/L and hepatic cholestasis without cytolysis (GGT 577 IU/L, PAL 652 IU/L). A positron emission tomography (PET) scan on July 21, 2023 revealed no hypermetabolic focus suggestive of IgG4-related disease or neoplasia. On September 1, 2023 the patient was free of abdominal pain and laboratory tests were normal: GGT 102 UI/L, PAL 136 UI/L, CRP 1 mg/L, total IgG 10,400 mg/L and IgG4 1.760 g/L. The last MRI scan performed on 4th July, 2025 showed further regression of the lesion.

Discussion

This case highlights the insidious nature of HA. Actinomyces infection generally occurs when the integrity of the digestive mucosa is compromised, often as a result of abdominal or pelvic surgery or the presence of foreign material [1]. HA predominantly affects middle-aged men and presents with nonspecific symptoms, including fever, abdominal pain, and weight loss. As in our case, imaging typically reveals a solitary right hepatic lesion and may closely mimic a malignant tumor [1, 2]. MRI is the preferred modality for characterizing hepatic actinomycosis, typically demonstrating hypointense signals on T1-weighted images and hyperintense signals on T2-weighted images. The infiltrative pattern, often extending to adjacent structures such as the hepatic capsule, bile ducts, peritoneum, or even lung parenchyma, may mimic a neoplastic process, particularly cholangiocarcinoma. Increased uptake on PET has also been reported; however, this finding could not be confirmed in our case, as the PET scan was performed retrospectively [2]. Diagnosis is based on histopathological analysis, usually performed after percutaneous drainage or hepatic lobar resection. Identification of granulomatous inflammation associated with sulfur granules is pathognomonic [2]. Culture is rarely informative due to frequent contamination and the fastidious, slow-growing nature of Actinomyces species. PCR targeting the 16s ribosomal RNA gene or, preferably, the tuf gene, can help confirm the diagnosis, as in our case.

In addition to raising diagnostic uncertainties, this case also highlights the differential diagnosis with IgG4-RD. IgG4-RD is characterized by a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate with an increased proportion of lymphocytes and IgG4-positive plasma cells (> 40% IgG4/IgG plasma cell ratio) [3]. Diagnostic criteria include the histological presence of a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, storiform fibrosis, increased eosinophils, and the non-obligatory presence of obliterative phlebitis. IgG4 immunostaining is essential for diagnosis, and the hepatic threshold is set at more than 10 IgG4-positive cells per high-power field (hpf) [3]. IgG4-RD liver disease typically affects men in the second half of life and presents as a solid mass, most often involving the left hepatic lobe, without biliary tract infiltration, and with elevated serum IgG4 levels. The recommended treatment is oral corticosteroid therapy. Although a fibrohistocytic hepatic lesion may mimic our presentation and partially overlap with the IgG4 spectrum, the absence of histiocytic cells, multinucleated giant cells, neutrophilic infiltrates, and microabscesses, including on the initial biopsy, argues strongly against this diagnosis.

As summarized in Table 1, reported cases share a remarkably similar profile, predominantly affecting older men with constitutional symptoms and liver masses mimicking malignancy. Lack of response or clinical worsening following appropriately administered glucocorticoid therapy constitutes a strong argument for exclusion and was consistently observed across reported cases (Table 1) [4].

In our case, it is difficult to determine whether HA was secondary to IgG4-related disease or whether it was pre-existing and subsequently revealed by corticosteroid therapy. The IgG4-rich infiltrate may represent a reactive response to infection, as previously suggested in chronic infectious diseases, potentially driven by a Th2-mediated immune response promoting eosinophilia, elevated IgG4 levels, and fibrosis [5].

Available data support initial parenteral antibiotic therapy followed by oral treatment, with duration guided by clinical, biological, and radiological response, typically for 3–6 months [1].

The origin of the infection remains uncertain. Multiple dental abscesses support a possible oral source. Although recurrence at the same site is intriguing, a normal liver MRI in 2012 makes iatrogenic inoculation during abscess drainage in 2008 unlikely. Similarly, inoculation of Actinomyces spp. during the initial liver biopsy cannot be completely excluded but appears improbable.

Conclusion

This is a new case describing an association between IgG4-RD and hepatic actinomycosis. These two entities share overlapping clinical and radiological features thereby posing a significant diagnostic challenge. Accurate diagnosis requires a multimodal approach integrating clinical evaluation, imaging, and histopathological analysis.

This case highlights three key issues: (i) the importance of systematically consider an infectious etiology in presence of an infiltrative and progressive liver lesion, (ii) the need to systematically send samples for anatomopathological and specific bacteriological analysis, and (iii) the necessity for a better understanding of the interplay between infection and IgG4-RD.
