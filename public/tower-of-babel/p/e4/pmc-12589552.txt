Inhibition of cytotoxic fibrillation of human insulin using silver nanoparticles capped by aqueous extract of Echium amoenum
Authors: Sedigheh Keramati, Somayeh Ahmadi, Mozhgan Omidi, Foad Rezaeifar, Mohammad Bagher Shahsavani, Ali Akbar Meratan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIranian Borage (Echium amoenum) is a medicinal plant with a wide spectrum of therapeutic properties, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects, which are commonly used in Iranian folk medicine for the treatment of sore throat, coughing, pneumonia, anxiety, and depression. In the present study, the capacity of aqueous extract of Iranian Borage (AEIB) to inhibit cytotoxic fibrillation of human insulin in vitro is investigated for the first time. Using LC–MS analysis, we identified caffeic acid and m-geranyl-p-dihydroxybenzoic acid as two main phenols and methylated derivatives of kaempferol and luteolin as the main flavonoids presented in AEIB. The impact of AEIB on the amyloid fibrillation and cytotoxicity of human insulin was examined using ThT fluorescence assay, circular dichroism spectroscopy, fluorescence microscopy, MTT-based cell viability assay, intracellular ROS content and mitochondrial membrane potential measurements, and hemolysis assay. The obtained results indicate that AEIB can significantly inhibit the amyloid assembly and cytotoxicity of human insulin. Based on the seeding experiments, we propose that binding to and stabilizing monomeric/partially unfolded species may be the main mechanism by which AEIB modulates fibrillogenesis of human insulin. Moreover, we found that green synthesis of metal-based nanoparticles is an effective strategy to improve the anti-amyloidogenic effects of AEIB. Molecular docking studies indicate the binding of two main constituents of AEIB to monomeric form of human insulin. Given significant neuroprotective effects of AEIB and its effectiveness in treatment of several neuropsychiatric disorders, and according to the obtained results, we believe that AEIB has the potency to be considered as a novel therapeutic drug for future research associated with neurodegenerative diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22374-8.Keywords: Echium amoenum, Amyloid fibrils, Cytotoxicity, Human insulin, Green synthesis of nanoparticlesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemical biology, Neuroscience, Plant sciences

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases cover a wide spectrum of age-related disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, characterized by the slow and progressive loss of neurons in the central and peripheral nervous systems. Despite diverse manifestations of neurodegenerative diseases1,2, misfolding and deposition of specific peptides and proteins into toxic aggregates with a fibrillar morphology in various regions of nervous system is a common feature shared by these diseases3,4. Accumulation of these amyloidogenic species can induce neuronal cell death through several mechanisms such as mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, impairment of proteasome/lysosome-dependent clearance systems, and neuroinflammation1. Accordingly, more recent interventions have focused on the identification or development of compounds that inhibit formation of amyloidogenic species, among them naturally-occurring polyphenols have attracted a great amount of interest5, due to the ease of accessibility, low cost and toxicity, particularly at low concentrations.

Medicinal herbs are a source of natural polyphenols and by delivering considerable amounts of polyphenols, the high intake of these plants has been linked to the decreased risk of neurodegenerative and chronic diseases caused by oxidative stress6. Previous studies have demonstrated that extracts from certain medicinal plants such as Ginkgo biloba7, Rosa damascena8, Allium roseum9, garlic extract10, and many others11–14 are potent inhibitors of amyloid fibrillation and related cytotoxicity. The significant effectiveness of herbal extracts may be attributed to the synergy among the various compounds present in the extract, although experimentally demonstrating these synergistic effects is challenging.

Due to its great climate diversity and geographic conditions, Iran has a rich and diverse cultural heritage and numerous species of medicinal plants grow in this country15 Iranian Borage (Echium amoenum Fisch. & C.A.Mey), known as Gol-Gavzaban, is one of the most important medicinal herbs in Iran with a range of therapeutic effects, including antiviral, antimicrobial, analgesic, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, antidiabetic, and antidepressant, reported by in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies16,17. Evaluation of the chemical composition of Borage indicates the presence of several types of bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, anthocyanidins, alkaloids, many types of unsaturated fatty acids, carotenoids, metal ions, and vitamins16. The neuroprotective effects of Borage have been substantiated by numerous experimental and clinical studies [reviewed in 18]. Regarding neurodegenerative diseases, the results obtained by Sadeghi et al.19,20 indicate the protective effects of Borage extract in animal models of Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease.

Despite the existing body of research, no studies have yet investigated the potential of Iranian Borage extract to inhibit cytotoxic amyloid fibril formation, a pathological hallmark common to various neurodegenerative and systemic amyloidosis diseases. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to assess the efficacy of the aqueous extract of Iranian Borage (AEIB) in modulating the fibrillogenesis and cytotoxicity of human insulin, a model protein widely used in amyloid research. Building on our previous findings21, we also explored the influence of green-synthesized silver nanoparticles on enhancing the anti-amyloidogenic properties of AEIB, potentially offering a synergistic approach to mitigate amyloid aggregation. Results and discussion

Considering their numerous therapeutic properties and lighter side effects, the use of medicinal plants during the past decade have gained more attention compared to synthetic chemical compounds. Clearly, the procedure and solvent used for the preparation of plant extracts can significantly influence the chemical composition and biological activities of medicinal plants extracts. In the case of Echium amoenum, the results reported by Pilerood and Prakash22 indicate that phenolic compounds of Echium amoenum are significantly soluble in water and that the aqueous extract of plant shows highest antioxidant activity. In order to mimic the method used by Iranian people to prepare Echium amoenum tea, extraction was performed using water and under indirect heat, as described under materials and methods.

Identification of the AEIB chemical constituents by LC–MS

It is well-known that plant extracts contain a wide variety of active compounds with significant reductive activity which can be used for green synthesis of metal nanoparticles23. To identify and qualify bioactive compounds of AEIB, we employed Liquid Chromatography − Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis. The compounds detected by LC–MS include six derivatives of phenols (caffeic acid, 3,4-dimethoxycinnamic acid, dihydroferulic acid, m-geranyl-p-hydroxybenzoic acid, syringaldehyde, and coumaroyl hydroxyagmatine), two derivatives of flavonoids (luteolin 7,3,4-trimethyl ether and kaempferol 3,7,4-trimethyl ether), and two acids (lithospermic acid B and caffeoyl shikimate) (Table 1 and Fig. 2). Among them two phenolic acids, including caffeic acid and m-geranyl-p-dihydroxybenzoic acid, are main constituents of AEIB followed by two methylated derivatives of kaempferol and luteolin polyphenols (Fig. 2). Interestingly, all compounds identified by LC–MS have reported in previous studies24, indicating that AEIB is a rich source of bioactive phenolic components.

Characterization of AEIB-capped AgNPs

The strategy used for the synthesis of Ag nanoparticles (AgNPs) is based on the reductive capacity of AEIB leading to reduction of Ag+ to Ago and consequent clustering of silver ions covered by polyphenolic compounds21,25,26. First, to confirm the formation of AEIB-capped AgNPs, the UV–Vis absorption spectrum of AEIB before and after nanonization was recorded. Similar to previous reports27, we observed a main peak between 280 to 290 nm, and a shoulder at wavelengths above 300 nm (Fig. 3A), characteristics of various derivatives of phenolic compounds and polyphenols, respectively28. Upon formation of AEIB-capped AgNPs, the intensity of AEIB absorption spectrum decreased with the appearance of a new peak at around 400 nm (Fig. 3A), indicating the presence of silver in the structure of nanoparticles29. This pattern of absorption changes is similar to those reported by Mirzaei-Behbahani et al.21, indicating that the synthesis of AEIB-capped AgNPs has been carried out successfully. Figure 3B shows the fluorescence emission spectra of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs excited at 280 nm, indicating that both compounds show fluorescence properties. The lower fluorescence emission of AEIB-capped AgNPs may be attributed to the structural changes/fusion of polyphenols during nanonization process. These modifications are proposed to reduce the number of ordered sp2 domains, which are responsible for the absorption and fluorescence properties of polyphenols30. The functional groups present in the structure of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs were identified using fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Fig. 3C). The characteristic peaks for AEIB are as follow: a broad peak at 3000–3500 with maximum intensity at 3263 (O–H in hydroxyl and carboxylic groups), and sharp peaks at 1595 (aromatic C=C and C–O) and 1027 (C–H). Similarly, for the AEIB-capped AgNPs all these peaks were characterized as follow: a peak at 3215 (O–H in hydroxyl and carboxylic groups) and sharp peaks at 1581 (aromatic C=C and C–O) and 1346 (C–H) (Fig. 3C). The FTIR results indicate that nanonization has not significantly alter the functional groups presented in the structure of AEIB. The thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) was employed to examine the thermal stability of AEIB upon formation of nanoparticles. As depicted in Fig. 3D, the slope of weight loss, particularly in temperatures above 320 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of polyphenolic and aromatic structures30,31, significantly decreased in AEIB-capped AgNPs. For instance, at 800 °C the amount of weight loss for AEIB and their corresponding nanoparticles was 73% and 55%, respectively (Fig. 3D), an indicative of the increased thermal stability of AEIB-capped AgNPs. The formation of AEIB-capped AgNPs was further confirmed by size distribution measurement and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The dynamic light scattering (DLS) data clearly shows a narrow size distribution for both AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs (Figs. 3E and F). Clearly, upon nanonization the pattern of AEIB size distribution shifted from an average diameter of 16.79 nm to larger sizes (with an average diameter of 88.62 nm), an indicative of nanoparticle formation. Finally, to examine the morphological features of AEIB-capped AgNPs, TEM analysis was carried out. As depicted in Fig. 4, the synthesized AEIB-capped AgNPs exhibit a spherical morphology with diameters ranging from 10 to 50 nm, consistent with our recent reports21. In contrast to the size distribution results which indicate a narrow population of nanoparticles (Figs. 3E and F), TEM images show relative heterogeneity in the size of nanoparticles (Fig. 4). A possible explanation for this observation may be related to the method used for the preparation of samples; where dropping of sample on the surface of grid and letting to dry may promote the self-aggregation of nanoparticles. According to the LC–MS, UV–Vis spectroscopy, TGA, size distribution measurement, and TEM images we can conclude that the extraction of Iranian Borage polyphenols and synthesis of AEIB-capped AgNPs are successfully performed.

The polyphenolic content and antioxidant activity of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs

Since the strategy used for the synthesis of nanoparticles is based on the reductive effects of Borage polyphenols21,25,26, which can modulate their functional groups and consequently their antioxidant features, the total phenol/flavonoid content and antioxidant activity of AEIB and their respective nanoparticles were determined. As shown in Table 2, AEIB contains considerable amounts of phenols and flavonoids. Nanonization, caused some changes in the content of phenols and flavonoids of AEIB. While we observed a decrease in the concentration of total phenol content (TPC) presented in the structure of AEIB-capped AgNPs, the amount of total flavonoid content (TFC) increased upon nanonization (Table 2). A possible explanation for these observations may be related to the higher capacity of flavonoids (containing more aromatic rings and functional groups) to incorporate into the structure of nanoparticles. However, further detailed studies are needed to confirm this proposition. This data suggests that nanonization has minimal, if any, impact on the TPC and TFC of nanoparticles, indicating that AEIB-capped AgNPs retain a diverse array of polyphenols present in the AEIB (Fig. 2 and Table 1). There are several reports indicating that many biological and neuroprotective effects of natural polyphenols are related to their antioxidant features32–34. Thus, the possible impact of nanonization on the antioxidant activity of AEIB was examined using a DPPH-based antioxidant assay. The results presented in Fig. 5 clearly indicate a dose-dependent antioxidant activity for both AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs, when compared to ascorbic acid as a natural antioxidant. Interestingly, nanonization enhanced the antioxidant properties of AEIB, which is in accord with previous reports21,35,36. We believe that the higher antioxidant capacity of AEIB-capped AgNPs may be attributed to their higher surface/volume ratio, which may promote more efficient interaction of nanoparticles with DPPH• radicals. A possible explanation for the decreased antioxidant activity of AEIB at higher concentrations may be related to the self-assembly and aggregation of polyphenols presented in AEIB, which can decrease their capacity to act as effective antioxidant. We can conclude that, nanonization not only haven’t had no significant effect on the TPC/TFC of AEIB, but have increased their antioxidant properties, suggesting that these nanoparticles may be suitable candidates for our subsequent experiments.

The anti-amyloidogenic effects of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs

In the next step, the anti-amyloidogenic effect of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs was examined. For this purpose, thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence measurement as a common assay for monitoring the kinetics of amyloid fibril formation and detection of amyloid fibrils was employed. Figure 6A and Figure S1A show the impact of AEIB on the amyloid fibrillation of human insulin indicating a decrease in the fluorescence emission intensity of ThT along with the prolongation of nucleation phase (Table 3). Treatment of protein samples with 150 µg/mL AEIB increased the lag time from 5.36 to 19.14 h. In samples incubated with the increasing concentrations of AEIB-capped AgNPs, the extent of inhibition was even more (Fig. 6B and Figure S1B). A significant and dose-dependent decrease in the ThT fluorescence intensity along with a considerable increase in the lag time of protein fibrillation kinetics were observed in samples containing various concentrations of AEIB-capped AgNPs. For instance, treatment of protein with 150 μg/mL AEIB-capped AgNPs caused about 80% decrease in the ThT fluorescence intensity with prolongation of the nucleation phase from 5.36 to 28.57 h (Fig. 6B and Table 3). According to the ThT fluorescence results we may conclude that binding to and stabilizing monomeric/partially unfolded species (resulting in prolongation of nucleation phase) and/or targeting prefibrillar species produced during the growth phase (resulting in decreasing ThT fluorescence intensity) may be the possible mechanisms by which AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs modulate the amyloid fibrillation process of human insulin. To approve this hypothesis and to reach a conclusion about the possible mechanism of action, the effect of seed addition on the inhibitory effects of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs was examined. As shown in Figs. 6C and D, the addition of seeds converted the sigmoidal curve of insulin amyloid fibrillation to a hyperbolic curve. This exponential growth of amyloid fibrils can be related to the accelerating effect of seeds acting as secondary nuclei which can promote the assembly process of insulin37. The presence of seeds considerably diminished the inhibitory effects of both AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs; the inhibition percentage was decreased from about 39% to 11% and from about 80% to 23% in samples containing the highest concentrations of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs, respectively (Table 3). Accordingly, we may conclude that elimination of monomeric/partially unfolded species as potential targets of AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs, through bypassing the nucleation phase upon addition of seeds, have significantly decreased the anti-amyloidogenic effects of compounds. This observation suggests that while AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs may have the ability to interact with both monomeric/partially unfolded species and prefibrillar aggregates (according to Figs. 6A and B), their tendency for binding to the species populated in the nucleation phase is likely higher than those produced in the course of growth phase. Accordingly, stabilization of monomers and/or prevention of structural changes required for promoting fibril formation may be the main mechanism by which AEIB and, in particularly, AEIB-capped AgNPs modulate the fibrillation process of human insulin.

To gain insight into the secondary structural transition of human insulin in the absence and presence of AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs, far-UV circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was performed. Incubation of human insulin under amyloidogenic conditions led to the appearance of a negative peak at around 218 nm (Fig. 6), indicative of β-structure as the main constituents of amyloid fibrils. Although the presence of either AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs led to a marked decrease in the intensity of negative peak at 218 nm, the structural transitions were only inhibited in sample incubated with AEIB-capped AgNPs (Fig. 6F). This finding indicates the higher capacity of nanoparticles in stabilizing of monomeric species, which is in accord with ThT results (Fig. 6A and B). Growing body of evidence demonstrates that hydrophobic interactions are main driving forces of protein aggregation38–42, and enhancement in the exposure of hydrophobic patches in the course of amyloid fibrillation is a characteristic feature of soluble oligomers populated during the initial steps (the end of nucleation phase and the beginning of growth phase) of fibrillation process of proteins43. Therefore, if stabilization of native structure of human insulin and/or preventing the structural/conformational changes required for the exposure of hydrophobic regions be the possible mechanism of action of AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs, we can expect that the presence of these compounds leads to the following events: (i) a marked decrease in the surface hydrophobicity of amyloidogenic species and (ii) formation of structures with an amorphous/non-fibrillar morphology instead of a fibrillar structure. To test these propositions, we employed Nile red (NR) and ThT fluorescence microscopy to examine the changes on the surface hydrophobicity of amyloidogenic species in the course of fibrillation and to observe the morphology of produced structures. Figure 7A shows changes on the surface hydrophobicity of protein samples incubated with various concentrations of AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs. While treatment with either AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs caused a significant and dose dependent decrease in the fluorescence intensity of NR, AEIB-capped AgNPs were more effective. In samples treated with 150 μg/mL AEIB-capped AgNPs we didn’t observe any significant fluorescence signal, suggesting that the exposure of hydrophobic patches has significantly diminished. As indicated in Fig. 7B, the incubation of human insulin under amyloidogenic condition have resulted in the formation of amyloid fibrils. In samples incubated with AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs, however, the formation of such fibrillar structures completely inhibited, and instead, amorphous aggregates with a spherical morphology were appeared (Fig. 7B). Clearly, in both NR and ThT fluorescence images, we observed that AEIB-capped AgNPs were more effective than AEIB in preventing the exposure of hydrophobic surfaces and formation amyloid fibrils, which is in accordance with fibrillation kinetics data (Figs. 6A and B). The higher efficacy of AEIB-capped AgNPs in modulating the assembly process of human insulin is correlated with the surface-assistance model reported by previous studies30,31,44–46.

The cell protective and anti-hemolytic effects of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs

The results presented in Fig. 8A indicate a considerable decrease in the viability of cells treated with 20 μM human insulin fibrils for 24 h. The presence of either AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs caused a dose-dependent increase in the viability of treated cells. However, AEIB-capped AgNPs were more effective in mitigating cytotoxicity associated with amyloid fibrils; we observed 93% viability in cells treated with protein samples containing the highest concentration of AEIB-capped AgNPs (Fig. 8A). According to the results reported by numerous studies, interaction with and permeabilization of biological membranes47–49 and induction of an oxidative status by promoting ROS production50–54 are proposed as two main mechanisms by which amyloid aggregates cause cell death. Regarding the membrane permeabilizing activity of amyloid aggregates, while both electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions appear to be important in amyloid-membrane interaction, the latter is a key factor determining the toxicity of amyloid fibrils47–49. Accordingly, we suggest that preventing the exposure of hydrophobic regions in protein samples treated with tested compounds (Fig. 7A) have decreased the potency of amyloidogenic species to effectively interact with SH-SY5Y cells membrane and cause cell death. To confirm this hypothesis, we examined the membrane permeabilizing effects of incubated samples using human erythrocytes as an in vitro biological model. First, we examined the hemolytic activity of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs. As shown in Figure S2, neither AEIB nor AEIB-capped AgNPs caused significant permeabilization of erythrocytes membrane even when they applied at concentrations up to 200 µg/mL. As depicted in Fig. 8B, treatment of erythrocytes with 20 µM human insulin fibrils resulted in a significant membrane permeabilization measured by the release of hemoglobin. However, a dose-dependent decrease in the extent of membrane permeabilization was observed in samples containing AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs (Fig. 8B). This observation means that the protein aggregates produced in the presence of either AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs have completely lost their hemolytic activity.

To determine the oxidative damage of insulin amyloid fibrils on SH-SY5Y cells and evaluate the protective effects of AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs, the effect of incubated proteins on reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and mitochondrial membrane potential of SH-SY5Y cells was determined. From the results provided in Fig. 9, we can conclude that treatment of SH-SY5Y cells with 20 µM insulin amyloid fibrils has led to a significant oxidative stress manifested by increased levels of intracellular ROS and a considerable loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. These results are in accord with previous reports indicating the increased production of ROS as a mechanism by which amyloid aggregates cause cell death50–54. In protein aggregates produced in the presence of AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs, however, we observed a significant and dose-dependent decreased in both ROS content and mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 9), suggesting that oxidative stress mediated by human insulin amyloid fibrils has been considerably inhibited in samples containing these compounds. The improved efficacy of AEIB in the form of nanoparticle in attenuating ROS production and membrane potential loss may be related to the higher antioxidant activity of AEIB-capped AgNPs (Fig. 5). These results are in accordance with previous reports indicating that both anti-amyloidogenic and antioxidant activities of polyphenols are responsible for their capacity to diminish cytotoxicity of amyloid aggregates55–58.

Evaluating the interaction of two main constituents of AEIB with human insulin by molecular docking

Molecular docking simulation was carried out to determine the putative binding sites and gain atomic insights on the intermolecular interactions occurring between two main constituents of AEIB and monomer of human insulin. The docking results corresponding to the binding energy scores, binding mode, and amino acids involved in the interaction of ligands with monomer of human insulin are provided in Table 4, Fig. 10 and Figure S3. The best binding energy score of -4.83 kcal mol−1 was obtained for the m-geranyl-p-hydroxybenzoic acid compared to − 4.26 kcal mol−1 for the caffeic acid (Table 4), indicating the stronger affinity of this ligand to bind human insulin monomer. As shown in Fig. 10, the preferred putative interacting site for both ligands seem to be the (B1-B18) amino acid residues in the chain B, which has been shown to play a critical role in the amyloid fibrillation of protein59. The ligands are mainly surrounded by aromatic Phe1 and hydrophobic Val2, Leu13, Leu17, and Val18 residues. The obtained results indicate the binding of two main constituents of AEIB to the aggregation prone region of human insulin located in the chain B of protein, which may provide evidence for the anti-amyloidogenic effects of AEIB.

Taken together, the presented results indicate the potential of AEIB to be considered as an inhibitor of amyloid fibrillation and cytotoxicity associated with various peptides and proteins and suggest green synthesis-based nanonization as an efficient strategy to improve the anti-amyloidogenic and cytoprotective effects of natural polyphenols. We believe that our work has characteristics that distinguish it from the others. First, the extraction of polyphenols was performed using a routine and simple method and nanoparticles were prepared using an inexpensive and eco-friendly procedure. Second, the produced nanoparticles are water-soluble with an appropriate size (10–50 nm), without limitations such as poor water solubility that greatly restrict therapeutic application of natural polyphenols. Third, the preliminary results show the higher efficacy of AEIB-capped AgNPs, compared to AEIB, in attenuating oxidative damage induced by hydrogen peroxide in rats (unpublished data). However, it is worth noting that there are some limitations which need to be addressed in our future research. The main limitation of the present study is that we used a mixture of phenolic compounds (identified by LC–MS analysis) and due to instrumental limitations, we were unable to purify the main constituent of AEIB. Moreover, to examine their applicability in relation to amyloid-related diseases, we need to evaluate the anti-amyloidogenic and cytoprotective effects of AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs using peptides and proteins associated with neurodegenerative diseases such as amyloid β and α-synuclein.

Conclusion

In this study, we report for the first time the efficacy of AEIB in modulating amyloid assembly and mitigating the cytotoxicity of human insulin fibrils. We propose that the anti-amyloidogenic and protective effects of AEIB may be attributed to the presence of a diverse array of phenolic compounds, such as caffeic acid and hydroxybenzoic acid derivatives, alongside various flavonoids, including kaempferol and luteolin, which are recognized for their anti-amyloidogenic and antioxidant properties60–69. Additionally, we observed that the green synthesis of AgNPs using AEIB significantly enhanced its inhibitory effects, suggesting that this approach represents an efficient strategy to augment the anti-amyloidogenic potential of plant extracts rich in reductive polyphenols. Based on ThT kinetics data and NR/ThT fluorescence imaging, we conclude that AEIB, and particularly AEIB-capped AgNPs, preferentially target protein species including monomers, partially unfolded intermediates, and soluble small oligomers that predominate during the initial stages of amyloid fibrillation. Upon binding, these compounds redirect the human insulin fibrillation pathway toward the formation of amorphous aggregates characterized by reduced solvent-exposed hydrophobic regions and diminished membrane permeabilizing and oxidative activities (Fig. 11). Given the established neuroprotective effects of Iranian Borage and its therapeutic potential in managing neuropsychiatric disorders such as anxiety and depression18, combined with the findings presented herein, we propose that AEIB holds promise as a novel therapeutic and protective agent for amyloid-related diseases.

A schematic presentation of possible mechanism by which AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs modulate amyloid fibrillation of human insulin. According to the obtained results we propose that AEIB, and with higher affinity AEIB-capped AgNPs, bind to monomers, partially unfolded intermediates, and soluble oligomers populated during the nucleation phase of amyloid fibrillation. The binding of AEIB/AEIB-capped AgNPs significantly alters the surface structural features of amyloidogenic species leading to redirection of human insulin assembly process toward the formation of amorphous aggregates with reduced surface hydrophobicity and diminished membrane permeabilizing and oxidative activities.

Materials and methods

Preparation of aqueous extract of Iranian Borage

The flowers of Iranian Borage were collected in May 2023 from the natural habitats of Anijdan village (Babol, Iran), under permission of the village residents. The plant samples were identified by Dr. Hossein Batooli (Isfahan Research Center of Natural Sources and Agriculture, Kashan Station, Kashan, Iran) and confirmed by comparison with the voucher specimen (IBRC P1006721) deposited in the Herbarium of National Botanical Garden of Iran. To prepare the AEIB, 3 g of flowers was powdered using a blender and added in 50 mL deionized water followed by mixing for 15 min at room temperature. The mixture was heated in an oil bath at 65 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 30 min21. The obtained supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, dried at 65 °C and kept at 4 °C for subsequent experiments (Figure S4A).

Liquid chromatography − mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis

The chemical constituents of AEIB were characterized using LC–MS analysis, performed on a Micromass Quattro Micro API mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Briefly, 5 µL of AEIB (adjusted to a final concentration of 10 µg/mL) was injected into a C18 column (4.6 × 120 mm, particle size 5 µm). Elution was carried out using a binary gradient comprising solvent A (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (water with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient profile transitioned from 10:90 (A:B) to 80:20 over 20 min, then returned to 10:90 over the subsequent 5 min, totaling a 25-min run70. The chromatographic separation was conducted at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and a column temperature of 35 °C. Mass spectrometry detection was optimized with a capillary voltage of 1.8 kV, a probe voltage of 3.5 kV, and a curved desolvation line (CDL) voltage of 20 V. High-purity Grade 5 nitrogen gas served as the nebulizer, delivered at a flow rate of 200 L/h. Data acquisition and processing were performed using LabSolutions™ software.

Green synthesis of AgNPs using AEIB

The green synthesis of AgNPs was performed according to our recent report with slight modifications21. Briefly, 5 mL of AEIB (20 mg/mL) was slowly added to 100 mL of 1 mM silver nitrate solution, containing 20 μM cysteine, while being stirred at room temperature. Upon addition of AEIB, the color of solution changed from colorless to light brown, and then to dark brown (upon adjustment of the solution pH to 11 using NaOH and incubating in an oil bath for 30 min at 65 °C), indicating nanoparticle formation (Figure S4). The solution was centrifuged at 22,000 rpm for 30 min at room temperature and the resultant pellet, corresponding to AEIB-capped AgNPs was collected, dried, and kept for subsequent experiments (Figure S4B).

Characterization of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs

To investigate the absorption and fluorescence properties of AEIB and to verify the synthesis of AEIB-capped AgNPs, a range of spectroscopic and microscopic techniques, including UV–Vis, fluorescence, and FTIR spectroscopies, TGA, DLS measurement, and TEM were employed.

Determination of the phenol and flavonoid contents and antioxidant activity of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs

The TPC of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, described by McDonald et al.71, with slight modifications. The TFC was determined based on the aluminum chloride colorimetry method described by Chang et al.72, with slight modifications. To measure the antioxidant activity of AEIB and to evaluate the possible effect of nanonization, a DPPH-based antioxidant assay was employed73.

In vitro induction of human insulin amyloid fibril formation

Human insulin was dissolved in 50 mM glycine buffer (pH 2.2) in a final concentration of 1.5 mg/mL. Dry powders of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs were dissolved in deionized water at a final concentration of 10 mg/mL. All stocks were stored at − 20 °C until use. The induction of human insulin amyloid fibrillation was performed according to our recent report21. For NR fluorescence microscopy, proteins were incubated alone or with various concentrations of AEIB or AEIB-capped AgNPs as same as the procedure described above.

Toxicity assays

Human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells, obtained from Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran), were cultured in DMEM medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and penicillin (100 U/mL). The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and were allowed to attach to the plate for 24 h before treatment. All cellular experiments, including MTT-based cell viability assay, intracellular ROS content and mitochondrial membrane potential measurements, and hemolysis assay were performed according to the procedure described by Mirzaei-Behbahani, et al.21. Further information on the spectroscopic and microscopic characterization of AEIB and AEIB-capped AgNPs, assays for total phenol and flavonoids contents and antioxidant activity, ThT fluorescence assay, seeding experiment, CD spectroscopy, NR fluorescence microscopy, cell viability assay, cellular ROS content and mitochondrial membrane potential measurement, hemolysis assay, and molecular docking experiments is provided in Supplementary information.

Statistical analysis

All assays were performed two or three times with triplicate repeats. Data are expressed as percentage of values in untreated control cells, and each value represents the mean ± SD (n = 3). An unpaired Student’s t-test was utilized to calculate the statistical significance where *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, significantly different from control and #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, significantly different from those exposed only to human insulin amyloid fibrils.
