Smoking, Alcohol Use, and Low Back Pain: A Large-Scale Study Identifying a Synergistic Lifestyle Risk
Authors: Sheng-Hao Chang, Szu-Chia Chen, Jiun-Hung Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignAnalytical Cross-sectional Study.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between smoking exposure and the prevalence of low back pain, and to evaluate whether this association varies by alcohol consumption in a large Taiwanese population.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed 22,471 adults aged 30–70 years from the Taiwan Biobank. Smoking exposure, alcohol consumption, and low back pain were ascertained using standardized questionnaires. Multivariable binary logistic regression models were employed to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for low back pain, controlling for demographic and comorbidities. Interaction between smoking and alcohol consumption was assessed through stratified analyses.ResultsAfter multivariable adjustment, smoking exposure was independently associated with a higher prevalence of low back pain (OR 1.15; 95% CI 1.06 to1.24). Alcohol consumption alone was not significantly associated with low back pain. A significant interaction between smoking and alcohol use was observed. Smoking was associated with low back pain among participants without alcohol exposure (OR 1.12; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.22), whereas an association was noted among those with alcohol exposure (OR 1.40; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.83).ConclusionsSmoking was associated with low back pain, with a potential interaction with alcohol use. However, the effect size was modest, and the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a highly prevalent musculoskeletal disorder worldwide and represents a leading cause of disability. Global burden estimates show that in 2020 over half a billion people were affected by LBP, which remains the top contributor to years lived with disability (YLDs) globally. 1 The public health impact is immense – LBP often leads to activity limitations, work absenteeism, and substantial healthcare utilization costs.2,3 In Taiwan, LBP poses a similarly significant burden: a large population-based survey reported about 25–26% of adults experiencing LBP within a 3-month period. 4 This pervasive prevalence and the associated disability underscore LBP as a major health concern both globally and in Taiwanese society, straining healthcare systems and quality of life.

Smoking and alcohol consumption, two common lifestyle behaviors, are widely recognized for their detrimental health effects, and emerging evidence implicates them in LBP pathophysiology. Cigarette smoking, in particular, has been associated with an elevated risk of LBP. Biologically, smoking may contribute to LBP through several mechanisms: nicotine-induced vasoconstriction impairs blood flow to spinal structures, tobacco toxins accelerate intervertebral disc degeneration and annular damage, and smoking fosters systemic inflammation and oxidative stress that can sensitize nociceptive pathways. 5 Epidemiological studies consistently report that smokers have higher odds of developing LBP and more severe or chronic back pain; Kawabata et al confirmed a significant association between smoking and LBP incidence and persistence, with a clear dose-response relationship. 6 In contrast, the impact of alcohol consumption on LBP is less definitive. While moderate alcohol use has not shown a strong link to LBP in most studies, heavy drinking or alcohol use disorder may contribute indirectly to poor spine health. A systematic review found only a slight overall association between alcohol intake and LBP, which was mainly evident in those with alcohol dependence and chronic LBP conditions. 7 Notably, one Mendelian randomization analysis found robust genetic evidence supporting a causal role of smoking in LBP, whereas it detected no significant causal effect of alcohol consumption on LBP. 8 These findings highlight smoking as a likely risk factor for LBP and raise the question of whether concomitant alcohol use might modulate this risk. The current study therefore focuses on both smoking and drinking habits, and their interaction, in relation to LBP.

Despite the above insights, there remain important knowledge gaps regarding lifestyle factors and LBP, particularly in non-Western populations. Most epidemiological research on LBP risk factors has been conducted in North America, Europe, or other Western regions. 9 Relatively few studies have examined in detail how smoking or alcohol use correlates with LBP in East Asian populations, and virtually none have assessed the combined effects of these two exposures on LBP outcomes. To address this gap, our study aims to investigate the independent and joint associations of smoking and alcohol consumption with low back pain in a large East Asian (Taiwanese) cohort. Using comprehensive multivariable models to control confounding factors, we seek to determine whether smoking and drinking are associated with increased LBP prevalence and whether their interaction is associated with differences in LBP prevalence. The findings from this research will provide novel evidence on modifiable lifestyle contributors to LBP in an East Asian context, with implications for public health prevention strategies and clinical risk factor management.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We obtained data from the Taiwan Biobank (TWB) for participants enrolled between 2008 and 2020. The TWB cohort consists of adults aged 30–70 years without a prior history of cancer. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment, permitting questionnaire collection, physical examinations, and laboratory testing.10,11 Individuals lacking valid information on low back pain status were excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 22,471 participants, including individuals both with and without low back pain. Participants were categorized according to smoking exposure status (non-smoking vs. smoking) for subsequent analyses, and those without low back pain served as the comparison group in the regression models (Figure 1). Baseline variables included age, body mass index (BMI), sex, marital status, educational level, bone mineral density, physical activity, alcohol consumption, and medical histories of arthritis, kidney stones, sciatica, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus (DM), and depression. 12 Figure 1.Study participants were classified by the presence of smoking exposure

Smoking Assessments

Smoking status was determined during standardized interviews by asking, “Do you ever or currently smoke?” Participants who answered yes were categorized into the smoking exposure group, whereas all remaining participants were classified as non-smoking exposure.

Low Back Pain Assessments

Between December 2008 and November 2020, participants in the TWB completed a detailed baseline questionnaire addressing pain-related symptoms. The survey gathered demographic data, medical history, and information on low back pain, along with other pain conditions such as joint pain, generalized stiffness, neck or shoulder pain, headache, sciatic nerve pain, and dysmenorrhea in women who had not entered menopause within the previous three months.

Study Outcome

This cross-sectional study sought to evaluate the association between smoking exposure and the prevalence of low back pain. In addition to assessing the overall relationship, we conducted separate analyses to examine the joint associations of smoking exposure and alcohol consumption with the prevalence of low back pain.

Statistical Analyses

Participants were categorized into non-smoking and smoking exposure groups. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations. Group differences were evaluated using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the independent t-test for continuous variables. Associations were assessed through binary logistic regression, with results presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 22,471 participants were included, of whom 16,921 (75%) were classified as having no smoking exposure and 5,550 (25%) as having smoking exposure (Figure 1). Alcohol use was substantially more frequent in the smoking group. Discussion

In this large Taiwanese cohort, smoking was independently associated with low back pain after adjustment for multiple confounders. In contrast, alcohol consumption alone was not significantly associated with low back pain. Importantly, our findings suggest a potential interaction between smoking and alcohol use, whereby the association between smoking and low back pain was more pronounced among individuals who also consumed alcohol. This pattern suggests that concurrent exposure to these lifestyle factors is associated with differences in the prevalence of low back pain. Taken together, these results identify smoking as a potentially modifiable factor associated with low back pain, with this association appearing stronger in the presence of alcohol use. Although smoking was independently associated with low back pain in the multivariable analysis, the magnitude of this association was modest. Therefore, the clinical relevance at the individual level may be limited. However, given the high prevalence of both smoking and low back pain, even a modest association may be relevant at the population level. From a clinical and public health perspective, smoking remains a modifiable lifestyle factor associated with low back pain, particularly in the presence of other risk factors. In addition, the observed interaction between smoking and alcohol consumption suggests that combined lifestyle exposures may be associated with higher odds of low back pain compared with either factor alone. Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the modest magnitude of the associations and the observational nature of the study.

The baseline characteristics of our study population highlight important differences between smokers and non-smokers that may inform the interpretation of LBP risk. Smokers in our cohort were younger and predominantly male (only 22% of the smoking group were women, vs 79% women among non-smokers). They also had a higher average BMI and more frequent co-morbid conditions such as kidney stones, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and depression. For example, dyslipidemia was present in 8% of smokers vs 6% of non-smokers, and this is notable given that dyslipidemia itself has been linked to LBP risk in prior studies. 13 Conversely, smokers were slightly less likely to report arthritis, perhaps reflecting their younger age, and had similar rates of sciatica as non-smokers. Lifestyle differences were also evident: only 40% of smokers engaged in regular physical activity (vs. 44% of non-smokers, p < 0.001) and, by definition, smokers were far more likely to consume alcohol (24% of smokers were drinkers, vs. only 3% of non-smokers, p < 0.001). This profile suggests that smokers tended to cluster multiple lifestyle risk factors – higher BMI, lower exercise, and other unhealthy behaviors – which may compound their risk of LBP. It is worth noting that, consistent with prior epidemiologic studies, the overall prevalence of low back pain in our cohort was higher in females. 14 However, the smoking subgroup was predominantly male and middle-aged, reflecting regional smoking patterns in East Asia. Therefore, while the overall sex distribution of low back pain aligns with previous reports,1,14 the observed associations related to smoking in our study largely reflect a male-predominant population. This demographic structure should be considered when comparing our findings with studies conducted in more sex-balanced populations. Nonetheless, the enrichment of metabolic and psychosocial comorbidities in the smoking group is consistent with the idea that smokers carry an overall greater burden of health risk factors, which could contribute to their higher LBP odds.

Our findings align with and extend the existing international evidence on lifestyle factors and back pain. A substantial body of literature has identified smoking as a modest but consistent risk factor for LBP across diverse populations. For example, a meta-analysis by Shiri et al reported that current smokers have about 30% higher odds of low back pain compared to never-smokers, and an even greater relative risk for chronic LBP (OR 1.79) and disabling LBP (OR 2.14). 15 Our observed magnitude of the association for smoking (OR 1.15 in multivariable analysis) is somewhat smaller, likely due to rigorous adjustment for confounders and the inclusion of both acute and chronic LBP outcomes, potentially attenuating the association compared with studies focusing on chronic pain. Still, the direction is consistent with global studies. Notably, Kawabata et al found in a Japanese nationwide survey that smoking was significantly associated with LBP prevalence and chronicity (e.g. OR 1.63 for current LBP, and smoking was the only lifestyle factor predicting chronic LBP with OR 1.70). 13 This parallels our conclusions that smoking contributes to both the occurrence and persistence of back pain. In contrast, evidence for alcohol’s role in LBP has been limited and mixed. A 2013 systematic review concluded that alcohol consumption by itself has at most a weak association with LBP, mainly confined to cases of heavy or dependent drinking and chronic pain patients. 7 In most general-population studies, light-to-moderate drinking showed no significant relationship with back pain.7,16 Our results are congruent with this literature in that alcohol did not show a significant independent association with LBP after adjustment. Moreover, recent causal inference research using Mendelian randomization provides strong support for a causal link between smoking and LBP, finding a genetic predisposition to smoking initiation raises LBP risk by 36%, while finding no evidence of a causal effect of alcohol on LBP. 8 This concurs with our observation that alcohol alone was not linked to LBP, and underscores that smoking is the more important of the two exposures for back pain. Importantly, our study adds novel insight to the international literature by examining the interaction between smoking and alcohol. To our knowledge, previous studies have largely treated smoking and alcohol consumption as independent factors. In our analysis, their combined exposure was associated with higher odds of low back pain compared with each exposure alone (Table 4). This pattern suggests a potential interaction between these lifestyle factors that has not been clearly described in prior studies. These findings provide additional insight into the joint role of smoking and alcohol consumption in relation to low back pain in an East Asian population.

Our research also builds upon prior studies from Taiwan and neighboring East Asian countries, helping to situate the findings in a regional context. Epidemiological data on LBP in Asian populations have been somewhat scarce compared to Western data, and previous studies often did not focus on smoking or alcohol. For example, a prior population-based survey in Taiwan (2002–2003) reported an LBP prevalence of 25.7% and identified female sex, lower education, and blue-collar occupations as significant risk factors, but smoking and alcohol were not analyzed in depth in that study. 4 Our analysis addresses this gap by specifically evaluating these lifestyle factors in a contemporary Taiwanese cohort. The results are generally consistent with broader Asian findings. A recent meta-analysis of risk factors for LBP in Chinese populations found that cigarette smoking was associated with a significantly higher odds of LBP (OR 1.55), along with other factors like high BMI and female sex being risks. 17 Meanwhile, evidence on alcohol in Asian cohorts remains limited; some studies have reported null or inconsistent findings on drinking. Interestingly, Kawabata et al in Japan did observe a moderate association between alcohol consumption and LBP (OR 1.37) in their multivariable model. 13 Our study did not find a significant overall association between alcohol consumption and low back pain, which could reflect differences in drinking patterns, or the fact that we accounted for more confounders. It suggests that the role of alcohol may vary across populations and requires further investigation in Asia. Crucially, our study extends prior Asian literature by evaluating the combined associations of smoking and alcohol. The interaction analysis in Table 4 reveals that in an Asian population, smokers who also drink have substantially higher LBP odds – a novel finding not previously reported in local studies. This interaction remained after controlling for numerous covariates, suggesting that the joint exposure is associated with higher odds of low back pain. Given that smoking in many Asian countries, including Taiwan, is largely a male behavior and often accompanies social drinking, this insight has considerable public health relevance. Our results suggest that in Asian contexts, where cultural patterns lead to clustering of smoking and drinking in the same individuals, the burden of LBP attributable to these combined lifestyle factors could be especially pronounced. By documenting this, our study adds to the regional evidence base and may stimulate further research to confirm and generalize the observed interaction between smoking and alcohol consumption in relation to low back pain.

Our stratified analysis revealed a meaningful interaction between smoking and alcohol consumption in relation to LBP. Among participants without alcohol exposure, smokers exhibited a modest but statistically significant increase in the odds of LBP compared to non-smokers (adjusted OR 1.12; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.22, p < 0.01). In contrast, among those with alcohol exposure, the association between smoking and LBP was notably stronger (adjusted OR 1.40; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.83; p = 0.01). These findings suggest that the association between smoking and low back pain may be stronger among individuals with alcohol consumption. Notably, some prior research has hinted that moderate alcohol use alone is not associated with higher back pain and may even have a minor protective correlation in certain contexts. 18 However, any potential protective association of alcohol was absent in smokers – instead, drinking smokers showed the highest prevalence of LBP. Back pain prevention efforts might need to address multiple lifestyle factors simultaneously: for instance, smoking cessation programs for LBP patients could consider also counseling on alcohol reduction, especially since these behaviors often co-occur. Overall, the interaction between smoking and alcohol highlights the complex interplay of lifestyle factors in LBP and the importance of a holistic risk factor management approach.

There are several biologically plausible mechanisms through which smoking – and to some extent alcohol – may contribute to low back pain, and these mechanisms help contextualize our findings. Cigarette smoking has well-documented detrimental effects on the spine’s intervertebral discs and musculoskeletal support structures. Nicotine and other tobacco toxins cause vasoconstriction and reduced microcirculation to spinal tissues, especially the small blood vessels that supply the vertebral endplates and discs.19,20 Diminished blood flow can lead to disc nutrient deprivation and impaired waste removal, accelerating degeneration of the intervertebral disc matrix. Indeed, experimental studies in animal models show that chronic exposure to cigarette smoke induces disc degeneration, with increased apoptosis of disc cells and loss of collagen and proteoglycans in the disc matrix. 5 Smoking also promotes a pro-inflammatory state in the body: it triggers the release of systemic inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) that can infiltrate disc and paraspinal tissues, contributing to chronic inflammation and sensitization of nerves. These cytokines and resultant inflammation can exacerbate pain perception and hinder tissue repair, thereby linking smoking to both the onset and persistence of LBP.21-23 Additionally, smoking may impair healing of spinal structures by increasing oxidative stress and decreasing blood oxygenation, and it can alter pain processing by affecting neurocirculatory function. 24 Beyond the disc, smoking negatively impacts bone health and spinal stability. It disrupts hormonal balances (for example, lowering estrogen levels) and directly affects bone metabolism: nicotine has been shown to inhibit osteoblast activity while promoting osteoclast activity, leading to reduced bone mineral density.25,26 Chronic smokers have higher rates of osteoporosis and vertebral compression fractures, which can manifest as back pain or structural spine problems. 25 The combination of weaker bones and degenerated discs in smokers results in a spine that is more prone to injury, deformity, and painful conditions such as spinal stenosis. In contrast, the impact of alcohol on LBP is less clearly defined, but excessive alcohol use may share some overlapping pathways. Heavy alcohol consumption is known to contribute to osteoporosis and vitamin D deficiency – for instance, individuals consuming two or more drinks per day have a relative risk of 1.63 (95% CI: 1.01–2.65) for developing osteoporosis compared to abstainers.27,28 Alcohol (particularly in large amounts) can raise cortisol levels and impair calcium balance, leading to decreased bone formation and increased bone resorption. 29 This suggests that a long-term heavy drinker might experience loss of vertebral bone strength, compounding any smoking-related skeletal damage. Alcohol abuse can also cause peripheral neuropathy and muscle weakness, which might indirectly worsen back pain by affecting postural support and pain threshold.30,31 Furthermore, animal studies hint that alcohol can adversely affect intervertebral discs: for example, chronic alcohol intake has been linked to disc dehydration and degeneration in some models, possibly due to its diuretic and cell-toxic effects. 32 Taken together, these mechanisms paint a coherent picture: smoking contributes to LBP through multi-faceted biological insults – disc degeneration, reduced spinal perfusion, systemic inflammation, and impaired musculoskeletal health – and alcohol may further exacerbate these insults when combined. Additionally, the co-occurrence of smoking and drinking often correlates with other unhealthy behaviors which we partially adjusted for (e.g. physical inactivity) but cannot entirely disentangle. Such unmeasured factors could synergistically increase LBP risk in dual-exposed individuals. In summary, existing pathophysiological evidence is consistent with our epidemiologic findings, in which smoking—particularly in combination with alcohol consumption—is associated with low back pain, providing biological plausibility for the observed associations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, both exposure variables and the outcome were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which introduces the potential for recall bias and misclassification. Participants may have underreported smoking or alcohol consumption due to social desirability bias, particularly in subgroups where such behaviors are less socially accepted. Similarly, low back pain was identified based on self-report rather than standardized clinical evaluation or imaging, which may have resulted in heterogeneity in case definition, including the inclusion of both acute and chronic conditions of varying severity. Such non-differential misclassification would likely bias the observed associations toward the null, potentially underestimating the true effect sizes. Second, the study lacked detailed quantitative measures of smoking and alcohol exposure. Information on smoking intensity, duration, cumulative exposure (e.g., pack-years), as well as frequency, quantity, and patterns of alcohol consumption (e.g., binge versus regular use), was not available. The absence of these data precluded dose–response analyses and limited the ability to explore potential threshold or gradient effects. This may have obscured more nuanced relationships and contributed to the modest effect sizes observed in the multivariable analyses. Third, although multiple relevant covariates were included in the regression models, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Important unmeasured factors—such as occupational workload, repetitive mechanical strain, ergonomic exposures, and psychosocial stress—are known to be associated with both smoking behavior and low back pain risk. The absence of these variables may have resulted in incomplete adjustment and could partially explain the observed associations. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference regarding causality or temporal sequence. It remains unclear whether smoking preceded the development of low back pain or whether individuals with pain were more likely to adopt or maintain smoking and drinking behaviors. The absence of longitudinal follow-up data precluded assessment of changes in exposure over time and their relationship with the incidence, persistence, or resolution of low back pain. Fifth, the study population was derived from the Taiwan Biobank and restricted to adults aged 30–70 years without a history of cancer, which may limit generalizability to other age groups, ethnic populations, or clinical settings. Furthermore, the relatively low prevalence of smoking among women in this cohort resulted in a marked sex imbalance, limiting statistical power for sex-stratified analyses and potentially affecting the external validity of sex-specific inferences. Despite these limitations, the present study leverages a large, well-characterized, population-based cohort and rigorous multivariable adjustment to minimize confounding and enhance internal validity. By identifying an independent association between smoking and low back pain, and further observing a clinically meaningful interaction with alcohol consumption, this work provides novel evidence on the joint associations of modifiable lifestyle behaviors in an East Asian population. These findings address a critical gap in the literature and offer a strong empirical basis for future longitudinal investigations and integrated lifestyle-focused prevention strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, smoking was associated with low back pain, with evidence suggesting a potential interaction with alcohol consumption. However, the magnitude of the associations was modest, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. These results suggest a role for lifestyle factors in low back pain and may have implications at the population level.
