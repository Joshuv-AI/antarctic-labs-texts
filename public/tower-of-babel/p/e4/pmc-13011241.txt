Circadian rhythm of melatonin receptors under monochromatic light and their regulatory effect on clock genes in the chick liver
Authors: Lei Liu, Nan Jiang, Zixu Wang, Jing Cao, Yulan Dong, Yaoxing Chen, Shiqing Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvians have a complex system of light sensation and circadian organization. We previously found that light wavelength could affect the circadian expression of clock genes in chick liver, and that melatonin participated in this process. Melatonin functions through membrane and nuclear receptors, but their circadian rhythm and the effect of monochromatic light remain unclear. The effects of different receptors on clock gene expression are also unknown. In this study, 144 newly hatched chicks were divided into sham-operation and pinealectomy groups. They were raised under white (WL), red (RL), green (GL) and blue (BL) light for two weeks, and their livers were sampled at 6 time points with 4 h intervals. Using qPCR and western blot, the mRNA and protein expression of three membrane receptors (Mel1a, Mel1b, Mel1c) and one nuclear receptor (RORα) were measured for circadian rhythm analysis. The results showed that melatonin receptors, especially Mel1a and Mel1b, displayed a significant circadian rhythm. The rhythm mesor and amplitude of the membrane receptors were enhanced by GL and reduced by RL; further, pinealectomy dampened their rhythm and diminished the effect of light wavelength. However, the rhythm of melatonin nuclear receptor contrasted that of the membrane receptors. In vitro experiments involving the addition of different agonists and antagonists into primary chick hepatocytes showed that Mel1a and RORα activation induced the expression of seven clock genes (cClock, cBmal1, cBmal2, cCry1, cCry2, cPer2, cPer3). Similarly, activation of Mel1b and Mel1c induced the expression of five clock genes (except cBmal1 and cCry1). Moreover, melatonin entrained the rhythm of cBmal1 and cPer2 in long-term cultured hepatocytes. Overall, this research elucidates the role of melatonin receptors in regulating clock gene expression, and enhances our understanding regarding the effect of light wavelength on chick circadian rhythm.Keywords: Melatonin receptor, Monochromatic light, Circadian rhythm, Clock gene, Liver

Introduction

For adapting to a light-dark cycle, nearly all species, including Cyanobacteria, birds, mammals, and humans, have evolved circadian rhythms (Bell-Pedersen et al., 2005). In mammals, the circadian rhythm is controlled by central oscillators and multiple peripheral oscillators (Dibner et al., 2010). All oscillators comprise a conserved clock gene feed-back loop (Bell-Pedersen et al., 2005). As a central oscillator, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) participates in several important physical and pathological processes, such as sleep, shift work, jetlag, and space activity (Hirayama et al., 2023; Sack et al., 2007). Peripheral oscillators also play a crucial role in many functions, including metabolism and immunity (Bolshette et al., 2023; Curtis et al., 2014).

Avian circadian rhythms demonstrate several common and unique characteristics. Specifically, avians have three central oscillators: the SCN, retina, and pineal gland. All these central oscillators can receive environmental signals and coordinate whole body responses. Similarly, avian peripheral tissues can maintain inherent rhythms and receive signals from central oscillators (Cassone, 2014). Light is the principal zeitgeber of circadian rhythm, and therefore, light pollution has attracted extensive attention worldwide. The avian circadian rhythm is a common and vital model for relative research about artificial light influence (Coker et al., 2025; Moaraf et al., 2025). Light can be described by three elements: the light period, light intensity, light wavelength. The light period can change the circadian rhythm as well as the seasonal rhythm of many migratory birds (Appenroth et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2015). Light intensity can change the daily locomotor activity rhythm (Dominoni et al., 2014). Further, light wavelength is reported to influence the activity and clock gene rhythm (Yadav et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2020). Our previous study has shown that monochromatic light can affect the circadian rhythm of core clock gene expression in the central oscillators of chicken, the SCN, retina and pineal gland (Cao et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2016, 2017). We also found that monochromatic light can influence the clock gene expression rhythm of peripheral tissues such as the liver and pancreas and that the hormone melatonin plays a crucial role in this process (Liu et al., 2019; Song et al., 2023).

Among multiple peripheral oscillators, the liver has received much attention. Indeed, the hepatic circadian rhythm plays an important role in glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, and drug metabolism (March et al., 2024; Zwighaft et al., 2016). Further, the liver can be entrained by different signals (Mohawk et al., 2012). In this study, we built on our previous study on the chicken liver, and investigated the effect of monochromatic light on the circadian rhythm of melatonin receptor, including three membrane receptors Mel1a, Mel1b, Mel1c, and one nuclear receptor RORα. We also conducted in vitro hepatocyte experiments to explore the role of the melatonin receptors in clock gene expression.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All procedures involving chickens were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the Animal Welfare Committee of the Agricultural Research Organization, China Agricultural University (Approval No. CAU20171114-2).

Animals and treatments

In total, 144 newly hatched male chicks (Arbor Acres broilers) were purchased from Beijing Huadu Breeding Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China), and randomly divided into two groups (sham-operation and pinealectomy). Each group was divided into four cages (length × width × height = 70 cm × 70 cm × 30 cm, n = 18/cage), which were exposed to either white light (WL, 400–700 nm), red light (RL, 660 nm), green light (GL, 560 nm), or blue light (BL, 480 nm) using a light emitting diode (LED) system until post-hatching day 14 (P14). As shown in Figure S1, two LED strips were positioned at the upper-left and upper-right edge of the cage. Each strip comprised eight LED lamps with a 6 cm interval. The light intensity was 15 ± 0.3 lux, and the light cycle was 12 h light: 12 h dark (light on at 08:30 and off at 20:30). The temperature was maintained at 32 ± 2 °C in the first week and then reduced by 1 °C every 2 days until it reached 30 °C in the second week. The mean relative humidity was 55–60%. Granulated feed (broiler chicken feed, Charoen Pokphand Group) and water were given twice (9:00 and 20:00) to ensure sufficiency and ad libitum availability. The diet was formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient recommendations of the National Research Council for poultry (1994).

To investigate the role of melatonin, a pinealectomy surgery (Pinx) was conducted at P3 as previously reported (Li et al., 2015): after being anaesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (30–40 μg/g body weight), chicks were placed on a stereotaxic apparatus (ALCBIO, Shanghai, China). The skin above the cranium was cut open, and a small portion of the skull was removed using a dental drill to expose the pineal gland. The meninges were cut away using microsurgical scissors, then the pineal gland was removed using a forceps. After pinealectomy, the opening was packed with gel foam to reduce bleeding, the skullcap was placed back on the bird, and the wound was closed with surgical sutures. Topical antibiotic ointment was used to avoid infection. Sham surgeries (Sham) were performed in the same manner as the pinealectomy, except the pineal gland was left intact.

Sampling

At the beginning of P14 of the light-dark cycle, the light was off, and chicks were maintained in constant dark conditions (DD). To quantify the circadian expression of melatonin receptor genes, the chicks were quickly sacrificed by decapitation under dim red light at 6 separate time points in 4 h intervals (8:30, 12:30, 16:30, 20:30, 00:30, and 04:30), namely CT 0, CT 4, CT 8, CT 12, CT 16, and CT 20 (CT, circadian time). CT 0–CT 12 corresponded to the subjective day whereas CT 12–CT 24 corresponded to the subjective night. The livers were removed, frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until used.

Primary hepatocyte culture and treatments

Chicks (n = 3) at P3 were anesthetized using pentobarbital sodium (30–40 μg/g body weight) and were anticoagulated using heparin (1750 U/kg) via intravenous injection. Primary hepatocyte isolation and culture procedures were conducted following a previous reported protocol (Fraslin et al., 1992; Ning et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2012). The abdominal cavity was opened under aseptic conditions, and the ribs along with the breastbone were removed to permit easy access to the liver. A catheter was inserted into the portal vein for in situ perfusion, and the right atria were incised to allow efflux. At first, calcium-free buffer Ⅰ (HEPES 10 mM, NaCl 137 mM, KCl 3 mM, Na2HPO4 3 mM, EDTA 5 mM, pH 7.4) was perfused at a flow rate of 10 ml/min for 10 min to remove blood. Secondly, buffer Ⅱ (HEPES 10 mM, NaCl 137 mM, KCl 3 mM, Na2HPO4 3 mM, pH 7.4) was perfused to wash off the EDTA. Finally, buffer Ⅲ (HEPES 10 mM, NaCl 137 mM, KCl 3 mM, Na2HPO4 3 mM, collagenase IV 0.4 g/L, CaCl2 5.4 mM, pH 7.4) was perfused until the liver looked mushy. The liver was then washed, transferred to a sterile dish, and cut into pieces. The cell suspension was filtered through a tissue 200 mesh, and washed thrice with William’s E medium (WMP02, Caisson, Smithfield, RI, USA). Finally, the hepatocytes were re-suspended in William’s E medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL of penicillin, 0.1 mg/mL of streptomycin at a density of 1 × 106 cells/ml. Cell viability was assessed using trypan blue dye exclusion, and cell viability was >95%. The isolated hepatocytes were seeded into a 12-well plate and cultured in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2.

To evaluate the effect of melatonin membrane receptor on clock gene expression, the medium of cultured hepatocytes after 12 h incubation was switched to serum-free William’s E medium and starved for 6 h. Hepatocytes were then pretreated with 1 μM luzindole (nonselective Mel1a/Mel1b antagonist, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.), 1 μM 4-phenyl-2-propionamidotetralin (4P-PDOT, selective Mel1b antagonist, Tocris Bioscience), or 1 μM prazosin (selective Mel1c antagonist, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.) for 30 min, and stimulated with 1 nM melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h. To assess the effect of melatonin nuclear receptor, hepatocytes after starvation were stimulated with 1 nM melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich), 5 μM SR3335 (selective RORα inverse agonist, MCE), or 10 μM SR1078 (RORα agonist, MCE) for 24 h. After stimulation, the hepatocytes were collected, centrifuged, and stored at −80 °C until used. To evaluate the effect of melatonin on the intrinsic rhythm of clock gene expression, hepatocytes were cultured for 116 h. At 12 h (around 8:30) and 60 h (around 8:30), the culture medium was changed to fresh William’s E medium with 10% fetal bovine serum. Melatonin (1 nM) was administered at 72 h (around 20:30). Hepatocytes were collected every 4 h from 0 h to 116 h and frozen at −80 °C until used.

Real-time qPCR analysis

The total RNA of chick liver or chick hepatocytes was extracted using Trizol reagent (CW0580A, CWBIO Company). cDNA was reverse-transcribed and amplified using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Thermo Scientific). To measure the mRNA expression of melatonin receptors and clock genes, real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using AceQ® qPCR SYBR® Green Master Mix (Q111-02, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, on a LightCycler® 480 instrument (Roche, LightCycler® 480 System). The relative mRNA level was normalized to that of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and fold change in expression was normalized to a specific time point expression in one group. The experiments were repeated in triplicate. The PCR primers used in the study are listed in Table S1 and Table S6.

Western blot analysis

The chick liver tissue was homogenized and centrifuged; the protein concentrations of the cytosol and membrane fractions were determined using a BCA kit (CW0014, CWBIO). An equal amount of protein was loaded and separated using 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore, Billerica) using the wet electroblotting method. To block nonspecific binding, membranes were incubated in 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then probed overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody (Mel1a, sc-13186, Santa Cruz; Mel1b, sc-13177, Santa Cruz; sc-18574, Santa Cruz; RORα, OM184933, Omnimabs; β-Actin, CW0096, CWBIO). After washing thrice with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated donkey anti-goat, goat anti-rabbit, or goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies for 1 h. The signal was detected using an ECL kit and an imaging system (5200 Multi, Tanon). The bands were analyzed using Image J software; the expression was normalized to the β-Actin expression level, and fold change was normalized to a specific time point expression in one group. The experiments were repeated in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Gene transcript expression was normalized to that of cGAPDH, and protein expression was normalized to that of β-Actin. To better illustrate the results, the expression levels were normalized to that at specific time point in one group. SPSS 20 software was used to analyze the differences among six daily time points using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); these were then regarded as the premise of cosinor analysis when p < 0.05. Origin 9.3 software was used to analyze the 24 h circadian rhythm of melatonin receptor mRNA and protein expression, based on the nonlinear cosinor regression function y = A + B × cos(x × π/12-C × π/12). A, B, and C represented the mesor, amplitude, and acrophase, respectively. The regression was considered significant at p < 0.05, and R2 indicated the goodness of regression. Differences among light wavelengths were tested using one-way ANOVA, and differences between the sham-operation and pinealectomy groups were analyzed using an independent sample t-test in SPSS 20 software. The above analysis follows the classical procedures used for the numerical analysis of circadian rhythms (Cornelissen, 2014; Refinetti et al., 2007).

Results

Circadian rhythm of melatonin receptor expression in the chick liver

Melatonin in chicken serum follows a circadian rhythm, and is influenced by monochromatic light (Jiang et al., 2020, 2016). Melatonin functions via three membrane receptors (Mel1a, Mel1b, Mel1c) and one nuclear receptor RORα (Kharwar and Haldar, 2012; Liu et al., 1995; Rafii-El-Idrissi et al., 1998; Reppert et al., 1995). In this study, we raised chicks under a light-dark cycle for 14 days, collected chick liver samples at 6 time points with 4 h intervals, and detected the mRNA and protein expression of melatonin receptors (Fig. 1A). The mRNA expression of all four melatonin receptors displayed daily variation among different time points (ANOVA p = 0.000, see Table S2 for F and p value; Fig. 1B-E). Further, their mRNA expression demonstrated significant circadian rhythms (Cosinor regression p = 0.000; Fig. 1F, see R2 and p value at Fig. 2A, Fig. 2G, 2M, 2S). The mRNA rhythms of cMel1a and cMel1b were more robust than those of cMel1c and cRORα (Mesor cMel1a 5.07, cMel1b 3.81, cMel1c 1.42, cRORα 1.24; Amplitude cMel1a 3.07, cMel1b 2.63, cMel1c 0.43, cRORα 0.27; Table S2). Specifically, cMel1a peaks in the subjective night (CT 20.1, Fig. 1F, Table S2), whereas the other three receptors peaked in the subjective day (cMel1b CT 2.9, cMel1c CT 3.4, cRORα CT 2.4; Fig. 1F, Table S2). Similarly, the protein expression of all four melatonin receptors displayed daily variation (ANOVA p = 0.000, see Table S4 for F and p value; Fig. 1G-J) and a significant circadian rhythm (Cosinor regression p = 0.000; Fig. 1K, see immune-blot image, R2 and p value at Fig. 3A, 3G, 3M, 3S). The rhythm of the four melatonin receptor proteins oscillated at similar magnitudes (Mesor Mel1a 1.63, Mel1b 2.64, Mel1c 2.02, RORα 2.28; Amplitude Mel1a 1.03, Mel1b 1.65, Mel1c 1.08, RORα 1.25; Table S4). Further, their rhythm acrophases were similar to the mRNA rhythm (Mel1a CT 15.6, Mel1b CT 3.1, Mel1c CT 3.8, RORα CT 0.9; Fig. 1K, Table S4). These findings indicate that the expression of chick liver melatonin receptors has a circadian rhythm.

Effect of monochromatic light and pinealectomy on the circadian rhythm of melatonin membrane receptor expression

Membrane receptors Mel1a, Mel1b, and Mel1c transduce melatonin signals from an extracellular environment into intracellular cAMP or Ca2+ signal (Okamoto et al., 2024). Our previous study showed that melatonin receptor expression could be affected by light wavelength (Chen et al., 2016). Therefore, we further investigated their circadian rhythm under different monochromatic lights (Fig. 1A). The mRNA expression of cMel1a, cMel1b, and cMel1c still showed daily variations (p = 0.000, Table S2), and their circadian rhythmicity was not changed after monochromatic light exposure (cMel1a Fig. 2A-D, cMel1b Fig. 2G-J, cMel1c Fig. 2M-P; Table S2). Compared with white light (WL), monochromatic light displayed distinct effects on the circadian rhythm (Fig. 2E-F, 2K-L, 2Q-R; Table S2). In general, green light (GL) enhanced the mRNA rhythm of three membrane receptors, elevated their mesor (cMel1a +57%, cMel1b +52%, cMel1c +19%) and amplitude (cMel1a +32%, cMel1b +50%, cMel1c +20%). However, the effects of red light (RL) contrasted those of GL and reduced their rhythm mesor (cMel1a −24%, cMel1c −16%). Blue light (BL) only elevated the mesor (+37%) and amplitude (+43%) of cMel1b. Regarding acrophase, monochromatic light showed no obvious advance or delay compared to that with WL (Δt cMel1a < 0.9 h, cMel1b < 1.2 h, cMel1c < 0.9 h). The protein expression of membrane receptors resembled their mRNA expression. Under different monochromatic lights, the protein expression of melatonin membrane receptors retained daily variations (p = 0.000, Table S4) and circadian rhythm (Mel1a Fig. 3A-D, Mel1b Fig. 3G-J, Mel1c Fig. 3M-P; Table S4). GL and RL showed the opposite effects (Mel1a Fig. 3E-F, Mel1b Fig. 3K-L, Mel1c Fig. 3Q-R; Table S4). GL enhanced their rhythm by elevating the mesor (Mel1a +67%, Mel1b +42%, Mel1c +54%) and amplitude (Mel1a +53%, Mel1b +35%, Mel1c +33%), whereas RL weakened their rhythm by reducing the mesor (Mel1a −36%, Mel1b −28%, Mel1c −36%) and amplitude (Mel1a −35%). Blue light (BL) only elevated the amplitude (+18%) of Mel1b. Monochromatic light showed no obvious change in acrophase (Δt Mel1a < 0.5 h, Mel1b < 0.3 h, Mel1c < 0.8 h). In summary, GL enhanced the circadian rhythm of melatonin membrane receptors, whereas RL dampened it.

Pineal gland functions as a core oscillator in chicks, as it can receive light input signals and secrete melatonin as the output signal to entrain peripheral tissues (Cassone, 2014). To explore the effect of monochromatic light, we conducted pinealectomy surgery in chicks at P3, and continued the monochromatic light exposure to detect the melatonin receptor expression rhythm in the liver (Fig. 1A). Notably, the mRNA expression of three membrane receptors was decreased at several time points compared with that in the sham-operation group, especially under GL treatment. Despite displaying daily variation and circadian rhythm, their mRNA oscillation was much attenuated (cMel1a Fig. 2A-D, cMel1b Fig. 2G-J, cMel1c Fig. 2M-P; see Table S3 for F and p value). Among the three membrane receptors, pinealectomy surgery affected the rhythm of cMel1a most severely (Fig. 2E-F, Table S3). In the cMel1a mRNA rhythm, the mesor was reduced (WL −33%, RL −9%, GL −54%, BL −39%), the amplitude was reduced (WL −50%, RL −44%, GL −60%, BL −51%), and the acrophase was delayed for approximately 4 h (Δt WL 4.0 h, RL 3.5 h, GL 3.4 h, BL 3.0 h). The mRNA rhythm of cMel1b was also affected severely by pinealectomy; its mesor and amplitude were decreased significantly (mesor WL −34%, RL −28%, GL −51%, BL −49%; amplitude WL −53%, RL −48%, GL −67%, BL −67%), but acrophase was not obviously changed (Δt < 1.0 h) (Fig. 2K-L, Table S3). The effect of pinealectomy on the cMel1c mRNA rhythm was not as remarkable as the cMel1a and cMel1b, but its mesor and amplitude were similarly decreased (mesor WL −23%, RL −10%, GL −36%, BL −20%; amplitude WL −21%, RL −27%, GL −35%, BL −27%), and its acrophase was not changed (Δt < 0.7 h) (Fig. 2Q-R, Table S3).

To confirm the effect of pinealectomy on the circadian oscillation of melatonin membrane receptors, we also measured their protein expression rhythm. Similar to mRNA expression, the protein levels of Mel1a, Mel1b, and Mel1c were decreased at several time points compared with those in the sham-operation group, especially under the GL treatment. They retained the daily variations and circadian rhythms, but the rhythm magnitude was impaired (Mel1a Fig. 3A-D, Mel1b Fig. 3G-J, Mel1c Fig. 3M-P; see Table S5 for F and p value). The protein rhythm change after pinealectomy was the most evident for Mel1a (Fig. 3E-F, Table S5). The reduction in mesor and amplitude was even more than that in mRNA (mesor WL −71%, RL −54%, GL −79%, BL −68%; amplitude WL −68%, RL −56%, GL −78%, BL −74%), and the acrophase was delayed by approximately 6 h (Δt WL 6.7 h, RL 5.9 h, GL 6.0 h, BL 5.4 h). Regarding the Mel1b protein rhythm, its mesor and amplitude were decreased significantly similar to those of Mel1a (mesor WL −57%, RL −45%, GL −66%, BL −64%; amplitude WL −60%, RL −64%, GL −72%, BL −67%), but its acrophase was not affected (Δt < 0.6 h) (Fig. 3K-L, Table S5). The effect of pinealectomy on the protein rhythm was smaller in Mel1c. To some extent, its mesor and amplitude were also decreased (mesor WL −45%, RL −12%, GL −64%, BL −38%; amplitude WL −12%, RL −8%, GL −35%, BL −14%), and its acrophase was not changed (Δt < 1.0 h) (Fig. 3Q-R, Table S5).

These findings indicated that pinealectomy reduced the circadian rhythm of melatonin membrane receptor expression, especially under GL. Notably, the rhythm difference among monochromatic lights was diminished after pinealectomy, including mesor and amplitude (mRNA rhythm cMel1a Fig. 2E-F, cMel1b Fig. 2K-L, cMel1c Fig. 2Q-R, Table S3; protein rhythm Mel1a Fig. 3E-F, Mel1b Fig. 3K-L, Mel1c Fig. 3Q-R, Table S5).

Effect of monochromatic light and pinealectomy on the circadian rhythm of melatonin nuclear receptor expression

Melatonin is lipid-soluble and membrane permeable (Yu et al., 2016). RORα is reported as the melatonin nuclear receptor that directly regulates gene expression (Venegas et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2019). Moreover, RORα is one component of the clock gene feedback loop, and controls hepatic lipid homeostasis (Kim et al., 2017; Sato et al., 2004). Therefore, we also measured its mRNA and protein rhythm under different monochromatic lights and upon pinealectomy. Our results showed that altered light wavelength did not change the daily variation and circadian rhythmicity of RORα mRNA and protein expression (mRNA Fig. 2S-V, protein Fig. 3S-V; see Table S2 and Table S4 for F and p value). However, the effect of monochromatic light on nuclear receptor rhythm parameters was contrary to that on the membrane receptors. The mesor of RORα was reduced by GL (mRNA −12%, protein −18%) and elevated by RL (mRNA +14% protein +17%) (mRNA Fig. 2W, Table S2; protein Fig. 3W, Table S4). Its amplitude was also elevated by RL (mRNA +22%, protein +16%) (mRNA Fig. 2X, Table S2; protein Fig. 3X, Table S4). BL slightly reduced the mesor of cRORα mRNA rhythm (−7%) and the amplitude of the RORα protein rhythm (−15%) (mRNA Fig. 2W, Table S2; protein Fig. 3X, Table S4). However, the acrophase was not changed under different monochromatic lights (Δt < 1.0 h) (mRNA Fig. 2X, Table S2; protein Fig. 3X, Table S4). After pinealectomy, the mRNA and protein expression of RORα were enhanced at several time points especially under GL, and they maintained daily variations and circadian rhythm (mRNA Fig. 2S-V, protein Fig. 3S-V; see Table S3 and Table S5 for F and p value). Further, pinealectomy treatment elevated the mesor (mRNA WL +37%, RL +20%, GL +56%, BL +48%; protein WL +68%, RL +54%, GL +100%, BL +104%), and increased the amplitude (mRNA WL +61%, RL +28%, GL +89%, BL +73%; protein WL +94%, RL +67%, GL +105%, BL +137%) (mRNA Fig. 2W-X, Table S3; protein Fig. 3W-X, Table S5). The acrophase was delayed by around 2.0 h for the cRORα mRNA rhythm, and was not changed for the RORα protein rhythm (Δt < 1.2 h) (mRNA Fig. 2X, Table S3; protein Fig. 3X, Table S5). Similar to membrane receptors, the differences in rhythm parameters among monochromatic lights were diminished after pinealectomy, including mesor and amplitude (mRNA Fig. 2W-X, Table S3; protein Fig. 3W-X, Table S5). These findings demonstrated that the expression of the melatonin nuclear receptor RORα was in contrast to that of the membrane receptors, and that its circadian rhythm was reduced by GL and enhanced by pinealectomy.

Effect of melatonin receptor on clock gene expression and intrinsic rhythm in hepatocytes

Our previous research suggested that monochromatic light could affect the circadian rhythm of the expression of seven core clock genes in chick liver, and that melatonin participated in this process (Liu et al., 2019). However, the mechanism of melatonin was not investigated. In this study, we isolated and cultured chick primary hepatocytes, and explored the effect of different melatonin receptors on the mRNA expression and intrinsic rhythm of clock genes using qPCR (Fig. 4A, see primers at Table S6). To distinguish three membrane receptors, we treated chick hepatocytes with luzindole (Mel1a and Mel1b non-selective antagonist), 4P-PDOT (Mel1b selective antagonist), and prazosin (Mel1c selective antagonist) before melatonin stimulation (Fig. 4B). Among membrane receptors, melatonin promoted the expression of all seven core clock genes. Luzindole inhibited the promotion effect of melatonin on seven clock genes. 4P-PDOT inhibited the promotion effect of melatonin on five clock genes (cClock, cBmal2, cCry2, cPer2, cPer3). The expression of all seven clock genes under luzindole treatment was less than that under 4P-PDOT treatment, indicating that Mel1a participates in the promotion effect of melatonin on the expression of these genes. Prazosin inhibited the promotion effect of melatonin on the same five clock genes affected by 4P-PDOT. These findings reveal that melatonin promotes the expression of cClock, cBmal2, cCry2, cPer2, and cPer3 via Mel1a, Mel1b, and Mel1c membrane receptors (Fig. 4C, 4E, 4G-I), and that its promotion of cBmal1 and cCry1 is dependent on the Mel1a membrane receptor (Fig. 4D, 4F). Besides, three membrane receptors displayed a synergetic effect after prazonsin and 4P-PDOT administration or prazosin and luzindole administration (Fig. 4C, 4E, 4G-I). The effect of melatonin nuclear receptor effect was explored by the agonist SR1078 and inverse agonist SR3335 (Fig. 4J). Similar to melatonin, SR1078 promoted the expression of all seven clock genes, whereas SR3335 reduced their expression (Fig. 4K-Q).

Clock genes are reported to maintain intrinsic circadian rhythm when the cells are cultured in vitro (Guenthner et al., 2014; Shang et al., 2012). Therefore, we isolated and cultured chick hepatocytes for 116 h to detect their intrinsic rhythm. Fresh medium was changed at 12 h and 60 h after seeding, and hepatocytes were collected every 4 h to detect the mRNA expression of cBmal1 and cPer2, which oscillate with high magnitude among the seven core clock genes (Fig. S2). Their expression was relatively higher at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h, exhibiting a 24 h cyclical rhythm. Further, the expression was gradually decreased over time, especially for cPer2 (Fig. 4R-S). Melatonin was administered at 72 h, cBaml1 expression still showed a 24 h cyclical rhythm, and its fluctuation magnitude was stronger than that of control group (Fig. 4R). The rhythm of cPer2 expression was also largely affected, with its peak delayed from 96 h to 104 h (Fig. 4S). These in vitro findings reveal that melatonin could influence the expression and intrinsic rhythm of chick hepatocytes.

Discussion

Plasma melatonin is known to display circadian rhythm and peak at midnight. Our results showed that the mRNA and protein expression of chick liver melatonin receptors also demonstrated circadian oscillation. This is consistent with other studies regarding the central and peripheral tissues in birds (Jones et al., 2012; Kharwar and Haldar, 2011). However, the rhythms of Mel1b and Mel1c were anti-phased to that of Mel1a. This may be related to their different transcriptional regulations. Ovine and rat Mel1a promoter contains pitx-1 binding cis-element, and GnRH-induced Egr1 inhibits its stimulation effect (Bae et al., 2014). Human Mel1b promoter contains BMAL1/CLOCK binding E-box, resulting its parallel phase with cPer2 (Beesley et al., 2015). Further, post-transcriptional regulation also contributed to the circadian rhythm of melatonin receptor (Huang et al., 2021). cMel1a and cMel1b oscillate stronger than cMel1c, similar to their expression in chick retina (Natesan and Cassone, 2002). RORα, which acts as a component of the clock gene network, and showed a parallel phase with cPer2 and an anti-phase with melatonin similar to that in quail lung (Kharwar and Haldar, 2012; Sato et al., 2004).

Monochromatic light showed multiple influences on chick, including growth, reproduction, immunity, and circadian rhythm (Dishon et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019; Tan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021). Our previous research showed that melatonin receptors in the chick hypothalamus and liver could be affected by monochromatic light; this study further explored the effect of light wavelength on their circadian rhythm (Li et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). GL strengthened the mRNA and protein circadian rhythm, whereas RL dampened them; these effects were diminished by pinealectomy. According to our previous studies, melatonin may participate in this phenomenon (Jiang et al., 2020). Pinealectomy attenuates the circadian rhythm of melatonin membrane receptors as reported in rat pars tuberalis (Gauer et al., 1994). The observed low-amplitude and low-mesor oscillations could be attributed to the liver-inherent rhythm without input signal, or weaker circulating melatonin input (Liu et al., 2019). After acute melatonin stimulation, membrane receptors undergo β-Arrestin-mediated internalization resulting in decreased protein levels on the cell membrane (Dupré et al., 2018). In this study, we speculate that long-term melatonin stimulation under GL enhanced the rhythm of membrane receptors via other mechanisms. In avian species, the pineal gland could receive light input signals, entrain the rhythm of the SCN, which then relays the rhythm signal to peripheral tissues (Cassone, 2014; Jiang et al., 2017; Karaganis et al., 2009). The expression of melatonin nuclear receptor RORα was elevated by RL, similar to its expression in the spleen (Xiong et al., 2020). However, the difference between membrane and nuclear receptor rhythms needs further study.

In primary chick hepatocytes, mealtonin receptor activation induced the expression of clock genes, and melatonin entrained the intrinsic rhythm, similar to the findings of other relative studies. Membrane receptors contributed to Bmal1 and Per2 induction in human hepatic stellate cells and rat SCN neurons (Kandalepas et al., 2016; Kim and Cheon, 2024). Further, pinealocyte co-culture entrained the clock gene rhythm in chick astrocytes (Paulose et al., 2009). Furthermore, microarray analysis of mouse pars tuberalis revealed that the melatoin membrane receptor controlled the expression of many transcription regulators, including clock gene network components (Unfried et al., 2010). However, Mel1a and Mel1b receptor showed different effects on rhythm entrainment (Liu et al., 1997; Von Gall et al., 2005). In summary, melatonin receptor regulates clock gene expression in a complicated manner, and the role of different Gi and Gq signaling pathways after receptor activation needs to be explored in future. Regarding nuclear receptor, we found that RORα activation could also enhance clock gene expression, similar to Per2 induction in zebrafish (Yang et al., 2025). We speculate that melatonin could the change the conformation of RORα and affect its transcriptional activity (Solt et al., 2011). Besides its receptor-mediated role, melatonin could help maintain circadian rhythm via post-translational regulation of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (Vriend and Reiter, 2015).

Conclusion

In summary, we elucidate the effects of monochromatic light on the circadian rhythm expression of melatonin receptors, and the role of melatonin receptors in clock gene expression in the chick liver. Our findings pointed out the complexity of avian circadian rhythm. The rhythmicity of peripheral tissues is reflected in multi-aspects, such as the hormone inputs, receptors, and clock genes, and they are orchestrated to maintain oscillation. Further, specific light-wavelength could be used as a stronger zeitgeber to entrain avian rhythm.
