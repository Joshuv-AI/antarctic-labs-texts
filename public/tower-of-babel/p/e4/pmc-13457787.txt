Retinal Pigment Epithelial Detachment Following Topical Travoprost Therapy
Authors: Meghana Narapareddy, Narayanan Balakrishnan, Mohamed Anjum Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstaglandin analogs, including travoprost, are widely used to lower intraocular pressure in primary open-angle glaucoma. While generally safe, rare posterior segment changes have been reported. Retinal pigment epithelial detachment (RPED) associated with travoprost is uncommon and poorly documented. We report three patients who developed serous RPED shortly after initiating topical travoprost, none of whom had preexisting macular disease. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) confirmed the detachments, and complete anatomical and visual recovery occurred following discontinuation of travoprost and initiation of alternative glaucoma therapy. A clear temporal relationship was observed between drug initiation and RPED onset. The absence of clinical or OCT features suggestive of alternative diagnoses—such as central serous chorioretinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, or pachychoroid spectrum disorders—combined with consistent reversibility, suggests a possible association between travoprost and RPED. These findings emphasize the importance of early recognition and timely intervention, as RPED appears fully reversible when identified promptly, preventing potential persistent visual impairment.

Introduction

Retinal pigment epithelial detachment (RPED) is characterized by the separation of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) from the underlying Bruch's membrane due to the accumulation of fluid, blood, or drusenoid material. It may occur in isolation or in association with several retinal disorders, most commonly age-related macular degeneration (ARMD), central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR), inflammatory chorioretinopathies, and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy.1,2 Small extrafoveal RPEDs may remain asymptomatic, whereas lesions involving or approaching the fovea can result in significant visual impairment. The underlying pathophysiology is generally attributed to dysfunction of the outer blood–retinal barrier, choroidal vascular hyperpermeability, and impairment of RPE pump function.3

While systemic and ocular diseases are well-recognized causes of RPED, medication-induced mechanisms have also been described. Drug-associated RPED, although uncommon, has been reported with systemic corticosteroids, interferon therapy, and, more recently, with topical prostaglandin analogs used in glaucoma management.4 This association is clinically relevant because prostaglandin analogs—including latanoprost, bimatoprost, and travoprost—are widely accepted as first-line therapy for primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and ocular hypertension owing to their potent intraocular pressure (IOP)-lowering efficacy and favorable systemic safety profile.5

Travoprost is a synthetic prostaglandin F2α analog that lowers IOP by remodeling the extracellular matrix within the ciliary muscle and sclera, thereby enhancing uveoscleral outflow.6 Although generally well tolerated, reported ocular adverse effects include conjunctival hyperemia, eyelash growth, iris pigmentation changes, periorbital fat atrophy, and cystoid macular edema (CME), particularly in eyes with compromised blood–retinal barrier integrity.7

Both experimental and clinical studies indicate that prostaglandin analogs can enhance choroidal blood flow and increase vascular permeability through prostaglandin-mediated vasodilation, offering a plausible explanation for posterior segment involvement.8 In addition, prostaglandins may impair retinal pigment epithelial tight junctions and disrupt the outer blood–retinal barrier, a mechanism similar to that implicated in prostaglandin-associated CME.9

Reports of RPED associated with prostaglandin analog therapy are limited, which may partly reflect underrecognition, as mild cases can be asymptomatic and identified only through routine optical coherence tomography (OCT). Considering the long-term use of travoprost, particularly in older patients who may have subtle chorioretinal susceptibility, even infrequent adverse effects can carry significant visual consequences.10,11 This case series contributes additional evidence to the existing literature by documenting RPED occurring during topical travoprost therapy.

Case Description

Case 1

A 60-year-old man, a known hypertensive, with no history of recent corticosteroid usage, psychosocial stress, or other systemic risk factors for CSCR, with POAG, was switched from a topical beta-blocker to travoprost 0.004% once daily due to inadequate IOP control. Baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 6/6, N8 in both eyes. Clinical fundus examination before starting travoprost when the patient was on beta blockers was unremarkable (Fig. 1). In the absence of any preexisting macular abnormality, baseline OCT imaging was not warranted. Within 6 weeks of initiating travoprost, his vision in the right eye decreased (BCVA 6/12, N10). Fundus examination revealed subtle macular elevation, which was further characterized on OCT as a serous (nondrusenoid) RPED, appearing as a focal, smooth to slightly steep dome-shaped elevation of the RPE with an optically clear sub-RPE space and no internal reflectivity, measuring approximately 100–150 µm in height and 300–600 µm in width. There was no associated subretinal or intraretinal fluid, outer retinal layers were preserved, no neurosensory detachments were noted, and choroidal thickness was within normal limits without pachychoroid spectrum features (Figs 2 and 3). Travoprost was discontinued, and dorzolamide 1% twice daily was initiated. After 3 months of stopping travoprost, BCVA spontaneously resolved to 6/6, N8 bilaterally, with improvement of retinal features.

Case 2

A 55-year-old female with POAG, with no comorbidities, no history of corticosteroid usage, and no identifiable stress factors or systemic risk factors for CSCR, presented with decreased vision in the left eye after 4 months of travoprost use. BCVA was 6/24, N10 in the left eye. OCT imaging demonstrated a serous (nondrusenoid) RPED, characterized by a well-defined dome-shaped elevation of the RPE with an optically clear sub-RPE space and no internal reflectivity, measuring approximately 80–120 µm in height and 200–400 µm in width. There was no evidence of subretinal fluid or neurosensory detachment, and choroidal thickness was within normal limits and no features of pachychoroidal variations (Fig. 4). Travoprost was discontinued, and timolol 0.5% twice daily was started. Complete resolution of visual symptoms and anatomical restoration of foveal contour were noted which was confirmed by OCT after 3 months (Fig. 5). These findings indicate a possible association with travoprost therapy rather than a confirmed causal relationship.

Case 3

A 65-year-old male with POAG, a known hypertensive with no history of corticosteroid use, psychosocial stress, or other systemic risk factors for CSCR, on travoprost therapy for 4 months presented with progressive visual decline in the right eye (BCVA 6/18, N10). OCT demonstrated a large serous (nondrusenoid) RPED involving the macula, characterized by a dome-shaped elevation of the RPE with an optically clear sub-RPE space and no internal hyperreflectivity, measuring approximately 100–150 µm in height and 300–500 µm in width. There was no associated neurosensory detachment or subretinal fluid, outer retinal layers were preserved, and choroidal thickness was within normal limits without pachychoroid features (Fig. 6). Travoprost was discontinued, and combination therapy with brimonidine 0.2% and dorzolamide 1% twice daily was initiated. On follow-up at 4 months, there was improvement in vision and spontaneous resolution of RPED after cessation of travoprost.

Discussion

The present case series highlights RPED as an uncommon yet reversible posterior segment finding observed in association with topical travoprost therapy. Similar observations have been reported in isolated case reports. Patheja described travoprost-associated serous retinal detachment with complete anatomical resolution following discontinuation of the drug.12 Likewise, Kalikivayi et al. reported reversal of RPED after cessation of topical travoprost, suggesting a probable association.13

Further support for prostaglandin analog-related posterior segment effects is provided by Mueller et al., who documented serous retinal detachment following prostaglandin analog use, with prompt resolution after withdrawal.14 Although their case involved an underlying choroidal vascular abnormality, the temporal sequence of onset and resolution indicates that increased choroidal vascular permeability may play a central role. In our series, no clinically evident preexisting retinal or choroidal pathology was identified.

Inflammatory pathways have also been implicated in certain prostaglandin-associated posterior segment complications. Massimo et al. reported granulomatous uveitis with associated choroidal detachment following bimatoprost therapy, which resolved with corticosteroid treatment and drug discontinuation.15 In contrast, none of our patients exhibited clinical signs of intraocular inflammation, suggesting that the mechanism in our cases is more likely related to altered vascular permeability rather than an inflammatory process.

Most safety evaluations of prostaglandin analogs have predominantly focused on anterior segment effects. Eraslan et al. demonstrated corneal alterations associated with long-term prostaglandin analog use without assessment of posterior segment involvement.16 Similarly, large-scale clinical trials and longitudinal studies have primarily emphasized efficacy and anterior segment safety, with relatively limited attention to posterior segment changes.17,18

Collectively, prior reports along with our findings indicate that prostaglandin analogs may be associated with a range of posterior segment alterations, including cystoid macular edema, serous macular detachment, choroidal detachment, and RPED. In all our cases, baseline fundus examination was clinically unremarkable, and OCT imaging demonstrated focal serous (nondrusenoid) PED characterized by a smooth RPE elevation with an optically clear sub-RPE space, without associated subretinal fluid or neurosensory detachment.19

Alternative explanations must be carefully considered. CSCR results from choroidal vascular hyperpermeability leading to increased hydrostatic pressure, RPE dysfunction, and accumulation of subretinal fluid.20 The pachychoroid spectrum comprises a group of disorders characterized by increased choroidal thickness, dilated outer choroidal vessels (pachyvessels), and attenuation of the choriocapillaris, resulting in RPE stress and focal detachments including PED.21 In contrast, isolated serous PED may arise due to localized RPE dysfunction and impaired RPE pump activity in the absence of choroidal thickening or subretinal fluid.22 Additionally, age-related RPE dysfunction can predispose to PED formation due to degenerative changes in Bruch's membrane and impaired fluid transport, even in the absence of overt drusen or neovascularization.

Age-related macular degeneration-related PED typically develops secondary to drusen accumulation and alterations in Bruch's membrane and is usually associated with irregular, heterogeneous elevations containing internal reflectivity.23 In the present series, the absence of increased choroidal thickness, pachyvessels, subretinal fluid, and drusen, along with the presence of smooth, optically clear PEDs, makes pachychoroid spectrum disease and ARMD less likely, favoring a nonpachychoroid, nondrusenoid mechanism. Idiopathic serous PED and age-related RPE dysfunction cannot be completely excluded; however, these changes disappeared after the cessation of travoprost therapy, and it is unlikely to be age related or idiopathic RPE dysfunction, as these conditions are usually nonresolving, tend to persist or progress, and do not have a temporal association with medications as noted in our case series.24

Given the absence of subretinal fluid, neurosensory detachment or pachychoroidal features, further angiographic evaluation was not deemed clinically necessary. The small sample size and observational nature of this case series further limit the ability to establish causality.

Given the widespread and long-term use of prostaglandin analogs in the management of glaucoma, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for posterior segment changes in patients presenting with new-onset visual symptoms. Early identification and prompt discontinuation of the suspected agent may facilitate both anatomical and functional recovery. However, the findings of the present series should be interpreted as suggestive of a possible association rather than establishing a definitive causal relationship.

Conclusion

This case series identifies serous (nondrusenoid) RPED as a rare but potentially reversible posterior segment finding associated with topical travoprost therapy in patients with POAG. The temporal relationship between drug initiation and visual symptom onset, coupled with anatomical and functional recovery after discontinuation, suggests a possible association rather than a definitive causal link. The absence of clinical or OCT features indicative of alternative etiologies—such as CSCR, pachychoroid spectrum disorders, or age-related macular degeneration—supports this observation. These findings emphasize the need for vigilance in monitoring posterior segment changes in patients on prostaglandin analogs, with prompt recognition, appropriate imaging, and timely therapeutic adjustment to preserve visual function.
