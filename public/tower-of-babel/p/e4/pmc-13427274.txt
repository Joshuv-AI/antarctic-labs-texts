In vivo imaging evidence of injectate spread following sacral erector spinae plane block: A case series
Authors: Sandeep Diwan, Ghansham Biyani, Nitin Kachru Gawai, Himanshu Dongre
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Sacral erector spinae plane block (S-ESPB) has emerged as a regional anesthetic technique providing analgesia to perineum, hip, leg, and ankle surgeries, without resulting in any motor weakness.[12] However, the spread pattern of the drug and the exact mechanism of action of S-ESPB remain uncertain.[34] In this case series, we analyzed the spread pattern of the injected radiocontrast agent mixed with local anesthetic solution using computed tomography (CT) imaging.

After obtaining institutional ethical committee clearance (SIOR (Sancheti Institute of Orthopaedics and Research) /IEC (Institute Ethics Committee)/2021-22/67) and informed consent from the patients, we enrolled five adult patients belonging to ASA (American Society of Anaesthesiologist) physical status I/II, aged between 30 and 65 years, scheduled to undergo elective foot or ankle surgery (calcaneal fracture fixation, tendoachilles repair, fusion of ankle joint). We initially performed spinal anesthesia in all five patients and administered 2.8 mL of 0.5% heavy bupivacaine. At the end of the surgery, we placed the patient in the lateral decubitus position with the operated side up and performed parasagittal continuous S-ESPB under ultrasound guidance. First, we identified the posterior superior iliac spine and dorsal sacral foramina using a linear ultrasound probe (5–12 MHz, Fujifilm Sonosite Edge II). Under aseptic precautions, an 18G Tuohy needle was then advanced in-plane in craniocaudal direction, and the tip of the needle was positioned deep to the sacral multifidus muscle (SMM) and superficial to intermediate sacral crest at the level of second dorsal sacral foramen [Figure 1a]. Following hydrodissection using normal saline, a 20G epidural catheter was threaded into the space for about 3–4 cm beyond the needle tip and a 20-mL mixture comprised of 17 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine and 3 mL of omnipaque 300 was administered through the catheter [Figure 1b], and patients were shifted for contrast-enhanced CT imaging. All imagings were performed within 30 min of injection. CT imaging in axial, sagittal, coronal, and 3D volume rendering planes was performed and was reviewed by an independent consultant radiologist. The following parameters were analyzed: Cephalocaudal spread of the injectate, dorsal versus ventral distribution, and spread through the intervertebral foramina into the sacral epidural space.

The imaging showed a consistent spread of the radiocontrast agent deep to the SMM from S4 to L2 vertebral level in all five patients [Figure 1c]. In two patients, we found ventral spread of the injectate through S3–S4 intervertebral foramina into the sacral epidural space [Figure 1d and 1e] and possible involvement of the distal sacral plexus [Figure 1f]. In the remaining three patients, there was minimal to no spread of the dye ventrally through the sacral foramina. The salient findings of CT imaging are summarized in Table 1. None of the patients developed footdrop.

Our case series findings add additional information regarding the in vivo spread of the contrast mixed drug during S-ESPB, which may provide a possible explanation for the mechanism of action of this block. However, the limitation of this case series was that we could not test for sensory dermatomes blocked or pain relief following the block. Therefore, further studies with a larger number of patients incorporating these outcomes will be needed to establish our findings.
