Systemic regeneration medicines for muscular dystrophy: progress and challenges in pro-regenerative drug development
Authors: So-ichiro Fukada, Atsushi Kubo, Motoi Kanagawa, Yoshitsugu Aoki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Regenerative medicine has the potential to restore lost organ and tissue functions, and research on cell transplantation is steadily advancing toward clinical application. However, significant challenges remain in developing cell therapies for skeletal muscle, largely because the human body contains more than 600 individual muscles, making it impractical to treat all affected muscles through local transplantation alone. One strategy to overcome this limitation is to enhance the intrinsic regenerative capacity of muscle through various modalities; in this review, we define these approaches as pro-regenerative drugs. Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors provide a compelling example, demonstrating that stimulation of endogenous muscle regeneration can confer therapeutic benefits in the genetic disease Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). This raises a fundamental question: why do pro-regenerative drugs provide durable benefits without Dystrophin restoration? This review summarizes the current status and importance of pro-regenerative drug development and discusses why sustained therapeutic effects can be achieved despite the lack of gene complementation. It also highlights key pathways that promote muscle regeneration as potential targets for future therapies.

Background

Skeletal muscle exhibits a remarkable regenerative capacity, mediated by muscle satellite cells (MuSCs), which are widely considered the resident muscle stem cells (Mauro 1961). Studies using genetically modified animals have demonstrated that depletion of MuSCs, or impairment of their proliferative or differentiation capacity, directly compromises muscle regeneration ability (Charge and Rudnicki 2004; Fukada et al. 2013; Mauro 1961). Successful muscle regeneration also requires the contribution of other cell types that support MuSC proliferation and differentiation (Fukada and Uezumi 2023). For example, mesenchymal progenitors (Uezumi et al. 2010), also referred to as fibro/adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), secret soluble factors that promote MuSC proliferation and recruit monocyte/macrophage to injured muscles (Joe et al. 2010; Wosczyna et al. 2019). Macrophages are also critical for muscle regeneration, as they remove debris of damaged myofibers and further support MuSC proliferation (Arnold et al. 2007; Segawa et al. 2008). During this process, the number of MuSCs is maintained through a self-renewal mechanism that ensures repeated regeneration capacity, although this mechanism is not yet fully understood (discussed in the latter section).

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a representative hereditary muscle disorder characterized by progressive muscle wasting and weakness caused by mutations in the Dystrophin gene, which encodes a membrane-anchoring protein (Campbell 1995; Koenig et al. 1988). This progressive symptom is thought to result from repeated cycles of myofiber death that stimulate myogenesis and fusion for myofiber regeneration, eventually leading to a decline in the regenerative capacity of MuSCs. However, the mechanisms underlying MuSC exhaustion—and whether all MuSCs undergo this process—remain poorly understood.

Animal models of DMD have played a crucial role in elucidating disease pathology and developing of therapeutic strategies. Among them, the C57BL/10ScSn-mdx mouse (widely known as B10-mdx or mdx) has the longest history and has made the most significant contributions (Bulfield et al. 1984). However, unlike DMD patients, muscle mass in mdx mice is bigger than that in wild-type C57BL/10ScSn controls, at least up to 52 weeks of age (Coley et al. 2016). One hypothesis to explain this discrepancy is species-specific differences. However, we demonstrated that mdx mice on a DBA/2 background exhibit more severe regenerative defects and pronounced DMD-like phenotypes than B10-mdx and other mdx-related mice (mdx-2cv, 3cv, 4cv, and 5cv) on a C57BL/10 or its subline, C57BL/6, background (Fukada et al. 2010; Gordish-Dressman et al. 2018; Rodrigues et al. 2016a, 2016b). Notably, the wild type DBA/2 mouse strain exhibits markedly reduced regenerative ability—particularly after repeated injury—compared with the commonly used C57BL/6 strain (Fukada et al. 2010). Other DMD models in rats, dogs, pigs, and nonhuman primates also exert pathological features more closely resembling human DMD than C57BL/10-mdx mice (Chen et al. 2015; Larcher et al. 2014; Moretti et al. 2020; Nakamura et al. 2014; Otake et al. 2024; Relaix et al. 2025; Shimatsu et al. 2003). Differences in muscular dystrophy symptoms between strains can be attributed to factors such as body size, growth period, and the magnitude of mechanical stress on myofibers (Grounds 2008; Grounds and Shavlakadze 2011), but differences in muscle regenerative capacity by MuSCs are also a candidate factor. In fact, DBA/2-mdx shows low regenerative signatures (number of central myonuclei and revertant myofibers) compared to C57BL/10-mdx. This suggests that enhancing regenerative capacity could potentially ameliorate severe muscular dystrophy symptoms even in the absence of gene replacement therapy. Consistent with this concept, an HDAC inhibitor, Givinostat, was shown to enhance muscle regeneration in preclinical models (Minetti et al. 2006; Mozzetta et al. 2013), and Givinostat showed clinical benefit in the phase III EPIDYS trial and was approved in the United States in 2024 (Administration 2024; Mercuri et al. 2024). In a phase II trial, the drug increased the CSA (+77.7%) and reduced the fraction of fibrosis (−27.4%), necrosis (−43.5%), and fatty replacement (−37.5%) in brachial biceps biopsy samples (Bettica et al. 2016). This raises a fundamental question: how can pro-regenerative drugs restore and sustain muscle strength in DMD patients in the absence of gene replacement therapy? Furthermore, what is required to develop next-generation pro-regenerative drugs that are effective for the treatment of muscular dystrophy? This review outlines these concerns, including the importance of pro-regenerative drug development for muscular dystrophy.

Importance of regenerative therapy for DMD

The advent of induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells has substantially advanced the feasibility of cell-based regenerative medicine. However, the application of cell transplantation therapies to skeletal muscle disorders, including DMD, remains challenging. Skeletal muscle is distributed across more than 600 anatomical sites and therefore requires systemic delivery. In this context, MuSCs lack the ability to circulate in the bloodstream or to traverse physiological barriers, which limits iPS cell–derived MuSC therapies to local transplantation. While iPS cell-derived cells are expected to contribute to in vitro patient models to recapitulate the disease onset and drug screening, these models need to overcome several technical obstacles and challenges, including maturity of myotubes, innervation and revascularization for mimicking complicated in vivo structures (Grounds 2018). The different properties between growing myotubes/myofibers and fully matured adult myofibers are discussed in a review by Grounds (Grounds and Shavlakadze 2011) and briefly mentioned in the latter part of this review.

In the context of in vitro studies, noninvasive patient-derived cell resources such as urine-derived stem cells (USCs) provide an attractive platform for DMD research and drug development. Urine cells can be efficiently reprogrammed into iPS cells, enabling scalable patient-specific models for screening pro-regenerative drugs and evaluating combination strategies (Zhou et al. 2011). Moreover, USCs can be myogenically induced and have been used to recapitulate Dystrophin genotype and phenotype in DMD, providing a minimally invasive source of myogenic cells for functional studies (Sato et al. 2019). In addition, USC-derived extracellular vesicles/exosomes have been reported to activate MuSCs and promote repair in muscle injury models, highlighting the potential of USC-derived secretomes as complementary regenerative modalities (Wu et al. 2019). It remains an important and conceptually compelling question whether these secretomes can effectively attenuate myofiber necrosis and fibrosis—two major pathological processes that critically constrain efficient muscle regeneration.

In contrast to cell transplantation therapies, current molecular therapies designed to restore Dystrophin expression—including exon-skipping antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)—are administered systemically and increase Dystrophin levels in skeletal muscles throughout the body. (Komaki et al. 2018). Despite the availability of these approaches, the development of pro-regenerative drugs remains essential. Pro-regenerative drugs enable the de novo generation of myofibers to replace those that have been lost. Therefore, regenerative medicine, including pro-regenerative drugs, is the only therapeutic strategy applicable to DMD patients at advanced stages, where up to 60% of skeletal muscle mass may be lost. But experiments in mice have shown that HDAC inhibitors are ineffective in 1-year-old C57BL/6-mdx mice (Mozzetta et al. 2013). This raises the question of whether the chronic phase of the disease can be treated by pro-regenerative drugs. Raimondo and Mooney demonstrated that regenerative capacity can be enhanced even in advanced-stage DMD model mice by modulating immune responses (Raimondo and Mooney 2021). In the study, mdx−4cv mice aged 10.5–11 months (C57BL/6 background) were subjected to daily muscle injury using micropins for two weeks as advanced DMD models (Desguerre et al. 2012). Therefore, the mice were not a naturally occurring chronic model, but this finding suggests that MuSCs with preserved regenerative capacity persist even at advanced stages of the disease and supports the feasibility of pro-regenerative drugs for patients with DMD across a broad range of disease stages.

Why does enhancing regenerative capacity represent a therapeutic strategy for hereditary diseases?

Unlike exon skipping or gene therapy, strategies that promote endogenous regeneration do not replace the deficient gene. Therefore, even when regeneration is enhanced, the newly formed myofibers in patients with DMD still lack Dystrophin, implying that they subsequently undergo death again. This raises a critical question: does promoting endogenous regeneration fail to achieve sustained functional recovery in patients? Evidence from the success of HDAC inhibitors suggests that the answer is no. Why, then, can regenerative approaches confer therapeutic benefits, including long-term improvement in muscle function? A compelling hypothesis arises from the observations that the timing of loss of the causative gene for muscular dystrophy profoundly influences the resulting muscle pathology.

Dystrophin supports myofiber integrity by interacting with glycoproteins to form the Dystrophin–glycoprotein complex (DGC). Dystroglycan serves as a central component of this complex, linking Dystrophin to the basal lamina protein laminin α2. Fukutin is a glycosylation enzyme essential for the binding of Dystroglycan to laminin α2. DGC and the glycosylation enzymes are causative genes for Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy (LGMD). Dystroglycan and Fukutin germline-null mice exhibit embryonic lethality. In Dystroglycan-null cases, the placental Reichert's membrane fails to form (Williamson et al. 1997), leading to early lethality. Fukutin-null mice also exhibit abnormal formation of the placental basement membrane (Kurahashi et al. 2005). Using MCK (Muscle creatine kinase)-Cre mice for depleting the gene of interest in myofibers, Cohn et al. generated Dystroglycan conditional knockout mice and reported a relatively mild dystrophic phenotype (Cohn et al. 2002). In MCK-Cre reporter mice, recombination in the tibialis anterior muscle occurs in approximately 55% of myonuclei at 12 days after birth and in about 95% at 1.5 months of age (Bi et al. 2016). Similar mild phenotypes were observed in MCK-Cre conditional knockout mice for Fukutin (Beedle et al. 2012; Kanagawa et al. 2013). While using Myf5-Cre (which allows the depletion of interest genes in all Myf5-positive cells, including those in the developmental stage and the majority of MuSCs), two independent studies demonstrated that Myf5-Cre::Fukutin conditional KO mice showed severe dystrophic phenotypes (Beedle et al. 2012; Kanagawa et al. 2013). These different dystrophic phenotypes proposed two ideas: 1) Dystroglycan and Fukutin proteins are partially retained in MCK-Cre conditional knockout mice during postnatal growth, which prevents myofiber necrosis. 2) The loss of Dystroglycan and Fukutin in MuSCs contributes to the severe phenotype in Myf5-Cre and patients (Dumont and Rudnicki 2016). In addition, we need to consider the different properties of growing and adult myofibers (Grounds and Shavlakadze 2011). It seems likely that some properties of the sarcolemma do indeed differ between postnatal growing myofiber and mature myofiber, e.g., with reference to signaling response to growth factors such as IGF-1, calcium regulation, membrane fluidity, lipid raft distribution, lipid composition and the role of Dysferlin (Grounds and Shavlakadze 2011). The responses of anti-Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) antibody also differ between growing myofibers and adult myofibers. Myofiber necrosis in 3-week-old mdx mice was prevented by blockade of TNF, but the treatment did not reduce necrosis in quadriceps muscles of adult mdx mice (Grounds 2008; Radley et al. 2008; Radley and Grounds 2006). Collectively, growing myofibers might be sensitive to the loss of Dystrophin and DGC proteins compared to adult mature myofibers. Further, these results suggest that, even when the causative gene for muscular dystrophy is deleted in adult myofibers, under sedentary conditions, myofibers do not undergo significant necrosis or exhibit severe scar formation, as observed in growing muscles. This hypothesis supports that regenerative therapy alone is sufficient to treat hereditary disorders such as DMD; however, such a conclusion warrants rigorous experimental and clinical validation. While it has also been demonstrated that MCK-Cre Fukutin conditional knockout mice were vulnerable to exercise-induced stress (Kanagawa et al. 2013), implying that myofibers in patients treated with regenerative medicine may likewise remain susceptible to mechanical stress.

What should be targeted to increase the regeneration process?

MuSCs, the key players in muscle regeneration, undergo multiple processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and fusion. In general, proliferation and differentiation are opposing processes, making their simultaneous enhancement challenging. Targeting MuSC (self-renewal)

MuSCs are the main targets for enhancing muscle regenerative ability (Dumont and Rudnicki 2016). Increasing their proliferative potential is essential for myofiber formation. However, growth factors stimulate proliferation while concurrently inhibiting differentiation, ultimately impairing myofiber formation, because proliferating MuSCs have to withdraw from the cell cycle to differentiate (Hawke and Garry 2001). Conversely, promoting differentiation limits MuSC proliferation, resulting in inefficient muscle regeneration. Indeed, factors reported to enhance regeneration are not simply MuSC growth factors, but rather molecules that regulate MuSC self-renewal. The most advanced effort aimed at promoting MuSC self-renewal has been led by the Rudnicki group. Based on their seminal observation that asymmetric division–mediated self-renewal of MuSCs is compromised in mdx mice (Dumont et al. 2015), they developed a small-molecule compound (SAT3247) reported to enhance muscle regeneration in preclinical models, and a Phase II clinical trial is currently in progress. However, the extent to which impaired intrinsic MuSC function contributes to disease progression remains a matter of ongoing debate in the field (Morgan et al. 2019). Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms that enhance muscle regeneration provides an important conceptual framework and a strong therapeutic rationale for targeting MuSC self-renewal in dystrophic muscle.

Targeting STAT3 has been reported to restore skeletal muscle function in mdx mice (Tierney et al. 2014). Zhu et al. demonstrated, using genetic knockout mice, that STAT3 is essential for MuSC proliferation and self-renewal (Zhu et al. 2016). Meanwhile, Tierney et al. showed that transient inhibition of STAT3 enhances MuSC proliferation in mdx mice (Tierney et al. 2014). These results appear to be controversial: sustained STAT3 inhibition inhibits both MuSC proliferation and self-renewal, whereas transient STAT3 inhibition promotes MuSC proliferation. However, these findings also suggest that pro-regenerative drugs target a more complex mechanism than a simple MuSC proliferation pathway.

Examples of pro-regenerative drugs that do not act through simple proliferative pathways have also been demonstrated in studies of CEP-701 or Integrin β1 activation (Buchanan et al. 2020; Rozo et al. 2016). CEP-701 (also known as Lestaurtinib) does not affect myoblast proliferation under standard culture conditions; however, it promotes the proliferation of freshly isolated MuSCs and significantly improves the engraftment efficiency of transplanted cells in mdx mice (Buchanan et al. 2020). Integrin β1 activation improves the regenerative capacity by promoting endogenous MuSC proliferation in mdx mice (Rozo et al. 2016). Integrin β1 is recognized as a cell adhesion molecule rather than a growth pathway that directly regulates cell proliferation. Nevertheless, activation of Integrin β1 has been shown to markedly potentiate growth factor signaling, including IGF-1 and bFGF, thereby promoting muscle regeneration (Rozo et al. 2016). Moreover, this study demonstrated that Integrin β1 is also required for MuSC proliferation and self-renewal (Rozo et al. 2016).

Another molecule targeting MuSCs for the treatment of DMD is Wnt7a. von Maltzahn et al. reported that a single intramuscular injection of Wnt7a treatment increased muscle function in mdx mice, accompanied by efficient induction of MuSC expansion and myofiber hypertrophy (von Maltzahn et al. 2012). Notably, Wnt7a is also involved in MuSC self-renewal (Le Grand et al. 2009). Thus, rather than simply inducing MuSC proliferation in vitro, identifying of pathways regulating MuSC self-renewal represents a critical advance for enhancing muscle regeneration in DMD model mice. Because many studies have been limited to local drug administration (Table 1), a major challenge in the future is the development of modalities capable of achieving systemic effects, such as HDAC inhibitors and SAT3247, as well as additional therapeutic targets involved in MuSC behaviors including self-renewal.

Targeting Fibro/adipogenic progenitors

FAP is involved in the homeostasis, regeneration, and hypertrophy (Kaneshige et al. 2022; Lemos et al. 2015; Uezumi et al. 2021; Wosczyna et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2024). Platelet-derived growth factor alpha (PDGFRα) is a well-established marker used to identify this cell population (Uezumi et al. 2010). Notably, FAPs play dual roles in both muscle regeneration and scar formation (Fukada and Uezumi 2023). In dystrophic muscles, FAPs are a major source of adipocytes and fibrotic tissue, thereby contributing to pathological fat accumulation and fibrosis, which are hallmarks of disease progression in muscular dystrophies (Joe et al. 2010; Uezumi et al. 2010, 2011). In contrast, under physiological regenerative conditions, FAPs support muscle repair by promoting macrophage infiltration and enhancing MuSC proliferation (Joe et al. 2010; Wosczyna et al. 2019). As excessive fibrosis and fatty infiltration impair muscle contractility, suppressing FAPs’ contribution to adipocyte/fibrosis formation or enhancing their regenerative support function is expected to promote muscle regeneration. Notably, HDAC inhibitors have been shown to target FAPs, which promote myogenic differentiation indirectly through the induction of Follistatin (Mozzetta et al. 2013).

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as Imatinib and Nilotinib, directly target FAPs and have been reported to ameliorate disease phenotypes in dystrophic muscle by reducing FAP-mediated fibrosis (Huang et al. 2009; Ito et al. 2013; Lemos et al. 2015). However, these pharmacological agents may also suppress MuSC proliferation and are associated with significant side effects, as documented in cancer patients receiving these treatments. Consequently, their applications as pro-regenerative drugs for muscular dystrophies are limited.

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells are a new modality for treating cancer. Fibrosis is also a target disease for CAR-T cells (Aghajanian et al. 2019). Ferrand et al. reported the reduction of fibrosis and enhanced gene therapy by targeting fibroblast activation protein expressed in FAPs with CAR-T cells in DBA/2-mdx (Ferrand et al. 2025). Research on CAR-T cells also suggests that FAPs are promising therapeutic targets.

Taken together, since FAPs contribute to both regeneration and scar formation, effective therapeutic strategies should suppress scar formation without compromising the regenerative ability mediated by FAPs. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms governing FAP cell fate decisions and identifying determinants that distinguish their beneficial and deleterious functions represent critical steps toward the development of safe and effective FAP–targeted pro-regenerative drugs for muscular dystrophy.

Targeting immune cells

Dexamethasone is an established medicine for DMD and is primarily used for its immunosuppressive effects. In this context, accumulating evidence indicates that excessive immune responses and dysregulated immune cell polarization contribute to impaired muscle regeneration and myofiber necrosis (Arnold et al. 2007; Villalta et al. 2014). Therefore, modulation of detrimental immune signals may represent a strategy to restore regenerative abilities in muscular dystrophies. Among immune cells, macrophages play a pivotal role in muscle regeneration (Arnold et al. 2007; Segawa et al. 2008), and appropriate macrophage subtype composition and phenotypic switching are critical for successful muscle regeneration (Capote et al. 2016; Villalta et al. 2009). For example, Giordano et al. demonstrated that TLR4 depletion (a key receptor for induction of inflammatory macrophages) in mdx mice significantly improved muscle function and histopathology, accompanied by a marked reduction in inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)-positive inflammatory macrophages and a concomitant increase in CD206-positive (a key marker of resolving/M2) macrophages (Giordano et al. 2015). Skewing macrophages toward a pro-resolving phenotype may be a target for pro-regenerative drugs.

Regulatory T cells (Tregs) have emerged as attractive therapeutic targets for inflammatory diseases, including muscular dystrophy. Villalta et al. demonstrated the involvement of Tregs in disease progression in both mdx mice and DMD patients, showing that Treg depletion exacerbates muscle pathology, whereas Treg augmentation ameliorates disease severity (Villalta et al. 2014). Furthermore, Raimondo and Mooney reported that intramuscular injection of interleukin-4 (IL-4)–conjugated gold nanoparticles improved tibialis anterior muscle function and increased myofiber size through the expansion of Tregs (Raimondo and Mooney 2021). Collectively, these findings suggest that modulation of macrophages and Tregs represents a promising therapeutic strategy for pro-regenerative drug development for muscular dystrophy.

Targeting MicroRNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) regulate protein expression at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level. Among them, miR-1, miR-206, and miR-133a/b are specifically expressed in striated muscle and are collectively referred to as myo-miRs. While myo-miRs have attracted attention as biomarkers of muscle damage, including muscular dystrophy (Cacchiarelli et al. 2011), they may also serve as potential therapeutic targets. miR-206 is upregulated in patients with DMD (Li et al. 2014), and its complete loss in mice results in delayed muscle regeneration after injury. Moreover, the absence of miR-206 exacerbates the dystrophic phenotype in mdx mice. Mechanistically, miR-206 promotes myoblast fusion by targeting Pax7 and Notch3 (Liu et al. 2012). Notably, AAV-mediated expression of a miR-206 decoy, leading to approximately 40% downregulation of miR-206, improved muscle histology and motor function in mdx mice (Bulaklak et al. 2018). In contrast, deletion of the miR-206/133b cluster does not affect skeletal muscle development, survival, or regeneration (Boettger et al. 2014), and miR-33a deficiency similarly shows no apparent phenotype in muscle development or survival (Deng et al. 2011).

In addition to myo-miRs, Wu et al. reported that miR-431 targets Pax7 and improves regenerative capacity in miR-431-transgenic/mdx mice by increasing the number of MyoD+ cells (Wu et al. 2015). Although MuSCs were not depleted during a second round of regeneration, caution may be warranted for long-term use, given the essential role of Pax7 in MuSC maintenance (Gunther et al. 2013; von Maltzahn et al. 2013). More recently, Sowa et al. demonstrated that systemic delivery of ASOs targeting miR-33 ameliorates muscular dystrophy by enhancing skeletal muscle regeneration (Sowa et al. 2025). Mechanistically, miR-33a/b targets genes such as CDK6, FST, and ABCA1, which are involved in cell proliferation and inflammation; inhibition of miR-33a/b increases the expression of these genes and promotes MuSC proliferation (Sowa et al. 2025). Although complete suppression of certain miRNAs may be detrimental, these molecules nonetheless hold significant potential for promoting systemic muscle regeneration.

Suppression of myofiber necrosis

In the therapeutic context, effective management of muscular dystrophy will require not only the enhancement of muscle regeneration but also the suppression of myofiber necrosis. In addition to the aforementioned research on myofiber properties during the growth period, elucidating the mechanisms of pathways thought to contribute to myofiber necrosis, such as inflammation, nutrition, and oxidative stress, is also an important research topic. An orally active inhibitor of fast skeletal muscle myosin, EDG-5506 (Sevasemten), suppresses fibrosis and serum creatine kinase (CK) levels in mdx mice (Russell et al. 2023). In addition, in a phase I clinical trial of EDG-5506, serum biomarkers of muscle injury (CK, fast skeletal muscle troponin I, and myoglobin) progressively decreased following Sevasemten treatment in adults with Becker Muscular Dystrophy, a milder type of DMD (Donovan et al. 2025). To develop methods for suppressing myofiber necrosis, it is necessary to resolve the fundamental question that remains unanswered: 'Why do myofibers undergo necrosis when Dystrophin or DGC is deficient?'

Innervation

Dystrophin protein is enriched at neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) (Zhao et al. 1992), meaning that dystrophin plays a primary role in the formation or structure of NMJ. In addition, repeated myofiber necrosis secondarily damages NMJs, as evidenced by increased levels of degeneration-related proteins (S100β and Tau5), in B10-mdx and DMD-rat, but not in D2-mdx (Krishnan et al. 2020a, 2020b). Even if myofiber regeneration is successful, the myofibers cannot function unless they are properly innervated and structured. Therefore, muscle function testing dependent on innervation is essential for the development of pro-regenerative drugs. Regarding the realization of therapies targeting elderly DMD patients through pro-regenerative drugs, careful analysis and validation are required not only for new myofiber formation but also for the restoration of innervation.

What is required to develop a systemic muscle regeneration-promoting drug?

Elucidating the mechanisms underlying MuSC self-renewal is also indispensable, because these processes remain incompletely understood. It is unclear whether MuSCs self-renew exclusively through specific mechanisms of cell division, such as asymmetric division, or stochastically through conventional cell divisions. The most straightforward model for maintaining stem cell numbers is that they undergo asymmetric division during their first division. However, during muscle regeneration, this model might not be applied to the MuSC self-renewal mechanism. In vivo imaging studies have demonstrated that apical-basal asymmetric division does not occur within the first four days post-injury, when nascent myotubes appear (Webster et al. 2016). Another model for maintaining MuSC pool is that a subset of MuSCs behaves as stem cells. However, using clonal cell labeling experiments in mice (Tierney et al. 2018) and zebrafish (Hughes et al. 2022) models, these studies show MuSCs undergo symmetric expansion with stochastic fate acquisition during tissue repair. Collectively, these results indicate that MuSC self-renewal does not occur when a specific subset of MuSCs undergoes initial division. So when does MuSC self-renewal occur? Studies by Cutler et al. and Evano et al. demonstrated that MuSC self-renewal predominantly occurs between days 5 and 7 after injury in muscle regeneration models, a period that coincides with the appearance of newly regenerated myofibers, suggesting that myofibers provide an essential environment for MuSC self-renewal (Cutler et al. 2022; Evano et al. 2020). The establishment of in vitro systems that faithfully recapitulate MuSC self-renewal in vivo is therefore expected to advance mechanistic understanding of MuSC self-renewal and facilitate the development of novel pro-regenerative drugs targeting MuSC self-renewal. Human iPS cell–derived MuSCs and non-invasive patient-derived cell sources (e.g., USCs) are anticipated to make a significant contribution to these efforts (Sato et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2011).

Alongside elucidating the mechanism underlying MuSC self-renewal, it is essential to pursue comprehensive studies of myogenesis centered on MuSCs. Although widely used injury models employing cardiotoxin (CTX) or BaCl₂ are indispensable for in vivo regeneration studies (Hardy et al. 2016), we believe that alternative models/aspects of myogenesis should also be considered. In these conventional injury models, all myofibers are simultaneously ablated, and myogenesis proceeds along the residual basement membrane (Collins et al. 2024; Webster et al. 2016). In contrast, in diseased muscles such as those affected by DMD, pro-regenerative drugs induce MuSC proliferation on living myofibers or on partially damaged myofibers. We have previously reported that myogenesis in overloaded muscles—where MuSC proliferation and differentiation occur on existing myofibers (Fukuda et al. 2019)—differs in several respects from the regenerative myogenesis observed in CTX-induced injury models (Fukada et al. 2022). We refer to this process as “non-regenerative myogenesis” (Fukada et al. 2026). Studying such in vivo myogenesis using models distinct from conventional injury paradigms may facilitate the identification of factors that regulate MuSC dynamics, including self-renewal, and may ultimately contribute to the discovery of therapeutic targets and the development of pro-regenerative drugs for DMD.

Although direct evidence remains limited, it is conceivable that, under pathological conditions such as DMD, a “pathological niche”—an environment that suppresses myogenesis—impairs MuSC function (Fig. 1). One potential role of pro-regenerative therapies is to convert this pathological niche into a regenerative niche. FAPs and immune cells are key players of these niches. As suggested by the mechanisms of action of HDAC inhibitors, effective pro-regenerative therapies likely need to target not only MuSCs but also these niche-associated cells. Accordingly, elucidating the properties of the pathological niche that impedes regeneration is as important as identifying factors that promote regeneration. In this context, a comprehensive characterization of the muscle degeneration–regeneration process in DMD patients is essential. Cutting-edge analytical approaches, such as single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) of human DMD samples, now enable direct investigation of human muscle pathology and will help address these concerns.

Another approach is to elucidate differences between models that exhibit progressive symptoms and those that do not. Examples include the underlying differences in regenerative ability between C57BL/10 and DBA/2 mice, as conducted by Heydemann et al. in Sgcg-null mice (Heydemann et al. 2009), as well as the distinctions between affected and unaffected muscles in DMD patients. Regarding the latter, Taglietti et al. have revealed a mechanism responsible for generating these differences (Taglietti et al. 2023). It has long been known that extraocular muscles (EOMs) in DMD are less affected by Dystrophin deficiency, and they identified the gene encoding thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR) as highly expressed in MuSCs of EOMs (Taglietti et al. 2023). It has been demonstrated that limb muscle regeneration is promoted by inducing cAMP, which is activated by TSHR, through Forskolin administration. Investigating other cell components besides MuSCs might lead to finding new targets for pro-regenerative drugs.

Conclusions and perspectives

Current therapeutic strategies primarily target younger patients, leaving those with advanced disease unable to benefit. Gene therapy and exon-skipping approaches depend on the presence of myofibers and are therefore ineffective in elderly patients with severe myofiber loss. In such cases, pro-regenerative drugs represent the only viable therapeutic option. Although there are issues such as innervation, reactivating the regenerative capacity of the remaining MuSCs could expand treatment eligibility to a broader patient population. Consequently, further advances in myology, particularly those focused on myogenesis through multiple approaches, are highly anticipated.

Another key challenge is the development of effective combination therapies. Pro-regenerative drugs are expected to play a central role in this strategy. For example, rebuilding muscle tissue in patients with advanced-stage disease using pro-regenerative drugs may enhance the efficacy of treatments such as AAV-based and exon-skipping therapies. By integrating these approaches, we may move closer to an ideal therapy that is more effective, durable, and capable of benefiting a broader patient population.

Finally, the rapid expansion of regenerative medicine underscores the need for systemically deliverable pro-regenerative drugs capable of reshaping the regenerative niche in dystrophic muscles. In parallel with the approaches reviewed here, emerging lines of collaborative research are exploring novel classes of regeneration-promoting compounds for muscular dystrophy. Future studies should clarify their target cell populations, establish robust pharmacodynamic biomarkers of response, and rigorously evaluate efficacy using complementary in vivo models and patient-derived in vitro platforms, including urine-derived cell–based resources, 3D cultured and organoid (Afshar Bakooshli et al. 2019; Andersen et al. 2020; Mavrommatis et al. 2023).
