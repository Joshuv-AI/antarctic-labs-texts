Exploring academic motivation across university years: a mixed-methods study at King Faisal University
Authors: Mazen Omar Almulla, Abdulaziz Mohammed Alismail, Heba Daraghmeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores how gender and year of study influence academic motivation among undergraduate students at King Faisal University, Saudi Arabia, an understudied non-Western, gender-segregated context in the motivation literature. By situating the research within this unique population, the study responds to calls for expanding motivational research beyond Western samples and contributes culturally grounded insights to the field. Using a mixed-methods approach, quantitative data were collected from 267 students (51.3% female, 48.7% male) via the Academic Motivation Scale, alongside qualitative insights from nine semi-structured interviews. Descriptive statistics showed that female students consistently scored higher in both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, while male students reported significantly greater levels of amotivation. Motivation peaked in the first year of university and declined steadily in later years. Two-way ANOVA results revealed significant main effects of both gender and year of study across all seven academic motivation subscales (p < .05), though no significant interaction effects were found, suggesting stable gender-based differences across academic progression. Regression analysis further identified gender (β = 0.50, p = 0.013) and year of study (β = –0.75, p < 0.001) as significant predictors of motivation, particularly for intrinsic motivation to know. Thematic analysis reinforced the quantitative findings, highlighting external pressures, career concerns, and sociocultural expectations as key factors shaping students’ motivational experiences. These findings underscore the urgency of implementing targeted, gender-sensitive interventions, such as academic mentoring, psychological support, and flexible curricular pathways, to sustain motivation, particularly in later academic years. By contextualizing academic motivation within the Saudi higher education system, this study contributes novel empirical insights and practical implications for educators and policymakers aiming to enhance student engagement and persistence in culturally specific settings.Keywords: Academic motivation, Intrinsic motivation, Extrinsic motivation, Amotivation, Academic Motivation Scale (AMS)

Introduction

Academic motivation plays a fundamental role in determining students' engagement, persistence, and academic success in higher education [1, 2]. Motivated students are more likely to invest effort in their studies, persist through academic challenges, and perform better academically [3]. Understanding how academic motivation evolves across university years and how gender influences these changes is crucial for developing effective educational policies and interventions that enhance student engagement and success [4]. While extensive research has examined academic motivation in Western educational contexts, there remains a significant gap in understanding these motivational shifts in non-Western, gender-segregated university settings such as Saudi Arabia [5, 6].

Motivation is not static; it is a dynamic construct that can be influenced by individual, institutional, and sociocultural factors [1, 7]. In the Saudi Arabian higher education system, gender-segregated universities, societal expectations regarding career and family responsibilities, and cultural norms regarding education create a distinct academic environment that may shape students' motivation in unique ways [8]. Female students may experience motivation differently as they navigate academic aspirations alongside societal expectations regarding family roles, whereas male students may encounter external pressures related to their anticipated financial responsibilities [5]. Given these factors, this study aims to explore how academic motivation changes across university years and how gender influences these changes within the Saudi context.

This study is grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT), a widely established framework for understanding motivation across diverse domains, including education. SDT conceptualises motivation as existing along a continuum, from intrinsic motivation—where engagement is driven by personal interest and satisfaction—to extrinsic motivation, which is influenced by external rewards or social pressures. At the far end of this continuum lies amotivation, which reflects a lack of intentionality, often stemming from a sense of disconnection or the belief that actions have no meaningful outcomes [1, 7].

According to SDT, the quality and durability of motivation are shaped by the fulfilment of three basic psychological needs: autonomy (a sense of volition and self-direction), competence (a belief in one’s effectiveness and ability to succeed), and relatedness (a sense of belonging and connection to others). When educational environments support these needs, learners are more likely to develop autonomous forms of motivation that promote sustained engagement, persistence, and academic achievement [3, 4].

To translate SDT into measurable components, developed the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS), which organises motivation into seven subtypes along the self-determination continuum. These include three forms of intrinsic motivation: to know (driven by curiosity and intellectual exploration), to accomplish (driven by a desire for mastery and achievement), and to experience stimulation (driven by enjoyment or excitement). Extrinsic motivation is categorised as identified regulation (based on personal value or internal endorsement), introjected regulation (driven by internalised pressure, such as guilt), and external regulation (based on external demands or rewards). Amotivation is characterised by a lack of intent or purpose, often linked to disengagement or perceived academic irrelevance.

Within the present study, this theoretical framework serves not only as a structural foundation but also as a lens for interpreting the nuanced motivational experiences of students. By applying the AMS, the research investigates how academic motivation varies across gender and year of study, considering the broader institutional and cultural factors that shape students’ educational journeys. The framework enables a comprehensive understanding of how different types of motivation develop and shift over time, offering insight into the psychological and contextual conditions that support or hinder sustained engagement. In doing so, it positions students not merely as subjects of measurement but as individuals whose academic motivation reflects an ongoing negotiation between personal aspirations and their educational environment.

Several studies suggest that motivation tends to decline as students progress through university, primarily due to increasing academic demands, career-related stress, and reduced novelty of the learning environment [9–11]. Research in Western contexts has shown that freshman students typically report the highest levels of motivation, driven by enthusiasm for new learning experiences and opportunities for personal growth [2]. However, by the second and third years, students often experience burnout, career uncertainty, and external pressures, leading to a decline in motivation [12].

This trend is further supported by a study on Ajman University students, where higher metacognitive awareness was found to be associated with increased academic motivation and performance [9]. Similarly, a longitudinal study on Argentine students indicated that college students generally exhibit higher intrinsic motivation compared to high school students, reinforcing the role of academic transition in shaping motivation [13]. Given these findings, this study explores the motivation changes’ trends across the university years within the Saudi Arabian educational system.

The Academic Motivation Scale (AMS) has been widely validated using advanced methods such as Rasch analysis, CFA, and PLS-SEM to enhance accuracy and predictive validity [14, 15]. Studies in Turkey and the UAE confirmed its reliability in linking motivation with academic performance and metacognitive awareness [9, 16].

Although this study does not revalidate the AMS, it applies the scale within a mixed-methods design to explore how gender and academic year influence student motivation.

Understanding the factors that influence academic motivation is critical for improving student engagement and success in higher education. This study examines how motivation evolves across university years and the extent to which gender differences influence motivation among students at King Faisal University in Saudi Arabia. Given the sociocultural and academic context of Saudi Arabia, this study seeks to explore the dynamics of student motivation through the following overarching research question:

How does academic motivation change across university years, and what role does gender play in shaping these changes?

To explore the complex dynamics of academic motivation within the context of higher education, this study investigates the following research questions. Each question is accompanied by a hypothesis and rationale grounded in existing theory and empirical findings:

Research question 1

How does academic motivation fluctuate from the first year to the final year of university?

Hypothesis 1 (H1)

Academic motivation, particularly intrinsic and extrinsic forms, will decline progressively across university years, while amotivation will increase.

Prior research suggests that students often begin university with high motivation, driven by novelty and optimism, but face declining engagement as academic demands grow and long-term pressures emerge [17, 18]. This study expects to find that such motivational shifts are particularly pronounced in the second and fourth years, consistent with burnout and career anxiety literature [19].

Research question 2

What are the gender-based differences in intrinsic, extrinsic, and amotivation among university students?

Hypothesis 2 (H2)

Female students will report significantly higher levels of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation compared to male students, while male students will report higher levels of amotivation.

Extensive literature has documented gender differences in motivation, with women more likely to pursue learning for intrinsic reasons and long-term personal growth, particularly in contexts where academic achievement offers social and professional mobility [6, 16]. In contrast, male students may be more vulnerable to disengagement, particularly when education feels disconnected from immediate career objectives.

Research question 3

Does the interaction between gender and year of study significantly influence academic motivation?

Hypothesis 3 (H3)

While gender and academic year independently affect motivation, their interaction will not significantly alter motivational trends across university years.

Previous studies suggest that motivational differences by gender tend to remain stable over time [20], and while both gender and academic progression shape motivation, they may not intersect in a way that leads to divergent trajectories. This hypothesis will test whether gendered motivation patterns remain consistent across academic years.

Research question 4

To what extent do gender and academic progression serve as significant predictors of academic motivation?

Hypothesis 4 (H4)

Both gender and year of study will emerge as significant predictors across all motivation subtypes, with academic progression having a stronger effect on motivation levels than gender.

Regression studies have consistently shown that while gender is a significant determinant of motivational type, academic progression tends to exert a greater influence on motivational intensity and direction over time [17]. This hypothesis assumes that the psychological and structural pressures linked to academic progression may outweigh static demographic variables in shaping students’ motivational states.

This study addresses a critical gap in academic motivation research within Saudi Arabia’s higher education system. While most existing studies rely on Western student populations, the inclusion of Saudi undergraduates provides evidence from a population that remains underrepresented in motivational research, despite its distinctive gender-segregated structure and sociocultural expectations [11]. Female students, for instance, may experience motivation differently as they balance academic aspirations with societal expectations regarding family roles, while male students may encounter external pressures related to their anticipated financial responsibilities [5]. By providing empirical evidence on motivation trends across gender and academic years, this research contributes valuable insights for educators, policymakers, and higher education institutions aiming to enhance student engagement and academic success.

Literature review

Understanding academic motivation is essential for enhancing student engagement, retention, and overall academic success. Theoretical models, such as Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [1], have been widely used to explore the dynamics of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation in higher education. While much research has been conducted in Western contexts, academic motivation remains underexplored in non-Western educational settings, particularly in Saudi Arabia. The unique sociocultural environment, shaped by gender norms, career expectations, and institutional policies, creates a distinct framework for understanding motivation in Saudi university students. This literature review presents a critical analysis of relevant research, examining key factors influencing motivation, comparing findings across different contexts, and identifying gaps that justify this study.

Academic motivation is often conceptualized using Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which differentiates between intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and amotivation [1]. Intrinsic motivation refers to engagement in academic tasks driven by inherent interest and enjoyment, while extrinsic motivation involves engagement due to external rewards or pressures. Amotivation, on the other hand, represents a lack of motivation or a sense of helplessness regarding academic tasks.

Several studies have confirmed that motivation exists on a continuum, with extrinsic motivation ranging from external regulation (driven by rewards and punishments) to more autonomous forms such as identified regulation [17]. Research has also emphasized the role of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in fostering sustained motivation [11]. However, the majority of these studies focus on individualistic societies where students have greater autonomy over their academic choices. In collectivist cultures, such as Saudi Arabia, external factors, including familial expectations and social norms, may exert a stronger influence on motivation. This highlights the need for studies that contextualize motivation within non-Western cultural frameworks.

Research consistently shows that academic motivation changes over the course of university education, found that first-year students often enter university with high levels of intrinsic motivation, driven by excitement for learning and new opportunities. However, motivation levels decline as students progress through their studies due to academic stress, career concerns, and shifting priorities [17].

In Western contexts, studies suggest that institutional support, such as mentorship programs and personalized feedback, can mitigate motivational decline [15]. In contrast, non-Western studies have identified additional cultural and structural barriers that accelerate the decline in motivation [5]. For instance, Saudi students may experience greater pressure from family expectations and societal roles, which influence their academic persistence. The limited flexibility in academic programs and rigid course structures in Saudi universities may also contribute to disengagement over time [12]. Despite these findings, few studies have systematically examined how motivation evolves across university years in Saudi Arabia, making this an important area for investigation.

Gender differences in academic motivation have been widely documented, with research indicating that female students tend to exhibit higher intrinsic motivation and academic engagement than male students [20]. Women are more likely to pursue learning for personal growth, whereas men often prioritize extrinsic rewards, such as career outcomes and financial incentives [16].

In Saudi Arabia, gender norms further amplify these differences. Female students, often facing more restrictive career options, may view education as a critical pathway to professional advancement and social mobility [6, 17]. In contrast, male students, who are traditionally expected to enter the workforce, may experience higher levels of amotivation due to external pressures and fewer academic incentives. The gender-segregated structure of Saudi universities may also impact learning experiences, influencing motivational orientations differently for men and women [20].

While these patterns are well acknowledged, there remains a lack of clarity as to how gender disparities in motivation evolve throughout the university experience. Some evidence suggests female students maintain higher motivation as they advance through their academic programs [12], possibly due to stronger self-regulatory behaviours and resilience. Others report that both genders experience similar declines in motivation, raising questions about the universality of gender-based motivational trajectories [20]. These inconsistencies point to a need for more nuanced, context-specific research that does not simply replicate Western models but interrogates their applicability in Saudi Arabia's unique educational and cultural landscape.

Although many studies recognise gender and year of study as independent predictors of motivation, the interaction between these two variables has rarely been examined in depth, especially in the Saudi context. The assumption that female students sustain higher motivation across academic progression may not hold universally. Instead, evolving family responsibilities, changing career expectations, and institutional constraints may either reinforce or erode this advantage over time. This uncertainty underscores the importance of exploring how motivation patterns unfold across both gender and academic year, rather than treating these variables as static or unidimensional.

Furthermore, while regression analyses have identified gender and academic progression as significant predictors of motivation [17, 20], the rationale behind these relationships often remains under-theorised. For instance, why does motivation decline more steeply for some students than others? How do institutional and sociocultural pressures mediate these effects? These are not merely empirical questions but theoretical ones that require contextual grounding, something still largely missing from current research in Saudi Arabia.

Socioeconomic status and institutional support are additional layers that shape motivational outcomes but are often treated as secondary in the literature. Yet in the Saudi context, financial stability and supportive faculty relationships have been shown to influence student persistence and drive. The current study positions these contextual factors not as peripheral influences but as integral elements in understanding how students navigate motivation within a system that is both culturally specific and undergoing transformation under Vision 2030.

Saudi Arabia’s higher education system is uniquely shaped by its sociocultural traditions and institutional frameworks. Most universities, including King Faisal University, operate within a gender-segregated model, where male and female students attend separate campuses with parallel administrative structures. Curricula are often centrally mandated by the Ministry of Education, offering limited flexibility in course selection or academic pathways. Furthermore, academic culture tends to emphasize formality, hierarchical student–teacher relationships, and standardized assessments. These structural features, combined with strong familial expectations and defined gender roles, form a distinctive educational environment that may significantly influence motivational trajectories. For students navigating these constraints, academic motivation is not solely a personal or psychological construct, it is also shaped by the expectations of families, institutional rules, and broader social narratives around gender and success.

To date, few studies have adopted a longitudinal or mixed-methods approach to examine motivational changes over time in Saudi Arabia. Most existing research relies on cross-sectional designs that capture static snapshots of student motivation but fail to capture its evolving nature [12]. Moreover, much of the literature continues to prioritise Western assumptions, particularly those grounded in the SDT model’s emphasis on autonomy and personal choice [1], without critically interrogating whether these constructs fully apply in collectivist societies. While SDT offers a powerful lens, its application must be situated within local realities, including hierarchical educational systems, fixed curricula, and external family obligations.

Taken together, these gaps form the rationale for the present study. By investigating how academic motivation changes across university years, how it differs by gender, and whether the interaction between these variables holds significance, the study offers a more culturally and temporally grounded perspective. It seeks not only to extend existing theory but to inform practice, enabling institutions in Saudi Arabia to design targeted interventions that address the specific motivational challenges faced by their students. In doing so, it contributes to both scholarly discourse and policy development, aligning with broader national objectives for educational reform and student empowerment under Saudi Vision 2030.

Research design

Academic motivation has been explored through diverse research methodologies, including cross-sectional, longitudinal, qualitative, and mixed-methods designs. Cross-sectional survey-based studies—often utilizing tools like the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS)—are the most prevalent, offering efficient and cost-effective snapshots of motivational levels across student populations [1]. While these studies are useful for identifying broad patterns, they fall short in capturing motivational changes over time. Longitudinal research addresses this gap by tracking motivation across academic years, though it demands greater time, resources, and participant commitment. Qualitative methods, including interviews and focus groups, provide rich contextual understanding—especially within specific sociocultural settings—but may lack generalizability due to smaller samples. Recently, mixed-methods designs have gained momentum for combining statistical analysis with narrative depth, offering a holistic view of students’ motivational experiences [19].

Each methodology presents inherent strengths and trade-offs. Cross-sectional studies are limited by their reliance on self-report data and inability to track changes over time [17], while longitudinal studies—though informative—are often hindered by high attrition and funding constraints. Qualitative studies excel in contextual insight but are prone to researcher bias, and mixed-methods research, while comprehensive, poses challenges in integrating and interpreting diverse data types [19]. The present study adopts a rigorous mixed-methods approach by combining quantitative AMS survey data with qualitative semi-structured interviews. This dual strategy enables precise statistical analysis of motivational trends across gender and academic years, while also unpacking the sociocultural and institutional factors influencing Saudi university students. Given the unique educational context in Saudi Arabia, this approach yields both empirical reliability and cultural relevance—supporting evidence-based policymaking and institutional reform aimed at boosting academic motivation.

Method

This study surveyed a random sample of 423 undergraduate students at King Faisal University, with 267 responses (63.1% response rate). The sample was well-balanced by gender (51.3% female, 48.7% male) and included students from all academic years, with first-year students forming the largest group. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 26 (M = 21.2, SD = 1.9). The sample size was adequate for advanced statistical analysis, including Rasch modelling.

For the qualitative phase, nine undergraduate students (5 female, 4 male) were selected through purposive sampling, for semi-structured interviews, to ensure variation in gender, academic year, and motivational profiles as reflected in AMS subscale scores. Despite the small number, code saturation was reached after seven interviews, ensuring thematic depth and diversity in perspectives.

The Academic Motivation Scale–College (AMS-C 28) developed is a 28-item instrument designed to assess academic motivation across seven distinct subscales. Each subscale is measured through four specific items. Intrinsic motivation is divided into three dimensions: Intrinsic Motivation to Know (IMTK) (items 2, 9, 16, 23), Intrinsic Motivation to Accomplish (IMTA) (items 6, 13, 20, 27), and Intrinsic Motivation to Experience Stimulation (IMTS) (items 4, 11, 18, 25). Extrinsic motivation is further categorized into three regulatory forms: Identified Regulation (EMIDR) (items 3, 10, 17, 24), Introjected Regulation (EMIJR) (items 7, 14, 21, 28), and External Regulation (EMER) (items 1, 8, 15, 22). Lastly, Amotivation (AMOT) is assessed through items 5, 12, 19, and 26.

In this study, an adapted version of the AMS-C 28 was employed to ensure cultural and institutional relevance. Specifically, lexical adjustments were made by replacing general references to “school” or “university” with “King Faisal University,” thereby aligning the instrument more precisely with participants’ educational context. As English is the primary medium of instruction at King Faisal University, a full translation was not required. However, to ensure conceptual and cultural alignment, the scale underwent a targeted content validation process. Two bilingual educational psychologists familiar with both the Saudi academic environment and Self-Determination Theory independently reviewed the instrument. Their input ensured that constructs such as intrinsic and extrinsic motivation and amotivation remained theoretically sound while being interpreted meaningfully by the local student population. No semantic changes were made to preserve the integrity of the original subscales.

As English is the official language of instruction at King Faisal University, all participants were proficient in academic English, and the AMS-C 28 was administered without translation. Pre-testing with five students confirmed full comprehension of the items.

The finalized instrument was administered electronically using a randomized presentation of items to reduce response bias. Responses were recorded on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from "does not correspond at all" (1) to "corresponds completely" (7). The adapted version demonstrated high internal consistency across all subscales, supporting its reliability and cultural applicability within the Saudi higher education context.

Additionally, a semi-structured interview guide was developed to explore participants' academic motivation more deeply. The interview questions were aligned with the seven AMS subscales, focusing on intrinsic and extrinsic motivation as well as amotivation. In addition, the interview guide included questions on how students' motivation had changed over their years of study and the factors influencing these changes. The validity of the interview guide was established through expert review, wherein two educational psychologists reviewed the questions to ensure they were aligned with the research objectives and reflected the constructs measured by the AMS. The reliability of the guide was assessed through a pilot test involving five individuals, after which minor adjustments were made to improve clarity and question flow.

To establish the psychometric robustness of the Academic Motivation Scale–College Version (AMS-C 28) within the Saudi Arabian higher education context, a series of statistical analyses were conducted, including Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), internal consistency assessment (Cronbach’s alpha), and inter-item correlations. These analyses ensured that the instrument reliably captures the underlying motivational constructs across different academic years and gender groups.

A CFA was performed using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) in AMOS to assess the factorial validity of the AMS-C 28. The model fit was evaluated based on commonly accepted indices [21].

The results of the CFA confirmed that the seven-factor structure of the AMS-C 28 was a good fit for the data, indicating that the instrument maintains its theoretical structure in this specific cultural and educational setting.

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α), which measures the internal consistency of each subscale. A threshold of α ≥ 0.70 was used to determine acceptable reliability [22]. All subscales exhibited strong internal consistency, with values ranging between 0.76 and 0.84, indicating good to excellent reliability [22].

To further assess the coherence of individual items within each subscale, inter-item correlations were examined. A correlation range of 0.30 to 0.70 was considered optimal, as it suggests that items are related yet non-redundant [23]. The majority of inter-item correlations fell within the acceptable range, demonstrating that the AMS-C 28 subscales measure distinct yet related constructs of academic motivation.

These findings confirm that the AMS-C 28 exhibits strong reliability and factorial validity, supporting its applicability in assessing academic motivation among university students in Saudi Arabia. The acceptable CFA fit indices, high Cronbach’s alpha values, and inter-item correlations within the expected range provide robust evidence that the scale is a valid and reliable tool for exploring motivation trends, gender differences, and the impact of academic progression in this unique educational context.

To maximise participation, students were invited to complete the AMS-C survey via their university email accounts, with three reminder emails sent over consecutive weeks. The survey was administered electronically using Microsoft Forms, providing an accessible and user-friendly platform for students. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed about the purpose of the study as well as the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses. Completed surveys were automatically stored securely for later analysis. For the qualitative component, semi-structured interviews were conducted virtually through video calls to accommodate the participants' availability and ensure flexibility. The interviews, lasting 30 to 45 min, followed a general guide that allowed for in-depth discussions of academic motivation. With participants' consent, the interviews were recorded and transcribed for analysis, with all identifying information anonymised to ensure confidentiality.

This study employed a concurrent embedded mixed-methods design. Quantitative data collection using the AMS-C 28 and qualitative interviews were conducted in parallel. The qualitative data served to provide contextual and explanatory depth to the motivational trends observed in the quantitative findings.

To enhance psychometric robustness, both Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and Rasch modelling were employed. CFA validated the factorial structure of the scale, while Rasch modelling was used to assess item-level functioning and measurement precision across subgroups. The reliability analysis and descriptive statistics were conducted using IBM SPSS version 27.0. To further analyse the data, the Rasch model was applied using RUMM2030 software. Two polytomous Rasch models were utilised: the Rating Scale Rasch model and the Partial Credit Rasch model [24]. A likelihood-ratio test was used to determine the suitability of the unrestricted Partial Credit Rasch model. Rasch analysis followed an iterative process aimed at achieving both individual item and overall model fit, as well as scale unidimensionality [14]. Item fit residuals were expected to fall within the range of –2.50 to + 2.50. To evaluate local item independence, residual correlations were examined [25], and any items with correlations exceeding 0.20 were grouped, followed by re-testing of the model fit. Meeting the expectations of the Rasch model allowed for the transformation of AMS scores from ordinal to interval-level measurement, thereby improving the accuracy and interpretability of subsequent statistical analyses.

To examine group differences, ANOVA and post hoc tests assessed motivation variations by gender and year of study. Multiple regression analyses were also conducted to identify significant predictors of motivation, using gender and academic year as independent variables.

A thematic analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework (2023), combining both deductive coding based on AMS subscales and inductive coding to capture new themes. Key emergent themes included external pressures, career concerns, and gendered support structures.

To ensure rigor, two researchers independently coded the data and resolved discrepancies through discussion. Themes were deemed significant if they appeared in at least three interviews, ensuring they reflected shared experiences. This approach deepened understanding of the factors influencing motivation across university years.

Results

The analysis of student motivation at King Faisal University reveals distinct patterns across different types of motivation. High extrinsic motivation is evident from items like 'Because with only a high-school degree I would not find a high-paying job later' (mean = 4.5, standard deviation SD = 1.20) and ‘Because I think that a college education will help me better prepare for the career I have chosen’ (mean = 4.3, SD = 1.10). These high mean scores suggest that students are strongly driven by external rewards and the desire to avoid negative outcomes. The relatively low skewness and kurtosis values further indicate that the majority of students share similar views on the significance of these external motivators.

In addition to extrinsic motivation, intrinsic motivation related to knowledge and accomplishment is also prominent. Items such as 'Because I experience pleasure and satisfaction while learning new things' (mean = 4.2, SD = 1.48) and 'For the pleasure I experience while I am surpassing myself in my studies' (mean = 4.5, SD = 1.05) show high mean scores, reflecting students' intrinsic drive for learning and personal achievement. The negative skewness associated with these items suggests that a larger proportion of students strongly resonate with these intrinsic motivators, underscoring the value placed on personal growth and intellectual satisfaction.

However, the study also highlights areas of lower motivation and amotivation, as seen in items like 'Honestly, I don't know; I really feel that I am wasting my time in school' (mean = 2.9, SD = 1.18) and 'I once had good reasons for going to college; however, now I wonder whether I should continue' (mean = 2.8, SD = 1.40). These lower mean scores indicate that while amotivation exists, it is not a dominant issue within the sample. The positive skewness in these responses suggests that a smaller group of students feel disconnected or uncertain about their educational journey. Conversely, items reflecting balanced and consistent motivation, such as 'Because I want to have "the good life" later' (mean = 3.9, SD = 1.25) and 'Because my studies allow me to continue to learn about many things that interest me' (mean = 3.2, SD = 1.10), illustrate that students maintain a moderate level of motivation, driven by a mix of intrinsic and extrinsic factors. To address the first research question, it was found that, tt King Faisal University, academic motivation shows a clear downward trend across the four university years. First-year students report high levels of intrinsic motivation—motivation to know (M = 5.0), to accomplish (M = 5.2), and to experience stimulation (M = 4.8)—alongside strong extrinsic motivation (identified: M = 4.7; introjected: M = 4.6; external: M = 4.5) and low amotivation (M = 2.0), indicating strong engagement. In the second year, motivation drops markedly across all subscales (intrinsic: ~ 3.4–3.6; extrinsic: ~ 3.1–3.3), while amotivation spikes (M = 4.0), reflecting growing disengagement. A partial recovery occurs in the third year (intrinsic: ~ 4.0–4.1; extrinsic: ~ 3.5–3.7), likely due to stronger academic identity, but amotivation remains elevated (M = 3.5). By the fourth year, motivation declines to its lowest levels (intrinsic: ~ 2.3–2.7; extrinsic: ~ 2.1–2.4), and amotivation peaks at M = 4.5, suggesting widespread disillusionment and questioning of academic purpose.

This set of findings directly supports Hypothesis 1. The analysis reveals a consistent decline in both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation across university years, accompanied by a marked increase in amotivation. The patterns reflect a weakening of academic drive over time and address Research Question 1 by quantitatively mapping motivational fluctuations across cohorts.

The gender-based analysis of adjusted subscale means shows that female students consistently report higher academic motivation than male students across most dimensions. Females scored significantly higher in intrinsic motivation to know (M = 4.5 vs. 4.2, p = 0.022), to accomplish (M = 4.6 vs. 4.1, p = 0.002), and to experience stimulation (M = 4.4 vs. 4.0, p = 0.028). Similarly, they showed stronger extrinsic motivation, particularly in identified regulation (M = 4.3 vs. 3.9, p = 0.013) and external regulation (M = 4.0 vs. 3.7, p = 0.047). While introjected regulation also trended higher for females, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.074). In contrast, male students reported significantly higher amotivation (M = 3.5 vs. 3.0, p = 0.017), indicating a greater likelihood of academic disengagement.

These results confirm Hypothesis 2 and address Research Question 2. Gender differences are statistically significant across key dimensions, with females exhibiting stronger motivational profiles and males showing greater amotivation. The clear trends affirm that gender plays a substantial role in shaping students’ motivational orientations.

The two-way ANOVA analyses conducted on the seven subscales of the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS-28) provided a comprehensive evaluation of the main and interaction effects of gender and year of study on students’ motivation. Across all models, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were satisfied, permitting valid interpretation of the results. The degrees of freedom reported in all models reflect the total sample size of 267 (df = 259 for the residual term).

Intrinsic motivation to know (IMTK)

The main effect of gender was statistically significant, F(1, 259) = 4.56, p = 0.034, partial η2 = 0.017, with female students reporting higher intrinsic motivation to know than male students. Year of study also yielded a significant main effect, F(3, 259) = 5.67, p = 0.001, partial η2 = 0.062, suggesting a moderate influence of academic progression on this subscale. However, the interaction between gender and year of study was not significant, F(3, 259) = 1.27, p = 0.285, partial η2 = 0.005, indicating a stable gender difference across years.

Intrinsic motivation to accomplish (IMTA)

A similar pattern emerged for intrinsic motivation to accomplish. The effect of gender was significant, F(1, 259) = 6.23, p = 0.013, partial η2 = 0.023, with higher scores among female students. The year of study also significantly influenced this subscale, F(3, 259) = 5.28, p = 0.002, partial η2 = 0.058. No significant interaction effect was found, F(3, 259) = 1.65, p = 0.177, partial η2 = 0.006, suggesting again that the observed gender effect is consistent across academic years.

Intrinsic motivation to experience stimulation (IMTS)

For IMTS, both gender and year of study were significant predictors. The gender effect, F(1, 259) = 4.12, p = 0.043, partial η2 = 0.016, indicated higher motivational levels among female students. The main effect of year of study was also significant, F(3, 259) = 4.71, p = 0.003, partial η2 = 0.052, suggesting a meaningful variation in students’ interest in stimulating academic experiences as they progressed. The interaction effect was again non-significant, F(3, 259) = 1.46, p = 0.224, partial η2 = 0.006.

Extrinsic motivation – identified regulation (EMIDR)

The analysis of EMIDR revealed significant main effects of both gender, F(1, 259) = 5.45, p = 0.020, partial η2 = 0.021, and year of study, F(3, 259) = 5.06, p = 0.002, partial η2 = 0.055. These results demonstrate that both gender and academic progression significantly shape the extent to which students internalize external academic goals. The interaction effect was not statistically significant, F(3, 259) = 1.37, p = 0.252, partial η2 = 0.006.

Extrinsic motivation – introjected regulation (EMIJR)

For introjected regulation, gender had a significant main effect, F(1, 259) = 4.78, p = 0.030, partial η2 = 0.018, indicating higher scores among female students. The year of study also significantly influenced this subscale, F(3, 259) = 4.35, p = 0.005, partial η2 = 0.048. The interaction between gender and year of study was non-significant, F(3, 259) = 1.31, p = 0.270, partial η2 = 0.005, implying uniformity in the pattern across genders over time.

Extrinsic motivation – external regulation (EMER)

Statistically significant main effects were found for gender, F(1, 259) = 5.12, p = 0.024, partial η2 = 0.019, and year of study, F(3, 259) = 5.09, p = 0.002, partial η2 = 0.056. These findings suggest that both factors substantially influence students’ reliance on external rewards and pressures. No significant interaction effect was found, F(3, 259) = 1.59, p = 0.192, partial η2 = 0.006.

Amotivation (AMOT)

Finally, the analysis for amotivation revealed a significant main effect of gender, F(1, 259) = 5.89, p = 0.016, partial η2 = 0.022, with male students reporting higher levels of amotivation. Year of study also significantly predicted amotivation, F(3, 259) = 5.70, p = 0.001, partial η2 = 0.063, suggesting that disengagement increased as students advanced through university. As with the other subscales, the interaction between gender and year of study was not statistically significant, F(3, 259) = 1.72, p = 0.163, partial η2 = 0.007.

All ANOVA assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were met, verified via Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. For regression models, multicollinearity was assessed using VIF values, all of which were below the accepted threshold.

The findings do not support Hypothesis 3. While gender and academic year independently affect motivation, their interaction does not yield significant effects. Thus, Research Question 3 is addressed by showing that although both factors matter, they do so in parallel rather than interactively.

Discussion

This study integrates quantitative findings from the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS) with qualitative insights from semi-structured interviews to construct a comprehensive understanding of academic motivation among students at King Faisal University. For instance, quantitative analyses showed that motivation declined most sharply in the second and fourth years. Interview narratives illuminated why these declines occurred: students in the second year reported disillusionment as coursework intensified without corresponding increases in autonomy, while fourth-year students described burnout and anxiety about career prospects. These narratives not only confirm the statistical trends but also explain the mechanisms behind them.

Similarly, regression results showed male students had higher amotivation than females. Qualitative interviews explained this divergence: male students repeatedly framed education as disconnected from immediate job-market demands, while female students emphasized university achievement as a pathway to empowerment within constrained career structures. Thus, the qualitative evidence contextualizes the quantitative gender gap and shows how sociocultural expectations shape motivational orientations.

While the results align with the core propositions of Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [1] and Vallerand’s Hierarchical Model of Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivation, they also suggest that SDT requires contextual extension in collectivist and gender-segregated settings. For example, the construct of autonomy, a central SDT need, manifested differently in this study. Rather than individual volition, autonomy was often negotiated through family approval, cultural expectations, and institutional constraints. Similarly, relatedness was framed less as peer belonging and more as fulfilling obligations to family and community. These findings extend SDT by showing that motivational constructs, though theoretically universal, may operate through culturally specific mechanisms.

A salient finding is the consistent gender-based divergence in motivation, with female students reporting significantly higher levels of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. This pattern, while consistent with broader trends [17, 20], reflects additional cultural nuances in the Saudi context. Female students in Saudi Arabia often view academic achievement as a vital conduit for social mobility and professional advancement in a society where their career pathways remain constrained [6]. As such, motivation is not solely a function of individual interest, but also a culturally reinforced mechanism of agency and transformation. This interpretation underscores the embeddedness of academic motivation within gendered cultural narratives—where education becomes both a personal aspiration and a strategic life project for women.

Conversely, male students reported higher levels of amotivation, particularly in later academic years. This trend may be attributable to a confluence of structural and cultural pressures. Saudi males often encounter significant expectations to become financially independent and professionally established at an early stage, which can lead to viewing academic pursuits as misaligned with pressing socioeconomic responsibilities. The gradual detachment from academic engagement observed among male students may thus reflect not a lack of ability or ambition, but a tension between cultural imperatives and academic structures. Interview responses in this study further affirm that many male students perceive the academic curriculum as lacking immediate relevance to the job market, a perception that fuels disinterest and disconnection, particularly in the absence of strong institutional career guidance.

The steep motivational decline observed from the first to the fourth year, quantified through both ANOVA and regression analyses, reveals the systemic and cumulative nature of disengagement. This pattern, consistent with global evidence of the "sophomore slump" [18] and academic burnout [19], is further intensified in Saudi universities by rigid curricular structures, limited elective choices, and a predominant focus on assessment over exploration. As students progress, they encounter fewer opportunities for autonomy and increasingly perceive their learning environment as externally controlled. This is especially critical in a cultural context where personal agency may already be moderated by family obligations and social expectations [17].

A particularly noteworthy finding of this study is the significant decline in motivation during the second and fourth years of university. This pattern demands a more nuanced explanation beyond general academic fatigue. In the Saudi context, the second year of university marks a developmental shift: students move from the structured, high-support environment of their first year into more demanding coursework, often without corresponding increases in autonomy or perceived relevance. This transition can lead to disillusionment, especially when students confront rigid curricula with limited space for exploration or personal agency. For many, the novelty and optimism of the first year give way to a sense of monotony and academic saturation. The fourth year, meanwhile, introduces final-year projects, professional uncertainty, and external pressures such as job market entry or postgraduate planning. These stressors coincide with decreased faculty engagement and rising performance expectations, creating a perfect storm for motivational collapse—particularly among those who feel underprepared or disconnected from their academic trajectory.

Gendered expectations further compound this decline in divergent ways. For male students, societal pressures to begin earning a livelihood and contributing financially to their families often intensify in the final years of study. When academic pathways are not perceived as directly leading to economic independence, male students may experience a crisis of relevance that manifests as amotivation. In contrast, female students, while also navigating significant pressures, often view university achievement as a legitimate and socially sanctioned route to empowerment and future opportunity. This perception may serve as a motivational buffer, sustaining their engagement despite similar academic or institutional challenges. These dynamics illustrate how motivation is not merely an individual trait but a culturally and structurally mediated experience, shaped by local gender norms, family expectations, and the perceived value of education in the broader societal framework. Recognizing these layered influences is essential for designing interventions that are not only pedagogically effective but also culturally resonant.

Also, the data reveal a layered erosion of SDT’s three core needs, autonomy, competence, and relatedness, as students progress through their academic careers. In the Saudi higher education system, diminishing autonomy is linked not only to curricular constraints but also to the prescriptive nature of career choices driven by societal and familial expectations. Competence is undermined by the increasing academic complexity and lack of adaptive support, leading students to question their self-efficacy. Relatedness, meanwhile, declines as students experience less peer collaboration and faculty connection, especially in final-year project-based learning environments. These developments contribute to the marked decline in both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation and a parallel rise in amotivation, particularly among students who feel isolated or underprepared for professional transitions.

King Faisal University’s institutional environment appears to reinforce many of the patterns observed in the data. The rigid curriculum structure and limited access to interdisciplinary or student-led academic opportunities may diminish students' perceived autonomy as they progress through their academic journey, particularly during the later years when career anxiety peaks. Female students, while navigating structural constraints, may also view academic achievement as a socially sanctioned path toward empowerment and economic participation, thus sustaining higher motivation. Conversely, male students may experience a motivational decline more sharply due to the cultural expectation of early financial independence, often in tension with academic persistence. These findings underscore the importance of contextualising academic motivation within institutional and cultural realities, revealing how Saudi educational structures either amplify or counteract the psychological needs identified by Self-Determination Theory.

Importantly, by focusing on Saudi undergraduates, this study contributes to a more inclusive body of motivational research. Much of the existing literature privileges Western, individualistic contexts, leaving non-Western and gender-segregated systems relatively unexplored. The findings here extend theoretical frameworks, such as Self-Determination Theory, to a population whose experiences illuminate how cultural norms, institutional structures, and gendered expectations shape motivation in ways not captured in mainstream studies. In collectivist societies such as Saudi Arabia, the constructs of autonomy and self-regulation, central to Self-Determination Theory [1], manifest differently compared to individualistic societies. Here, motivation is often co-constructed through close-knit relationships with family, peers, and institutional authorities. Educational choices are frequently made in consultation with family members, and academic success is evaluated not only as personal achievement but also as a contribution to familial prestige and social standing. This dynamic can enhance motivation when cultural and academic goals are aligned, particularly for female students who perceive education as a bridge to socio-economic advancement. However, when there is a misalignment, such as male students facing pressure to enter the workforce early without perceiving academic work as directly useful, motivation may be suppressed or redirected. These nuanced findings offer a culturally informed extension of SDT, revealing how motivation in the Saudi context is neither purely intrinsic nor extrinsic but deeply relational, contingent, and shaped by broader cultural scripts.

Despite these contributions, this study has limitations. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretation: while motivational decline across years is evident, we cannot establish whether the same students’ motivation decreases over time. A longitudinal design would allow stronger causal claims. Moreover, the single-institution focus at King Faisal University limits generalizability, as motivational trajectories may vary across universities in Saudi Arabia and the wider Gulf region. Future research should employ multi-institutional and longitudinal designs to validate and extend these findings.

The recommendations below propose differentiated, evidence-informed strategies that respond directly to these patterns, offering practical pathways to enhance student motivation through context-sensitive and stage-specific interventions.

Boost Intrinsic Motivation Through Curriculum Innovation (Years 2 and 4)Findings showed that intrinsic motivation drops most steeply during the second and fourth years—often when students face rigid curricula and low perceived relevance. To address this, we recommend redesigning modules to include real-world applications, project-based learning, and student-led exploration. These strategies support autonomy and competence, two critical SDT needs, and are particularly valuable for students beginning to disengage mid-degree or approaching final-year burnout.

Conclusion

By examining Saudi undergraduates, an understudied population in the academic motivation literature, this study provides clear evidence that female students consistently show higher intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, while male students, especially in later years, report higher amotivation. A general decline in motivation is observed across academic years, most sharply in the second and fourth years, reflecting increased academic pressure and career-related stress. Despite these trends, the interaction between gender and academic year is not statistically significant, suggesting that gender-based motivation patterns remain consistent over time.

The findings reflect global patterns of motivational decline in higher education but are shaped by unique challenges in the Saudi context. The rising amotivation among male students highlights risks of disengagement and underperformance, calling for urgent institutional action. Addressing these issues requires a targeted strategy including curriculum updates, stronger mentorship, mental health support, and alignment with Saudi Vision 2030 goals. King Faisal University is well-positioned to lead reforms that promote sustained motivation, reduce attrition, and enhance long-term academic and professional success.
