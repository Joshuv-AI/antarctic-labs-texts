Chemoproteomic Profiling of Reactive Cysteines in Response to Oxidative Stress Induced by 6‐Hydroxydopamine
Authors: Rayan Murtada, Chiho Kim, Xu‐dong Wang, Youkai Yang, Yonghao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough oxidative stress is a well‐established driver of neurodegeneration, it remains poorly understood as to how the global cysteine (Cys) proteome is remodeled under oxidative stress conditions. Proteins with aberrantly modified cysteines in response to oxidative stress can induce and exacerbate neurodegeneration, contributing to disorders like Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, frontotemporal dementia, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. In this study, we induced oxidative stress in SH‐SY5Y neuronal cells by subjecting them to the neurotoxin 6‐hydroxydopamine (6‐OHDA). To identify proteins with altered cysteine oxidation or PTM status, we used a desthiobiotin iodoacetamide (DBIA) probe, which selectively labels cysteines with unmodified and preserved thiols. Using these unbiased chemoproteomic strategies, we identified proteins with reduced Cys reactivity to DBIA in response to 6‐OHDA‐induced oxidative stress. Many of these proteins are critically involved in biological processes linked to cell stress responses (e.g., mitochondrial oxidative stress and apoptosis). Furthermore, we found that two key Cys on UCHL1 (a deubiquitinase critically involved in neurodegeneration) exhibited enhanced reactivity under oxidative stress conditions. Our study defines the remodeling of the Cys proteome under 6‐OHDA‐induced oxidative stress conditions. Furthermore, these findings suggest potential cysteine‐mediated regulatory mechanisms in response to oxidative stress, providing a valuable resource for further exploration of cysteine modifications in the context of neurodegenerative signaling.

Main Text

Oxidative stress occurs when the balance between pro‐oxidant and antioxidant mechanisms is disrupted, leading to the creation of harmful reactive oxygen species (ROS) [1]. This stress causes extensive damage to cellular components like lipids, proteins, and DNA, resulting in cell death through various pathways involving the activation or deactivation of different apoptotic signaling molecules [2]. For example, the unchecked production of ROS disrupts calcium balance, leading to either excitotoxic cell death or dysfunction of mitochondria, triggering the apoptotic caspase cascade [3, 4]. Previous studies have provided evidence that points to the crucial role of heightened oxidative stress in driving neuronal degeneration, especially in neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [3, 5]. For example, in the context of PD, dopaminergic (DA) neurons are known to be sensitive to oxidative stress, and various studies have shown that ROS could cause mitochondrial damages, leading to the death of DA neurons in patients with PD [6, 7, 8]. Indeed, the neurotoxin 6‐hydroxydopamine (6‐OHDA) is often used to study the causes and progression of neurodegeneration, especially in cellular and animal models of Parkinson's disease [9, 10, 11]. 6‐OHDA has been observed to impair mitochondrial function and activate caspases in various cell lines and primary neuronal cultures [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Insights from studies involving 6‐OHDA suggest that cell death induced through oxidative stress significantly contributes to the selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD models. 6‐OHDA undergoes auto‐oxidation to generate oxidative products like p‐quinones, hydrogen peroxide, superoxide anions, and hydroxyl radicals [18, 19]. These oxidative products can mediate the activation of caspases 3/7, triggering subsequent apoptotic cell death.

Over the past two decades, ROS has emerged as a crucial regulator of intracellular signaling pathways [20, 21]. The effects of ROS are regulated, in part, through the modification of specific cysteine (Cys) residues on target proteins that are sensitive to redox changes. This oxidative post‐translational modification (Ox‐PTM) of Cys residues plays a vital role in governing protein structure and function [22]. Despite only 2%–3% of the human proteome encoding Cys residues, their functionality could vary depending on their oxidative states (i.e., reduced or oxidized) within proteins [23, 24]. Although many strides have been made toward understanding the oxidation states of specific Cys residues, it remains poorly understood how 6‐OHDA causes neuronal cell death via crucial thiol dysregulation.

In this study, we performed global Cys reactivity profiling using a methodology known as streamlined cysteine activity‐based protein profiling (SLC‐ABPP) [25]. The key to the SLC‐ABPP technology is the utilization of the desthiobiotin iodoacetamide (DBIA) probe, which reacts with Cys residues on the global proteome level. The DBIA molecule has three moieties, consisting of an iodoacetamide group, a desthiobiotin group, and a linker connecting them. The reaction between DBIA and the Cys thiol group introduces a desthiobiotin moiety onto the peptide and enables the DBIA‐labeled peptides to be enriched with the use of streptavidin beads. However, the redox change of a Cys residue (e.g., oxidation and disulfide bond formation) blocks this Cys and prevents it from further reacting with the DBIA probe [25]. The “drop‐out” of a peptide from the global Cys‐containing peptide pools points to a Cys residue with altered reactivity (e.g., redox states) against the DBIA probe. However, a limitation of this workflow is that it cannot fully decipher this “drop‐out” as a direct consequence of redox changes. The ratio of the Cys‐containing peptide abundance in the control versus the treatment group is referred to as the competition ratio (CR), which is a means to quantify the degree of altered reactivity of a Cys residue [25].

Our analysis was performed using 6‐OHDA‐treated human neuroblastoma SH‐SY5Y cells, which is a widely used cell line model for Parkinson's disease [26]. Briefly, SH‐SY5Y cells were treated with either DMSO control, or 6‐OHDA (100 µM) over the course of two different time durations of 1 and 6 h, respectively (Figures 1 and 2). SH‐SY5Y cells exposed to the 6‐OHDA treatment showed reduced cell survival relative to the control (Figure 3A). The cells were lysed, and the proteins were extracted for reaction with the desthiobiotin iodoacetamide (DBIA) probe. During this reaction, unmodified Cys residues were tagged with the DBIA probe via the reaction between DBIA and the thiol group in a Cys residue. On the contrary, oxidized Cys or Cys involved in disulfide bridges were protected, and therefore were hidden from reaction with the DBIA probe [25]. Afterward, the proteins were digested, with the resulting peptides labeled with the corresponding tandem mass tag (TMT) reagents. The combined TMT‐tagged peptides were then subjected to biotin‐streptavidin enrichment to selectively enrich for peptides with DBIA‐labeled Cys residues. The enriched peptides were analyzed via LC‐synchronous precursor selection (SPS)‐MS3 experiments. Finally, for these experimental conditions (i.e., control, 1 h 6‐OHDA treatment, and 6 h 6‐OHDA treatment), we also performed a separate set of TMT‐based quantitative MS experiments to generate global protein expression data (Figure 2C and Table S3). These protein expression results were cross‐referenced against the DBIA‐based chemoproteomic data to dissect regulatory Cys modification events that are driven by altered Cys reactivity versus altered protein expression.

From these samples, we were able to achieve excellent reproducibility (Figure S1). Additionally, we were able to identify 28,608 DBIA‐modified peptide‐spectrum matches (PSMs) out of the total 30,904 PSMs (i.e., 92.6% enrichment) (Figure 2A). Of the total 7,618 peptide groups, these DBIA‐modified PSMs corresponded to 6,965. These 6,965 DBIA‐modified peptide groups were identified from 2,980 distinct DBIA‐modified protein groups, representing a wide array of biological processes [e.g., mRNA splicing (p = 4.5 × 10−29), chromatin remodeling (p = 1.2 × 10−26), DNA repair (p = 8.1 × 10−23), cell division (p = 3.6 × 10−18), protein ubiquitination (p = 6.7 × 10−18), and protein folding (p = 3.1 × 10−17)] and cellular compartments [e.g., cytosol (p = 4.0 × 10−216), nucleoplasm (p = 9.1 × 10−211), nucleus (p = 1.2 × 10−155), extracellular exosome (p = 3.5 × 10−49), mitochondrion (p = 2.1 × 10−45), and ribonucleoprotein complex (p = 3.3 × 10−42)], suggesting a comprehensive profiling of Cys reactivity using these chemoproteomic technologies. In total, 8,542 unique Cys sites were quantified (site probability threshold ≥ 75). The data obtained from cells subjected to 6‐OHDA treatment were compared against the control for each time duration (1 and 6 h) to investigate the effects of 6‐OHDA on the global Cys residue reactivity state. Peptide intensities were consistent among all TMT channels, suggesting that the total peptide loading was balanced and that the 6‐OHDA treatment did not instigate global proteome degradation or overwhelming changes in protein recovery (Figure 2B).

Compared to the control, samples that were oxidatively stressed for 1 and 6 h led to a marked change in the Cys reactivity states, with many proteins that had a Log2 competition ratio (Log2CR) >1 (i.e., reduced DBIA reactivity after 6‐OHDA treatment) (p < 0.05) (Figure 2 and Tables S1, S2). Specifically, a total of 44 proteins had presumably altered Cys redox states after 1 and 6 h of 6‐OHDA treatment. Gene ontology (GO) analysis conducted on the 6‐h treatment group had shown that these proteins were involved in biological processes linked to the response to 6‐OHDA treatment, particularly cell division (p = 5.9 × 10−3) and apoptotic process (p = 3.0 × 10−2) (Figure 2F, Table S4).

We also performed a GO analysis based on cellular compartment and interestingly, a number of these proteins with presumably altered Cys redox states are localized in the mitochondria (p = 3.4 × 10−2; e.g., GCSH, HCCS, VDAC2, MTCH2, NDUFA10, CYCS, ANK2, and AKAP1), consistent with the prominent role of the mitochondrion in regulating ROS production and cellular redox balances (Figure 2F, Table S5). For example, we found that Cys47 on VDAC2 (voltage‐dependent anion‐selective channel protein 2) was unchanged in its reactivity toward DBIA after 1 h of 6‐OHDA treatment yet showed reduced reactivity by 2.4‐fold (i.e., Log2CR = 1.25) after 6 h (Figure 3B, C, and Tables S1, S2). The expression levels of VDAC2 were relatively unchanged between the control and 6‐OHDA treatment groups, suggesting that the observed Cys reactivity changes likely occurred at the posttranslational level (Figure 3B and Table S3). VDAC2 is a mitochondrial outer membrane protein that is essential in forming the ion channel which regulates membrane potential and general metabolite flux. Within the human VDAC family, VDAC2 has the largest number of Cys residues in its sequence with a total of nine Cys residues [27, 28, 29]. These Cys residues participate in various signaling pathways that sense oxidative stress and activate ROS scavenging enzymes [29, 30, 31]. In particular, the Cys47 residue is situated within the linker domain which might play a key role in regulating cellular redox balances [28]. It is therefore possible that the redox changes on Cys47 from VDAC2 could function as an important mediator of oxidative stress induced by 6‐OHDA. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that VDAC2 possesses anti‐apoptotic properties [32]. Specifically, it was shown that VDAC2 forms a complex with BAK, a multidomain proapoptotic protein located in the mitochondria [32]. In doing so, VDAC2 maintains BAK in an inactive form, and prevents cell death. In cells that are deficient in VDAC2, BAK shows increased oligomerization, enabling cells to become more susceptible to apoptotic death. In this context, the functional relevance of the redox changes of VDAC2 Cys47 as a potential mediator of the cytotoxic effects of 6‐OHDA in neuronal cells warrants future studies.

In addition to VDAC2, we found that several other proteins with altered Cys redox states upon 6‐OHDA treatment were similarly connected to cell death pathways. For example, we found that the DBIA reactivity of two Cys residues (Cys170 and Cys184) on CASP3 (caspase 3) was diminished by 1.3‐fold (i.e., Log2CR = 0.33) after 1 h and by 2.1‐fold (i.e., Log2CR = 1.07) after 6 h of 6‐OHDA treatments (Figure 3B, C, and Tables S1, S2). Furthermore, the expression levels of CASP3 were unchanged upon 6‐OHDA treatment (Figures 2, 3, and Table S3), suggesting the observed Cys reactivity changes likely occurred at the posttranslational level. CASP3 is a key player of apoptosis in neuronal cells [33]. Additionally, more recent data showed that caspase 3 is also involved in other non‐apoptotic aspects of neuronal biology, including neural stem cell differentiation and embryonic neural development [34, 35]. Such apoptotic and non‐apoptotic functions highlight the potential significance of CASP3 in mediating the cytotoxicity and other cell stress responses as induced by 6‐OHDA. Interestingly, previous studies have shown that CASP3 is posttranslationally modified by glutathiolation (e.g., at Cys184), and importantly, this modification is critical for the suppression of CASP3 activation [36]. Mutation of Cys184 into a Ser residue prevented Cys184 glutathiolation, leading to downstream CASP3 cleavage and its subsequent activation. In our study, we observed that the DBIA probe reactivity at Cys170 and Cys184 of CASP3 was markedly reduced after 6 h of 6‐OHDA treatment (Figures 2, 3, and Table S2). It is plausible that 6‐OHDA treatment induces a Cys184 oxidation event on CASP3, impairing its glutathiolation. This could then enhance CASP3 activation, and eventually cause neural cell death. Future studies are warranted to investigate this interesting mechanism in driving the cytotoxic effects of 6‐OHDA in neuronal cells.

Finally, besides the aforementioned examples, we also unexpectedly identified several proteins that had increased DBIA reactivity after 6‐OHDA treatment. Interestingly, we found that Cys132 and Cys152 on UCHL1 (ubiquitin C‐terminal hydrolase L1) became more reactive toward DBIA, leading to a Log2CR < –1 after both, the 1 and 6‐h 6‐OHDA treatments (Figures 2, 3, and Tables S1, S2). Although there was a modest increase in protein expression of UCHL1 by ∼1.12‐fold in both time groups, protein expression alone cannot account for the ∼5.5‐ and ∼3.2‐fold increases in DBIA reactivity in the 1 and 6‐h time groups, respectively (Figure 2E and Table S3). It is known that UCHL1 is highly expressed in the brain where it constitutes up to 5% of total soluble protein [33, 37, 38]. UCHL1 has been shown to be crucially responsible for maintaining the axonal health and stability [33, 39]. Loss‐of‐function mutations of UCHL1 have been associated with early‐onset neurodegeneration in humans [40]. UCHL1 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of both sporadic and familial PD by co‐aggregating with alpha‐synuclein in Lewy bodies [41, 42]. Moreover, it has been shown that prostaglandin‐mediated modification of UCHL1 results in the partial unfolding and increased aggregation of this protein, a feature that may be associated with sporadic PD [43]. In addition to PD, more recent studies showed that UCHL1 could function as a neuron‐specific biomarker for various neurological disorders and neurodegenerative diseases, including brain trauma and frontotemporal dementia [44, 45, 46]. Specifically, it was found that UCHL1 was elevated in cerebrospinal fluid samples from all symptomatic frontotemporal dementia patients (i.e., mutation carriers in GRN, C9orf72, and MAPT), compared to controls [45].

Previous studies showed that UCHL1 plays a key role in regulating redox homeostasis in neurons. Importantly, spectroscopic analysis showed that under oxidative stress conditions, UCHL1 undergoes profound structural changes, leading to the loss of its enzymatic function [47]. Thus, in the context of our observations, it is plausible to postulate that Cys132 and Cys152 are inaccessible to DBIA under basal conditions. However, upon 6‐OHDA treatment, these Cys residues are exposed in a manner that renders them reactive toward DBIA. Still, further investigation would be necessary to determine the underlying mechanism of this interesting regulatory event.

To summarize, we used the SLC‐ABPP approach and examined how 6‐OHDA regulates the global Cys modification status in neuronal cells. We found that the oxidative stress as induced by 6‐OHDA treatment led to a profound change in reactivity of many Cys to the DBIA probe, presumably through potential changes in oxidative status, posttranslational modifications, protein–protein interactions, and/or protein stability. Although further investigation is necessitated to confirm changes to oxidative status, the proteins involved are known to be critically involved in regulating cellular stress responses to reactive oxidative species (e.g., CASP3, VDAC2, and UCHL1). Besides these proteins, our dataset also serves as a resource for future mechanistic studies to understand how altered oxidative status of Cys residues contribute to 6‐OHDA‐induced neuronal cell death. Because oxidative stress is an established hallmark of many neuropathological diseases, such studies may hold promise toward addressing some of the unanswered questions in the field of neurological disorders and neurodegenerative diseases, especially those relevant to PD, AD, FTD, and ALS.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Human neuroblastoma SH‐SY5Y cells purchased from ATCC were maintained in the high‐glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; MilliporeSigma) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; MilliporeSigma) at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were found to be mycoplasma‐free using the e‐Myco kit (Boca Scientific).

Cell Viability Measurement

Cells were plated into 96‐well plates at densities of 1000–2000 cells/well. Two days later, cells were treated with 100 µM 6‐OHDA (MilliporeSigma) for an indicated time duration. Cell viability was measured using the CellTiter‐Glo assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after incubation, room temperature CellTiter‐Glo reagent was added 1:1 to each well, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 2 min. Luminescence was measured with the Synergy HT Multi‐Detection Microplate Reader and was normalized against control cells treated with vehicle.

Sample Preparation for Mass Spectrometry

Human neuroblastoma SH‐SY5Y cells were treated with or without 6‐OHDA (100 µM) for 1 or 6 h, respectively. Cells were lysed with 1% SDS lysis buffer containing 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 2 mM MgCl2, 20 U/mL universal nuclease. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and diluted to 1 mg/mL before 100 µg of protein was obtained for each sample. Samples were incubated with 500 µM of the desthiobiotin iodoacetamide (DBIA, CAS # 2924824‐04‐2; MedChemExpress, Catalog # HY‐150230) probe for 1 h, reduced with 3 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 20 min, and alkylated with 25 mM iodoacetamide (IDA) for 30 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. The detergents were removed by methanol/chloroform precipitation. The protein pellets were re‐solubilized in 8 M urea and digested by Lys‐C at a 1:100 (w/w) enzyme/protein ratio for 2 h, followed by tryptic digestion at a 1:100 (w/w) enzyme/protein ratio for overnight at RT in 2 M urea. Digested peptides containing DBIA‐conjugated cysteines were desalted using Oasis HLB solid‐phase extraction (SPE) cartridges (Waters) [48] and peptides from each sample were re‐suspended in 200 mM HEPES, pH 8.5 before ACN was added toward a final concentration of 30% v/v. The peptides were then labeled with 10 µL of the amine‐based TMT 10‐plex reagents (Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at RT. The reaction was quenched by adding 5% hydroxylamine solution before combining the labeled peptide samples into equivalent ratios. For cysteine peptide enrichment, dissolved samples were incubated with 100 µL of streptavidin agarose beads (50% v/v) in 1X PBS, pH 7.4, by rotating end‐over‐end for overnight at 4°C to enrich for TMT‐labeled, DBIA‐conjugated cysteine peptides. The flowthrough (non‐cysteine‐containing peptides) was retained toward global protein expression analysis. After incubation, beads were washed to remove nonspecific binding using the following procedure: 5 × 1 mL of 1X PBS, pH 7.4, and finally, 3 × 1 mL of HPLC‐grade water. To elute cysteine‐containing peptides, 500 µL of 0.1% TFA in 50% acetonitrile was added, and the beads were mixed at 1000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. Eluted peptides were transferred to a new tube, and the beads were additionally washed with 200 µL of 50% acetonitrile with 0.1% TFA and combined. Cysteine‐containing peptides were dried to completion via SpeedVac. Non‐cysteine‐containing peptides were fractionated by off‐line high pH HPLC, as described in Table S6, where 17 fractions were collected and lyophilized. Next, the TMT‐labeled, cysteine‐containing (and non‐cysteine‐containing) peptides were resuspended in 120 µL of 0.1% FA and were desalted using StageTips containing Empore C18‐HD bonded silica (12 µm particle size; CDS Analytical).

Quantitative Proteomic Analysis by LC‐SPS‐MS3

The TMT samples were analyzed using an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer in‐line with an UltiMate 3000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated using an in‐house packed capillary nanocolumn with 15 cm of C18 resin (3 µm, 200 Å; The Nest Group) using either 200 min gradients of 2%–42% (whole proteome) or 240 min gradients of 2%–60% (ABPP) acetonitrile in 0.1% FA per run, unless otherwise noted. Eluted peptides were quantified using the SPS‐MS3 method for TMT quantification. Briefly, MS1 spectra were acquired at 120‐K resolving power with a maximum of 50‐ms ion injection in the Orbitrap. MS2 spectra were acquired by selection of the top ten most abundant features via collisional induced dissociation in the ion trap using an automatic gain control (AGC) setting of 10K, quadrupole isolation width of 0.5 m/z and a maximum ion accumulation time of 35 ms. Then the spectra were passed in an SPS–MS3 scan performed using up to 10 b‐ and y‐type MS2 fragment ions as precursors with an AGC of 200 K for a maximum of 86 ms, with a normalized collision energy setting of 48%.

Proteomics Data Processing

All acquired data were searched utilizing ProteomeDiscoverer v2.4. Spectral searches were performed using a custom FASTA‐formatted database (human_uniprot_08122020) and the following parameters: 50 ppm precursor tolerance, fully tryptic peptides, fragment ion tolerance of 0.9 Da, and a modification by TMT10 (+229.1629 Da) on lysine and peptide N‐termini was set as a static modification, while oxidation of methionine residues (+15.9949 Da), carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues (+57.0214 Da) and DBIA on cysteine residues (+239.1628 Da) were set as variable modifications. Peptide spectral matches were filtered to a peptide false discovery rate (FDR) of <1%. Resulting peptides were further filtered to obtain a 1% protein FDR at the entire dataset level, and proteins were collapsed into groups. Overlapping peptide sequences generated from different charge states, elution times and tryptic termini were grouped together into a single entry. A single quantitative value was reported, and only unique peptides were reported. Peptide quantification was performed by using the CoreQuant algorithm implemented in Proteome Discoverer Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The labeling scheme for the TMT experiments is 126: DMSO for 1 h; 127N: 6‐OHDA (100 µM, 1 h); 127C: 6‐OHDA (100 µM, 1 h); 128N: 6‐OHDA (100 µM, 1 h); 128C: DMSO for 1 h; 129N: DMSO for 6 h; 129C: 6‐OHDA (100 µM, 6 h); 130N: 6‐OHDA (100 µM, 6 h); 130C: 6‐OHDA (100 µM, 6 h); 131: DMSO for 6 h. For TMT quantification, a 0.03‐Th window was scanned around the theoretical m/z of each reporter ion to detect the presence of these ions. The maximum intensity of each ion was extracted, and the signal‐to‐noise (S/N) value of each protein is calculated by summing the reporter ion counts across all identified peptides. Reporter ion intensities were adjusted to correct for impurities during synthesis of different TMT reagents according to the manufacturer's specifications. Peptide quantitative values were normalized so that the sum of the signal for all peptide amount in each channel was equal, to account for sample loading differences (column normalization).

Statistical Analysis

All the other statistical analyses including unpaired Student's t‐tests were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (v9.2.0). Data were calculated as mean ± SEM or mean ± SD.
