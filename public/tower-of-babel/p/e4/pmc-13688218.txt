Comparison of Relapse Risk Prediction Models During Active Surveillance in Stage I Seminoma: A Retrospective Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Andreas Banner, Eveline Daetwyler, Stefanie Fischer, Stephanie Schöpfer‐Schwab, Karl Pemberger, Michael Scholz, Kathrin Olesch, David Oswald, Angelina Strauch, Tim Nestler, Lukas Koren‐Wagner, Robin Zeder, Clemens Mikulits, Sebastian Lenart, Christoph Seidel, Angelika Terbuch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo improve the prediction of relapse for clinical stage I seminoma, we aimed to validate the Boormans model (incorporating tumor size, rete testis invasion, and lymphovascular invasion) against the conventional classification (tumor size > 4 cm/rete testis invasion). Consecutive cSI seminoma patients managed with active surveillance after orchidectomy across multiple centers were retrospectively analyzed (1994–2024). Part of the data was provided by the prospective Swiss‐Austrian‐German Testicular Cancer Cohort Study Group. The primary outcome was relapse. Among the 1025 patients, 116 (11.3%) relapsed during a median follow‐up of 45 months (95% CI 42–48). In the descriptive multivariable analysis, TS (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.04) and RTI (HR 2.73, 95% CI 1.83–4.06) were found to be independent predictors of relapse. The Boormans model classified 645 (62.9%) men as low‐, 356 (34.7%) as intermediate‐, and 24 (2.3%) men as high‐risk candidates. The 5‐year relapse risk was 0.07 (95% CI 0.04–0.09), 0.24 (0.19–0.29), and 0.35 (0.10–0.53) for the low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk groups, respectively. The Boormans model outperformed the conventional model (C‐index 0.66 vs. 0.61; Δ0.06, p < 0.001). Despite improved discrimination, clinical utility was limited across risk‐adapted treatment scenarios compared with the conventional model. The Boormans model demonstrated superior discrimination but limited gains in clinical utility. Integrating novel biomarkers may enable more accurate, risk‐adapted management.

Introduction

Testicular cancer is the most common solid tumor in young men aged 15–44 years [1]. Approximately 94% are germ cell tumors, which can be further divided into seminomatous (SGCT) and nonseminomatous germ cell tumors (NSGCT) [2]. Approximately 80% of SGCT patients present with clinical stage I (cSI) disease, with 12%–20% of unselected patients relapsing after orchidectomy alone [3, 4]. Historically, tumor size (TS) and rete testis infiltration (RTI) have been reported as histological risk factors, but their validity remains controversial due to methodological limitations [2, 5].

Because TS relies on arbitrary cutoff values, relapse counseling should ideally consider it as a continuous parameter [6]. Similarly, RTI has been studied only in small, heterogeneous retrospective series with variable definitions of RTI. Consequently, the European Association of Urology Testicular Cancer Guideline Panel states the limited clinical use of TS and RTI for relapse prediction and guidance on adjuvant treatment in cSI seminoma and endorses active surveillance (AS) as the preferred management for all patients [2, 5]. However, identifying patients at greater risk of relapse may justify the use of adjuvant carboplatin to avoid the need for cisplatin‐based combination therapy in the event of relapse.

Additional risk factors proposed to predict relapse in cSI seminoma include lymphovascular invasion (LVI), preorchidectomy b‐human chorionic gonadotropin (b‐hCG), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). LVI is an established predictor in NSCGTs, but its prognostic value in patients with SGCT remains unclear [7, 8]. Furthermore, preorchidectomy serum tumor marker (STM) levels have not been associated with relapse risk in prior small studies [6, 9].

Recent studies suggest superior prognostic performance of two novel, independent models. Wagner et al. [10] analyzed 924 cSI seminoma patients under AS from a prospective database with central pathology review, identifying hilar invasion (soft tissue or rete testis), LVI, b‐hCG, and LDH as key factors. The 5‐year relapse risk ranged from 10% to 16% with one risk factor to 62% with five risk factors. However, hilar soft tissue invasion (HSTI) is not routinely assessed and was centrally reviewed in their study, limiting its external applicability and clinical utility. Boormans et al. [11] proposed a different model with 1016 retrospectively studied patients, incorporating TS, RTI, and LVI. The 5‐year relapse probabilities were 8% (low‐risk), 20% (intermediate‐risk), and 44% (high‐risk).

To enhance clinical applicability, we aimed to validate the Boormans risk model in an independent cohort of cSI seminoma patients managed with surveillance after orchidectomy. In addition, we compared it with the conventional TS > 4 cm/RTI classification to assess its prognostic performance and clinical utility.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective, multicenter study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariate Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) recommendations. Data were pseudonymized and entered separately by each center into a standardized, secure database template.

The data were partially derived from the prospective Swiss‐Austrian‐German Testicular Cancer Cohort Study Group (SAG TCCS).

Study Population

Eligible patients had primary, unilateral cSI seminoma managed with surveillance after radical inguinal orchidectomy between 1994 and 2024. cSI was defined by (i) normal postorchidectomy STMs (b‐hCG, LDH) and (ii) the absence of metastases on cross‐sectional imaging. Patients with borderline retroperitoneal nodes (1.1–1.5 cm) were excluded unless restaging at 6 weeks ruled out metastasis. Follow‐up protocols adhered to local guidelines with regular STM testing and imaging.

The exclusion criteria included nonseminomatous or spermatocytic tumors, elevated alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) before or after surgery, persistent b‐hCG elevation 3 weeks post‐surgery, prior contralateral testicular cancer, or missing data on key variables (TS, RTI, LVI, preoperative AFP, postoperative AFP or b‐hCG, or follow‐up). Patients with isolated LDH elevation without other evidence of disease were not excluded.

Assessment of histopathology and risk factors was conducted independently at each center, without central review. However, all participating centers were expected to adhere to international guidelines and standardized protocols. Notably, TS was defined as the largest tumor in case of multifocality. STMs were assessed independently, and center‐specific reference ranges for AFP, bHCG, and LDH varied across institutions (Table S2).

Objectives and Endpoints

The primary objective was to validate the Boormans risk classification for predicting the primary endpoint, relapse during surveillance. The time to relapse was measured from orchidectomy to the last follow‐up, nonrelapse death, or metachronous contralateral testicular cancer. Relapse was defined as radiological evidence or b‐hCG elevation (±LDH); isolated STM elevation was not considered relapse without imaging/biopsy confirmation. Contralateral cancer was not considered a relapse. For comparison, patients were stratified according to conventional risk factors (TS > 4 cm and RTI); the presence of both factors defined high‐risk, whereas one or none defined low‐risk.

The secondary objectives were to describe the mode of relapse detection (imaging, markers, or both) and the location of relapse. Exploratory post hoc analyses included a competing risks analysis accounting for death and contralateral tumors and the addition of STM status to the Boormans model. Furthermore, the low‐risk group was stratified according to the number of risk factors present to explore differences in relapse rates.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline data were summarized as medians (IQRs) or frequencies (%). Patients with missing key variables (postoperative STMs, TS, RTI, LVI, or outcome variables) were excluded. Follow‐up was described using reverse Kaplan–Meier analysis.

Predictors of relapse (preoperative b‐hCG and LDH, TS, RTI, LVI) were analyzed using uni‐ and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, stratified by center. Model assumptions were assessed using martingale residuals and Schoenfeld residuals. Log‐transformed, center‐specific ratios (marker/upper level of normal) were calculated for preoperative b‐hCG and LDH levels and compared to a binary classification (normal/elevated) using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Harrell's C‐index (C‐index). Both b‐hCG and LDH were further analyzed as dichotomized predictors because continuous modeling provided no meaningful improvement, whereas TS remained continuous.

Relapse probabilities were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by both risk models (Boormans vs. conventional). Prognostic performance (AIC, C‐index) was compared using Cox models stratified by center. To simulate sampling variability, nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was performed to estimate the mean C‐index difference (ΔC = C new − C old) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI). A two‐sided p‐value was obtained using permutations, which means that for each bootstrap pair of C‐indices, the labels of the two models were randomly exchanged to generate a null distribution. The p‐value was computed as the proportion of permutated mean differences at least as extreme as the observed ΔC [12]. Additionally, two sensitivity analyses were conducted, excluding patients diagnosed before 2000 and 2010, respectively. Because one center defined the observation period only for the overall cohort (2010–2023) without providing individual dates, the second sensitivity analysis (excluding those diagnosed before 2010) was repeated after excluding this center.

Clinical utility was assessed in a hypothetical decision scenario. Although all patients were managed with surveillance, we modeled adjuvant treatment for patients classified as high‐(±intermediate) risk by each model. Overtreatment and undertreatment were defined as hypothetically treated patients without relapse and untreated patients with relapse, respectively (Figure S3). Exact 95% binomial CIs were calculated, and bootstrap resampling was used for visualization.

Two exploratory post hoc analyses were conducted. The cumulative incidence function (CIF) was used to estimate relapse rates at 1 and 5 years, accounting for competing events (death and contralateral testicular cancer). The prognostic performance of b‐hCG and LDH status was explored by adding them to the Boormans model.

All the statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The sample size was chosen to reflect the Boormans distribution and include ≥ 20 high‐risk patients. The analyses used R 4.4.1 (packages: dplyr v1.1.4, cmprsk v2.2.12, survival v3.8.3, survminer 0.5.1).

Characteristics of Relapse

Overall, 116 patients (11.3%) relapsed, with 83.6% of all events occurring within 2 years. One late relapse was observed beyond 5 years among the included patients (0.9%).

Data on relapse detection were missing for 18 patients (15.5%); among the remaining patients, most relapses were detected by imaging (86.7%), followed by both STM and imaging (10.2%) and STM (3.1%). Regional nodes were the predominant site of recurrence (92.2%), followed by nonregional nodes (5.2%), lungs (0.8%), and viscera (0.8%). One patient (0.8%) experienced recurrence in the regional nodes with additional suspicion of peritoneal metastases. Competing events (contralateral testicular cancer, death) occurred in 12 patients (1.2%).

Validation of the Boormans Model

To compare both prognostic classifications, two Cox proportional hazards models were fitted. The Boormans model demonstrated better fit (AIC: 1142.7 vs. 1164.2) and greater discriminatory ability (C‐index 0.66 vs. 0.61). The mean difference in the C‐index was 0.06 (95% CI: 0.01–0.10) based on 1000 bootstrap samples (p < 0.001; Figure S2). Results remained directionally unchanged in two sensitivity analyses, excluding patients diagnosed before 2000 (n = 13) and 2010 (n = 129) (Figures S3 and S4). Similarly, the results of the second sensitivity analysis were unchanged after additional exclusion of the center lacking individual dates of diagnosis (n = 204) (Figure S5).

To assess potential clinical utility, we applied hypothetical treatment rules to the surveillance cohort. The Boormans model (high‐risk patients treated) resulted in potential overtreatment of 68.2% (95% CI 45.1%–86.1%) and undertreatment of 11.0% (95% CI 9.1%–13.1%) compared with 70.0% (95% CI 60.5%–78.4%) and 9.2% (95% CI 7.4%–11.3%) with the conventional model (high‐risk patients treated). Expanding hypothetical treatment to include intermediate‐risk patients increased potential overtreatment to 78.6% (95% CI 74.0%–82.6%) and reduced undertreatment to 5.6% (95% CI 4.0%–7.7%) compared with the novel high‐risk‐only scenario (Figure S6).

Post Hoc Analyses

Competing risk analysis (n = 12) using the CIF yielded relapse estimates that were descriptively similar to those from the Kaplan–Meier method (Figure S7).

The addition of the preoperative STM status to the Boormans model resulted in a C‐index of 0.67 for b‐hCG and 0.70 for LDH, compared with 0.66 for the original model. The cumulative probabilities of relapse for the Boormans model, stratified by STM status, are presented in Figure S8.

In the low‐risk group, 404 patients (62.6%) had one risk factor (TS 2–5 cm, RTI, or LVI), whereas the remaining 241 (37.4%) had no risk factor. Among patients without any risk factors, the 1‐ and 5‐year relapse probabilities were 0.02 (95% CI 0.00–0.04) and 0.04 (95% CI 0.01–0.06), respectively.

Discussion

In this multicenter study, we externally validated the prognostic risk classification for cSI seminoma proposed by Boormans et al., confirming its superior discriminatory performance compared with the conventional model including TS > 4 cm/RTI. The novel three‐tier model incorporating TS (≤ 2, > 2–5, and > 5 cm), RTI, and LVI demonstrated 5‐year relapse probabilities of 0.07, 0.24, and 0.35 for low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐risk patients, respectively. It also showed better model fit (AIC: 1142.7 vs. 1164.2) and discrimination (mean ΔC = 0.06) compared to the conventional approach. We further assessed its clinical utility by analyzing over‐ and undertreatment rates in various scenarios.

Previous systematic reviews have reported inconsistent findings on the TS > 4 cm/RTI classification as a predictor of relapse in cSI seminoma, resulting in weak recommendations for adjuvant carboplatin [2, 5, 6, 13, 14]. The current patient selection criteria perform poorly, with substantial overtreatment even among patients categorized as high‐risk [5, 6]. Moreover, adjuvant carboplatin has shown only modest reductions in relapse risk among patients with TS > 4 cm, RTI, or both, with relapse rates of 15.5% under AS versus 9.3% following adjuvant carboplatin [15]. Although the Boormans model improved discriminatory performance, its clinical utility remained limited, with high overtreatment rates (68.2% vs. 70% in the conventional model). Expanding adjuvant treatment to intermediate‐risk patients further increased overtreatment (78.6%) while only slightly reducing undertreatment (5.6%). Therefore, we recommend adjuvant treatment only for high‐risk patients, using shared decision‐making, and surveillance for low‐ and intermediate‐risk patients.

Another recent study by Wagner et al. identified hilar HSTI, LVI, and STM as strong predictors of relapse among 924 cSI seminoma patients, with a 5‐year relapse probability of 0.62 in the highest‐risk group—potentially outperforming the Boormans model [10, 11]. The hilar soft tissue consists of a small amount of extratesticular fat and vessels adjacent to the RTI, and its invasion may indicate an advanced local stage [16]. Importantly, HSTI has been shown to be a stronger predictor of relapse than RTI alone [10]. However, histopathological assessment of the testicular hilum lacks uniform recommendations among pathologists, and a recent study suggested only moderate interobserver reproducibility [16, 17]. Future prospective studies and the establishment of uniform guidelines for evaluating the testicular hilum may yield promising results.

LVI has been investigated in previous studies, with inconsistent findings [7, 8, 18, 19, 20, 21]. In our study, LVI was significantly associated with relapse in the univariable analysis, but not after adjustment (HR 1.20, 95% CI 0.73–1.97). Nonetheless, the Boormans model demonstrated superior discriminatory performance compared with the conventional TS > 4 cm/RTI classification.

Patients defined as low‐risk according to Boormans et al., or presenting with 0–1 risk factors according to Wagner et al. (with the exception of HSTI) showed low relapse rates from 0.06 to 0.11 across studies. Furthermore, in our post hoc analysis of low‐risk patients without any risk factors, the 5‐year relapse probability was 0.04 (95% CI 0.01–0.06). Notably, only 3.1% of relapses were initially detected by STMs alone and were subsequently confirmed by imaging. These findings suggest a potential to de‐escalate follow‐up in low‐risk patients, which should be explored in future studies.

Beyond histopathological risk factors, other approaches have been explored to improve relapse prediction in cSI seminoma. Imaging‐derived radiomic features from baseline CT scans showed promising performance for predicting lymph node metastasis (AUC 0.87–0.89) [22]. MicroRNA biomarkers, particularly miR‐371a‐3p, demonstrated excellent sensitivity (100%) and specificity (96.3%) for relapse detection during surveillance, clearly outperforming traditional STMs [23]. However, pre‐ to postorchidectomy changes in miR‐371‐3p levels did not predict relapse [24]. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has consistently demonstrated high prognostic value across other cancers [25, 26]. For TGCTs, a small study (n = 35) reported increased relapse risk among patients with detectable ctDNA within 1–12 weeks post‐orchidectomy (HR 7.2, 95% CI 1.4–36.7) [27].

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and use of data from multiple institutions may introduce selection bias and heterogeneity. Importantly, relapse probabilities were slightly higher in the Boormans cohort, which may reflect natural sampling variability as well as potential selection bias due to differing rates of adjuvant carboplatin use between cohorts. However, inter‐study comparisons suggest that our cohort may represent a lower‐risk, as indicated by a lower proportion of patients with established risk factors compared with cohorts reported by Boormant et al. and Wagner et al. [10, 11]. Although inclusion criteria and variable definitions were standardized, inter‐center and temporal variability in the pathological assessment of RTI and LVI cannot be excluded, as no central review was performed. Furthermore, follow‐up protocols differed between the centers, which may have affected the detection of relapses. Variability in hCG and LDH reference ranges across laboratories may influence patient classification and risk stratification. Nevertheless, as interpretation is based on values relative to the local ULN, the impact on external validity is likely limited. Despite the large overall cohort, the high‐risk group—particularly patients with events—was small, reducing the precision of the estimates and power of the subgroup analyses. Finally, stratification of Cox models by center was constrained by the inclusion of the SAG TCCS, which aggregates multiple centers.

Conclusions

In this large multicenter cohort of cSI seminoma patients undergoing surveillance, we externally validated the Boormans model. This confirmed its superiority in terms of discrimination and fit compared with the conventional TS > 4 cm/RTI classification. While it may refine relapse risk counseling and follow‐up, its clinical utility for guiding adjuvant therapy remains limited. Future research should integrate novel biomarkers (e.g., radiomics, ctDNA) to enable accurate risk‐adapted management. A multicenter study is currently underway to evaluate the model's performance in patients receiving adjuvant carboplatin.
