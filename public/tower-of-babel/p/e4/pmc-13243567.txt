Convection enhanced phase change composite fibers for advanced thermal management
Authors: Yu Du, Siyuan Ding, Omar MMA Moustafa, Yueni Zhong, Fangzheng Zhen, Ming Yong, Yinghui Wu, Zihan Chen, Weiren Fan, Qijun Zheng, Ean Hin Ooi, Baofu Ding, Sheng Yan, Jianli Wang, Ruiping Zou, Ling Qiu, Aibing Yu, Minsu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High heat fluxes challenge electronic thermal management, as phase change materials (PCMs) suffer from low heat transfer efficiency. In this work, we present a convection enhanced strategy using molten phase transport in phase change composites (PCCs). We fabricate porous fibers with over 90 vol% interconnected core channels and a dense, conductive shell via nonsolvent induced phase separation (NIPS). Experimental and numerical analyses reveal that specific channel structures activate internal convection, contributing over 100% more than intrinsic thermal conduction. Optimized PCC fibers achieve a thermal conductivity of 1.05 W·m−1·K−1 with only 3 wt% additives, which increases to 2.48 W·m−1·K−1 upon melting, outperforming fibers with substantially higher intrinsic conductivity. Device-level demonstrations confirm the thermal buffering potential of our PCC fibers. Overall, this work highlights the vital role of thermal convection, which is often overlooked, in the design and evaluation of PCCs.

Introduction

The relentless drive towards miniaturization and increased power densities in modern electronics, fueled by advancements in 5 G communication1,2, artificial intelligence3,4, and high-integration computing modules5,6, has precipitated an escalating thermal management challenge that critically impacts device performance, operational stability, and overall longevity7,8. While active cooling solutions such as fans and liquid cooling systems offer high heat dissipation capacities, their associated bulk, complexity, and power requirements limit their applicability in the burgeoning field of lightweight, energy-efficient smart electronics that demand passive or intermittent thermal buffering solutions9. Phase change materials (PCMs), by their substantial latent heat absorption during solid-liquid transitions, have emerged as highly promising candidates for thermal management to prevent sudden hotspots and regulate heat fluxes10–13. Nevertheless, the practical use of PCMs is limited by their inherently low thermal conductivities of typically 0.1–0.5 W·m−1·K−1, a value that falls considerably short when fabricating thermal management materials14–17.

The convection of fluid provides an effective way to transfer heat, which also promises great potential to solve the low thermal conductivity problem in phase change composites (PCCs). This approach has been validated by the success of advanced two-phase systems, such as heat pipes and vapor chambers18,19. In these systems, the fluid convection driven by temperature differences transfers heat so effectively that their equivalent thermal conductivity reaches ultrahigh values of 5000–100,000 W·m−1·K−1 through efficient convective heat transfer mechanisms in vapor-liquid phase change20–22. However, current designs of PCCs in thermal management usually focus on developing thermally conductive leak-proof matrices with paraffin-based PCMs23–26. These approaches use a heat-transfer matrix. Yet, they also confine the main heat transfer within the matrix because these matrices, usually in branched or networked geometries, increase flow resistance and significantly suppress the potential convective heat transfer in the molten fluidic phase of PCMs27–29. As a result, although porosity is widely presented in PCCs, it is typically treated as a storage volume rather than being systematically engineered as an active convective heat transfer unit30. An alternative approach that integrates thermally conductive pathways with thermal convection contributions is thus crucial to overcoming the bottleneck imposed by conventional filler-matrix designs.

Herein, we propose a convection-enhanced strategy for PCCs that improves heat transfer performance by activating molten-phase fluid transport. By combining the coaxial wet spinning method with the non-solvent-induced phase separation (NIPS)31 technique, we fabricate core-shell fibers with continuous internal fluid channels. The robust shell layer ensures mechanical integrity and leakage resistance, while facilitating efficient axial heat transfer, which enables the PCC fibers to achieve a thermal conductivity of 1.05 W m−1 K−1 with only 3 wt% conductive additives. Meanwhile, the highly porous fiber core (with a volume fraction of 90%) provides ample space for molten PCM flow and convection. Through precise control of the NIPS process, the internal channel diameter can be programmably tuned from 500 to 5000 nm, allowing for a systematic investigation of structure-dependent heat transfer behavior. Through a combination of experimental investigations and multiphysics simulations, we further demonstrated that specific channel configurations can markedly enhance thermal convection of the molten PCM, yielding an effective thermal conductivity enhancement exceeding 100%, with values increasing from 1.05 W m−1 K−1 to 2.48 W m−1 K−1. This finding reveals a previously underappreciated, yet pivotal, mechanism for enhancing the thermal performance of PCCs. Furthermore, both simulations and experiments show that this convection-enhanced mechanism is particularly effective for horizontally placed, low thermal conductivity, fiber-based PCC thermal management materials, which closely reflect practical application scenarios.

Finally, we demonstrated the advantages of using these fibers as effective thermal buffers for central processing unit (CPU), further underscoring their significant potential in advanced thermal management. Our work introduces a design paradigm for PCCs, emphasizing structural engineering of convection as an effective route to fully unlock the latent potential of PCCs for next-generation thermal regulation technologies.

Results

A major challenge for PCM composites is to achieve the balance of high PCM loading, sufficient mechanical strength32, enhanced thermal transport33, and reliable leakage resistance34. To address this multifaceted problem, we propose a hierarchical core-shell fiber structure with multiple functional domains (Fig. 1a): (i) a continuous and interconnected pore network within the core that ensures uniform thermal contact and efficient heat uptake by the encapsulated PCM; (ii) a high internal void fraction that provides sufficient volume for PCM accommodation; and (iii) a mechanically reinforced aramid nanofiber/boron nitride nanosheets (ANF/BNNS) outer shell that offers structural stability, directional thermal pathways, and an effective barrier against liquid leakage.Fig. 1Design, fabrication and morphological characterization of phase-change composite fibers.a Schematic illustration of the core-shell fiber structure highlighting the macrochannels for phase change material (PCM) encapsulation and the dense outer shell; b Diagram of the coaxial wet-spinning fabrication process; c Proposed mechanism for formation of the macrochannel structure via non-solvent-induced phase separation (NIPS) during solvent exchange (blue, water; yellow, aramid nanofibers/dimethyl sulfoxide (ANF/DMSO); gray, poly(vinyl butyral)/N,N-dimethylacetamide (PVB/DMAc)); d Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of a representative core-shell fiber, revealing the distinct core and dense shell; e Computed tomography (CT) reconstructed axial section showing a tortuously connected pore-network distribution; f Pore-size distribution of the core material determined by mercury intrusion porosimetry.

Our initial focus was on the efficacy of macro channels as PCM hosts. Compared with conventional strategies for creating porous scaffolds, such as supercritical drying35 and freeze-drying36, our one-step coaxial wet-spinning process (Fig. 1b) enables direct formation of this hierarchical structure without post-processing. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, once contact with the coagulation bath, the solvent in the core gel rapidly diffuses outward, triggering NIPS37, and generating radially expanding finger-like macropores31. In contrast, the ANF-rich shell rapidly solidifies due to its dense hydrogen-bonded network38,39, producing a compact and mechanically robust sheath in which BNNSs exhibit orientational alignment and contribute to structural and interfacial stability40,41.

Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 1d) verify the well-defined core-shell morphology, showing a uniform shell thickness and finger-like pore structure within the core. Notably, the core with thermal conduction paths points from the periphery towards the center (Fig. S1), suggesting enhanced, structured heat transfer compared to the simple addition of thermally conductive fillers42. Computed Tomography (CT) in Fig. 1e further revealed that these macropores form a continuous and tortuous channel network rather than isolated voids. Such interconnected pore pathways are essential for complete infiltration and subsequent mobility of molten PCM throughout the fiber. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis (Fig. S2) indicates a large internal surface area of 15.7 m2·g−1, suggesting large solid-liquid interfacial contact between PCM and the conductive skeleton, which is beneficial for their heat exchange43.

Mercury intrusion porosimetry (Fig. 1f) shows that the pore diameter ranges from 500 nm to 10 µm, far exceeding the hydrodynamic radius of the Polyethylene glycol (PEG) molecule (PEG 2000, hydrodynamic radius 12.5 nm), indicating that molecular-scale confinement is negligible44. The experimentally measured porosity of 89% closely matches the theoretical estimate of 92%, confirming that the fiber possesses an almost ideally interconnected pore system45, capable of accommodating liquid PCM infiltration, retention, and migration46. Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) analysis (Fig. S3) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Fig. S4) indicate that the fibers exhibit a latent heat of ~118 J g−1, corresponding to a PEG loading of ~90 wt%. Collectively, these results confirm that the hierarchical core-shell structure offers high porosity, abundant interfacial area, structural stability, and excellent encapsulation capability, making it an effective host platform for phase-change materials.

We then emphasized the role of the ANF/BNNS composite shell in reinforcing and stabilizing the porous fiber. The stress-strain curves in Fig. 2a show that the shell-free porous fiber (PF-5) exhibits a low tensile strength of only 0.92 MPa, whereas the porous fiber with the addition of the shell (PFS-5) increases the strength to 5.55 MPa. The fracture-surface SEM images (inset of Fig. 2a) further illustrate this difference: PF-5 shows a clean and flat cross-section characteristic of brittle fracture47, while PFS-5 undergoes substantial deformation before failure, indicating that the shell effectively dissipates mechanical energy and delays crack propagation48. The flexural properties were also investigated and shown in Fig. 2b. PF-5 breaks readily upon bending, whereas PFS withstands bending and twisting without structural failure. The shell also plays a crucial role in enhancing heat transfer. Transient electrothermal (TET) measurements49 in Fig. 2c reveal that the thermal conductivity of PF-5 is only 0.01 W·m−1·K−1, whereas that of PFS-5 increases to 1.16 W·m−1·K−1 owing to the additional ANF/BNNS shell. PCM infiltration further improves thermal conductivity by increasing density and filling internal voids50, raising the conductivity to 0.46 W·m−1·K−1 for PIF-5 and to 1.22 W·m−1·K−1 for PIFS-5 (detailed data in Tables S3–S4). These results highlight that the shell contributes dominantly to the overall conduction pathway. Dynamic thermal performance was evaluated via thermal test (setup in Fig. S5, thermal images in Fig. 2d). At an equal distance from the heat source, the temperature of PIFS-5 reaches 65 °C after 200 s, whereas PIF-5 reaches only 61 °C (Fig. 2e), indicating that the shell facilitates faster heat redistribution along the fiber.

The poor shape stability of liquefied PCM inevitably causes leakage51. Long-term thermal cycling (Fig. 2f, g) illustrated the shell’s crucial role in effectively encapsulating the PCM. After 100 heating-cooling cycles, PIF-5 loses 22.0% of its mass and 21.7% of its latent heat, and SEM images reveal voids left by leaked PCM (Fig. 2f inset). In contrast, PIFS-5 retains 99.1% of its mass and 98.7% of its latent heat, and cross-sectional SEM confirms the absence of detectable PCM loss. These findings demonstrate that the core-shell structure effectively addresses the shortcomings of the porous host, resulting in substantial improvements in mechanical strength, thermal conductivity, and long-term thermal stability.

In PCC systems, the configuration of the channel structure plays a crucial role in the melting profile and heat transfer distribution28,52. To build a structurally programmable platform, we systematically varied the BNNS content in the core dope to tailor the porous skeleton of the PIFS fibers. As shown in Fig. 3a, owing to the strong structuring ability of 2D BNNS, we could regulate the fiber core’s structure by adjusting the BNNS content in the core spinning solution. The 2D BNNS shows a narrow lateral size distribution (Fig. S6), and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping confirms that they are homogeneously dispersed in the polymer matrix without forming large agglomerates (Figs. S7–S10 and Table S5). This allows changes in morphology to be attributed to the BNNS loading rather than filler variability. Coaxial spinning produces an identical ~33 μm ANF/BNNS shell for all samples, and CT analysis shows that this shell occupies ~13% of the total fiber volume (Fig. S11 and Table S6). Cross-sectional SEM images of PFS-1 to PFS-7 (Fig. 3b–i) reveal an evolution in core morphology with increasing BNNS content53. At low BNNS loading, the core contains sparse, highly branched finger-like macropores separated by thin walls, characteristic of NIPS-dominated demixing54. As the BNNS content increases, these fingers gradually become narrower and more closely spaced, while the skeleton thickens and forms a denser framework. At the highest BNNS loading (70 wt%), the core is transformed into a compact network55 with smaller pores. SEM cross-sections of the extreme cases PFS-0 and PFS-9 (Fig. S12) further show that insufficient BNNS leads to hollow collapse due to a lack of mechanical support. In contrast, excessive BNNS causes binder-deficient collapse, thus defining a practical working window of 10-70 wt% BNNS for stable channel formation. CT and schematic models (Fig. S13) confirm that the voids observed in the SEM are surface manifestations of a tortuous, interconnected channel network that permeates throughout the fiber core, rather than isolated cavities. This connectivity is a common structural feature across the entire PFS system and is essential for sufficient PCM access. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra (Fig. S14) show no new absorption bands upon mixing BNNS with PVB56, indicating that the evolution in pore structure is governed by physical structure modulation rather than chemical reactions57.

To mechanistically understand how BNNS loading influences the formation of the porous skeleton, we examined the phase-separation dynamics of the core dope using rheology, optical images, and numerical modeling. Rheological curves (Fig. S15) show that increasing BNNS content substantially elevates the viscosity and slows the relaxation of the polymer-rich phase58, implying a reduced effective diffusivity and a shift toward a viscosity-dominated demixing regime. Consistent with this trend, optical microscopy of the coagulated core dope (Fig. S16) reveals that the characteristic NIPS morphology becomes progressively refined as BNNS loading increases.

Guided by these experimental results, we conducted a NIPS model based on a Cahn-Hilliard framework (details in Note S1). In the simulations, increasing the BNNS content is reflected in a higher viscosity of the polymer-rich phase and reduced mobility of both the polymer and the solvent. The resulting morphologies reproduce the experimentally observed transition from coarse, widely spaced finger-like pores to compact, finely segmented channel networks, establishing a direct mechanistic link between BNNS loading and the evolution of the porous skeleton (Fig. 3j).

Despite differences in pore structure, quantitative porosimetry shows that all PFS samples maintain a high porosity of ~90% (Fig. S21). Mercury intrusion porosimetry reveals the decrease in average pore diameter from 5309 nm for PFS-1 to 502 nm for PFS-7. Even in PFS-7, the pores remain far larger than the hydrodynamic radius of PEG2000 ( ~ 12.5 nm), so confinement effects are negligible. This ensures that PCM can fully infiltrate the accessible pore volume. This is also confirmed by SEM images of PIFS cross sections (Fig. S22) and TGA analysis (Fig. S23), which show that all compositions achieve a similar PCM loading of ~90 wt% with no obvious unfilled voids. Additional mechanical tests and thermal-cycling experiments (Supplementary Figs. S24–S27) demonstrate that the increased loading of BNNS not only strengthens as the pore network becomes denser but also retains its morphology, latent heat, and mass with greater than 98% retention after 100 cycles. After removing the PCM by water washing, the macrochannel framework remains intact (Fig. S28), confirming the structural robustness of the porous skeleton. Overall, core BNNS loading provides control over the channel scale without compromising the overall thermal stability, which also provides a clear structural basis for the thermal behavior analysis that follows.

We then investigated how these structural differences influence heat transfer. Figure 4a reveals that the intrinsic thermal conductivity increases with core BNNS content59: for PFS samples, thermal conductivity rises from 0.85 to 1.49 W·m−1·K−1, and after PCM infusion, from 0.89 to 1.55 W·m−1·K−1. Density and latent heat remain nearly constant across all compositions (Fig. 4b), ensuring that variations in thermal behavior arise from structural factors rather than changes in PCM loading. However, dynamic IR thermal testing reveals a contrary trend. The thermal images of the PIFSs during heating and cooling are shown in Fig. 4c. Quantitatively, Fig. 4d shows the temperature-time curves at point A. During the initial heating stage, all the fibers exhibited similar temperature distributions due to the same thermal conductive shell. However, when the temperature rose into the phase change range, PIFS-3 demonstrated accelerated temperature rising despite its lower thermal conductivity, whereas PIFS-7 with higher thermal conductivity was slower than it. This mismatch does not occur in fibers without PCM infusion (Fig. S29) or at temperatures below the melting point (Fig. S30), indicating that the phenomenon originates from molten-phase transport rather than solid conduction. Analysis of the temperature-time profiles (Fig. 4e) reveals two characteristic features: a low-temperature peak associated with the expansion of the PCM during melting, which enhances thermal contact with the solid framework60, and a higher-temperature peak attributed to convection within the molten PCM61. To assess the influence of pore structure on molten-phase transport, we conducted water flow experiments on the corresponding PIF samples (Fig. 4f and S31). PIF-1, which contains the largest and most open channels, exhibits the highest and fastest water flow, indicating the lowest hydraulic resistance. In contrast, PIF-7, with the smallest and most tortuous pores, shows the most significant resistance to flow. These opposing trends highlight a fundamental competition in the system: solid-phase conduction increases with BNNS loading, whereas molten-phase mobility decreases. Considering both effects, Fig. S32 provides a schematic illustration of this competition between enhanced conduction and suppressed liquid mobility. At the balance point between these two tendencies, PIFS-3 offers sufficient solid conduction while still maintaining appreciable molten-phase mobility, resulting in the most favorable overall thermal performance.

To further quantify the convection contribution, we conducted TET measurements (Fig. S33, data in Table S7) to record the thermal conductivity of the fibers at different temperatures. As shown in Fig. 4g, the effective thermal conductivity remains nearly constant below the melting point but increases significantly above it, fitting the trends observed in the infrared (IR) thermal test. We attribute this enhancement to thermal convection within the molten PCM. Notably, PIF-1 and PIF-3 exhibit an increase exceeding 100% (Fig. 4h), far beyond what can be accounted for by thermal conduction alone, thus accounting for the convective contribution. To isolate the role of channel structure from compositional effects, we also examined a control sample, PIFS-3R, which has the same content as PIFS-3 but lacks the engineered directional macrochannels. Unlike PIFS-3, PIFS-3R shows only a minimal increase in effective thermal conductivity above the melting point. This contrast confirms that the convection-assisted thermal enhancement arises from the engineered pore alignment rather than from material composition alone.

To understand the influence of channel structure on convective heat transfer, numerical simulations were performed with four geometries (Fig. 5a) based on the characteristic features of the PIFS samples. All models share the same material composition and solid-phase thermal conductivity, with a fixed porosity of 90%. Variations among the geometries were introduced solely by adjusting the thickness and spacing of the internal framework to redistribute the fluid flow. Under these constraints, any differences in heat-transfer behavior originate exclusively from changes in internal convection rather than from variations in solid conduction. As the skeleton becomes progressively thicker and more densely packed from Geom-1 to Geom-4, the flow channels narrow and the resistance to fluid motion increases markedly (Fig. S34). This trend is quantitatively reflected in the hydraulic diameter and Darcy permeability (Fig. 5b), both of which decrease by orders of magnitude. To visualize how these structural differences affect fluid motion, the development of buoyancy-driven convection during heating was analyzed. While heating up, the volume-average fluid velocity increases as natural-convection circulation establishes, reaches a maximum at ~2.5 s, and subsequently decays as the system approaches a quasi-steady state (Fig. 5c). At 2.5 s, when convection is most intense, the velocity fields exhibit clear distinctions among the four geometries (Fig. 5d). Geom-1 supports large-scale circulation loops spanning most of the pore volume, enabling efficient transport of hot fluid from the heated boundary toward the cold side (Fig. S35). In contrast, as the skeleton becomes denser, recirculation is progressively restricted, and in Geom-4 the fluid remains nearly stagnant. To quantify these stagnant regions, “dead zones” in Fig. 5e were defined as grid cells in which the local velocity was below 50% of the global average velocity. In such regions, heat transfer is primarily dominated by conduction, with convection providing some enhancements. At the characteristic time of maximum convection, the dead-zone fraction reaches 17.7% and 70.1% for Geom-1 and Geom-2, respectively. It then increases sharply to 98% for Geom-3 and remains nearly 100% throughout the entire process for Geom-4. This indicates that the denser structure converts the pore space into a less convective region.

These flow-field differences directly affect thermal performance. Figure 5f presents the effective thermal conductivity keff calculated from the total heat flux and temperature drop, together with a conduction-only reference case in which fluid motion is fully suppressed. All four geometries exhibit convective enhancement relative to pure conduction, but the magnitude of enhancement is strongly structure dependent. Geom-1 achieves the most pronounced increase in keff from 1.75 W·m−1·K−1 to 3.51 W·m−1·K−1, over 100 % enhancement. Geom-4, with highly confined channels and extensive dead zones, yields only a modest 10% enhancement. Notably, the evolution time windows of keff closely follow that of the fluid velocity, further confirming that the enhancement originates from fluid convection. Consistent trends are also observed in the thermal images and cold-side temperature-time curves (Fig. 5g, h).

Considering the practical deployment of fiber-based thermal management components, we investigated how heat flux and gravity orientation influence buoyancy-driven convection within the porous network. Taking Geom-1 as a representative case, three representative cases were examined: gravity perpendicular to the imposed heat flux direction, gravity aligned with the heat flux, and gravity opposing the heat flux. Thermal images (Fig. 5i) visualize how gravity orientation reorganizes the internal heat transport. When gravity is perpendicular to the heat flux, the temperature field reveals an extended recirculation pathway spanning the porous domain (Fig. S36), enabling efficient lateral heat redistribution62–64. In contrast, when gravity is aligned with or opposes the heat flux, buoyancy-driven motion becomes localized, leading to stronger thermal stratification65,66 and reduced utilization of the pore volume. These differences are also reflected quantitatively in the evolution of the cold-side temperature and the effective thermal conductivity (Fig. 5j, k), both of which reach their highest values in the perpendicular case. We further conducted the temperature-dependent TET measurements of PIFS-1 under different orientations. It shows that convection enhancements of over 123% for the perpendicular direction, 103% for the gravity-aligned case, and only 47% for the opposing one (Fig. 5l). These results indicate that horizontal placement of the structured fiber on device surfaces is more favorable for heat dissipation, which is also consistent with engineering intuition drawn from heat pipes and vapor chambers.

Beyond gravity orientation, parametric analyses further show that convective enhancement is most pronounced in systems with low intrinsic thermal conductivity of both the skeleton and the phase-change material (Fig. S37). In such cases, the system tends to form a larger temperature gradient, and buoyancy-driven convection becomes the dominant pathway for thermal enhancement67. Together, these findings show the importance of considering heat convection when designing PCC for practical thermal management applications68.

In compact electronic devices, heat dissipation is often constrained by limited space, weak ambient convection, and limited room for bulky heat sinks69. Under such conditions, stabilizing device temperature and suppressing thermal fluctuations can be more critical than maximizing thermal conductivity alone70,71. To evaluate the practical performance of thermal buffering in this case, a mat fabricated by PIFS-3 fibers was applied directly onto the CPU of a development board (Orange Pi) for device-level testing. The CPU was subjected to a sustained maximum-load stress test for 1200 s, while surface temperatures were monitored by infrared imaging. Internal CPU temperature and operating frequency were recorded from system logs, and additional thermocouples monitored component surface temperatures. The performance of our thermal buffer was compared with that of a 5 W·m−1·K−1 heat spreader, and with a case without any additional thermal management. Infrared images in Fig. 6a reveal that, in the absence of thermal management, heat generated by the CPU progressively accumulates within the confined space and spreads across the entire printed circuit board (PCB), reaching temperatures of over 90 °C. Both the heat spreader and the thermal buffer effectively reduce the overall PCB temperature. Analysis of the CPU core temperature evolution in Fig. 6b shows that the heat spreader maintains a lower CPU temperature during the initial stage of operation. However, the thermal buffer leads to a slow temperature rise and results in a lower CPU temperature after 600 s of full-load operation. As shown in Fig. 6c, excessive CPU temperature triggers frequency throttling under high computational load. This behavior is notable, given that the intrinsic thermal conductivity of the PIFS fibers forming the thermal buffer is below 2 W·m−1·K−1, which is significantly lower than that of the heat spreader. Temperature measurements collected by thermocouples placed on the CPU, WiFi, and memory module (Fig. 6d–g) further show that the thermal-buffered case shows the lowest surface temperatures across all monitored components. This effect arises from the latent heat absorption of the phase-change material, which temporarily stores thermal energy and suppresses peak temperature propagation72. In contrast, under air convection-limited conditions, a highly conductive heat spreader can redistribute heat from the CPU to adjacent components, leading to unintended temperature increases elsewhere on the PCB. Numerical simulations were conducted further to examine this behavior (Fig. 6h and S38 and Supplementary Note S2). The simulated temperature distributions are consistent with the experimental observations, confirming that thermal buffering more effectively limits temperature rise across the PCB, CPU, and surrounding components in confined environments. Beyond electronic cooling, our PIFS fibers can also provide additional thermal management capabilities. As shown in Fig. S39 and Supplementary Note S3, our thermal storage fabrics can be rapidly “charged” under quick heating up, yet release heat slowly during cooling, thereby extending the thermal comfort window with improved temperature stability.

Discussion

Our study highlights the critical role of thermal conduction and thermal convection in the design, performance, and practical deployment of PCC. Using a coaxial wet spinning with the NIPS technique, we have successfully fabricated PCC fibers with core-shell structures that integrate mechanical robustness with controlled heat-transport pathways. Combined experimental measurements and numerical simulations demonstrate that the interconnected channel activates buoyancy-driven convection in the molten PCM, resulting in pronounced thermal enhancement. Structures with larger channels favor molten-phase mobility and convective transport, whereas denser networks suppress flow and shift heat transfer toward conduction-dominated regimes. These results establish a structure-based design framework in which convection-enhanced heat transfer becomes an essential factor for the performance of PCC fibers under practical operating conditions. Furthermore, device-level experiments confirm the effectiveness of these fibers as thermal buffers in compact electronic systems, as well as their applicability in thermal storage. Overall, this integrated conduction-convection perspective provides guiding principles for the rational design of PCCs and broadens their applicability in thermal management systems.

Methods

Materials

Raw hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) with an average size of 5 μm and a purity of 99.5 % was procured from Eno High-Tech Material Development. Kevlar was acquired from IVA Carbon Fiber Materials Co. Chemicals including dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, purity ≥99.9%), potassium hydroxide (KOH, purity ≥90.0%), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, purity ≥99.5%), absolute ethanol (EA, purity ≥99.7%), dimethylacetamide (DMAc, purity ≥99.0%), and polyethylene glycol (PEG, chemically pure) MW2000 were sourced from Titan Scientific Co. Polyvinyl butyral (PVB, chemically pure) MOWITAL® SB 70 HH was obtained from Kuraray Co., Japan. The Millipore Milli-Q® Reference Ultrapure Water Purification System supplied deionized water with a resistance of 18.2 megaohms.

Preparation of boron nitride nanosheets

The synthesis of boron nitride nanosheets (BNNS) followed the protocols outlined in our previous studies73. A ball milling jar was typically loaded with 20 g of pristine boron nitride powder (PT110, 5 μm) and 50 g of Dimethylacetamide (DMAc). The mixture was filled with 300 g of large milling beads (10 mm) and 100 g of small milling beads (2 mm). The jars were locked and transferred to a planetary ball mill. The milling process started at 600 rpm for 24 h. After milling, a white slurry was obtained, diluted to 10 mg/ml, and initially centrifuged at 100 × g for 30 min to remove incompletely exfoliated large flakes. The sediment was collected and dispersed to achieve a 10 wt% BNNS@DMAc dispersion.

Preparation of ANF dispersion

The dispersion process of ANF entails deprotonating PPTA fibers in alkaline, as introduced in previous studies74,75. Briefly, 10 g of short-cut K69 Kevlar fibers were mixed with 10 g of KOH and then added to 480 g of DMSO. The mixture was sealed in a bottle and then shaken reciprocally at 50 °C, 150 rpm for 72 h in a shaker to obtain a transparent dark red viscous liquid, indicating that the deprotonation reaction has reached its endpoint76.

Preparation of PVB@DMAc solution

At room temperature, 10 g of PVB and 90 g of DMAc were magnetically stirred in a sealed container for 30 min. This process resulted in the formation of a transparent solution of 10 wt% PVB@DMAc.

Preparation of spinning solution

The obtained 10 wt% BNNS@DMAc dispersion and 2 wt% ANF@DMSO were mixed and diluted with DMSO for a spinning solution with a solids content of 1 wt% as the shell spinning solution. In this case, BNNS content in the shell is set at 70 wt%. The core spinning solution has 10 wt% BNNS@DMAc dispersion and 10 wt% PVB/DMAc solution at different proportions. The content of BNNS varied at weight percentages of 0, 10, 30, 50, 70, and 90. All the mixing is carried out using a high-speed shear mixer to process the spinning solution at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. The precise value is determined by the TGA results depicted in Fig. S8.

Preparation of fiber and PCC fiber

Fiber fabrication: Coaxial wet spinning was conducted with a 20 mL glass microsyringe on a syringe pump (LSP01-1A) fitted with a coaxial needle (core: 25 G, inner diameter 0.50 mm; shell: 18 G, inner diameter 0.86 mm). The core and shell feed rates were each 0.8 mL min−1. Spinning was performed at 24 °C and 25% RH into a rotating deionized-water coagulation bath (linear surface speed 30 cm s−1). As-spun filaments were rinsed overnight in deionized water to promote core fibrillation, collected on a drum, and dried at 60 °C for 120 min.

Shell-free porous fibers were prepared by uniaxial wet spinning of a single PVB/BNNS dope containing 50 wt% BNNS (inner channel only), followed by the same coagulation, water-soak, and drying steps. This control is denoted PF-5.

In all PFS-x samples, both shell and core contained BNNS. The shell dope was ANF/BNNS with a fixed BNNS content of 70 wt%. The core dope was PVB/BNNS with BNNS at 10, 30, 50, or 70 wt%, yielding PFS-1, PFS-3, PFS-5, and PFS-7, respectively.

PCC Fiber fabrication: The vacuum infusion method was used to fill the PCM. The completely dried fibers and PEG were placed in a long trough container in a vacuum drying oven at 55 °C. They were preheated for 2 h until the PEG melted. The fibers were immersed in the liquid PEG and kept under vacuum for 24 h. The fibers were removed from the liquid PEG after impregnation, placed between two filter papers, and kept in the oven at 55 °C for 2 h to remove the PEG residue from the surface.

PF-5 after PEG infusion is denoted PIF-5. PFS-x after PEG infusion is denoted PIFS-x, where x matches the BNNS mass fraction in the PVB/BNNS core dope of the corresponding PFS-x.

Notably, PIFS-3R was prepared as a reference sample with the same composition as PIFS-3. Specifically, the core phase of PIFS-3 was first removed by washing with IPA. Subsequently, a molten mixture of PEG, PVB, and BNNS was refilled into the fiber core at the same composition ratio as that used in PIFS-3. In contrast to PIFS-3, the refilled core in PIFS-3R represents a randomly distributed microstructure without any deliberate orientation or structural design. As a result, PIFS-3R serves as a composition-matched control sample to isolate the effect of core structural organization on thermal transport performance.

Cyclic thermal tests were conducted to evaluate the thermal stability of the PIF and PIFS fibers. The PIF and PIFS fibers were sandwiched between two pieces of filter paper and loaded with a 50 g weight. The assembly was then placed on a heating pad, as shown in Fig. S40. The heating pad’s heating program was set to hold at 60 °C for 30 min and then cool naturally for 30 min, constituting one cycle.

Fabrication of PCM mats and PCM fabrics was accomplished through the assembly of PIFS. For PCM mats, PIFS bundles were uniformly laid flat within a mold to ensure surface smoothness. A 5 wt% fluorocarbon resin was applied as a binder, and the assembly was compressed under a constant load of 50 N overnight. Upon removal, excess material was trimmed to obtain the final mat. The fabrication process for PCM fabrics followed a similar approach to that used for anisotropic fiber mats77. PIFSs were continuously collected on a cylindrical roller, and a 0.5 wt% PVB adhesive was applied dropwise to form a fiber mat with an oriented layout.

Numerical study

The numerical study was performed in COMSOL Multiphysics. For simulations focusing on structure-dependent convection enhancement, the simulations employed a core-shell rectangular domain with a length of 1000 μm as a two-dimensional axial cross-sectional model of the cylindrical fiber. The shell thickness was fixed at 30 μm, and the inner core diameter was set to 300 μm.To maintain a constant porosity of 0.9, different geometric models were constructed by systematically adjusting the number, length, and width of the skeleton structures within the core. These variations correspond to the distinct geometries investigated in this case. Meshing was performed using physics-controlled meshing with the element size set to extra fine. Specifically, the Geom-1 model consisted of 170 vertex elements, 5,198 boundary elements, and 213,486 domain elements, whereas the Geom-4 model contained 410 vertex elements, 43,293 boundary elements, and 433,669 domain elements (Fig. S41).

For the multiphysics heat transfer simulations, all models were initialized at a uniform temperature of 25 °C. A constant temperature boundary condition of 70 °C was applied to drive heat transfer. Based on prior measurements of the compacted PVB framework (Fig. S42), the thermal conductivity of the solid framework was uniformly set to 2.00 W m−1 K−1 for all samples, while the outer shell was set to 10.00 W m−1 K−1. The PCM filling was assigned a thermal conductivity of 0.26 W m−1 K−1, an initial density of 1225 kg m⁻³, and a dynamic viscosity of 0.3 kg m−1 s−1. These properties were assumed to vary linearly once the temperature exceeded the phase transition temperature of 53 °C. The phase-change temperature interval was set to 3 °C. Gravitational effects were incorporated using the Boussinesq approximation. Transient behavior was analyzed using a time-dependent study with a convergence criterion of 1E-6. A time step of 0.001 s was employed, and the heat-transfer process was simulated over a total duration of 20 s to capture transient thermal dynamics. Note that Fig. S43 presents the FTIR spectra of the fibers before and after vacuum impregnation with PCM. Without new absorption peaks, the spectra suggest that no chemical interactions occurred between the PCM and the fiber host. Based on this, no adsorption between the fluid and the wall is considered.

Additional simulation cases and corresponding parameters presented in this work are described in detail in the Supporting Information.

Characterization

The crystal structures of the samples were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique using a Bruker D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer. Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted with a TA Q600 thermal analyzer to determine the BNNS concentration in each sample. A NICOLET iS50 FT-IR instrument provides information on the sample’s chemical bonding. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) images were obtained using a Quanta 400 FEG to characterize the sample's microscopic morphology. X-ray computed tomography CT was performed using an X-Mech2000 system (Hangzhou Zhonglian Zhijian Technology Co., Ltd.). Continuous scanning mode was employed with an exposure time of 1 s per projection, yielding a total of 1800 projection images. The projection data were reconstructed using an artifact-correction algorithm, followed by three-dimensional rendering and analysis in Avizo software. The optical micrographs were captured using an optical microscope (Leica DM750M). Tensile stress-strain curves were recorded utilizing a tensile testing machine (SANS UTM5000). In the thermal demonstration, the NEC AVIO R500 infrared camera records the temperature distribution and changes in the sample. The sample’s thermal management capabilities were evaluated using an Orange Pi development board (Orange Pi 3 LTS). The samples’ densities were measured using a Mettler analytical balance (MS204TS) with a drainage densitometer. The TET method was used to calculate the sample’s thermal diffusion coefficient.
