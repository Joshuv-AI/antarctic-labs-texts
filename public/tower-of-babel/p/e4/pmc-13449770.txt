Advances in radiotherapy-based multimodal treatment for non-small-cell lung cancer
Authors: Chenfei Wu, Ming Chen
Published: 2026

Over the past decade, radiotherapy for non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has evolved from a predominantly local modality into an integral component of multimodal cancer management across early-stage, locally advanced, and advanced disease. Its contemporary relevance lies in both local tumor control and rational integration with systemic therapy, while balancing efficacy against treatment-related toxicity.

Early-stage NSCLC (T1-2N0M0): radiotherapy shifts from alternative to first-line treatment in medically inoperable patients

For early-stage NSCLC patients who are ineligible for surgery due to advanced age, comorbidities, or other contraindications, radiotherapy—particularly stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT)—has become the standard nonsurgical treatment [Figure 1].

SBRT becomes the standard treatment

Conventional fractionated radiotherapy, typically delivered over 20–30 fractions, has historically provided limited local control in medically inoperable early-stage NSCLC. The development of SBRT enabled the safe delivery of ablative doses in a small number of fractions and thereby changed the treatment standard in this setting. RTOG 0236 established SBRT as an effective and tolerable definitive approach for peripheral early-stage disease,1 and CHISEL subsequently showed its superiority over conventional radiotherapy, with improvements in 2-year local failure-free survival from 65% to 89% and in overall survival (OS) from 59% to 77%.2 A propensity-matched analysis of the revised STARS trial suggested that SBRT may yield survival outcomes that are non-inferior to surgery in operable patients, though this finding requires confirmation in prospective randomized trials.3 Current guidelines and expert recommendations regard SBRT as the standard curative-intent nonsurgical treatment for medically inoperable early-stage NSCLC.

Expanding the role of SBRT for central lung tumors

Central lung tumors proximal to the bronchi or great vessels were historically regarded as contraindications for SBRT because of the risk of severe toxicity from high-dose irradiation. Refinements in fractionation and treatment planning have made SBRT feasible in selected patients with centrally located early-stage NSCLC. Results from the RTOG 0813 trial demonstrated that a total dose of 60 Gy/5 fractions is safe and effective for centrally located early-stage NSCLC.4 The maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was identified as 12.0 Gy per fraction, with a dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) rate of approximately 7.2%, and the 2-year local control was comparable to that observed in peripheral tumors.4 These results should not be interpreted as uniform safety across all central lesions. The therapeutic window for ultracentral tumors remains narrow. The HILUS trial illustrated that SBRT for tumors within 1 cm of the main bronchi and trachea presented a high risk of Grade 5 hemoptysis.5 Current efforts should focus on risk-adapted dose-fractionation, precise organ-at-risk constraints, and identification of patients in whom ablative treatment can be delivered without unacceptable toxicity.

Treatment optimization with immunotherapy (IO)

Although SBRT provides local control approaching that of surgery in early-stage NSCLC, regional and distant relapse remain the principal modes of failure outside the treated volume, providing the rationale for systemic intensification. Recent interest has focused on combining SBRT with systemic therapy. In the randomized phase II I-SABR trial, the addition of nivolumab to SBRT improved 4-year event-free survival from 53% to 77% compared with SBRT alone.6 This signal was tempered by early results from the phase III SWOG/NRG S1914 trial, which showed no survival improvement with atezolizumab added to SBRT and higher rates of Grade 3 adverse events (AEs) than with SBRT alone (12% versus 2%).7 On current evidence, IO-based escalation after SBRT remains investigational, with particular attention to pneumonitis and treatment-related quality-of-life deterioration in medically inoperable patients.

Locally advanced NSCLC (IIIB-IIIC): Synergistic Effects of combined treatments

Unresectable stage III NSCLC is characterized by substantial clinical, pathological, and molecular heterogeneity, resulting in marked variation in treatment strategies. Definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) now serves as the foundational therapeutic backbone upon which subsequent systemic strategies are layered, rather than representing the sole definitive treatment. Current advances in this setting center on multimodal integration—including IO, targeted therapy, and other novel agents—with increasing emphasis on treatment sequencing, patient selection, and biomarker-informed adaptation [Figure 1]. This shift has also changed the toxicity landscape. Patients now receive prolonged systemic therapy before or after thoracic CRT, making pneumonitis, interstitial lung disease (ILD), cardiac injury, and quality of life central components of treatment evaluation.

Consolidation IO as the current standard of care (SoC) after definitive CRT

The PACIFIC trial, first reported in 2017, established durvalumab after concurrent CRT as the SoC for unresectable stage III NSCLC, improving the median PFS from 5.6 to 16.9 months and the 5-year OS rate from 33.4% to 42.9% compared with placebo in patients without progression after CRT.8 The rationale for post-CRT IO is that radiation-induced DNA damage can activate innate immune-sensing pathways, such as cGAS-STING and type I interferon signaling, promoting antigen presentation and subsequent T-cell priming. Programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) blockade may then sustain or restore these anti-tumor immune responses after CRT. The phase III GEMSTONE-301 and R-ALPS trials further broadened this paradigm by showing that consolidation IO could also provide benefit after sequential CRT.9,10 In this setting, pneumonitis has become the key overlapping toxicity of thoracic irradiation and IO, occurring as any grade treatment-related pneumonitis in 33.9% versus 24.8% of patients in the PACIFIC trial.

Beyond post- CRT consolidation, earlier integration of IO remains an active area of investigation. Single-arm AFT-16 and APOLO studies suggest that induction IO prior to definitive CRT may be feasible.11,12 PACIFIC-2 showed that concurrent durvalumab with CRT did not improve progression-free survival (PFS) or OS relative to CRT alone, and concurrent durvalumab was associated with higher rates of AEs leading to treatment discontinuation (25.6% versus 12.0%).13 One plausible explanation is that, although radiotherapy can enhance anti-tumor immune response, concurrent CRT simultaneously induces lymphopenia, potentially depleting the effector T cells required for PD-(L)1 blockade. Lessons from PACIFIC-2 do not argue against earlier introduction of IO, but underscore the need for more precise patient selection to balance efficacy and toxicity.

Biomarker-informed treatment adaptation might be a promising approach to identify patients most likely to benefit from treatment intensification. Subgroup analyses from the PACIFIC trial suggested no OS benefit in patients with PD-L1 tumor cell expression <1%, although this finding was limited by its post hoc nature and missing data. More compelling signals come from dynamic biomarkers. In the phase II DART study, detectable circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) during the first four months after CRT was associated with significantly shorter PFS,14 suggesting that molecular residual disease may identify patients who require treatment intensification or alternative consolidation strategies. Imaging-based adaptation remains uncertain. Mid-treatment FDG-PET–guided dose escalation in RTOG 1106 was safe but did not improve locoregional control.15 Given that the evidence is still largely derived from phase II trials, exploratory analyses, and retrospective cohorts, prospective validation of these biomarkers as predefined stratification or adaptive-treatment variables is urgently needed.

Targeted therapy consolidation redefines oncogene-driven subsets

In EGFR-mutated unresectable stage III NSCLC, the phase III LAURA trial established consolidation with osimertinib, an EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), as the new standard after definitive CRT, with a marked PFS benefit over placebo in patients without progression after concurrent or sequential CRT (median 39.1 versus 5.6 months, P < 0.001).16 This benefit was accompanied by increased pulmonary toxicity, including higher rates of any grade radiation pneumonitis (48% versus 38%) and ILD (8% versus 1%) with osimertinib. Unlike immune-checkpoint blockade, EGFR-TKIs do not primarily induce immune-mediated pneumonitis, but TKI-related ILD may overlap with or aggravate radiation pneumonitis after thoracic irradiation. In parallel with ongoing efforts to refine post-CRT consolidation, EGFR-targeted therapy is also being explored earlier across the treatment continuum, including induction osimertinib before CRT, as evaluated in the NEOLA trial.17 These earlier integration strategies should be developed together with pulmonary safety monitoring. More broadly, as targeted therapies continue to be validated in advanced NSCLC across additional oncogenic subsets, such as ALK, ROS1, and KRAS G12C, integration of CRT with genotype-matched targeted therapy is a rational direction for patients with unresectable stage III disease.

IO-based combinations seek to improve on the consolidation backbone

Additional immune-modulating strategies constitute an important area of ongoing clinical investigation for unresectable stage III NSCLC. The phase II COAST trial showed that consolidation durvalumab combined with oleclumab (anti-CD73) or monalizumab (anti-NKG2A) prolonged PFS relative to durvalumab alone in a pre-planned interim analysis, with median PFS of 21.1 and 19.8 months versus 7.3 months, respectively.18 These findings require confirmation in the ongoing phase III PACIFIC-9 study.19 Consolidation benmelstobart with or without the anti-angiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitor anlotinib in the phase III R-ALPS trial improved PFS versus placebo after CRT, with the greatest benefit observed in the combination arm (median PFS of 15.1 months versus 4.2 months).10 The benefit of intensification has not been uniform, as the phase III SKYSCRAPER-03 trial failed to demonstrate a PFS benefit for tiragolumab plus atezolizumab consolidation over durvalumab consolidation, while showing higher rates of AEs leading to treatment withdrawal.20 Although PD-L1/TIGIT co-blockade is supported by mechanistic rationale, such as the restoration of CD226-dependent anti-tumor CD8+ T-cell responses, this biological synergy has not yet translated into consistent clinical benefit, highlighting a translational gap between the laboratory results and clinical efficacy. Additional immune-modulating consolidation strategies remain promising but should not be viewed as interchangeable extensions of the PACIFIC regimen.

Emerging roles for antibody–drug conjugates and bispecific antibodies

Novel agents, particularly antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) and bispecific antibodies, represent another promising direction in unresectable stage III NSCLC. ADCs such as datopotamab deruxtecan (TROP2 ADC), trastuzumab deruxtecan (HER2 ADC), and izalontamab brengitecan (EGFRxHER3 ADC), and bispecific antibodies such as ivonescimab (PD-1-VEGF) and amivantamab (EGFR-MET) have shown clinically meaningful activity in advanced NSCLC. The translation of these agents into the curative-intent stage III setting remains preliminary. The challenge is not simply to improve on the current post-CRT backbone, but to do so without compounding thoracic toxicity, particularly pneumonitis and ILD. In unresectable stage III NSCLC, ADCs and bispecific antibodies should still be regarded as investigational, with ongoing work focused on patient selection, treatment sequencing, and defining an acceptable safety framework for integration.

Advanced stage NSCLC (IV): from palliation to active disease control

As systemic therapy has become more effective in advanced-stage NSCLC, radiotherapy has increasingly been incorporated into planned treatment aimed at consolidating response, eradicating refractory lesions, and reshaping the tumor microenvironment [Figure 1]. In this setting, radiotherapy should no longer be regarded solely as a palliative tool for symptom relief.

Radiotherapy in oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease expands the therapeutic role of radiotherapy

According to the American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO)- European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO) guideline, oligometastatic NSCLC is generally defined by up to 5 extracranial lesions that are technically feasible and clinically safe to treat with definitive local therapy.21 The randomized phase II trial by Iyengar et al. supports local consolidative radiotherapy to all radiographically identifiable residual disease in patients with oligometastatic NSCLC after first-line systemic therapy, improving median PFS from 3.5 to 9.7 months compared with maintenance chemotherapy alone.22 These data help define oligometastatic disease as a therapeutically distinct state and provide a rationale for integrating local radiotherapy into first-line management in metastatic NSCLC, rather than deferring it to late salvage. Oligoprogression similarly denotes limited progression after first-line systemic therapy, typically involving up to five existing or new extracranial lesions.21 The phase II CURB trial, which included patients with breast cancer or NSCLC, showed that the addition of SBRT to standard systemic therapy substantially improved median PFS from 2.2 months to 10.0 months in NSCLC patients with oligoprogressive disease.23 In this setting, ablative radiotherapy can be used to eradicate resistant sites while preserving the benefit of the ongoing systemic regimen, delaying the switch to later-line therapy. These data position radiotherapy in advanced NSCLC not only as palliation, but also as a disease-control strategy in carefully selected patients. It should be noted that imaging-defined oligometastasis or oligoprogression based on lesion size and number might be an imprecise surrogate for curative potential, which depends on multiple factors, including disease kinetics, molecular subtype, and immune microenvironment. Future trials should move beyond lesion-count–based selection and adopt multimodal stratifications.

Integration of radiotherapy with IO and targeted therapy

In advanced NSCLC, the rationale for combining radiotherapy with immune-checkpoint blockade is to convert selected irradiated lesions into antigenic sources that broaden systemic anti-tumor immune recognition. The PEMBRO-RT trial, a small randomized study involving 92 patients, provided an early signal suggesting that SBRT before pembrolizumab might enhance systemic antitumor activity in metastatic NSCLC, with longer median PFS than pembrolizumab alone (6.6 vs 1.9 months).24 This signal was not confirmed in the randomized phase II/III NRG-LU002 trial, in which the addition of local consolidative therapy to maintenance systemic therapy failed to improve PFS or OS and increased Grade 3 or higher pneumonitis from 1% to 10%.25 The negative result indicates that when thoracic lesions are chosen as immunogenic targets, safety considerations such as field size, lung dose, and history of immune-related pneumonitis should be prespecified. Another determinant might be whether local irradiation can generate systemic immune control sufficient to eradicate distant disease. The abscopal effect, defined as regression of non-irradiated lesions after irradiation, represents one manifestation of such radiation-induced systemic immunity. Because this phenomenon is uncommon and poorly predictable, it cannot yet be relied upon as a reproducible mechanism.

A more coherent signal emerges when radiotherapy is used in oncogene-driven stage IV NSCLC. In patients with treatment-naïve synchronous oligometastatic EGFR-mutant disease, the phase III SINDAS trial showed that radiotherapy to all known sites before first-generation EGFR TKI therapy prolonged median PFS from 12.5 to 20.2 months and median OS from 17.4 to 25.5 months relative to TKI alone.26 Randomized phase II data from NorthStar showed that adding local consolidative therapy after 6–12 weeks of osimertinib extended median PFS from 17.0 to 25.4 months, with benefit also observed in patients with more than three metastases.27 These findings suggest that radiotherapy-based combinations in stage IV NSCLC might be most effective in biologically selected settings, where local therapy can suppress residual resistant clones and extend the benefit of active systemic therapy.

Summary and future directions

Over the past decade, radiotherapy for NSCLC has evolved from a local treatment modality into an integral component of multimodal cancer management. In early-stage disease, SBRT has become a curative-intent option for medically inoperable patients. In unresectable stage III disease, CRT has been combined with IO, targeted therapy, ADCs, and other novel agents. In advanced NSCLC, radiotherapy is increasingly used to consolidate systemic response and eradicate resistant lesions.

Further progress will require clinical trials to treat radiotherapy technology as part of the therapeutic strategy. The transition from three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) to more advanced techniques, such as volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), has improved dose conformity. Online CT-or MRI-guided radiotherapy allows assessment of tumor motion, adaptive adjustment of target volumes, and better sparing of normal tissues. Advances in respiratory motion management may further improve the precision of lung radiotherapy. A bi-polar gated strategy, in which radiation is delivered during brief breath-holds at both end-inhalation and end-exhalation, reduced internal target volumes and lung exposure while maintaining a high treatment duty cycle with reduced technical complecity in lung SBRT. 28 Particle beam radiotherapy provides another technological approach of precision radiotherapy through the superior physical and biological properties of protons and carbon ions. A global analysis of 416 trials documented broad clinical exploration across multiple tumor types, reflecting the potential value of proton therapy for normal-tissue preservation and carbon-ion therapy for selected radioresistant tumors.29 These technologies should be incorporated into trial design for combined-modality therapy, including radiotherapy, IO, and ADCs, especially when the therapeutic ratio is constrained by toxicity such as pneumonitis.

The next phase of NSCLC radiotherapy should move beyond empirical escalation. Negative results from studies such as PACIFIC-2, SKYSCRAPER-03, and NRG-LU002 indicate that treatment intensification is not uniformly beneficial, and that its success depends on appropriate biological rationale, patient selection, therapeutic timing, and toxicity control. Future trials should incorporate prospective stratification and adaptive designs to test these variables explicitly rather than treating stage or lesion number alone as sufficient selection criteria. Biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression, ctDNA dynamics, and functional imaging may help identify optimal treatment strategies. Large prospective studies should give equal weight to efficacy, toxicity, and quality of life.
