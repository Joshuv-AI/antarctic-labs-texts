Subjective Visual Vertical Can be an Effective Tool to Evaluate Vestibular Function of Stapedotomy
Authors: Tao Jiang, Juan Zheng, Wen‐Yan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate if the subjective visual vertical/horizontal (SVV/SVH) can be used to evaluate the otolith organ of otosclerosis and stapedotomy.DesignA total of 19 patients who were diagnosed with otosclerosis and underwent surgical treatment in our hospital were collected. Based on the postoperative vertigo difference, we evaluated the otolith organ function between preoperative and postoperative SVV/SVH degrees between the dizzy and non‐dizzy groups.ResultsPatients who complained of dizziness after prosthesis implantation generally have worse SVV and SVH results than those who have no complaint. It is suggested that the vestibule may be perturbed during the stapes implantation, which may disturb functions of the otolith organs that result in postoperative dizziness in patients.ConclusionSVV/SVH, as an evaluation tool of otolith organ function, can effectively assess the vestibular function of patients with vertigo after stapes implantation.Keywords: clinical practice, otosclerosis, stapedotomy, subjective visual horizontal, subjective visual vertical, vertigo

Introduction

Otospongiosis of the otic capsule in otosclerosis caused transduction and even neurosensory hearing loss [1]. To rescue the hearing of the patients, replacing the fixed stapes with a small prosthesis piston as the stapedotomy has become the preferred procedure with fewer complications nowadays [2, 3]. However, it is still about 11% post‐surgery vertigo has been reported [4, 5]. Several surgical reasons proposed before that may lead to the vertigo, including intravestibular protrusion of prosthesis, damage to the membrane labyrinth, surgical manipulation of the footplate, or unexpected perilymph gusher, etc [3, 6]. However, little was known about how to evaluate the post‐surgery dizziness. Given that the vestibule beyond the footplate houses two otolith organs that are responsible for linear acceleration perception anatomically [7, 8]. Attempts were made to evaluate the saccular function after stapedotomy by cervical vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (cVEMPs) [6]. But, the presumed changes caused by surgery were minor in cVEMPS, probably due to the required sensitivity to evaluate the post‐surgery vertigo being beyond the diagnostic abilities of cVEMPs. Moreover, there was an attempt to use the video head impulse test (vHIT), which is sensitive to quick eye movements during head rotations, to investigate whether the vestibular function can be diagnosed [9]. Even though the vHIT results demonstrated its sensitivity to reveal the semicircular canal system that may be affected, it did not do so significantly. In this case, finding a sensitive and maneuverable tool to assess the dizziness post‐stapedotomy is still a challenge during clinical practices.

To overcome the above‐listed disadvantages and to better evaluate the function of otolith organs, we focused on another test that moves from the laboratory to the clinics, subjective visual vertical/horizontal (SVV/SVH). With developing technologies, SVV/SVH perceive the input from otolith organs of the head position to gravity and are mediated by the bilateral central circuitry connections in the central nervous system [10, 11]. Until recently, SVV/SVH testing became reliable in clinical practices with a virtual reality (VR) head‐mounted display that was demonstrated by several studies [12, 13]. With the advantages of SVV/SVH in sensitivity and convenience, we applied the test to the otosclerosis patients before and after surgery to evaluate if the otolith organs were disturbed during the surgery. Our results indicated that this test might be a good tool to achieve our goal during the daily practices.

Methods

Nineteen patients who were diagnosed with otosclerosis were recruited for our experiments. Subjects were enrolled under a protocol approved by the hospital. All procedures involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed consent was obtained from the participants.

A single trained clinician guided all participants through tests. Each patient completed at least three experimental sessions with 10 adjustments per session for both SVV and SVH tests. The testing was performed with a commercially available system (Verti‐SVV, Zehnit, Shanghai, China). During the test, the participants adjusted the random tilted angle of a yellow dotted luminous line displayed in the VR Google through the controller to exactly the vertical or horizontal plane [12, 13]. Each testing trial contains the head upright (0°), and the head tilted 30° toward each shoulder. Head tilts were instructed by the examiner to keep the head in a consistent position. To ensure the head tilt position, the examiner would give feedback during each experiment.

Each patient was guided through the SVV and SVH tests the day before and after the stapedotomy surgery. Questionnaires were obtained from patients on the post‐surgery day. We applied Visual Analog Scale for vertigo (VAS‐V) to the patients who gave a score of their dizziness after the surgery. The patients who felt dizzy before surgery were excluded. We used the Grad‐graph to perform statistics. The Student's t‐test was applied to perform the statistical analysis. Statistical significance was taken with a p < 0.05.

Results

Nineteen patients, including 10 males and 9 females, ranging from 21 to 67 years old, were recruited for our experiments. Nine out of the 19 had the left ear performed stapedotomy, while the rest had the right ear operated (Table 1). Before the surgeries, the hearing tests were performed to assess the level of conduction hearing loss. The average air‐conducted (AC) hearing of the ipsilateral ear was 54.68 dB, while the bone‐conducted (BC) hearing was 26.42 dB, which had about 28.26 dB differences in general (Figure 1A). For each patient, we had the hearing test results of the ipsilateral ear listed (Figure 1B). Stapedotomy of patients was performed by five well‐recognized surgeons, during which three types of prosthesis were chosen to replace the stapes (Table 1).

On the postoperative day, we applied VAS‐V on the patients. Out of the 19 patients, 11 claimed no vertigo at all and scored 0 on the questionnaire. In contrast, eight patients claimed various degrees of dizziness (Figure 2A). The scores range from 1 to 5, with an average of 2.6 out of 10. However, none of the 19 patients displayed any nystagmus, even though they claimed dizziness. To further evaluate the vestibular function of post‐stapedotomy dizziness, we separated the patients into non‐dizzy and dizzy groups to analyze the SVV and SVH results.

Given that the dizzy group claimed vertigo, we analyzed their perceptions of vertical and horizontal at the head upright position (0°, Figure 2B). In comparison to the non‐dizzy group, the dizzy ones have an increased deviation from the designated planes. However, according to previous publications, the deviated degrees are still within the normal range. In this case, even though patients claimed vertigo and the perception of axis in our results deviated more, the otolith function was still within the normal range.

The vertigo complained of by patients may be caused by disturbance of the vestibule during stapedotomy. Given that the otolith organs are more activated when gravity is added, we further evaluated the perception of orientation at the head tilt position besides the 0°. In the non‐dizzy group, patients have had fewer differences around the surgeries, which is less than 1.7° (Figure 3). In contrast, the dizzy group performed worse after surgery, especially tilted to the ipsilateral side. The deviation degrees post‐surgery for the dizzy group are 6.9 in SVV and 8.86 in SVH testing, in comparison to 3.98 in SVV and 4.65 in SVH before surgery. The differences between deviation degrees could be more than −4.213. Worthwhile to point out that the dizzy group had statistical significance on the ipsilateral side in comparison to the non‐dizzy group when performing the SVV (p = 0.019). These results revealed that the SVV/SVH picked up the vestibular disturbance of vertigo patients after stapedotomy, which indicated it was an effective tool to assess the vestibular functions of otosclerosis patients.

Discussion

We performed the SVV/SVH for the patients who underwent stapedotomy. For the operations on the stapes, vertigo is a common complication. As stated previously, there are several hypotheses on how the stapes surgery affected vestibular systems, like decreased perilymph, manipulation of the footplate, inappropriate piston prosthesis, trauma to the inner ear, or labyrinthitis [6, 7, 9]. A previous paper found that otosclerosis and stapedotomy may have an effect on the lateral semicircular canal that can be evaluated by vHIT [9]. This could have been attributed to the general influence of the inner ear by the stapedotomy on the whole vestibular system, including the semicircular canals. Since ampullae were relatively far from the oral window, the stapes surgery disturbance on the otolith organs, but not the whole vestibular system, may cause patients to experience subclinical dizziness or vertigo. However, there was a group that tried to use VEMP to assess the otolith organs after stapedotomy [6]. But decreased sound transmission or vestibular system resistance lowered the sensitivity of VEMP, so that stapedotomy didn't elicit changes.

To solve the assessment problem of the post‐stapes surgery dizziness, we found an effective tool that is sensitive enough for these “subclinical” dizziness [10, 11, 14]. First of all, with better sensitivity, SVV/SVH is able to pick the deviated degree at one‐tenth digit level, which allows the clinician to pick subtle differences. Besides, SVV/SVH testing conveys the perception of graviceptive signals that are helpful for the unilateral vestibular disorders, especially in the acute phase, as a good indication for stapedotomy. In our studies, 8 out of 19 patients claimed dizziness, but none showed any nystagmus or balancing problems. Before then, these patients are generally ignored since no symptom or sign can be identified or evaluated. But, based on our results, SVV/SVH testing is able to pick these dizzy patients and assess their severity. Thus, we proposed to apply the SVV/SVH for the otosclerosis patients to evaluate their vestibular functions around surgery in clinics.

There are several limitations of this study that point us to some future directions. We only gained a relatively small sample size, even though it already revealed to us the statistical significance of SVV/SVH in post‐surgery vertigo. In addition, SVV/SVH revealed both central and peripheral otolith organ‐related vestibular function. This test may not be precise enough in evaluation. In our paper, we control the single variation as the surgery. But there could be some underlying aberrance that affects our results. Given that there are also several other tests for otolith organs that bypass the transduction system of the middle ear, like ocular‐counter tests or off‐axis rotations [14, 15], they can be tested later on to see if they have similar sensitivity to assess the vestibular functions of otosclerosis patients. Thus, in the future, a larger scale of clinical trial to explore the universality, specificity, and predictability of SVV/SVH for otosclerosis patients is needed to help us gain more knowledge about the dizziness brought by stapedotomy.
