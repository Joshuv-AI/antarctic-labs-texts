Bibliometric analysis of deprescribing in older adults based on web of science
Authors: Yaling Wang, Zhe Liu, Chen Yu, Fang Li, Ling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:With the deepening of societal aging, the older adults with comorbidities continues to grow, leading to a rise in the phenomenon of polypharmacy. As a key strategy to address polypharmacy, deprescribing has garnered increasing attention. This study aims to systematically review the current research status and development trends in the field of deprescribing for older adults.Methods:English literature related to deprescribing in older adults, published from the establishment of the Web of Science Core Collection database up to June 8, 2025, was retrieved. CiteSpace and VOSviewer software were employed for visual analysis of indicators such as the number of publications, authors, institutions, and keywords.Results:A total of 703 papers on deprescribing in older adults were included in this study. The annual volume of publications in this field showed an overall upward trend. Australia and the United States dominated the field, with Emily Reeve from Australia being the author with the highest number of publications. The research hotspots in this field included the implementation environment of deprescribing, intervention measures, drug-related issues, and patient-centered treatment plans.Conclusion:Through systematic sorting and in-depth bibliometric analysis of relevant domestic and international literature, this study outlines the current research landscape and trends in the field, with the aim of providing theoretical references and practical guidance for academic research and clinical practice in the field of prescription simplification for older adults.

1. Introduction

According to the World Population Prospects 2024 released by the United Nations, the global proportion of the population aged 65 and above is approximately 10.5%, and this ratio is projected to rise to 16% by 2050. In China, the proportion of people aged 65 and above is about 14.7%, which is expected to climb to 30.9% by 2050, indicating that China has entered a stage of deep population aging.[1] With the decline in bodily functions, older adults often suffer from multiple chronic diseases and require treatment with a variety of medications. Consequently, the phenomena of multimorbidity and polypharmacy are highly prevalent among this group.

Multimorbidity refers to the coexistence of 2 or more chronic health conditions in an older individual.[2] Reports show that the prevalence of multimorbidity among the global population aged 65 and above exceeds 60%, and more than 50% of older adults have 3 or more chronic diseases simultaneously.[3] In China, over 190 million older adults live with chronic diseases, and 43% of them have 2 or more diseases simultaneously.[4]

Polypharmacy is defined as the concurrent use of 5 or more medications per day, including prescription drugs, over-the-counter drugs, and Chinese herbal medicines.[5] Studies indicate that the overall incidence of polypharmacy among older adults in European countries is approximately 36.2%,[6] while in China, this rate is as high as 70.8%.[7] Polypharmacy not only increases the risk of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), but also may lead to a series of drug-related problems, including adverse drug events, drug–drug interactions, and reduced medication adherence. These issues further elevate the risks of hospitalization and mortality and impose a heavier burden on healthcare expenditures.[8]

The concept of deprescribing was 1st proposed by Australian expert Woodward in 2003 to guide the management of polypharmacy in patients with chronic diseases.[9] Its core principle is to discontinue or reduce medications that may cause harm to patients or from which patients no longer derive benefits. This approach aims to alleviate the burden of medication use and reduce adverse drug reactions while maintaining or improving patients’ quality of life.[10] A meta-analysis revealed that deprescribing does not reduce all-cause mortality in patients; however, when deprescribing is implemented with specificity or followed by long-term follow-up (>6 months), it significantly reduces patient mortality (P < .05). Additionally, although the number of patients who experienced falls did not decrease significantly, the average number of falls per patient declined substantially.[11]

With the accumulation of evidence-based data, deprescribing has gained increasing attention worldwide and has been designated by the World Health Organization as a core component of safe medication management. The World Health Organization emphasizes that the deprescribing process should be “as robust as that of prescribing.”[12] Nevertheless, despite the fact that this concept was proposed more than 2 decades ago, there is no systematic sorting and comprehensive analysis in this field using bibliometric methods, especially for older adults.

Bibliometric analysis is a quantitative research method used to systematically study literature in a specific disciplinary field. It aims to explore the developmental trajectory, evolution of research hotspots, and academic influence of the field.[13] By applying bibliometric methods, this study systematically collates and visually analyzes relevant literature at home and abroad. The objectives are to systematically depict the development trends and collaborative networks in this field, clarify its core knowledge base, and track the evolutionary process and cutting-edge directions of research hotspots. Ultimately, this study intends to provide theoretical references and practical guidance for academic research and clinical practice in the field of deprescribing for older adults.

2. Data sources and methods

2.1. Literature retrieval and inclusion/exclusion criteria

On June 8, 2025, a literature search was conducted on the Web of Science Core Collection database, covering the Social Sciences Citation Index, Science Citation Index Expanded, Arts and Humanities Citation Index, and Conference Proceedings Citation Index. The search was performed without a time restriction, and the resulting publications covered the period from 1997 to 2025. The search query used was TS = (elder* OR old OR aged OR senior OR geriatric*) AND (deprescribing OR deprescribe). A preliminary search yielded 1426 papers. After excluding medical record reports, conference abstracts, repeatedly published documents, and those inconsistent with the topic, 703 papers were finally included (Fig. 1A). These papers were exported in “RefWorks” for further analysis.

(A) Flowchart of bibliometric analysis. (B) Trends in the volume of publications on deprescribing for older adults.

2.2. Data analysis and visualization

The exported “RefWorks” data was imported into the softwares of CiteSpace 6.4.R1 and VOSviewer 1.6.20 respectively for visual analysis. Firstly, the annual number of publications after deduplication was obtained using the “Remove Duplicates” function in CiteSpace and then imported into Excel 2019 for publication trend analysis. Subsequently, CiteSpace was mainly used for co-citation analysis of authors and journals, co-occurrence, clustering, and emergent analysis of keywords, as well as clustering and emergent analysis of references, to showcase the development context and research hotspots of the topic. The key indicator of this analysis is betweenness centrality, which is used to measure the importance of nodes in the network. VOSviewer is used for the analysis of authors and institutions to reveal collaborative networks. The specific settings for CiteSpace were as follows: time span, 2011 to 2025; time slice, 1 year; g-index (k = 25); pruning methods, Pathfinder, Pruning Sliced Network, and Pruning the Merged Network; other parameters were set to default values. For VOSviewer, “Co-authorship” and “Co-occurrence” under “Type of analysis” were selected for analysis respectively. An appropriate minimum co-occurrence threshold was set, and other parameters remained default.

4. Discussion

4.1. General information

This study retrieved literature related to deprescribing in older adults from the Web of Science database and used CiteSpace and VOSviewer software for visual analysis of indicators such as the annual number of publications, authors, institutions, and keywords. The aim was to present the hotspots and trends of the research in this field and provide references for the future research and application of deprescribing in China.

Since 2011, the annual number of published papers on deprescribing in older adults worldwide has generally shown a steady upward trend. Although research in this field started relatively late in various countries, with the popularization of the concept of rational drug use and the increasing attention paid to it, relevant research achievements have gradually accumulated, and the number of publications has also exhibited a steady growth momentum. Particularly in 2023, the number of publications and annual growth in this field reached a peak, marking that research on deprescribing in older adults has entered a new stage of rapid development.

Emily Reeve from Monash University, Australia, has the highest number of publications and co-citation frequency in this research field, which fully confirms her leading position in academic output and influence. Emily Reeve core contributions lie in deepening the theoretical definition of deprescribing and conducting a systematic analysis of the key barriers and enablers for implementing deprescribing in older adults.[18,20,21] Although the collaboration map shows that she has established a preliminary cooperation framework with different teams, the depth and breadth of such connections still need to be further enhanced.

The number of publications by a research institution is an important indicator to measure its research activity and academic influence.[22] In this research field, the top 10 institutions in terms of the number of publications are mainly concentrated in the United States and Australia, reflecting the leading position and dominant role of these 2 countries in research on deprescribing in older adults. It should be pointed out that betweenness centrality does not equate to the number of published articles, but rather reflects the role of an institution as a connection and bridge within the topic network. It is worth noting that the University of Sydney in Australia is not only the institution with the highest number of publications globally but also plays a key hub role in the collaboration network, leading multiple important research directions and cooperation pathways. This also reveals from the side that the current cooperation structure in this field is highly dependent on a few core institutions. However, the collaboration network analysis also shows that direct connections between non-core institutions are sparse, and cross-team and cross-regional collaboration have not been fully developed, resulting in an unbalanced overall cooperation pattern. In the future, it is necessary to further promote the construction of broader, more diverse, and stable cooperative relationships to facilitate the effective integration of global research resources and collaborative innovation of academic achievements.

In the journal co-citation analysis, BMC Geriatrics ranks first in co-citation frequency, indicating that this journal has extensive academic influence and a good disciplinary reputation in the field of geriatrics. This study further shows that among the top 9 journals in terms of co-citation frequency, more than half belong to the JCR Q1 category, reflecting the dominant position of high-impact journals in this research direction. To improve the visibility and dissemination of research on deprescribing in older adults, more high-quality research results should be encouraged to be published in Q1-level journals in the future, thereby promoting in-depth development and academic accumulation in this field.

4.2. Knowledge base

Co-citation analysis of literature can be used to gain an initial understanding of the foundation of a field, and these literatures serve as important bases for conducting new research. Therefore, this study sorted out the knowledge base of deprescribing in older adults by analyzing co-cited references.

The top 10 highly cited references mainly focus on 3 aspects: screening criteria for deprescribing in older adults (3 articles),[14,23,24] evidence synthesis and effect evaluation (3 articles),[25–27] and implementation strategies and influencing factors (4 articles).[15,18,28,29] Among them, the screening criteria for deprescribing in older adults include the American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria and the STOPP/START Criteria, which provide clinical workers with key bases for identifying and reducing PIMs. This study shows that the research on the update of the Beers Criteria published by Fick et al[14] in 2019 has the highest citation frequency, reflecting its core guiding role in global geriatric practice.

Literatures on evidence synthesis and effect evaluation has systematically evaluated the impact of deprescribing on the survival rate, health status, and clinical outcomes of older adults, confirming the necessity and effectiveness of its implementation. A systematic review and meta-analysis on the feasibility of deprescribing in older adults and its impact on mortality and health, published by Page et al[25] in 2016, shows that non-randomized controlled studies indicate that deprescribing can reduce mortality, while randomized studies show no reduction in mortality; however, when patient-specific interventions are used to implement deprescribing, mortality is significantly reduced.

Literatures on implementation strategies and influencing factors focuses on exploring the definition, process, influencing factors, and specific intervention strategies of deprescribing. Studies by Scott[15] and Reeve[28] further indicate that the early theoretical construction of polypharmacy intervention and deprescribing processes, patients’ attitudes, and pharmacist-led medication education have exerted a profound impact on subsequent research.

The cluster structure of references not only reveals the interdisciplinary nature of research on deprescribing in older adults but also reflects the deepening of this field from simply focusing on drug lists to research on patient individualization and scenario specialization. In recent years, the themes of “burst literatures” have gradually expanded to “barriers and enablers,” “patient attitude assessment,” and “randomized controlled trials,” indicating that research has gradually shifted from concept interpretation to implementation science and clinical translation. This suggests that in the future, more attention should be paid to the feasibility of intervention strategies and their promotion paths in real-world settings.

4.3. Hotspots and frontiers

Visual analysis of keywords can provide an intuitive basis for initially grasping the development trends and research hotspots in the field of deprescribing in older adults. The analysis results show that the research focus in this field mainly lies in the implementation environment of deprescribing, intervention measures, drug-related issues, and patient-centered treatment plans, among others. Studies show that patients generally accept the reduction of prescriptions.

A meta-analysis indicated that approximately 84% of patients are willing to discontinue 1 or more medications on the advice of physicians.[30] Although specific medication decisions vary from person to person, the majority of patients tend to follow medical advice.[31] In nursing institutions, deprescribing has shown significant effects. Meta-analyses indicate that it can reduce PIMs use by 59% in residents and effectively lower drug-related risks.[32] At the primary health care level, general practitioners are usually responsible for the long-term medication management of older adults. Through regular follow-ups and patient education, deprescribing helps optimize polypharmacy, especially in the management of chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes, ensuring that treatment plans meet the actual needs of patients. Further research has confirmed that comprehensive intervention led by general practitioners can significantly reduce PIMs, with high patient acceptance and no increased risk of adverse events.[33] The strategy of deprescribing is also targeted at specific patient groups. For instance, older adults with type 2 diabetes often use multiple hypoglycemic drugs. However, as they age, the risk of hypoglycemia increases. At this point, it is advisable to consider simplifying the insulin regimen or discontinuing high-risk drugs such as sulfonylureas.[34] For older adults at the end of their lives, deprescribing focuses on discontinuing non-essential medications, shifting the treatment goal towards symptom control and comfort maintenance. This not only respects the wishes of patients and reduces the treatment burden but also helps improve the quality of life at the end of life.[35] The research by Abernethy et al indicates that for patients with life-limiting illness, discontinuation of statins is unlikely to lead to adverse health outcomes and may even be beneficial.[36]

Deprescribing is an important intervention measure for reducing inappropriate polypharmacy and high-risk medications. When implementing deprescribing, the principle of patient-centered care should be followed. Based on evidence-based medicine, shared decision-making between physicians and patients should be adopted to improve patient prognosis and quality of life.[37] A meta-analysis shows that deprescribing can not only significantly reduce the incidence of polypharmacy, PIMs, potential prescription omissions, and adverse drug reactions but also improve patients’ medication adherence.[38] The release of the Deprescribing proton pump inhibitors (PPIs): evidence-based clinical practice guideline provides a standardized simplification process and operational guidance for the rational use of PPI in clinical practice, assisting clinicians in determining when and how to reduce or discontinue PPI.[39] However, in clinical practice, due to the existence of multiple priorities and the need to review medication therapy regimens, most physicians lack confidence in the process of shared decision-making. It is recommended to use a variety of algorithms, criteria, and tools – such as the Drug Burden Index (DBI), Medication Appropriateness Index, Beers Criteria, and STOPP/START Criteria – to evaluate PIMs,[40,41] so as to ensure the safety and effectiveness of medication use in patients.

Among older adults with comorbidities, frail patients and those in the end stage of disease are the key targets for medication management. Frailty is a complex geriatric syndrome characterized by decreased physiological reserve, which significantly amplifies the harms of polypharmacy. A meta-analysis shows that deprescribing not only helps to improve depressive symptoms and cognitive function in frail patients but also effectively reduces their mortality, hospitalization rate, and the incidence of related complications.[42] Pharmacotherapy is a core measure for end-stage patients to control pain, manage symptoms, and optimize quality of life; however, the occurrence of PIMs is still common.[43] As the patient’s disease progresses, changes in body metabolism and gradual decline or failure of organ function lead to changes in pharmacokinetics, which in turn increase the risk of drug-related toxicity and the possibility of drug–drug interactions, ultimately causing the individual’s response to drugs to deviate from expectations.[44] In addition, when the patient’s health status deteriorates, new symptoms or complications often lead to the addition of new medications, which can easily trigger a prescribing cascade.[45,46] This not only may induce new health problems and prolong the hospital stay but also increase the burden of medical expenditures.[47] In view of the particularity of this group of patients, guidelines recommend the use of specific medication assessment tools – such as the STOPP Frail Criteria and Oncpal Tool – for deprescribing.[48] Kim S et al evaluated the medication use of patients with limited life expectancy using the STOPP Frail criteria and the Oncpal tool. The results showed that 60% of the patients had polypharmacy, and 50% of the patients had at least 1 potentially inappropriate medicine, reflecting the necessity of deprescribing in such populations.[49] In addition, physicians need to pay special attention that deprescribing is not a 1-time event but a dynamic process that requires continuous evaluation and monitoring. After each medication discontinuation, the patient’s drug and non-drug intervention regimens should be continuously monitored to ensure that they are consistent with the patient’s care goals and life expectancy.

Drug-related problems in older adults are mostly concentrated on DBI-related medications, namely sedatives and strong anticholinergic drugs. Studies have shown that the use of sedatives is significantly associated with intellectual impairment, delirium, falls, and fractures in older adults,[50,51] and long-term use of high-dose anticholinergic drugs can lead to constipation, urinary retention, cognitive decline, and increase the risk of infections, strokes, and dementia.[41] Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) is closely related to the risk of falls and delirium in older adults and is an important modifiable risk factor for these 2 common geriatric syndromes. Studies show that forevery 1-point increase in the ACB score, the risk of falls for patients increases by 8 to 18%,[52] and the risk of delirium in terminally ill patients with dementia increases by 16%.[53] Meanwhile, the ACB score of delirium patients is significantly higher than that of non-delirium patients. When ACB ≥3, the risk of delirium occurrence is 3 to 6 times that of those who do not use anticholinergic drugs.[54] Therefore, paying attention to and timely adjusting or discontinuing DBI-related medications in older adults is an an important measure to reduce adverse outcome events.

It can be seen from the keyword burst map that DBI-related medications have run through the entire process of research on deprescribing in older adults, and current research has gradually shifted from sedatives (such as benzodiazepines) to anticholinergic drugs. For benzodiazepines, existing studies have suggested that when treating anxiety and insomnia in older adults, non-pharmaceutical intervention can be given priority to reduce the use of such drugs. For patients who have been using benzodiazepines for a long time, drug withdrawal should strictly follow the principle of gradual reduction, and cognitive behavioral therapy should be combined during this process to enhance the safety and success rate of drug withdrawal.[55] With the increase of age, the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of older adults’ body are constantly changing, and people’s medical goals and priorities may also change.[56] How to balance the benefits of disease treatment and the risks of medication use has become a dynamic challenge in clinical practice.

Domestic and foreign studies have proved that multidisciplinary medication therapy plans led by clinical pharmacists have significant advantages in improving the treatment outcomes of patients with polypharmacy. A study by Ailabouni et al shows that 6 months after the implementation of pharmacist-led deprescribing, the DBI score, depression score, frailty index score, number of falls, and risk of adverse reactions in older adults were significantly reduced.[57] A study by Bryant et al shows that community pharmacists’ participation in clinical medication review and collaboration with general practitioners can significantly improve patients’ Medication Appropriateness Index.[58] The multidisciplinary joint diagnosis and treatment clinics established in many hospitals in China (which include clinical pharmacists) provide comprehensive medication assessment and treatment plans for older adults with polypharmacy, and this practice has also been widely recognized in the industry.[59]

5. Strengths and limitations

This study used bibliometric methods to conduct a visual analysis of literature related to deprescribing in older adults, systematically presenting the research status and development trends in this field, which can provide references for subsequent research and practice.

The limitations of the study are as follows: first, the database for literature retrieval is limited to Web of Science, and the subject screening process involves subjective judgment, which may lead to the omission or misclassification of some literature. Second, the study results only include English literature, which may exclude research from some non-English-speaking countries and limit the global generalizability of the results.

6. Conclusion

Deprescribing has important clinical application value in improving medication safety and clinical outcomes for older adults. At present, research in this field is mainly concentrated in developed countries such as the United States and Australia, where relatively rich research achievements have been accumulated and professional talent teams have been established. In contrast, although developing countries such as China have actively carried out relevant explorations, there is still a need for continuous deepening in terms of research systems and clinical promotion.

From the perspective of research hotspots and frontier trends, drug-related problems in older adults and multidisciplinary collaborative medication therapy plans have become the core focus of the academic community, which are worthy of further in-depth exploration. In the future, promoting the balanced development of this field globally and strengthening the translation of evidence into clinical practice will be important development directions.
