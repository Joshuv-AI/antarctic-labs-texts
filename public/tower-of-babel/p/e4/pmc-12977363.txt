Cancer Survivorship Practices Among Cancer Care Providers and Cancer Centers in Brazil: Results From a National Survey
Authors: Cibele Barbosa Carroll, Juliana Ribeiro de Carvalho, Ana Luiza Cardona Miranda Machado, Kim Dittus, Priyanka Pophali, Noelle K. LoConte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSECancer survivorship evolved over the past few years in developed countries although in developing countries, guidelines and evidence-based recommendations on survivorship care are still lacking. We sought to gather information on cancer survivorship practices in Brazil to identify assets and areas for improvement.METHODSTwo surveys were developed regarding survivorship knowledge and the provision of services. Brazilian providers (physicians and nurses) and cancer center leaders received the surveys' invitation via WhatsApp groups of Brazilian oncology professionals. In addition, they were informed to share the study information with their networks (snowball sampling). The link to the surveys was advertised at Brazilian oncology conferences between August and December 2024. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the results.RESULTSBetween August 13 and December 17, 2024, 194 unique providers and 28 cancer center leaders responded to the online survey. Among providers, 70% considered a cancer survivor a patient who completed cancer treatment and has no evidence of disease. The majority (60%) reported never consulting a survivorship guideline. The most common tool used to support survivors was a treatment summary (52.1%). Among cancer center leaders, 32.1% reported that their institutions have survivorship programs. The most common services offered to cancer survivors include medical follow-up, nutrition, and psychology services (100%), whereas reproductive medicine and sexual therapy were offered by 7.7% of cancer centers. Although 28.6% of the cancer center leaders reported that nurses specialized in oncology participate in follow-up care of cancer survivors who completed curative treatment, 14.9% of providers responded that these nurses are involved in survivorship follow-up.CONCLUSIONCancer care providers and cancer centers from Brazil can benefit from culturally tailored survivorship guidelines to better address cancer survivors' needs.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer survivorship care has evolved over the years, and survivorship is acknowledged as a specific phase of the cancer care continuum.1,2 Although there are guidelines and accreditation requirements regarding survivorship care in developed countries,1,3-5 the subject still needs attention in developing countries.1,4,6

With a population of nearly 212 million people,7 Brazil has five geographical regions with diverse cancer profiles and socioeconomic characteristics. Health care is provided through a tax-funded Universal Health System (UHS) that delivers care for approximately 75% of its population and through supplemental health care with expenses covered by health insurance, used by roughly 25% of the population.8 The UHS offers cancer treatment through institutions of public and philanthropic (ie, nonprofit) administrations, certified by the federal government as the Center of High Complexity in Oncology (Centro de Alta Complexidade em Oncologia—CACON), the Unity of High Complexity in Oncology (Unidade de Alta Complexidade em Oncologia—UNACON), or General Hospital with Cancer Surgery.8 In the UHS, health care is organized as integrated networks, and researchers reported on care fragmentation and a lack of coordination between specialized and primary care levels,9 which can be detrimental for cancer survivors with unmet needs after cancer treatment.

Brazil was expected to have 704,000 new cases of cancer per year from 2023 to 2025,10 and national statistics on cancer survivors' numbers is lacking. The National Policy for Cancer Prevention and Control implemented in December 2023 (Law No. 14758) aims to ensure access to comprehensive cancer care, reduce cancer incidence and mortality, improve quality of life, and reduce disability for cancer survivors.11 However, cancer survivorship is not well-defined in that law.

Given the lack of policy recommendations and culturally tailored guidelines (ie, guideline on survivorship care adapted for the Brazilian context), survivorship-related research from Brazil showed that survivors of several types of cancer experience various consequences of cancer treatment, particularly related to psychological and socioeconomic aspects of life.12,13 Researchers have emphasized the need for having structured interdisciplinary survivorship care—organized comprehensive care, with multiprofessional teams to address physical, emotional, and socioeconomic effects of cancer and its treatments.12,13

Regarding Brazilian providers, a study with members of the Brazilian Society of Medical Oncology, assessing the availability of Adolescent and Young Adults cancer programs, reported that only 33% of surveyed physicians had access to a survivorship program (SP).14 Research comparing cancer survivors and adults without a history of cancer showed that adherence to healthy habits (physical activity, healthy body weight, and smoke cessation) was similar between the two groups.15 However, cancer survivors consumed nearly 3 times more fruits and vegetables,15 suggesting that providers emphasize more nutrition than other healthy habits after cancer treatment.

Discussing survivorship care in Brazil is important to improve care beyond cancer treatment. This study was designed to gather information about cancer survivorship practices in Brazil. To our knowledge, this is the first national assessment of Brazilian providers and cancer centers' leadership. The insights of these two groups allow better understanding of the scenario of survivorship care in the country.

METHODS

Setting

The research team is composed of medical oncologists, surgical oncologists, and internal medicine physicians, based in Brazil and the United States. The cross-sectional study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Wisconsin Madison and approved by the Comitê de Ética em Pesquisa—Comissão Nacional de Ética em Pesquisa (CEP/CONEP) in Brazil (CAAE: 75222223.1.1001.5274). Researchers located in Brazil and the United States met virtually and regularly to design the surveys and discuss barriers to survey responses.

Recruitment

Brazilian providers (physicians and nurses) and cancer center leadership (hospital directors, medical directors, technical directors, and nonprovider members of the administration) received an anonymous survey link via closed groups of oncology professionals on WhatsApp. These groups have administrators, are convened through invitation, and are used to exchange information on a variety of topics16 (ie, conferences, job postings, research surveys, etc). WhatsApp uses end-to-end encrypted messages16 and is used by various socioeconomic groups from all geographic regions.17 The providers who received the survey link were informed to share the study information with their professional networks, using a snowball sampling approach.18 In addition, recruitment occurred at Brazilian oncology conferences. To increase recruitment of cancer center leaders, a research coordinator in Brazil communicated with the diverse services using publicly available contact information (e-mail and phone number of the institutions).

Assessments

Two surveys were created based on the published literature about providers' assessments on cancer survivorship.19-21 The surveys were built as one project on the Global Heath instance of the University of Wisconsin-Madison School of Medicine and Public Health Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).22,23 REDCap was chosen because it has an intuitive interface for data entry and allows tracking data manipulation and automated data export to common statistical packages.22,23 All the participant facing materials were created in English and then translated into Portuguese by the bilingual research team members (C.B.C., J.R.d.C., A.L.C.M.M.). Thus, participants could change the language between Portuguese and English. In both surveys, the number of questions varied according to the respondents' answers and branching logic.

Provider Survey

The provider survey had up to 46 questions directed to physicians and nurses who see cancer survivors. The questions assessed participants' characteristics, knowledge, and practices regarding survivorship care. To participate, providers self-identified as a physician or a nurse who provides care for patients with cancer in Brazil. Internet protocol addresses were used to check for duplicates.

Cancer Center Survey

The cancer center survey had up to 64 questions directed to cancer centers' leadership. The questions assessed cancer centers' characteristics, availability, and provision of survivorship-related services. Cancer center leaders were eligible to participate if they worked in the leadership of facilities that treat patients with cancer. Only one response per cancer center was accepted. If the cancer center submitted more than one survey, researchers accepted the one with the largest number of responses to the multiple-choice questions.

Participants were excluded if unwilling to participate in the surveys or if selected “prefer not to answer” and/or skipped all the questions regarding survivorship. The surveys were pilot tested with 10 Brazilian physicians who provided feedback on branching logic functioning, grammar, and comprehension. Appropriate changes were made accordingly.

Outcomes

The main outcome of the project was to generate a report of the current state of survivorship in Brazil. The research team located in Brazil ensured participation of a diverse population of providers and cancer centers leadership based on job category, sex, race, and geographic location by purposefully disseminating the study among WhatsApp groups of providers with lower responses.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to compile the survey results. Missing data were excluded from the analysis of each variable, and the percentage of responses were calculated considering the total number of valid responses to each question as the denominator. The responses to the open-ended questions were organized using the software NVivo v.14 and analyzed verbatim when provided in English. When provided in Portuguese, the responses were translated into English before the analysis. Codes were assigned using an inductive approach. The bilingual researchers coded the data independently and met to reach consensus.

RESULTS

Between August 13 and December 17, 2024, online surveys were open for data collection. Survey response rate cannot be calculated because of the recruitment methods (ie, survey link WhatsApp distribution, snowball sampling, and QR code presentation in Brazilian conferences).

Provider Survey

One hundred ninety-four providers met the inclusion criteria, and 57.7% (112 of 194) of them were in the southeast region, 9.3% (18 of 194) in the northeast, 4.6% (9 of 194) in the south, 2.1% (4 of 194) in the north, and 1.5% (3 of 194) in the midwest (Fig 1). Regarding roles, 65% (126 of 194) were physicians, 14.9% (29 of 194) were nurses, and 20.1% (39 of 194) did not report their roles. Providers' specialties are presented in Appendix Figure A1. Attending health care professionals represented 64.4% (125 of 194), and 7.7% (15 of 194) were trainees. Cisgender women represented 52.6% (102 of 194), other sex represented 1% (2 of 194), and 20.6% (40 of 194) was missing. Nearly 29% (56 of 194) worked in academic institutions; 48.9% (95 of 194) reported working at institutions of public administration, followed by 44.3% (86 of 194) in private institutions and 24.2% (47 of 194) in philanthropic institutions. A total of 59.3% (115 of 194) worked at government-certified institutions to treat cancer within the UHS (ie, treat patients with cancer without health insurance in a CACON, UNACON, or general hospital with cancer surgery). The mean age of providers was 42.3 (±9.9) years. Approximately 61% (118 of 194) reported patients with cancer diagnosis in their practices to wait more than 1 month to initiate cancer treatment.

Regarding the survivor definition, 70% (136 of 194) considered a patient a survivor if they completed cancer treatment and had no evidence of disease, whereas 27.3% (53 of 194) considered all individuals affected by cancer to be survivors. The majority (60%, 116 of 194) never consulted survivorship guidelines. Among providers who consulted guidelines, 42% (32 of 76) mentioned the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), 11.8% (9 of 76) ASCO, and 10.5% (8 of 76) the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines. Providers ranked six aspects of survivorship in order that they are discussed with patients treated with curative intent, with 1 being the most discussed aspect. Follow-up to detect disease recurrence or progression was the most discussed, whereas screening for new primary tumors was the least (Fig 2). After completion of treatment, cancer treatment was always discussed by 73.2% of providers, followed by management of cancer-related symptoms, by 71.3% (Fig 3). Conversely, financial problems were always discussed by only 8.9% of providers.

All providers had the opportunity to leave a comment about their experience with cancer survivorship care. Write-in answers of 27 providers were analyzed. The main themes from the qualitative data were Weaknesses (22 quotes) and Opportunities for improvement (six quotes). Among weaknesses, providers reported depreciation and need for a multidisciplinary team (four quotes), challenges to refer patients to PCPs (three quotes), having limited contact with survivors (three quotes), lack of guidance on survivorship care (two quotes), and discussing survivorship per patients' demand (two quotes). Interest in learning about survivorship (three quotes) and acknowledgment that survivorship care is needed (one quote) were opportunities for improvement.

Cancer Center Survey

There were 34 respondents to the cancer center leadership's survey. Three cancer centers provided more than one survey. After applying the exclusion criterion mentioned above, 28 cancer centers were included in the analysis. One cancer center (3.6%) received patients 18 years and younger exclusively, 35.7% (10 of 28) received patients 18 years and younger to 65 years and older, and 60.7% (17 of 28) received adult patients from various age groups. Regarding the type of administration, 25% (7 of 28) of cancer centers were public, 32% (9 of 28) were private, and 28.6% (8 of 28) were philanthropic. Appendix Figure A2 presents cancer centers' characteristics.

Electronic medical records (EMR) were used by 64.3% (18 of 28) of cancer centers to coordinate survivors' care, followed by phone calls by 28.6% (8 of 28) and patient navigation by 25% (7 of 28). Patient portals (PP) and survivorship care plans (SCPs) were reported by 7.1% (2 of 28) each. The functionalities available in the PP included appointment scheduling for one cancer center, whereas the other also offered calendar of medical events, channel for message exchange, and test results. Risk stratification to organize survivors' follow-up was used by 17.9% (5 of 28) of cancer centers.

Among the cancer centers' leadership, 32.1% (9 of 28) responded that they had SPs. One SP was overseen by a medical oncologist only. All the others had an oversight of a group of professionals including medical oncologists or hematologists for 77.8% (7 of 9), nurses specialized in oncology for 77.8% (7 of 9), surgical oncologists for 44.4% (4 of 9), social workers for 33.3% (3 of 9), and cancer center directors for 22.2% (2 of 9). Most cancer centers (78.5%—22 of 28) reported having services for family members and caregivers, including educational materials (60.7%—17 of 28), psychology (39.3%, 11 of 28), support groups (35.7%—10 of 28), spiritual support (28.6%—8 of 28), and instructions on caregiving (25%—7 of 28). The medical oncologist or hematologist was the professional who most often addressed family members and caregivers in outpatient clinics (57.1%—16 of 28), followed by social workers (50%—14 of 28) and psychologists (46.4%, 13 of 28).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this study is the first national assessment of Brazilian oncology providers and cancer center leadership on the current state of cancer survivorship care. Cancer survivorship is a growing field in Brazil, and learning about health care providers and cancer centers' practices is crucial to identify assets and gap areas that need further attention. The study was strengthened by assessing nurses and physicians from multiple specialties and cancer centers' leadership from multiple Brazilian states; it addresses the need for a comprehensive understanding of the survivorship scenario in the country and serves as a benchmark for future comparison in Brazil and other Latin American countries.

Most providers considered a survivor an individual who completed cancer treatment and had no evidence of disease and focused on cancer-related symptoms, cancer treatment, and follow-up to detect disease recurrence or progression when talking with their patients. Findings highlight the need to increase awareness of Brazilian cancer providers on the various domains of survivorship care.24 Ideally, providers would discuss survivors' risk of new primary cancers, lifestyle habits, and chronic conditions, in addition to managing physical and psychosocial effects of cancer, as recommended by the Institute of Medicine,2 NCCN,5 and ESMO.25 It is important to acknowledge that the survivorship domains are inter-related24 and some interventions can improve multiple aspects of survivors' life. For example, physical activity is a health promotion intervention that can improve sleep disturbances26 and enhance both physical and mental functioning26,27 among survivors of various types of cancer.

Fewer participants reported that PCPs or family medicine specialists participate in cancer survivors' care. In addition, 40% of cancer center leadership felt that PCPs are not up to date with the cancer treatment survivors received. PCPs' participation in survivorship care is acknowledged as essential, whether as the leading provider or in a shared care model, because of the increasing number of cancer survivors, complex interplay of previous comorbidities with conditions secondary to cancer treatments, and progressive shortage of oncologists.28,29 PCPs can play an important role in managing survivors' health maintenance, comorbidities, and long-term and late effects of cancer treatments.28,29 In the United States, PCP participation in survivors' care includes challenges such as lack of training on managing cancer-related effects30 and survivors' preferences for being followed by oncology specialists.31 Increasing oncology specialists' awareness of PCP roles in survivors' follow-up and training PCPs on post-treatment cancer survivors' needs are areas for improvement in Brazil. Brazilian researchers and cancer centers could use frameworks proposed in developing countries,32 in which the health care systems’ organizational structure shares similarities with the Brazilian health system.33 Integrating PCPs into the care of patients with cancer, as proposed by an academic institution from Colombia,32 can be a first step.

While 28.6% of the cancer center leaders reported that nurses specialized in oncology participated in the follow-up of cancer survivors after curative treatment, only 14.9% of providers responded that nurses specialized in oncology participated in survivors' follow-up. These findings contrast with a study conducted among Commission on Cancer (CoC)-accredited facilities, in which SP team members include nurses as reported by 87% of facilities, advanced practice providers (nurse practitioner, physician assistant—roles not available in Brazil) as reported by 65.6%, among other healthcare providers.34 In high-income countries, nurses specialized in oncology play a central role in supporting survivors in the transition of care after curative treatment as they can assess survivors' needs, provide SCPs, conduct patient education on self-management, and promote healthy habits.35 Increasing nurses specialized in oncology's role in the transition after treatment and follow-up of cancer survivors is an area for improvement in Brazil, similar to other developing countries.33

Treatment summary (TS) was the most used tool to support survivors both among providers and cancer center leaders, and utilization of SCPs was reported by 11.3% of providers. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in a private Brazilian cancer center showed that the provision of TS and SCP improved self-efficacy and physical well-being of breast cancer survivors over time.36 Approximately 75% of women in the RCT had college degree,36 contrasting with 21.3% of women with college degree across the country in 2022.37 The utility of SCPs in supporting survivors of other types of cancer, seen at the UHS, without a college degree is yet to be determined. The utilization of EMR, PP, and telehealth as tools to support survivors is an opportunity for survivors of vulnerable groups to get information about their health38,39 and care.40

In Brazil, cancer centers of all types of administration (public, private, or philanthropic) had medical follow-up, nutrition, and psychology services available for cancer survivors. Other supportive services are available to survivors at lower rates compared with US cancer centers.21,34 There is a substantial gap in the provision of reproductive medicine and sexual therapy. Although the gap areas are similar to those identified in international studies,21,34 in the United States, more than 50% of SPs from CoC-accredited facilities provide sexual health and fertility services.34

Genetic counseling, integrative medicine, nurse and lay navigation, and program for smoke cessation were more often available in cancer centers with private administration, suggesting that these centers offer more personalized practices. Centers with public administration more often had research/clinical trials and psychiatry services. Philanthropic cancer centers more frequently offered spiritual support, rehabilitation, and occupational therapies, which is partially explained by the historical connections with charity and religious entities.41 These differences underscore the importance of federal policies in regulating cancer care provision. To receive government certification to treat cancer, centers must offer a list of services for inpatient and outpatient care that includes psychology, social workers, nutrition, physical therapy, ostomy care, dentistry, psychiatry, and renal replacement therapy, in addition to oncology-specific services.42 Rehabilitation services must match the complexity of oncologic care provided by the cancer center.42 Provision of navigation services for patients with cancer diagnosis was introduced in the UHS as a national policy in December 202311 and will likely increase the availability of these services in public cancer centers in future assessments.

Study limitations that restrict generalizability of study findings include WhatsApp survey distribution and snowball sampling, which might have introduced selection bias. Similar to other studies with volunteer participants, results might have been affected by nonresponse bias. A survey response rate cannot be calculated because of the recruitment methods. Nearly 30% of participants were surgical oncologists, which might have contributed to the limited awareness of survivorship. However, in the Brazilian UHS, surgical specialists are often responsible for conducting follow-up of cured patients, allowing medical oncologists to manage patients on systemic treatments. Finally, participants were centralized in the southeast region, aligning with previous research on the distribution of cancer centers in Brazil.8

In conclusion, cancer care providers and cancer centers from Brazil can benefit from culturally tailored survivorship guidelines to better address cancer survivors' needs. Introducing survivorship-related recommendations in federal policies can expedite survivorship care adoption at the national level and increase awareness of survivorship among providers and cancer centers in the UHS, where most Brazilians receives care. Multispecialty and multidisciplinary teams, including nurses specialized in oncology and PCP, could be more involved in the follow-up of survivors to improve health promotion and the management of chronic conditions that emerge after treatment.
