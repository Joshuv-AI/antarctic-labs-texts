Isolated funicular hydrocele in an infant mimicking inguinoscrotal hernia: diagnostic challenges and surgical lessons in a low-resource setting: a case report
Authors: Mathayo Shadrack, Ahmed Ali Shabhay, Zaitun Bokhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Funicular hydrocele is a congenital spermatic cord hydrocele caused by the partial persistence of the processus vaginalis. In infants, it may mimic an inguinal hernia clinically and radiologically, creating diagnostic uncertainty. Differentiating these conditions is important because the anatomical subtype determines surgical management.Case presentation:A 6-month-old male infant presented with a painless left inguinoscrotal swelling of 3 months’ duration. Examination revealed a non-tender, non-reducible cystic swelling extending from the inguinal canal into the upper scrotum. The lesion did not enlarge with crying or straining. Faint transillumination was appreciated near the upper scrotal component, maintaining hydrocele within the differential diagnosis. Ultrasound suggested an inguinoscrotal hernia. Because of persistent swelling and the inability to confidently exclude hernia, surgical exploration was performed. Intraoperatively, a cystic fluid-filled sac was identified along the spermatic cord, extending from the internal ring to the upper testis without communication with the tunica vaginalis, confirming a true funicular hydrocele. Hydrocelectomy with high ligation of the patent processus vaginalis was performed successfully. Recovery was uneventful, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up.Discussion:Funicular hydrocele may closely resemble an inguinal hernia and remains difficult to distinguish clinically in some infants. Although ultrasound improves diagnostic accuracy, interpretation remains operator dependent. Accurate intraoperative identification is important because management differs from encysted hydrocele, and incomplete repair may predispose to recurrence.Conclusion:This case highlights the diagnostic overlap between funicular hydrocele and inguinal hernia, and it reinforces the importance of careful clinical assessment and intraoperative anatomical recognition for appropriate management.

Introduction

Congenital hydroceles arise from incomplete obliteration of the processus vaginalis (PV) and are common during infancy. Funicular hydrocele represents a spermatic cord hydrocele in which the distal PV near the testis is closed, while the proximal segment remains partially patent, allowing fluid accumulation along the cord without surrounding the testis itself[1,2]. Clinically, it may mimic an inguinal hernia because both conditions present as inguinoscrotal swellings.

Distinguishing funicular hydrocele from inguinal hernia is important because the anatomical subtype influences surgical management and recurrence prevention[3]. Although ultrasonography is useful, interpretation may be operator dependent, and some lesions remain difficult to classify preoperatively[4]. This report describes an infant with funicular hydrocele initially suspected to be an inguinal hernia and highlights important diagnostic and surgical considerations.

Case presentation

A 6-month-old male infant presented with a 3-month history of painless left inguinoscrotal swelling. There were no vomiting, abdominal distension, fever, irritability, or bowel obstruction symptoms. Growth and development were normal.

Physical examination demonstrated a 2.5 × 3 cm cystic swelling predominantly occupying the left inguinal canal, with slight extension into the upper hemiscrotum (Fig. 1A). The swelling was non-tender and irreducible, and it did not increase with crying or straining. Faint transillumination was appreciated near the upper scrotal component. The ipsilateral testis was separately palpable.

Ultrasonography suggested a left inguinoscrotal hernia. Despite clinical suspicion of a cystic lesion, persistent swelling and the inability to confidently exclude a hernia justified surgical exploration.

Under general anesthesia, a left inguinal crease incision was performed. Exploration revealed a fluid-filled cystic sac extending along the spermatic cord from the internal ring toward the upper pole of the testis (Fig. 1B).

The distal sac did not communicate with the tunica vaginalis, confirming a funicular hydrocele (Fig. 2A and 2B). Clear, straw-colored fluid was aspirated. The sac was carefully dissected from the spermatic cord structures and excised completely. High ligation of the patent proximal processus vaginalis was performed at the internal ring (Fig. 3A and B). Hemostasis was secured, and the wound was closed in anatomical layers. The infant recovered uneventfully and resumed feeding within 24 hours. He was discharged on postoperative day one. Follow-up at 2 weeks and 1 month showed normal wound healing without recurrent swelling.

Discussion

Funicular hydrocele results from partial obliteration of the processus vaginalis and represents a recognized but less common spermatic cord hydrocele subtype[1,5]. Because the lesion occupies the inguinal canal and upper scrotum, it may clinically resemble an inguinal hernia[6].

In this patient, the lesion was non-reducible and showed faint transillumination, findings favoring a cystic pathology rather than a classic hernia. However, because the swelling occupied the inguinal canal and ultrasonography suggested a hernia, definitive differentiation remained difficult before surgery. This case, therefore, reinforces the importance of integrating clinical findings with imaging rather than relying solely on ultrasonography.

Unlike simple, non-communicating hydroceles that may resolve spontaneously, funicular hydroceles with proximal patency may persist and potentially predispose to future hernia formation. Surgical exploration was, therefore, considered appropriate in this infant because persistent processus vaginalis pathology could not be excluded confidently[7].

Management differs according to hydrocele subtype. Encysted hydroceles require cyst excision alone, whereas funicular hydroceles require both excision and high ligation of the patent proximal processus vaginalis. Failure to recognize this anatomy may increase the recurrence risk[8].

Previous pediatric studies have demonstrated frequent diagnostic confusion between spermatic cord hydroceles and inguinal hernia. Patel et al reported that many infants with spermatic cord hydroceles were initially diagnosed clinically as hernias[7]. Yong et al highlighted limitations of ultrasound interpretation in low-resource settings[8]. Our findings align with these reports.

The main limitation of this report is the short follow-up duration, which limits the long-term assessment for recurrence.

Conclusion

Funicular hydrocele should remain an important differential diagnosis in infants presenting with persistent inguinoscrotal swelling. Careful clinical examination remains fundamental, while ultrasound findings should be interpreted cautiously because of potential operator dependency. Accurate intraoperative anatomical recognition is essential to guide appropriate repair and prevent recurrence.
