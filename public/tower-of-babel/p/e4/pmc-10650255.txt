Prevalence and risk factors associated with Toxocara canis in dogs and humans in Egypt: A comparative approach
Authors: Asmaa Gaber Mubarak, Eman Sayed Mohammed, Haitham Elaadli, Hind Alzaylaee, Rabab S Hamad, Walaa A Elkholy, Asmaa Gahlan Youseef
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background: Dogs are the most popular pet animals worldwide, and their frequent and close contact with humans poses an increased risk of zoonotic parasite transmission. Toxocara canis infection is a highly pervasive and economically significant zoonotic infection transmitted by dogs worldwide, commonly in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly in developing countries. Objectives: This study evaluates the epidemiological profile and associated risk factors of T. canis exposure among humans and T. canis infection in domestic dogs in two climatically different governorates in Egypt. Methods: Faecal samples from 360 domiciled dogs were examined using the flotation technique to detect T. canis eggs. In addition, 276 human serum samples were evaluated by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay over a period of 10 months from May 2021 to February 2022 in the Alexandria and Qena Governorates, Egypt. Results: Shedding of T. canis was identified in 33.33% (120/360) of dogs and the overall seroprevalence in the human population was 20.65% (57/276). Lower Egypt, represented by the Alexandria Governorate, had higher canine infection (39.47%) and human seropositivity (29.87%) rates than those of Upper Egypt, represented by Qena Governorate (26.47% and 9.02% in dogs and humans, respectively). Statistical analysis of the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants revealed that handwashing, washing of vegetables and fruits and sex were associated with human T. canis exposure. Conclusion: The prevalence rates of confirmed T. canis infection in the Egyptian dogs population and the associated human seropositivity rates reflect its importance as a public health concern and support the call to increase public awareness of this issue. The risk factors identified in this study can contribute to the development of more effective control and prevention strategies.Keywords: domiciled dogs; ELISA; humans, Egypt; risk factors; T. canis

INTRODUCTION

Toxocariasis is a zoonotic disease caused by nematode parasites of the genus Toxocara, which are distributed worldwide (Ma et al., 2018). A systematic review and meta‐analysis revealed that 19% of the global human population has specific anti‐Toxocara species antibodies, indicating previous exposure to toxocariasis (Rostami et al., 2019), and it is considered one of most neglected parasitic infections of public health concern (Ma et al., 2018; Overgaauw & van Knapen, 2013).

Of all domesticated animals, humans have the most intimate relationship with dogs and, worldwide, they are considered the most popular pet species (Freedman & Wayne, 2017). Toxocara canis is considered the main cause of human toxocariasis with a global seroprevalence of 11.1% in dogs (Rostami et al., 2020). Infection occurs through the ingestion of embryonated eggs containing third‐stage (L3) larvae, or through transplacental or transmammary transmission of larvae. Adult female helminths live in the small intestines of dogs and produce thousands of eggs, which are shed in the faeces over a period of 2–5 weeks. Once in the environment, the eggs embryonate and become infective (Mughini‐Gras et al., 2016; Rezaiemanesh et al., 2019).

In humans, the estimated global seroprevalence of toxocariasis is 19.0% but exposure rates vary between and within countries (Abdelkareem et al., 2022). Humans become infected through the accidental ingestion of embryonated T. canis eggs present in contaminated soil, untreated water, unwashed fruits or vegetables or contact with contaminated dog hair (Holland, 2015). Consumption of larvae in raw or poorly cooked meat of infected paratenic hosts, such as rabbits, sheep and chickens, can also lead to human infections (Zibaei et al., 2016). Several factors have been associated with higher rates of T. canis infection, including close human contact with dogs and soil, consumption of raw meat, younger age and higher humidity and precipitation (Rostami et al., 2019). Children are particularly susceptible to infection due to their intimate contact with dogs and their frequent exposure to contaminated soil in public parks, yards, playgrounds and outdoor sandpits (Delai et al., 2021).

Most human T. canis infections are asymptomatic and go unnoticed. Nevertheless, larvae present internally may migrate to various organs, such as the lungs, liver, heart, muscles, eyes and brain, resulting in serious local reactions (Liu et al., 2018). Four clinical syndromes have been identified: visceral larva migrans, ocular larva migrans, covert toxocariasis and neurotoxocariasis (Rezaiemanesh et al., 2019). The clinical presentation is dependent on the organs involved and encompasses a variety of clinical signs, such as dyspnea, cough, chest discomfort, asthma, pruritus and gastrointestinal disorders (Ma et al., 2018).

Humans constitute a paratenic host in which maturation of larvae to the adult form is absent, thus microscopic stool examination has no diagnostic value. As tissue biopsy is considered an invasive procedure, the diagnosis of human toxocariasis is mainly based on exposure history and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for detecting IgG‐specific antibodies against T. canis larval excretory–secretory antigens (TES Ag). Test kits are widely available and recommended for use by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Zhan et al., 2015).

Over recent years, Egypt has seen a significant increase in the number of dog breeders, as well as in the number of stray dogs roaming freely in public places, which has increased the risk of zoonotic parasitic infections. Several studies have investigated the prevalence of T. canis in eggs in domestic dog faeces (Abdel Aziz et al., 2019; Arafa et al., 2014; El‐Tras et al., 2011; Radwan et al., 2009) with incidences of 53.4%, 10.7%, 40% and 53.04%, respectively. Previous studies have investigated the role of dogs in transmitting zoonotic intestinal parasites to humans with special reference to T. canis (Awadallah & Salem, 2015); however, data on the zoonotic link between T. canis and its transmission to humans in Egypt are scarce. Thus, our study aimed to determine the prevalence of T. canis infection in dogs and of exposure in their owners. The impact of environmental conditions and sociodemographic factors on the prevalence and zoonotic transmission was also addressed through a comparative study between Lower and Upper Egypt, represented by the Alexandria and Qena Governorates, respectively.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study area

This study was conducted in two governorates, namely Alexandria, which covers a wide territory in the north of Egypt (Lower Egypt) and Qena, which is located in the south of Egypt (Upper Egypt) (Figure 1). Alexandria Governorate is located approximately 219 km from Cairo (31° 12′20″N, 29° 55′28″E) and has an average temperature range of 17–26°C. Conversely, Qena Governorate is located approximately 576 km from Cairo (26°10′12″N, 32° 43′38″E) and has an average temperature range of 21–38°C. Furthermore, Alexandria is one of the wettest areas of Egypt with an average annual precipitation of about 200 mm unlike Qena (0.55 mm), compared with the national annual average of 80 mm.

Geographic location of Alexandria and Qena Governorates of Egypt.

Study design and sample size

This study adopted a cross‐sectional seroepidemiological approach to evaluate the prevalence of T. canis in dogs and seropositivity rate in the corresponding human populations in the Egyptian Governorates of Alexandria and Qena from May 2021 to February 2022.

Canine faecal sampling

A total of 360 fresh dog faecal samples were collected, 190 from Alexandria Governorate and 170 from Qena Governorate. The samples were taken with the permission of the dog owners, who also confirmed their dogs’ age, sex and history of anthelmintic therapy. Body condition and the presence of diarrhoea, pruritus in the perianal region, pale mucous membrane and abdominal pain were also recorded.

The sample size was determined using Epi info with 1‐alpha of 95%, power of 80. Based on an estimate of 14,300 total number of domiciled dogs in Alexandria Governorate as reported by the Veterinary Medicine Directorate of Alexandria reported, a prevalence rate of 51.23% (Biu et al., 2012), and an odds ratio of 2.5, the minimum number of dogs needed for the study was 182 dogs. In Qena Governorate, a total number of dogs was of 10,700 was assumed as per the Veterinary Medicine Directorate of Qena stratified, and according to a previous prevalence rate of 27.10% (Abdelkareem et al., 2022) and an odds ratio of 2.6, the minimum number needed was 168 dogs.

Microscopical examination of dogs’ faecal samples

The faecal samples were assessed using the flotation technique. A 50‐g sample of faecal matter was placed in a 250 mL glass beaker and topped with 0.95% sodium chloride solution to a final volume of 200 mL. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized with a glass stick and was left to stand for 5 min before being filtered to eliminate large particles. The mixture was poured through a fine‐mesh tea strainer and transferred to a pre‐numbered 15‐mL conical tube, which was then centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min. An additional 0.95% sodium chloride solution was pipetted into each tube to create a positive meniscus onto which a coverslip was placed. After 5 min, the coverslip was removed and placed on a pre‐numbered slide for examination under a light microscope at 40× magnification (Szell et al., 2014). The diagnosis of T. canis infection was confirmed by the presence of dark, brown‐coloured eggs with thick pitted shells (Zajac & Conboy, 2012).

Human serum sampling

Samples were collected from 276 apparently healthy individuals, 154 from Alexandria Governorate with a mean age of 7.5 ± 0.76 years and 122 from Qena Governorate with a mean age of 7.5 ± 0.64 years, including 176 dog owners. Human participants were sampled following informed consent and the completion of an epidemiological questionnaire, which collected information on gender, age, place of residence, history of contact with dogs and hygiene and dietary habits, such as handwashing, washing of vegetables and fruits and consumption of undercooked meat.

The sample size was determined using Epi info with 1‐alpha of 95% and power of 80. Based on a prevalence rate of 42.50% from a previous study (Abbas et al., 2020), the minimum number needed for our study in Alexandria Governorate was 154 participants with odds ratio of 2.5. On the other hand, in Qena Governorate the minimum number needed was 112 participants with odds ratio of 2.94.

Peripheral venous blood samples (2 mL) were collected in tubes without anticoagulant, maintained at room temperature to allow clotting and then centrifuged at 800 g for 5 min. The serum was separated and kept at −20°C until processing.

Detection of anti‐Toxocara canis IgG antibodies

Anti‐T. canis IgG antibodies were detected using a commercial ELISA kit (DEIA05740, Creative Diagnostics), which had a sensitivity and specificity of 87.5% and 93.3%, respectively. Both negative and positive controls were provided in the kit, and the test was performed following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 100‐μL diluted test sample was added to pre‐coated wells and incubated at room temperature (15–25°C) for 10 min. The wells were washed three times with a diluted wash buffer and then incubated with 100‐μL enzyme conjugate at room temperature for 5 min. The chromogen was added at 100 μL to every well and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Two drops of the Stop Solution were added and mixed by tapping the strip holder. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm/620 nm using an automatic microplate reader. Samples were considered positive based on an absorbance reading ≥0.3 OD units and negative if the absorbance reading <0.3 OD units. Samples with inconclusive results were retested.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analysed using SPSS version 22. The Pearson χ 2 test was performed at 5% significance to determine the association between the qualitative categorized variables. The odds ratio and 95% confidence interval (CI) were stratified to determine the dichotomous risk factors. The Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio estimate was performed to preclude the confounding risk factors. The Mantel–Haenszel CI and binary logistic regression were used to identify the non‐dichotomous risk factors. Receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) curve values were used to determine the significant risk factors in each governorate.

Prevalence of anti‐T. canis IgG antibodies in humans and risk factors assessment

This study included 276 participants, including 176 dog owners, with 154 living in Alexandria (Lower Egypt) and 122 in Qena (Upper Egypt). A total of 57 (20.65%) samples were seropositive for anti‐T. canis antibodies, with 46/154 (29.87%) and 11/122 (9.02%) in Alexandria and Qena Governorates, respectively. The seropositive rate in Alexandria was significantly higher than that in Qena (χ 2 = 18.065; p = 0.000) (Table 1).

The associations between T. canis infection and sociodemographic characteristics of the examined human populations are summarized in Table 3. The overall seropositivity rate of female participants (37.64%) was higher than that of male participants (20.28%). Interestingly, statistical analysis revealed a significantly higher infection rate in females in Alexandria than in Qena (p = 0.000, 95% CI: 0.04–0.312), whereas male seropositivity was comparable between the two regions (p = 0.196, 95% CI: 0.187–1.426). When stratified by age, the highest seroprevalence rate was found among participants aged 4–15 years (39.65% and 14.29%) in both Alexandria and Qena. Sequentially, the exposure rate in Alexandria decreased with age, with rates for the 16–30, 31–42 and 43–55‐year age groups at 27.90%, 21.87% and 19.04%, respectively. In Qena Governorate, the second highest seroprevalence was among participants aged 31–42 years (10.34%), followed by 16–30 years (5.88%), whereas anti‐T. canis IgG antibodies could not be detected in the 43–55 age group. The Pearson χ 2 test exposed a regional age difference in T. canis seropositivity with younger age groups (4–15 and 16–30 years) being exposed more frequently in Alexandria than in Qena (p = 0.006 and 0.013, respectively; 95% CI: 0.092–0.698 and 0.033–0.781, respectively).

Anti‐T. canis antibodies were more frequent in individuals residing in rural areas than in urban areas in both the Alexandria (71.1% and 8.8%, respectively) and Qena (12.8% and 3.85%, respectively) Governorates (p = 0.000, 95% CI: 0.024–0.150) with an attributed risk of 58.29%. The prevalence of anti‐T. canis antibodies in both Alexandria and Qena Governorates was also higher in dog owners (31.95% and 10.13%, respectively) than in individuals who were not in contact with dogs (26.31% and 6.98%). Furthermore, in both Alexandria and Qena Governorates, seropositivity rates were significantly lower in individuals who washed their hands either after contact with dogs or before meals (14.28% and 0%, respectively) than in those who did not (82.85% and 29.73%, respectively). Exposure rates were also higher among those who did not wash vegetables and fruits before consumption (72.91% and 16%, respectively) than those who washed them thoroughly (10.37% and 4.17%, respectively). Similarly, seropositivity rates were higher in participants who habitually consumed raw or poorly cooked meat in both Alexandria (42.10%) and Qena (12.5%).

DISCUSSION

Determining the prevalence of parasitic zoonotic diseases is critical in reducing the threat to human health. In this study, 33.33% of faecal samples from domestic dogs were positive for T. canis eggs. Compared with previous studies in Egypt, our faecal shedding rate was higher than those recorded by Arafa et al. (2014) in Alexandria (5%) and Awadallah and Salem (2015) in Qalyubia and Sharkia provinces (5.38%). Conversely, Abdel Aziz et al. (2019) reported lower prevalence rates in the Egyptian localities of Sohag (75.4%), Ismailia (48.88%), Menofia (40%) and Alexandria (51.23%). Reported prevalence rates also vary dramatically across other parts of the world, most likely attributable to geographic variation, sample size, inconsistent anthelminthic use, managemental practices and defecation site of the dogs evaluated (Sowemimo, 2009 in Nigeria (33.8%); Kumar et al., 2014 in India (28.93%); El Maghrbi et al., 2019 in Libya (27.5%); Lamsal and Prajapati, 2020 in Nepal (13.64%) and Silva et al., 2020 in Portugal (29%).

The prevalence of canine toxocariasis in our study was higher in Alexandria Governorate (39.47%) than in Qena Governorate (26.47%) which could possibly reflect differences in the climate conditions; year‐round clement weather (temperature range 17–26°C) and the high‐humidity levels (70%–80%) of Alexandria promoting the maturation of T. canis eggs and extending their viability in the soil. In addition, a larger number of domiciled dogs are found in Alexandria, as indicated by the statistics from the Veterinary Medicine Directorates. In contrast, the low humidity and high temperature in Qena Governorate may promote early hatching and desiccation of T. canis eggs resulting in a lower prevalence (Erofeeva & Vasenev 2020).

The higher prevalence of T. canis found in male dogs (43.84% compared with 19.74% in females) is corroborated by previous publications (Swai et al., 2010; Rostami et al., 2020) and may be due to immunosuppressive testosterone activity and increased roaming behaviours (Curi et al., 2016). Interestingly, male dogs in Alexandria showed a significantly higher toxocariasis incidence (51.81%) than those in Qena (34.40%) (p = 0.013, 95% CI: 0.276–0.861) and a similar but not statistically significant finding was observed in female dogs (Alexandria, 22.50%; Qena, 16.88%) (p = 0.377, 95% CI: 0.316–1.548). The similarities in prevalence rates between male and female dogs in Alexandria may be due to the more frequent exposure of dogs in the region to sources of infection.

Although not statistically significant, dogs aged <6 months showed a higher prevalence of T. canis (43.52%) than those aged >6 months (24.21%). These results are consistent with previous findings (Biu et al., 2012; Panigrahi et al.,2014; Rostami et al., 2020) and may reflect the underdeveloped immune system of younger dogs, as well as the possibility of prenatal and lactogenic transmission of toxocariasis as a result of the arrested larvae in adult female dog (Lamsal & Prajapati, 2020). Other factors, such as age‐dependent acquired immunity (Kennedy, 2010) and parasite fertility, could also affect this phenomenon (Pereckienė et al., 2007).

Toxocariasis is a zoonotic disease that affects millions of paediatric and adolescent patients globally, and several authors have emphasized the higher prevalence of anti‐Toxocara antibodies in developing countries (Kanobana et al., 2013; Nguyen et al., 2016). Our study showed an overall seroprevalence of 20.65% (57/276) for anti‐T. canis antibodies among the combined study populations of Alexandria and Qena Governorates which is higher than that reported by El‐Shazly et al. (2009) (7.7%) but lower than that reported by Awadallah and Salem (2015) (29.85%). Incidence rates ranging from 5.1% to 71.8% have been reported across other regions of the world (Berrett et al., 2017; Baghani et al., 2018; Nicoletti et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2016; Araújo et al., 2018; Na‐Ek et al., 2022) with the differences mainly attributed to climate, social, economic and cultural conditions.

Males and females in Alexandria were more likely to have been exposed to T. canis (20.28% and 37.64%, respectively) than those in Qena (9.23% and 8.77%, respectively) which may be attributed to climate and behavioural differences between the two governorates. In Qena, males had higher infection rates than females, which concur with the findings of Silva et al. (2017), Raissi et al. (2018), Rezaiemanesh et al. (2019), Phasuk and Punsawad (2020), Wang et al. (2020) and Na‐Ek et al. (2022). The results in Alexandria were the opposite, with higher infection rates among females than males, which agrees with the findings of Kyei et al. (2015) and Delai et al. (2021). This may be explained by differences in time spent engaging in outdoor activities, differences in time spent with dogs and the possibility of lower immunity among females (Klein & Flanagan, 2016).

As found in previous studies (Rezaiemanesh et al., 2019; Chieffi et al., 2021; Delai et al., 2021; Na‐Ek et al., 2022), the highest T. canis seropositivity was observed in the youngest age group (4–15 years) at 39.65% and 14.29% in Alexandria and Qena, respectively (p = 0.006). This may be due to the high frequency of children and adolescents in public places, such as parks, playgrounds, beaches and sandboxes, which are considered established territories for Toxocara infection. In contrast, other authors report increased exposure rates with increasing age (Lee et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2018), a phenomenon that possibly relates to the longevity of anti‐Toxocara antibodies leading to incremental increases in population exposure.

Toxocara seroprevalence was significantly higher in rural areas of both Alexandria and Qena Governorates, which may be attributable to animal husbandry and disposal of animal faeces. The economic status of residents in rural areas is also crucial in Toxocara epidemiology (Antonios et al., 2008; Delai et al., 2021; Doğan et al., 2007; El‐Shazly et al., 2009; Shahat et al., 2019). Nevertheless, Alavi and Sefidgaran (2008) found comparable Toxocara seropositivity between children living in urban and rural areas in Iran.

T. canis seropositivity was significantly associated with dog contact with the highest exposure rates observed among dog owners (31.95% and 10.13% in Alexandria and Qena, respectively). These findings agree with many previous studies (Na‐Ek et al., 2022; Phasuk et al., 2020; Kyei et al., 2015; Rezaiemanesh et al., 2019; Shahat et al., 2019) in which significant correlations have been found between toxocariasis and contact with pets. Conversely, Zibaei et al. (2019) and Delai et al. (2021) found no association between seroprevalence and dog ownership.

Of particular note was that in Qena Governorate, no seropositive cases were reported among people who washed their hands before eating or after coming into contact with dogs, whereas 29.73% of those who did not practice handwashing had been exposed. Consistent with this, Cassenote et al. (2014), Kyei et al. (2015) and Na‐Ek et al. (2022) found that handwashing before meals appeared to be the only significant risk factor associated with T. canis infection. Conversely, Martínez et al. (2018) and Delai et al. (2021) reported a nonsignificant association between handwashing and Toxocara seropositivity. In both Alexandria and Qena, people who consumed unwashed vegetables and fruits were also more likely to be seropositive (attributed risk 56.91%) and other surveys have shown a similar association (Baghani et al., 2018; Raissi et al., 2018; Shchelkanov et al., 2020). Furthermore, seropositivity rates in both Alexandria and Qena suggest that the consumption of raw or undercooked meat from paratenic hosts is another risk factor for T. canis infection, albeit with significant variation. This result agrees with those reported by Rezaiemanesh et al. (2019), Rostami et al. (2019) and Chou and Fan (2020).

CONCLUSIONS

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the comparative prevalence of T. canis infection in North (Lower) Egypt and South (Upper) Egypt, one of the developing countries in which toxocariasis is widespread among dog and human populations. A higher T. canis infection rate was found in dogs from Alexandria Governorate and was attributed to its warmer and more humid climate when compared with Qena Governorate. Sociodemographic characteristics associated with T. canis exposure included handwashing before meals and after being in contact with dogs, washing of vegetables and fruits before consumption and dog ownership. Increasing awareness of the zoonotic potential of T. canis is necessary to minimize its impact on society.
