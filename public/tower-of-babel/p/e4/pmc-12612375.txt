Acute Postoperative Pain in Orthopaedic Trauma and Non‐Trauma Patients: An Analysis of the China Acute Postoperative Pain Study (CAPOPS)
Authors: Lei Li, Shiyi Han, Feng Zhao, Huikai Yang, Xuecai Lv, Jing Wu, Aisheng Hou, Fan Li, Duo Hou, Changsheng Zhang, Hao Li, Min Li, Jingfeng Li, Zongwang Zhang, Changming Wang, Weidong Mi, Yanhong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe rising volume of orthopaedic surgeries highlights the importance of optimal postoperative pain management, especially for trauma patients who frequently suffer inadequate analgesia and delayed recovery. This multicenter study aims to evaluate the prevalence of acute postoperative pain and identify contributing factors in traumatic and non‐traumatic orthopaedic patients.MethodsThis multicenter study analysed adult orthopaedic surgery patients from the China Acute Postoperative Pain Study between September 2019 and August 2023. Patients with preoperative acute physical injuries requiring surgical intervention were defined as trauma patients. Using multivariate regression, we compared pain outcomes between trauma/non‐trauma groups and identified risk factors for moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain.ResultsAmong 10,892 orthopaedic patients (2833 trauma, 8059 non‐trauma), trauma patients showed higher rates of moderate‐to‐severe pain (57.3% vs. 50.1%), experienced more anxiety and helplessness, and had a greater desire for additional pain management than non‐trauma patients. Trauma was associated with the development of moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain in both uni‐ and multivariate analyses (p < 0.001). Risk factors for postoperative pain differed: age over 65 years and preoperative chronic pain were significant in non‐trauma patients, whereas intraoperative non‐opioid analgesics and local anaesthetic infiltration were associated with a decreased incidence of moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain in trauma patients.ConclusionsOver half of orthopaedic surgery patients experienced moderate to severe postoperative pain, with trauma patients reporting higher levels and greater impacts on their activities and emotional state, along with a greater need for additional pain relief. Distinct risk factors for trauma and non‐trauma patients highlight the need for personalised perioperative analgesic strategies.Significance StatementThis study advances perioperative pain management by identifying trauma as an independent risk factor for moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain in orthopaedic patients, with distinct risk profiles for trauma and non‐trauma subgroups. Trauma patients reported higher pain levels, greater distress and increased analgesic needs. These findings directly inform clinical practice, emphasising the need for personalised, risk‐stratified pain management to improve recovery. The results provide an evidence‐based framework for optimising analgesia in both trauma and elective orthopaedic surgery.

Introduction

The number of orthopaedic surgeries has been increasing over recent years (Meara et al. 2015). Effective pain management strategies can enhance patient comfort and reduce the risk of developing chronic pain pathways (Glare et al. 2019; Hsia et al. 2018). However, despite the availability of numerous preventative and therapeutic strategies, postoperative pain management in orthopaedic patients remains a significant challenge (TASMAN Collaborative 2024; Finnerup 2019). In addition to individual patient characteristics, the selected anaesthesia protocol and perioperative medications administered play pivotal roles in shaping the postoperative pain experience (Memtsoudis et al. 2013; Sort et al. 2021).

Notably, trauma patients, in contrast to non‐trauma patients, often receive trauma control treatments during the early stages of hospitalisation, with less emphasis on early pain management, despite a pain prevalence of up to 70% in this group (Berben et al. 2011). Several studies have demonstrated that insufficient initial pain management can prolong trauma recovery, hinder functional restoration, and compromise immune function, potentially exacerbating acute pain into a chronic condition or even disability (Keene et al. 2021; Kolstadbraaten et al. 2019; Lavand'homme 2017). Furthermore, poor pain control can heighten patient anxiety and agitation and disrupt effective communication between healthcare providers and patients (Herrera‐Escobar et al. 2018). Thus, to enhance postoperative pain control and optimise patient outcomes, identifying anaesthetic and analgesic techniques that effectively mitigate postoperative pain is crucial.

This multicenter study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of acute postoperative pain among orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients and investigate the underlying factors contributing to severe postoperative pain in these patient populations. It also sought to assess the efficacy of pain management interventions tailored to this patient cohort (Liu et al. 2023). These efforts are intended to enhance the safety and efficacy of perioperative acute pain management protocols for orthopaedic surgical patients, thereby improving their overall quality of life.

Methods

CAPOPS Network

This study utilises data derived from the China Acute Postoperative Pain Study (CAPOPS), a nationwide, multicenter registry established to investigate factors affecting patient satisfaction with pain management. CAPOPS was registered on 17 August 2019, with the identifier ChiCTR1900025237. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, with written or oral consent obtained from each participant. The manuscript follows the STROBE/RECORD guidelines.

Following approval of the research plan by the CAPOPS commission, an anonymized dataset of 10,902 patients from 94 participating centers who underwent orthopaedic surgery between September 2019 and August 2023 was made available for analysis. The date of first orthopaedic patient enrollment was 1 September 2019.

Study Design

All orthopaedic patients who met the inclusion criteria were screened in the CAPOPS database. The inclusion criteria required that participants must be at least 18 years of age, capable of providing informed consent, and assessed on the first postoperative day (POD1) after being transferred back to the ward from the recovery room for at least 6 h, and have agreed to participate in the survey.

Patient were excluded from CAPOPS if they had missing or “unrated” evaluations of the worst pain degree, the least pain score exceeded the worst pain score, missing information on the anaesthesia method, or absent data on surgery duration. For the current analysis, patients who received only local infiltration anaesthesia (n = 8) or missing information on the name of the surgical procedures (n = 2) were further excluded.

Patients were categorised into two groups: orthopaedic trauma (n = 2833) and non‐trauma (n = 8059) patients. Orthopaedic trauma denotes a significant injury to the musculoskeletal system resulting from external force and necessitating surgical intervention. Orthopaedic trauma included conditions such as dislocations, fractures, breaks, sports hernia, impingement, overuse injuries and sprains (Table S3). Correspondingly, the types of surgery included fracture and dislocation reductions, repairs of articular cartilage and ligament injuries, soft tissue surgeries and laceration repairs.

Data Collection

For the CAPOPS database, information pertaining to data collection has been thoroughly reported in our previously published article (Liu et al. 2023). Briefly, patient demographics, general information, anaesthesia, analgesia, surgery‐related data and pharmacological details were retrieved from medical records. International Pain Outcome Questionnaire (IPOQ) was used to evaluate pain‐related outcomes on the POD1. In IPOQ, pain intensity was rated on the numeric rating scale (NRS: NRS 0 = no pain, NRS 10 = worst pain imaginable). Additionally, the duration of time spent in severe pain and satisfaction with pain management (NRS 0 = very unsatisfied, NRS 10 = highly satisfied) were assessed. Furthermore, pain‐related functional disturbances, adverse events, emotional impairments, such as anxiety and helplessness, and the desire for more pain management were evaluated. Responses were classified as ‘yes’ (NRS ≥ 1) or ‘no’ (NRS = 0), and the percentage of patients answering ‘yes’ was calculated. Chronic pain was defined as patient self‐report persistent pain lasting ≥ 3 months prior to the surgical admission (Schug et al. 2019).

Preoperative medication use was recorded using a dedicated section of the Case Report Form (CRF). Sedatives primarily included benzodiazepines and barbiturates, along with Haloperidol, Promethazine and Zopiclone. Postoperative pain medications were classified as non‐opioids or opioids, and the percentage of patients who received each type of pain medication was calculated. The use of patient‐controlled analgesia (PCA) and multimodal analgesia, defined as the concurrent use of two or more distinct analgesic techniques or pharmacologic agents with differing mechanisms of action (Chou et al. 2016), was recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of moderate‐to‐severe pain on POD1, defined as the worst pain score rated on the NRS (0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain imaginable) ≥ 4.

Secondary outcomes included the worst pain score, the prevalence of severe pain (NRS ≥ 7), percentage of time spent in severe pain, pain interference with physical activity and sleep, anxiety, helplessness, side effects (nausea, drowsiness, pruritus and dizziness) and perception of pain treatment. Perceptions of pain treatment included the degree of pain relief, the desire for more pain management, awareness of pain treatment options, involvement in treatment decisions and satisfaction with pain treatment.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), depending on their distribution. The normality of the distribution was assessed using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables are summarised as frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, comparisons were performed using an independent t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analysed using the χ 2 test.

Logistic regression analyses were employed to evaluate the worst pain score, the percentage of time spent in intense pain, emotional and physical activity impairments, postoperative adverse effects, and patient perceptions of care in orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs). Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the effects of age, sex, preoperative chronic pain, comorbidities and perioperative medication use on the occurrence of moderate‐to‐severe pain on POD1 in orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients. Variables included in this model were chosen based on a combination of clinical relevance and findings from univariate analyses (p < 0.05 was used as a screening threshold for inclusion). Results from this model are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Only participants with complete data for all variables were included in the logistic regression model. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.5.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patients with orthopaedic trauma were younger, exhibited lower BMI values, had fewer comorbidities, such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease, and were less likely to have comorbid preoperative chronic pain. However, they had a higher incidence of multiple traumas.

A greater proportion of patients in the orthopaedic trauma group received regional anaesthesia compared to those in the non‐trauma group. Regarding perioperative medication use, fewer patients in the orthopaedic trauma group received preoperative sedatives, intraoperative opioid analgesics and non‐opioid analgesics. Fewer orthopaedic trauma patients received opioids, non‐opioids and regional anaesthesia in the post‐anaesthesia care unit (PACU), but more received these on the wards compared to non‐trauma patients. Multivariate regression analysis, which incrementally incorporated patient demographic, surgical and pain management‐related parameters, revealed that trauma was an independent risk factor for postoperative pain in all three models (Table 3). For both groups, using general anaesthesia was associated with a reduction in moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain. The preoperative administration of sedatives and the use of non‐opioid analgesics in the post‐anaesthesia care unit were associated with a reduction in moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain, whereas the use of opioid and non‐opioid analgesics on the ward was associated with moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain in both groups.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare acute postoperative pain experiences between orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients. Our key findings directly addressing this aim reveal that trauma patients experience significantly higher rates of moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain, greater psychological distress (anxiety, helplessness), and a stronger desire for additional pain treatment compared to non‐trauma patients. Crucially, predictors of pain severity diverged substantially between groups. While advanced age (> 65 years) and pre‐existing chronic pain were prominent risk factors in non‐trauma patients, the administration of intraoperative non‐opioid analgesics and local anaesthetic infiltration were protective in trauma patients. These results underscore fundamental differences in the pain experience and its determinants across these distinct surgical populations.

Trauma inflicts physical harm and psychological stress on patients while also imposing social and economic burdens. Previous studies have found that patients undergoing surgery after exogenous trauma experience significantly higher levels of emotional stress, greater disruption of normal life activities, and more severe pain intensity (Jones Jr. et al. 2017; Nota et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2020). The present study provides a comprehensive assessment of the multidimensional aspects of pain and reveals that trauma patients experienced significantly higher pain severity, a greater proportion of time with severe pain, and a greater impact of pain on their activities compared to non‐trauma patients. Furthermore, compared to non‐trauma patients, trauma patients exhibited a higher risk of anxiety and helplessness and expressed a stronger desire for additional treatment. Therefore, analysing the reasons for the significant differences in postoperative pain outcomes between orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients will aid in developing more effective individualised pain management strategies for different patient populations.

Previous studies have identified several factors associated with postoperative acute pain, including age, sex and a history of preoperative chronic pain (Abrecht et al. 2019; Othow et al. 2022). Younger patients are commonly believed to experience more pronounced postoperative pain (Cachemaille et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2024). The present study found that younger age is positively correlated with increased pain levels in orthopaedic trauma patients. Conversely, in orthopaedic non‐trauma patients, increasing age was associated with higher levels of pain. This may be related to the fact that trauma patients are generally younger than non‐trauma patients. The proportion of male patients in the trauma group was significantly higher than that of females, in stark contrast to the sex distribution in the non‐trauma patient population. Given that levels of postoperative pain are relatively high in trauma patients, the sex distribution difference may account for the lack of a significant sex effect on postoperative pain observed in the present study.

Preoperative chronic pain is a well‐established predictor of postoperative pain in orthopaedic surgical patients (García‐López et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2014). In the current analysis, trauma patients had a significantly lower prevalence (7.4%) and severity of preoperative chronic pain compared to non‐trauma patients (46.0%). Consequently, while chronic pain was associated with increased moderate‐to‐severe postoperative acute pain in non‐trauma cases, it was not a significant risk factor in trauma patients. These findings suggest that non‐trauma orthopaedic patients who often experience more severe chronic pain before surgery should receive more attention in preoperative pain evaluation and management.

Our study demonstrated that trauma patients reported lower satisfaction with pain management and expressed a higher demand for pain treatment than non‐trauma patients. Regarding preoperative medication, the use of preoperative sedative medications serves as a protective factor against moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain, with an OR of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.59–0.81, p < 0.001). However, in our cohort, the use of preoperative sedative medications among orthopaedic patients was limited to 7.3%, with an even lower percentage of 5.5% among trauma patients. Orthopaedic trauma patients received a higher proportion of opioid and non‐opioid analgesics than non‐trauma patients (7.9% vs. 5.7% and 13.9% vs. 11.6%, respectively). Nevertheless, considering the acute pain frequently experienced by trauma patients before surgery and the fact that 43.9% of patients expressed a need for more pain treatment, we believe that adequate preoperative analgesia and sedation would be beneficial for these patients.

Among the various factors influencing postoperative pain, the type of anaesthesia administered is a notable consideration (Wahood et al. 2019; Weinstein et al. 2018). However, a gap exists in its differential impact on trauma versus non‐trauma orthopaedic patients. Our findings suggest that for trauma patients, the use of general anaesthesia is more effective in mitigating moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain compared to regional anaesthesia. Notably, most regional anaesthesia was limited to intraoperative use and did not persist postoperatively. Consequently, the proportion of postoperative regional analgesia was only 0.7% in trauma patients, which was even lower than that in non‐trauma patients (4.5%). Therefore, the relatively low utilisation rate of postoperative regional analgesia should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

Multimodal analgesia is widely recommended in guidelines for managing perioperative pain in patients (Wick et al. 2017). In the present study, we analysed the combined use of opioids, non‐opioids, and regional analgesia with local anaesthetics in our cohort, with 52.7% of the patients receiving two or more analgesic modalities. However, in our study, the use of multimodal analgesia did not significantly affect the incidence of moderate‐to‐severe postoperative pain. This may be due to the significant variations in multimodal analgesic protocols employed across different study institutions. Consequently, further investigations are warranted to refine the selection and customization of multimodal analgesic regimens within specific clinical contexts.

This study had some inherent limitations. First, significant differences were observed at baseline between orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients, including age, sex, preoperative conditions and type of surgery. These factors represent potential confounders of the relationship between patient group (trauma vs. non‐trauma) and acute postoperative pain outcomes. We conducted multivariate analyses adjusting for these identified confounders to mitigate their influence. However, residual confounding may persist, particularly due to unmeasured factors, such as preoperative psychological states (e.g., anxiety, stress, depression), chronic preoperative use of specific oral analgesics, or imperfect measurement of the included variables, and its potential impact on the findings cannot be entirely excluded. Second, we only collected prognostic indicators related to pain within the first 24 h postoperatively and did not analyse the long‐term prognosis or the incidence of chronic pain. Further research is required to investigate the long‐term prognosis associated with pain in orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients.

Conclusions

This study establishes that orthopaedic trauma and non‐trauma patients represent two distinct populations with divergent pain experiences and risk factors. Beyond confirming that trauma patients suffer more severe pain and psychological burden, our analysis provides a novel, evidence‐based framework for personalised pain management: effective strategies are not universal. For non‐trauma patients, focus should be on assessing age and pain history. For trauma patients, the proactive use of intraoperative non‐opioids and local infiltration analgesia is critical. The primary significance of this work lies in moving from recognition of this difference to actionable insights, guiding clinicians in tailoring interventions to improve outcomes and patient satisfaction in both groups.
