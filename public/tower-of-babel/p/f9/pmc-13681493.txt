Nervonic acid alleviates stroke and its associated poststroke depression behaviors
Authors: Fengrong Zhang, Yong Fan, Nghi Van Phung, Boyang Ji, Junmiao Chen, Xingyue Xu, Fuli Li, Peifeng Ji, Hongjun Yang, Xianyu Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNervonic acid (NA) is an important long-chain monounsaturated fatty acid found in mammalian nervous tissue. It has recently garnered research attention due to its therapeutic potential in treating psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders. In this study, we investigated the efficacy of NA in treating ischemia/reperfusion and poststroke events in a rat model. Specifically, there was significant reduction in the infarct area, cell death, and neuronal swelling after NA treatment, and the improvement in cerebral blood flow was also observed on day five after middle cerebral artery occlusion. Moreover, NA treatment led to the upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factors and myelin basic protein genes. NA displayed improved effects on depressive-like behavior of rats by three validated assays—the sucrose preference test, open-field test, and forced swim test. Regarding the mechanism of action, direct supplementation of NA in the brain was observed. We also observed the indirect effects of NA on the gut microbiota. Notably, the NA group gradually restored the bacterial diversity and the EGb group exhibited no impact based on observed-out analysis. We found an increase in the abundance of Blautia and Sutterella, which participated in phenylalanine metabolism. The metabolomics of plasma and brain samples revealed a decrease in the levels of phenylalanine-based amino acids, which alleviated the inhibitory effects on glutamine metabolism and promoted the recovery and signaling transmission of neurons after stroke. Altogether, our findings suggest that NA can be a viable treatment option for alleviating stroke and its associated poststroke depressive-like behaviors.Keywords: nervonic acid, poststroke depressive-like behaviors, gut microbiota

Introduction

Nervonic acid (C24:1 Δ15, NA) is a very long-chain monounsaturated fatty acid found in various organisms, including microalgae, mammals, fish, and oleaginous plants [1]. It functions as both a free molecule and a crucial component of sphingolipids (SPs), which are involved in several biological processes such as membrane formation, apoptosis, and neurotransmitter transmission [2]. Previous studies have revealed that NA plays an intrinsic role in brain development. Furthermore, the supplementation of NA can alleviate many diseases, including cancer, diabetes, obesity, and neurological disorders such as stroke, Parkinson's disease, and schizophrenia [3,4]. Naturally, NA has been synthesized by numerous microalgae, fungi, and plants. In addition, NA is abundantly synthesized by genetically engineered microorganisms [5,6], with the highest titer reaching 44.2 g/L [6]. Such high production is expected to facilitate future studies on NA functions and reduce the cost of NA-containing drugs.

Stroke is a life-threatening condition ranked as the second leading cause of mortality and the leading cause of disability worldwide [7]. In western countries, approximately 25% of the population is estimated to experience stroke during their lifetime [8]. Ischemic stroke, which accounts for approximately 87% of all strokes, is an acute injury of brain tissue due to the blockage of the cerebral artery [9]. Several stroke survivors face physical disabilities and neuropsychiatric issues, which can be categorized as follows: poststroke neurological disorders and poststroke psychiatric disorders. The former includes poststroke pain and dementia, whereas the latter includes poststroke depression (PSD), anxiety, and fatigue.

Previous studies have reported that stroke and PSD is associated with several processes, including inflammatory responses, dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical axis, decreased serotonin levels, glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, and mitochondrial dysfunction. In recent decades, significant progress has been made to address the prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and follow-up of PSD.

Microbiome-based therapeutics mediated by probiotics show immense potential for treating stroke and PSD [10]. Another advance is the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) which have demonstrated clinical effectiveness in the prevention and treatment of PSD [11]. Extract of Ginkgo biloba (EGb) Leaves Tablets have been used to treat PSD, but its therapeutic effects on PSD have remained unknown. Previous studies have shown that EGb exhibits antidepressant effects in mice by regulating intestinal microbial metabolism [12]. However, these therapeutic strategies are effective in only about 30%–40% of all PSD cases [10]. Therefore, it is urgent to investigate novel targets and treatment strategies that can be utilized either independently or in combination with existing therapies [13,14]. Recent studies revealed that NA is involved in many processes within glial cells. Particularly, its SPs are not only a major component of the cell membrane (myelin sheath) [15,16], but also participate in the signal transduction processes [[17], [18], [19]]. Based on the aforementioned findings, we hypothesize that using NA to treat neurological diseases might be beneficial, especially for PSD.

Presently, many fatty acid-containing drugs are administered orally, making the investigation of their interactions with the intestinal flora an intriguing research avenue. Previous studies have shown that the gut microbiota undergoes considerable changes throughout the human lifespan, playing an indispensable role in the occurrence and development of stroke and PSD [20]. The crosstalk between the gut microbiota and brain involves the synergistic activities of several pathways including neuroimmune and neuroendocrine pathways, metabolites such as vitamins and neurotransmitters, and cell types such as vagus nerve [21]. The dysbiosis of intestinal microorganisms impacts the gut–brain axis and is associated with focal cerebral ischemia-induced depression. Gut microbes can lead to the development of depression by triggering inflammatory responses, disrupting monoamine neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factor balance, and activating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical axis [22]. Probiotics have been suggested to play important roles in regulating the gut microbiota, which is crucial in developing stroke and PSD through the gut–brain axis [10]. Therefore, investigation of NA's effects on gut microbiota alterations is of great significance for developing effective treatment strategies for stroke and PSD.

In this study, we investigated the efficacy of NA in the rehabilitation of rats with ischemia/reperfusion injury. The effect of orally administered NA was evaluated based on its association with the gut microbiota and metabolite activity. We aim to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective function of NA.

Results

Improved Neurological Recovery after Ischemic Stroke by NA

We observed the effect of NA administration for 5 days on infarct volume and neurological deficit in rats following cerebral ischemia (Figure 1A). To confirm the positive effects of NA supplementation, we also included another control group with EGb treatment. In the Model group, the neurological deficit score increased. However, the NA group did not show a significant decrease compared with the Model group after 2 h. The significant differences in neurological scores among groups were observed on day 5 after initial treatment, particularly the scores of the NA and EGb groups significantly decreased compared to that of the Model group (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01, Figure 1B). As shown in Figure 1C, nerve cells in the Sham group remained structurally intact with regular arrangements and no observable pathological changes. However, the rats in the Model group showed increased nerve cell death, disorganized arrangements, increased cell necrosis, and more inflammatory cell infiltration. Notably, in the NA and EGb groups, we observed only a slight swelling of nerve cells and reduced cell necrosis. The nerve tissue exhibited a regular structure, and we observed a significant decrease in the level of inflammatory cell infiltration. Altogether, results suggest that NA can improve middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO)-induced changes in cell morphology, as observed by the hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Following MCAO, the infarct volume in the Model group significantly increased compared with that in the Sham group, whereas a significant decrease was observed in the NA group (P < 0.05) and EGb group (P < 0.05) (Figure 1D-1E). Analysis of brain blood flow showed that NA restored and improved blood flow in brain tissue after MCAO induction (Figure 1F-1G). To further determine whether NA exhibits protective effects on myelin sheaths during a stroke, we performed myelin basic protein (MBP) staining on brain tissues (Figure 1H). In the ischemic optic nerves, bright and large areas were observed at the point of tissue dissolution. While the Sham group exhibited consistent results, and uniform coloration, MBP staining of myelin sheaths showed the degeneration and loss of myelin sheaths after ischemia in the Model group. Notably, in rats treated with NA, signs of demyelination were reduced, and MBP levels significantly increased in the brains of rats treated with NA for 5 d. Altogether, these results reveal that orally ingesting NA can potentially improve neurological recovery by ameliorating brain infarct volume, neurological deficits, cerebral blood flow (CBF), and the levels of MBP.

NA Ameliorates Depression-like Behaviors Induced by MCAO in Rat

Rats were exposed to MCAO for 24 h and subsequently received 5 d of NA treatment. Rats subjected to the MCAO exhibited a significant suppression in sucrose preference. In contrast, NA administration successfully reversed this effect and restored sucrose preference to normal levels within 5 d of treatment (Figure 2A-2B). As depicted in Figure 2C, considerable differences in movement trajectories were observed among the groups. In the NA-treated group, there was a significant decrease in residence time and an increase in movement trajectories compared with the Model group, which exhibited contrary performance compared to the Sham group. In the forced swimming test (FST), the MCAO model rats showed increased accumulated immobility time over 6 min (Figure 2D-2E). To assess depression-like behavior, we conducted open-field tests (OFTs). The rats from the MCAO Model group exhibited remarkable signs of depression-like behavior compared to the Sham group. Specifically, they spent less time in the center zone, had lower average speed, and showed increased resting and central activity time (Figure 2F-2J). Notably, these pathological behaviors were substantially mitigated by NA treatment, confirming its efficacy in alleviating MCAO-induced depression. NA-treated rats displayed significant improvements in depression-like behavior. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) has been implicated as a biomarker in depression. In comparison to the Sham group, the BDNF expression in the MCAO Model group was significantly decreased in the stained area of the brain. However, the expression of BDNF in the brains of rats treated with NA for 5 d was significantly increased compared to the Model group. A similar phenotype was also identified in the non-infarcted cortex region (Figure 2K). These results indicate that NA treatment can effectively mitigate MCAO-induced depression-like behaviors and restore BDNF expression, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for PSD.

Oral Administration of Nervonic Acid Can Achieve In Situ Supplementation of Cerebral Stroke Injury

We initially assessed whether NA plays a direct or indirect role in intervening in PSD (Figure 3A). The data obtained from the ZenoTOF™ 7600 (AB SCIEX, Massachusetts, USA) showed significant differences in the metabolites between the brain and plasma samples when tested under positive and negative ion conditions. The important metabolites in the brain and plasma triggered by PSD were identified (Figure 3B, Tables S1–S4). The metabolite data were obtained using negative ionization for the brain metabolites at 916 and plasma metabolites at 539. Furthermore, positive ionization was used for the brain metabolites at 871 and plasma metabolites at 345. Nevertheless, no significant increasing trend was observed in NA levels in the brain or plasma after 5 d of continuous administration (Figure 3C-3D). This suggests that NA is likely not found in a free state in the plasma and brain tissue. Hence, we extracted total lipids from the plasma and brain and evaluated NA content postmethylation. We found that NA content was significantly higher in the experimental group than in the control group. Nevertheless, the highest NA content was found in the Sham group, suggesting an association between NA content in neural tissue and the functional state of the neural tissue (Figure 3E). Oral supplementation of NA can replenish endogenous NA. In the plasma, we did not find a significant increase in NA, which may be due to the tight regulation of lipids in plasma or the improved role of the lymphatic system in the transportation of long-chain fatty acids (Figure 3F).

NA is a vital fatty acid in the cell membrane of glial cells, playing an indispensable role in brain tissue by treating damages in situ. However, how NA exhibits a positive emotional reparative effect compared with positive drugs remains unknown. Metabolomics analysis of all groups (Sham, Model, NA, and EGb; n = 6) was performed. The volcano plot revealed significant differences and multiple variations in metabolite levels within the brain and plasma of these groups (Figure 3G-3H). We performed Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses to investigate the potential biological functions and signaling pathways affected by these metabolites. These differential metabolites were significantly associated with several metabolomic pathways related to riboflavin metabolism, butanoate metabolism, d-glutamine, and d-glutamate metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism in the brain (Figure 3I). Contrarily, changes in plasma metabolites were significantly associated with bile acid biosynthesis, malate–aspartate shuttle, ketone body metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, and glutathione metabolism (Figure 3J). Additionally, a significant correlation was found among the following categories of enriched metabolites in the brain: small peptides (16%), benzene and substituted derivatives (13%), organic acids and their derivatives (12%), and heterocyclic compounds (12%) (Figure 3K). The enriched categories in the plasma included benzene and substituted derivatives (36%), small peptides (23%), and organic acids and their derivatives (16%) (Figure 3L). We found that benzene and its substituted derivatives account for a large proportion of metabolites in the brain and plasma. Altogether, these results suggest that NA may indirectly regulate amino acid metabolism and lipid metabolism to improve PSD.

Altered Gut Microbiota and Glutamate/Phenylalanine Metabolism by NA

Because NA is administered by gavage, we hypothesized that its beneficial effects on PSD symptoms arise from its metabolism and interactions with gut microbiota. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the changes in gut microbiota in rats from the Sham, Model, NA, and EGb groups using shotgun metagenomics sequencing (n = 6 for each group). By principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), we found that the microbiome of the Sham and MCAO Model groups was indistinguishable from the treatment groups of NA and EGb. Notably, NA treatment significantly changed the beta diversity of rats with PSD, whereas the EGb did exhibit such an effect (Figure 4A, Table S5). At the α-diversity index level, NA did not significantly affect the Shannon index, Pielou-e index, and Faith phylogenetic diversity of gut microbial. The Observed-out analysis reflected that gut bacterial diversity of the Model group was decreased vs the NA group gradually restored the bacterial diversity and EGb had no effect (Figure 4B, Table S5). As shown in Figure S1, each pair of rat groups shared distinct families and genera of gut microorganisms (P < 0.05). Furthermore, an increase in the proportion of Blautia and Sutterella was observed in the NA group compared with the MCAO Model group (P < 0.05, Figure 4C, Table S5). Moreover, many other bacterial genera, including Prevotella, Turicibacter, and Romboutsia, increased in the NA group, whereas the proportion of Escherichia–Shigella, Parabacteroides, and Bilophila reduced (Figure 4C, Table S5). However, the abundance of Escherichia–Shigella, Parabacteroides, and Bilophila increased, whereas the abundance of Sutterella, Prevotella, Ruminococcus, Turicibacter, and Romboutsia decreased in the Model group than in the Sham group (P < 0.05). Three genera, Blautia, Dorea, and Ruminococcus were positively associated with the metabolite component and belong to the Lachnospiraceae family, which is one of the most abundant families of gut microbes. A previous study showed a strong association between plasma glutamate-related metabolites and gut bacteria, particularly the Blautia, Dorea, and Ruminococcus genera [24]. Beneficial bacteria, such as Blautia, are also associated with the phenylalanine metabolism pathway [25]. The functional analysis of gut microbiota showed that NA treatment significantly regulates the abundance of functional genes associated with phenylalanine and glutamate metabolism in the gut microbiota. Altogether, these findings indicate that NA treatment plays an important role by providing NA supplementation and regulating the gut microbiota. It also contributes to the improvement of phenylalanine and glutamate metabolism, thus exerting beneficial effects on PSD treatment.

The hippocampus dominates the brain metabolome and plays a crucial role in learning and memory development (Figure 5E). Based on this, adult neurons in the hippocampus play a role in mood regulation and the desired effects of antidepressants. Their involvement in pattern recognition and cognition can prevent the development of depression by improving recognition and coping with stressors [26]. Furthermore, enrichment regions overview including the spinal cord, nervous tissue, basal ganglia, myelin, and striatum played a major role in stabilizing the metabolome. A majority of brain metabolites are ubiquitously distributed in the entire brain region and are crucial for the maintenance of key neurological functions. An initial mechanical insult inducing a spinal cord inflammatory response can have a significant effect on locomotor functions and lead to the development of neuropsychiatric conditions such as anxiety and depression [27]. Depression markers may be reflected in regional activation changes in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and precuneus regions. However, the hyperactivation of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and striatum can represent a state-dependent compensatory mechanism during depression remission [28]. These differences in the regulation of metabolism can also be observed in changes in individual metabolic pathways. In our previous study, we enriched pathways of metabolites, including d-glutamine and d-glutamate metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism. The top 25 most important pathways are shown in Figure 5F, suggesting a sufficient breadth of pathway modules to interpret metabolic changes in brain regions during PSD. Assessing the changes in glutamate, glutamine, and Glx levels in the voxel of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of patients with major depressive disorder using proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) reveals a large shift from increased glutamine levels. This is indicative of increased synaptic glutamate release, adding to previous findings on the glutamate hypothesis of major depressive disorder [29]. Poststroke neurological deficits are clinically observed with a concurrent increase in glutamate levels. Peak l-glutamate levels are observed 24 h post-MCAO [30] (Figure 5H). Additionally, highly enriched ratios of amino acids, dipeptides, benzyl alcohols, and polyethylene glycols can serve as biomarkers for PSD diagnosis (Figure 5G).

Heatmaps illustrating changes in metabolites across the Sham, Model, NA, and EGb groups are shown in Figure 5I. We performed integrated clustering of transcriptomic and metabolomic data using the cluster algorithm. The results effectively differentiated different groups based on their samples. Additionally, as an l-glutamine transporter, Slc7a7 plays a crucial role in the transport of small molecules (Figure 5J, Table S6). This is because transporters facilitate the transmembrane transport of metabolites. Furthermore, the expression trends of metabolites in the plasma and brain tissue are the same. However, the transport metabolites of other transporters are expressed only in the brain tissue or only in the plasma or an opposite trend is observed in the expression of transport metabolites in the brain tissue and plasma. As shown in Figure 5J, the pairwise comparison showed an increase in the expression of Slc7a7 in the Model–Sham group and a decrease in the NA–Model and NA–Sham groups (P < 0.01). Subsequently, the levels of N2-(1-oxo-4-phenylbutyl)-l-glutamine, l-glutethimide, and phenylacetylglutamine were notably diminished in the drug group compared with those in the Model group (Figure 5K-5M). In the brain, the levels of various metabolites, including folic acid in the NA group, decreased compared with those in the Model group. On the other hand, the levels of l-tyrosine significantly increased in the NA group than in the Model group (Figure S2). Notably, the decrease in phenylacetylglutamine in the brain may decrease its inhibitory effect on glutamine metabolism, which is potentially associated with the alleviation of depressive symptoms.

Discussion

PSD presents a difficult clinical challenge, serving as a critical complication of stroke with great implications for patient disability and mortality. Previous studies have reported that PSD affects between 11% and 41% of stroke survivors within two years [31]. The early initiation of antidepressant therapy for patients with PSD has been associated with improved physical and cognitive recovery poststroke and potential long-term survival benefits, extending up to a decade after the initial stroke event [33]. Recent investigations have further highlighted the association between PSD and various factors, including inflammation, genetics, epigenetics, white matter disease, cerebrovascular issues, changed neuroplasticity, and disruptions in glutamate neurotransmission [32].

NA is abundantly found in the human brain, liver, and kidney tissues, playing an important role in treating psychotic disorders and neurological development. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effect of using NA for stroke rehabilitation treatment. Our findings revealed that the administration of NA can significantly alleviate the damage due to stroke. We propose that free NA first undergoes conversion into high-energy acetyl-CoA, which then participates in ceramide synthesis in the endoplasmic reticulum via the de novo or salvage pathways. The resulting nervonyl ceramide is subsequently transported to the golgi apparatus to produce more complex SPs, including nervonyl sphingomyelins and glycosphingolipids. These NA derivatives are notably present among very long-chain fatty acids in myelin lipids, serving as crucial components in the formation of the myelin sheath of the central nervous system (CNS). During myelination, oligodendrocytes extend the membrane sheath of myelin to gulf the axons, creating an insulating layer. The thickness of this structure plays an important role in determining the transmission process. The important functions of the myelin sheath largely depend on the presence of nervonyl glycosphingolipids. Among these, nervonyl galactosylceramide and sulfatide are particularly important in the paranode and microdomain. Nervonyl galactosylceramide contributes to CNS node formation, oligodendrocyte maturation, compact myelin maintenance, and myelin integrity and stability, whereas sulfatide negatively regulates oligodendrocyte differentiation [2] (Graphical abstract). Additionally, we also found that NA exerts significant effects beyond clinical medication in treating and ameliorating PSD-like behaviors. Compared with the Model group, NA supplementation notably decreased infarct volumes, pathological scores, and inflammatory infiltration, while also improving neurological scores and CBF in the brain. Our findings suggest that NA exhibits prominent anti-neuroinflammatory and cerebral protective effects in stroke and stroke-induced depression rats. Furthermore, these effects are closely associated with the increased expression of MBP and BDNF in the brain [33]. MBP is a proteolipid present in myelin membranes, predominantly in central myelin, and to a lesser extent, in peripheral myelin [34]. Ischemic injury to the CNS activates and disintegrates cells, releasing cell-specific proteins such as MBP [35]. Furthermore, BDNF, a neurotrophin, plays an important role in improving cerebral stroke, neuropathic pain, and depression [36]. Previous studies have shown decreased BDNF levels in the postmortem hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of patients with depression [37]. In patients with PSD, the presence of a stroke can contribute to the development of depression, affecting the expression of BDNF, which was indicated by lower serum BDNF levels in patients with PSD compared with those without depression [38]. Additionally, a limited number of studies have shown that plasma NA can serve as a promising biomarker for the diagnosis of depressive states in major depressive disorder [39].

The gut microbiome constitutes a significant microbial community within the human body, exerting a profound effect on the host's nutrition, metabolism, and immune function [40,41]. Previous studies have reported a link between a changed gut microbiome and conditions such as stroke and depression. Moreover, microbial metabolites have been implicated in these two conditions. Studies have shown that mice with depression exhibit disruptions in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolisms [42]. In the present study, we performed a comparative analysis of the gut microbiome and metabonomics in PSD and Sham-operated rats. PSD rats exhibited reduced diversity of microbial species compared with the Sham group, as evidenced by decreased species richness indices. This decrease in species richness among PSD rats may signify disruptions in physiological processes. Notably, NA administration was observed to enhance the diversity of gut microbiota and metabolites in rats. Although the gut microbiome of PSD rats has been extensively explored previously, previous studies on depression have shown disturbances in microbial families such as Lachnospiraceae, Lactobacillaceae, Streptococcaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Ruminococcaceae associated with depression. In our investigation, similar changes in microbial taxa were observed in PSD rats, such as Blautia, Sutterella, Escherichia–Shigella, Parabacteroides, Prevotella, Bilophila, Ruminococcus, Turicibacter, and Romboutsia in PSD rats (Figure 4C). Additionally, these changes in bacterial operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were strongly correlated with the results of behavioral tests in PSD rats, providing strong evidence of the abnormal microbial state in PSD at the behavioral level. Notably, these changed gut microbial OTUs exhibit strong associations with various metabolites. Their changes may facilitate improved metabolism of phenylalanine-based amino acids, thus reducing the absorption of metabolites of phenylalanine-based amino acids into the liver and brain. This is especially relevant for compounds such as phenylacetylglutamine, which can decrease their inhibitory effect on glutamine metabolism, thus improving the recovery and signaling of neuronal cells after stroke.

The primary excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters are glutamate and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), respectively. They play an important role in regulating information flow within the brain. After a stroke, glutamate levels increase in the brain. Additionally, excessive glutamate stimulation triggers glutamate receptor activation, leading to nerve cell swelling, apoptosis, and finally, the development of neurological disorders [43]. The glutamatergic system is also implicated in mood disorders such as depression [44]. Acute stress increases extracellular glutamate levels in brain regions such as the medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, contributing to glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity via the extrasynaptic N-methyl-d-aspartate receptors (NMDARs) and subsequent neuronal atrophy. Through transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, we identified significant deregulation in glutamine and phenylacetylglutamine metabolism in the brains of PSD rats. The NA group showed a substantial improvement. Based on this result, along with the changes in gut microbiota, we propose a potential metabolic intervention pathway. NA appears to indirectly influence gut microbiota and its use of phenylalanine. Therefore, the effect of phenylalanine-derived amino acids on neurotransmitters is reduced during the stroke rehabilitation phase. Furthermore, the antidepressant effects of EGb are largely dependent on its effect on glutamate and aspartate metabolism [45,46]. In our study, the effect of NA was significantly superior to that of EGb, showing exceptionally promising potential. Notably, our transcriptome data revealed differential expression of SLC7a7 involved in the transport of these amino acids and their derivatives in the brain. SLC transporters serve as drug targets, with examples such as SSRIs for depression and sodium/glucose co-transporter inhibitors for diabetes [47]. Additionally, SLC1a5 and SLC7a7 are involved in mediating glutamine uptake in ST2 cells. The overactivation of SLC7a7 in the MCAO Model group is due to the increase in glutamate stimulation. Because this transporter cooperates in providing enough glutamine, Slc7a7 plays a major role in mediating acute increases in glutamine uptake in response to WNT [48]. Furthermore, the association of the Slc7 family with susceptibility to schizophrenia was reported [49], and the exacerbation of multiple sclerosis due to excessive glutamate transport and excitotoxicity mediated by SLC proteins was revealed. The complications due to SLC7a7 mutations include persistent hypotonia, coma, and mental developmental disorders [50,51].

To summarize, our findings suggest that the use of NA can improve neurological functions, reduce infarct size, alleviate neuronal cell swelling, and decrease cell death after stroke. Furthermore, CBF can increase considerably 5 d poststroke occurrence. Our results revealed that NA intake increases BDNF levels. We observed positive trends in behavioral experiments, including SPT, OFT, and FST, indicating that NA may help in the improvement of depressive symptoms in rats. Notably, global metabolomics in brain tissue revealed that NA supplementation may have indirect effects on l-glutamate metabolism in the brain and plasma, thus positively influencing poststroke depressive behavior. Because gavage administration was used, we analyzed the gut microbiota and observed that NA intake contributed to the recovery of gut bacterial diversity and beneficial bacteria, Blautia and Sutterella, which are associated with metabolites. Considering the in situ supplementation of NA in the brain and the changes in a range of metabolites, we hypothesize that NA may exert neuroprotective effects against stroke and PSD in humans. Owing to the recent advancements in biotechnological microbial fermentation of NA via synthetic biology, its cost-effectiveness is gradually garnering attention. We believe that NA will be more comprehensively validated in food additives and clinical applications in the future. Lastly, subsequent studies using more models are warranted to determine the additional targeted functions of NA, including how NA plays an important role in situ membrane reconstruction and signal transduction and the pathological processes associated with PSD behavior.

Materials and methods

Animal Model Construction

Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (specific pathogen-free [SPF] grade), aged 6–8 weeks, were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Certificate number: SCXK [Jing] 2016-0001) (Beijing, China). Rats were housed in a controlled environment with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, temperature maintained at 22–26 °C, and humidity levels at 40%–50%. They had ad libitum access to standard chow and water throughout the experiment. All experimental procedures were conducted following the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care and the Animal Care and Use Guidelines of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. For the MCAO model procedure, rats were induced with 1% pentobarbital sodium anesthesia. A midline neck incision was made to expose the right common carotid artery. The artery was dissected from its bifurcation to the base of the skull and separated from the surrounding nerves and fascia. External carotid arterial branches were isolated, dissected, and coagulated. The internal carotid arteries were carefully isolated from the adjacent vagal nerve and the pterygopalatine artery was tied off. A 0.2 mm diameter silicone head threaded plug (Beijing Cinontech Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) was then inserted through the common carotid into the internal carotid, reaching the circle of Willis and effectively occluding the middle cerebral artery. The threaded plug was removed to allow reperfusion of blood into the internal carotid artery after 90 min of occlusion of the right middle cerebral artery.

Except for the MCAO occlusion, the Sham group underwent the same surgical procedure. Following surgery, surviving rats were randomly assigned to four groups: Sham, Model, NA (8 mg/kg, irrigation), and EGb (20 mg/kg, irrigation). The Sham and Model groups received an equal volume of carboxymethylcellulose sodium (CMC-Na) via intragastric administration for 5 d. Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta of all rats and separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Plasma samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent metabolomics assays. Meanwhile, fresh brain tissues were collected and stored at −80 °C, and sections of the brains were immediately fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for staining.

Behavioral Testing

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

Rats were individually housed, subjected to a 12-h food and water deprivation period, and subsequently provided access to a 1% sucrose solution (A) and water (B) for a 24-h duration. To prevent side preference bias, the positions of the bottles were daily switched. The SPT for each rat was calculated weekly using the following formula: 100 × [Vol A/(Vol A + Vol B)]. Each rat's sucrose consumption was normalized to its body weight [23,24].

Open-field test (OFT)

Rats were placed in an open-field box (40 × 50 × 60 cm3) and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. All test sessions were videotaped to archive the information. At the end of each testing session, the open-field box was cleaned with 70% ethanol and dried to minimize the presence of olfactory cues before the next rat was tested. The EthoVision software was used to analyze the distance traveled and velocity.

Forced swimming test (FST)

Twenty-four hours before the test, rats were individually placed in a white cylinder measuring 30 cm in height and 15 cm in diameter, filled with water at a temperature of 23–26 °C to a depth of 20 cm. The following day, rats were again placed in the same conditions, and the immobility time during the last 4 min of the 6-min FST was recorded by an observer who was blinded to the experimental conditions.

Cerebral Blood Flow (CBF)

The CBF of mice was monitored using a Laser Speckle Imaging System (RFLSI III, RWD Life Science Co., Shenzhen, China) 5 d after surgery. The ratio of the ipsilateral and contralateral areas of the average perfusion value in 3 min was used to calculate the reduction in blood flow on the ipsilateral side of the brain. To enhance the stability of blood flow data, high-intensity signals in the sinus were excluded from the calculation.

2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium Chloride Staining

Infarction volume was assessed on the 5th day using 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC, cat. #T8877; Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA). The brains were briefly cut into 2 mm thick slices. The slices were then immersed in 2% TTC solution for 20 min, 10 min on each side. Subsequently, the sections were fixed with a 4% PFA solution for 30 min at room temperature. Brain slices were digitally imaged and analyzed using ImageJ 1.8 (National Institutes of Health, Maryland, USA).

H&E Staining

After the rats were sacrificed, the brain samples were isolated, washed, and fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin for 48 h. The tissues were then sliced into 5-μm-thick sections and subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration through a series of xylene and aqueous alcohol solutions. Histological sections of the tissues were stained with H&E for pathological morphological analysis. The sections were observed under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse CI, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence Staining

Following the collection of rat brain tissue, a portion of the tissue was embedded in paraffin after immersion in a 4% formalin solution. Immunofluorescence was employed to assess the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF, Servicebio, Hubei, China) and MBP (Servicebio, Hubei, China) in rat brain tissue. The sections were examined using an Olympus BH22 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) for morphological analysis. Ultrathin sections were inspected using a Leica RM2235 (Leica, Hessian, Germany) at 80 kV.

Gut Microbiota Profiling and 16S rRNA Microbiome Sequencing

Fresh stool samples from rats, weighing 200–250 mg, were collected from the intestine and immediately frozen at −80 °C. These samples were collected to compare the composition and quantify the intestinal flora. The E.Z.N.A. Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Georgia, USA) was used to extract bacterial genomic DNA samples according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quantity and quality of the extracted DNAs were measured to ensure suitability for analysis. Sequencing of the gut microbiome was performed using paired-end 300 bp MiSeq (Illumina, California, USA) profiling of the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. The primer sequences used were as follows: 383F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Subsequently, the obtained sequencing data were analyzed using R packages and the online platform provided by Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com, Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Untargeted Metabolomics

Sample collection and preparation

Brain and blood samples were collected from rats following a period of fasting. The blood samples were collected in 5 mL Vacutainer tubes containing the chelating agent ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). These samples were then subjected to centrifugation for 10 min at 3000 rpm at 4 °C. Aliquots of 200 μL from both the plasma and brain samples were stored at −80 °C until ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time of flight-mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF/MS) analysis. For the plasma samples, a thawing step at 4 °C was followed by mixing 100 μL aliquots with 400 μL of cold methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) to precipitate proteins. The supernatant was dried using a vacuum centrifuge after centrifuging the mixture for 15 min at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C. For liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis, the dried samples were reconstituted in 100 μL of acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) solvent.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

The analysis was conducted using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph (Exion LC 30AD, AB SCIEX, Massachusetts, USA) coupled with a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (ZenoTOF 7600, AB SCIEX, Massachusetts, USA). All sample separation was performed on a 2.1 mm × 150 mm Kinetex F5 2.6 μm column (Phenomenex, California, USA). On both electrospray ionization (ESI) positive and negative modes, the mobile phase consisted of A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient started at 2% B and sustained for 2 min, then linearly increased to 98% at 15 min and kept for 2 min, followed by a decrease to 2% at 17.1 min with a 3 min re-equilibration period. The flow rate of the gradient was set at 0.3 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. Each sample was injected with a 2 μL aliquot. Mass spectrometry parameters included a TOF MS acquisition with a mass/charge (m/z) range of 60–1200 Da, an accumulation time of 0.15 s, and automated MS/MS acquisition within the m/z range of 40–1200 Da with an accumulation time of 0.03 s. TOF MS/MS scans were acquired using information-dependent acquisition (IDA) in high sensitivity mode with dynamic background subtraction (DBS) and Zeno trap setting, fifteen candidate ions were monitored per cycle in IDA mode, and the collision energy (CE) was fixed at 35 V with ±15 eV for the TOF MS/MS spectrum. Decluttering potential (DP) is set at 40 V for TOF MS and TOF MS/MS. Ion source and gas parameters for both negative and positive modes: spray voltage is −4500 V/5500 V; source temperature is 550 °C; the nebulizing gas (GS1) is 55 psi; the heating gas (GS2) is 55 psi; curtain gas is 35 psi and collision gas (CAD gas) is 9 psi. Metabolites were identified by comparing accurate m/z values and MS/MS spectra with authentic standards in our in-house database. After normalization to total peak intensity, univariate statistical analyses were performed using the package in R to identify differential metabolites. Metabolites were considered differentially expressed if log2 (fold change [FC]) > 1.5 or log2 (FC) < 0.7 with a P value < 0.05. MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) was used to conduct metabolic pathway enrichment analysis in order to investigate the biological functions related to the differential metabolites.

Transcriptomics Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

Total RNAs were extracted from the rat's brain and subjected to a transcriptome sequencing library. According to the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-Seq Library Prepkit for Illumina (NR 604-01/02, Vazyme, Jiangsu, China) was selected to establish the database. The mRNA is purified using Oligo (dT) magnetic beads that exclusively bind poly A tails. Subsequently, the mRNA is employed as a template to synthesize the first cDNA strand using six-base random hexamers. Thereafter, the RNA template strand is degraded by RNaseH. The double-stranded cDNA was purified and underwent terminal repair, A-tail addition, sequencing linker connection, fragment size selection, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification in order to generate the final cDNA library. The RNA concentration of the library was measured using the Qubit® RNA Assay Kit in Qubit® 3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) for preliminary quantification. After the library was qualified, sample pooling was performed according to the effective concentration of the library and the target required data volume, sequencing was performed using the Illumina platform (Illumina, California, USA), and the PE150 sequencing strategy was run to obtain 150 bp double-ended sequencing reads.

Determination of the NA Content by Gas Chromatography (GC)

To analyze the fatty acid profile in the brain and plasma, 0.1g of brain tissue and 100 μL of plasma were collected. The samples were placed in a freeze-dryer and freeze-dried overnight, and their dry weight was determined. The lyophilized samples were mixed with chloroform and methanol (v/v = 2:1) and then shocked at 45 °C for 3 h. Then, KCl (0.9%) was added to the samples, which were centrifuged at 4000 g for 5 min. The bottom layer was transferred to a glass tube and weighed after drying with nitrogen gas. The lipid was added to n-hexane and sulphuric acid methanol (2%), and samples were placed in a baking oven for methyl esterification at 85 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the samples were removed and cooled on ice, and KCl (0.9%) was added. Then, samples were vortexed thoroughly and centrifuged at 4000 g for 5 min. Finally, the upper organic layer was taken for GC, which was performed using a GC system (7890A, Agilent Technologies, Inc., California, USA). INNOWAX (30 m × 320 μm × 0.25 μm) was selected as the chromatographic column. In the GC analysis program, an inlet temperature of 250 °C and an injection volume of 1 μL were employed. The temperature program settings were 120 °C for 5 min, linear ascension at 3.5 °C/min to 240 °C, and a constant temperature for 10 min. N2 was utilized as the carrier gas with a speed of 28.5 mL/min and a split ratio of 10:1 (v/v).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS software (version 21.0, Illinois, USA) and Origin Pro 8 software (Origin Lab Corporation, Massachusetts, USA). The statistical significance level was set at P < 0.05. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and multiple hypothesis testing were used to compare continuous variables between the study groups. Spearman's rank correlation was employed to measure correlations. Hierarchical clustering utilized the unweighted pair group method with an arithmetic mean. White's non-parametric t-test analysis was conducted, and P values were corrected for false discovery rate using the Benjamini/Hochberg method to identify characteristic fecal taxa in different groups. Further significant details can be found in the figure legends.
