Preoperative Computed Tomography-Based Prediction of Device Size for Lumbar Stabilization with Interspinous Process Device for Lumbar Spinal Canal Stenosis
Authors: Taisuke Yoshinaga, Kyongsong Kim, Toyohiko Isu, Takato Tajiri, Fumiaki Fujihara, Juntaro Matsumoto, Masanori Isobe, Hiroshi Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction Lumbar stabilization using an interspinous process device is a surgical option for lumbar spinal canal stenosis. This study retrospectively evaluated the relationship between preoperative computed tomography (CT) measurements and inserted device size. Methods A total of 21 consecutive patients who underwent lumbar stabilization with an interspinous process device (Swift systemⓇ) for one- or two-level lumbar spinal canal stenosis were included. The mean age of the patients was 73.2 years. Surgery involved one level in seven patients and two levels in 14 patients. On preoperative CT images, the interspinous process distance was measured at the posterior line of the bilateral facet joints (basal distance) and at a point 8 mm dorsal (dorsal distance), corresponding to the Swift systemⓇ diameter. Results The mean basal and dorsal distances were 6.5±2.1 mm and 5.3±2.4 mm, respectively. In 33 levels (93.5%) with a basal distance ≥4 mm, the device was successfully inserted between the spinous process bases. In the two levels with a basal distance of 2 mm, the device could not be inserted at the base and was placed dorsally. Except for these two cases, the size of the inserted device significantly correlated with the basal distance (p<0.01, r=0.694). Conclusions The basal interspinous distance on preoperative CT was useful for predicting the appropriate device size. The device could be inserted between the spinous processes in all cases with a basal distance of ≥4 mm.

Introduction

Lumbar spinal canal stenosis is a degenerative disease that causes intermittent claudication due to lower limb symptoms. When conservative treatment fails, lumbar decompression and/or fusion surgery is considered. Another surgical option is lumbar stabilization using an interspinous process device that limits excessive lumbar extension and alleviates symptoms arising from neural compression, which typically improve when patients with lumbar spinal stenosis flex forward1,2). Multiple studies have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of this technique1-9). In Japan, the X-STOP interspinous spacer (Medtronic; Minneapolis, MN, USA) was previously used; however, it was discontinued because of a high rate of device-related complications and reoperations.

A novel interspinous spacer (Swift systemⓇ; Ammtec Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was developed in Japan in 2020. Its flat surface provides a large contact area that reduces load on the spinous processes and minimizes fracture risk. Favorable short-term surgical outcomes using this device have been previously reported10). Predicting the appropriate implant size based on preoperative imaging may assist in surgical planning. Therefore, in this study, we retrospectively evaluated the relationship between preoperative computed tomography (CT) findings and the inserted device.

Materials and Methods

Between March 2024 and June 2025, lumbar stabilization using an interspinous process device (Swift systemⓇ; Ammtec Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was performed in 21 patients with one- or two-level lumbar spinal canal stenosis. The study included 21 consecutive patients (10 men and 11 women) with a mean age of 73.2±8.8 years (range, 48-90 years). Surgery involved one level in seven patients and two levels in 14 patients (L1/2 in one, L2/3 in four, L3/4 in seven, L4/5 in 15, and L5/S in eight). Height and weight of the patients were measured upon admission; body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight of a patient divided by the square of their height (kg/m2).

Indications included intermittent claudication due to lower extremity neurological symptoms associated with lumbar spinal canal stenosis, with mild or no symptoms at rest. Patients with severe resting leg pain, paralysis, or bladder and rectal dysfunction were excluded, as were those with acute vertebral fracture, pseudoarthrosis from chronic vertebral fracture, vertebral metastasis, or vertebral infection10).

Surgery was primarily performed under general anesthesia, although local anesthesia was an option depending on patient preference. In this study, procedures were conducted in the prone position under general (n=18) or local anesthesia (n=3). A midline skin incision was made at the surgical level to expose the spinous processes (approximately 3 cm for one-level and 5 cm for two-level surgeries). Paraspinal muscles adjacent to the spinous processes were gently dissected to create space for spacer placement. The supraspinous ligaments were preserved, and the spacer was inserted between the target vertebral spinous processes10,11). Spacer size was determined intraoperatively using trial spacers inserted in 1-mm increments. Mild tension of the interspinous ligaments was often observed on insertion of the spacer during surgery; thus, the interspinous space may have been slightly dilated unintentionally. The device was placed as close as possible to the base of the spinous processes. A surgical drain was not inserted. Postoperative activity was unrestricted, and all patients wore a soft corset for approximately 1 month.

Image measurements were performed using a picture archiving and communication system by two authors (TY and TI). Because the spacer cannot be inserted anterior to the bilateral facet joints, the interspinous process distance was measured on sagittal CT images of the lumbar spine at the posterior line of the bilateral facet joints (basal distance) (Figure 1). In addition, as the Swift systemⓇ (Ammtec Inc., Tokyo, Japan) has a diameter of 8 mm, the interspinous process distance was also measured 8 mm dorsally from the posterior line of the bilateral facet joints (dorsal distance) (Figure 1). In measurements for cases with advanced facet degeneration, the spur was not included in the facet, and measurements were obtained at the posterior margin of the facet (Figure 2). The size of the Swift systemⓇ inserted for each patient was compared with the CT measurements described above. Interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). A two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement [ICC(2,2)] was used.

Surgical outcomes were evaluated using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) for leg symptoms and the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores before surgery and at the final postoperative follow-up (mean, 11.5±4.4 months; range, 6-19 months).

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Pre- and postoperative scores were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All data are expressed as the mean±standard deviation, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman's rank correlation analysis was used to assess relationships between the inserted spacer size and CT measurements, as well as between the flexion range of the insertion level and the difference between basal distances and inserted device size. Correlation coefficients (r) were calculated, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

All procedures involving human participants in this study were conducted in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects (Provisional Translation as of March 2015) and its later amendments, as well as with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its later revisions or comparable ethical standards. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution (approval code: 21219).

Results

The patients had a mean height was 158±11.3 cm and a mean BMI of 24.7±4.3 kg/m2 (Table 1). In all cases, the Swift systemⓇ was successfully placed between the spinous processes. No surgery-related complications occurred. The spacer diameter was 6 mm in 19 cases, 7 mm in nine cases, 8 mm in six cases, and 10 mm in one case. In 33 levels (93.5%) across 19 patients, the spacer was appropriately inserted at the base of the spinous processes. In two levels (L3/4 and L4/5) of two patients, it could not be inserted at the base and was placed dorsally. NRS for leg symptoms and JOA scores significantly improved after surgery: the NRS for leg symptoms decreased from 7.5±2.1 to 1.2±1.4 (p<0.05) and the JOA score increased from 17.8±2.8 to 25.5±2.4 (p<0.05).

The interobserver reliability was excellent for the basal distance (ICC=0.90; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.81 to 0.95) and was good for the 8-mm distance (ICC=0.96; 95% CI, 0.83 to 0.98). Regarding preoperative CT measurements, the mean basal interspinous distance was 6.5±2.1 mm (range, 2-13 mm). Mean distances at each level are listed in Table 2. In both cases with a 2-mm basal distance, the device could not be inserted into the base and was positioned dorsally. In all other cases with a basal distance ≥4 mm (no case measured 3 mm), the device was successfully inserted between the spinous process bases (Figure 3). The mean dorsal interspinous distance was 5.3±2.4 mm (range, 2-14 mm), and level-specific values are also shown in Table 2. This group included three cases with a 2-mm and four cases with a 3-mm distance. Except for the two cases with a 2-mm basal distance, the device was inserted in the correct position when the basal distance was ≥4 mm.

Except for the two dorsally placed cases, the inserted device size (mean, 6.8±1.0 mm) closely matched the basal interspinous distance (mean, 6.8±1.8 mm). The mean difference was −0.1±1.2 mm (range, −3 to +2 mm; 95% CI, −2.37 to +2.25). A significant correlation was observed between these two variables (p<0.01, r=0.694) (Figure 4). In contrast, the mean difference between the dorsal interspinous distances and device sizes was 1.3±1.9 mm (range, −4 to +5 mm; 95% CI, −2.53 to +5.08), and no significant correlation was found (p=0.065, r=0.325) (Figure 3). Furthermore, no significant relationship was observed between the flexion range at the insertion level and the difference between basal distance and inserted device size (p=0.161, r=0.250).

In contrast, at the dorsal region, the mean difference was 1.3±1.9 mm (range, −4 to +5 mm; 95% CI, −2.53 to +5.08), showing greater variability and no significant correlation (p=0.065, r=0.325).

Discussion

In this study, the Swift systemⓇ was successfully inserted at the base between the spinous processes in 93.5% of levels. However, in two cases (one level each at L3/4 and L4/5), the device could not be placed at the base and was positioned dorsally. The distance between the spinous process bases on preoperative CT significantly correlated with the size of the inserted device, and the device was successfully positioned in all cases with a distance of ≥4 mm. Furthermore, the Bland–Altman plot demonstrated an agreement rate of 32/33 levels (97%) within a ±2 mm range.

In this study, preoperative measurements were based on CT images obtained in the supine position, whereas surgery was performed in the prone position. Previous magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based studies have reported that lumbar lordosis increases significantly when patients are in the prone position compared with that in the supine position12). However, the effect of positional change on the interspinous process distance has not been sufficiently addressed. In a previous report measuring the interspinous process distance using MRI and X-ray13), values for L2/3 ranged from 6.3±3.6 to 11.8±3.8 mm, for L3/4 ranged from 4.9±2.5 to 10.0±3.6 mm, and for L4/5 ranged from 4.4±2.4 to 7.9±3.1 mm. Lin et al.14) evaluated interspinous spaces at three points and analyzed spinous process morphology in patients undergoing surgery for lumbar spinal canal or foraminal stenosis. They reported that the ventral portion of the interspinous space is most suitable for device placement because it is wider and has a thicker spinous process cortex. They also suggested that dorsal placement may increase the risk of device extrusion as the dorsal interspinous space is narrower.

In our experience, the device cannot be inserted anterior to the posterior facet line due to anatomical constraints. Therefore, our goal is to place the spacer at the spinous process base, adjacent to the posterior facet joint, to prevent device dislocation and to achieve stable fixation. In this study, insertion was possible in all cases where the basal interspinous distance was ≥4 mm. This finding may serve as a valuable preoperative indicator for predicting technical feasibility and stability in this surgical approach.

Nelson et al.15) retrospectively examined device sizing in patients who underwent implantation of the Superion interspinous device. Interspinous process space measurements obtained at the junction of the lamina and spinous process using preoperative MRI (11.2±1.4 mm) and intraoperative assessment (11.4±1.2 mm) did not differ significantly, and the interspinous process space strongly correlated with device size (median, 10 mm; range, 8-14 mm; r=0.807). They concluded that preoperative imaging can predict device size. However, MRI-based measurements may be influenced by soft tissues, whereas CT provides clearer osseous delineation and may therefore yield more reliable measurements.

In our study, we similarly investigated whether the inserted device size could be predicted from preoperative CT images. The inserted device size closely matched the basal interspinous distance (mean difference, −0.1±1.2 mm). Device size significantly correlated with basal distance but not with the 8-mm dorsal distance or the flexion range at the surgical level. These findings suggest that the interspinous distance along the posterior line of the bilateral facets is a key parameter for preoperative planning.

In two cases, the device was placed dorsally because the basal interspinous space was narrow. Although creating additional space in flexion using distraction instruments may be possible, oversizing can lead to spinous process fracture or postoperative pain caused by excessive distraction of the supraspinous ligament or spinous processes16-18). Therefore, we opted for dorsal placement. Both cases achieved satisfactory surgical outcomes without implant dislocation. Nevertheless, as the sample size was small and the follow-up was short, further research is needed to determine whether dorsal positioning provides comparable long-term stability. In this cohort, the mean interspinous distance was 6.8±1.8 mm and the median spacer size was 6 mm, values smaller than those reported previously13). Because the Swift systemⓇ is smaller than devices such as X-STOP and Superion, our findings may be particularly relevant to surgical planning involving this implant.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. It was a retrospective study with a small sample size. Our surgical strategy avoids aggressive distraction of the interspinous space, which may differ from techniques employing posture correction or adjunctive distraction devices. In this study, the analysis was based on preoperative CT images and postoperative day 1 CT images. To evaluate subsidence of the spinous processes associated with the Swift systemⓇ more accurately, serial follow-up CT examinations would be needed over time. However, owing to concerns regarding radiation exposure, routine repeat CT scans were not feasible and were therefore not performed in this retrospective study. Furthermore, the mean follow-up period was relatively short. Although clinical outcomes were favorable, longer-term follow-up is necessary to evaluate delayed complications such as device migration, spinous process erosion, or loss of effectiveness.

Conclusions

In this study, the Swift systemⓇ for lumbar stabilization was successfully inserted at the base between the spinous processes in 93.5% of levels. The basal interspinous distance measured on preoperative CT was a reliable indicator for predicting appropriate device size, and insertion was possible in all cases with a basal distance of ≥4 mm.
