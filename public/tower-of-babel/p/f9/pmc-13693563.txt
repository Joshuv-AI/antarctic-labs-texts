Assessment of Immune and Proteomic Markers in Exhaled Breath Condensate: A Feasibility Study
Authors: Evi De Ryck, Eline Verscheure, Vivi Schlünssen, Anne Mette Lund Würtz, Rob Stierum, Sebastien Carpentier, Steven Ronsmans, Anjoeka Pronk, Lode Godderis, Manosij Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exhaled breath condensate (EBC) is a promising matrix for biomarker characterization that reflects the condition of the respiratory system. We assessed the practical feasibility of marker characterization in EBC, including quantification, comparison with plasma and saliva levels, and repeated sampling after six months, focusing on immune markers and proteomics. We measured several immune markers twice, six months apart, in EBC, saliva, and plasma, including cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases, and untargeted proteomics in EBC. α-Amylase activity was almost 10,000-fold lower in EBC than in saliva, suggesting at most limited saliva contamination during EBC collection. Generally, chemokine and eicosanoid levels were lower and more often below the limit of quantification in EBC versus plasma and saliva. Participants were healthy; still, EBC markers could be quantified in ≥50% of samples, except interleukin 12p70 and thromboxane B2. Correlations across matrices were often low, supporting EBC’s matrix-specific properties. EBC proteomic analysis identified up to 209 proteins, several related to lung function. This study highlights EBC as a promising non-invasive method for respiratory biomarker discovery and demonstrates the practical feasibility of detecting immune and proteomic markers in EBC. Additionally, we showed that the EBC proteome can be generated on single EBC samples.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are responsible for 41 million deaths yearly, representing almost three-quarters of all deaths globally. As one of the “big four” NCDs, chronic respiratory diseases like asthma and COPD are annually responsible for over 4 million deaths globally, marking them as a leading cause of death. A combination of occupational, environmental, and behavioral exposures underlies the development of these diseases. , With more than 200 occupational exposures identified with consistent or limited evidence for associations with chronic respiratory disease, many individuals may be at risk. Yet most occupational non-communicable respiratory diseases are preventable with proper safety measures and effective control strategies. ,

Internally, this wide variety of associated environmental and occupational exposures results in several responses. To measure the exposures or their internal responses, minimally invasive and non-invasive sampling has been promoted to allow frequent monitoring, essential for prevention and early detection of biological indicators of disease. In the field of occupational health, minimally invasive sampling is needed to be compatible with the diversity of work floors, where the collection, handling, and storage of invasive materials are challenging. Additionally, workers might not be eager to provide invasive samples.

To foster the implementation of non-invasive sampling, our group previously validated the use of dried blood spots in occupational setting. For respiratory conditions, exhaled breath condensate (EBC) has gained popularity. By sampling pulmonary epithelial lining fluid, EBC is a non-invasive method offering potential for biomarker-based exposure and respiratory health monitoring. EBC has, as non-invasive sampling method, many advantages, including less participant discomfort, higher user acceptance, and increased participation rate, and it is generally cost-effective. , Additionally, EBC can be collected from participants of all ages and via self-sampling. Together with its non-invasive nature this allows for easy, repeated, or longitudinal sampling making it an effective and accessible monitoring method. −

Existing studies on EBC have largely focused on a limited number of markers. ,, Immune markers are among the most extensively studied markers in EBC. ,, Immune responses play a critical role in the development of respiratory diseases, making immune markers in EBC particularly relevant for early detection and health surveillance in occupational settings. EBC is considered a valuable matrix to assess oxidative stress and lung inflammation. , Although immune responses are typically nonspecific, protein profiles may offer greater specificity for specific exposures or health effects and represent a complementary approach worth exploring in EBC. For biomarker discovery, an unbiased assessment, such as untargeted proteomics, is also important. By now, several studies have explored the broader EBC proteome; however, many used pooled EBC samples to overcome the low protein concentrations. − Pooling samples increases overall protein content, but inherently does not allow one to evaluate individual proteomic profiles, disregarding inter-individual variability.

This study was undertaken within the framework of the Exposome Project for Health and Occupational Research (EPHOR) project, focusing specifically on the impact of the working-life exposome in relation to lung function and obstructive lung disease. The main aim of this study is to assess whether EBC reflects airway-specific immune and proteomic signatures. Specifically, we aim to investigate to what extent immune markers are detectable in EBC and how their levels compare with those measured in plasma and saliva. Second, we evaluate untargeted proteomic profiling in individual EBC samples and evaluate what biological pathways the identified proteins represent. Finally, we explore if the proteomic profile of EBC associates with lung function parameters, suggesting relevance for respiratory (patho)­physiology. Our hypothesis is that EBC contains a measurable and distinct immune marker profile compared to plasma and saliva. We hypothesize that untargeted proteomics can identify biologically meaningful proteins in individual, non-pooled EBC samples. Finally, we hypothesize that the selected EBC markers explain more variance in lung function than markers in saliva or plasma.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

We recruited healthy working participants, mostly researchers, aged 18–65 years (n = 19) in Belgium. Written informed consent was obtained upon recruitment. This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol, including all procedures involving human subjects, was approved by the UZ/KU Leuven Committee for Medical Ethics (S64599).

Samples were collected from November 2021 to June 2022. Upon recruitment, a general questionnaire was used to collect information on demographics, health status, and lifestyle. Saliva, blood, EBC samples, and lung function measurements (spirometry) were collected twice, at timepoint 1 (T 1) and timepoint 2 (T 2), approximately six months apart (Figure ).

Study set-up. Healthy participants were recruited and signed informed consent. Questionnaire data was collected at each timepoint. Spirometry measurements, EBC, saliva, and plasma were collected twice, six months apart. T 1: timepoint 1; T 2: timepoint 2.

Sample Collection

Biological samples were collected according to the EPHOR standard operating procedure, available at RDR KU Leuven. Participants were asked to refrain from eating, drinking (except water), smoking, and brushing their teeth 1 h before their lab visit. Upon arrival, first, the lung function measurements were taken.

Lung function (spirometry) was measured by using the Microlab Spirometer MK8 (Vyaire Medical, USA). At each of the respective timepoints within the approximately six month time window, at least three measurements were taken, and the best measurement was selected by the software and used for further analysis. The quality of the spirometry graphs was reviewed by a clinical practitioner.

Next, EBC, saliva, and blood were collected in the mentioned order. EBC was collected using the Medivac Turbo DECCS System, Turbo 14 (Medivac, Italy), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equipment temperature was set to −5 °C. Participants performed tidal breathing for 15 min. Because of differences in biological marker levels between mouth and nose breathing, participants were asked to inhale and exhale through the mouth during EBC collection, and were provided with a nose clip to prevent nasal in-/exhalation. EBC was stored at −80 °C.

Saliva was collected using Cotton Salivette Cortisol (51.1534.500, Sarstedt, Germany) in a standardized manner according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After rinsing the mouth with tap water, participants chewed the swab for 1–2 min before placing it back in the tube. After collection, samples were stored in the fridge (2–8 °C) until being centrifuged (2000g, 5 min), after which saliva was stored at −80 °C.

Blood was collected by phlebotomy in Vacutainer EDTA tubes according to WHO guidelines (2010). Blood was centrifuged (2000g, 10 min), and plasma was stored at −80 °C.

Sample Analyses

Immune Markers

Pulmonary diseases are often linked to immune alterations and inflammation. Immune markers in EBC are of particular interest as early indicators for lung-related diseases, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Cytokines are among the most studied biomarkers of effect across many diseases. Further, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) have been previously associated with angiogenesis and immunomodulation in asthma. Finally, eicosanoids are a class of lipid mediators. Leukotriene B4 (LTB4) is a potent neutrophil chemoattractant but also stimulates macrophages to produce interleukin (IL)­1β and IL6, and has been associated with cystic fibrosis and asthma. Cysteinyl leukotrienes (CysLTs) contribute to allergic responses and bronchial asthma by regulating vasodilation, mucus secretion, smooth muscle contraction, and recruiting eosinophils. Elevated levels of CysLTs in sputum have been found in asthmatics. Due to the previous shown lung related changes in respiratory disease, these markers were selected for validation in EBC. The concentration of several immune markers was assessed in EBC, plasma, and saliva. Interferon-γ (IFNγ), IL1β, IL2, IL4, IL6, IL8, IL10, IL12p70, IL13, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) were measured using the multiplex MSD V-PLEX Proinflammatory Panel 1 (human) Kit (#K15049D, Meso Scale Diagnostics, USA). E-selectin, MMP2, MMP7, MMP9 (total), P-selectin, and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 1/fms-related receptor tyrosine kinase 1 (VEGFR1/Flt1) were measured with the multiplex MSD U-PLEX Metastasis Combo 1 (human) Kit (#K15602K, Meso Scale Diagnostics, USA). MMP1 was measured using the MSD U-PLEX Human MMP-1 Assay (#K151AHDK, Meso Scale Diagnostics, USA). CysLTs, LTB4, and thromboxane B2 (TXB2) were measured with the Cysteinyl Leukotriene ELISA Kit (#500390), Leukotriene B4 ELISA Kit (#502390), and Thromboxane B2 ELISA Kit (#501020; Cayman Chemical, USA), respectively, for EBC and saliva samples. EBC was used undiluted, and plasma and saliva were diluted 2-fold.

Total Protein Measurement and Untargeted Proteomics

Total protein content was measured with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (cat no. 23225 and 23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For untargeted proteomics, EBC was lyophilized, then dissolved in 8M urea. Next, the samples were alkylated and digested according to the iodo dithiothreitol (DTT)-trypsin digestion from the liquid sample (version 20191205) of Sybioma, KU Leuven (Supporting Information, Appendix 1). This methodology is hereafter referred to as proteomics with a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) workflow.

Because of the low number of proteins identified using the DDA workflow, we tested an alternative protocol, further termed proteomics with S-Trap and data-independent acquisition (DIA) workflow. Because the remaining volume of the twelve paired EBC samples was insufficient to reanalyze for untargeted proteomics methods 1 and 2, another set of four EBC samples was analyzed with both methods 1 and 2 to allow comparison of identified proteins. These EBC samples were from four participants who participated in the second collection. Proteomics methodology is further described in the Supporting Information, Appendix 1.

α-Amylase Activity

α-Amylase activity was measured using the Amylase Activity Assay Kit (MAK009, Merck, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol in EBC and saliva. For EBC, undiluted samples were used. For saliva, samples were measured in two dilutions: 1:50 and 1:200. If one dilution showed an activity measure below the detection limit, we used the α-amylase activity measure for the other dilution. If both dilutions showed quantifiable activities, they were averaged.

Data Analysis and Statistics

For immune markers in EBC, percentages of values within the detection range were between 38% and 100% (Supporting Information, Appendix 2). Values below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) were imputed by replacing them with a random value between the lowest quantified detected value over timepoints for that specific matrix and its half, to preserve variability and to keep values biologically relevant.

Correlations were assessed between immune markers in different matrices by using Spearman correlation coefficients, as the normality criterion was not fulfilled by the majority of the markers. Additionally, to investigate the intra-individual biological variation in immune marker levels with six months between the collection moments, we compared the immune marker levels.

Protein tissue expression was assessed by using STRING v12 (https://string-db.org/). Enrichment analyses involved using OmicsNet (https://www.omicsnet.ca/). InnateDB was selected as the database, the “Add edges only” box was checked, Minimum Network was applied, and Tissue Filter was set to the lung based on the Genotype-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx).

Explorative associations of marker levels with lung function were investigated with partial least-squares (PLS) using the mixOmics R package. We explored marker fold changes across the six month period in relation to fold change in FEV1/FVC. One model was run, including all targeted markers, excluding markers with >50% missing (TXB2 and IL12p70 in EBC were excluded), and a separate model for the proteins identified with the proteomics DDA approach.

Data analysis involved using R version 4.3.3 (R Core Team 2022). Figures were created with GraphPad 11.0.1 (USA). All statistical tests were performed two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

In total, nineteen individuals participated in the study at at least one timepoint. For all participants, α-amylase activity was assessed in available samples of EBC and saliva (n = 19). Immune markers and proteomics were assessed in T 1 and T 2 samples for participants with available EBC, saliva, and plasma samples for both timepoints (n = 12; 7 men; mean age 33.25 ± 10.67 years; mean BMI 23.55 ± 4.16 kg/m2). Two of these participants were current smokers, and two were of non-European origin. The participants typically drank one to two standard drinks of alcohol two to four times per week, on average (Supporting Information, Appendix 3).

α-Amylase Activity

As a first step in our protocol optimization for EBC collection and analysis, α-amylase levels were used to determine the presence of salivary contamination of EBC samples. Out of all EBC samples (n = 32, of which 14 paired), most samples (n = 29) had α-amylase activity below the assays LLOD. Three EBC samples had quantifiable α-amylase activity (mean 0.73 ± 0.64 mU/mL (range 0.21–1.44)). In saliva (n = 32, timepoint-matched to EBC), for 30 samples, at least one saliva dilution was above the LLOD. The mean α-amylase activity was 6.74 ± 4.66 U/mL (range: 0.57–20.16). For two samples, both dilutions were below the LLOD.

To investigate the potential cross-matrix inconsistency, we examined correlations of immune marker levels between matrices (Supporting Information, Appendix 3). Between EBC and saliva, VEGFR1/Flt1 levels were significantly correlated (correlation coefficients: 0.56, p < 0.05). Additionally, also E-selectin (0.58), MMP7 (0.50), and CysLTs (−0.41) showed substantial correlation coefficients. Between EBC and plasma, IL10 levels were significantly correlated (0.68, p < 0.05), and IL1β and MMP2 levels were correlated between saliva and plasma (0.58, p < 0.05; −0.55, p < 0.01). Overall, correlation magnitudes between EBC and saliva and EBC and plasma were for multiple markers below 10%. This observed divergence in EBC relative to the others may indicate matrix-specific characteristics.

To assess the variability in marker levels, we determined the fold change of immune markers between T 1 and T 2 (Supporting Information, Appendix 3). For none of the assessed markers, levels significantly changed over the six month period, which suggests that the assessed markers might be relatively stable markers suitable for biomarker assessment.

EBC Proteins (Untargeted Protein Analysis)

Apart from immune markers, the EBC also contains other proteins that could be of interest. The total protein content in EBC samples was too low to be detected using the selected standard BCA assay. Therefore, we relied on an untargeted proteomic analysis for characterizing EBC peptides.

Untargeted proteomics in EBC using the DDA workflow resulted in the detection of 29,606 peptides; 179 could be linked to 28 different proteins involved in several biological processes, originating from different cellular components, with different molecular functions (Supporting Information, Appendix 4).

Because of the low number of proteins identified with the DDA workflow, we used an alternative method for proteomic analysis with the S-Trap DIA workflow. We compared the number of identified proteins in four other EBC samples of sufficient volume by using the DDA workflow and the S-Trap DIA workflow method. Use of the DDA workflow revealed a mean of 135 ± 23 peptides, mapping to a mean of 17 ± 1 proteins (range: 16–18), and the S-Trap DIA workflow revealed a mean of 1114 ± 48 peptides, mapping to a mean of 198 ± 12 proteins (range 183–209). Overlap in identified Gene IDs showed 20 of 25 IDs with DDA workflow also identified with S-Trap DIA workflow, which suggests that the latter is mainly an extension of the proteins identified with the DDA workflow (Supporting Information, Appendix 4).

Tissue expression of proteins revealed respiratory-tract-based origins, such as larynx, pharynx, and bronchial epithelial cells (BECs) (Figure ). We displayed identified proteins in a network showing the interactions between them.

Spirometry and Associations with Protein Evolution

The lung function of participants was measured using spirometry. To explore associations between changes in EBC marker levels and changes in FEV1/FVC ratio, a PLS model was constructed for their fold increase (Supporting Information, Appendix 5). To explore the marker contributions, only the main component was investigated. First, PLS was run for targeted markers, for which the main component explained 69.23% of the variance in the markers. PLS showed that fold change in MMP1 and E-selectin in EBC was in the top five markers having the greatest covariance for FEV1/FVC fold change for this population. Additionally, IL10 and CysLTs had higher covariance for lung function changes when measured in EBC, compared to plasma and saliva, which is in line with the hypothesis that EBC samples represent the pulmonary status more than plasma or saliva samples. A PLS for the proteins identified using the DDA approach, with the main component explaining 70.13% of variance in FEV1/FVC, showed that HKVRG2 and H6VRF8 (keratin type II cytoskeletal 1 (KRT1)), P31151 (S100-A7), and P05109 (keratin type I cuticular Ha2 (KRT32)) among the EBC proteins showed the largest covariance in FEV1/FVC ratio.

Discussion

Although several studies assessed immune markers in EBC, saliva, or plasma, studies comparing matrices and markers are scarce and have often included a limited set of markers. While prior EBC research has largely examined disease populations ,, or specific exposures, , so far, studies in occupational settings are limited and are largely focused on exposure biomonitoring, leaving internal molecular alterations insufficiently explored. , Additionally, longitudinal designs investigating the associations of EBC markers and lung function are limited. In this study, we showed the practical feasibility of measuring targeted immune markers, including several cytokines, a selection of selectins, MMPs, and eicosanoids, in addition to untargeted proteomics, showing the presence of up to 209 proteins. As several of these proteins have been reported before to be associated with lung-specific disorders, often in more invasive samples, our study confirms that it is feasible to employ EBC-targeted analysis and proteomics in the future for studying occupational respiratory diseases. Finally, we showed the reliability of EBC immune markers and proteomics by looking at six month fold change and by determining that saliva contamination during EBC collection is at most limited.

Even minor saliva contamination during EBC collection could greatly influence levels of quantified markers , and, therefore, previous studies have used α-amylase activity as an indicator of potential saliva contamination. , In the present study, α-amylase activity was 10,000-fold lower in EBC than in saliva samples, indicating that saliva contamination was at most limited. The same α-amylase ratio was seen previously by Huszár et al., showing consistency across studies. The collection device we used is equipped with a saliva trap, but whether the α-amylase activity, detectable in only a small number of EBC samples, results from pulmonary tissue or passing through the oral cavity remains uncertain. α-Amylase activity in saliva in this study was lower than in similar other studies. ,

We showed the practical feasibility of assessing immune markers in EBC, although in general, more values were below the LLOD in EBC than in other matrices. Previous research showed similar results. Protein concentrations in EBC were low and showed differences in magnitude as compared with plasma and saliva, as previously reported. , This observation is not surprising because EBC is considered to be highly diluted and therefore contains low protein concentrations. , Cytokine levels in EBC are often near the LOD of the assay, reducing precision and accuracy; however, levels were quantifiable in at least 50% of our EBC samples, except for IL12p70 and TXB2. Suggestions for improving detection of markers could include utilizing assays with superior analytical sensitivity. Additionally, utilizing a different collection device, temperature upon collection to minimize degradation, or deep breaths during collection , to increase analyte levels have been suggested to improve the low detection rates of certain analytes.

IL-8 levels in EBC in this study were consistent with those in previous studies, and IL-1β and IL-8 levels were higher in saliva than in EBC, as shown by Ichikawa et al. While the latter showed higher cytokine levels in EBC than saliva after total protein correction, this opposite effect after total protein correction was not seen in this study.

Alterations in the VEGFR1/Flt1 pathway have been well established in asthma and COPD. , VEGF and VEGFR1 are involved in airway remodeling and angiogenesis. , VEGFR1 has been shown to induce MMP9, , and sputum MMP1, MMP2, and MMP9 have also been shown to differentiate between asthma and COPD. MMPs are involved in development, cell trafficking, and wound healing in the respiratory tract, and their dysregulation plays a role in the pathophysiology of many lung diseases. While MMP9 in EBC has been the focus of investigation, − this study is among the first to look at MMP1, MMP2, MMP7, VEGFR1/Flt1, and E- and P-selectins in EBC. These markers had detection levels in the range 63–100% of samples, suggesting the chosen methods are sensitive enough for detecting these markers in EBC. The demonstrated feasibility of these approaches enables further research opportunities, e.g., to assess these markers in EBC in disease populations or populations with specific exposures. E-selectin and MMP7 levels were of similar magnitude in EBC, plasma, and saliva. MMP7 levels in EBC in this study were lower than previously reported in children, and similar levels of magnitude were seen in plasma and saliva. The latter was not the case for MMP1, 2, or 9. A potential hypothesis for this specific pattern is MMP7’s relatively small molecular weight compared to other MMPs. Therefore, it might be transported more easily through cells and with passing air. Notably, EBC MMP7 levels are in line with previously reported bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) levels. MMP1 in BALF has been shown to be increased in emphysema, and MMP1 has been associated with the prognosis of lung carcinoma, suggesting that its detection in EBC could be a useful non-invasive biomarker for lung disease. Mean total MMP9 level in EBC was 4.52 and 4.25 pg/mL at the two timepoints, similar to concentrations reported previously.

Eicosanoids TXB2, LTB4, and CysLTs (LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4) in plasma or serum have been studied in lung-related diseases; however, eicosanoids in serum and urine are thought to reflect inflammation on a systemic rather than pulmonary level, as for many other markers. IL12p70 and TXB2 levels in EBC were frequently below the detection threshold, which indicates their low abundance in EBC from this healthy population. The low eicosanoid levels in EBC could be related to the health status of the participants, or because these bioactive lipid metabolites have a hydrophobic structure, which might prevent transport in EBC, due to its high water content. Previously, TXB2 and the metabolite of TXB2, 11-dehydro-TXB2, have also shown difficulties to measure in EBC. Apart from cytokines, LTB4 has been extensively studied before. In this study, LTB4 levels in EBC were 50-fold lower than in the study by Cruickshank-Quinn et al., but 5-fold higher than the study by Tufvesson et al. Gaber et al. noted LTB4 levels of 140–1358 pg/mL in saliva, in line with the salivary levels in our study. LTB4 has shown potential as a non-invasive biomarker for small airway diseases in fractionated EBC, while TXB2 and CysLTs are involved in airway contraction.

Finally, we investigated the cross-matrix correlations of the assessed markers. There was a substantial, although not significant, correlation of E-selectin between EBC and plasma levels, with E-selectin in EBC explaining more variance in lung function than that in saliva or plasma. In line with those findings, IL10 also significantly correlated between EBC and plasma, while explaining more variance in lung function evolution in EBC. Furthermore, this study showed only a slight correlation between plasma and EBC for MMP1, and for EBC CysLTs inverse correlation with saliva, while MMP1 and CysLTs explained most variance in lung function changes when measured in EBC. The latter further highlights the added value of EBC biomarker characterization.

To cost-effectively improve the detection of protein content using limited sample volumes and to allow for unbiased discovery, we performed untargeted proteomic analyses of EBC samples. None of the targeted markers was identified by using the untargeted approaches, highlighting the advantage of this complementary approach. Due to low protein concentrations, previously, many proteomic analyses on EBC employed pooled samples. , Overall, research on proteomics data in single EBC samples is still limited, which highlights the need to validate the methodology. In this study, we used two methods: with a DDA workflow, we identified up to 28 proteins in 24 EBC samples, whereas an S-Trap DIA workflow, which is a more sensitive method, revealed up to 209 proteins in four EBC samples, clearly showing its added value. The lower number of proteins in the DDA workflow is as expected, as DDA is biased toward selecting precursor ions with high signal strength, while DIA assesses all precursor ions in a certain m/z range. Considering the low protein content, previous research also indicated DIA to be more suited for EBC proteomic profiles than DDA. The number of identified proteins using the S-Trap DIA workflow is in line with research by Lacombe et al., who identified >200 proteins from two pooled EBC samples. This suggests that our DIA approach is sensitive enough to measure the EBC proteome without the need to pool samples. However, more recently, up to 1150 proteins and 928 proteins in EBC were identified in patients with lung cancer or pulmonary embolism and controls, ,, likely due to disease-related physiological changes not present in healthy individuals and differences in sensitivity of the used methodology.

None of the targeted markers was identified in the untargeted approaches. This is most likely because of the sensitivity of the methods. While the used ELISAs employ a tailored approach to detect immune markers, the method in DIA focuses on identifying a large number of markers simultaneously. Additionally, subtle differences in marker levels might result in detection or nondetection in the DIA approach, with smaller proteins harder to detect as they result in fewer peptides compared to larger proteins. Further, highly abundant proteins, such as keratins, might mask interleukins in the MS. This highlights the complementary characteristics of targeted and untargeted approaches. Using both methods, we identified multiple proteins that have previously been associated with lung function. This indicates the potential of EBC proteomics for biomarker characterization. The presence of calmodulin-like protein 5 (CALML5) and prolactin-inducible protein showed an overlap between the EBC proteome and salivary proteome, with previous research showing their salivary origin. We identified multiple keratins, which are considered highly abundant proteins; nonetheless, BECs and alveolar cells have been shown to express keratins, suggesting that the EBC proteome might potentially be a non-invasive approach to measure changes in BECs and alveolar cells. These results confirm that EBC composition could cover, and therefore be informative for, the status from the oral mucosa to deep in the lungs.

Several of the proteins identified were previously listed as potential technical contaminants; however, these proteins were previously also linked to lung function and disease. For example, keratin type II cytoskeletal 1 (KRT1) was identified as the protein explaining most variance in lung function in this study and KRT1 is involved in lung tissue remodeling. Further, dermcidin, S100A9, keratin type II cytoskeletal 6A, 6B, and 6C have been investigated in relation to smoking, COPD, and lung cancer. Identified proteins also included hornerin (S100A2), which is frequently found in EBC samples and can be downregulated in smoking, COPD, and lung cancer, and keratin type I cytoskeletal 10 (KRT10), which is upregulated in certain types of lung cancer. Additionally, we identified heat shock protein A1 in EBC, which has a part in innate immunity. Finally, several of the identified proteins have been linked to lung function when detected in plasma: plasma serpin A3 has been linked to fibrosis and idiopathic interstitial pneumonias. These data confirm the usefulness of the method and its potential for assessment in a disease context.

To explore associations of proteomic data with lung function, PLS showed that KRT1, S100-A7, and KRT32 explained the most variance. Keratins, including KRT1, have been shown to be increased in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, airway brushing cells, and bronchioalveolar lavage of asthmatics. Among S110 Alarmins, S100-A7 has been shown to be highly enriched in BECs of severe asthmatics, indicating that S100-A7 might be secreted from BECs, leading to its presence in EBC. Proteomics has a bias toward the most abundant proteins, which is enlarged when the identified proteins are limited, as is the case in the DDA approach; however, these exploratory associations confirm the method’s usefulness.

The chosen approach to investigate a wide variety of markers, including inflammatory markers, lung-related biological markers, and untargeted proteomics in a longitudinal manner from healthy participants in an occupational setting represents the major strength of this study. In addition, we compared measured markers in EBC across different biological matrices, including the gold standard plasma but also less invasive matrices such as saliva. This study is among the first to assess MMP1, MMP2, MMP7, VEGFR1/Flt1, and E- and P-selectins in the EBC. In molecular epidemiological studies, the number of individuals analyzed has to be considered. Here, we did not aim for differential protein expression between different exposure and/or disease conditions. The main purpose of this study was a pilot proof-of-concept study, exploring the practical and analytical feasibility using targeted and untargeted assays for the detection in EBC of possible biomarkers of lung disease in relation to respiratory exposures, compared to detection in saliva and plasma. The results clearly demonstrate the analytical feasibility and potential biological relevance, and therefore, we considered the number of individuals, albeit limited (n = 12), sufficient. In addition, because our population consisted of healthy adults, inter-individual variation was estimated to be low, and samples were compared intra-individually where applicable. In a next step, these validated methodologies will be applied in EBC collected in workers with respiratory symptoms or disease in the EPHOR project, and on workers with certain exposure scenarios in EXPOSIM grant no. 101156311 (https://exposim.eu/).

Conclusion

We showed that immune markers as well as proteomics could be assessed in the EBC. We assessed several immune markers, previously associated with respiratory diseases/exposures, in EBC, in comparison to saliva and plasma as the gold standard, and noted correlations between marker levels across the matrices. Immune markers were stable over a six month period. EBC proteomics, using the most optimized protocol, identified 209 proteins, with several previously reported to be associated with lung function or disease. Finally, saliva contamination during EBC collection was at most limited. This study confirms that methods are available to robustly assess immune and proteomic markers in the EBC.
