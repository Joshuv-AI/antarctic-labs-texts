Active aeration enhances tissue hydration and fresh mass in hydroponic lettuce and modulates calcium-mobilizing biostimulant efficacy
Authors: Jonathan Ries, Yujin Park, Qingwu Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tipburn is a physiological disorder in lettuce (Lactuca sativa) that causes leaf margin necrosis, reducing marketability, and is exacerbated in hydroponic systems where calcium demand exceeds supply. Although a calcium-mobilizing biostimulant can mitigate tipburn, it is unclear whether its efficacy depends on nutrient solution dissolved oxygen (DO), which is critical for water and nutrient uptake. We evaluated the effects of this calcium-mobilizing biostimulant (0, 0.25, and 0.5 mL⋅L–1) and root-zone DO (achieved without and with varying degrees of moderate active aeration: 2.9, 3.5, and 4.0 mg⋅L–1) on growth and tipburn of hydroponic butterhead lettuce ‘Nancy’ in deep-water culture. At 21 days after transplanting (DAT), increasing DO from 2.9 to 3.5–4.0 mg⋅L–1 increased shoot fresh mass by 98–139%, moisture content by 5–7%, and plant diameter by 31–48%, without affecting dry mass, whereas the biostimulant did not affect growth. At medium DO, the biostimulant reduced percent burnt leaves from 33 to 12%. At 28 DAT, similar DO-dependent growth trends persisted, while high biostimulant concentration reduced tipburn, especially at low DO. These results indicate that active aeration enhances tissue hydration and fresh mass, and that the biostimulant mitigates tipburn primarily under root hypoxia.

Introduction

Lettuce (Lactuca sativa) is one of the most widely cultivated crops in controlled-environment hydroponic systems. A major challenge for growers is the development of tipburn, a physiological disorder characterized by necrotic patches on the margins of young inner leaves1. Tipburn results from a localized calcium deficiency in developing leaves, even when calcium is present in the nutrient solution but immobilized within the plant2. This immobilization can occur under conditions of high humidity, poor air circulation, or when plant growth exceeds the capacity for calcium transport through tissues3. Lettuce affected by tipburn exhibits reduced marketability and may experience yield losses, imposing significant economic costs on growers4.

Various strategies have been developed to mitigate tipburn, though many involve tradeoffs in yield or increased production costs. One approach is to slow plant growth by reducing light intensity or lowering temperature, which decreases calcium demand but also reduces yield5. Enhancing air circulation with vertical or horizontal airflow fans can reduce leaf-zone humidity, increase transpiration, and facilitate calcium transport, but this can be costly to implement6. Similarly, lowering ambient humidity can enhance calcium movement, yet precise control is challenging in indoor environments such as large-scale greenhouses and vertical farms7. Recently, biostimulants that enhance calcium mobility within plants have shown promise in alleviating tipburn without major tradeoffs in growth or yield8. For example, Biradar and Meng ,9 demonstrated that a calcium-mobilizing biostimulant (codenamed CC US-2105; Croda, Inc., Atlas Point, DE, USA) applied via the nutrient solution reduced tipburn incidence by 85% and severity by 88% in lettuce ‘Rex’ while maintaining biomass accumulation.

Although the effects of light, humidity, air circulation, and calcium mobility on tipburn have been studied, the potential influence of oxygen availability in the root zone remains poorly understood. In hydroponic systems, sufficient dissolved oxygen (DO) in the root zone is critical for root function and overall plant growth10,11. Oxygen is essential for root respiration (oxidative phosphorylation) to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and can influence reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling in roots, affecting cellular function12. Oxygen deprivation inhibits root metabolism and transport, reducing water and nutrient uptake and leading to stunted growth13–15. Although DO has not been extensively examined in the context of tipburn, several studies have demonstrated that elevating DO can increase lettuce biomass. For instance, Nitu et al.16 reported an 18% increase in fresh mass of lettuce ‘Lugano’ by raising DO from 7.2 to 8.6 mg⋅L–1 in a nutrient-film-technique (NFT) system, while Ouyang et al.17 found a 33% increase in fresh mass of lettuce ‘Grand Rapids’ grown in pots with sandy loam soil when DO in irrigation water increased from 6.5 to 8.5 mg⋅L–1. However, despite evidence that DO influences growth, the relationship among DO, calcium uptake, and tipburn severity has not been thoroughly investigated.

Calcium is not remobilized from older to younger leaves, and lower transpiration rates in the core of lettuce heads contribute to tipburn in young leaves7. Unlike actively transported nutrients such as nitrogen or potassium, calcium moves passively with water in the xylem. Consequently, older outer leaves with higher transpiration accumulate more calcium, while inner leaves with lower transpiration receive less (White and Broadley 2003). Although higher DO can enhance root respiration and uptake of actively transported nutrients, it is unclear whether passive calcium transport would also benefit from increased DO-driven changes in water flow. In a study that directly assessed DO and tipburn, Anthony (2022) observed that increasing DO from 6 to 21 mg⋅L–1 did not affect tipburn severity in lettuce ‘Crispa’ grown in an NFT system; however, 6 mg⋅L–1 may have already exceeded the threshold needed to elicit potential benefits. Most research on tipburn has focused on environmental or nutritional factors, leaving it unclear whether DO can influence tipburn development or alter the efficacy of calcium-mobilizing biostimulants that show promise in reducing tipburn.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of moderate supplemental DO on growth and tipburn development in greenhouse-grown lettuce ‘Nancy’ in a deep-water-culture system and to determine whether the efficacy of a calcium-mobilizing biostimulant in controlling tipburn depends on DO concentration. We hypothesized that (1) moderately increasing DO would promote lettuce growth and reduce tipburn incidence by improving root respiration and tissue hydration, and (2) the biostimulant would reduce tipburn incidence at lower DO concentrations, where calcium mobility is more limiting, but would promote growth at higher DO concentrations, where increased root oxygenation supports faster leaf expansion and higher calcium demand.

Materials and methods

Seedling propagation

This greenhouse lettuce experiment followed a randomized complete block design with three blocks (replications) and nine nutrient solution treatments per block (three DO concentrations × three biostimulant concentrations) (Fig. 1). For each block, on day 0, we sowed 200 seeds of green butterhead lettuce ‘Nancy’ (Johnny’s Selected Seeds, Winslow, ME, USA) in rockwool cubes (one seed per cube, 25 × 25 × 4 mm; AO 25/40, Grodan, Milton, ON, Canada), which were pre-saturated with reverse-osmosis water. We placed the rockwool cubes in growing trays in a temperature-controlled, glass-glazed greenhouse with an ambient temperature setpoint of 25 °C (day/night). The trays were covered with transparent humidity domes and placed under supplemental white + red light-emitting diode fixtures (VL1; Volt Grow, Lutz, FL, USA) with a 24-h photoperiod. In combination with natural sunlight, the mean photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD, 400–700 nm) provided to plants was 300 µmol⋅m–2⋅s–1 for the duration of the experiment. Following the emergence of visible cotyledons, we removed the humidity domes and began sub-irrigating the seedling trays as needed with a nutrient solution [pH = 5.8; electrical conductivity (EC) = 1.3 dS⋅m− 1] by sequentially dissolving a water-soluble base fertilizer (12-4-16 RO; JR Peters, Inc., Allentown, PA, USA) (1.04 g⋅L− 1) and magnesium sulfate (JR Peters, Inc.) (0.28 g⋅L− 1) in reverse-osmosis water. The nutrient composition was as follows (in mg⋅L− 1): 125 N, 18 P, 138 K, 73 Ca, 48 Mg, 39 S, 0.12 B, 0.47 Cu, 1.77 Fe, 0.52 Mn, 0.13 Mo, and 0.56 Zn. The nutrient solution pH was adjusted to 5.8 with a diluted potassium hydroxide and potassium carbonate solution (pH Up, General Hydroponics, Inc., Santa Rosa, CA, USA) and/or a diluted phosphoric acid solution (pH Down, General Hydroponics, Inc.). The pH and EC were measured using a portable meter (HI9814; Hanna Instruments, Smithfield, RI, USA).

Transplanting and nutrient solution treatments

For each block, on day 11 after sowing, we transplanted 162 uniform lettuce seedlings into nine deep-water-culture hydroponic trays (1.22 m L × 0.61 m W × 0.18 m H, Active Aqua AAHR24W; Hydrofarm, Petaluma, CA, USA) on a greenhouse bench. Each deep-water-culture tray had a Styrofoam floating raft (Lettuce Raft 18; Beaver Plastics, Ltd., Acheson, Alberta, Canada) that floated above 12 cm of a nutrient solution (≈ 89 L). The nutrient solution was made by dissolving 1.25 g⋅L− 1 of a water-soluble base fertilizer (12-4-16 RO; JR Peters, Inc.) and 0.34 g⋅L− 1 of magnesium sulfate (JR Peters, Inc.) sequentially in reverse-osmosis water. The nutrient composition was as follows (in mg⋅L− 1): 150 N, 22 P, 166 K, 88 Ca, 58 Mg, 47 S, 0.15 B, 0.56 Cu, 2.13 Fe, 0.63 Mn, 0.15 Mo, and 0.68 Zn. In a randomized complete block design, three greenhouse benches were assigned as blocks (replications). In each block, we randomly assigned the nine treatments to the nine hydroponic trays using a split-plot design, with 18 plants/treatment/block (Fig. 1).

Before transplanting, three aeration treatments [no aeration (low DO), moderate aeration (medium DO), and high aeration (high DO)] were created using air pumps (65 L·min–1 Air Pump; Simple Deluxe, Duarte, CA, USA) and air stones (10.7 cm Air Stone Disc; Kathson, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) evenly distributed at the bottom of each tray, except for the low DO treatment, which had no aeration. For medium and high DO treatments, two air stones were placed on the bottom of each hydroponic tray, center-aligned to the tray width and positioned at the 1/4 and 3/4 points along the tray length, to supply DO to the nutrient solution. Airflow valves on a manifold connected to the air pump were adjusted roughly halfway open to achieve medium DO and fully open to achieve high DO. From transplant to the final harvest, a DO meter (HI9147-04; Hanna Instruments) was used to measure and record the DO concentration daily in each hydroponic tray. Degrees of aeration, rather than strict DO setpoints, were maintained in this experiment. The mean measured DO concentrations under the low, medium, and high DO treatments throughout the experiment were 2.9, 3.5, and 4.0 mg⋅L–1, respectively, in absolute values and 32.5, 42.0, and 46.4%, respectively, in percent saturation. These DO levels resulted from none to moderate aeration and represented a practically relevant range for deep-water-culture systems, including hypoxic conditions (2.9 mg⋅L–1) and moderate aeration levels (3.5–4.0 mg⋅L–1), rather than to establish a full dose-response gradient. In addition, at each DO concentration in each block, we randomly assigned three nutrient solution treatments to the three hydroponic trays with or without a calcium-mobilizing biostimulant (CC US-2105; Croda, Inc.), at concentrations of 0, 0.25 and 0.5 mL⋅L–1 of the nutrient solution volume. Where the biostimulant was applied, it was mixed into the nutrient solution at concentrations of either 0.25 or 0.5 mL⋅L–1 after fertilizers were dissolved. To dissolve the biostimulant, we placed 500 mL of the nutrient solution from each tray in a beaker and added the biostimulant, mixed the solution using a magnetic stirrer, and added it back into the tray. Whenever the nutrient solution pH was out of the 5.6–6.0 range throughout the experiment, it was adjusted to 5.8 as previously described. After transplanting, we did not add any water, fertilizers, or biostimulants. The biostimulant was applied once at transplanting following manufacturer guidance to reflect common commercial practice for nutrient solution additives in deep-water-culture systems and to isolate its interaction with root-zone DO, without confounding effects of repeated dosing. Greenhouse environment

Plants were grown under natural sunlight with continuous supplemental lighting throughout the experiment as previously described. A quantum sensor connected to a datalogger (SQ-500; Apogee Instruments, Inc., Logan, UT, USA) was placed at canopy height in the middle of one hydroponic tray from each block to measure the PPFD at 1-h intervals. We positioned a temperature and humidity sensor housed in a white radiation shield and connected to a datalogger (HOBO MX2301; Onset Computer Corporation, Bourne, MA, USA) at plant height next to one hydroponic tray in the center of each greenhouse bench to record the hourly air temperature and relative humidity. The air temperature, relative humidity, and daily light integral (DLI) (mean ± SD) for each of the three blocks are reported in Table 2. To minimize confounding effects of airflow on tipburn development, greenhouse exhaust fans remained closed during the experiment. The DLIs varied across the three blocks (22.2 ± 6.9, 27.0 ± 8.0, and 24.0 ± 10.4 mol·m–2·d–1 for blocks 1–3, respectively) due to proximity to supplemental lighting edges and evaporative cooling pads, which partially shaded block 3 in the afternoons. Block effects were included in the statistical analyses (Table 3).

Data collection and analysis

For each block, at 21 and 28 d after transplanting (DAT), we randomly selected six plants per tray from each treatment and block for data collection. During each harvest, we photographed a representative plant from each treatment and block to document any visible treatment effects. Photographs of representative plants at 28 DAT are shown in Fig. 2. Roots were photographed along with shoots to document qualitative differences among treatments; however, root morphology, mass, or coloration were not quantitatively analyzed. Shoot fresh mass was recorded at harvest, and shoot dry mass was measured after ≥ 5 days at 65 °C in a forced-air drying oven (SMO28–2; Sheldon Manufacturing, Inc., Cornelius, OR, USA) using an analytical balance. Tissue moisture content (percentage moisture) was subsequently calculated as: 100% × (shoot fresh mass − shoot dry mass) ÷ shoot fresh mass. We measured the diameter of each selected plant with a ruler and recorded the number of leaves ≥ 2 cm in length. To assess tipburn development, we counted the number of leaves ≥ 2 cm that exhibited visible tipburn symptoms and calculated the percentage of burnt leaves. In addition, each plant in each growing tray was assigned a visual tipburn severity rating on a scale from 0 to 5 (0 = no tipburn; 1 = mild; 3 = moderate; 5 = severe) following protocols established in Moosazi-Nezhad and Meng8.

Data were analyzed in JMP Student Edition (version 19; SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) using a factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA) with dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration, biostimulant concentration, and their interaction (DO × biostimulant) as fixed effects, and block as a random effect (Table 3). The model was fitted using a mixed-effects approach. When significant main or interaction effects were detected (P < 0.05), means were separated using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test.

Results

Throughout the experiment, DO concentrations remained stable and well separated among treatments (Table 1). At 21 DAT, there was no DO × biostimulant interaction for any parameter except for burnt leaf number and percent burnt leaves (Table 3). The tipburn rating remained low (< 1.3) across treatments and was unaffected by the biostimulant, although it was higher under medium DO than low and high DO (Fig. 3A). Total leaf number increased with increasing DO or biostimulant concentration (Fig. 3B). Neither DO nor biostimulant concentration affected burnt leaf number and percent burnt leaves at low and high DO (Figs. 3C–D). However, high DO decreased burnt leaf number and percent burnt leaves by 57–58% compared to medium DO in the absence of the biostimulant (Figs. 3C–D). In addition, increasing the biostimulant concentration from 0 to 0.25 or 0.5 mL⋅L–1 decreased burnt leaf number and percent burnt leaves by up to 59% and 65%, respectively, at medium DO (Figs. 3C–D).

At 21 DAT, while medium and high DO increased shoot fresh mass, moisture content, and plant diameter by 94–139%, 4–7%, and 31–48%, respectively, compared to low DO across all biostimulant concentrations, shoot dry mass was unaffected by DO (Figs. 3E–H). The biostimulant did not affect shoot fresh or dry mass, moisture content, or plant diameter.

At 28 DAT, there was no DO × biostimulant interaction for any parameter except for total leaf number, burnt leaf number, shoot fresh mass, and moisture content (Table 3). The tipburn rating (ranging between 1.7 and 2.6) was unaffected by DO but decreased when the biostimulant was used (Fig. 4A). Total leaf number and burnt leaf number were unaffected by the biostimulant concentration under medium and high DO but decreased with increasing biostimulant concentration under low DO (Figs. 4B–C). Burnt leaf number decreased by 35% under high DO compared to low DO in the absence of the biostimulant (Fig. 4C). In contrast, total leaf number increased by 49–51% under medium DO compared to low DO while burnt leaf number was unaffected by DO in the presence of the biostimulant. Increasing DO or biostimulant concentration decreased percent burnt leaves (Fig. 4D).

At 28 DAT, while medium and high DO increased shoot fresh mass, moisture content, and plant diameter by 98–174%, 5–7%, and 37–45%, respectively, compared to low DO across all biostimulant concentrations, shoot dry mass was somewhat higher under low DO than medium and high DO (Figs. 2A and 4E–H). The biostimulant did not affect shoot fresh mass, moisture content, or plant diameter, except that increasing the biostimulant concentration from 0 to 0.25 mL⋅L–1 decreased shoot fresh mass and moisture content by 26 and 2%, respectively, at low DO. Shoot dry mass was somewhat lower under the 0.25 mL⋅L–1 biostimulant concentration compared with the other two concentrations. At 28 DAT, plants grown under low DO exhibited visibly smaller and darker root systems compared with those grown under medium and high DO (Fig. 2B), although these differences were qualitative and were not quantitatively assessed.

Discussion

Increased dissolved oxygen improved lettuce growth primarily by enhancing tissue hydration rather than increasing dry mass accumulation

Raising the root-zone DO concentration from 2.9 to 3.5–4.0 mg⋅L–1 substantially increased shoot fresh mass and plant size, consistent with previous research showing that supplemental DO improves lettuce growth in hydroponic systems16,17,18. However, unlike those studies, which reported fresh-mass gains accompanied by proportional or greater increases in dry mass, our results revealed a decoupling between fresh and dry mass. At 28 DAT, shoot dry mass declined slightly at higher DO, whereas shoot fresh mass and moisture content increased substantially at higher DO. Because moisture content was expressed on a fresh-mass basis, increases in this metric directly reflect greater tissue hydration contributing to shoot fresh mass. This pattern indicates that the growth promotion observed under higher DO was driven predominantly by increased tissue water content and hydration, rather than by increased carbon assimilation. This interpretation is supported by a recent study showing that increasing DO did not affect net photosynthetic rate in arugula (Eruca vesicaria ‘Astro’ and ‘Esmee’) or kale (Brassica oleracea ‘KX-1’ and ‘Red Russian’)19. Because lettuce is typically more than 90% water, small increases in tissue moisture content (~ 5%–7%) can produce very large increases in fresh mass (~ 100%–170%). The accompanying increase in plant diameter at medium and high DO further supports the interpretation that higher DO enhanced tissue hydration in the leaves, enabling greater leaf expansion and a wider canopy. Although whole-plant water uptake and transpiration were not directly measured, the higher tissue moisture content strongly suggests increased water acquisition and/or retention, which likely reflects enhanced root water uptake and hydraulic conductance under improved oxygen availability. It should be noted that the DO range evaluated here reflects a narrow but operationally relevant window, and therefore these results demonstrate plant sensitivity to incremental changes within this range rather than defining broader physiological thresholds.

Limited oxygen availability is known to impair root respiration, reduce water absorption, and constrain leaf growth16, and DO concentrations below ~ 3–4 mg·L–1 often inhibit both root and shoot development through root hypoxia, tissue browning, reduced stomatal conductance, and decreased water use15. Although root morphology and respiration were not quantified, the visibly reduced and darker root systems observed under low DO (2.9 mg·L–1) compared with medium and high DO at 28 DAT are consistent with partial root hypoxia. Thus, even modest DO increases from a low baseline (2.9 mg⋅L–1) likely alleviated root hypoxia enough to restore hydraulic conductance, improving leafy hydration and enabling more robust leaf expansion.

Supplemental dissolved oxygen reduced tipburn independently of the biostimulant

Across both timepoints, DO itself had notable effects on tipburn. At 21 DAT, high DO decreased the tipburn rating, burnt leaf number, and percent burnt leaves relative to medium DO in the absence of the biostimulant. By 28 DAT, increasing DO reduced burnt leaf number and percent burnt leaves in the absence of the biostimulant. These patterns demonstrate that improved root oxygenation can reduce tipburn risk, even when plant growth, and therefore potential calcium demand, increases under higher DO. Although calcium is absorbed into roots primarily via passive mass flow, its transport to the shoot is enabled by root vitality and xylem loading, both of which depend on aerobic respiration20,21. Low DO can impair membrane function, suppress water uptake, and reduce transpiration, thereby restricting calcium flux to expanding leaves13,14,22,23. The increases in plant moisture content at medium and high DO in our study indicate greater leaf hydration, which likely reflects increased water uptake and transpirational flow—the primary drivers of calcium delivery to expanding leaves. Thus, higher DO likely improved calcium mobility sufficiently to offset the higher calcium demand associated with faster growth, reducing tipburn even without the biostimulant. Although we did not directly measure calcium uptake or leaf calcium concentration in this study, the observed tipburn reduction is consistent with enhanced calcium delivery via the transpiration stream.

The observation that tipburn was minimized under both low and high DO at 21 DAT, relative to medium DO, may reflect dynamic interactions among DO, growth rate, and water movement early in development. At this stage, medium DO may have accelerated leaf expansion before root hydraulic capacity fully increased, creating a temporary mismatch between calcium demand and supply. As plants grew larger, this imbalance resolved, and high DO became more consistently effective at reducing tipburn by 28 DAT.

Biostimulant responses were dissolved oxygen-dependent and shifted over time

The efficacy of the calcium-mobilizing biostimulant varied with DO concentration and plant developmental stage. At 21 DAT, the biostimulant had no effect on the tipburn rating; however, it reduced burnt leaf number and percent burnt leaves at medium DO but had no effect at low or high DO. At 28 DAT, the biostimulant decreased the tipburn rating and percent burnt leaves regardless of DO concentration, and decreased burnt leaf number under low DO, but not under medium or high DO. This temporal and DO-dependent shift suggests that aeration influenced biostimulant efficacy by altering growth rate, tissue water content, and likely water uptake-driven calcium delivery, thereby modifying the severity of calcium limitation. At 21 DAT, medium DO likely produced a “near-limiting” calcium condition, where leaf expansion increased calcium demand slightly faster than root hydraulic capacity, making plants responsive to enhanced calcium mobility. The biostimulant used in this study is marketed as a synthetic molecule proposed to enhance calcium mobility by stimulating selective ion-transport channels in plant membranes, thereby increasing cytosolic calcium concentration and improving symplastic calcium movement in tissues with low transpiration rates24. High DO provided sufficient calcium transport, leaving little room for additional benefit, whereas low DO supported slow growth with low calcium demand, resulting in minimal calcium limitation. By 28 DAT, as plants grew larger and calcium demand increased, calcium limitation became more pronounced under low DO due to likely restricted water uptake. Under these conditions, the biostimulant provided a measurable benefit to reduce burnt leaf number at low DO. Together, these results suggest that the biostimulant operates within a relatively narrow physiological window, where calcium delivery becomes limiting, and that aeration determines when this window occurs by modulating root oxygenation, hydraulic conductance, and growth.

These results are consistent with previous studies on the same biostimulant, which showed strong tipburn reduction with no yield penalty at similar concentrations (0.22–0.5 mL⋅L–1) in lettuce ‘Rex’ grown without active aeration8,9. In those non-aerated systems, root-zone DO remained low throughout production, creating sustained calcium limitation and a broad window for the biostimulant to enhance calcium mobility. In contrast, the present study included treatments with active aeration, which increased hydraulic conductance and reduced calcium limitation at medium and high DO, narrowing the conditions under which the biostimulant could confer additional benefit. Collectively, these results indicate that the biostimulant is most effective under environments where calcium transport is moderately limiting, and that the degree of aeration, and therefore root-zone DO, can modulate the effectiveness of the biostimulant. Moreover, biostimulant performance appeared sensitive to cultivar-specific susceptibility to tipburn. In the present study, lettuce ‘Nancy’ developed only mild-to-moderate tipburn even at 28 DAT (tipburn rating < 3), suggesting greater inherent tolerance compared to ‘Rex’, which developed severe tipburn (tipburn rating ≈ 5) by 28 DAT in the absence of the biostimulant, vertical airflow fans, or active aeration8,9. These differences suggest that the magnitude of DO and biostimulant effects may be greater in tipburn-susceptible cultivars, and that cultivar choice should be considered when extrapolating these results.

Study limitations

This study evaluated a single butterhead lettuce cultivar with relatively low inherent tipburn susceptibility, and responses to DO and biostimulant treatments may differ in more sensitive cultivars. Another limitation of this study is that calcium uptake and tissue calcium concentration were not directly measured. Although the observed interactions among DO, plant water status, and tipburn are consistent with calcium mobility as a mediating mechanism, direct quantification would strengthen mechanistic interpretation. In addition, although the biostimulant is marketed as enhancing calcium mobility via stimulation of ion-transport channels and improved symplastic movement24, this mechanism remains speculative in the context of our study because changes in ion-channel activity or internal calcium flux were not measured. Future work should evaluate root calcium uptake rates and leaf calcium distribution under interacting DO and biostimulant treatments. Furthermore, the biostimulant was applied only once at transplanting, and its persistence in the nutrient solution was not assessed. Repeated or foliar applications and measurements of solution stability and tissue calcium dynamics would help clarify dose-response relationships and the duration of biostimulant activity. In addition, although representative images indicated smaller and darker roots under low DO than under medium and high DO at 28 DAT, root responses to DO were not quantitatively assessed. Future studies should directly measure root biomass, respiration, and anatomical traits to more rigorously characterize hypoxia responses. Lastly, the DO treatments evaluated in this study spanned a relatively narrow range typical of deep-water-culture systems. While this design allows for the assessment of plant responses to small, practically achievable changes in DO, it does not capture responses across a broader oxygen gradient. Future studies should incorporate a wider range of DO concentrations and include detailed physiological measurements (e.g., root morphology, gas exchange, and nutrient uptake) to more fully characterize the mechanisms underlying plant responses to root-zone oxygen availability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that relatively small increases in root-zone DO, from 2.9 to 3.5–4.0 mg⋅L–1, substantially enhanced lettuce growth within the tested DO range primarily by increasing tissue water content and leaf expansion, likely through enhanced water uptake and transport, rather than by increasing dry mass accumulation. The calcium-mobilizing biostimulant reduced tipburn under specific DO and developmental conditions, with its efficacy shifting from medium DO at 21 DAT to low DO at 28 DAT. Supplemental DO alone effectively reduced tipburn, consistent with enhanced water-driven calcium delivery, though calcium concentrations were not directly measured. Together, these results highlight the importance of root oxygenation as a driver of both growth and calcium-related physiological disorders in deep-water-culture hydroponic lettuce and indicate that biostimulant efficacy depends on the root-zone DO environment.

These findings have practical implications for hydroponic lettuce tipburn management. Sufficient root-zone DO can reduce reliance on the calcium-mobilizing biostimulant, while the biostimulant application can, in turn, reduce the need for active aeration and its associated infrastructure and energy costs. Some cultivars may benefit from a combination of both approaches. Optimizing DO and biostimulant use according to system design and crop growth conditions can help maximize growth and minimize tipburn while lowering production costs.
