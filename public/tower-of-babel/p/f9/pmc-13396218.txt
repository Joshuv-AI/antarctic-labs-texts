DeepShieldIDS: an AI-powered intrusion detection system leveraging HybridIDSNet for robust network security
Authors: Sayyada Mubeen, Balakrishna Gudla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs) are largely unable to provide the level of protection required to safeguard modern network environments against ever-evolving cyber threats. Conventional machine-learning-based feature representations are static and cannot capture temporal behaviour in network traffic, limiting performance under heterogeneous conditions. Deep learning approaches have improved detection accuracy, but challenges remain in feature diversity, class imbalance, and cross-environment generalisation. To overcome these limitations, we propose DeepShieldIDS, a hybrid deep learning–based network intrusion detection framework built on established convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and an attention mechanism, all integrated into a single evaluation pipeline. The HybridIDSNet utilises spatial feature extraction, temporal sequence modelling and feature-weighting attention in a single framework to improve traffic classification performance. We conduct experiments on two benchmark datasets, CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15, in independent and cross-dataset evaluation settings. The model outperforms classical machine learning models (Random Forest, SVM, and XGBoost) and Categorical CNN and LSTM baselines, achieving accuracies of 97.98% and 98.02% with ROC-AUC scores of 99.05% and 99.12%, respectively. Evaluation is confined to supervised benchmark settings, while cross-dataset validation suggests better generalisation with heterogeneous traffic distributions. The proposed framework focuses on a deployment-oriented architecture and a formalised pre-processing procedure, providing an overview of scalable hybrid deep learning solutions for network intrusion detection.Keywords: Intrusion Detection, Deep Learning, Network Security, Hybrid Neural Networks, Anomaly DetectionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The recent growth of interconnected devices, systems, and applications, coupled with the increasing complexity of cyberattacks, has driven a surge in demand for sophisticated anomaly detection solutions. Network-based and host-based traditional signature-based (IDS) systems fail to detect new sources of threats due to rapid changes in network topologies, new attack types, and/or new attack behaviour patterns. Recent developments in DL and ML methods have shown promise in addressing these needs, with their ability to learn complex interactions among various factors that lead to attacks and to detect outliers in network traffic. Past work1–3 examined models such as Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, and deep NNs. However, they may have several deficiencies, including limited generalisation across different network scenarios, an inability to capture temporal patterns in traffic flows, and suboptimal performance on class-imbalanced datasets. CNNs have been used for deep feature representation4, and RNNs for exploiting temporal dependencies5. However, combining these with an attention mechanism to learn which features matter most has not been thoroughly investigated.

The current study aims to address these gaps by introducing DeepShieldIDS, an artificial intelligence-enabled intrusion detection framework that employs a newly designed HybridIDSNet deep learning model. In this model, the CNNs and LSTMs facilitate sequential learning, and the attention mechanism captures highly complex relationships across different fields of network traffic data. The robustness of anomaly detection in various network environments by utilising hybrid deep learning, while also improving feature learning and addressing issues related to attack diversity. A hybrid model structure that combines spatial and temporal learning, a cross-dataset validation technique to assess generalisation, and a user-oriented system design suitable for real-time deployment are key novelties of this work.

The main novelty of this work is not in proposing a new neural architecture for hybrid intrusion detection, but rather in the design and evaluation of a deployment-oriented hybrid intrusion detection framework. Although combinations of CNN–LSTM–Attention have been investigated in previous IDS research, the proposed DeepShieldIDS offers multiple system-level and methodological enhancements to improve robustness and the realism of evaluation. Firstly, we propose a cross-dataset generalisation pipeline that consistently unifies heterogeneous feature spaces across CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 through a combination of preprocessing and a feature-intersection strategy, enabling systematic evaluation across different network environments. Third, the contribution includes both spatial feature extraction and temporal sequence modelling. Still, unlike most similar works in the literature, it emphasises reproducibility and interpretability over mere architectural novelty, within a single end-to-end IDS framework and realistic (i.e., scalable) deployment scenarios.

Third, extensive experiments provide evidence of consistent performance gains over classical machine learning models and deep learning baselines that treat temporal and spatial components independently; this underscores the advantages of joint temporal–spatial learning for attention-guided learning. Lastly, the framework delivers a variety of design aspects ready for deployment, including individual modules for pre-processing, imbalance handling, and integration. It provides a perspective on how we translate hybrid deep models into actual IDS pipelines. With this in mind, these contributions characterise DeepShieldIDS as a hybrid deep–learning–based intrusion detection system with an evaluation-driven, system-integrated approach, rather than as a fundamentally new neural network architecture. The novelty of this work lies in its evaluation strategy and system integration—not in new neural network components. The main technical contribution is a cross-dataset generalisation protocol that aligns heterogeneous IDS datasets to a common feature space and analyses a hybrid CNN–LSTM–Attention model with two-way cross-dataset validation (where single-dataset validation is insufficient to assess robustness).

Related work

Intrusion detection systems (IDSs) have evolved from rule-based mechanisms to complex AI-driven systems capable of identifying both historical and novel cyberattacks. Previous studies can be grouped into four domains: rule-based IPS, deep learning-based IPS, integrated and advanced models, and trend- and modern-direction AI solutions.

Machine learning-based IDS

Mainly, classical IDS is grounded in self-learning-based classifiers such as the Random Forest classifier4, Support Vector Machines5, Naïve Bayes6, k-Nearest Neighbours7, or k-Nearest Neighbours ensemble models8. They worked moderately well in tracing conventional attacks. But their image was just as crisp and still as existing feature representation schemes, and they could hardly migrate to novel threats. A few papers improved detection performance using resampling methods6 or feature engineering processes7. Few5,8 extended Industry 4.0 and the smart grid landscape to ML-based detection. Later work goes into large-scale anomaly detection over feature networks9. Nevertheless, problems remain, as machine learning methods fail to classify these complex, multi-dimensional datasets, particularly when they are skewed or raggedly distributed.

Deep learning-based IDS

Deep learning has significantly improved intrusion detection by enabling it to learn features automatically from raw network data. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been used to extract spatial patterns10,11, while sequential models such as LSTMs and GRUs have been used to capture temporal dependencies12–14. Meanwhile, the detection of novel attacks by using unsupervised and self-taught methods—Autoencoders15 and Contrastive Learning16—has been dramatically improved. Other techniques have achieved high accuracy in SDN networks17 and IoT environments14. Specific articles based on– Product development surveys, common issues in imbalance or concept drift18, Techniques applied to vehicular systems19, in-vehicle anomaly detection20, and embedded feature encodings20, extended application to other domains. Despite strong results—often exceeding 98% accuracy17,21—deep learning models remain resource-intensive, prone to overfitting, and unable to generalise.

Hybrid and advanced models

To take advantage of different architectures, Hybrid frameworks appeared. CNN-LSTM ensembles show good performance in a traffic detection test22–24. However, attention-enhanced models25,26 are likely to give a better feature ordering. Swarm intelligence27,28 is a class of optimisation-based techniques that could supplement their capability. Richer modes of thought subsequently moved toward prototypical developments in quantum-assisted detection1, blockchain-mediated security fusion29, and domains such as IoT30, industrial monitoring31, and creative systems32. Adaptive models33 made conceptual drift redundant. The CNN34 with a similar architecture also achieved competitive results in anomaly detection in unsupervised tests11; the method later demonstrated its usefulness in practice for electricity theft8, energy management28, and software-defined network (SDN) systems35,36. DBN-based architectures37 and other hybrid innovations have been presented, but obdurate difficulties in efficiency, scalability, and real-time flexibility7,12,25 still need to be solved, although accuracies up to 99% have sometimes been reached.

Explainable AI and emerging directions

In critical physical infrastructure, when IDS systems are installed, they must be transparent and trusted. Recent advances have also explored anomaly detection beyond traditional network traffic domains. A deep learning-based framework for real-time video anomaly detection has demonstrated the effectiveness of temporal modelling in identifying abnormal patterns in dynamic environments2. Although this approach focuses on surveillance data, it underscores the importance of sequential deep learning techniques for real-time anomaly detection.

Attack-robust layers introduced defences38 to resist adversarial conditions, initially because they work well against black-box attacks, whose outputs yield maximal force against heuristics, even when these are available. While attempts were made to defend against these follow-up studies in CPS34 and federated IDS approaches9,27, distributed and adaptive approaches for environments were also developed. Anomalous intrusion detection based on examples of top-down self-supervision has also found broad acceptance16,19.

Meanwhile, the attention-based in-vehicle models25 in my recent work received enthusiastic praise from people who came to see me. However, it is not true that attention used for in-vehicle models would work well. Challenges abound for industrial systems with high-performance IDS: Flagship-level cities, robots, and even something as blunt and brutal as speculative logic to translate into software. DS research has expanded into smart-city IoT39, power grids40, and large-scale real-time IDS30,32. Domain specialisation is increasing overall24,28,35. This is shown, for example, by hybrid deep belief networks37 in network intrusion detection models for industrial systems, where multiple electrodes gradually uncover the entire attack surface.

Summary and research gaps

The evolution of intrusion detection systems (IDSs) has progressed from classical machine learning classifiers to hybrid deep learning and attention-enhanced architectures. Previous research has used a wide variety of approaches, including classical ML-based IDS models such as Random Forest, SVM, and k-Nearest Neighbors4–7; deep learning techniques with CNNs for spatial feature extraction10,11; sequential LSTM/GRU architectures for temporal representation learning of network traffic12–14; and hybrid CNN–LSTM structures with attention mechanisms to enhance feature selection25,26. More recently, we have found studies on imbalance-handling strategies30, optimisation-driven IDS models32, and specific applications in IoT, SDN, and cyber-physical systems27,28,35. While such methods have generally yielded high detection accuracies, a closer examination of previous approaches reveals consistent technological constraints that continue to complicate research to this day. In addition, recent studies highlight the growing importance of AI-enabled threat detection frameworks for modern cybersecurity applications, emphasising adaptive learning, intelligent automation, and scalable threat mitigation strategies3.

The state-of-the-art behaviour-based NIDS have gradually begun to change in response to emerging trends such as transformer-based models18, incremental learning strategies39, and deployment-aware anomaly detection frameworks21. Cerasuolo et al. To enable certain models to adapt to potential changes in traffic distribution without full retraining, Yang et al.41 propose an explainable intrusion detection system for IoT environments based on an integrated class-incremental and domain-incremental learning approach. Ajayan et al. A more recent work, Cascade-XIDS28, proposes a cascade network in an attempt to satisfy the throughput demands of practical IDS implementations over hardware-driven models, and Distil-XIDS42, another lightweight transformer-based IDS with implementations for real-time deployment, suggests the direction towards more efficiency and attention-guided architectures, which also capture the resource-awareness-performance trade-off for practical purposes. Similarly, Kirubavathi et al.43. However, a resource-efficient framework for streaming anomaly detection in edge environments was proposed, capable of handling continuous data streams while ensuring scalability and low-latency processing. Simultaneously, Kheddar44 provides a broad survey of the application of transformers and large language models to intrusion detection and highlights the tangled issues of interpretability, generalisation, and the practical applicability of these models to such tasks. These developments highlight the increased need for IDS methodologies that focus on cross-environment robustness, scalability and deployability, such as the DeepShieldIDS framework proposed here. Even though hybrid and explainable deep learning models have shown promising results, three important research gaps remain. This is due to several reasons: First and foremost, current approaches to IDS rarely generalise across datasets, as they are typically evaluated in a single benchmark environment, preventing their application to heterogeneous environments. Third, although modelling strategies have advanced, a few attack types, including infiltration and R2L, remain difficult to address, indicating that class imbalance and heterogeneous features persist. Third, while previous IDS frameworks conceptualise challenges associated with practical deployment, they fall short of formally articulating concerns regarding computational efficiency, scalability, and interpretable decision-making. Central to this work is our DeepShieldIDS framework that not only challenges two strong assumptions made in previous work (namely, that cross-dataset generalisation is not achievable, and that preprocessing must be its own module) but does so based on a more thorough appreciation of the shortcomings of the current literature than an exciting new neural architecture. We address gaps in robustness, scalability, and real-world applicability with DeepShieldIDS, a novel deep learning IDS that utilises CNN-based spatial feature extraction, LSTM-based temporal modelling, and attention-guided feature weighting within a unified evaluation pipeline.

Proposed framework

To address the challenges of the traditional IDS construct, this section introduces the DeepShieldIDS framework to improve network anomaly detection using a Hybrid deep learning model. This includes an improved preprocessing phase, a new deep learning architecture (HybridIDSNet) and real-time activities. Our system is an enhanced approach to detecting anomalies in network traffic using CNN, LSTM, and Attention.

Threat model

The proposed DeepShieldIDS framework is based on an assumption about the network monitoring environment, in which a collection of traffic flows is monitored through monitoring points, typically an intrusion detection sensor, a gateway, or a security information and event management (SIEM) system. A system that aims to classify the nature of network flows as benign or malicious, using statistical and behavioural features extracted from connection–level data. We assume that the defender has access to flow-based telemetry — rather than raw packet payloads — consistent with realistic enterprise and cloud monitoring practices. Since detection can be performed using supervised learning on labelled datasets for normal and attack behaviours in the network,

This study considered a threat landscape in which the adversary can generate various types of malicious traffic, such as scanning, brute-force attempts, DoS activity, and other abnormal behaviours that benchmark datasets like CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 commonly include. The adversary is, in one sense, very capable; they know how to change traffic patterns, timing, or some aspect of the connection from what the model expects to escape detection. In another sense, they appear to have only limited knowledge, in that they cannot be assumed to know all parameters of the trained model, nor the training pipeline by which it was created. In this evaluation, the closely related malice patterns are localised from heterogeneous traffic distributions, especially under cross-dataset testing settings that simulate realistic environmental changes in the feature space and network behaviours.

Unseen attacks are understood as distributions or traffic patterns that differ from those in the training set, rather than as formally described zero-day attacks within this framework. The study uses a supervised setting for intrusion classification, where model performance is assessed on labelled data and compared against baseline machine learning and deep learning models. Although the deployment architecture shows a conceptual way the framework may fit into enterprise monitoring pipelines, quantitative real-time deployment metrics, such as latency and throughput, are beyond the scope of this work. Also note that adversarial example generation, poisoning attacks, or formal guarantees against adaptive adversaries are not part of the threat model; these issues are acknowledged as areas for future work.

A large portion of the unseen attacks in this study correspond to attack-like traffic patterns that were never presented during training due to distribution shifts across datasets and network environments. In practice, this can be evaluated through cross-dataset evaluation (i.e., training on one benchmark dataset and testing on another), the differences in traffic generation processes, feature distributions, and attack implementations causing unfamiliar conditions. Thus, unseen implies ood or cee traffic behaviour at the flow-feature level instead of cryptographically guaranteed zero-day exploits.

Dataset description and preprocessing

The performance and generalisation of DeepShieldIDS are evaluated using two popular network security datasets: CIC-IDS201745 and UNSW-NB1546. These synthetic datasets, typically including both benign and anomalous traffic at the network level, are used to train and validate HybridIDSNet for intrusion detection and anomaly classification. Because the two datasets above are derived from different network environments, they exhibit quite distinct feature distributions, making them suitable for cross-dataset evaluation and robustness to unseen traffic patterns.

The CIC-IDS2017 dataset, published by the CIC, includes labelled traffic flows of both normal and malicious behaviours. It encompasses various types of modern cyberattacks, including DDoS, brute-force attacks, botnets, web attacks, and port scanning. For each network flow instance in CIC-IDS2017, 80 + features are extracted, covering statistical, time-based, and content-based parameters. Before training, redundant and meaningless features, such as timestamps and flow IDs, must be removed.

UNSW-NB15 dataset

Provided by the ACCS, this dataset simulates real-time network attacks alongside legitimate traffic, ensuring a balanced representation. It comprises 49 features, including packet header, application-layer, and connection features. In contrast, the UNSW-NB15 dataset generates realistic hybrid attack scenarios, including fuzzers, Backdoors, Exploits, Reconnaissance, DoS, and Shellcode injections. It thus provides a more realistic and comprehensive database for evaluating IDS models against current-day attacks than the CIC-IDS2017 dataset.

Both security-driven rationale and learning-theoretic arguments inform DeepShieldIDS preprocessing decisions. To strengthen cross-environment robustness, we apply feature harmonisation and common-feature intersection to remove dataset-specific artefacts, forcing the model to learn behavioural patterns instead of dataset identifiers. Normalisation stabilises gradient updates and prevents the dominance of features with large scales during optimisation, and is a prerequisite for deep neural networks to converge. Sampling strategies for addressing class imbalance adjust the majority-to-minority sample ratio to avoid bias toward the majority class, enhance the identification of minority attacks, and preserve statistical properties during training. Preprocessing, from a security perspective, reduces noise, eliminates redundant attributes, and unifies the distributions of features that could become weak spots or lead to misleading correlations. Together, these steps enable stable training dynamics and allow the model to generalise over diverse network characteristics without adding any assumptions beyond the supervised evaluation environment.

We built the common feature space used in this study by finding the intersection of semantically equivalent flow-level attributes that exist in both data sets–CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15. Attributes with similar statistics (e.g., packet counts, duration metrics, and flow-based stats) were retained to maintain consistency among datasets, while dataset specific attributes had no direct equivalent so they were removed to reduce the dataset bias. This harmonisation strategy guarantees that the model learns behavioural patterns instead of artefacts specific to any dataset.

Feature selection before training was not performed using explicit feature-importance ranking methods (SHAP, mutual information, permutation importance, etc.). The model utilise the attention mechanism built-in in HybridIDSNet to learn feature importance implicitly during optimisation rather than the feature importance was determined beforehand. We chose to do this design to avoid additional bias from feature scoring based on dataset features and also to keep the rigidity of cross-dataset evaluation.

HybridIDSNet: deep learning model for anomaly detection

The DeepShieldIDS framework is primarily built around the HybridIDSNet model, as shown in Fig. 3, which serves as its backbone and incorporates several deep learning components to detect anomalies in network traffic data. Several machine learning-based intrusion detection systems utilize the feature engineering approach and require high-effort pre-processing with a thorough understanding of the data; benefiting from a significant portion of the execution time, the HybridIDSNet framework does not include the involvement of extracting features; therefore, it adopts (CNNs), (LSTMs), and Attention Mechanisms to learn spatial and temporal characteristics in the enormous amount of network traffic. This combined scheme enables the model to understand both static attack patterns and sequential dependencies in network flows, thereby enhancing its ability to detect various types of attacks.

HybridIDSNet model architecture integrating CNN, LSTM, and attention for network anomaly detection.

The LSTM/GRU units are intended to model long-term dependencies in traffic series via a memory mechanism. Whereas regular RNNs suffer from the problem of “vanishing gradients,” LSTMs include gates (input, forget, and output) that decide which information to preserve or forget, allowing for memory persistence across more extended periods. GRUs, which are an equivalent form of LSTMs at a special point in the weight space, have fewer gates than LSTMs but can perform as well with reduced computational complexity. These two attention networks and the underlying BiGRUs process traffic flows one by one in HybridIDSNet to well capture dynamic attack behaviours among packet streams.

Experimental Setup

DeepShield IDS is evaluated experimentally on an Intel Core i7 processor, 32 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU, running Ubuntu 20.04 LTS. The implementation is developed in Python 3.9, with the following key libraries: TensorFlow 2.13, scikit-learn 1.2.2, Imbalanced-learn 0.11, and Pandas 2.0.3. I used the Jupyter Notebook environment to run all the experiments.

Both benchmark datasets, CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15, were used separately and in cross-validation mode. The datasets were preprocessed by extracting a standard set of features, scaling numeric values using Min-Max normalisation (scaled to the range) and one-hot encoding for categorical variables. The SMOTE was applied to the training data.

The datasets are composed of training, validation, and test sets with proportions of 80:10:10. Empirical tuning of the HybridIDSNet model’s hyperparameters was performed with the following settings: a batch size of 64, a learning rate of 0.001, a dropout rate of 0.5, and training for 10 epochs using the Adam optimiser. The validation loss was checked to prevent early stopping and avoid overfitting. We also evaluated the models using six metrics. Accuracy metrics and ROC curves were used as visual indicators of how well HybridIDSNet classified the individual datasets and how well it generalised across them (i.e., over a cross-dataset validation).

Given the extreme class imbalance in minor attack categories, such as low-frequency probing behaviours and infiltration, SMOTE is applied to mitigate it. SMOTE focused on the minority classes of the data, synthesising examples in the feature space to provide the learning algorithm with more examples and representations of its rarest attack patterns, thereby allowing higher recall and F1-score for the majority classes without harming precision during the same trials. Nevertheless, the use of synthetic oversampling might yield distributional smoothing, thereby decreasing the sharpness of the margin between closely related attack types. For this reason, SMOTE was utilised only during model training, without model evaluation, as this would introduce an optimistic bias. The observed gains should then be interpreted as improved learning of minority patterns in supervised settings, but not as (explicitly) robustness to rare or adaptive attack strategies.

Since SMOTE generates synthetic samples by interpolation, it can oversmooth decision boundaries and create feature combinations that are impossible in practice. To mitigate this risk, oversampling and virtual noise generation are applied only to the training split, while evaluation is performed on the original, non-transformed test data.

While SMOTE improves class balance, it operates in feature space and does not explicitly preserve temporal dependencies inherent in sequential network traffic. As a result, some synthetic samples may not fully reflect realistic temporal patterns. This limitation is acknowledged and will be addressed in future work through sequence-aware data augmentation techniques.

Error analysis of misclassified attack types and robustness evaluation

Finally, a quantitative error analysis is carried out using confusion matrices across generations and class-wise detection behaviour, extending beyond overall accuracy metrics on the CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 datasets. At the macro level, HybridIDSNet performs well, and residual misclassification occurs only for low-frequency attack types and for traffic patterns that behave similarly. In this sub-section, we study the distribution of this error & discuss how it affects practical intrusion detection.

For CIC-IDS2017, most misclassifications are anti-fine-grained web-based attacks and reconnaissance traffic. WebAttack and PortScan cause greater confusion with benign or other probing categories due to noticeable similarities in flow-level statistics, including short-lived connections and overlapping service features. Our quantitative analysis shows that the false negative rates for dominant attack families (e.g., DoS, brute-force) are lower than 5%, but misclassification percentages are larger for minority categories. The results suggest that HybridIDSNet can consistently maintain separation from large-scale attacks, but has difficulty differentiating rare behaviours in a few sample instances.

Similar patterns can be seen in UNSW-NB15, where the reconnaissance and generic anomaly classes sometimes appear in the same layers, indicating that probing-based attacks share similar features. Cross-dataset evaluation indicates a slight increase in false negatives due to distribution shifts, confirming the need for feature harmonisation and fair preprocessing. Crucially, nearly all misclassifications are limited to closely related categories rather than large-scale attack-versus-benign confusion, suggesting that the model retains strong high-level discrimination power.

As shown in Table 8, HybridIDSNet retains high detection rates across major classes but suffers misclassifications against minority or behaviourally overlapping attack types. These observations suggest that the greater challenge is fine-grained class discrimination rather than coarse-scale anomaly detection.

Cross-dataset performance summary

We evaluated the generalisation capability of HybridIDSNet using a cross-dataset validation strategy (i.e., training on one dataset and evaluating on the other dataset and vice versa). Data distribution shift is a common issue in real-world deployment of intrusion detection systems, where systems are expected to encounter a new traffic distribution for which they have not been trained.

The results demonstrate that HybridIDSNet achieves consistently high performance in cross-dataset settings, with a marginal decrease relative to single-dataset evaluations. This shows that the model successfully learns inherent natural patterns in network traffic rather than merely fitting specific features of the dataset.

The proposed feature harmonisation strategy aligns heterogeneous feature spaces across datasets, and the hybrid CNN–LSTM–attention architecture jointly captures spatial and temporal dependencies, thereby producing the observed robustness. These components help the model generalise across various network environments and perform better on unseen attack patterns.

Ablation study of HybridIDSNet components

An ablation study was performed to analyse the role of each architectural component in HybridIDSNet by comparing it with simplified variants that represent only spatial learning (CNN Only) or only temporal learning (LSTM Only), as well as with classical machine-learning approaches. In this analysis, we aimed to explore whether the performance gains were primarily due to the CNN, LSTM, and attention modules, while excluding gains attributable solely to dataset preprocessing or optimisation. For consistent comparison, accuracy and ROC–AUC are employed for analysis across both CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 datasets.

CNN Only: CNN Only achieved 96.88% accuracy & 98.15% ROC–AUC on the CIC-IDS2017 dataset, a weak model due to feature effects from spatial flow patterns. For the LSTM-only model, which performed slightly worse, with 96.42% accuracy and 97.95% ROC–AUC, this implies that sequential modelling alone is insufficient to capture discriminative features in heterogeneous traffic flows. Overall performance improved to 97.98% accuracy and 99.05% ROC–AUC when the attention mechanism is integrated with both modules in HybridIDSNet. This shows that the attention layer improves temporal feature prioritisation by adaptively weighting patterns, thereby capturing the informative patterns learned from CNN–LSTM representations.

On the UNSW-NB15 dataset, a similar trend is noticed. Compared to the CNN Only of 96.40 and the LSTM Only of 96.00. By incorporating a performance upgrade over HybridIDSNet in spatio-temporal modelling, we achieved 98.02% accuracy and 99.12% ROC–AUC, indicating that these features combine to improve generalisation across traffic distributions. Also shown is a gain in Recall and F1-score over standard class-balancing and class-confusion-matrix measures, indicating that our approach improves the detection of minority attack classes rather than simply optimising the majority class at the expense of minority-class accuracy.

Discussion

In the DeepShieldIDS framework, the experimental results on the UNSW-NB15 and CIC-IDS2017 datasets show that HybridIDSNet demonstrates high robustness and strong adaptation across different attack categories and network environment scenarios. In conclusion, high performance across independent and cross-dataset testing indicates that the model generalises beyond the properties of a single dataset.

The hybrid architecture of HybridIDSNet, which integrates CNNs to extract spatial feature patterns in traffic flows, LSTM to learn sequential traffic behaviours, and an attention mechanism to emphasise key traffic features, supports robust model performance. Using this combination, the model successfully identifies both high-volume DoS attacks and low-volume attacks, such as Probe and Infiltration attacks.

HybridIDSNet achieves near-perfect classification results on key attack types, including DoS and BruteForce, in the CIC-IDS2017 dataset. At the same time, it performs competitively on low-frequency categories (2.1703% and 2.3993%, respectively) (i.e., WebAttack and Infiltration). Third, the examination of the confusion matrix and ROC-AUC curves confirms that the model maintains a fairly well-balanced false-positive-to-negative ratio across classes, despite the initial class distribution imbalance.

In this way, the model achieved high accuracy across various attack types and distributions (e.g., DoS and Probe attacks in the UNSW-NB15 dataset), including r2l and u2r across the entire dataset. Although a few low-frequency classes are slightly misclassified, overall performance is better than that of traditional ML and of the entirely separable DL baselines.

These cross-dataset validation experiments further confirm the model’s generalisation capability to new, unseen networks and attack profiles. HybridIDSNet achieves high ROC-AUC and F1 scores when training and testing on the datasets, demonstrating its generalisation and robustness to dataset characteristics that affect laboratory results.

However, we can use a cross-dataset test to evaluate DeepShieldIDS’s robustness to heterogeneous traffic distributions and assess its sensitivity to dataset bias. Even if the proposed preprocessing pipeline reduces feature differences through harmonisation and normalisation, benchmark datasets may still contain artefacts resulting from traffic-generation procedures, such as class imbalance. Thus, performance gains can only be understood in the context of supervised benchmarking rather than as a promise of robustness against adversarial manipulation. From an adversarial standpoint, the current work assumes attackers can alter traffic patterns or behaviour but does not address adversarial training, evasion attack simulation, or poisoning. As a consequence, robustness claims are interpreted in terms of distributional generalisation instead of formal adversarial security guarantees. Perturbation-based testing under domain-adaptation strategies and bias-aware evaluation protocols will be useful for future work to enhance the robustness of reliable deployment further.

Overall, the findings illustrate HybridIDSNet’s consistent performance across different attack categories and varied traffic distributions in all benchmark environments analysed. The results indicate that DeepShieldIDS could offer a relatively robust framework for detecting anomalies across such diverse network conditions. Still, generalisations are limited to supervised experimentation rather than real-world deployment. Since this study does not cover adversarial training, temporal holdout testing, or real-world deployment telemetry, any interpretation of exposed vs. unseen attack behaviour is limited to cross-dataset generalisation analysis and should not be regarded as formal zero-day detection claims.

While SMOTE improves minority-class recall, other imbalance methods, such as cost-sensitive loss functions or focal loss, were considered for complementary use. We will explore these methods in our future work, which do not require synthetic data generation and may better preserve sequential characteristics.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge some limitations: formal security guarantees cannot be provided, as the proposed DeepShieldIDS framework is evaluated under supervised benchmark conditions. The detection performance is limited to statistical generalisation, but not to adaptive or zero-day adversaries, since the model is trained and tested on labelled datasets. Second, the work does not consider adversarial training, evasion attack simulation or poisoning scenarios and therefore robustness claims can only be made in terms of generalisation (cross-dataset evaluation) rather than adversarial robustness. Third, while a deployment-oriented architecture is described, experimental validation against real-time numerical metrics such as latency, throughput, and stress testing in production environments is not included; thus, operational performance cannot be guaranteed in a commercial enterprise setting. Moreover, feature harmonisation and sampling strategies may inject biases typical of benchmark datasets, thereby affecting model functionality in real-world scenarios that remain unseen. This indicates a clear opening in the literature for more adversarial evaluation, temporal holdout, and full develop-to-deploy studies to demonstrate the efficacy of these safety mechanisms.

Conclusion and future work

In this work, we presented a novel hybrid AI-based intrusion detection framework, called DeepShieldIDS, that uses the HybridIDSNet model to classify flow-based intrusions in heterogeneous network environments. In this paper, we present HybridIDSNet, which combines spatial feature extraction via convolutional neural networks, sequential modelling with LSTM, and an attention mechanism that highlights more informative traffic features. Extensive experiments on the CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 benchmark datasets show that our proposed framework outperforms traditional machine learning models and standalone deep learning baselines in detection performance. The full Cross-dataset evaluation also shows that this may generalise better across varying traffic distributions and thus be more robust to dataset-specific biases in a supervised benchmarking setting. Instead of focusing on novel architectural designs, the study highlights a structured evaluation strategy that includes spatial–temporal modelling and attention, providing higher classification consistency with fewer false positives. While the framework is built with deployment-centric thinking and API integration, quantitative real-time deployment measurements, such as latency and throughput, are out of scope for this work. This means that conclusions are always limited to controlled experimental validation rather than deployable operational claims. We discuss a few future directions for research. First, we can extend the framework by adding packet-level features and/or raw traffic traces, which may facilitate the representation learning of more complex traffic behaviour rather than relying on class labels. Next, applying explainable AI (XAI) methods may enhance interpretability by helping cybersecurity analysts understand model decisions. Third, validation in different contexts, for example, industrial control systems, IoT networks, and next-generation 5G infrastructures, might be the topics of future studies. Lastly, federated learning approaches may be explored for collaborative intrusion detection across distributed networks without compromising data confidentiality. These directions are oriented towards making hybrid deep learning–based intrusion detection systems even more adaptable and practical.
