Bioinspired porous metal-amino acid nanozymes enable multi-pathway treatment of atherosclerosis through anti-oxidation and pro-efferocytosis
Authors: Sihui Shao, Wenqi Pan, Jingyun Cheng, Yi Zheng, Liang Chen, Yu Chen, Rong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherosclerosis is characterized by chronic inflammation, persistent oxidative stress, intimal lipid accumulation, and impaired efferocytosis within plaques. Here, we develop a copper-based porous nanozyme assembled through metal-amino acid coordination (MAzyme) for loading an inhibitor of the antiphagocytic CD47-SIRPα signaling pathway and cloaking with macropahge membrane. The resulting biomimetic nanoplatform (MMAzyme-S) inherits both the multi-enzymatic activities of the porous nanozyme and the inflammation-homing features of the biomembranes. The prominent antioxidant effects of MAzyme, comparable to those of natural superoxide dismutase, enable efficient inflammatory resolution via the elimination of reactive oxygen species. Our findings also demonstrate that MMAzyme-S can block CD47-mediated anti-phagocytosis to restore impaired efferocytosis in lesional macrophages, thereby boosting the phagocytic clearance of apoptotic cells. Additionally, cholesterol transport pathways are strengthened through synergistic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, which profoundly reduce foam cell formation and plaque burden. Consequently, with the distinctive advantages of homologous inflammation targeting and multilink intervention in pathogenesis, MMAzyme-S reduces atherosclerotic plaque burden in male ApoE−/− mice. This study highlights the potential of biomimetic porous MAzyme with combined antioxidant and pro-efferocytosis activities as a multifaceted intervention strategy against atherosclerosis.Subject terms: Biomaterials - cells, Biomedical materials, Atherosclerosis

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a lipoprotein-driven inflammatory process that underlies cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and is the leading cause of global mortality and morbidity1–3. AS is a multifactorial pathology characterized by a complex interplay between chronic inflammation and lipid accumulation in the arterial wall4,5. In the early stages, oxidative modification and intimal infiltration of plasma lipoproteins trigger endothelial cell activation and dysfunction, resulting in activated inflammatory responses and overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS)3,6. Subsequently, persistent local inflammation promotes further recruitment of monocytes that differentiate into macrophages, which engulf oxidized low-density lipoproteins (ox-LDL) to form foam cells, a hallmark of early atheromas. While foam cell formation is beneficial for lowering the concentration of pro-inflammatory lipoproteins at the early stage, excessive digestion and cholesterol retention induce premature death of foam cells, laying the foundation for the generation of a plaque necrotic core3,7. Understandably, this pro-atherogenic spiral of atherosclerotic plaque development necessitates the concurrent resolution of inflammation and inhibition of the accumulation of debris and apoptotic cells (ACs) for effective AS treatment5. However, non-specific targeting of inflammation may negatively affect endogenous innate defense mechanisms and compromise plaque-resolving immune processes8,9. Efferocytosis, a highly regulated process that favors the phagocytic clearance and degradation of ACs, is undesirably impaired due to the abnormal upregulation of CD47 in lipid-rich atherosclerosis plaques10–13. CD47 functions as a ligand for the signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα) on macrophages. Upon binding, SIRPα recruits and activates Src homology 2 domain-containing phosphatase-1 (SHP-1), initiating an intracellular signaling cascade that suppresses phagocytic activity14–16. Defective efferocytosis is believed to be the primary contributor to necrotic core formation and persistent vascular inflammation17. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop alternative therapeutic strategies that possess multifaceted properties to simultaneously address inflammatory microenvironments and vulnerable plaques.

Given that ROS overproduction significantly contributes to an array of pathological processes in atherosclerosis, including the formation of ox-LDL, endothelial cell dysfunction, and macrophage polarization and infiltration, antioxidant biomaterials with ROS-scavenging capabilities have emerged as promising platforms to overcome the above-mentioned limitations18,19. Several studies have demonstrated that nanozymes can significantly attenuate pro-atherogenic inflammation and apoptosis in macrophages based on their multi-enzymatic mimicking antioxidant activities20. However, the unsatisfactory clinical outcomes of anti-inflammation treatment suggest that a single-mode ROS-eliminating strategy using antioxidant nanozymes may be suboptimal in addressing the multiple contributors to atherosclerosis. Recently, the drug-carrying capability of antioxidant nanozymes has been harnessed to load various small molecules, such as inflammation-resolving lipid mediators21 and anti-inflammation drugs22,23, for enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Although the distinctive benefits of nanozymes in lipid regulation24, autophagy activation25, and anti-senescence26–28 for AS treatment have been previously revealed, the collaboration between antioxidant nanozymes and efferocytosis has rarely been explored. However, several drawbacks of the currently developed nanozymes, including low surface area, inability to collaboratively address multiple pathogenic factors, and insufficient targeting ability, warrant the development of antioxidant nanozymes that can seamlessly integrate abundant porosity, high enzymatic activity, and precise pathologic targeting modules for multilink AS treatment. Inspired by the intriguing metal-ion binding sites and conformational changes in natural metalloenzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), metal coordination-driven assembly has drawn mounting interest for the innovative fabrication of nanozymes with protein-like nanoarchitectonics and comparable catalytic activities29–32. In particular, amino acids can serve as preferable building blocks with simpler structures than proteins and peptides for coordination with metal ions to construct artificial nanozymes33,34. However, the formation of porous metallonanozymes remains a formidable challenge.

Here, we designed and engineered a metal-amino acid nanozyme (MAzyme) assembled from copper ions and glycine under alkaline conditions (Fig. 1a). The prepared copper-based MAzyme features a distinct porous nanostructure that is generated from the spontaneous packing of glycine-stabilized small nanocrystals35. Benefiting from that, MAzyme not only exhibits favorable antioxidant enzyme-mimicking activities but also serves as a nanocarrier for an inhibitor of SHP1 (SHP1i) to interrupt the antiphagocytic CD47-SIRPα signaling pathway between damaged cells and phagocytes. Subsequently, the inhibitor-loaded MAzyme (MAzyme-S) was camouflaged with macrophage membranes. The resulting biomimetic nanozyme (MMAzyme-S) integrated several distinctive characteristics designed for AS management (Fig. 1b). First, macrophage membrane cloaking endows MMAzyme-S with plaque-specific targeting ability via inflammatory homing behavior36,37. Second, the ROS-scavenging effect of copper-containing MAzyme significantly attenuates oxidative stress and inflammation in the plaque microenvironment, further facilitating the restoration of damaged vascular endothelium function7. Third, the loaded SHP1i can block the antiphagocytic CD47-SIRPα signaling pathway to specifically renovate the defective efferocytosis, thereby boosting the clearance of apoptotic debris and inflammatory reversal. Finally, the orchestrated multifaceted anti-atherogenic effects improve cholesterol efflux and maintain homeostasis, thus substantially reducing the formation of foam cells. Mechanistically, this effect can be attributed to the upregulation of ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) and ATP-binding cassette transporter G1 (ABCG1), which are responsible for reverse cholesterol transport (RCT). As the synergistic anti-atherogenic effects of antioxidants and pro-efferocytosis has not been previously developed, to the best of our knowledge, the engineered biomimetic MMAzyme-S can be utilized as a pathogenesis-specific nanoplatform for multifaceted atherosclerosis treatment.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of MMAzyme-S

Biomimetic coordination interactions between metal ions and amino acids have been harnessed as a reliable route for fabricating a wide range of nanomaterials with exceptional functions38–40. To synthesize a porously structured nanozyme, glycine was selected as the coordinating molecule and divalent copper ions as the inorganic precursor. Initially, Cu (II) ions were coordinated with glycine to obtain the Gly-Cu (II) complex. The chemical structure and purity of the Gly-Cu (II) complex were verified via 1H nuclear magnetic resonance and high-resolution mass spectrometry (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Hydrolysis was then performed under alkaline and ambient conditions to form crystal-packed mesoporous MAzyme (Fig. 2a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images indicated that the prepared MAzyme displayed a distinct spherical morphology composed of small, closely packed nanocrystals with a relatively rough surface and an average size of ~131.76 nm (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3). The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image also revealed an unambiguous crystalline lattice (Fig. 2c), suggesting that the MAzyme is constituted by small subcrystals. The corresponding elemental mapping results showed uniform distributions of Cu, O, N, and C (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 4) and quantified the atomic composition of the MAzyme (Supplementary Table 1), revealing atomic ratios of Cu:O (1.24), Cu:C (0.61), and C:O (2.04). Nanoscale MAzyme can only be produced in the presence of a glycine-coordinated complex; otherwise, the direct hydrolysis of copper (II) salts only leads to the precipitation of undispersed copper hydroxide (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Since Gly-Cu (II) molecules remained dispersed in the solution and failed to form nanoparticles with a specific morphology in the absence of hydroxide ions, we utilized molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) to elucidate the role of hydroxide ions in the formation of functional nanozymes. First, three models containing 60 Gly-Cu (II) molecules (60 copper ions and 120 glycine molecules) and different numbers of sodium hydroxide molecules (0 sodium hydroxide for Model 1, 20 sodium hydroxide for Model 2, and 60 sodium hydroxide for Model 3) were constructed. The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) decreased as molecules aggregated to form nanostructures, making it an important indicator of molecular aggregation. According to the simulation convergence parameter analysis, no significant decrease in SASA was observed in Model 1 without sodium hydroxide. The Gly-Cu (II) molecules tend to aggregate as the number of NaOH molecules increases. Notably, when the molecular ratio of NaOH to Gly-Cu (II) reached 1:1, the SASA value experienced the most significant decrease, dropping sharply from the initial 94.830 to 50.719 nm2 and stabilizing at 10 ns (Supplementary Fig. 6a). As another reliable index for the stability of the simulation systems, the root mean square deviation fluctuated remarkably without the introduction of NaOH, whereas it decreased rapidly and plateaued in Model 3 (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Next, conformational clustering analysis was performed to simulate the self-assembly behavior of the molecules. Gly-Cu (II) molecules were dispersed after 50 ns of MDS in the absence of NaOH and presented a modest aggregation tendency with the assistance of 20 NaOH (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 7). Consistent with the above results, tightly integrated and stable nanoclusters were formed only with a further increase in the NaOH concentration (Fig. 2g). Besides, the number of hydrogen bonds between the Gly-Cu (II) molecules increased rapidly and stabilized at 30 ns in Model 3 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Finally, we analyzed the molecules located at the center to further investigate the intermolecular interaction patterns in the entire composite system (Supplementary Fig. 9). Taken together, hydroxyl ions act as a binder to connect a coordination network between Gly-Cu (II) molecules, which enabled the continuously aggregation and the final formation of a relatively stable nanocluster structure.

Next, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to survey the chemical compositions of MAzyme (Fig. 2h). The broad characteristic bands at 3567, 3295, 919, and 706 cm−1 are associated with the stretching and bending vibrations of the -OH groups from the subcrystals induced by the coordinated interactions of metal ions and glycine41. The bands at 1602 and 1386 cm−1 can be assigned to the asymmetric and symmetric vibrations of COO- from glycine42, further demonstrating the presence of amino acid coordination effects in MAzyme. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was also used to measure the elemental composition of MAzyme (Supplementary Fig. 10). The peaks in the high-resolution Cu spectrum at binding energies of 934.7 eV and 954.5 eV were assigned to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2, respectively. Additionally, a satellite peak at 943.1 eV between Cu 2p1/2 and Cu 2p3/2 corresponded to Cu2+ in the paramagnetic chemical state, confirming the existence of Cu2+ in MAzyme (Fig. 2i). X-ray diffraction patterns of MAzyme showed characteristic diffraction peaks at 16.73°, 23.83°, 34.09°, 35.92°, 39.81°, 53.39°, and 64.71°, corresponding to the (020), (021), (002), (111), (130), (132), and (152) planes of orthorhombic copper hydroxide, respectively (Fig. 2j). Furthermore, nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured to characterize the porous structures. Accordingly, MAzyme exhibited a typical type-IV curve with a specific surface area of 101.8 m2/g, calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller method, and a pore size of ~10.3 nm, which is beneficial for subsequent drug loading (Fig. 2k).

The synthesis parameters were optimized by varying the precursor concentrations and reaction temperatures. TEM images showed that the size of the assembled nanostructures increased significantly with increasing concentrations of the glycine-copper precursor. In addition, the morphology evolved from spherical nanoparticles to irregular microfibers (Supplementary Fig. 11). The impact of the reaction temperature was evaluated by performing the assembly process at different temperatures (RT, 55, 75, and 90 °C). As expected, the diameter of the obtained nanostructures increased with increasing temperature. MAzyme formed at room temperature exhibited the most uniform spherical morphology (Supplementary Fig. 12), which was ascribed to the accelerated assembly rate at high temperatures. Therefore, an appropriately low concentration of the Gly-Cu (II) complex at room temperature was used to prepare the copper-composed MAzyme.

As a small-molecule inhibitor of CD47’s downstream effector, SHP1i was loaded into MAzyme to rescue defective efferocytosis. Owing to the abundant mesopores of MAzyme, SHP1i was loaded into the void space via physical adsorption without any obvious alteration in its nanoscale morphology (Supplementary Fig. 13). The visible color change in the MAzyme and MAzyme-S solutions mirrored the successful encapsulation of SHP1i (Supplementary Fig. 14). In addition, nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were obtained to characterize the porous structures of MAzyme after SHP1i loading. Compared with MAzyme, the pore size of MAzyme-S decreased to ~8.5 nm with a corresponding specific surface area decline (64.6 m2/g) (Supplementary Fig. 15), reflecting the loading of SHP1i into the void space. Finally, ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra showed the characteristic absorption peaks of SHP1i at 230, 320, and 490 nm in the MAzyme-S sample (Supplementary Fig. 16), further confirming the efficient loading of SHP1i. The loading efficiency (LE) of SHP1i on MAzyme was approximately 14.6%. Subsequently, to construct biomimetic MMAzyme, macrophage membranes (MM) derived from RAW 264.7 were coated on the surface of MAzyme via a typical ultrasonic method43. As shown in Fig. 2l, MMAzyme exhibited a similar spherical structure, with a single layer of MM at approximately 9 nm wrapped around the MAzyme. Western blot analysis further demonstrated the presence of the main membrane antigens on the surfaces of MMAzyme and MM extracts, such as CD36, CD47, and chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2), confirming the successful coating of MM on MAzyme (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 17). Meanwhile, the surface zeta potential changed from positive to negative after MM decoration (Fig. 2n). Dynamic light scattering analysis confirmed that the average hydrodynamic diameter of the assembled nanozyme increased from 178.5 to 211.4 nm after membrane coating, while maintaining a uniform size distribution and high dispersibility, with polydispersity indices (PDI) of 0.105 and 0.076 for MAzyme and MMAzyme (Fig. 2o), respectively. Furthermore, both MAzyme and MMAzyme showed no significant change in average size after storage for 7 days, verifying their favorable colloidal stability (Supplementary Fig. 18). In addition, the drug-release behavior of SHP1i was evaluated. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, MAzyme-S and MMAzyme-S exhibited similar releasing profiles of SHP1i. The cumulative release rate of SHP1i was markedly accelerated for both samples in the acidic solution (pH 5.5) compared to that in the neutral solution (pH 7.4). This may be attributed to the weakened adsorption and improved dissolution of the inhibitor molecules under acidic conditions. Nevertheless, such porous carriers can be further optimized through sophisticated modifications, such as pH-responsive polymer incorporation or other gatekeeper strategies, to enhance their controlled-release performance44,45. Altogether, a biomimetic MMAzyme-S nanoplatform with exceptional physicochemical properties was successfully constructed for AS treatment.

Enzyme-mimicking activities of MAzyme

The multiple enzyme-mimicking properties of MAzyme were assessed, and its universal catalytic abilities are schematically illustrated in Fig. 3a. Electron spin resonance (ESR) was conducted to preliminarily explore the ROS-scavenging activities of MAzyme using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin trap for hydroxyl radical (•OH) and superoxide anion (O2•−). The intensity of characteristic ESR signals declined dramatically after introducing the MAzyme in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3b, c), implying the capacity of MAzyme in catalyzing the elimination of •OH and O2•−. We further confirmed the enzymatic activities of MAzyme, including SOD, CAT, GPx, and POD. As one of the major antioxidant enzymes, SOD exerts a critical protecting role against various oxidative stress-related diseases by catalyzing the disproportionation of O2•− into O2 and H2O246. Typically, the SOD-mimetic activity of a nanozyme is evaluated using nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) as a probe. The characteristic absorption at 550 nm remarkably declined after the introduction of MAzyme into the O2•− generation system (Supplementary Fig. 20a). The scavenging rate of O2•− reached approximately 80.69% at the MAzyme concentration of 40 μg mL−1 (Supplementary Fig. 20b), demonstrating the prominent O2•− quenching ability of MAzyme. Furthermore, a commercial test kit (WST-8 assay) was used to profile the SOD-like activity of the MAzyme synthesized at different temperatures. Notably, SOD activity rapidly weakened as the temperature increased (Supplementary Fig. 21), highlighting the structural and intrinsic antioxidant properties of the MAzyme synthesized under mild temperatures. Subsequently, the SOD-mimicking performance of the MMAzyme with the MM coating was evaluated. Unsurprisingly, both MAzyme and MMAzyme exhibited dose-dependent O2•− elimination effectiveness, scavenging approximately 86.06% and 74.59% of O2•− at a low dose of 40 μg mL−1, respectively (Fig. 3d). MAzyme (IC50: 26.97 μg mL−1) displayed a superior SOD-like activity of 1853.9 U/mg, exceedingg the activities reported for a diversity of previously reported SOD-like nanozymes (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 2).

CAT is another essential antioxidant enzyme capable of ameliorating oxidative stress by decomposing H2O2 into O2 and H2O. The CAT-like activity of MAzyme was assessed by monitoring the change in the absorbance of H2O2 at 240 nm and detecting the generated O2. In the presence of MAzyme, there was a notable reduction in the characteristic absorbance of H2O2, accompanied by increased O2 production over time in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3f, g). According to the Michaelis–Menten (Fig. 3h) and Lineweaver–Burk plot (Fig. 3i) curves, the maximum velocity (Vmax) and Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) were determined to be 12.11 mg L−1 min−1 and 182.38 mM, respectively. MAzyme exhibited a comparable CAT-mimicking behavior and optimized steady-state kinetics with H2O2 as the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Table 3). Among the antioxidant enzymes, glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and POD are responsible for detoxifying H2O2 and play crucial roles in sustaining redox homeostasis in the body. Thus, POD-like activity of MAzyme was examined using 3,5,3′,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as the chromogenic substrate. When MAzyme catalyzes H2O2 into H2O, TMB is converted into blue oxidized TMB (TMBox) with a characteristic absorbance at 652 nm. While no obvious color variation was detected in the control system without MAzyme or H2O2, the absorbance at 652 nm increased with increasing MAzyme concentrations (Fig. 3j). This observation explicitly corroborates the POD-like activity of MAzyme. The GPx-like activity of MAzyme was probed using a GPx assay kit based on a classical glutathione reductase (GR)-coupled assay. Typically, GPx detoxifies organic peroxides, accompanied by the transformation of reduced GSH into oxidized glutathione (GSSG). Subsequently, GSSG is reduced to GSH with the assistance of GR while simultaneously consuming coenzyme nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). Accordingly, a decrease in the absorption of NADPH at 340 nm was used as an indicator. It can be found that MAzyme enabled the continuous GSH-GSSG transformation and NADPH consumption, as evidenced by the distinctly declined absorbance at 340 nm compared to the control system, revealing the GPx-like activity of MAzyme (Fig. 3k).

Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were used to examine the electronic structures and reaction mechanisms of copper hydroxide and MAzyme to elucidate the contributions of glycine to the electronic configuration and catalytic performance. First, the structural models of pure copper hydroxide and MAzyme were optimized (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Fig. 23). The pathways and corresponding free-energy diagrams for SOD and CAT reactions were mapped (Supplementary Fig. 24). For CAT, the adsorption energy of H2O2 on MAzyme (−0.86 eV) was more negative than that of copper hydroxide (−0.39 eV) (Fig. 3m), implying the energetically advantageous for H2O2 adsorption. Subsequently, H2O2 decomposed into H2O* and O* with an energy drop. Then, the H2O molecule desorbed with an energy increase of 0.75 eV, which is the rate-determining step (RDS) for copper hydroxide. Subsequently, H+ was transferred to O* to form OH*, which underwent a second H+ transfer to form H2O*. Ultimately, the H2O molecule desorbed from MAzyme with an energy increase of 0.53 eV, which is the RDS for MAzyme. The dissociation of H2O reverted MAzyme to its initial state and allowed it to participate in subsequent catalytic reactions. For the SOD path, the reaction started as the adsorption of O2•− (HOO•) on a Cu atom. Subsequently, the O2 molecule was released, leaving H* species on the Cu atom (Fig. 3n). The energy barrier for this RDS was reduced by MAzyme (1.25 eV) compared with that of copper hydroxide (1.69 eV). Subsequently, another HOO• group approached and reacted with H* to form H2O2, which finally desorbed and returned the MAzyme to its initial state. The results demonstrated that glycine coordination accelerated electron transfer in O2•− and H2O2 decomposition, leading to a low energy barrier for the RDS of MAzyme and promoting catalytic efficiency.

In addition, the projected density of states (pDOS) and electron localization function (ELF) were employed to further reveal the hybridization of the electronic states and the catalytic mechanism. As exhibited in Fig. 3o, the position of the d-band center of the Cu orbital for MAzyme (−1.977 eV) was closer to the Fermi level than copper hydroxide (−2.153 eV), facilitating the stronger interaction between the Cu atom and HOO• and charge transfer acceleration. Moreover, ELF (Fig. 3p, q) depicted more intensified and delocalized electrons of Cu-O bonds in MAzyme than in copper hydroxide, implying an enhanced electron transfer ability for rapid cycles of the redox process with the coordination of glycine. Calculations of the pDOS indicated that the adsorbed O atom in HOO• was strongly coupled and hybridized with the Cu 3d orbitals of MAzyme, facilitating the catalytic process (Supplementary Fig. 25). Such strengthened metal-oxygen covalent bonding enables electron hopping as well as the variation of chemical states. A higher covalency bonding was observed between the Cu and N atoms of glycine (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26), thereby impacting the electrons of the Cu-O bonds by enhancing the charge-binding ability of copper. These DFT calculations confirmed that the coordination of glycine was responsible for improving the SOD-mimicking activities of MAzyme.

In vitro anti-oxidation and anti-inflammatory abilities of MMAzyme-S

Oxidative stress and inflammation play critical roles in the initiation and progression of AS plaque7. Hence, the synthesized antioxidant MAzyme is expected to concurrently exert ROS-scavenging and anti-inflammation effects, thereby restoring vascular homeostasis and vascular cell function. We evaluated the cytotoxicity of MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S in RAW 264.7 cells and HUVECs. After co-incubation with different samples at various concentrations for 24 h, no obvious cytotoxicity was observed in two cell lines at the concentrations ranging from 0 to 40 μg mL−1 (Supplementary Fig. 27). We evaluated the effect of MM decoration on the cellular uptake of MAzyme. Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis indicated that the internalization rate of bare MAzyme by normal macrophages reached a maximum of 68.07% at 12 h (Fig. 4a), whereas only 52.33% of MMAzyme was internalized at the same time point. This may be explained by the fact that MM cloaking can maximally block the unwanted peripheral macrophage clearance of MMAzyme47,48. Because macrophages in atherosclerotic plaques generally suffer from inflammatory conditions, the internalization profiles of these two samples by inflammatory macrophages were further explored. Inflammatory macrophages exhibited a higher phagocytic ability towards both MAzyme and MMAzyme49,50 (Fig. 4a). Although the MM-coated MMAzyme achieved a comparable uptake rate (84.17%) to that of MAzyme (83.5%) at 12 h, our time-course uptake assays revealed that MMAzyme exhibited a faster internalization rate (Supplementary Fig. 28), with higher uptake observed as early as the 6-h time point (65.13% vs. 47.2%). This was largely due to the high affinity of macrophage membranes for inflammatory cells. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images consistently showed a noticeable fluorescence signal in LPS macrophages incubated with both FITC-labeled MAzyme and MMAzyme (Fig. 4b). We further assessed the cellular uptake of MMAzyme across three major plaque cell types (macrophages, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells). The results demonstrated that macrophages exhibited significantly higher and more rapid uptake of MMAzyme (Supplementary Fig. 29), which might be attributed to their inherent homotypic-targeting capacity51,52. These findings demonstrate that the MM-camouflaged strategy can circumvent macrophage phagocytosis during circulation and simultaneously ensure the inflammatory-homing properties of the developed nanozyme.

ROS are normally required to regulate cellular signaling and redox homeostasis, whereas ROS overproduction leads to aberrant intracellular oxidative stress, accelerates inflammation, and significantly contributes to AS progression4,53,54. Therefore, we evaluated whether MMAzyme-S could eliminate excess ROS in lesional macrophages. The fluorescent probe DCFH-DA was used to detect intracellular ROS. Untreated and H2O2-treated cells were used as the negative control and oxidative stress groups, respectively. Intense green fluorescence was observed in the H2O2-treated group in the CLSM image, suggesting high ROS levels in the macrophages induced by H2O2. Conversely, the fluorescent signals were distinctly weakened after treatment with MAzyme, MAzyme-S, or MMAzyme-S, primarily owing to the multiple antioxidant enzyme-like properties of the copper-containing nanozymes (Fig. 4c). Remarkably, MMAzyme-S exhibited the weakest fluorescence, highlighting its superior antioxidant activity. To simulate the inflammatory microenvironment within AS plaque, RAW 264.7 cells were further pretreated with LPS as an additional oxidative stress model, and the pronounced fluorescence intensity in the model group revealed excessive ROS generation. Similarly, all MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S exerted antioxidant effects, as evidenced by reduced ROS signals, particularly in the MMAzyme-S group (Supplementary Fig. 30). Furthermore, quantitative FCM analysis proved that the overproduction of intracellular ROS could be significantly scavenged by nanozyme treatment, with the strongest efficacy detected in the MMAzyme-S group (Fig. 4d, e). These results definitively identify MMAzyme-S as a potent ROS-scavenging agent against oxidative stress. Subsequently, their protective effects against H2O2-mediated oxidative cytotoxicity were evaluated. While H2O2 caused obvious cytotoxicity on RAW 264.7 cells (Supplementary Fig. 31), MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S effectively mitigated H2O2-mediated oxidative damage within the concentrations ranging from 2.5 to 10 μg mL−1 (Fig. 4f). The cellular viability of injured macrophages increased from 41.69 to 83.51% with the addition of 10 μg mL−1 of MMAzyme-S, further confirming its robust antioxidant protective efficacy.

AS involves inflammation from its initial to advanced stages, making the suppression of inflammatory pathways a promising option for AS treatment5. By scavenging overproduced intracellular ROS, antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, can interrupt detrimental inflammatory cascades55,56. Accordingly, we investigated the anti-inflammatory capacity of MMAzyme-S, given its proven antioxidant activity. In the model group, LPS-stimulated macrophages showed a sharp increase in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, along with downregulated anti-inflammatory cytokines as IL-10. In sharp contrast, MMAzyme-S treatment exhibited substantially inhibited secretion of both IL-6 and TNF-α, signifying the intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties of MAzyme (Fig. 4g, h). It was also observed that typical anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, were upregulated in the biomimetic group (Fig. 4i). In addition, we performed a transwell assay to examine the efficacy of MMAzyme in preventing the exacerbation of inflammation. Macrophages seeded in the lower chambers were treated with LPS to induce the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which promoted the migration of inactivated macrophages in the upper chambers (Fig. 4j). As shown in Fig. 4k, a large portion of macrophages were attracted and migrated through the Transwell membrane when the bottom chamber cells were treated with LPS alone. The number of migrated cells decreased significantly when MAzyme and MAzyme-S were added to the lower chamber, and only a few migrating cells were observed in the presence of MMAzyme-S (Supplementary Fig. 32). These results jointly validate the anti-inflammatory potential of MMAzyme-S.

Furthermore, suppression of inflammation and oxidative stress regulates macrophage polarization. Macrophages, which feature significant plasticity, can polarize into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which perpetuates inflammation and promotes lesion rupture, or the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which facilitates inflammation resolution and engulfing of ACs57,58. To verify this effect, FCM analysis was conducted to reveal the phenotypic shift between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophages after treatment with LPS and exposure to different samples. As expected, there was a significant reduction in M1 polarization and an upregulation of M2 polarization when macrophages were treated with MAzyme or MAzyme-S (Fig. 4l). Notably, MMAzyme-S exhibited the most pronounced regulation of macrophage polarization, reducing the proportion of the M1 type from 10.89 to 3.18% and increasing the proportion of the M2 type from 6.05 to 23.2% (Supplementary Fig. 33). Collectively, the alleviation of oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions as well as the beneficial regulation of M1/M2 macrophage polarization by biomimetically assembled nanozymes are highly desired to reshape the atherogenic vascular environment and lay the foundation for anti-atherosclerosis therapy.

In vitro pro‑efferocytosis and enhanced cholesterol efflux enabled by MMAzyme-S

Defective efferocytosis, as a sign of insufficient inflammation resolution, leads to the pathological accumulation of apoptotic debris, secondary necrosis, and the formation of advanced plaques prone to rupture12. The timely removal of dead cells is a critical mechanism for plaque burden inhibition. However, lesional macrophages exhibit reduced phagocytic ability under chronic inflammatory conditions57,58. To simulate an inflammatory intraplaque microenvironment, LPS and IFN-γ inductions were conducted to establish pro-inflammatory macrophages for assessing the pro-efferocytic effect of the inhibitor-loaded antioxidant nanozyme. After co-incubating the phagocytic cells with standard ACs for 2 h, FCM analysis revealed that MMAzyme-S-pretreated phagocytes displayed substantially enhanced phagocytosis of standard ACs under inflammatory conditions compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 34), indicating recovered efferocytosis by biomimetic treatment.

The chronic inflammatory condition negatively impacts both the phagocytic ability of macrophages and the capacity of the target ACs to be removed17,58,59. The overexpression of CD47 in ACs is another critical mechanism that impedes efferocytosis. To explore whether the inhibitor-carrying MMAzyme-S could enhance the engulfment of phagocytosis-resistant ACs (Fig. 5a), the target cells were pretreated with TNF-α and ox-LDL to induce upregulation of CD47 expression17. As presented in Fig. 5b, control macrophages exhibited negligible phagocytosis of ACs. Conversely, phagocytes pretreated with MAzyme-S and MMAzyme-S showed not only whole-cell engulfment but also the internalization of multiple apoptotic bodies or cell fragments. FCM results further indicated that the phagocytic index dropped to only 2.49% under phagocytosis-resistant conditions but increased to 8.56% after treatment with biomimetic MMAzyme-S (Fig. 5c, d). Western blotting results indicated that SHP1 phosphorylation (p-SHP1), an efferocytosis-related marker, was significantly reduced in the MMAzyme-S group (Supplementary Fig. 35). These observations verified that the specific delivery of SHP1i can reactivate phagocytes towards CD47-overexpressed ACs by interrupting the antiphagocytic CD47-SIRPα signaling pathway. Notably, compared to anti-CD47 antibodies, MMAzyme-S yielded the highest degree of ACs clearance. This might be attributed to the high affinity of inflammatory cells, together with the antioxidant and pro-efferocytosis effects (Supplementary Figs. 36 and 51). Current evidence suggests that efferocytosis can stimulate the production of pro-resolving mediators in resolution signaling, thereby reconstructing inflammatory homeostasis and facilitating tissue repair60–62. We observed that MMAzyme-S markedly restored the post-efferocytic production of IL-10, accompanied with an inhibition of TNF-α secretion in efferocytosing macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 37). These data indicate that MMAzyme-S contributes to efficacious efferocytosis and subsequent inflammation reversal. Mechanistically, efferocytosis involves panoplies of molecules, among which Mer receptor tyrosine kinase (Mertk), a phagocytic cell-surface receptor, is implicated in driving adhesion and internalization of ACs during efferocytosis. As atherosclerosis progresses, inflammatory stimuli drive A disintegrin and metalloprotease 17-mediated shedding of the MertK ectodomain from the cell surface, thereby compromising efferocytosis within the plaque59,63. Therefore, we evaluated Mertk expression in phagocytes subjected to the different treatments. Western blotting showed a significant upregulation of Mertk expression in the MMAzyme-S group (Fig. 5e, i), which concomitantly confirmed the restored efferocytic activity due to the synergistic effects of the biomimetic nanozyme. These findings suggest that MMAzyme-S enables the specific delivery of the pro-efferocytic inhibitor SHP1i to macrophages, reviving the phagocytosis of ACs and remodeling inflamed and vulnerable plaques under pro-atherosclerotic microenvironments.

As another characteristic feature of AS, foam cells not only undergo cell apoptosis but also exhibit an impaired capacity to decompose ingested lipids and resolve inflammation. The activation of cholesterol efflux processes can preserve macrophage health by ridding cells with lipids that are cytotoxic when in excess. Previous studies have demonstrated that inflammation and insufficient antioxidant systems may retard RCT and lead to a severe lipid burden64–66. Hence, this nanozyme-based biomimetic strategy may offer an alternative mechanism for lipid retention and cellular function recovery (Fig. 5f). Specifically, Oil Red O (ORO) staining was performed to visualize the intracellular lipid levels. Noticeable red spots were observed in the macrophages treated with ox-LDL and LPS, indicating intracellular lipid accumulation and foam cell formation. However, the foaming process was markedly attenuated by these treatments, and the lowest number of lipid droplets was detected in the MMAzyme-S group (Fig. 5g). Furthermore, a total cholesterol detection kit was used to evaluate the efficacy of the biomimetic nanozymes in promoting cholesterol efflux. Notably, an increased cholesterol content was detected in the supernatant of the samples treated with MMAzyme-S, confirming enhanced cholesterol efflux from foamy cells (Fig. 5h).

To further investigate the underlying mechanism, Western blotting was performed to evaluate the expression of RCT-related proteins in macrophages after different treatments. As a PPARγ-mediated cholesterol transport pathway, the PPARγ-LXRα-ABCA1 and ABCG1 axis is mainly responsible for the RCT. Notably, the expression of PPARγ, LXRα, ABCA1, and ABCG1 was increased following MMAzyme-S treatment (Fig. 5i, j–m, and Supplementary Fig. 38). The upregulated expression of these vital proteins involved in RCT showed that MMAzyme-S could efficiently promote cholesterol efflux in lipid-laden macrophages and restore lesional cholesterol homeostasis. More importantly, efferocytosis of apoptotic foam cells also leads to an overloaded intracellular cholesterol burden and even death of cholesterol-containing phagocytes, which in turn results in a vicious cycle between intensified inflammation and necrotic cores7. The biomimetic MMAzyme-S can overcome these problems by simultaneously promoting the timely efflux of excess cholesterol and facilitating the digestion of apoptotic bodies and continual efferocytosis13. Furthermore, MMAzyme can upregulate the expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1, which has been reported to protect against oxidative stress-induced macrophage apoptosis during efferocytosis67–69. Therefore, MMAzyme-S is expected to exhibit collaborative anti-atherogenic synergy through its antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, pro-efferocytic, and cholesterol homeostasis-regulating properties.

In vivo biocompatibility and plaque-targeting capability of MMAzyme-S

To systemically evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility, healthy C57 mice were intravenously injected with saline or MMAzyme-S (10 mg kg−1) on days 7, 14, and 28 before euthanasia. The routine blood parameters and biochemistry indices of samples from MMAzyme-S-treated mice were within the normal range, and no appreciable hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity was detected compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 39). Moreover, MMAzyme-S did not induce anemia, a major complication that hinders the translation of antibody-mediated pro-efferocytic therapy. Histological studies also demonstrated that the injected MMAzyme-S caused minimal damage to the main organs of the treated mice, including the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 40). To assess the metabolism of MMAzyme-S in vivo, we determined Cu levels in major organs at various times using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). After intravenous administration, the concentrations of Cu in the major organs gradually declined over time, implying systemic clearance of MMAzyme-S (Supplementary Fig. 41). Collectively, these data indicate that MMAzyme-S is biocompatible and well-tolerated for in vivo applications.

Extensive inflammatory cell infiltration is a critical pathological indicator of vulnerable plaques prone to rupture. The inflammation-homing behavior of the macrophage membrane imparts plaque-targeting and vulnerable plaque-detection abilities to the nanozyme. To validate this, cyanine 5.5 NHS ester (Cy5.5)-labeled MAzyme or MMAzyme was administered to assess in vivo plaque enrichment. Ex vivo fluorescence images showed higher fluorescence accumulation of MMAzyme than that of MAzyme in the plaque-bearing aortic root and thoracic aorta (Fig. 6a). No significant differences were observed in the distribution in the other main organs (Supplementary Fig. 42). Furthermore, non-invasive photoacoustic (PA) imaging was used to evaluate the accumulation of nanozymes in vulnerable plaques. Specifically, the FDA-approved dye, indocyanine green (ICG), was loaded into the porous nanozyme to render it with PA imaging capability. A concentration-dependent intensity of PA signals at a wavelength of 800 nm was observed in both MAzyme and MMAzyme, suggesting that the imaging efficacy was not affected by the decoration of MM (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 43). We investigated the in vivo vulnerable plaque detection performance using PA imaging (Fig. 6c). After intravenous injection of labeled nanozymes, both cross-sectional and longitudinal sections were used to detect PA signals in the carotid artery. Owing to the inflammatory homing effects, the MMAzyme group exhibited a stronger signal with high contrast than the surrounding normal tissue. In contrast, passive accumulation of MAzyme resulted in a weak signal in the carotid artery (Fig. 6d). Quantitatively, the PA signal intensity in the MMAzyme group was approximately 2.1-fold higher than that in the MAzyme group (Supplementary Fig. 44). These observations confirm that MMAzyme-S is effective in targeting and identifying vulnerable plaques. Nevertheless, the targeting specificity and detection sensitivity of the ICG-loaded MMAzyme may need further improvement when compared with previously developed nanoprobes (Supplementary Table 4). Incorporating intelligent switching mechanisms, such as microenvironment-responsive properties, may enable more precise plaque vulnerability assessment and risk stratification70,71.

In vivo synergistic anti-atherosclerosis effects of MMAzyme-S

Based on these encouraging in vitro results, the anti-atherosclerotic performance of the biomimetic MMAzyme-S was systematically explored in vivo. Typically, ApoE−/− mice were obtained after a 5-week high-fat diet (Fig. 6e) and randomly assigned to four groups: saline, MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S. Treatment was performed weekly by intravenously injecting the samples for 8 weeks in combination with an additional high-fat diet. Upon completion of the treatment, ultrasound/PA fusion imaging was utilized to noninvasively visualize the therapeutic efficacy through the window of the carotid artery. Quantitative analyses of photoacoustic signal intensities acquired exclusively from the plaque-bearing arterial wall revealed that mice treated with MMAzyme-S exhibited the weakest PA signal in the carotid artery compared to other groups (Supplementary Fig. 45). While hemoglobin serves as an essential endogenous PA contrast agent, inherent inter-individual variability among mice is unavoidable. Given that background signals also existed and varied in previous photoacoustic imaging studies of atherosclerotic plaques, we confirm the robustness and reliability of our imaging conclusions72,73. These results demonstrate that the biomimetic multifunctional MMAzyme-S can effectively alleviate the plaque burden.

After the treatment period, the experimental mice were euthanized, and their aortas were separated and stained with ORO to evaluate plaque distribution. While the saline group showed the largest plaque area, MAzyme and MAzyme-S treatments resulted in moderately reduced ORO-stained areas in the aorta (Fig. 6f). The mildest AS plaques were observed in mice receiving MMAzyme-S therapy. The quantification results indicated that the average plaque area decreased from 26.59% in the saline group to 8.84% in the MMAzyme-S group (Fig. 6g). Consistently, the ORO-stained cryosections of the aortic root also revealed the most significant plaque inhibition by the biomimetic MMAzyme-S, implying its superior anti-atherogenic efficacy based on its multifaceted plaque inhibition capacity (Fig. 6h, i).

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses of aortic root sections were performed to investigate the underlying mechanism of the collaborative anti-atherogenic treatment. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed remarkable lipid-rich necrotic cores in the saline group, whereas the necrotic core significantly shrunk in the MMAzyme-S group, with a reduction from 31.50 to 13.74% (Fig. 7a, b). Masson staining and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) staining were employed to evaluate the accumulation of collagen and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) in plaques. MMAzyme-S treatment demonstrated notable anti-proliferative effects on VSMCs and reduced collagen formation by approximately 22.09% (Fig. 7a, b), which is highly advantageous for stabilizing plaques and preventing further progression. Additionally, immunofluorescence images showed that MMAzyme-S treatment effectively inhibited the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) in atherosclerotic lesions (Fig. 7a, b), a marker closely associated with plaque vulnerability. To validate the antioxidant efficacy of MMAzyme-S observed in vivo, ROS expression in aortic root sections was assessed. Plaques from mice subjected to 3 months of high-fat diet treatment displayed strong ROS, whereas MMAzyme-S-treated mice exhibited markedly reduced fluorescence signals, indicating the ability of MMAzyme-S to alleviate oxidative stress (Supplementary Fig. 46). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining results suggested that intraplaque SHP1 phosphorylation was remarkably suppressed in the MMAzyme-S group (Fig. 7c). This validated the hypothesis that the loaded SHP1i in MMAzyme can effectively inhibit the downstream key effector in the typical antiphagocytic CD47-SIRPα signaling.

Furthermore, the efferocytosis activity of macrophages within the plaques was assessed. Specifically, cleaved caspase-3 staining was used to mark ACs in plaques, whereas co-staining with Mac-3 and cleaved caspase-3 was used to identify the phagocytosis of macrophages on ACs. Consistent with the in vitro phagocytosis analysis, the ratio of free apoptotic bodies to macrophage-associated ACs was significantly lower in plaques from MMAzyme-S-treated mice (Fig. 7d). This finding corroborates that treatment with biomimetic nanozymes can augment the removal of dead cells from the vascular bed. Given the inherent ROS-scavenging ability of MMAzyme-S, treatment with MMAzyme-S led to a significant reduction in inflammatory-related fluorescence signals of TNF-α within atherosclerotic plaques (Supplementary Fig. 47). Because persistent inflammation is known to promote plaque vulnerability, the synergistic capacity of pro-efferocytic MMAzyme-S to simultaneously enhance efferocytosis and resolve inflammation is particularly compelling. Additionally, the protein expression levels of ABCA1 and ABCG1 were significantly upregulated in aortic plaques of ApoE−/− mice following MMAzyme-S treatment (Supplementary Fig. 48). These findings indicate that MMAzyme-S positively modulates intraplaque lipid metabolism, promotes the restoration of lipid homeostasis within atherosclerotic lesions, and contributes to plaque stabilization. Collectively, these results provide strong evidence for the desirable in vivo therapeutic effect of the biomimetic MMAzyme-S on AS by stabilizing lesional plaques and reducing the plaque burden.

To investigate the sustained inhibitory effects of MMAzyme-S on plaque progression, destabilization, and rupture in vivo, we conducted a 4-week post-treatment observation period, after which the mice were euthanized for arterial tissue collection. Whole aorta ORO staining revealed that untreated mice in the model group exhibited the highest percentage of ORO-positive areas (48.01%), whereas the MMAzyme-S-treated group demonstrated the lowest plaque burden (15.28%) (Supplementary Fig. 49a, b). Similar results were observed in the ORO-stained cryosections of the aortic root, where the smallest ORO-stained positive area was observed in the MMAzyme-S group (Supplementary Fig. 49c, d). H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining of the aortic arch revealed that MMAzyme-S treatment significantly reduced the plaque lesion area (Supplementary Fig. 50) and enhanced plaque stability by reducing the necrotic core. Furthermore, compared with the saline, MAzyme, and MAzyme-S cohorts, MMAzyme-S treatment reduced the incidence of disrupted or buried fibrous caps and increased fibrous cap thickness, indicating improved plaque stability and a reduced risk of plaque destabilization and rupture. These follow-up findings underscore the sustained inhibitory effects of MMAzyme-S on plaque progression and destabilization in atherosclerotic Apoe-/- mice, highlighting its remarkable anti-atherosclerotic efficacy and its potential as a promising therapeutic agent for long-term plaque stabilization.

Discussion

In summary, a multifaceted pathogenesis-targeting nanoplatform, MMAzyme-S, was constructed by loading pro-efferocytosis inhibitors into a biomimetic porous antioxidant, MAzyme, coated with MM for synergistic anti-atherosclerosis therapy. Given the well-documented role of defective efferocytosis in plaque progression, preclinical prophagocytic therapies are currently being explored to enhance the phagocytic clearance of ACs. Flores et al. developed SWNT-SHP1i to reactivate lesional phagocytosis and reduce plaque burden74. However, persistent cholesterol efflux during the AS pathological process, which is also critical to ensure the timely clearance of excess cholesterol in phagocytes during efferocytosis and to maintain their sustained phagocytic function, is underestimated. Additionally, antioxidant nanocarriers have a high potential to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, which also impairs the phagocytic capacity of macrophages. Sha et al. developed MM-coated SHP1i-loaded liposomal NPs to inhibit AS progression75. In addition to MM-mediated cytokine sequestration, liposomes merely serve as delivery vehicles without intrinsic therapeutic effects, limiting their ability to target multifactorial drivers of plaque progression. The copper-glycine assembled MAzyme developed in this study was characterized by highly desirable porous nanostructures owing to distinctive metal-amino acid coordination interactions. Notably, the multi-enzymatic properties of MAzyme ensured its superior antioxidant activity, close to that of natural SOD, presumably because of the bioinspired active sites and high specific surface area. Owing to the inflammatory-homing feature of macrophage membranes, biomimetic MM cloaking also rendered MMAzyme-S with an active targeting accumulation ability. Additionally, the ROS-scavenging capability of MMAzyme provided potent protection against macrophages under oxidative stress. Suppression of inflammation-related cytokines and promotion of anti-inflammatory phenotype polarization were also observed in macrophages treated with MMAzyme-S. Moreover, with the encapsulation of SHP1i in mesopores, MMAzyme-S can interrupt antiphagocytic CD47-SIRPα signaling to normalize the defective efferocytosis of macrophages, which is evidenced by the enhanced clearance of ACs and upregulated pro-efferocytic proteins. Furthermore, foam cell formation, a key hallmark of AS plaque lesions, was significantly suppressed by MMAzyme-S treatment. Mechanistic analysis revealed that the reverse cholesterol transport pathway was markedly potentiated by MMAzyme-S treatment. In vivo evaluations corroborated the specific accumulation of MMAzyme-S in lesional plaques. However, the precise quantification of liberated copper ions within plaques and their safe therapeutic concentration range requires systematic characterization in future studies. By taking advantage of the orchestrated enzyme-mimicking antioxidant activity and pro-efferocytotic effects, MMAzyme-S ultimately demonstrated a remarkable alleviation of AS progression compared to bare MAzyme and MAzyme-S without MM cloaking. Overall, the biomimetic copper-amino acid self-assembled MAzyme synergizing with pro-efferocytosis inhibitors constitutes a pathogenesis-targeted nanoplatform for multilink intervention in AS progression across imbalanced oxidative stress, inflammatory microenvironments, impaired efferocytosis, and aberrant cholesterol efflux.

Methods

Synthesis of MAzyme and MAzyme-S

To prepare the coordination precursor, 2 g copper acetate monohydrate and 1.5 g glycine were separately dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water and heated to 70 °C. Then, the solutions were mixed and added with 25 mL of ethanol and subsequently, cool down to 0 °C using ice bath. The precursor precipitate was washed with ethanol five times at 8594 × g for 5 min and dried via high vacuum. Subsequently, 140 mg of precursor was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water and stirred for 15 min, then 960 μL of NaOH (1.2 M) solution was added quickly, followed by stirring for another 15 min. After that, the precipitate was obtained through centrifugation, and washed with deionized water and ethanol twice each (8594 × g, 5 min).

MAzyme-S was prepared by mixing MAzyme (1 mg mL−1, 5 mL) with SHP1i (NSC-87877, Sigma-Aldrich; 1 mg mL−1, 5 mL), a small-molecule inhibitor of SIRPα’s downstream effector molecule SHP-1. The mixture was stirred overnight at 4 °C to allow complex formation. The unloaded SHP1i was removed by centrifugation at 8594 × g for 5 min. The standard curve of SHP1i at different concentrations was determined, and the concentration of the loaded SHP1i was calculated by subtracting the free SHP1i measured by UV–vis spectroscopy at its characteristic absorption of 320 nm. Synthesis of MMAzyme-S

The MM were extracted from RAW 264.7 cells. The collected RAW 264.7 cells were rinsed with PBS and then resuspended in cell membrane extraction buffer on ice for 15 min. The macrophage cells were further crushed via an ultrasonic disruptor. Subsequently, the obtained suspension was centrifuged at 2000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min to remove the cell debris. The resulting supernatant was subjected to further centrifugation at 20,000 × g and 4 °C for 30 min, and the precipitate was collected. To obtain the macrophage membrane-coated biomimetic MAzyme-S, the isolated MM was mixed with MAzyme-S in a weight ratio of 5:1, and then the mixture was sonicated in a bath sonicator for 1 min. The free membrane was removed via centrifugation at 8594 × g and 4 °C for 5 min.

Assessment of SOD-like activity

The SOD-like activity of MAzyme was evaluated by monitoring the reduction of WST-8 formazan dye formation according to the manufacturer’s instructions of a SOD assay kit. Briefly, WST-8 can react with O2•− via the oxidation of Xanthine to generate WST-8 formazan dye. The amount of formazan dye is correlated to the level of O2•− radicals. The O2•− radicals can be scavenged by MAzyme. Thus, the SOD-like activity can be calculated by detecting the concentration of the remaining WST-8 formazan, which is monitored at 450 nm using a multiple plate reader (n = 3 for each group). The half-inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated using dose-response-inhibition functional analysis in GraphPad Prism 8 software.

The O2•− quenching efficiency of MAzyme was further evaluated in another similar way. Typically, O2•− produced by riboflavin (20 μM) and L-methionine (12.5 mM) under LED lamp for 5 min can react with nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT, 75 μM) to form NBT formazan. The remaining amount of formazan is directly correlated to the amount of O2•−, and the absorbance of the characteristic peak at 550 nm was measured by a UV–vis spectrophotometer and a multiple plate reader. The O2•− inhibition capability was calculated according to the following formula: [(Ac − As)/Ac] × 100%. Ac refers to the absorbance of the reaction system without MAzyme, while As refers to the system treated with MAzyme.

Assessment of CAT-like activity

The CAT-like activity of MAzyme was detected by monitoring the consumption of H2O2 by MAzyme as well as the oxygen generation. The consumption of H2O2 (10 mM) by different concentrations of MAzyme was measured directly by observing the absorption spectra of H2O2 at 240 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. Assessment of GPx-like activity

The GPx-like activity of MAzyme was probed using a GPx assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, glutathione peroxidase utilizes reduced glutathione (GSH) to catalyze organic peroxides to generate water or organic alcohols, and GSH is transformed to GSSG. Then, the GSSG is reduced to GSH under the assistance of GSH reductase (GR) and consuming coenzyme nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). Accordingly, the GPx-like activity of MAzyme was determined by a microplate reader continually measuring the decrease of NADPH level at 340 nm.

Assessment of POD-like activity

The POD-like activity of MAzyme was assessed using TMB as the POD substrate in the presence of H2O2. TMB (0.8 mM), H2O2 (5 mM), and different concentrations of MAzyme were mixed at room temperature in the acetate buffer solution. The blue color developed as the reaction proceeded, and the absorbance changes at 652 nm were measured spectrophotometrically to assess the POD-like activity.

Assessment of •OH and O2•− scavenging activity

The capability of MAzyme to scavenge •OH was confirmed by using ESR spectroscopy. The •OH was generated by the Fenton reaction. Briefly, FeSO4, H2O2 aqueous solution, MAzyme, and DMPO were incubated, and ESR spectra were detected after 5 min of incubation. The O2•− quenching efficiency of MAzyme was further demonstrated by using ESR spectroscopy. Typically, riboflavin (20 μM), L-methionine (12.5 mM), MAzyme, and DMPO were incubated under LED lamp for 5 min, and the remained O2•− were trapped by DMPO.

In vitro protective effect against oxidative injury

Firstly, RAW 264.7 were seeded in a 96-well plate overnight (2 × 104 cells per well). Then, the medium was discarded, and the RAW 264.7 was exposed to fresh medium containing H2O2 (400 μM) in the presence of varied concentrations of MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S (0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 μg mL−1) for an additional 12 h incubation. Lastly, the cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay.

In vitro ROS scavenging ability

The intracellular ROS level was assessed using the fluorescent dye DCFH-DA. RAW 264.7 were seeded and routinely cultured (2 × 105 cells per well). Then, cells were exposed to fresh medium containing H2O2 (100 μM) or LPS (1 μg mL−1) in the presence of MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S (10 μg mL−1) for additional 12 h. Cells were rinsed with PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA for 30 min in the dark at 37 °C. Then, the cells were washed with PBS and subjected to CLSM and FCM.

Anti-inflammatory effects on macrophages polarization

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates overnight (1 × 105 cells per well). Then, the cells were treated by LPS in the presence of MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S (10 μg mL−1) for 24 h. The control group was treated with fresh medium, while the model group was stimulated with LPS alone. Next, the supernatant containing inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TNF-α, and IL-6) was collected and detected by ELISA kit per the manufacturer’s instructions (Abclonal, China).

To evaluate macrophage polarization, the cells were treated in a similar procedure. Subsequently, activated cells were collected (2 × 105 cells per well). After that, M1 and M2 macrophages were labeled with FITC anti-mouse CD86 (BioLegend, 105005) and CD206-APC (BioLegend, 141707) antibodies, respectively. After three times of washing, the resuspended cells were kept on ice, and the polarization of M1 and M2 macrophages were immediately detected by FCM.

In vitro transwell macrophage migration assay

RAW 264.7 cells were initially cultured in the bottom chambers (2 × 105 cells per well) and pretreated with LPS in the presence of MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S for 12 h. Then the untreated RAW 264.7 cells were suspended in serum-free medium and plated on the upper chamber inserting in the medium of the lower chamber, DMEM. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, RAW 264.7 cells that had moved to the bottom side of the membranes were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and captured under optical microscope.

Efferocytosis assay

Briefly, for phagocytes, RAW 264.7 macrophages (1 × 105 cells per well) were pretreated with LPS and IFN-γ (LPS, 1 μg mL−1; IFN-γ, 20 ng mL−1) for 24 h. Then the macrophages were labeled with CellTracker Red (2.5 μM) and treated with MAzyme, MAzyme-S and MMAzyme-S. For standard ACs, RAW 264.7 cells (2 × 105 cells per well) were labeled with CellTracker Green (2.5 μM) and then exposed to staurosporine (STS, 1 μM) for 4 h to induce apoptosis. For CD47 overexpressed ACs, macrophages (2 × 105 cells per well) were pretreated with TNF-α (50 ng mL−1) and ox-LDL (50 μg mL−1) for 24 h before inducing apoptosis. CellTracker Green-labeled ACs were added to CellTracker Red-labeled phagocytes (4:1, ACs: phagocytes) and co-incubated for 2 h in serum-free media. In addition to assess basal efferocytosis activity, similar experiments were executed with CD47 overproduced ACs. Next, cells were rinsed with PBS to remove non-internalized ACs, imaged by CLSM, or dissociated from wells for FCM analysis. Efferocytotic index was defined as the percentage of double-positive cells using FlowJo software.

To evaluate the post-efferocytic resolution effects, LPS and IFN-γ-induced macrophages (1 × 105 cells per well) were pre-treated with MAzyme, MAzyme-S, MMAzyme-S, and then incubated with or without ACs for 2 h and subsequently rinsed with PBS to remove unbound ACs. After a 6-h incubation, the supernatant containing IL-10 and TNF-α was collected and detected by ELISA kit per the Manufacturer’s instructions (Abclonal, China).

Cholesterol efflux and foam cell formation inhibition effects

To measure the in vitro cholesterol efflux, RAW 264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were cultured overnight and activated by exposing to ox-LDL (50 μg mL−1) and LPS (1 μg mL−1) for 24 h. Subsequently, the old medium was discarded, and fresh medium with 10 μg mL−1 of MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S were added, respectively. For the control group treated with fresh medium and the foamy-induced model group, the old medium was discarded and replace with fresh medium without nanoparticles. After incubation for additional 24 h, the supernatant was collected, and the content of cholesterol was detected by a total cholesterol detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance at a wavelength of 500 nm was recorded via a microplate reader. To verifying the anti-foamy effect of nanozyme, the above procedure for incubation, induction of foam cells, and treatment were repeated. Then, the cells were fixed and stained with ORO. Finally, images were captured under an optical microscope.

Western blot experiments

RAW 264.7 cells were routinely seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well), foam cells were induced, and subsequently experienced different treatments. The treated cells were collected and rinsed twice with precooled PBS, followed by the addition of RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice for 20 min. Unwanted lysates were removed by centrifugation at 4 °C, and the protein content was assessed by a BCA kit. The extracted proteins were diluted in loading buffer and heated up to 100 °C for 10 min. Next, the extracted proteins were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinyl difluoride membranes. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% non-fat skim milk at room temperature for 1 h, and subsequently incubated with target primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washed with TBST, the membrane was further incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The expression levels of target proteins were visualized by ECL reagent using the Bio-RAD ChemiDoc Imaging System. The expression level of GAPDH was used as a control to ensure equal protein loading.

The following primary antibodies were used in this study: CD47 Rabbit pAb (A1838, ABclonal,1:750), CD36/SR-B3 Rabbit pAb (A5792, ABclonal,1:2500), CCR2 Rabbit pAb (A2855, ABclonal,1:750), Na-K-ATP Rabbit pAb (GB11400, Servicebio,1:2000), MERTK Rabbit pAb (A5443, ABclonal,1:750), LXRα Rabbit mAb (A3974, ABclonal,1:3000), ABCG1 Rabbit mAb (A17907, ABclonal,1:1500), PPARγ Rabbit pAb (WL01800, Wanleibio,1:1000), Anti-ABCA1 antibody (AB307536, Abcam,1:1000), GAPDH Rabbit pAb (AC001, ABclonal,1:20000), p-SHP1 (8849, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (AS014, ABclonal,1:5000).

Experimental animals

All animal studies were conducted with the approval of the Shanghai General Hospital Clinical Center Laboratory Animal Welfare & Ethics Committee (Approval number: 2023AW017). All mice were kept in accordance with the policies on animal research of the National Ministry of Health. Male C57 mice (6–8 weeks) were chosen for in vivo biosafety and biodistribution evaluation. To induce atherosclerosis, 6-week-old male C57BL/6J ApoE−/− mice were treated with a high-fat diet. And the sex of the mice does not affect the results of the experiments. All mice were housed under a 12 h light/ dark cycle in a temperature-controlled environment (22 °C) with 50% humidity. Bedding, food, and water were sterilized to maintain SPF status.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy

Six-week-old male ApoE−/− mice were fed with a high-fat diet. After 5 weeks of high-fat diet, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10) and intravenously administered with saline, MAzyme, MAzyme-S, and MMAzyme-S at a dosage of 5 mg kg−1 (0.1 mL in saline) per week for additional 8 weeks. After an 8-week treatment period, the plaque-bearing mice from different groups were randomly selected for PA imaging. Ultrasound/PA fusion imaging was performed on the window of the carotid artery. Imaging parameters: wavelength, 800 nm; frequency, 40 MHz; 2D gain, 24 dB; PA gain, 35 dB.

After completing imaging and varied treatments, all mice were sacrificed. The whole aorta from the ascending aorta to the abdominal aorta were collected for ORO staining to assess the plaque area of the whole aorta. Besides, frozen sections of the aortic root were prepared and stained with ORO for further evaluation of the plaque area. The lesion area was calculated.

For histological analysis, aortic roots from mice under varied treatments received further hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, antibodies to α-SMA and MMP-9 for immunohistochemistry evaluation, as well as phospho-SHP1, Mac-3 and cleaved caspase-3 for immunofluorescence estimation.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results. Representative experiments shown (e.g., micrographs) were independently repeated three times, yielding similar results. In the Figure legends, n indicates the number of independent samples, biological samples, or the number of mice used. All data were shown as mean ± standard deviation, and GraphPad Prism 8.0. was utilized for statistical analyses. The significance of the data between groups in this work was analyzed on the basis of one-way/two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Ns represented no significance.
