Introduction

Cardiology is faced with the challenge of treating patients with multiple diseases. Even a standard procedure such as atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation can be difficult in patients with severe heart failure (HF) who are being treated with left ventricular mechanical support, as well as those with clotting disorders such as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Consideration should be given to providing adequate thromboprophylaxis during invasive cardiac procedures, especially in patients with left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) who are at risk of thrombosis. Direct thrombin inhibitors protect patients with HIT against the potentially life-threatening clotting disorder that may occur after heparin administration.1,2 Despite the limited data available on pulsed field ablation (PFA) in patients with LVADs, it appears to be the optimal choice for such patients because of the duration and efficacy of the procedure in patients with a high disease burden.3

Case report

We present the case of a 48-year-old man with multiple comorbidities and a history of postinfarction HF with a reduced ejection fraction of ∼20%, classified as New York Heart Association functional class II. The patient had an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implanted for primary prevention, and a left ventricular assist device implanted to alleviate symptoms of HF. During LVAD implantation, he developed HIT, which had a successful outcome. Recently, he was admitted several times to the cardiac arrhythmia unit for persistent AF, which led to an exacerbation of right ventricular HF requiring urgent electrical cardioversion. The patient underwent long-term anticoagulant therapy with warfarin under control of the international normalized ratio >2 during the hospital stay. Considering all his medical conditions, he was qualified for PFA of the substrate of AF because of its highly symptomatic character and underwent the procedure 1.8 years after LVAD implantation.

In the electrophysiology laboratory, after receiving antibiotic prophylaxis, the patient was connected to an intracardiac recording system (Bard) and a Farapulse PFA System (Boston Scientific). The HeartMate LVAD (Abbott) was powered and monitored via an external power module connected to the system. System parameters were visible in real time on a wireless tablet, HeartMate Touch (Abbott), connected throughout the procedure. The patient was in sinus rhythm during the entire procedure. A decapolar diagnostic catheter was placed in the ostium of the coronary sinus via the right femoral approach. The Farawave PFA catheter (Boston Scientific) was then introduced into the left atrium via transseptal puncture under intracardiac echocardiography guidance using the AcuNav catheter (Biosense Webster). Figure 1 shows this catheter in the ostium of the left superior pulmonary vein. Before the transseptal puncture, the patient received an intravenous bolus of bivalirudin, followed by continuous infusion. Activated clotting time was controlled every 15 minutes, which remained within the therapeutic range of ∼300 throughout the procedure. There was no interference between the LVAD, the electroanatomic system, and the pulsed field energy system, especially during the application of energy (Figure 2).

Pulmonary vein isolation as a first-line therapy was performed with additional application to both carinas and the ridge between the left superior vein and the left atrial appendage. A total of 48 PFA energy treatments were delivered. Bidirectional block was successfully achieved in all pulmonary vein ostia. Pulmonary vein isolation–only strategy was chosen because of the absence of pathological potentials recorded on the posterior wall of the left atrium.

The procedure was performed under anesthetic support, and the patient required conversion from analgosedation to general anesthesia with intubation during the procedure due to coughing caused by a small sub-bleed in the oral cavity. A transient decrease in flow through the LVAD was observed during intubation. After protecting the airway, the procedure was successfully completed.

No other complications were noted during or after the procedure. Control laboratory blood tests were performed the next day after the procedure. Red blood cell count, hemoglobin level, and international normalized ratio were 4.84 million cells/μL, 14.7 g/dL, and 2.14, respectively. Compared with baseline levels, which were 5.48 million cells/μL, 16.4 g/dL, and 2.21, respectively, only a slight hemolytic effect associated with the procedure was observed. The patient was discharged home in good general condition the next day after the procedure. During follow-up, 1 episode of AF recurrence was noted 5.5 months after the ablation procedure. Effective electrical cardioversion was performed with an energy setting of 360 J. No further arrhythmia recurrences were observed. To date, the patient is awaiting an urgent heart transplant.

Discussion

Atrial tachyarrhythmias, primarily AF, affect 21%–54% of patients with advanced HF. Furthermore, 20%–30% of patients develop de novo supraventricular arrhythmias after LVAD implantation. In certain clinical situations, atrial arrhythmias with a fast ventricular rate can result in hemodynamic decompensation, even in patients after LVAD implantation. Loss of atrial systolic function because of AF (“atrial kick loss”) may be one of the possible reasons for hemodynamic imbalance. Another mechanism is right ventricular failure due to arrhythmias with rapid ventricular activity.4 This makes it likely that patients with LVADs may have strong indications for AF treatment with catheter ablation as well. Despite the limitations imposed by the location of the LVAD and the risk of electromagnetic interference,5 catheter ablation remains an effective and safe procedure, as demonstrated in several reports.6,7 Using an external power supply and controller enabled LVAD functions to be continuously monitored and prevented potential interference during application delivery.

There is a growing quantity of evidence suggesting that PFA is an effective and safe method of treating AF, regardless of the severity of the arrhythmia. Despite limited data on PFA in patients with LVADs, PFA is actually the optimal choice for such patients because of the duration of the procedure and its efficacy in patients with a high disease burden.

Patients with LVADs can be expected to have anatomical variability and limitations in catheter positioning due to significant heart damage with extensive structural remodeling, a history of surgery, and implanted devices. The use of intracardiac echocardiography allowed us to increase the safety of transseptal puncture and to control the position and contact of the ablation catheter with the atrial wall.

Unfractionated heparin is preferred for reducing the risk of thromboembolism during cardiac procedures. However, an immune reaction mediated by antibodies formed in response to heparin in patients with type II HIT leads to a hypercoagulable state with potentially life-threatening complications.8 Although bivalirudin is associated with a higher risk of thrombosis than heparin,9,10 it can be the treatment of choice in cases of HIT.

Our case study demonstrates that PFA can be successfully performed in patients diagnosed with HIT after LVAD implantation.
