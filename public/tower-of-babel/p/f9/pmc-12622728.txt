Integrated bioinformatics of PANoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy related genes in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and their association with immune cells
Authors: Tingting Mei, Yuxuan Ren, Zhuying Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common respiratory issue caused by smoking and pollution, resulting in airway obstruction. The aim of this study was to investigate a prognostic model for genes related to apoptosis and mitophagy in COPD. This study investigates the molecular mechanisms linking COPD with PANoptosis and mitophagy, 2 critical cellular processes. Firstly, we integrated Gene Expression Omnibus datasets (GSE10006 and GSE20257) to analyze the differential expression of genes associated with PANoptosis and mitophagy in COPD. Batch effects were removed using the R package sva, followed by differential expression analysis using limma. Next, gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis were performed for PANoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy related genes (PAMRDEGs). Then, STRING and GeneMANIA were used to construct protein–protein interaction network of PAMRDEGs, and ChIPBase and StarBase were used to create regulatory network. Finally, The expression of key genes was verified, the diagnostic efficacy was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, and the immune characteristics related to COPD were explored by immune infiltration analysis. A total of 7 PAMRDEGs were obtained, namely AIM2, DNM1L, MAPK1, CASP8, CDK1, SAMHD1, NLRP3, which showed significant expression changes in COPD, linked to inflammatory response pathways like NOD-like receptor and tumor necrosis factor signaling pathway. Immune infiltration analysis revealed a notable negative correlation with M2 macrophages, highlighting the immune microenvironments importance in COPD progression. This study highlights the critical roles of PANoptosis and mitophagy-related genes in the pathogenesis of COPD, suggesting potential therapeutic targets. In the future, in-depth functional experiments and prospective large sample cohort validation are needed to further clarify the regulatory mechanism of the key targets and promote their clinical translation and application.

1. Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) represents a prevalent respiratory condition that leads to persistent airflow limitations, primarily resulting from extended exposure to detrimental substances such as tobacco smoke and ambient air pollution. According to estimates from the World Health Organization, approximately 251 million individuals are affected by COPD globally. It is projected to rank as the third leading cause of mortality by the year 2030, with an estimated annual death toll of around 3.23 million.[1] COPD is characterized by the gradual emergence of respiratory symptoms, including dyspnea, chronic cough, and sputum production, which markedly impair patients’ quality of life and escalate healthcare expenditures.[2] The complex mechanisms of COPD involve various inflammatory reactions that cause structural changes in the airways and lung tissue, resulting in airway obstruction and respiratory failure. Additionally, the pathophysiological aspects of COPD are influenced by complex interactions between genetic predispositions and environmental factors, culminating in inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular death processes. These mechanisms are pivotal for comprehending the molecular pathways implicated in COPD.

PANoptosis drives death of airway epithelial cells and releases proinflammatory factors such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and IL-18 by activating the multi-pathway complex (PANoptosome), which directly aggravates airway inflammation and lung tissue damage.[3] At the same time, defective mitophagy leads to insufficient clearance of damaged mitochondria, induces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species accumulation, oxidative stress and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and further promotes epithelial cell apoptosis, mucus hypersecretion, and pulmonary fibrosis.[4] Notably, damaged mitochondria not only serve as a key trigger for PANoptosis, but also amplify cellular death and immune microenvironment dysregulation through oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling.[5,6] These 2 mechanisms synergistically promote COPD pathophysiological processes such as chronic inflammation and airway remodeling by regulating airway epithelial cell death, inflammatory cytokine release, oxidative stress, and immune microenvironment remodeling.

This study aims to integrate PANoptosis and mitophagy pathways into a unified bioinformatics analysis process for the first time. By applying bioinformatics techniques, including batch effect correction, differential expression analysis, and protein interaction network construction, it systematically and multidimensionally reveals their synergistic regulatory mechanisms and potential molecular targets in COPD.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data download

Through the R package GEOquery[7] (Version 2.70.0) from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database[8] (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) download COPD datasets GSE10006[9] and GSE20257.[10] The samples of both datasets were derived from Homo sapiens, with the tissue source being airway epithelial cells. The chip platform for both datasets was GPL570. Detailed information can be found in Table 1. Among them, dataset GSE10006 included 27 COPD samples and 31 control samples, while dataset GSE20257contained 23 COPD samples and 112 control samples (with samples of the same number as those in GSE10006 removed). All selected COPD and control samples were included in the present investigation. The sample size of the control group was significantly larger than that of the case group (39 COPD cases vs 135 controls in GSE10006 and GSE20257), and there may be a risk of batch effect: large sample control group may contain more batch heterogeneity, so TCGA and 2 independent GEO cohorts were strictly merged, and batch effect was removed by sva package to ensure comparability of data analysis and robustness of conclusions.

This study independently searched for PANoptosis and mitophagy-related genes through GeneCards database[11] (https://www.genecards.org/) and PubMed literature database[12] (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). During the literature search, we identified candidate genes using the keywords “PANoptosis” and “mitophagy” respectively. In the GeneCards database, only protein-coding genes with explicit annotations were included, excluding noncoding RNAs (e.g., lncRNA, microRNA [miRNA], snRNA) and pseudogenes. For PubMed literature, we further incorporated experimentally validated protein-coding genes associated with PANoptosis or mitophagy from recent studies.[13] The gene sets from both databases were merged and de-duplicated to form a high-confidence gene set for PANoptosis and mitophagy. The complete list of genes is detailed in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q603.

R package sva[14] (Version 3.50.0) was used to batch process the data sets GSE10006 and GSE20257, and the integrated GEO data set (combined datasets) was obtained. The combined datasets of GEO included 39 COPD samples and 135 control samples. The combined datasets was standardized by R package limma[15] (Version 3.58.1), and the annotation probe was standardized and normalized. In this study, we first conducted preprocessing on the downloaded raw data. The specific steps included: converting probes to corresponding gene symbols using annotation information from the dataset platform. To address annotation inconsistencies across different data platforms, only overlapping genes were retained for subsequent analysis after probe conversion. Next, we standardized the data using the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the R package limma to eliminate systematic errors between samples and enhance comparability. Subsequently, batch-wise effects were addressed using the Combat method from the R package sva for the integrated GEO dataset, minimizing interference from different batch sources on the expression matrix. Through this comprehensive preprocessing workflow, we ensured cross-platform and cross-batch data comparability and robustness, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent differential analysis and bioinformatics mining. Furthermore, the expression matrix before and after the removal of batch effect was verified by principal component analysis (PCA).[16] PCA is a method of data dimensionality reduction, which extracts the feature vector (component) of data from high-dimensional data, and transforms them into low-dimensional data and uses 2D or 3D graphs to show these features.

2.2. Differentially expressed genes associated with COPD PANoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy

According to sample grouping in the combined datasets, the samples were divided into COPD and control groups respectively. R-packet limma[15] (Version 3.58.1) was used to analyze the differences in genes in COPD and control groups. The thresholds for |log FC| > 0 and P value < .05 were set as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Genes with log FC > 0 and P value < .05 were upregulated genes. And genes with log FC < 0 and P value < .05 were downregulated genes. The results of the differential analysis were mapped by R package ggplot2 (Version 3.4.4).

To identify PANoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy related genes (PAMRDEGs) associated with COPD, all DEGs meeting the criteria |log FC| > 0 and P value < .05 from the combined datasets were intersected with PAMRGs, and a Venn diagram was created to depict the PAMRDEGs. Additionally, a heatmap was generated using the R package pheatmap (Version 1.0.12) to illustrate the PAMRDEGs, while chromosome localization maps were constructed using the R package RCircos[17] (Version 1.2.2). In the context of limited sample size and the incompletely understood molecular mechanism of COPD, a relatively loose log FC threshold (log FC|> 0) can maximize the capture of potentially functionally relevant genes and avoid missing key signaling pathways. At the same time, the statistical significance constraint of P value < .05 was retained to ensure the reliability of subsequent analysis.

2.3. Enrichment analysis of gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)

Gene ontology (GO) analysis,[18] which is a widely recognized method to conduct large-scale functional enrichment studies including biological process (BP), cell component (CC), and molecular function. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)[19] is a widely used database that catalogs information on genomes, biological pathways, diseases, and pharmacological agents. The R package clusterProfiler[20] (Version 4.10.0) was employed for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the PAMRDEGs, utilizing selection criteria of adj.P < 0.05 and a false discovery rate value (q value) < 0.25, with the Benjamini–Hochberg method applied for P value correction.

2.4. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)[21] was used to assess the distribution trend of genes with predefined gene sets in the gene list ranked with phenotypic correlation, so as to judge their contribution to phenotype. To reveal the core pathological background of COPD, the genes of the Combined GEO Datasets were first ranked according to the log FC value between the COPD group and the control group. The R package clusterProfiler[20] (Version 4.10.0) was subsequently used to conduct GSEA on the entire gene list from the combined datasets. The parameters for GSEA included 2020 seeds, with each gene set containing a minimum of 10 genes and a maximum of 500 genes. Through Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Database access to c2.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols for GSEA, The screening criteria for GSEA were adj.P < .05 and false discovery rate value (q value) < 0.25, and the P value correction method was Benjamini–Hochberg.

2.5. Protein–protein interaction network (PPI network)

Protein–protein interaction network (PPI network), which is composed of proteins and proteins through their interactions with each other, participating in biological signal transmission, gene expression regulation, all aspects of life processes, such as energy and material metabolism and cell cycle regulation. Systematic analysis of the interaction between proteins within biological systems is crucial for comprehending the working principle of proteins in biological systems, understanding the reaction mechanism of biological signaling, and understanding energy and material metabolism during specific physiological conditions, such as diseases, as well as the functional interconnections among proteins.

The STRING database[22] (https://cn.string-db.org/) serves as a resource for exploring both established and anticipated protein interactions. In this study, the STRING database was applied based on PAMRDEGs with a minimum required interaction score >0.700. This high-confidence criterion facilitated the construction of a PPI network linked to PAMRDEGs. We identified genes that interact with other genes in the PPI network as hub genes, which were subsequently included in further analyses. To visualize this network, we used Cytoscape software[23] (https://cytoscape.org/).

The GeneMANIA database[24] (https://genemania.org/) was used to generate hypotheses about gene function, analyze gene lists and prioritize genes for functional analysis. Given a query gene list, GeneMANIA uses extensive genomic and proteomic data to find functionally similar genes. In this model, it weighted each functional genome dataset based on the predicted value of the query. Another use of GeneMANIA is gene function prediction. We used GeneMANIA’s online website to predict functionally similar genes of hub genes and construct the PPI Network.

2.6. Construction of control network

Transcription factor (TF) controls gene expression through interaction with hub genes in the transcriptional states. We retrieved TFs from the ChIPBase database[25] (http://rna.sysu.edu.cn/chipbase/) to analyze their regulatory effects on hub genes. And visualized the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA)−TF Regulatory Network by Cytoscape[26] software.

Moreover, miRNAs plays an important regulatory role in biological development and evolution, capable of modulating multiple target genes, while a single target gene may be influenced by various miRNAs. In order to analyze the hub genes and the relationship between miRNA, through StarBase v3.0 database[26] (https://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) to get to the hub genes associated miRNAs. In addition, the mRNA–miRNA Regulatory Network was visualized by Cytoscape software.

2.7. Differential expression verification of hub genes and ROC curve analysis

To further investigate the differential expression of hub genes between COPD samples and control samples within combined datasets, a grouping comparison map was drawn based on the expression levels of hub genes. The Packet R pROC[27] (Version 1.18.5) was used to plot the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of hub genes and calculate the area under the curve (AUC) to evaluate the diagnostic effect of hub genes on the development of COPD. The AUC of the ROC curve is generally between 0.5 and 1. The closer the AUC is to 1, the better the diagnosis. The AUC has low accuracy at 0.5 to 0.7, moderate accuracy at 0.7 to 0.9, and high accuracy at above 0.9.

2.8. Immunoinfiltration analysis of COPD (CIBERSORT)

CIBERSORT[28] is a deconvolution of the transcriptomic expression matrix based on the principle of linear support vector regression to estimate the composition and abundance of immune cells in the mixed cells. The CIBERSORT algorithm used in this study was based on the reference gene set LM22, and multiple hypothesis tests were performed using Bonferroni correction. By applying the CIBERSORT algorithm alongside the characteristic gene matrix of immune cells, we filtered out data with immune cell enrichment scores above 0, ultimately yielding a detailed immune cell infiltration matrix from the Combined Dataset, which was then represented visually through proportion histograms. Subsequently, the R package was employed for further analysis.

The R package ggplot2 (Version 3.4.4) was used to draw group comparison plots to show the difference in expression of immune cells between the control group and COPD group in the combined datasets. Following this, the Spearman algorithm was used to calculate the correlation between immune cells, and R package pheatmap (Version 1.0.12) was used to draw the correlation heatmap to show the correlation analysis results of immune cells themselves. The Spearman algorithm was used to calculate the correlation between hub genes and immune cells, and R package ggplot2 (Version 3.4.4) was used to draw the correlation bubble map to show the analysis results of the correlation between hub genes and immune cells.

2.9. Statistical analysis

All data processing and analysis in this were study based on R software (Version 4.2.2). Unless otherwise specified, the statistical significance of normally distributed variables was estimated by independent Student t test for the comparison of 2 groups of continuous variables. And the Mann–Whitney U test, namely Wilcoxon rank sum test, was used to analyze the difference between variables with non-normal distribution. Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the 3 or more groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used to calculate the correlation coefficients among different molecules. If not specified, all statistical P values were bilateral, and P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3.3. Differentially expressed genes associated with COPD PANoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy

The data obtained from the combined datasets of GEO were categorized into 2 groups: the COPD group and the control group. To evaluate the differences in gene expression levels between these 2 cohorts, a differential expression analysis was performed on the Combined Dataset utilizing the limma package in R. This analysis revealed a total of 4990 DEGs that met the established criteria of |log FC| > 0 and a P value < .05. Within this dataset, there were 2148 genes exhibiting upregulation (log FC > 0 and P value < 0.05) and 2842 genes demonstrating downregulation (log FC < 0 and P value < .05). 3.5. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

In order to evaluate the influence of gene expression levels across the Combined GEO Datasets on the development of COPD, GSEA was performed utilizing the log FC values for all genes identified in the Combined GEO Datasets, comparing COPD patients to control subjects. This analysis aimed to elucidate the correlation between gene expression and the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions involved, and the mountain map was shown (Fig. 5A). Specific results were shown in Table 3. The GSEA results showed that the gene set was significantly enriched in the arachidonic acid metabolism pathway (NES = 2.41, P = 1.45E−08, q = 2.59E−06), Altemeier response to LPS with mechanical ventilation (NES = 2.37, P = 1.07E−10, q = 3.19E−08), Rutella Response to HGF versus CSF2RB and IL4 Up (NES = 2.29, P = 1.00E–10, q = 3.19E−08) and Wilensky Response to Darapladib (NES = 2.27, P = 3.05E−06, q = 2.99E−04). These high NES values and extremely low P/q values suggest that the relevant signaling pathways have a strong potential driving effect in the pathogenesis of COPD. Notably, the analysis revealed that genes within the Combined dataset exhibited significant enrichment in pathways related to arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 5B), the Altemeier Response to LPS with Mechanical Ventilation (Fig. 5C), and the Rutella Response to HGF versus CSF2RB and IL4 Upregulation (illustrated in Fig. 5D), as well as the Wilensky Response to Darapladib (Fig. 5E), alongside various other biologically significant functions and signaling pathways.

4. Discussion

COPD is a common respiratory condition that significantly threatens public health due to high rates of illness and death. The disorder is characterized by enduring respiratory symptoms and restricted airflow, primarily attributed to airway and/or alveolar abnormalities stemming from substantial exposure to harmful particles or gases, predominantly those released from cigarette smoke.[29] Recent studies have underscored a concerning upward trend in the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of COPD, particularly within nations with high socio-demographic indices. A notable study assessing data from the Global Burden of Disease Study revealed an alarming 86% increase in the incidence of COPD from 1990 to 2019, accompanied by a 30% rise in mortality associated with the disease on a global scale, highlighting an urgent necessity for public health interventions targeting this disease.[30,31] There is an urgent need to understand the basic mechanisms of this condition due to its progressive nature and the limited effectiveness of current treatments.

At the methodological level of research, this study integrated the GSE10006 and GSE20257 transcriptome datasets and effectively removed the batch effect by using the sva package Combat method. Subsequently, the limma package was utilized for standardization processing, thereby enhancing the comparability among different data sources and the robustness of the analysis. ROC curve analysis further verified the diagnostic efficacy of the 7 hub genes, and their AUC values ranged from 0.589 to 0.667 (AIM2: 0.656; DNM1L: 0.667; MAPK1: 0.608; CASP8: 0.628; CDK1: 0.628;) SAMHD1: 0.603; NLRP3: 0.589) indicates that the predictive power of a single-gene is limited, but it lays the foundation for subsequent multi-gene joint modeling. Meanwhile, both GO/KEGG enrichment analysis and GSEA achieved highly significant statistical results (such as NoD-like receptor signaling pathway adj.P = 1.11 × 10−5; arachidonic acid metabolism NES = 2.41, q = 2.59 × 10−6), providing strong support for the reliability of the results. These methodological treatments and verifications jointly ensure the robustness of the research conclusions and provide a solid foundation for subsequent functional experiments and clinical translation.

At the biological level, the 7 key genes (AIM2, NLRP3, DNM1L, MAPK1, CASP8, CDK1, SAMHD1) identified in this study revealed the significant role of PANoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy in the pathogenesis of COPD. Among them, AIM2 and NLRP3, as the core components of inflammasomes, can promote the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and aggravate chronic airway inflammation.[32–34] DNM1L and MAPK1 are involved in mitochondrial division and dysfunction, leading to an enhanced oxidative stress response.[35,36] CASP8, CDK1, and SAMHD1 also play key roles in cell death and immune regulation.[37–39] The results of pathway enrichment showed that these genes were significantly associated with the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling pathway, suggesting their core position in inflammatory responses and programmed cell death. Combined with GSEA analysis, it also revealed that processes such as arachidonic acid metabolism and LPS response are closely related to the theory of COPD. In terms of immune infiltration analysis, among the 22 types of immune cells, 5 types showed significant differences in COPD samples, especially the reduction in the number of M2-type macrophages. This change weakened the anti-inflammatory and repair functions and was significantly negatively correlated with the expression of DNM1L (r = −0.336, P < .05). It is suggested that there is a coupling relationship between mitochondrial homeostasis imbalance and immune microenvironment disorder. In conclusion, the synergistic effect of PANoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy may be the core driving force for chronic inflammation and structural damage in COPD, which provides a theoretical basis for the future development of multi-target intervention strategies (such as jointly regulating the NLRP3/TNF pathway and the immune microenvironment).[40,41] Meanwhile, multi-gene combined markers may have better diagnostic and predictive value than single genes, which is worthy of in-depth verification in subsequent experiments and clinical studies.

This study systematically analyzed abnormally activated signaling pathways and associated immune infiltration characteristics in COPD by integrating multi-omics data. The results revealed significant enrichment of NOD-like receptor signaling pathways (represented by NLRP3 inflammasomes) and TNF signaling pathways in COPD samples. These pathways are closely associated with inflammatory responses, programmed cell death (such as PANoptosis), and mitochondrial autophagy. Activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes promotes the release of inflammatory factors like IL-1β, while the TNF signaling pathway exacerbates airway epithelial cell damage and programmed death processes through upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, leading to persistent airway structural destruction and repair dysfunction. Additionally, immune infiltration analysis showed a marked reduction in M2 macrophages in COPD, indicating impaired anti-inflammatory and tissue repair capabilities along with further imbalance in the inflammatory microenvironment. The abnormal immune cell infiltration forms a positive feedback loop with the activation of these molecular pathways. The deficiency of M2 macrophages not only weakens inflammation resolution but may also enhance NLRP3/TNF-mediated inflammatory cascades, accelerating chronic inflammation and airway remodeling. Further analysis revealed significant negative correlations between key genes (e.g., DNM1L) and M2 macrophage abundance, supporting the hypothesis of synergistic interactions between molecular mechanisms and immune microenvironment. These mechanisms suggest that the coordinated activation of NLRP3/TNF pathways and immune cell dysfunction serves as the core driver of persistent inflammation and structural damage in COPD.[42–44] This study provides a theoretical foundation for future multi-target combination interventions targeting inflammatory pathways and immune microenvironments. Subsequent research could focus on the interaction mechanisms between key pathways and immune cells, further validating their direct impact on COPD progression through animal models and clinical samples. These findings will lay the groundwork for discovering novel therapeutic targets and developing precision treatment strategies.

The limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. First, the research was based solely on 2 public databases (GSE10006 and GSE20257), with a total sample size of 39 COPD patients and 135 controls. The relatively small sample size may affect statistical power and the robustness of the results. Second, the population in the dataset primarily comes from specific regions and demographic backgrounds, lacking representativeness of patients from different regions, genetic backgrounds, and clinical subtypes. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Third, the bioinformatics analysis of transcriptomic data should be regarded as exploratory findings. Their clinical translational potential requires further validation in larger, multicenter studies with diverse populations. Future efforts combining cell and animal experiments, along with prospective cohort studies, will enhance the robustness and broader applicability of the results at both molecular mechanisms and population levels.

In conclusion, this study provides a set of novel candidate genes closely associated with COPD, namely AIM2, DNM1L, MAPK1, CASP8, CDK1, SAMHD1, NLRP3. These discoveries not only deepen our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying COPD but also lay the groundwork for future diagnostic tools and therapeutic strategies. Moving forward, we will verify our conclusions through animal and cellular experiments and facilitate the translation of these findings into clinical applications.
