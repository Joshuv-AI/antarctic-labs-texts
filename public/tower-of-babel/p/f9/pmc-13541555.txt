Meta-analysis of the safety of outpatient Sotalol initiation in clinical practice
Authors: Alhasan Saleh Alzubi, Ali M. Abdelaziz, Mawadda Chukr, Mohamed Shelig, Abdelrahman F. Abdelwahed, Khalid Abozguia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSotalol is a class III anti-arrhythmic medication commonly used in maintaining sinus rhythm in patients with arrhythmias, most notably atrial fibrillation. Sotalol is conventionally initiated in an inpatient setting (typically three days) to allow close monitoring of its effects, particularly corrected QT interval (QTc) prolongation, which may predispose patients to fatal arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes.ObjectiveIn this meta-analysis, we aimed to assess the safety of initiating sotalol in an outpatient setting.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Virtual Health Library, and Google Scholar for observational studies and randomized controlled trials assessing the safety of outpatient initiation of sotalol until January 2026. Our endpoints were the change from baseline in QTc interval at the longest follow-up and the rate of sotalol discontinuation due to adverse events. Standardized mean differences from baseline in QTc interval with 95% confidence intervals were pooled with a random-effects model using CMA V3 software.ResultsWe included three observational studies with a total cohort of 1248 patients. At the longest follow-up post-sotalol initiation in an outpatient setting, there was no statistically significant change in QTc interval from baseline (SMD = +0.15, 95% CI: −0.357 to 0.658, p = 0.56). Across 1140 patients, the rate of drug discontinuation due to adverse events at one-month post-sotalol initiation ranged from 4.7% (41/880 patients; 95% CI 3.4%–6.3%) in one study to 8.1% (21/260 patients; 95% CI 5.3%–12.1%) in another.ConclusionIn carefully selected patients, the initiation of sotalol in an outpatient setting appears to be safe and associated with no significant adverse events. However, the current evidence is limited to a small number of observational cohorts, and large randomized controlled trials are warranted to establish the safety and efficacy of outpatient sotalol initiation.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is highly prevalent and contributes substantially to mortality and disability worldwide [1,2]. The incidence and prevalence of AF continue to increase across regions. Almost 12.1 million AF cases will be expected in 2030 [3]. This burden keeps pressure on health systems to deliver rhythm-control care that is both safe and resource-conscious [4].

While pharmacological rhythm control is a treatment alternative to catheter ablation for suitable patients, specific safety concerns surround the initiation of drugs. While some antiarrhythmic drugs, such as Amiodarone, Flecainide, and Propafenone, are safe to initiate in the outpatient setting. Drugs such as Dofetilide carry a paradoxical risk of inducing dangerous arrhythmias and require close monitoring, particularly at therapy onset or reinitiating. The need for in-hospital initiation of sotalol has been controversial. Upon initiation, sotalol poses a high risk of early QT prolongation, torsade de pointes and bradyarrhythmia [5]. This has traditionally driven conservative inpatient initiation practices. However, hospital-based initiation and surveillance are costly and logistically intensive, motivating interest in models that leverage remote ECG technologies to shift portions of care outside the hospital while maintaining safety.

To address this need, we systematically evaluated the safety of outpatient sotalol initiation under protocolized monitoring, synthesizing contemporary evidence to guide practical implementation and program development.

Methods

The study was conducted according to the PRISMA statement 2020 standard reporting guidelines [6]. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD420251230389.

Search strategy

We systematically searched the medical literature in the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Virtual Health Library, and Google Scholar using relevant terms from inception until January 2026 without language restrictions. We also attempted a manual citation-based search to identify any further missing studies.

Inclusion criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational (prospective and retrospective) studies investigating the initiation of Sotalol in an outpatient setting. Search strategy is mentioned in Appendix 1.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded studies not reporting our outcomes of interest, studies with overlapping data or without available full-text, review articles, case reports, and editorials.

Study selection and data extraction

Following the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, with any conflicts resolved by the first authors. The first authors subsequently assessed full texts of potentially relevant studies and discussed resolving any remaining discrepancies to confirm eligibility for final selection.

While acknowledging that peak QTc prolongation risk occurs early in inpatients, our outcomes of interest were the QTc change from baseline at the longest follow-up post Sotalol initiation as an outpatient, and the rate of drug discontinuation or dose reduction due to adverse events censored at one month.

Data extraction was systematically performed using an Excel sheet and organized into three sections: an overview of the included studies, baseline characteristics of study subjects, and our review outcomes. Two reviewers initially performed the extraction and the extracted data were subsequently checked independently by two reviewers.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using CMA version 3 software. A random-effects model was applied to calculate the pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval for the mean change from baseline in QTc interval. Rates of drug discontinuation or dose reduction due to adverse events were reported descriptively for the studies providing one-month data, without pooling.

Publication bias

We could not assess publication bias using Egger's test since we did not have a minimum of 10 studies for any of the outcomes, which is necessary to achieve reliable results.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis suggests that carefully selected patients can begin Sotalol as outpatients without clinically important QTc prolongation or high early discontinuation rates. The eligible population across included studies comprised patients with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, baseline QTc <450 ms, normal or corrected electrolytes, adequate renal function, and access to structured ECG surveillance. Across 1248 patients, we found no significant change in QTc from baseline at the longest follow-up (SMD = +0.15; 95% CI –0.36 to 0.66; p = 0.56) and among 1140 patients, the observed event rates of Sotalol discontinuation or dose reduction ranged from 4.7% to 8.1%.These findings align with results from contemporary real-world series showing that protocolized outpatient initiation often paired with remote ECG monitoring can be both feasible and safe when strict selection and surveillance criteria are used [[7], [8], [9]]. The findings of this analysis should be interpreted within the context of these lower-risk populations and may not be generalizable to patients with reduced LVEF or advanced structural heart disease.

In the randomized, double-blind SAFE-T trial (n = 665), Amiodarone, Sotalol, and placebo were all initiated on an outpatient basis; Sotalol was 80 mg BID for 1 week then 160 mg BID with weekly trans telephonic ECG. Major adverse events did not differ significantly; mortality was underpowered, and investigators noted that the rare torsades observed under protocolized monitoring may permit outpatient initiation of Sotalol therapy [10]. Complementing these data, several centers have implemented intravenous (IV) Sotalol as a monitored, accelerated alternative to 3-day oral loading; a 60-min infusion followed by two oral doses with creatinine-clearance–based dosing and frequent QTc checks which achieves near–steady-state within <24 h when CrCl >60 mL/min, reduces length of stay, and facilitates earlier discharge into the same remote-ECG follow-up frameworks used for outpatient oral protocols [[11], [12], [13]]. However, real-world data from the PEAKS registry, which prospectively evaluated 167 patients undergoing elective IV Sotalol loading, reported a 5% discontinuation rate due to adverse events including QTc prolongation (n = 3), bradycardia (n = 1), and recurrent atrial arrhythmia (n = 2), with an additional 2 patients requiring infusion cessation for bradycardia or hypotension [14]. These findings underscore that even with accelerated, in-hospital protocols, the risks of QT prolongation and bradyarrhythmia remain clinically significant and warrant close observation. They further reinforce that the success of any initiation strategy whether oral outpatient or intravenous inpatient, hinges on meticulous selection and follow-up. Across the included cohorts, patients typically had baseline QTc <450 ms, near-normal electrolytes, and structured ECG surveillance (e.g., CIED remote telemetry or personal 6-lead devices). Practical safeguards should follow labeling and pharmacology: avoid initiation if baseline QTc ≥450 ms, reduce or stop therapy if QTc reaches ≥500 ms, correct electrolytes, review interacting QT-prolonging drugs, and adjust dose for renal function given Sotalol's almost exclusive renal elimination [5].

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, only three observational studies met the eligibility criteria, limiting the overall strength of the evidence and precluding robust assessment of publication bias. Second, the included studies demonstrated substantial clinical heterogeneity with respect to patient selection, indications for Sotalol therapy, initiation protocols, and monitoring strategies. These differences may have influenced both safety outcomes and treatment discontinuation rates. Third, baseline clinical characteristics were not uniformly reported across all studies, limiting comprehensive patient-level risk stratification and reducing the ability to directly compare study populations. Notably, thromboembolic risk scores such as CHA2DS2-VASc were not consistently reported despite atrial fibrillation being the most common indication, further reflecting incomplete data across cohorts.

Additionally, the included cohorts predominantly consisted of carefully selected patients with preserved ventricular function, acceptable baseline QTc intervals, adequate renal function, and access to structured monitoring, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to higher-risk populations. Finally, although QTc prolongation is a clinically relevant surrogate marker of proarrhythmic risk, the included studies were not designed or adequately powered to detect rare but serious adverse events such as torsades de pointes or sudden cardiac death. Furthermore, mortality was not a primary endpoint in any of the included studies. Therefore, while the available evidence suggests a favorable short-term safety profile for outpatient Sotalol initiation in selected patients, the absence of observed torsades de pointes or excess mortality should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive evidence of safety. Despite these limitations, the consistency of findings across three independent cohorts—including one pharmacist-led clinic utilizing remote ECG monitoring and two studies employing structured ECG surveillance provides supportive evidence that outpatient Sotalol initiation may be feasible and safe in carefully selected patients.

Recommendations for future research

To explore the safety and feasibility of outpatient initiation of Sotalol in ambulatory patients, large, sufficiently powered RCTs are necessary to compare the rates of clinical events between inpatient and outpatient initiation protocols.

Conclusion

In certain patients the initiation of Sotalol in an outpatient setting appears to be safe and associated with no significant adverse events requiring drug discontinuation or dose reduction. Although these results are promising, the evidence remains limited to a small number of observational cohorts, and we believe it is premature to recommend widespread adoption of this approach. At present, large randomized controlled trials are warranted to establish the safety and efficacy of outpatient initiation of sotalol.
