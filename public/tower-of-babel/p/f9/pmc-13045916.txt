Extracellular Vesicle‐Mediated Nucleolin Transfer in Glioblastoma: A Targetable Axis Driving Blood‐Tumour Barrier Formation
Authors: Hongzhen Chen, Junyi Zhao, Fang Qiu, Zhuqian Wang, Jie Li, Lingqiang Zhang, Aiping Lu, Chao Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlioblastoma (GBM) remains a significant therapeutic challenge. While GBM‐derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) are known to remodel the normal blood‐brain barrier (BBB) into a blood‐tumour barrier (BTB), the underlying mechanism is largely not understood. Here, we reveal that nucleolin (NCL) is transferred via GBM‐derived EVs to the surface of brain endothelial cells, where it promotes BTB formation. Furthermore, the NCL‐specific aptamer AS1411 exploits this pathway, crossing the BTB through receptor‐mediated transcytosis and selectively entering GBM cells in an NCL‐dependent manner. Proteolysis‐targeting chimeras (PROTACs), heterobifunctional molecules that recruit E3 ligases to degrade target proteins, show therapeutic potential but are hindered by inefficient brain penetration in GBM. Capitalizing on the BTB‐penetrating capability of AS1411 and our prior finding that AS1411 can intracellularly recruit the E3 ligase MDM2 via employing NCL as a molecular bridge, we engineered AS1411‐based PROTACs against VEGFR2 and EGFR. These PROTACs induced NCL‐ and MDM2‐dependent ubiquitination and degradation of VEGFR2 or EGFR in GBM cells, demonstrating potent anti‐tumour activity. Collectively, our findings identify EV‐transferred NCL as a key mediator of BTB formation and a functional transcytosis receptor for AS1411, providing a promising strategy for developing BTB‐permeable, targeted therapy for GBM.Keywords: AS1411, blood‐tumour barrier, glioblastoma, nucleolin, PROTACs

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is one of the most aggressive and lethal brain malignancies, characterized by rapid progression, extensive invasiveness and a dismal prognosis (Louis et al., 2021). Despite advances in standard treatments, including surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, clinical outcomes for GBM patients remain unacceptably low (Desjardins et al., 2018; Hendricks et al., 2015; Chaichana et al., 2015; Mitragotri et al., 2014), with a median survival time of only 15–18 months and fewer than 5% of patients surviving beyond 5 years post‐diagnosis (Chaudhry et al. 2013). A major obstacle to effective treatment is the blood‐brain barrier (BBB), a highly selective structure comprised of endothelial cells connected by tight junctions that line brain capillaries (Van Tellingen et al. 2015). The BBB plays a crucial role in maintaining central nervous system homeostasis by facilitating the transport of essential nutrients while preventing most therapeutic agents in the bloodstream from entering the brain (Arvanitis et al. 2020).

Recently, it has been recognized that GBM alters the brain microenvironment and reshapes the adjacent BBB, resulting in the formation of a dysfunctional vasculature known as the blood‐tumour barrier (BTB) (Testa et al. 2022; Xie et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2017). Within the BTB, capillary endothelial cells exhibit distinct phenotypic and functional characteristics compared to those in the healthy BBB (Zhou et al. 2017; Agnihotri et al. 2023; Testa et al. 2022). However, the precise mechanisms by which GBM induces pathological changes in BTB capillaries remain poorly understood (Lim et al. 2024). Extracellular vesicles (EVs), a family of membrane‐limited vesicles, have emerged as key mediators of intercellular communication between GBM and capillary endothelial cells via the transfer of biomolecules (Salomon et al. 2022; Chandran et al. 2024). Specifically, GBM‐derived EVs can travel to the brain capillaries and deliver pro‐angiogenic factors to capillary endothelial cells, facilitating the abnormal transformation of the BBB into the BTB (Simon et al. 2020; Agnihotri et al. 2023; Villa et al. 2024; Yekula et al. 2020). In contrast, normal astrocyte‐derived EVs lack this capability (Treps et al. 2016; Simon et al. 2020). Thus, a deeper exploration of mechanisms by which GBM‐derived EVs contribute to BTB formation is essential, as such insights will not only clarify the differences between the BTB and the BBB but also provide a foundation for designing BTB‐penetrating, GBM‐targeting therapies (Zhou et al. 2017).

In this study, we revealed that EVs derived from GBM cells carried a substantial amount of nucleolin (NCL), a multifunctional protein known to shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm and, which is also aberrantly localized on the surface of cancer cells, including GBM, but not on normal astrocytes (Wang et al. 2024; Balça‐Silva et al. 2018; Feng et al. 2025). We established an in vitro BBB or BTB model using a coculture system, revealing that NCL was transferred via GBM‐derived EVs to the surface of cocultured capillary endothelial cells. In contrast, EVs derived from normal astrocytes did not mediate NCL translocation to endothelial cells. Moreover, NCL from GBM‐derived EVs actively altered the phenotypes of the cocultured endothelial cells, facilitating the transformation of the BBB into the BTB. Furthermore, NCL has previously been identified as an internalization receptor for its ligands (Jing et al. 2020; Fu et al. 2025). AS1411 is a 26‐base G‐quadruplex‐forming single‐stranded DNA (ssDNA) aptamer that specifically targets NCL (Bates et al. 1999). We found that NCL, upon transfer from GBM‐derived EVs to the surface of BTB endothelial cells, served as a receptor for receptor‐mediated transcytosis (RMT), enabling the selective transcytosis of AS1411 across the BTB. Following its BTB transport, AS1411 specifically recognized and was internalized by GBM cells in a surface NCL‐dependent manner.

Proteolysis‐targeting chimeras (PROTACs) represent an emerging class of therapeutic entities designed to induce the degradation of specific proteins of interest (POIs) (Zhong et al. 2024). These molecules are heterobifunctional, consisting of a recruiter for an E3 ligase and a ligand that binds to the target POI, connected by a chemical linker (Li and Crews 2022). This design enables ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of the POIs (Békés et al. 2022; He et al. 2022). Among various E3 ligases, murine double minute 2 (MDM2) is particularly noteworthy due to its frequent overexpression in multiple cancers, including GBM (Liu et al. 2023), rendering it an attractive E3 component for anti‐tumor PROTACs (Luo et al. 2022). Intracellularly, NCL interacts with MDM2 not as a ubiquitination substrate, but rather as a binding partner (Huang et al. 2024). This NCL binding stabilizes MDM2 and can enhance MDM2‐mediated ubiquitination of its substrate p53, suggesting that NCL acts as a cofactor that potentiates the E3 ligase activity of MDM2 (Lim et al., 2015). Exploiting this partnership, we recently demonstrated that AS1411, upon internalization into cancer cells, can recruit MDM2 by employing NCL as a molecular bridge (Fu et al. 2024). This unique property enables the integration of AS1411 into PROTAC designs, inducing selective degradation of POIs specifically in cancer cells (Fu et al. 2024).

Collectively, we hypothesized that conjugating AS1411 with ligands for POIs could facilitate the development of PROTACs capable of crossing the BTB and treating GBM. Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) are promising therapeutic POIs that are overexpressed in GBM (Batchelor et al. 2013; Arif et al. 2018; Parker et al. 2013). To test our hypothesis, we conjugated AS1411 with a VEGFR2 inhibitor, Cediranib (Ced) (Peach et al. 2018), or an EGFR inhibitor, Gefitinib (Gef) (Noronha et al. 2020), to create PROTACs. Our results showed that these PROTACs effectively brought the NCL‐MDM2 complex into spatial proximity with VEGFR2 or EGFR, leading to their ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation in both NCL‐ and MDM2‐dependent manners. The AS1411‐based PROTACs exhibited potent anti‐tumour effects both in vitro and in vivo, with no significant toxicity to normal tissues.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that GBM cells transfer NCL to brain capillary endothelial cells via EVs, thereby promoting the conversion of the BBB into the BTB. Furthermore, the translocated NCL on endothelial cells acts as a receptor for RMT, enabling specific and efficient transport of AS1411 across the BTB. By integrating this newly identified function of AS1411 in BTB penetration with its previously established ability to recognize and enter GBM cells and then recruit MDM2 in an NCL‐dependent manner (Fu et al. 2024), we ultimately show that AS1411‐based PROTACs represent a promising strategy for the targeted therapy of GBM.

Results

GBM‐Derived EVs Transfer NCL to Brain Capillary Endothelial Cells, Facilitating BTB Formation

To determine the mechanism of GBM‐derived EVs in mediating BTB formation, we isolated EVs from human GBM cell lines (U87MG and U251), human glial cell line SVG p12 and mouse normal astrocytes. These EVs exhibited a characteristic lipid bilayer structure with a peak diameter of approximately 170 nm (Figure S1A–D). Since the orthotopic model with human GBM cell implantation represents a standard preclinical system, we performed a comparative proteomic analysis using EVs derived from U87MG cells and mouse normal astrocytes (Figure 1A). Our data confirmed the presence of EV‐expressing markers, including CD63, ARF6, TSG101 and ANXA6 (Jiang et al., 2022), and the absence of an EV‐negative marker GM130 (Théry et al., 2018; van Niel et al., 2022) in both EV samples (Table S1). Principal component analysis (PCA) indicated a clear separation between U87MG‐derived EVs and normal astrocyte‐derived EVs (Figure 1B). Notably, U87MG‐derived EVs exhibited a significantly higher level of NCL and previously reported pro‐angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and fibroblast growth factors (FGF2 and FGF13) (Simon et al. 2020), compared to normal astrocyte‐derived EVs (Figure 1C). Western blotting analysis confirmed the pronounced enrichment of NCL for U87MG‐ or U251‐derived EVs relative to those EVs from SVG p12 cell line or mouse normal astrocytes (Figure S1E). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that the protein profile of U87MG‐derived EVs was linked to signalling pathways involved in cell proliferation, migration and angiogenesis (Figure 1D). Flow cytometric results confirmed high surface expression of NCL on U87MG and U251 cells but not on mouse normal astrocytes and SVG p12 cells (Figure S1F), consistent with previous studies (Balça‐Silva et al. 2018; Schwab et al. 2023).

To investigate whether NCL carried by GBM‐derived EVs mediated the crosstalk between GBM and brain capillary endothelial cells, we established an in vitro model of BBB or BTB (Yang et al. 2017; Petrovskaya et al. 2022; Tylawsky et al. 2023; Li et al. 2020; Murphy et al. 2024). This model involves coculture of mouse normal astrocytes or U87MG cells with the mouse brain capillary endothelial cell line bEnd.3 in a Transwell system. Specifically, bEnd.3 cells were grown on Matrigel‐coated Transwell inserts, while normal astrocytes or U87MG cells were plated at the bottom of the culture wells (Figure 1E). Following continuous coculture, bEnd.3 cells form tight junctions, effectively replicating the structural and functional properties of the brain endothelium (Yang et al. 2017; Petrovskaya et al. 2022; Tylawsky et al. 2023; Li et al. 2020; Murphy et al. 2024). We detected no NCL protein expression on the surface of bEnd.3 cells cocultured with normal astrocytes. In contrast, a high level of surface NCL protein was detected on bEnd.3 cells cocultured with U87MG cells (Figure 1F,G). However, the level of NCL mRNA in bEnd.3 cells cocultured with U87MG cells did not increase when compared to bEnd.3 cells cultured alone or cocultured with normal astrocytes (Figure S1G). We generated U87MG cells with stable NCL knockdown using shRNA (shNCL) and confirmed reduced NCL levels compared with those cells transfected with scrambled control shRNA (shCtrl) (Figure S1H). Coculture of bEnd.3 cells with shNCL‐transfected U87MG cells resulted in lower surface NCL levels than coculture with shCtrl‐transfected U87MG cells (Figure 1F,G). Moreover, pretreatment of U87MG cells with GW4869, an inhibitor of EV release (Catalano and O'Driscoll 2020), led to a significant decrease in surface NCL on bEnd.3 cells after coculture (Figure 1F,G), suggesting that GBM cells transferred NCL to the surface of capillary endothelial cells via EVs. We also observed that bEnd.3 cells, when cocultured with U87MG cells, showed promoted proliferation, migration and angiogenesis and decreased apoptosis when compared to those cocultured with normal astrocytes, as assessed by 5‐ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine (EdU), Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick‐end labelling (TUNEL), Transwell and tube formation assays (Figures 1H–K and S2). These phenotypic alterations are consistent with the functional properties of the BTB. Conversely, bEnd.3 cells cocultured with shNCL‐transfected or GW4869‐pretreated U87MG cells showed suppressed proliferation, migration and tube formation, as well as increased apoptosis, compared to those cocultured with shCtrl‐transfected or DMSO‐pretreated U87MG cells (Figure 1H–K and S2).

To validate the role of EVs in mediating NCL transfer from GBM cells to the surface of cocultured capillary endothelial cells, we treated bEnd.3 cells with U87MG‐ or normal astrocyte‐derived EVs (Figure 1L). There was a substantial increase in NCL protein on the surface of bEnd.3 cells treated with U87MG‐derived EVs rather than those treated with normal astrocyte‐derived EVs (Figure 1M). However, bEnd.3 cells incubated with EVs derived from NCL‐silenced U87MG cells displayed a reduced level of surface NCL (Figure 1M). After treatment with U87MG‐derived EVs, bEnd.3 cells demonstrated enhanced proliferation, migration and tube formation, and decreased apoptosis compared to those incubated with normal astrocyte‐derived EVs (Figures 1N–R and S3), mimicking the transformation of the BBB into the BTB. In contrast, bEnd.3 cells treated with EVs from NCL‐silenced U87MG cells exhibited diminished BTB characteristics, including reduced proliferation, migration and tube formation, and increased apoptosis, when compared to those cells incubated with shCtrl‐transfected U87MG‐derived EVs (Figure 1N–R and Figure S3).

To further validate the above findings, we employed the human cerebral microvascular endothelial cell line hCMEC/D3 to establish in vitro BBB or BTB models by coculturing it with the human glial cell lines SVG p12 or U87MG (Figure S4A). Consistent with the observations in bEnd.3 cells, no NCL protein was detected on the surface of hCMEC/D3 cells cocultured with SVG p12 cells. In contrast, a high level of surface NCL protein was detected on hCMEC/D3 cells cocultured with U87MG cells (Figure S4B). Coculture with NCL‐silenced U87MG cells led to a reduction in surface NCL protein on hCMEC/D3 cells relative to those cocultured with shCtrl‐transfected U87MG cells (Figure S4B). Similarly, surface NCL expression on hCMEC/D3 cells was decreased after coculture with GW4869‐pretreated U87MG cells (Figure S4A,B). However, NCL mRNA level in hCMEC/D3 cells showed no significant increase after coculture with U87MG cells compared to hCMEC/D3 cells cultured alone or cocultured with SVG p12 cells (Figure S4C). We also treated hCMEC/D3 cells with U87MG‐ or SVG p12‐derived EVs (Figure S4D). A marked increase in surface NCL protein was detected on hCMEC/D3 cells treated with U87MG‐derived EVs, but not on those treated with SVG p12‐derived EVs (Figure S4E). Moreover, hCMEC/D3 cells incubated with EVs from NCL‐silenced U87MG cells showed a significant reduction in surface NCL level (Figure S4E).

To explore the role of GBM‐derived NCL in remodelling the brain capillary endothelium and promoting BTB formation in vivo, we established the orthotopic GBM mouse model by inoculating U87MG cells expressing GFP and firefly luciferase (U87MG‐GFP‐Luc), along with either shNCL or shCtrl, into the brains of BALB/c nude mice (Figure 1S). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed that mice implanted with shCtrl‐transfected U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells exhibited a significant increase in CD31‐positive capillary endothelial cells within the tumour region compared to adjacent non‐tumour brain areas. Both the tumour cells and CD31‐positive capillary endothelial cells exhibited high NCL expression, as evidenced by the co‐localization of NCL with GFP and CD31 (Figure 1T). In contrast, mice inoculated with NCL‐silenced U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells showed a reduction in both the density of CD31‐positive endothelial cells in tumour tissues and the level of NCL expression on these cells relative to the shCtrl group (Figure 1T). These findings collectively indicate that NCL is transferred from GBM cells to brain capillary endothelial cells, facilitating the in vivo transformation of the BBB into the BTB.

NCL Induces the Selective Transcytosis of AS1411 Across the BTB for Targeting GBM Cells

To evaluate the ability of the NCL‐specific aptamer AS1411 to cross the in vitro BBB or BTB, we added Cy5‐labeled AS1411 into the Transwell inserts of the coculture system (Figure 2A). AS1411 showed strong binding affinity to bEnd.3 cells cocultured with U87MG cells, but minimal binding to those cocultured with normal astrocytes (Figure 2B). When NCL expression was silenced in U87MG cells by shNCL, or when these cells were pretreated with the EV release inhibitor GW4869, the binding affinity of AS1411 with bEnd.3 cells were reduced following their coculture with these modified U87MG cells (Figure 2B). Remarkably, we observed substantial penetration of AS1411 across the in vitro BTB model formed by coculturing bEnd.3 cells with U87MG cells, in contrast to the BBB model established with bEnd.3 cells and normal astrocytes (Figure 2C). By contrast, a Cy5‐labeled negative control (NC) sequence or Cy3.5‐labeled 70 kDa dextran was incapable of crossing either the BTB or the BBB model (Figure S5A–B). Moreover, silencing NCL in U87MG cells or inhibiting EV release with GW4869 significantly decreased the penetration efficiency of AS1411 across the BTB model compared to the shCtrl‐ or DMSO‐treated controls (Figure 2C).

We treated bEnd.3 cells in the Transwell inserts with EVs from either U87MG cells or normal astrocytes, followed by the addition of Cy5‐labeled AS1411 into the inserts (Figure 2D). The results demonstrated that AS1411 exhibited a strong binding affinity with bEnd.3 cells treated with U87MG‐derived EVs, while minimal binding was observed with those exposed to normal astrocyte‐derived EVs (Figure 2E). Furthermore, treatment with EVs from NCL‐silenced U87MG cells significantly reduced AS1411 binding to bEnd.3 cells compared to treatment with EVs from shCtrl‐transfected U87MG cells (Figure 2E). We also observed that the penetration of AS1411 across the BTB model was markedly enhanced when bEnd.3 cells were treated with U87MG‐derived EVs compared to normal astrocyte‐derived EVs (Figure 2F). In contrast, bEnd.3 cells incubated with EVs from NCL‐silenced U87MG cells showed reduced AS1411 penetration compared with those treated with EVs from shCtrl‐transfected U87MG cells (Figure 2F).

The RMT process involves a series of steps, including receptor‐ligand binding, endocytosis and exocytosis (Pulgar 2019), facilitating the transport of therapeutic cargo from the apical to the basolateral plasma membranes of brain capillary endothelial cells (Delsing et al. 2018). To determine whether AS1411 crosses the BTB via an RMT‐dependent mechanism, we added endocytic or exocytic inhibitors into the Transwell inserts of the coculture system prior to assessing AS1411 transport across the BTB model (Figure 2G). We showed that either Chlorpromazine (CPZ, an inhibitor of clathrin‐mediated endocytosis) (Bouley et al. 2020), Amiloride (AMR, an inhibitor of macropinocytosis) (Koivusalo et al. 2010), or Endosidin‐2 (ES2, an inhibitor of exocytosis) (Zhang et al. 2016) reduced the penetration of AS1411 across the BTB model formed by coculturing bEnd.3 cells with U87MG cells (Figure 2H). We also incubated bEnd.3 cells with EVs derived from U87MG cells in the Transwell inserts and added AS1411 into the inserts in the presence or absence of CPZ, AMR, or ES2 (Figure 2I). Our results demonstrated that the BTB‐penetrating ability of AS1411 was inhibited by CPZ, AMR, or ES2 (Figure 2J).

We also cocultured hCMEC/D3 cells with SVG p12 cells or U87MG cells to validate the BTB penetration capability of AS1411 (Figure S5C). Consistent with the observation in bEnd.3 cells, significant penetration of AS1411 was detected across the BTB model formed by coculturing hCMEC/D3 cells with U87MG cells, whereas minimal penetration occurred in the BBB model established with hCMEC/D3 cells and SVG p12 cells (Figure S5D). In contrast, neither the NC sequence nor 70 kDa dextran could cross the BTB or the BBB model (Figure S5D). Silencing NCL expression in U87MG cells or pretreating them with GW4869 reduced the penetration ratio of AS1411 across the BTB model after their coculture with hCMEC/D3 cells, compared to hCMEC/D3 cells cocultured with shCtrl‐transfected or DMSO‐pretreated U87MG cells (Figure S5E,F). We treated hCMEC/D3 cells in the Transwell inserts with EVs derived from U87MG cells or SVG p12 cells before adding Cy5‐labeled AS1411 (Figure S5G). Penetration of AS1411 across the BTB model was notably enhanced when hCMEC/D3 cells were incubated with U87MG‐derived EVs compared to SVG p12‐derived EVs (Figure S5H). Moreover, inhibition of endocytosis with CPZ or AMR, or inhibition of exocytosis with ES2 reduced the penetration of AS1411 across the BTB model when hCMEC/D3 cells were cocultured with U87MG cells (Figure S5I,J). Similarly, when hCMEC/D3 cells were treated with U87MG‐derived EVs and AS1411 in the presence of CPZ, AMR, or ES2 (Figure S5K), all three inhibitors suppressed the BTB penetration of AS1411 (Figure S5L). These findings indicate that NCL transferred via GBM‐derived EVs functions as an RMT receptor on BTB endothelial cells rather than on BBB endothelial cells, facilitating the selective transcytosis of AS1411 across the BTB.

We evaluated the in vivo BTB‐penetrating and GBM‐targeting capacity of AS1411 using the orthotopic GBM mouse model established by intracranial inoculation of U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells. Cy5‐labeled AS1411 or NC sequence was intravenously injected into the mice, and fluorescent intensities were detected at 1, 4 and 8 h using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Figure 2K). The strong bioluminescent signal from firefly luciferase indicated the development of GBM in mice inoculated with U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells (Figure 2L). There was an abundant accumulation of fluorescent intensity of Cy5‐labeled AS1411 in the brain tissues of GBM mice at different time points, whereas the fluorescent signal of the Cy5‐labeled NC sequence was not detectable in the brain tissues of GBM mice (Figure 2L). When inoculating NCL‐silenced U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells into the brains of mouse models, we observed less distribution of AS1411 in the brain tissues when compared to mice inoculated with shCtrl‐transfected U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells (Figure 2L). Moreover, no brain accumulation of AS1411 was observed in normal mice without tumour inoculation (Figure 2L). Ex vivo analysis confirmed that AS1411 could penetrate the BTB rather than the BBB, and the BTB‐penetrating ability of AS1411 was dependent on GBM‐derived NCL (Figure 2M). Our IF staining further showed that AS1411, rather than the NC sequence, was co‐localized with U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells, but not with non‐tumour cells on brain sections from GBM mice (Figure 2N). Silencing of NCL decreased the in vivo co‐localization of AS1411 with U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells compared to shCtrl‐transfected U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells (Figure 2N). No distribution of AS1411 was observed in brain sections from normal mice without inoculation of U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells (Figure 2N). Quantitative data confirmed the BTB‐penetrating and GBM‐targeting capacity of AS1411 (Figure 2O). Additionally, we extended the observation time for the BTB penetration and GBM targeting of AS1411 and the NC sequence to 24 h. At this extended time point, the NC sequence showed negligible accumulation in the brain tissues of GBM‑bearing mice. In contrast, AS1411 continued to efficiently cross the BTB and accumulate specifically within the tumour region of the GBM tissues (Figure S6A–C).

AS1411 Exhibits Selective Internalization Into GBM Cells

We determined the GBM‐internalizing ability of AS1411 in vitro. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the binding ability of Cy5‐labeled AS1411 with U87MG cells was significantly higher than that of the Cy5‐labeled NC sequence (Figure S7A). Confocal imaging showed that Cy5‐labeled AS1411 was internalized into U87MG cells, whereas no entry of Cy5‐labeled NC sequence into U87MG cells was observed (Figure S7B). Similarly, we also demonstrated that AS1411 exhibited stronger binding affinity with another GBM cell line, U251, compared to the NC sequence in vitro (Figure S7C). The uptake of AS1411 into U251 cells was more pronounced than that of the NC sequence (Figure S7D). In contrast, AS1411 displayed no binding ability with either mouse normal astrocytes or a non‐tumorigenic epithelial cell line MCF 10A, and internalization of AS1411 into these cells was not detectable in vitro (Figure S7E–H).

Multicellular tumour spheroids are considered promising in vitro 3D models that bridge the gap between in vitro 2D cellular monolayers and in vivo animal models (Roy et al. 2021), as they more closely mimic the characteristics of solid tumours, such as spatial distribution of tumour cells, extracellular matrix, cell adhesion, hypoxia and acidic microenvironment (Liang et al. 2021). We established 3D spheroids of U87MG and U251 cells and observed that Cy5‐labeled AS1411 could penetrate these spheroids, whereas the NC sequence was incapable of entering the spheroids (Figure S8A–C). When silencing NCL in spheroids of U87MG cells, AS1411 could not penetrate the spheroids, suggesting that the penetration of AS1411 into GBM spheroids is NCL‐dependent (Figure S8D–F).

AS1411‐Based PROTACs Promote Spatial Proximity Between the NCL‐MDM2 Complex and POIs

Since we previously demonstrated that AS1411 could be repurposed as an MDM2 recruiter in PROTACs by forming an NCL‐bridged complex, that is, AS1411‐NCL‐MDM2 (Fu et al., 2024), we aimed to develop AS1411‐based PROTACs for degrading VEGFR2 or EGFR, both are promising POIs against GBM (Batchelor et al. 2013; Arif et al. 2018; Parker et al. 2013). Our co‐immunoprecipitation (IP) assays confirmed that NCL was associated with MDM2, forming a complex that did not involve VEGFR2 or EGFR in U87MG cells (Figure 3A). We labelled AS1411 with biotin and conducted pull‐down assays using streptavidin‐coated magnetic beads. Our results showed that AS1411 captured both NCL and MDM2, but not VEGFR2 or EGFR, in U87MG cells (Figure 3B). However, when silencing NCL in U87MG cells, AS1411 could not capture MDM2 (Figure 3C), suggesting that the recruitment of MDM2 by AS1411 was dependent on NCL. Furthermore, to address the possibility that NCL recruits additional E3 ligases other than MDM2, we performed AS1411‐based pull‐down assays followed by mass spectrometry (MS) analysis. Among the most enriched proteins, MDM2 was highly abundant, whereas no other E3 ligase was significantly involved (Table S2). We also observed the in vivo co‐localization of AS1411 with both NCL and MDM2 in the tumour region of brain tissues from GBM mice (Figure S9A,B). We then conjugated AS1411 to a VEGFR2 inhibitor, Ced (Peach et al. 2018), or an EGFR inhibitor, Gef (Noronha et al. 2020), via a linear alkyl linker, yielding heterobifunctional molecules termed AS1411‐Ced (Figure 3D and Scheme 1) or AS1411‐Gef (Figure 3E and Scheme 2). AS1411 is a G‐rich aptamer that folds into a G‐quadruplex, which contributes to its specific binding to NCL (Carvalho et al. 2019). N‐methylmesoporphyrin IX (NMM) is a widely utilized fluorescent probe for the detection of G‐quadruplex (Dobrovodsky et al. 2024). After incubation with NMM, we observed comparable fluorescent absorption spectra for AS1411, AS1411‐Ced and AS1411‐Gef (Figure 3F), suggesting that conjugation of AS1411 to Ced or Gef did not compromise the NCL‐binding ability of AS1411.

To test whether AS1411‐based PROTACs could induce spatial proximity between the NCL‐MDM2 complex and POIs, we treated U87MG cells with PBS or AS1411‐based PROTACs and performed co‐IP assays. Our results demonstrated that NCL and MDM2 were coprecipitated with VEGFR2 in U87MG cells incubated with AS1411‐Ced, but not in cells treated with PBS (Figure 3G), indicating that AS1411‐Ced enabled the formation of a quaternary complex involving MDM2, NCL, VEGFR2 and itself. When NCL was silenced in U87MG cells, we observed that VEGFR2 failed to coprecipitate MDM2 in the presence of AS1411‐Ced (Figure 3H). However, VEGFR2 was still coprecipitated with NCL in MDM2‐silenced U87MG cells treated with AS1411‐Ced (Figure 3I). These findings suggest that NCL acts as a molecular bridge between MDM2 and VEGFR2, facilitating the assembly of the MDM2‐NCL‐PROTAC‐VEGFR2 quaternary complex. Similarly, NCL and MDM2 were effectively coprecipitated with EGFR in U87MG cells incubated with AS1411‐Gef (Figure 3J). Silencing of NCL destroyed the coprecipitation of MDM2 with EGFR in U87MG cells (Figure 3K), while silencing of MDM2 did not affect the coprecipitation of NCL with EGFR (Figure 3L), suggesting that AS1411‐Gef induced the formation of a complex involving MDM2, NCL, EGFR and itself, with NCL acting as a molecular bridge between MDM2 and EGFR.

AS1411‐Based PROTACs Induce Degradation of POIs in Both NCL‐ and MDM2‐Dependent Manners

To assess whether AS1411‐Ced could induce VEGFR2 degradation, we treated U87MG or U251 cells with increasing concentrations and incubation times of AS1411‐Ced. Our results showed that AS1411‐Ced decreased VEGFR2 protein levels in both cell lines in concentration‐ and time‐dependent manners (Figure 4A,B). We treated U87MG or U251 cells with PBS, AS1411, Ced, AS1411/Ced Mix (the physical mixture of AS1411 and Ced), or AS1411‐Ced. In contrast to PBS, AS1411‐Ced induced the degradation of VEGFR2, whereas AS1411, Ced, or AS1411/Ced Mix had no effect on VEGFR2 level (Figures 4C and S10A), suggesting that the spatial proximity of the NCL‐MDM2 complex with VEGFR2 induced by AS1411‐Ced was responsible for the degradation of VEGFR2. IF staining results confirmed that AS1411‐Ced reduced the VEGFR2 protein level in U87MG cells (Figure 4D). Proteomic analysis validated that AS1411‐Ced induced VEGFR2 degradation and modulated the expression of several proteins that act as potential downstream effectors of VEGFR2 or participate in the diverse biological processes regulated by VEGFR2 signalling (Figure 4E and Table S3). Consistently, pathway enrichment analysis revealed that AS1411‐Ced remarkably downregulated VEGFR2‐mediated signalling pathways, including those regulating cell migration, cell cycle, blood vessel development and epithelial cell differentiation, while upregulating signalling pathways related to cell apoptosis and growth inhibition (Figure 4F). We also evaluated whether AS1411‐Gef was an effective PROTAC for degrading EGFR. Treatment of U87MG and U251 cells with AS1411‐Gef resulted in a concentration‐ and time‐dependent decrease in EGFR protein level (Figure 4G,H). AS1411‐Gef, rather than AS1411, Gef, or AS1411/Gef Mix (the physical mixture of AS1411 and Gef), induced EGFR degradation compared to PBS (Figures 4I and S10B). IF staining showed that AS1411‐Gef treatment reduced EGFR protein levels in U87MG cells (Figure 4J). Proteomic analysis validated that AS1411‐Gef promoted EGFR degradation and altered the abundance of numerous proteins acting as downstream effectors of EGFR or participating in a broad spectrum of biological responses orchestrated by EGFR signalling (Figure 4K and Table S4). Consistent with these results, pathway enrichment analysis revealed that AS1411‐Gef downregulated EGFR‐mediated signalling pathways involved in cell proliferation, epithelial cell migration and differentiation, while promoting signalling pathways associated with cell death and survival suppression (Figure 4L).

We investigated whether AS1411‐Ced and AS1411‐Gef utilized the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway to trigger the degradation of VEGFR2 and EGFR, respectively. We showed that AS1411‐Ced increased ubiquitination of VEGFR2 in U87MG cells (Figure 4M). The addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 inhibited the degradation of VEGFR2 induced by AS1411‐Ced (Figure 4N). Furthermore, silencing of NCL or MDM2 abolished the AS1411‐Ced‐mediated degradation of VEGFR2 in U87MG cells (Figure 4O,P). In parallel, we also demonstrated that AS1411‐Gef promoted the ubiquitination of EGFR in U87MG cells (Figure 4Q). The presence of MG132 prevented the degradation of EGFR induced by AS1411‐Gef (Figure 4R). Treatment of NCL‐ or MDM2‐silenced U87MG cells with AS1411‐Gef did not result in efficient EGFR degradation (Figure 4S,T). Proteasome subunit beta type 4 (PSMB4) is a non‐catalytic component of the 20S core proteasome complex (Montenegro‐Venegas et al. 2021). IF imaging showed the co‐localization of PSMB4 with VEGFR2 or EGFR in U87MG cells following treatment with AS1411‐Ced or AS1411‐Gef (Figure 4U,V). These results indicate that AS1411‐Ced and AS1411‐Gef induce the proteasomal degradation of VEGFR2 and EGFR in both NCL‐ and MDM2‐dependent manners, respectively.

AS1411‐Based PROTACs Display Anti‐GBM Effects in Vitro

We evaluated the anti‐GBM potential of AS1411‐Ced in vitro using U87MG and U251 cells. Both cell lines were treated with PBS, AS1411, Ced, AS1411/Ced Mix and AS1411‐Ced, respectively. Cell proliferation was measured using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay and EdU staining, which revealed that AS1411‐Ced significantly inhibited the proliferation of both U87MG and U251 cells compared to PBS, AS1411, Ced, or AS1411/Ced Mix (Figures 5A,B and S11A,B). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that AS1411‐Ced markedly increased the apoptotic rate in both cell lines (Figures 5C and S11C). To assess the impact of AS1411‐Ced on cell invasion, we employed a 3D spheroid invasion assay, which mimics the native 3D tumour microenvironment by embedding tumour spheroids in an extracellular matrix (Vinci et al. 2015; Castillo et al. 2016). Notably, AS1411‐Ced treatment significantly attenuated the invasion of U87MG cell spheroids (Figure 5D). Similarly, we evaluated the in vitro effects of AS1411‐Gef on proliferation, apoptosis and invasion of GBM cells. AS1411‐Gef demonstrated high efficacy in reducing the proliferation of U87MG and U251 cells compared to other treatments, as evidenced by CCK‐8 and EdU staining assays (Figures 5E,F and S11D,E). Flow cytometric assays showed that AS1411‐Gef significantly increased the apoptotic rate in both cell lines (Figures 5G and S11F). AS1411‐Gef reduced the invasion of U87MG cell spheroids in 3D spheroid invasion assays (Figure 5H).

AS1411‐Based PROTACs Exhibit Robust Anti‐Tumor Potency in Orthotopic GBM Xenografts

We further evaluate the in vivo therapeutic effects of AS1411‐Ced in the orthotopic GBM mouse model with intracranial inoculation of U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells. Seven days post‐xenograft, the mice were randomly grouped and intravenously received PBS, AS1411, Ced, AS1411/Ced Mix and AS1411‐Ced every other day for 8 days (Figure 6A). Mice were monitored daily for GBM progression indicators, including seizures, ataxia and weight loss, and were euthanised if a symptomatic tumour burden developed (El Meskini et al. 2015). Brain tumour growth was assessed by bioluminescent imaging using IVIS and survival rates were analysed using Kaplan‐Meier curves (Frenster and Placantonakis 2018). Mice treated with AS1411‐Ced exhibited remarkable tumour growth inhibition and extended median survival compared to those receiving PBS, AS1411, Ced, or AS1411/Ced Mix (Figure 6B–D). Histological examinations of whole‐brain sections by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed smaller GBM tumours from the mice treated with AS1411‐Ced compared to those from the mice in other treatment groups (Figures 6E and S12). IF analysis of GBM tumour sections showed significant degradation of VEGFR2 protein and fewer Ki‐67‐positive proliferative cells in the mice treated with AS1411‐Ced (Figure 6F,G). TUNEL assays demonstrated that AS1411‐Ced treatment induced substantial apoptosis in GBM tissues (Figure 6F,G). Notably, AS1411‐Ced did not significantly affect the body weight (Figure S13A) or alter levels of hepatic and renal function markers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin (ALB) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Figure S13B). H&E staining of non‐tumour organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) showed no obvious systemic toxicity or abnormality in AS1411‐Ced‐treated mice (Figure S13C).

To determine the therapeutic potential of AS1411‐Gef for GBM, the orthotopic GBM mice were administered with PBS, AS1411, Gef, AS1411/Gef Mix and AS1411‐Gef via tail vein injection every other day for 8 days (Figure S14A). Mice treated with AS1411‐Gef showed reduced tumour size and extended median survival when compared to other treatment groups, as evidenced by bioluminescent imaging, Kaplan‐Meier curve and HE staining (Figures S14B–14E and S15). IF and TUNEL staining of GBM tissues from AS1411‐Gef‐treated mice showed enhanced degradation of EGFR protein, reduced Ki‐67‐positive cells and increased apoptotic activity (Figure S14F,G). Furthermore, AS1411‐Gef treatment did not significantly alter body weight, serum hepatic and renal function parameters (ALT, AST, ALB and ALP), or the histology of non‐tumour organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) compared to other treatment groups (Figure S16A–C).

We also established an orthotopic GBM mouse model via intracranial inoculation of patient‐derived GBM cells expressing GFP and firefly luciferase (GBM‐GFP‐Luc) (Figure S17A). Using this model, we assessed the BTB‑penetrating ability of AS1411. Cy5‐labeled AS1411 or NC sequence was intravenously injected, and fluorescent intensities were detected 8 h post‐injection. There was an abundant accumulation of fluorescent intensity of Cy5‐labeled AS1411 in the brain tumour tissues of GBM mice, whereas no detectable signal was found in mice receiving the Cy5‐labeled NC sequence (Figure S17B). To determine the therapeutic potential of AS1411‐Gef and AS1411‐Ced against patient‐derived GBM cells, the orthotopic model was administered with PBS, AS1411‐Ced, or AS1411‐Gef via tail vein injection every other day for 8 days. Both AS1411‑Ced and AS1411‑Gef significantly reduced tumour size and extended median survival compared to the PBS group, as evidenced by bioluminescent imaging, Kaplan‐Meier curve and HE staining (Figure S17C–F). These results align with the above findings obtained from the U87MG‑GFP‑Luc intracranial model, further reinforcing the therapeutic efficacy of AS1411‑based PROTACs.

Discussion

The development of efficient and safe therapies for GBM remains an arduous journey, as reflected by the largely negative outcome of most clinical trials (Ntafoulis et al. 2022). This dilemma is primarily attributed to the presence of biological barriers, including the BBB and its modified form, the BTB, which restrict the targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to tumour sites (Mokarram et al. 2025). The distinction between the BBB and BTB, however, remains a subject of ongoing debate (Sarkaria et al. 2018). While some studies suggest that the BTB retains certain features of the BBB, such as the expression of specific transporters and efflux pumps, others argue that it is markedly compromised in GBM, exhibiting increased vascular leakiness and aberrant signalling pathways (Blethen et al. 2021). This ongoing controversy underscores the complexity of the BTB and highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of its biological and functional properties (Zhou et al. 2017).

EVs, loaded with diverse cargoes such as proteins and nucleic acids (Di Bella 2022), play a vital role in maintaining GBM by reprogramming the adjacent brain microenvironment into a tumour‐promoting system (Yekula et al. 2019). EVs are released from donor cells through the outward budding of the plasma membrane and can subsequently fuse with recipient cells (Abels and Breakefield 2016). Recent studies have emphasized the crucial involvement of GBM‐derived EVs in facilitating crosstalk between GBM and brain capillary endothelial cells, driving the transformation of the BBB into BTB (Chandran et al. 2024). For example, numerous microRNAs carried within GBM‐derived EVs have been reported to participate in this process by modulating the permeability and integrity of the BBB (Lucero et al. 2020; Indira Chandran et al. 2024). In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that GBM cells, rather than normal astrocytes, act as ‘donor’ cells that transfer NCL via EVs to the surface of brain capillary endothelial cells (‘receptor’ cells) (Figure 7A,B). This finding resolves a long‐standing controversy regarding the structural and functional specialization of the BTB and constitutes a mechanistic insight. Considering that NCL is overexpressed in GBM cells and can be partially translocated to their surface (Balça‐Silva et al. 2018; Schwab et al. 2023), we speculate that NCL carried by EVs originates from the surface of GBM cells and anchors on endothelial cells through direct membrane fusion. In addition to NCL, we demonstrated that GBM‐derived EVs also carried well‐established pro‐angiogenic factors, including FGF2, FGF13 and VEGFA, which have been implicated in GBM‐derived EVs‐mediated BTB formation (Simon et al. 2020).

(A) No transfer of NCL from astrocytes to the surface of brain capillary endothelial cells via EVs, maintaining the normal function of the BBB. (B) EV‐mediated transfer of NCL from GBM cells to the surface of brain capillary endothelial cells, facilitating the transformation of the BBB into the BTB. (C) Transcytosis of AS1411 across the BTB in an NCL‐dependent manner, enabling its selective accumulation in GBM cells. (D) Development of BTB‐penetrating, GBM‐targeting PROTACs via conjugating AS1411 with ligands of POIs such as VEGFR2 and EGFR. Briefly, the AS1411‐based PROTACs traverse the BTB, enter GBM cells selectively, and bring the POIs into proximity with the NCL‐MDM2 complex. This spatial arrangement induces POI ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, leading to targeted anti‐GBM therapy.

Regarding the role of NCL in BTB formation, we found that EV‐mediated transfer of NCL from GBM cells to brain capillary endothelial cells promoted the pathological transformation of the BBB into the BTB, as evidenced by the enhanced survival and activities of the capillary endothelial cells. This observation aligns with previous reports showing that GBM‐associated capillary endothelial cells exhibited reduced apoptosis and increased resistance to cytotoxic treatments (Charalambous et al. 2006; Rui and Green 2021), underscoring the active participation of NCL in the pathological reorganization of the BTB, similar to the functions previously described for VEGFA and FGFs (Simon et al. 2020). Importantly, we confirmed that normal astrocytes do not express NCL on their surface, supporting the established view that NCL is specifically localized on the surface of cancer cells and is not translocated to the membrane of normal cells (Palmieri et al. 2015). Furthermore, astrocytes were unable to deliver NCL via EVs to capillary endothelial cells, highlighting the unique role of GBM‐derived EV‐carried NCL in mediating the transformation of the BBB into the BTB. These findings elucidate the critical role of EVs‐loaded NCL in mediating communication between GBM cells and brain capillary endothelial cells, providing a strong foundation for developing NCL‐targeting strategies to enhance drug delivery across the BTB for targeted treatment of GBM.

RMT has been extensively explored as a means for delivering therapeutics into the brain (Deeken and Löscher 2007; Oller‐Salvia et al. 2016; Jiang et al. 2018; Israel et al. 2019). AS1411, originally developed as an anticancer agent, failed in clinical trials due to limited efficacy (Rosenberg et al. 2014). However, its high affinity for NCL, which is overexpressed on the surface of many cancer cells, has since made AS1411 a frequently used ligand for targeted delivery (Tong et al. 2022). Despite its broad application, no prior evidence had demonstrated that AS1411 can cross the BTB via RMT. Here, we reveal that AS1411 specifically traverses the BTB but not the BBB. This selectivity depends on AS1411 recognizing NCL that is transferred from GBM cells to the surface of BTB endothelial cells via EVs (Figure 7C), as supported by our findings that silencing NCL in GBM cells or blocking EV secretion from GBM cells reduced AS1411 penetration into the BTB endothelium. Previous reports indicate that cell‐surface NCL facilitates AS1411 cellular internalization via clathrin‐mediated endocytosis and macropinocytosis (Reyes‐Reyes et al. 2010; Van den Avont and Sharma‐Walia 2023), both of which, along with exocytosis, are essential for transcytosis (Pulgar 2019). Consistent with this, we found that blocking clathrin‐mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, or exocytosis in capillary endothelial cells abolished AS1411 transport across the BTB, confirming the involvement of a canonical RMT process. Collectively, our work establishes that EV‐transferred NCL functions as a bona fide receptor on BTB endothelial cells, enabling specific and efficient RMT of NCL‐targeting ligands across the BTB. This insight directly addresses the prevailing view that leveraging differences between the BBB and BTB is crucial for discovering BTB‐penetrating ligands. Using AS1411 as a proof‐of‐concept, we show that NCL targeting represents a promising strategy for BTB penetration—an advance that could be extended to other NCL‐directed agents and broaden the therapeutic toolkit for GBM.

The development of PROTACs capable of effectively targeting intracranial lesions faces significant challenges (Tashima 2023). Recently, a few PROTACs, such as C004091, XL01126 and ARV‐102, have been developed with the ability to penetrate the BBB (Liu et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2021; Gregory et al. 2024). However, these PROTACs are designed for neurogenerative diseases, targeting tau and leucine‐rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), and their mechanisms of BBB penetration remain unclear (Liu et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2021). To date, no PROTACs capable of crossing BTB to degrade POIs in GBM cells have been reported. As an alternative, researchers have proposed encapsulating PROTACs within nanoparticles that can penetrate the BBB for GBM therapy (Yang et al. 2022; Zou et al. 2024; He et al. 2022). In our previous work, we repurposed AS1411 as a recruiter of the E3 ligase MDM2, as it selectively entered cancer cells via targeting surface NCL and then recruited MDM2 using intracellular NCL as a molecular bridge (Fu et al. 2024). By incorporating AS1411 into PROTACs, we successfully modulated the expression of various oncogenic POIs, such as STAT3, c‐Myc, c‐MET and VEGF165(Feng et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2024). In this study, we discovered that AS1411 also possesses the ability to penetrate BTB, allowing us to design BTB‐penetrating and GBM‐targeting PROTACs (Figure 7D). Using VEGFR2 and EGFR as examples of POIs (Batchelor et al. 2013; Arif et al. 2018; Parker et al. 2013), we demonstrated that AS1411‐based PROTACs induced the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of these targets in GBM cells. This led to the inhibition of tumour cell growth and significant shrinkage of GBM tissues in mouse models. Moreover, these PROTACs exhibited minimal toxicity, likely due to their BTB‐specific penetration and GBM targeting facilitated by AS1411. Notably, AS1411‐based PROTACs offer advantages over the nanoparticle‐loaded PROTACs, as they integrate the triple capabilities of BTB penetration, GBM targeting and MDM2 recruitment into a single heterobifunctional molecule. These serve primarily as a proof‐of‐concept to validate the feasibility of exploiting the newly discovered EV‐NCL‐dependent BTB‐crossing mechanism for targeted therapy against GBM. Importantly, this strategy is not limited to PROTACs; it will open avenues for delivering diverse therapeutics (siRNAs, small molecules, antibodies, peptides, etc.) across the BTB via NCL‐targeting ligands, offering a general solution to a major challenge in GBM‐targeted therapy.

Despite the promising advancements demonstrated in this study, several limitations warrant further investigation. First, it has been established that both the length and composition of linkers play critical roles in determining the physicochemical properties and efficacy of PROTACs (Troup et al. 2020; Weerakoon et al. 2022). Our AS1411‐based PROTACs, hybrid molecules consisting of oligonucleotides conjugated to small molecules, were evaluated using only a single, commonly employed alkyl linker. We did not systematically examine how variations in linker length or composition affect their performance, representing an important area for future optimization. Second, while our study provides a proof‐of‐concept demonstration of the potential of AS1411‐based PROTACs for targeted therapy of GBM, several key translational parameters, including the long‐term stability, pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity of these PROTACs in vivo, remain to be fully characterized. Addressing these aspects will be essential for advancing these agents toward clinical application. Finally, it will also be valuable to explore the broader utility of AS1411 and other NCL‐targeting ligands in diverse therapeutic modalities for GBM, extending beyond PROTACs to include siRNAs, antibody conjugates and other targeted delivery systems.

In conclusion, our findings establish that NCL, transferred from GBM cells to brain capillary endothelial cells via EVs, plays a pivotal role in promoting BTB formation. The NCL‐specific AS1411 exhibits three functionally distinct and therapeutically valuable properties: (1) the ability to penetrate the BTB, (2) selective targeting of GBM cells, and (3) recruitment of the E3 ligase MDM2. By leveraging these capabilities, AS1411‐based PROTACs represent a highly promising strategy for the targeted treatment of GBM, combining potent anti‐tumor efficacy with a favourable safety profile.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

U87MG (p53 wild‑type), SVG p12, MCF 10A, HEK293T and bEnd.3 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). U251 (p53 mutant) and hCMEC/D3 cell lines were obtained from Wuhan Servicebio Biotechnology. Patient‐derived primary GBM cells were obtained from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology. The U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cell line and patient‐derived GBM‐GFP‐Luc cells were obtained by transduction using a viral vector expressing firefly luciferase and GFP protein (pCH‐EF1a‐GFP‐T2A‐Luc2‐Ires‐puro). The transduction was carried out as previously described (Maes et al. 2009). After transduction, cells were treated with puromycin, sorted and single‐cell clones were generated. Mouse normal astrocytes were isolated according to the previous protocol and seeded on a T75 flask coated with poly‐L‐lysine (0.005 mg/ml, Beyotime, CHN) (Schildge et al. 2013). bEnd.3 and hCMEC/D3 cells were cultured in EGM‐2 Endothelial Cell Growth Medium‐2 BulletKit (Lonza, CH). All other cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Corning, USA) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cell cultures were maintained at a standard condition of 37°C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2.

Plasmid Constructs, Lentivirus Packaging and Transduction

To silence NCL gene expression, cells were transfected with Plko.1 construct (IGE BIO). All plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequencing. For lentivirus packaging and infection, HEK293T cells were seeded onto a 10 cm dish at a density of 1 × 106. The transfection procedure was performed after 24 h, using 1 mg of target plasmids, 0.75 mg of psPAX2 and 0.25 mg of pMD2.G. The culture media were replaced at 6 h following the transfection. The initial batch of material was collected at 48 h after transfection, without further changes to the medium. After a medium refreshment, an additional collection of media was performed at 72 h after transfection. The two medium batches were combined and centrifuged at 1250 r/min for 5 min to remove cell debris. The resulting supernatant was then kept in 1 mL portions at a temperature of −80°C. 1 × 106 U87MG cells were seeded onto a 6‐well plate for each well and, after 24 h, were given 1 mL of the thawed lentivirus solution, which was gently stirred to ensure thorough mixing. The medium was substituted 24 h after the introduction of the virus. The antibiotic selection process began 48 h after transfection, using 2 mg/mL puromycin in the media. This selection continued until most cells died, leaving only the resistant cells, which finally attained confluency. Following that, the cells were prepped for extended preservation in liquid nitrogen.

EV Isolation and Characterization

Cells were left in culture until they had reached 70%–80% confluency, washed three times with PBS, and further cultured in FBS‐free medium for 24 h before collection of the conditioned medium for EV purification (Rontogianni et al. 2019). EVs were isolated by differential ultracentrifugation as previously described with some modifications. Briefly, conditioned medium (100 mL; 10 × 10‐cm dishes per replicate) was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to pellet cells. Then, the supernatant was centrifuged for 30 min at 10,000 × g in a SW 28 rotor to pellet apoptotic bodies and cellular debris. The collected media was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min to pellet EVs. Finally, the EV pellet was resuspended in PBS, carefully washed and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min to collect the final EV pellets. All centrifugation steps were performed at 4°C. The total EV protein concentrations were determined by the BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins from isolated EVs were prepared for proteomics. EVs were characterized according to the guidelines of the International Society for EVs. EV size and concentration were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (Particle Metrix, PMX‐120, DE). The presence of EV markers CD9, CD63 and Alix and the absence of a Golgi marker (GM130) as a NC was evaluated by proteomics. For TEM analysis, EV samples were loaded onto copper EM grids, stained with phosphotungstic acid for 1 min, and then washed with deionized water and observed by TEM (Hitachi, ht‐7700, JPN).

Sample Preparation for Proteomics

Protein extracts from EVs or U87MG cells were prepared using the EasyPep Mini MS Sample Prep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) (Varnavides et al. 2022). Initially, EVs or cells were lysed with the provided lysis buffer to extract proteins. 100 µg of the protein extract was then transferred to a fresh microcentrifuge tube, and the volume was brought up to 100 µL with the same lysis buffer. Subsequent steps involved the addition of reduction and alkylation solutions to the protein sample. The mixture was gently mixed and subjected to incubation at 95°C for 10 min to facilitate the reduction and alkylation processes. Post‐incubation, the sample was allowed to cool to room temperature. The reconstituted Trypsin/Lys‐C Protease Mix was then introduced to the prepared protein sample, followed by incubation with shaking at 37°C for 2 h, to achieve protein digestion. Upon completion of the digestion, the stop solution was added to terminate the enzymatic reaction. Peptides were then purified using a peptide clean‐up column. The samples were dried via vacuum centrifugation and subsequently reconstituted in a 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution, preparing them for LC‐MS/MS analysis.

Nanoflow LC‐MS/MS Analysis

The Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used in conjunction with an Easy‐nLC 1000 ultrahigh‐pressure liquid chromatography pump (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for the LC‐MS/MS analysis. Separation was achieved using a trap column and an analytic column with a spray tip, both filled with 3 µm/120 Å ReproSil‐Pur C18 resins (Dr. Maisch GmbH, DE). The employed separation buffers were comprised of 0.1% formic acid in both water and acetonitrile. A fraction of the collected samples was initially introduced into the trap column with a 2 µL/min flow rate, and then, it was separated via the analytical column at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The separation gradient was established as starting with 3%–7% acetonitrile over 2 min, increasing to 22% acetonitrile over the next 50 min, then to 35% acetonitrile in 10 min, surging to 90% acetonitrile within 2 min, maintaining at 90% for 6 min, dropping back to 3% acetonitrile in 2 min and finally stabilizing at 3% acetonitrile for a duration of 13 min. Full MS scans were performed in an Orbitrap mass analyser over m/z range of 395–1205 with a mass resolution of 60,000. Data was processed and analysed for DIA‐Based proteomics using Spectronaut version 14.9 (Biognosys, CH) (Bruderer et al. 2015).

AS1411 Transwell Assay

For the in vitro BBB model, bEnd.3, or hCMEC/D3 cells were plated onto the apical side of transwell inserts (0.4 µm pores, 12 mm diameter) at 1 × 105 cells/cm2 per well alone or in coculture with astrocytes or SVG p12 cells, which were seeded on the basolateral side at 1 × 105 cells/cm2 per well. For the in vitro BTB model, the U87MG cells were seeded into the basolateral chambers of the transwell plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/cm2 per well, and bEnd.3 or hCMEC/D3 cells were seeded onto the upper inserts of the transwell plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2 per well. Medium was refreshed every 48 h, and cultures were maintained for 5 days until a continuous monolayer developed. Paracellular integrity was then verified by quantifying the flux of 70 kDa TMR‐dextran; fluorescence was read at 555/580 nm (ex/em) on a multi‐mode microplate reader (PerkinElmer Enspire 2300, USA). After confirming barrier restriction, 1 µM AS1411 or NC was introduced into the upper chamber. At designated intervals, the entire basolateral volume was collected and AS1411 or NC accumulation was determined via fluorescence (Yang et al. 2017; Petrovskaya et al. 2022; Tylawsky et al. 2023; Li et al. 2020). AS1411 sequence (Fu et al., 2024): 5′‐GGTGGTGGTGGTTGTGGTGGTGGTGG‐3′, NC sequence (Fu et al., 2024): 5′‐CCTCCTCCTCCTTCTCCTCCTCCTCC‐3′.

Western Blotting

Western blot analysis was conducted as previously described (Hong et al. 2020). For the extraction of total protein, cells were lysed using the RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, CHN), and supernatants were collected. For membrane protein, extraction was performed by using the Membrane and Cytosol Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, CHN), following the manufacturer's instructions. Then the protein levels were quantified and loaded onto an SDS‐PAGE gel. The separated proteins were then transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) via the Bio‐Rad Trans‐Blot Turbo transfer apparatus (USA). Following blocking with 5% non‐fat dry milk in TBST, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against VEGFR2 (1:1000, CST, USA), EGFR (1:1000, CST, USA), NCL (1:1000, Proteintech, CHN), TSG101 (1:500, Abclonal, CHN), CD63 (1:500, Proteintech, CHN), MDM2 (1:1000, Proteintech, CHN), Na+/K+‐ATPases (1:1000, Proteintech, CHN) and ACTIN (1:2000, Abclonal, CHN). Following this, the membrane was rinsed thrice using TBS‐T and then incubated with suitable HRP‐linked secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. Chemiluminescent detection was subsequently carried out using the enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Abclonal, CHN), and the blots were visualized using the Tanon Multi5200 chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Multi5200, CHN).

Membrane NCL Detection via Flow Cytometry

Cells were enzymatically detached and washed three times with PBS. After fixation with 10% formalin, the cells were blocked with a blocking buffer for 30 min. The cells were then incubated with 488‐conjugated NCL antibody (1:200, Proteintech, CHN) for 1 h at room temperature. After three times of washes with PBS, the cells were re‐suspended in 500 µL PBS and analysed by a BD flow cytometer (BiotreeDB, USA). Data acquired from the flow cytometer were analysed with the FlowJo software (BiotreeDB, USA).

IF Staining

For IF staining of cells, 2 × 103 cells were plated in cell culture slides. After fixation with 10% formalin in PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin. The cells were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated to a fluorescent dye (Alexa Fluor 488, 555, or 594, Abcam, USA). After counterstaining the nuclei with DAPI (Beyotime, CHN), coverslips are mounted onto microscope slides using an anti‐fade mounting medium. For IF staining of tissue sections, the sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton X‐100. Blocking was carried out with 5% BSA for an hour before incubating overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. The sections were then rinsed and incubated with either anti‐mouse or anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 488, 555, or 594 secondary antibodies (1:200, Abcam, UK) for one hour at room temperature. Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, CHN), and images were acquired using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss 980, DE).

Cellular Binding and Uptake of AS1411 In Vitro

U87MG, U251, astrocytes, or MCF 10A cells were seeded at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per well into 6‐well plates. After overnight incubation in 2 mL of DMEM with 10% FBS for adherence, the cells were incubated with Cy5‐labeled NC or AS1411. After washing three times with PBS, a flow cytometer (BD, FACSCanto SORP, USA) was used to determine the binding affinity of NC or AS1411 with the cells. Analysis was performed using the FlowJo software package. For confocal observations, cells were plated at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in cell culture slides. Following overnight incubation, the cells were treated with Cy5‐labeled NC or AS1411. The cells were washed with PBS three times and fixed with 10% formalin. After counterstaining with DAPI (Beyotime, CHN), confocal images were acquired using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss 980, DE).

3D Spheroid Tumour Model

The 3D tumour spheroids of U87MG were established as follows. Briefly, U87MG cells were plated at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in a 96‐well spheroid microplate (Corning, USA). After 4 days, the tumour spheroids were treated with Cy5‐labeled NC or AS1411. Then, tumour spheroids were washed and fixed with 10% formalin. The permeability of Cy5‐NC or Cy5‐AS1411 into tumour spheroids was investigated by a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss 980, DE).

Detection of the G‐Quadruplexes

AS1411, AS1411‐Ced, AS1411‐Gef and NC were diluted to 10 µM in cell‐cultured medium and transferred to a black 96‐well plate and then co‐incubated with 1 µM NMM (MCE, USA) for 1 h. Fluorescence spectra from 550 to 750 nm was collected using a multi‐mode microplate reader (PerkinElmer Enspire 2300, USA). Excitation was at 399 nm, and slit width was set to 1 nm for both excitation and emission.

Gene Silencing by siRNA

The siRNAs were purchased from OBiO (CHN). Cells were cultivated on 6‐well plates up to 50%–70% confluency for siRNA transfection. The siRNA at a final concentration of 160 nM was diluted in 150 µL Opti‐MEM medium (Gibco, USA). 9 µL Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was diluted in 150 µL of Opti‐MEM medium for 5 min at room temperature. The two solutions were mixed and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. After adding the siRNA‐lipid complex to cells, cells were incubated for 2 days at 37°C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. siNCL: 5’‐GGAUGACGACGACGACGAAGATT‐3’, siMDM2: 5’‐GCUUGGCCUACAGUCAUCUTT‐3’, siCtrl: 5’‐UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT‐3’.

Cell Viability Assay

Cells were plated at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in a 96‐well plate and allowed to adhere overnight. After treatment, cell viability was determined using the CCK‐8 assays. This involved washing the cells with PBS, adding 10 µL of CCK‐8 reagent to each well along with 100 µL of medium, and then incubating the plates for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multi‐mode microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA) at one‐day intervals to monitor cell viability (Liang et al. 2017).

Apoptosis Assay by Flow Cytometry

Cells were plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well into 6‐well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. After treatment, cells were enzymatically detached and washed twice with PBS to ensure purity. Subsequently, cells were stained using an Annexin V‐FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, CHN) to label apoptotic cells. Flow cytometric analysis was carried out using a BD flow cytometer (BiotreeDB, USA) to quantify the proportion of apoptotic cells. Data acquired from the flow cytometer were analysed with the FlowJo software (BiotreeDB, USA) to interpret the apoptotic profiles (Hong et al. 2020).

EdU Cell Proliferation Assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using EdU cell proliferation assays according to the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime, CHN). Cells were plated at a density of 2 × 103 cells in cell culture slides and incubated overnight for adherence. After treatment, a total of 500 µL EdU (10 µM) reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h to label the cells. After three times of washing with PBS, cells were fixed in a 10% formalin for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X‐100 (Beyotime, CHN) for another 15 min, and then incubated with the click‐reaction reagent for 30 min at room temperature in a dark environment. After counterstaining with DAPI (Beyotime, CHN), confocal images were acquired using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss 980, DE).

3D Tumour Spheroid Invasion Assay

Cells were plated at a density of 2 × 103 per well in 96‐well spheroid microplates (Corning, USA). After 4 days, 100 µL Matrigel Basement membrane matrix (Corning, USA) was added to the well. The plate was transferred to an incubator at 37°C and allowed the Matrigel Basement membrane matrix to solidify. After treatment, digital images were obtained using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss 980, DE) and quantitative analysis of the area of invasion was performed utilizing ImageJ software (Vinci et al. 2015).

TUNEL Assay

After different treatments, the cells and tissue sections were incubated with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase enzyme and Cy3‐labeled dUTP mixture from the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, CHN) for 1 h at 37°C. Finally, the nucleus was counterstained by DAPI (Beyotime, China). Images were taken with a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, LSM980, DE).

Tube Formation Assay

The tube formation assay was performed as previously described, with some modifications (Decicco‐Skinner et al. 2014). In total, 100 µL Matrigel (Corning, USA) was added to 48‐well plates and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. bEnd.3 cells were counted and diluted into a solution containing 2.5 × 105 cells/mL. Mixed cell suspension (100 µL; 25,000 cells) was seeded on the Matrigel in 24‐well plates and incubated for 12 h. bEnd.3 were stained with Calcein AM (Beyotime, CHN) and then imaged using a fluorescent inverted microscope equipped with a 485 nm excitation filter and a 520 nm emission filter. The vascular network was quantified using the ImageJ software (NIH, USA) with the angiogenesis analyser plug‐in.

Transwell Migration Assay

The Transwell migration assay was performed as previously described with some modifications (Chen et al. 2024). Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells in 100 µL of serum‐free medium into the upper chamber of a Transwell polycarbonate culture insert, 6.5 mm in diameter with an 8 µm pore size (BIOFIL, USA). These inserts were placed into 24‐well plates containing 600 µL of medium supplemented with 10% FBS in the lower chamber. The assay plates were incubated for 12 h. Post‐incubation, the Transwell inserts were carefully removed, and the upper chamber was cleared of non‐migratory cells using a cotton swab. The cells that had migrated to the underside of the membrane were fixed with 10% formalin for 20 min, stained with 0.2% crystal violet for 30 min, and subsequently visualized under a Nikon Eclipse Ts2 microscope. To quantify migratory cells, three distinct non‐overlapping fields were counted using ImageJ.

IP and Ubiquitination Assay

The IP procedure was performed as previously described (Lei et al. 2020). 5 × 104 cells were lysed in lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, USA) with a proteinase inhibitor cocktail. The lysate was centrifuged at 13,000 × g at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti‐EGFR primary antibody (1:50, CST, USA), an anti‐VEGFR2 primary antibody (1:50, CST, USA), or an anti‐NCL primary antibody (1:100, CST, USA). Subsequently, the mixture was attached to Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Scientific, USA) at room temperature for 2 h. The beads are then washed five times extensively to remove non‐specifically bound proteins by using DynaMag‐2 Magnet (Invitrogen, USA). The immunoprecipitated proteins were then prepared in loading buffer, heated at 100°C for 10 min, and subjected to SDS‐PAGE and western blot analysis using primary antibodies against ubiquitin (1:2000, CST, USA), VEGFR2 (1:1000, CST, USA), EGFR (1:1000, CST, USA), NCL (1:1000, CST, USA), MDM2 (1:1000, CST, USA) and ACTIN (1:2000, CST, USA).

Mice

Male BALB/c nude mice aged 6–8 weeks were obtained from GemPharmatech (CHN). The study conducted on these animals took place at the Experimental Animal Centre of the Southern University of Science and Technology. This facility guarantees a controlled environment free from pathogens, offers unrestricted access to food and water, maintains a consistent temperature of 22°C, and follows a 12‐h light/dark cycle. All animal handling protocols and experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) at Southern University of Science and Technology (SUSTech‐JY202507012), guaranteeing adherence to recognized animal care criteria.

Animal Models of Orthotopic GBM

Orthotopic GBM mouse models were established through stereotactical inoculation of U87MG‐GFP‐Luc cells or patient‐derived GBM‐GFP‐Luc cells (3×105 cells per mouse in 7 µL of PBS) into the brain (1.8 mm lateral and 1 mm anterior to the bregma; 3 mm of depth) of the mice that were anesthetized using 2.5% avertin (15 µL/g). After 1 week of tumour inoculation, mice were assigned to different treatment groups. The growth of the tumour was monitored by bioluminescence using an IVIS (PerkinElmer; IVIS Spectrum, USA), starting 10 min after the mice were injected with chloral hydrate (5% w/v) at a dose of 62.5 mg/kg combined with the luciferase substrate D‐luciferin potassium (15 mg/mL in PBS) at 75 mg/kg (Haddad et al., 2021).

Histological Analysis

Brain or organs were dissected and fixed overnight in 10% formalin at 4°C. For the brain, a daily sucrose gradient (15% and then 30% sucrose in PBS) was used to wash out the formalin. Fixed brains were then frozen on dry ice and cryosectioned axially into 20 µm slices (Leica, CM1950, DE) for IF analysis. Organs were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 µm for H&E staining. Images of the entire ankles were acquired using a NanoZoomer S60 Digital slide scanner (HAMAMATSU, JAN) and analysed using NDP.view2 software (HAMAMATSU, JAN).

Serum Biochemical Assays

Blood samples were collected from mice after treatment via hepatic portal vein puncture. Serum was collected via centrifugation of blood samples in 4000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. Serum biochemical parameters, including ALT, AST, ALB and ALP, were analysed by an MS‐480 Automatic Biochemistry Analyzer (Medicalsystem Biotechnology, CHN).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software. For assessing differences among multiple independent groups, one‐way ANOVA followed by a post‐hoc test was employed. Comparisons between two independent groups were made using the Student's t‐test. For examining differences among groups categorized by two factors, two‐way ANOVA with a subsequent post‐hoc test was utilized. The statistical significance of the difference for survival analysis was analysed by using the log‐rank (Mantel‐Cox) test. Data in the figures are presented as mean ± SEM, with levels of statistical significance denoted by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001. Sample sizes for in vivo studies were determined based on a prior power analysis. Mice were assigned to groups in a random and blinded manner, and any mice in poor health prior to the start of the study were excluded.
