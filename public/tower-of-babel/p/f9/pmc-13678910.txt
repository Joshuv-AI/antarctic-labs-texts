Device-derived skin moisture, oil and softness scores in infants and young children with atopic dermatitis: a prospective case-control study
Authors: Halil Alkaya, Uğur Altaş, Zeynep Meva Altaş, Ebru Oğultekin Vazgeçer, Yakup Söğütlü, Mehmet Yaşar Özkars
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, inflammatory skin disease that predominantly affects infants and children. AD is associated with impaired skin barrier function, increased transepidermal water loss and reduced skin hydration. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) has been used as a non-invasive approach for estimating skin moisture-related parameters. This study aimed to investigate skin moisture percentage and additional device-derived skin surface scores (MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS) in children with AD and to evaluate their relationship with disease severity.MethodsThis prospective case-control study was conducted from January to March 2025 at the University of Health Sciences Ümraniye Training and Research Hospital. The study included 50 children with AD and 50 control children without clinically apparent AD or visible inflammatory skin disease. All participants were aged 0–2 years, defined as children younger than 3 years. Skin moisture percentage and device-derived MOIST, OIL and SOFTNESS scores were measured using a portable bioelectrical impedance device. AD severity was assessed using the SCORing Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD) index and grouped according to commonly used total SCORAD severity thresholds. Complete blood count parameters were recorded for both groups, whereas serum total IgE, serum specific IgE levels for hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and nuts, and skin prick test results were additionally recorded in the AD group.ResultsChildren with AD had significantly lower skin moisture percentage than controls (33.15% versus 44.0%; p = 0.001). Device-derived MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS scores were also significantly lower in the AD group than in controls. These parameters were lower in children with severe AD than in those with mild-to-moderate AD. In the AD group, food allergen sensitization was detected in 20 patients (40%); however, in this exploratory subgroup analysis, device-derived skin parameters did not differ significantly between patients with and without allergen sensitization.ConclusionsChildren with AD had lower device-derived skin moisture percentage and skin surface scores than controls without clinically apparent AD or visible inflammatory skin disease. These parameters were also lower in severe AD. Device-derived skin surface measurements may provide supportive, non-invasive information on skin surface changes in children with AD; however, further validation studies are needed before routine clinical use.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Skin moisture, Bioelectrical impedance analysis, Skin surface measurement, Pediatric dermatology, Infants, Children, Eczema, Pediatric allergy

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is one of the most common inflammatory, recurrent, and chronic skin diseases [1, 2]. Atopic dermatitis results from a complex interaction of genetic, immunological, and environmental factors and is characterized by chronic or recurrent pruritic inflammatory skin lesions that may significantly impair quality of life, particularly in infants and children [2]. Population-based data indicate that AD affects approximately 14% of adults in Sweden, while childhood studies suggest that AD affects over 20% of children in many developed or affluent country settings [3, 4]. AD is often considered the first step in the “atopic march,” a process in which early-life skin inflammation may precede the later development of allergic rhinitis and asthma [5]. Moisturizers, topical corticosteroids, and topical calcineurin inhibitors are commonly used in the treatment of AD [6]. The skin serves as a barrier against microorganisms, allergens, and environmental irritants [7]. The epidermal barrier, particularly the stratum corneum, limits the penetration of microorganisms and allergens while reducing transepidermal water loss [8]. Filaggrin and its degradation products are critical for epidermal barrier integrity and skin hydration [9].

In AD, loss-of-function mutations in the filaggrin gene and reduced filaggrin expression may contribute to impaired epidermal barrier function. According to the epithelial barrier hypothesis, disruption of epithelial barriers may promote type 2 inflammation and contribute to allergic diseases such as AD, asthma, AR, CRS, eosinophilic esophagitis, and food allergy [10, 14]. The skin barrier is influenced by biophysical parameters such as epidermal hydration, sebum/lipid content, transepidermal water loss, and skin pH [11]. These epidermal barrier-related parameters may vary according to age, sex, race/ethnicity, sweating, anatomic site, skin temperature, and environmental temperature and humidity [12, 13]. The protective barrier is impaired in AD [14]. Environmental allergens such as house dust mite may aggravate AD severity in children and may act as nonspecific irritants as well as allergens in AD skin lesions [15]. Although patients with asthma and AR may not exhibit skin symptoms, they may show altered skin barrier-related biophysical parameters, possibly reflecting shared pathophysiological mechanisms with AD [16]. Sebum and moisture levels in the skin are essential for maintaining healthy skin [17]. In infants with AD, average skin moisture and sebum percentages have been reported to be decreased, even in unaffected areas [18]. One study found that the sebum lipidome differs in patients with AD and that lipid metabolism-related abnormalities may involve sebaceous gland-related pathways [19].

Dryness of the skin in AD is both a symptom and a significant contributor to disease progression. Assessment of skin moisture in children with AD may help characterize skin surface changes and better understand the relationship between disease severity and moisture balance. However, evaluating moisture levels during childhood poses challenges due to the delicate structure of pediatric skin. In recent years, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) has been used as a non-invasive approach for estimating hydration-related parameters. Established skin bioengineering instruments, such as the Corneometer for stratum corneum hydration and the Sebumeter for skin-surface lipids, have long been used for non-invasive assessment of skin surface properties. The portable impedance-based analyzer used in the present study is conceptually similar to these instruments in that it provides non-invasive skin-surface measurements; however, its outputs are device-specific and should not be considered directly interchangeable with Corneometer or Sebumeter values [17, 20]. Bioelectrical impedance–based measurements are device-specific and may be influenced by electrode design and placement, anatomical site, calibration requirements, body temperature, movement, and environmental/testing conditions [20].

Therefore, the portable LCD Digital Skin Moisture and Oil Analyzer used in the present study was considered a supportive tool for obtaining device-derived skin moisture, oil, and softness measurements, rather than a validated diagnostic instrument. Non-invasive assessment of skin moisture and sebum levels may complement clinical evaluation by providing objective information on skin surface hydration and lipid-related changes in children with atopic dermatitis [17, 18]. This study aimed to investigate skin moisture percentage and device-derived MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS scores in children with AD using a portable bioelectrical impedance–based device and to evaluate their relationship with disease severity.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective case-control study was conducted between January 1, 2025, and March 1, 2025, at the Pediatric Allergy and Immunology outpatient clinic of Ümraniye Training and Research Hospital, University of Health Sciences, Istanbul, Türkiye. It was designed as a case-control study with prospective data collection and analysis. The study included 50 children diagnosed with atopic dermatitis (AD) and 50 control children without clinically apparent AD or visible inflammatory skin disease.

Eligible participants were children aged 0–2 years. In accordance with the ethics-approved protocol, the term “0–2 years” was interpreted as children who had not yet reached their third birthday. Accordingly, all included participants were younger than 3 years at enrollment.

AD was diagnosed by a pediatric allergy specialist according to Hanifin-Rajka criteria. AD severity was assessed using the SCORing Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD) index. For severity-group analyses, patients were categorized as having mild AD (total SCORAD < 25), moderate AD (total SCORAD 25–50), or severe AD (total SCORAD > 50) according to commonly used total SCORAD severity thresholds. For group comparisons, mild and moderate AD were combined and compared with severe AD.

Children were excluded if they had infectious skin disease, acute or chronic systemic disease, immunodeficiency, or another dermatological condition that could affect the skin barrier, including seborrheic dermatitis, dermatitis herpetiformis, contact dermatitis, nummular dermatitis, scabies, molluscum contagiosum, tinea corporis, tinea capitis, mycosis fungoides, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, or psoriasis. Children were also excluded if they had bathed within 24 h before assessment or had used topical steroids, moisturizers, sunscreens, creams, baby oils, soaps, shampoos, detergents, or other topical products on the measurement area within 24 h before assessment.

Control group definition

The control group consisted of children aged 0–2 years who attended the general pediatric outpatient clinic for routine evaluation or non-dermatological complaints and had no physician-diagnosed AD, no visible eczematous or inflammatory skin lesions on physical examination, no known chronic dermatological disease, no known chronic systemic disease, and no history of topical steroid or moisturizer use within 24 h before measurement. Control status was determined based on parental history, review of available medical records, and physical examination at enrollment.

Total SCORAD scoring, serum total IgE, serum specific IgE testing, and skin prick testing were not routinely performed in the control group because these children did not have clinically apparent AD or visible inflammatory skin disease. Therefore, the control group was defined as children without clinically apparent AD or visible inflammatory skin disease rather than as a confirmed non-atopic control group.

Laboratory assessments

Age, sex, sociodemographic data, complete blood count parameters, and device-derived skin measurements were recorded for both groups. In children with AD, total SCORAD scores, serum total IgE, serum specific IgE levels for hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and nuts, and skin prick test results were additionally recorded.

Complete blood count parameters were obtained from hospital laboratory records and analyzed in the hospital central laboratory using an automated hematology analyzer. Serum total IgE and serum specific IgE levels for hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and nuts were measured using the ImmunoCAP system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Uppsala, Sweden). Serum specific IgE values of ≥ 0.35 kUA/L were considered positive.

Skin prick testing in the AD group was performed to evaluate concomitant food allergen sensitization and not to establish the diagnosis of AD. Skin prick tests were performed using commercial allergen extracts used in routine clinical practice, including hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and nut allergen extracts, together with histamine as the positive control and saline as the negative control. The manufacturer details of the skin prick test extracts were not available in the extracted study records. These allergens were selected because food sensitization is commonly assessed in infants and young children with AD in pediatric allergy practice.

Before skin prick testing, recent antihistamine use was assessed by history. When recent antihistamine exposure that could interfere with the wheal response was reported, testing was postponed according to routine clinical practice. Skin prick tests were interpreted only when the histamine positive control response was adequate and the saline negative control was appropriate. Results were considered positive when the wheal diameter was ≥ 3 mm greater than that of the negative control in the absence of dermographism.

Food allergen sensitization was defined as positivity in serum specific IgE and/or skin prick testing for hen’s egg, cow’s milk, or nuts. No additional blood sampling was performed solely for research purposes; laboratory parameters were extracted from available hospital laboratory records.

Device-derived skin surface measurements

Before assessment, children rested for approximately 10–20 min to minimize the effect of recent physical activity on skin-surface measurements. It was confirmed that no moisturizers, lotions, creams, topical steroids, sunscreens, baby oils, soaps, shampoos, detergents, or other topical products had been applied to the test area within the previous 24 h. Parents were instructed in advance to clean the designated skin area using only water on the day of measurement. Measurements were performed under standard outpatient clinic conditions, with a relative humidity ranging between 20% and 40%; however, ambient temperature was not formally recorded. After each assessment, the probe was cleaned with a soft alcohol wipe and allowed to dry before reuse.

Skin moisture percentage and device-derived skin surface scores were measured non-invasively using a pen-shaped, handheld LCD Digital Skin Moisture and Oil Analyzer (Reyoung-Beauty, Guangdong, China). The device is lightweight and portable, with dimensions of 7.85 × 3.5 × 2.2 cm and a weight of 27 g. It was applied directly to bare skin and generated device-derived outputs using a bioelectrical impedance–based sensor.

Measurements were performed in the antecubital region of the non-dominant upper extremity because this site provides ease of access in infants and young children, minimizes contamination risk from saliva or feeding residues, which may be encountered on facial sites such as the cheeks, and improves measurement reproducibility.

The device provided 1%-based skin hydration output, recorded as MOISTURE (%), and three device-displayed semi-quantitative numerical scores ranging from 1 to 5: MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS. Because these device-displayed scores do not represent independently validated quantitative hydration, sebum, or softness measurements, they were analyzed as device-derived skin surface scores.

SOFTNESS was recorded as an ancillary device-displayed output generated during the same non-invasive measurement procedure; no additional intervention, measurement site, or participant burden was introduced for this variable.

The analyzer was used as a supportive, device-specific tool and not as a validated diagnostic instrument for pediatric AD. User-performed calibration was not available, and formal calibration against reference instruments such as the Corneometer or Sebumeter was not performed.

The skin area was visually inspected before measurement. Measurements were avoided on exudative, crusted, infected, or open lesions when such lesions were present; however, the presence and extent of active eczematous involvement exactly at the measurement site were not scored separately from the global total SCORAD assessment. The child’s arm was gently extended to expose the antecubital fossa and minimize skin-to-skin occlusion at this flexural site. The probe was placed perpendicular to the skin surface with gentle contact and without excessive pressure. A single measurement was obtained from the site over a few seconds and recorded for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows version 25.0. Data distribution was assessed visually and using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov/Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with minimum and maximum values, whereas categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare non-normally distributed continuous variables between two independent groups. The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The comparison of AD patients with and without food allergen sensitization was considered exploratory. No separate a priori sample size calculation was performed for this subgroup comparison.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ümraniye Training and Research Hospital under decision number 420, dated December 12, 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children before enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Data from 100 children, including 50 patients with atopic dermatitis and 50 controls, were analyzed. Age and sex distributions were statistically similar between the AD and control groups. The median age was 10 months (range: 1.0–26.0) in children with AD and 8.5 months (range: 2.0–28.0) in controls (p = 0.154). All participants were younger than 3 years and therefore met the predefined 0–2-year age criterion. Twenty-eight children in the AD group and 29 children in the control group were male (p = 0.840). The median serum total IgE value in children with AD was 13.5 IU/mL (range: 0–515.0). No statistically significant differences were observed between the AD and control groups in WBC, neutrophil, basophil, eosinophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte values (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The comparison between AD patients with and without allergen sensitization was considered exploratory because the study was primarily designed to compare children with AD and controls and to evaluate skin parameters according to AD severity. No separate a priori power calculation was performed for this subgroup analysis. Because the sensitized subgroup included only 20 children, the analysis may not have had sufficient statistical power to detect small or moderate differences in device-derived skin parameters. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of no association.

Discussion

Data from 100 children were analyzed, including 50 patients diagnosed with atopic dermatitis (AD) and 50 controls without clinically apparent AD or visible inflammatory skin disease.

The AD and control groups were statistically similar in terms of age and sex. Children with AD had significantly lower skin moisture percentage and device-derived MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS scores than controls. In addition, lower skin moisture percentage and device-derived MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS scores were observed in patients with severe AD compared with those with mild-to-moderate AD. However, no statistically significant difference in these device-derived skin parameters was observed between AD patients with and without food allergen sensitization.

In our study, the median serum total IgE value of children with AD was 13.5 IU/mL (0–515.0). This value appears lower than that reported in some pediatric AD cohorts. Günaydın et al. reported higher serum total IgE levels in patients with moderate/severe AD compared with those with mild AD, and in AD patients with food allergy compared with those without food allergy [21]. In contrast, the relatively low median total IgE level in our cohort suggests that serum total IgE was not uniformly elevated among our patients with AD. This discrepancy may be related to differences in age distribution, disease severity, food allergy frequency, environmental allergen exposure, regional sensitization patterns, and laboratory methodology. The interpretation of serum total IgE in infancy and early childhood is challenging because IgE levels are age-dependent and may remain low even in clinically allergic children. Orgel et al. suggested that serum IgE levels above 100 IU/mL at any age, or above 20 IU/mL in infants, may strongly suggest the possibility of atopic disease; however, low serum IgE levels do not exclude atopic disease [22]. Therefore, serum total IgE findings in the present study should be interpreted as supportive laboratory data rather than diagnostic markers or direct indicators of disease severity.

Similarly, the median eosinophil count in our AD group was 430 cells/mm³ (30–1440), whereas Jenerowicz et al. reported a mean peripheral blood eosinophil count of 290.0 ± 205.7 cells/mm³ in patients with AD [23]. Although the eosinophil count in our cohort appears numerically higher, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously because our data are expressed as median and range, whereas Jenerowicz et al. reported mean ± standard deviation. In addition, eosinophil counts may vary according to age, disease activity, concomitant atopic diseases, sensitization profile, infections, treatment status, and methodological differences between studies. Taken together, the relatively low median total IgE level and variable eosinophil counts in our cohort support the heterogeneous nature of pediatric AD and indicate that laboratory parameters should be considered complementary to clinical evaluation rather than diagnostic determinants.

In the present study, the median skin moisture percentage in AD patients was 33.15% (10.0–60.0), whereas it was 44.0% (25.0–60.0) in the control group. This decrease in skin moisture percentage in AD patients was statistically significant (p = 0.001). In addition, device-derived MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS scores were significantly lower in AD patients compared with the control group. The lower MOIST and OIL scores are consistent with previous pediatric AD data showing reduced skin moisture and sebum levels, even in clinically unaffected areas [18]. These device-derived findings are also biologically plausible in the context of AD pathophysiology, in which epidermal barrier dysfunction, altered stratum corneum hydration, lipid/sebum-related abnormalities, and increased transepidermal water loss as a marker of barrier impairment contribute to skin dryness and inflammation [11, 18, 19]. However, because SOFTNESS is a device-specific output and TEWL was not directly measured in the present study, these findings should be interpreted as supportive skin-surface biophysical alterations rather than direct or standardized measurements of epidermal barrier function.

The present study should be interpreted in relation to, but distinct from, the previous study by Güneş et al. [18], which evaluated skin moisture and sebum content in infants with AD and healthy controls. In that study, measurements were obtained from multiple anatomical sites, and the authors reported lower average moisture and sebum percentages in AD, but no significant correlation between mean skin moisture or sebum percentage and SCORAD. In contrast, the present study focused on a standardized antecubital fossa measurement site and evaluated moisture percentage together with device-derived MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS scores. Moreover, we specifically compared device-derived skin surface parameters according to total SCORAD severity groups and food allergen sensitization status. Therefore, the present study adds to previous findings by providing a standardized-site, severity-focused assessment of device-derived skin surface parameters and by further evaluating these parameters according to food allergen sensitization status in young children with AD.

A previous clinic-based study evaluated skin moisture and sebum levels in children with allergic diseases, including asthma, allergic rhinitis, and AD, and examined the relationship of these parameters with house dust mite sensitization [16]. In that study, children aged 0–18 years were included, and skin moisture and sebum percentages were measured non-invasively using a portable digital skin analyzer. The present study differs from that previous work in several important aspects. First, the current cohort was restricted to children aged 0–2 years with AD and an age-similar control group without clinically apparent AD or active inflammatory skin disease. Second, the present study focused specifically on AD severity using total SCORAD categories. Third, in addition to moisture and oil-related parameters, device-derived softness was also evaluated. Therefore, the previous clinic-based study provides methodological and local contextual support for the feasibility of non-invasive skin surface measurements, but it should not be considered directly equivalent to the present AD-specific case-control analysis.

The lower device-derived OIL scores observed in children with AD may indicate altered skin surface lipid-related properties; however, these scores should not be interpreted as direct quantitative measurements of sebum production.

According to commonly used total SCORAD severity thresholds, 38 patients were classified as having mild-to-moderate AD and 12 as having severe AD. Device-derived skin moisture percentage and MOIST, OIL, and SOFTNESS scores were significantly lower in patients with severe AD. Greater AD severity may be associated with more pronounced skin-surface alterations, although the present measurements do not directly quantify epidermal barrier dysfunction. These findings support the relevance of barrier-related skin surface changes in AD severity, but they do not establish whether barrier-directed interventions would modify these device-derived parameters.

Previous studies have evaluated skin moisture and sebum content in children with AD using different instruments and study designs [6, 18]. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted more specifically: data remain limited regarding the combined evaluation of device-derived moisture, oil, and softness values using a portable LCD bioelectrical impedance–based analyzer in infants and young children with AD. Future studies with larger sample sizes and multicenter designs may help to better clarify the relationship between AD severity and device-derived skin surface parameters.

Among the AD patients in the present study, 20 (40%) had food allergen sensitization. Of these, 5 (10%) had multiple food allergen sensitization. Sensitization to hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and nuts was detected in 17, 4, and 4 patients, respectively. Günaydın et al. reported multiple food allergy in 13 patients (10%) with AD; however, this finding was based on clinical food allergy diagnosis, whereas the present study evaluated food allergen sensitization. Therefore, the similar proportion should be interpreted as numerically comparable but not directly equivalent [21]. In the study by Vezir et al., food sensitization was detected in 43 patients (41.3%), and egg sensitization was the most common finding, reported in 37 patients (35.6%). In the subgroup with persistent AD lesions, egg, milk, and nut sensitization were observed in 7 (50%), 2 (14.3%), and 1 (7.1%) patients, respectively [24]. These findings suggest that food allergen sensitization may be detected in a subset of children with AD and that egg is frequently among the leading sensitizing foods. However, because oral food challenge was not systematically performed in the present study, these results should be interpreted as sensitization rather than confirmed food allergy.

Differences in sensitization patterns between studies may be related to patient age, disease severity, persistence of AD lesions, geographic factors, dietary habits, allergen exposure, and diagnostic methodology. These observations indicate the need for larger studies evaluating food allergen sensitization profiles together with clinically confirmed food allergy outcomes in young children with AD.

Additionally, further studies are needed to assess the impact of food allergen sensitization on AD severity, duration, and treatment response. Such an approach could not only improve our understanding of AD but also contribute to the development of personalized treatment strategies for patients.

The relationship between allergen sensitization and skin measurements in AD patients was examined. While the median skin moisture percentage was lower in patients with allergen sensitization, no statistically significant difference was observed (p = 0.297). Similarly, no significant difference was found in device-derived MOIST, OIL, or SOFTNESS scores between patients with and without food allergen sensitization. To our knowledge, evidence is limited regarding the relationship between food allergen sensitization and the combined device-derived assessment of skin moisture, oil, and softness in infants and young children with AD. Further investigation is needed to better understand the effects of allergen sensitization on skin parameters in AD patients.

Several methodological and clinical factors may explain the lack of a statistically significant association between food allergen sensitization and device-derived skin parameters. First, this subgroup analysis was exploratory and the study was not primarily powered to detect differences according to sensitization status. Second, sensitization based on serum specific IgE or skin prick testing does not necessarily indicate clinically active food allergy or ongoing allergen-driven skin inflammation, because oral food challenge remains the reference standard for confirming clinical food allergy. Third, AD severity, local inflammation, skin care practices, anatomical measurement site, and environmental factors may influence device-derived skin surface values independently of sensitization status. Fourth, because measurements were obtained from a single anatomical site and established objective barrier assessments such as TEWL, Corneometer-based hydration, skin pH, filaggrin expression, or ceramide profiling were not performed, subtle barrier differences related to sensitization status may not have been captured.

Poorly controlled AD is associated with a substantial disease burden in children, including cutaneous symptoms, sleep disturbance, psychosocial difficulties, and impaired health-related quality of life [25–27]. Clinical manifestations such as pruritus and sleep loss may contribute to school absenteeism or impaired school functioning and may negatively affect children’s quality of life [26, 27]. Preserving the skin’s protective barrier function is crucial for limiting the entry of microorganisms, allergens, and irritants and for maintaining epidermal homeostasis [28, 29]. In children with house dust mite sensitization, regular use of moisturizers has been suggested as a supportive approach to protect the skin barrier and reduce skin symptoms [29]. The prevalence of AD has increased substantially in industrialized countries, making it an important public health issue [30].

The clinical relevance of the present findings lies in the potential supportive role of non-invasive skin surface measurements in characterizing AD-related skin changes. The lower device-derived moisture, oil, and softness values observed in children with AD, particularly in severe disease, suggest that these measurements may reflect skin surface alterations associated with AD severity. However, the lack of objective evaluations of the skin barrier in the present study should be interpreted in light of the existing literature. Established assessments such as transepidermal water loss measurement, Corneometer-based stratum corneum hydration, skin pH assessment, filaggrin-related markers, and lipid or ceramide profiling provide more direct information about epidermal barrier function. Since these methods were not used in the present study, our results should be interpreted as device-specific supportive skin surface measurements rather than definitive evidence of barrier dysfunction.

This study has several limitations. First, SCORAD scoring, serum total IgE, food-specific serum IgE testing, and skin prick testing were not routinely performed in the control group; therefore, asymptomatic allergen sensitization could not be completely excluded among controls. Second, the portable LCD Digital Skin Moisture and Oil Analyzer was not independently validated or formally calibrated against reference instruments such as the Corneometer or Sebumeter, and its outputs should therefore be interpreted as device-specific supportive measurements rather than standardized hydration or sebum biomarkers. Third, only a single device measurement was obtained at each assessment, and ambient temperature was not formally recorded; both factors may have increased measurement variability. Fourth, the presence and extent of active eczematous involvement exactly at the measurement site were not scored separately from the global total SCORAD assessment. Fifth, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, particularly for subgroup analyses according to allergen sensitization status. Sixth, an additional age-matched subgroup comparison between children with mild-to-moderate AD and controls was not performed because the control group was selected to be age-similar to the overall AD cohort rather than individually matched to each SCORAD severity subgroup. Therefore, severity-based findings should be interpreted as exploratory. In addition, because allergen sensitization status was evaluated as an exploratory subgroup variable and no predefined effect size was available, a reliable sample size requirement for this comparison could not be estimated within the scope of the present study.
