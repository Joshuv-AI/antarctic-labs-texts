Does having bad epilepsy make you a good surgical candidate? A number needed to treat analysis
Authors: Sallie Baxendale, Dominic Heaney, Matthew C Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy surgery is an established treatment for drug‐resistant epilepsy, offering a realistic prospect of seizure freedom in well‐selected candidates. However, surgery is sometimes offered to individuals with a low probability of seizure freedom when it is framed as potentially “life‐saving” on the basis of the elevated mortality associated with uncontrolled epilepsy. We examined this rationale using absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat (NNT) analyses derived from published mortality rates (deaths per 1000 person‐years) in unoperated drug‐resistant epilepsy, postoperative seizure‐free patients, and postoperative patients who were not seizure‐free. Weighted averages were used to estimate ARR and NNT for all‐cause and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) mortality across varying probabilities of seizure freedom and postoperative life expectancy. Compared with unoperated patients, mortality reductions were greatest in those who became seizure‐free after surgery but remained modest in patients who did not. At a 50% probability of seizure freedom, the NNT to prevent one all‐cause death was 146 person‐years and to prevent one SUDEP was 243 person‐years; with higher seizure‐freedom probabilities (80%), the NNT improved to 124 and 225 person‐years, respectively; at 20%, it worsened to 178 and 264 person‐years. Lifetime modeling demonstrated that NNT decreases sharply with increasing life expectancy, reaching 3.5 and 4.7 at 60 years postoperative life expectancy for all‐cause and SUDEP mortality, respectively. These findings highlight the complexity of invoking a “life‐saving” rationale for surgery in poor candidates. Patients with the highest mortality risk, those with frequent generalized seizures, are also those least likely to achieve postoperative seizure freedom and therefore least likely to realize a mortality benefit. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy surgery is a proven, effective treatment for a carefully selected subgroup of people with medically intractable epilepsy. 1 In the epilepsy surgery population as a whole, over 60% of epilepsy surgery candidates achieve ILAE Class 1 (completely seizure‐free) or Class 2 (only auras) outcomes 2 years after surgery. 2 In some subgroups, seizure‐free rates (ILAE Classes 1 and 2) are even higher. For example, over 70% of patients with drug‐resistant, mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) due to mesial temporal sclerosis (MTS) achieve seizure freedom 2 years after an anterior temporal lobectomy. 3 Factors associated with poorer outcomes are often biomarkers for more diffuse brain dysfunction, be they neuropathological, electrophysiological, or neuropsychological (see Table 1).

Length of follow‐up is also an important factor in the seizure freedom rates reported in outcome studies. Ten years after surgery, seizures are present in approximately 50% of even “good” surgical candidates. 2 Sustained seizure freedom in the long term is relatively unusual in MRI negative patients, with just 20% reported to be seizure‐free at 10‐year follow‐up 4 and very rarely achieved in those with vascular pathologies. 4 Nevertheless, enthusiasm for epilepsy surgery is growing, and it is recognized that many strong candidates could benefit but do not yet have access to the treatment. 5

While the clinical and neuroradiological characteristics associated with good surgical outcomes are well known, surgery is still offered to people with low odds of seizure freedom, even in the short term. Sometimes these odds can be as low as 20%–30%. 6 The grounds for offering a surgical option for these patients can be justified on the basis of anticipated improvements in quality of life, reductions in polytherapy, and medication side effects and greater independence. However, the rationale for proceeding can also include the end of the road justification ‐when patients have exhausted all other treatment options and offering even a small chance of seizure freedom or reduction in seizure frequency is thought to be worth trying. This is often combined with a conviction that we must do something, or else they will die—where the severity of someone's epilepsy is such that it is deemed to be life threatening. This framing can significantly tip the balance in favor of surgery when multidisciplinary teams weigh up the potential risks/harms/benefits of the surgical option. If epilepsy surgery is offered to some candidates on the basis of it being ‘life saving’, the treatment shifts from being an elective procedure into one more akin to the high‐risk procedures offered in neuro‐oncology.

Epilepsy‐related deaths

Three measures of mortality are typically reported in the epilepsy surgery literature: all‐cause mortality, epilepsy‐related deaths, and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). These distinctions are important since the differences in deaths per 1000 patient‐years (/1000 PY) are significant. In a population study of 34 647 people with drug‐resistant epilepsy, Shankar et al. reported all‐cause mortality rates three to four times higher than epilepsy‐related deaths (all cause deaths: 3.4–4.4/1000 PY vs. epilepsy‐related deaths: 1.0/1000 PY). 7

SUDEP accounts for around 40% of epilepsy‐related deaths 8 with poor seizure control, high seizure frequency, generalized tonic–clonic seizures, high numbers of antiseizure medications, and a long duration of epilepsy identified as significant risk factors. 8 , 9 Other epilepsy‐related causes of death include status epilepticus and injuries sustained in seizures, most commonly falls, drowning, and suffocation. Attributing suicide to a single cause is discouraged since suicide is a complex phenomenon influenced by multiple factors. Nevertheless, individuals with epilepsy face a suicide risk approximately 2.6–5 times higher than the general population. 10 A recent systematic review of suicide following epilepsy surgery reported completed suicide incidence ranging from 0% to 3% over follow‐up periods of 1–4 years following surgery. 11 Those who have undergone temporal lobe surgery are most at risk even if seizures are well‐controlled postoperatively. 12

Given the legitimate clinical concerns about epilepsy‐related deaths highlighted by these studies, this paper critically examines the we must do something, or else they will die rationale for offering surgery to surgical candidates with a low chance of seizure freedom, via the lens of absolute risk reductions (ARRs) and a number needed to treat (NNT) analysis.

Rheims et al. 8 conducted a meta‐analysis of studies reporting mortality in drug‐resistant epilepsy, with a particular focus on the impacts of epilepsy surgery. They noted the significant methodological limitations of many of the studies in this field and recommended caution when comparing reported standardized mortality rates in different epilepsy cohorts, noting that these figures are highly dependent on the demographic characteristics of the population studied. Given the significant heterogeneity of methodologies with respect to sample size, duration of follow‐up, and patient characteristics, the authors emphasized the importance of examining follow‐up data in terms of patient years in any comparative studies.

Three studies have reported mortality/1000 PY, disaggregated by all‐cause deaths and SUDEP, in patients evaluated for epilepsy surgery but who did not proceed. 13 , 14 , 15 The weighted averages from these studies, based on 5096 person‐years, were used as the baseline values against which the ARR of surgery were calculated (see Tables 2 and 3). DISCUSSION

Examining the impact of surgery on mortality rates at a group level indicates a clear benefit of epilepsy surgery. All‐cause mortality and SUDEP rates are significantly lower in surgical samples compared to unoperated drug‐resistant patients, particularly if surgery results in seizure freedom, where all‐cause and SUDEP mortality rates are reduced by around 80%.

However, the clinical picture is more nuanced when examining the numbers needed to treat in order to achieve this benefit. As a group, for patients with a 50% or lower chance of seizure freedom, one all‐cause death is prevented by treating patients for a total of 161 person‐years (e.g., 161 patients for 1 year or equivalent), and one SUDEP is prevented by treating patients for a total of 253 person‐years. For a 5‐year treatment period, the NNT for patients not achieving seizure freedom is approximately 207 for all‐cause mortality and 280 for SUDEP, based on ARR of 4.83 and 3.57 per 1000 person‐years, respectively. Although average mortality rates are lower following surgery—even among patients who are not seizure‐free—the ARR remains small because SUDEP and other epilepsy‐related deaths are rare events. Consequently, most individual patients will not personally benefit from an epilepsy‐related death prevented by surgery, even though the overall population‐level mortality rate is improved.

While the majority of patients who undergo surgery will not personally benefit from a reduction in all cause mortality or SUDEP, the NNT is relatively low in children and young people and a ‘life‐saving’ rationale may be appropriate to consider in some of these cases. The numbers needed to treat to prevent one all cause death and one SUDEP are just 3.5 and 4.7, respectively, for people who survive 60 years following surgery. However, the NNT rises significantly for older adults. These analyses add to the growing literature that supports early surgical intervention whenever possible. 5

In both children and adults any potential benefits of surgery on mortality rates must be carefully weighed against the risks and complications of epilepsy surgery itself. Hader et al. report 16 perioperative mortality in 0.4% of temporal lobe surgeries, and 1.2% in extra‐temporal operations. In addition to perioperative mortality, the risks of other major and minor complications of epilepsy surgery should also be considered. Harder et al. report major neurological complications in 4.7% of epilepsy surgery candidates.

Prospective patients must also consider the likely cognitive costs of undergoing epilepsy surgery. 17 For many, the postoperative cognitive deficits conferred by surgery are not true complications, since they are anticipated and are regarded as an expected outcome of the procedure. 17 The nature and extent of postoperative changes in cognitive function are dependent on the complex interaction of a number of protective and risk factors that are different for every patient, 18 but patients who are not seizure‐free are more likely to experience long‐term impairments in memory function compared to those who are seizure‐free. 19

The ethical and neuropsychological implications of offering surgery to poor candidates also warrant careful consideration. Surgery in these individuals not only carries the usual perioperative risks but also introduces an irreversible surgical insult to neural networks that may already be compromised by years of uncontrolled epilepsy. When seizure freedom is not achieved, this combination of ongoing epileptic activity and surgical disruption can result in cumulative neurocognitive burden. Long‐term follow‐up studies have shown that persistent postoperative seizures are associated with progressive deterioration in episodic memory, language fluency and executive functioning, particularly following temporal lobe resections. 20 These patients must therefore contend with both the direct effects of the surgery and the continued effects of recurrent seizures and all that an ongoing diagnosis of epilepsy confers following surgery. 21 , 22 , 23

Over the lifespan, this dual burden may accelerate trajectories of cognitive aging, leading to earlier onset of clinically significant memory impairment compared with surgical patients who achieve full seizure control. 20 Structural imaging studies demonstrate progressive hippocampal and cortical atrophy in individuals with persistent seizures, mirroring some of the accelerated brain aging observed in chronic epilepsy more generally. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Thus, even modest gains in life expectancy or SUDEP reduction must be weighed against the risk that these patients may live longer with worsening cognitive and functional decline.

In this context, patients may survive their epilepsy but at the cost of reduced cognitive reserve and quality of life. The “double loser” concept therefore extends beyond immediate outcomes to encompass accelerated neurocognitive deterioration across the lifespan, a reality that underscores the need for more nuanced preoperative counseling, lifelong cognitive monitoring, and pause for thought when the likelihood of seizure freedom is low.

It is those with frequent generalized tonic clonic seizures (GTCS) for whom the we must do something or they will die rationale for surgery will be most often invoked. Unfortunately these are the patients who are least likely to be seizure‐free after surgery. 28 Moreover, data from Sperling et al. 29 indicate that postoperative patients with more than 2 GTCS per year experienced an all‐cause mortality rate of approximately 15/1000 person‐years—higher than the 11.3/1000 person‐years observed in unoperated drug‐resistant epilepsy. This finding highlights that in a subset of patients who continue to have frequent GTCS, the anticipated survival benefit may be lost or even reversed. This observation reinforces the need for careful candidate selection and transparent communication when surgery is framed as potentially “life‐saving.”

LIMITATIONS

The numbers in this analysis are based on a simple dichotomy of seizure‐free vs. not seizure‐free. The weighted mortality rates do not account for the type of ongoing seizures and the increased risks of GTCS on mortality in both pre‐ and postoperative patients. 29 , 30 In reality postoperative seizure patterns exist along a continuum. The presence or absence of generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS) is particularly critical when considering mortality and SUDEP risk. Recent work by Alcalá‐Zermeno et al. 31 demonstrated that resective epilepsy surgery can substantially reduce the frequency of GTCS even in patients who are not completely seizure‐free. In their cohort, 58% of patients with preoperative GTCS became entirely free of these seizures postoperatively, and a further 33% experienced at least an 80% reduction in GTCS frequency. De novo GTCS emerged in only 6% of patients and were typically rare, underscoring that the postoperative landscape is rarely one of simple success or failure. Because GTCS are the seizure type most strongly associated with SUDEP and all‐cause mortality, this selective reduction in convulsive seizures is likely the key mediator of the survival benefit captured in our NNT analyses, even among those who continue to experience focal events.

We derived weighted averages from multiple studies to minimize bias from individual centers. However, the wide variation in reported mortality rates underscores the substantial heterogeneity within the literature, as also noted by Rheims et al. in their meta‐analysis. This variation likely reflects differences in background population mortality, surgical practices, methodological approaches, and the complexity of the case load across centers. Despite the use of weighted averages, this heterogeneity increases uncertainty around these values, which must be acknowledged when interpreting their clinical significance. Consequently, as the underlying studies differed in methodology, follow‐up duration, and patient characteristics, our ARR and NNT figures should be regarded as approximate indicators of effect rather than precise predictive estimates.

Further work is underway to confirm these relationships in a large sample from a single center. This will allow a more detailed analysis of epilepsy‐related deaths and suicide in addition to all‐cause and SUDEP mortality in epilepsy surgery patients, controlling for some of the methodological variations that limit the conclusions that can be drawn from data from multiple centers. These analyses will also allow a more detailed examination of the postoperative seizure patterns that may increase postoperative mortality rates in addition to an exploration of the impacts of different types of surgery.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

This analysis does not call into question the value of surgery for optimal candidates. The focus of this paper is on poor candidates where the “life‐saving” rationale is often invoked in surgical discussions. This NNT analyses has important ethical and clinical consequences. The frequently cited justification—we must do something, or else they will die—needs to be set into the context of a numbers needed to treat analyses and the likely neurological cost associated with the odds of a reduction in mortality risk.

Offering surgery to individuals with a low probability of seizure freedom has the potential to create “double losers”: patients who endure the risks and harms of surgery without deriving its primary benefit. 32 The possibility of becoming a triple‐loser may be invoked for some, whereby surgery results in an increased risk of mortality in those who continue to experience more than two generalized tonic clonic seizures a year. Unfortunately, the very patients for whom the life‐saving rationale for surgery is most often invoked—those with frequent generalized seizures and at the highest risk of epilepsy‐related death—are also the least likely to achieve seizure freedom, the least likely to benefit from a reduction in all‐cause mortality, and the most likely to face an increase in epilepsy‐related mortality after surgery.

Cognitive biases are pervasive in clinical decision‐making, particularly in emotionally charged contexts such as epilepsy surgery for patients with severe, treatment‐resistant seizures. Action bias—the tendency to favor intervention over inaction—can make surgical teams more inclined to “do something” even when the evidence for benefit is weak. Optimism bias may lead clinicians to overestimate the likelihood of seizure freedom, particularly when desperate circumstances amplify hope. Availability bias makes dramatic success stories, or tragic cases of patients dying following rejection of surgery, disproportionately salient in memory, further reinforcing a narrative of surgery as a moral imperative rather than an elective procedure. These biases, although deeply human, can subtly distort multidisciplinary deliberations and shift discussions away from probabilistic reasoning toward emotionally driven action.

Several strategies can help mitigate these effects. Structured decision aids, which explicitly present individualized estimates of seizure freedom, surgical risk, cognitive outcome, and mortality benefit, can anchor discussion in data rather than intuition. Shared decision‐making frameworks ensure that the patient's values—such as tolerance of risk, examinations of the likely impacts of cognitive decline, or quality‐of‐life priorities—should be formally integrated into the decision process rather than inferred by clinicians. These frameworks also promote equipoise by framing “not operating” as an active, evidence‐based choice rather than a failure to act.

Emerging AI‐supported prognostic models represent another promising tool for countering cognitive bias. By combining multimodal clinical, neuroimaging, and neuropsychological data, machine‐learning algorithms can provide probabilistic forecasts of seizure freedom and cognitive outcomes that are less influenced by anecdotal clinical experience. These models can augment, rather than replace, expert clinical judgment—helping surgical teams calibrate their expectations and communicate risk transparently to patients and families.

Ultimately, the goal is not to eliminate intuition from clinical judgment but to introduce discipline through structured reasoning and data‐informed dialogue. Recognizing and managing cognitive biases should be viewed as an ethical responsibility within surgical teams—particularly when framing epilepsy surgery as potentially “life‐saving.” The use of decision aids, standardized case conferences, and probabilistic modeling can help ensure that these high‐stakes decisions are guided by evidence and patient values rather than by urgency, fear, or misplaced hope.

In summary we should neither overestimate our capacity to heal, nor underestimate our capacity to harm. 33 While epilepsy surgery undoubtedly brings benefits to a carefully selected group of people with drug resistant epilepsy, our analysis indicates that caution should be applied when framing epilepsy surgery as a “life‐saving” intervention for poor surgical candidates with life threatening epilepsy.
