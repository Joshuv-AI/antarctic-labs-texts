Rapid diagnosis of viral infections and application to infection control
Authors: Miki Endo, Reiko Tokuyama-Toda, Tatsuo Shirota, Kazuhito Satomura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid and accurate detection of viral infections is crucial in clinical settings, yet traditional quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) methods, though highly accurate, are typically laboratory-based and require significant time and resources, limiting their effectiveness in point-of-care environments. In this study, we applied a rapid qPCR method, originally developed for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, to a range of viral infections. The primary aim was to establish a novel diagnostic platform that combines mobile qPCR with multiplex detection for rapid, chairside application in clinical settings, and to explore its potential for detecting environmental contamination.MethodsThe viral infections targeted for rapid diagnosis included herpes simplex, herpes zoster, herpesviruses 4 or 5 -related diseases, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, herpangina, and respiratory syncytial virus infections. Specific primers and probes were designed to enable multiplex detection. The detection of viral nucleic acids using both benchtop and mobile qPCR devices was evaluated. Furthermore, viral synthetic nucleic acids were spread into the clinical environment, and swabs of the environmental surfaces were used as samples to examine whether this rapid method could be employed to detect contamination of environmental surfaces. Clinical samples from patients suspected of having labial herpes were also tested to examine whether viral detection is possible using this method.ResultsViral synthetic nucleic acids could be detected on both benchtop and mobile qPCR devices. This method can also detect environmental contamination, and differences in the detection of viral synthetic nucleic acids were observed before and after cleaning the clinical environment.ConclusionThis study introduces a novel approach combining mobile qPCR with multiplex detection for the rapid, chairside diagnosis of multiple viral infections. Furthermore, it demonstrates potential use in detecting environmental contamination in the clinical environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11823-4.

Background

Viral infections have various symptoms. Clinical symptoms of viral infections in the oral cavity include erosions, blisters, and tumors [1]. These viral infections are generally diagnosed empirically based on clinical symptoms. However, for conditions such as hand-foot-and-mouth disease, the accompanying symptoms and sequelae vary depending on the causative virus, and its early definitive diagnosis can aid in devising appropriate responses [2]. Clinically viable definitive diagnostic methods for viral infections include quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) testing [3, 4] virus isolation [3, 5], antigen testing [3, 6], and antibody testing [6, 7]. Each method has advantages and disadvantages [8]. For example, in virus isolation, although many viruses can be isolated and unknown viruses can be detected, this method requires a long time to obtain a definitive diagnosis, specialized skills, and facilities, and some viruses cannot be cultured [3, 5]. Antigen tests can relatively easily detect causative agents in a short time; however, they have lower sensitivity than other testing methods and can detect a limited number of viral infections [6]. Antibody testing is a relatively simple method that can show the progression of infection from the past to the present; however, the diagnosis is retrospective [6, 7]. Among these testing methods, qPCR is currently the most reliable and widely used [3]. However, conventional qPCR tests require a long detection time, a laboratory technician with specialized knowledge and skills, and relatively large tabletop equipment and have a complicated sample collection process that poses an infection risk to the operator [9].

Therefore, in the context of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic, we focused on the possibility of using PCR1100 (Gofoton, Ibaraki, Japan), a small, portable qPCR device recently developed for research purposes, as a point-of-care testing (POCT) device. Using this device, we succeeded in establishing a rapid diagnostic method that can directly detect the SARS-CoV-2 virus using a small amount of human saliva as a sample [10, 11]. Regarding the performance of the PCR1100 device, in a study conducted prior to the establishment of the SARS-CoV-2 rapid diagnostic method, we confirmed that it could detect SARS-CoV-2 synthetic RNA with a correlation between concentration and Ct value [12]. During method development, we hypothesized that this method could be applied to other viral infections. Multiplex qPCR allows for the simultaneous detection of multiple target genes in a single reaction, making it highly useful for rapid differential diagnosis in clinical settings. This approach is particularly important when multiple pathogens can cause similar symptoms, as in many viral infections. In addition, multiplexing reduces assay time, sample volume, and reagent cost, which makes it especially suitable for point-of-care (POC) diagnostics. Thus, in this study, we attempted to establish a rapid diagnostic method for various viral infections using a mobile qPCR device. Contact infection, one of the infection routes, occurs when pathogenic microorganisms attached to surfaces invade the human body through contact [13]. Therefore, the presence or absence of contamination on environmental surfaces must be detected to assess the risk of contact infection. Then, we hypothesized that the reagents and methods for rapidly diagnosing viral infections could also be applied to detecting environmental contamination. In this study, the potential of the proposed method to detect environmental contamination after the treatment of patients suspected of having a viral infection was investigated.

When implementing this method on actual patient samples, human Ribonuclease P (RNase P) was set as an endogenous control factor for detecting Cy5 fluorescence to ensure that qPCR was working properly. By designing primers and probes (Table 1) for the remaining two types of detection fluorescence, the possibility to detect viruses contained in the samples and perform a rapid diagnosis was investigated. For the selection of assays to combine, the viruses that could be diagnosed and lead to more effective clinical responses were determined. For this reason, we thought that there would be little need to distinguish whether the causative virus was herpesviruses 1 or 2 (HHV-1 or HHV-2), so we selected an assay specific to a region common to HHV-1 and HHV-2 (named HHV1/2). In addition, for the hand-foot-and-mouth disease, the analysis focused on distinguishing whether the causative virus was enterovirus 71 or coxsackievirus A group, and multiple assays were mixed so that all strains of coxsackievirus group A could be detected by VIC. Similarly, for herpangina, to distinguish whether the causative virus was coxsackievirus group A or B, a combination of multiple assays that could be detected by FAM or VIC was used, respectively.

qPCR and reverse-transcription (RT)-qPCR conditions for the benchtop-type qPCR device

TaqMan™ Microbe Detection Assay and TaqMan™ Gene Expression Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) were used as primers and probes corresponding to each virus (Table 1). For DNA viruses, 10 µL of TaqPath™ BactoPure™ Microbial Detection Master Mix, no Rox (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a master mix, and 1 µL of primers and probe for each virus was added to make the final reaction solution 20 µL in total. For RNA viruses, 5 µL of TaqPath™ 1-Step Multiplex Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a master mix, and 1 µL of primers and probe for each virus was added to make the final reaction solution 20µL in total. First, the sensitivity and specificity of the primers and probes for each virus were confirmed. Using a benchtop-type qPCR device (Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus™, Thermo Fisher confirmed), whether the three types of fluorescence could be detected simultaneously using the combinations of viruses and RNase P (Table 1) was investigated. As a positive control, synthetic nucleic acids corresponding to the primers and probe regions in each assay ID were prepared and mixed at various concentrations 1 × 10–105 copies/µL. The qPCR conditions for the benchtop-type qPCR device were programmed as follows: pre-read and initial denaturation/enzyme activation were serially performed at 60 °C for 30 s and 95 °C for 2 min. Afterward, it was cycled 40 times at 95 °C for 10 s for denaturation and 60 °C for 30 s for annealing/amplification. Finally, one cycle was used as a pre-read at 60 °C for 30 s. The RT-qPCR conditions for the benchtop-type qPCR device were programmed as follows: Uracil-N-Glycosylase incubation, RT, and polymerase activation were serially performed at 25 °C for 2 min, 53 °C for 10 min. and 95 °C for 2 min. Afterward, it was cycled 40 times at 95 °C for 15 s for denaturation and 60 °C for 1 min for annealing/amplification.

qPCR and RT-qPCR conditions for the mobile-type qPCR device (PCR1100)

The reaction solution used was the same as that used in the benchtop qPCR device. Annealing/amplification conditions were set for each virus combination (Table 2). The qPCR conditions that were adequate for the PCR1100 device were programmed as follows: enzyme activation was serially performed at 95 °C for 15 s. Afterward, it was cycled 50 times at 95 °C for 3.5 s for denaturation and 63–65 °C for 15 s for annealing/amplification. The RT-qPCR conditions that were adequate for the PCR1100 device were programmed as follows: RT and enzyme activation were serially performed at 50 °C for 150 s and 95 °C for 15 s. Afterward, it was cycled 50 times at 95 °C for 3.5 s for denaturation and 63–67 °C for 15–25 s for annealing/amplification (Table 2). Synthetic nucleic acids were prepared at concentrations ranging from 1 × 10–10⁹ copies/µL for each viral combination, and the sensitivity and specificity of the assay were assessed accordingly.

Environmental contamination detection

Moreover, whether this method could be applied to detect environmental contamination was investigated using the following method. A 3 × 3 cm area was randomly designated on the dental unit, and 2 µL of 1 × 10⁶ or 1 × 10⁹ copies/µL of synthetic virus nucleic acid was spread to each area using a cell scraper and allowed to dry for 15 min. The designated area was wiped three times with a cotton swab (medical swab, JCB industry limited, Tokyo, Japan) moistened with pure water for 10 s, and this was used as the pre-cleaning sample. Subsequently, as is done after regular medical treatment, the designated area was cleaned with a cloth containing hypochlorous acid (Zia-Cloth, Saraya Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and then wiped with a moist cotton swab in the same manner as before, and this was used as the post-cleaning sample. The swabs were placed in 15-mL centrifuge tubes containing 2 mL of pure water, immersed for 5 min, and vortexed, and the top 3µL was used as the sample for the qPCR test. This was mixed with the reaction solution for each target viral infection, and PCR 1100 was then used to verify whether viral synthetic nucleic acids could be detected.

Rapid diagnosis of patients with viral infections

The feasibility of rapid diagnosis was tested by this method using specimens of patients suspected of having herpes labialis infection by the following method. This study was approved by the Tsurumi University School of Dentistry Ethics Review Committee (No.124001) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Four patients suspected of having herpes labialis were included in the study. After obtaining consent to participate in this study, they rinsed their mouth with 2 mL of pure water for 15 s, which were used as a mouthwash sample. The affected area was also wiped three times with a moist cotton swab and placed in a 15-mL centrifuge tube containing 2 mL of pure water, immersed for 5 min, vortexed, and then used as a swab sample. 3µL of each sample was mixed with the reaction mixture for HHV-1/2 and HHV-3, and tests were conducted to verify whether rapid diagnosis was possible using this method with the PCR1100 device. At the same time, for the purpose of comparison with the methods currently used in clinical practice, detection was performed using an antigen test kit (Derma Quick HSV Maruho Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and a current benchtop qPCR device.

Rapid diagnosis for patients with herpes simplex infection

Finally, this method was performed using samples from a patient suspected of having a viral infection to examine its usefulness as a rapid diagnostic method. A typical case will be specifically shown. The patient had been complaining of itching since the morning of the examination day, and a very shallow ulcerative lesion measuring 2 mm in diameter was observed on the lower lip (Fig. 3A). Although the finding was not typical, the symptoms were suspected due to herpes labialis, and a rapid diagnosis was performed using this method. Mouthwash and swab samples of the affected area (lower lip) were used as samples. As a result, HHV-1/2 was detected in both the mouthwash and swab samples, (Fig. 3B and C). A diagnosis was obtained after approximately 23 min. Discussion

In viral infections that present with various symptoms throughout the body, including the oral region, identifying the causative virus at the first visit can lead to appropriate responses to associated symptoms and sequelae [2, 14, 15]. Then, the rapid diagnostic method [10, 11] established for diagnosing SARS-CoV-2 infection was applied to other viral infections. In this study, the viral infections targeted for rapid diagnosis were limited to those in which the determination of the causative virus was considered clinically useful. In herpes simplex infection and herpes zoster, the initial symptoms are similar to those of stomatitis and are not particularly distinctive. However, by accurately diagnosing them at this early stage and starting treatment, viral proliferation could be slightly suppressed which helps prevent the worsening of symptoms and ensures the patient’s quality of life (QOL). Even in cases presenting with atypical clinical features such as those shown in this study, a definitive diagnosis can be made, allowing for appropriate treatment and reassuring the patient.

Oral lesions that appear in association with HHV-4 or HHV-5 reactivation include infectious mononucleosis, cytomegalovirus infection, and Burkitt’s lymphoma. These are often seen in patients with underlying immunosuppression due to infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, etc. These patients must receive appropriate symptomatic treatment for each lesion; thus, the possibility of rapid diagnosis will help improve the patient’s QOL.

Many viruses have been identified as the causative virus in hand-foot-and-mouth disease. Cases caused by enterovirus 71 are associated with symptoms and sequelae, such as aseptic meningitis and polio-like paralysis. Determination of the causative virus at the first visit will allow for a rapid response to these cases and will be useful for explaining the situation to patients and their guardians.

Herpangina presents as a blistering rash on the soft palate. In certain cases, determining whether a blistering rash is spreading is difficult. If a rapid definitive diagnosis can be made at the first visit, these concerns can be dispelled.

RS virus infection is one of the leading causes of hospitalization in young children. If qPCR testing, which is more sensitive than commonly used antigen tests, could be used for rapid diagnosis at the chairside, both medical professionals and the child’s patients would be able to share a common understanding that the condition may become severe, which could present a decline in the child’s QOL.

Based on the aforementioned clinical background, the target viral infections were selected, and the combination of each causative virus was determined. As a result, rapid diagnosis using this method was possible for all combinations. The results obtained by this rapid diagnostic method were comparable to those obtained by the current benchtop type qPCR device. In addition, this method was able to detect the virus even in cases where the antigen test kit gave false negative results, proving that it is a diagnostic method with higher sensitivity and specificity. This method will enable the definitive diagnosis of viral infections that previously could be diagnosed through experience and will solve problems such as sensitivity in antigen tests. Furthermore, with qPCR tests, the design of the primers and probes can be easily modified, so the target virus can be changed easily. This indicates the potential to respond to many viral infections in the future. In addition, in cases of viral mutations or emerging infectious diseases, primers and probes can be designed immediately after decoding the viral sequence, making this method effective against future infectious disease pandemics.

Regardless of the virus, the transmission of infectious diseases must be prevented. Currently, infection control measures are manualized to deal with common infectious diseases; however, whether these measures are sufficient is insufficiently verified. In this study, we attempted to apply the proposed method to environmental contamination control. We investigated the possibility of detecting viral synthetic nucleic acids in a dental unit after treating patients with viral infections. As a result, contamination was detected, and the adequacy of cleaning was determined. However, this study used synthetic nucleic acids, and the results were not based on actual infectious viruses. In the future, when conducting large-scale clinical studies using patient samples at multiple facilities, it will be necessary to consider whether this method can be used to determine contamination in the environment after the examination of actual patients with viral infections. The results of this study demonstrated that the use of the proposed method of rapidly diagnosing patients suspected of having a viral infection can help protect their health. In addition, detecting environmental contamination after the patient’s treatment can protect the health of medical staff and other patients.

This diagnostic method is very simple and easy to use, so it can be used by medical staff with no technical or knowledge, making it suitable for implementation in various clinical environments. However, there are technical limitations that need to be resolved before it can be widely applied. These are the possibility of false positives or false negatives due to factors such as sample contamination and patient-to-patient variability. As a result of this study, this diagnostic method has superior sensitivity and specificity to antigen test kits, and requires less time and less human resources than current benchtop qPCR devices. On the other hand, although this method has shown its effectiveness in detecting viruses, its cost-effectiveness in daily clinical practice needs to be further evaluated. The possibility of interference from human-derived nucleic acids and environmental pollutants also needs to be considered, as these may affect the specificity and sensitivity of the test. However, even considering these issues that need to be resolved, it is true that this method has many advantages over other currently used methods. Importantly, this assay was also validated using actual clinical and environmental samples, including saliva, swabs from affected lesions, and surface swabs collected before and after cleaning. These results showed successful detection even in the presence of potential inhibitors or degraded nucleic acids, indicating the method’s applicability in practical clinical settings. Nevertheless, further evaluation is necessary, and large-scale clinical studies will be required in the future to verify diagnostic accuracy and assess clinical utility. In the future, we will increase the number of clinical cases to confirm that rapid diagnosis is possible for all target viral infections and clarify how long the virus is shed from the patient’s body into the oral cavity through longitudinal testing, and this information may be used to develop infection control measures. To establish this rapid diagnostic method as a more useful method for infection control, we plan to examine the relationship between the detected Ct value and the severity of symptoms and consider whether symptoms onset can be predicted from the Ct value.

Conclusions

This study established methods for the rapid diagnosis of viral infections using mouthwash and swabs from affected areas as samples and detection of environmental contamination. In future research, we will increase the number of patients with various viral infections for whom this method can be used and apply this method clinically at an early stage.
