Mayo Test Drive Español: A model of digital cognitive test adaptation
Authors: Aimee J Karstens, Paula A Aduen, Natalia Luna Andrade, Patricia Diaz-Galvan, Margot Barber, Dixie J Woolston, Jay S Patel, John L Stricker, Jason Hassenstab, John A Lucas, Christian Lachner, Nikki H Stricker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Test adaptations for Spanish are typically 1:1 translations of traditional paper-and-pencil tests that prioritize the fidelity of the original measure and have not undergone cross-cultural adaptation. Mayo Test Development through Rapid Iteration, Validation, and Expansion- Español (Mayo Test Drive-Español) is an adaptation of digital cognitive tests (Stricker Learning Span, Symbols Test) for and by heterogeneous Spanish speaking populations via a mixed methods approach.Method:Participants who spoke Spanish as a first and/or primary language, living in or outside of the United States, were recruited for the Mayo Test Drive-Español registry to complete surveys assessing linguistic background, familiarity with cognitive testing, cognitive diagnoses, and technological accessibility. A subsample completed additional questionnaires and virtual focus groups including cognitive interviewing and direct feedback on test instructions/stimuli. Iterative adaptations and alternative sets of stimuli/instructions were presented to subsequent focus groups and underwent team review. Saturation was determined by team consensus. Professional transcriptions of focus groups were analyzed qualitatively.Results:A subsample of Mayo Test Drive-Español registry participants (n = 85) were enrolled into 10 focus groups (n = 38, 1–7 per group). 8 iterative revisions of test stimuli (including 54/92 words) and 9 iterative revisions of instructions were made based on focus group feedback, team meetings, and quality control procedures. Transcribed sessions were analyzed for themes related to the platform, paradigms/construct, instructions, and content.Conclusions:Mayo Test Drive-Español demonstrates the importance of early input from stakeholders (professionals, patients, community members) from various regions to improve the adaptation of cognitive measures for different languages and contexts.Keywords: Cognition, neuropsychological tests, linguistics, memory, executive functioning, cultural

Introduction

The United States (U.S.) is home to the second largest population of Spanish speakers after Mexico (Galan, 2026). Hispanics are 1.5x more likely than non-Hispanic Whites to develop Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or other types of dementia, yet Hispanics are highly underrepresented (0–12%) in AD clinical trials (Alzheimer’s Association, 2021; Aranda et al., 2023; Chin et al., 2011; Franzen et al., 2022). Hispanic older adults also experience delayed dementia diagnoses (O’Bryant, Johnson, et al., 2013; O’Bryant, Xiao, et al., 2013; Silva-Rudberg et al., 2024) and are less likely to receive care in specialty clinics (Mejia et al., 2004). A lack of culturally appropriate clinical tools and culturally competent providers represents a significant barrier to timely and accurate diagnosis (Daugherty et al., 2017; Duggan et al., 2019; Fernandez Cajavilca et al., 2025; Funes et al., 2016; Rabin et al., 2020). Moreover, existing neuropsychological tests and normative datasets often fail to capture the substantial heterogeneity among Spanish speakers in the U.S., including variation in culture, acculturation, ethnicity, dialect, educational background, and country of origin (Daugherty et al., 2017; Moslimani et al., 2023; Pedraza & Mungas, 2008).

With the widespread adoption of technology (85% of Hispanics in the US use smartphones for web access), there is an opportunity to leverage digital tools to expand access to sensitive cognitive screening for Spanish speakers (Atske, 2021). Recently, the International Test Commission (ITC) guidelines have been adapted for neuropsychological testing have been put forth to improve the standards of linguistic and cultural test development (Nguyen et al., 2024) and increased recognition of the need for formal harmonization of clinical tools (Briceño et al., 2023; Manly et al., 2022). For digital measures that have undergone rigorous cultural adaptations, the technology does not reduce the need for in-person supervised or facilitated administration (Gershon et al., 2020; Possin et al., 2018). Spanish digital cognitive assessments that can be self-administered across operating systems (e.g. PART digital cognitive screening, NIH Mobile Toolbox has only been validated in English speakers), still require the examinee to download a web application for remote use (Gershon et al., 2022, 2025; Lelo de Larrea-Mancera et al., 2025). Other digital tests developed and normed in various Spanish speaking countries require additional cross-cultural validation and normative datasets to address the needs of heterogeneous populations of Spanish speakers in the U.S. (Duggan et al., 2019; Giudicessi et al., 2024; Strutt et al., 2016). The digital age has introduced a shared language and culture shaped by how individuals interact with technology that has the potential to both enrich cross-cultural communication or introduce bias (Alsaleh, 2024). Innovation and scalability in care delivery cannot happen without leveraging technology to increase test accessibility and cultural sensitivity. Self-administered Spanish cognitive tests have the potential to improve early detection of cognitive impairment, expand clinical services and clinical trial enrollment, and more easily facilitate large scale normative data collection needed to use such measures effectively. Thus, the effort to digitalize cognitive measures must be strategic in using technology and multicultural methodology to advance accessibility.

The aim of the current study is to adapt digital cognitive measures for primary Spanish speakers in the multicultural context of the U.S. Mayo Test Development through Rapid Iteration, Validation, and Expansion (Mayo Test Drive) is a remote platform for neuropsychological test development and self-administration that is accessible through any web-based device (Boots et al., 2024; Nicosia et al., 2023; Stricker et al., 2025). The English version of the Mayo Test Drive screening battery shows sensitivity to early changes associated with AD biomarkers and large effect sizes in differentiating individuals with mild cognitive impairment from cognitively unimpaired individuals (Boots et al., 2024; Stricker et al., 2024), supporting its potential utility for improving early detection of cognitive impairment in Spanish-speaking populations. The Mayo Test Drive model of test development expects that frequent iterations are needed for digital tests due to the constantly evolving nature of technology and focuses on specific use cases to drive test development priorities in alignment with software development principles. Novel and open-source cognitive measures are iteratively adapted through piloting, and the platform’s user-centered interface is designed for simplicity and to minimize potential administration/scoring bias across device types.

In this study, we expand the Mayo Test Drive model and platform to include multicultural and community engaged approaches to feasibly and iteratively adapt digital cognitive tests for Spanish speakers. Mayo Test Drive-Español involves flexible bidirectional components for rapid data collection and test adaptation through: (1) data driven adaptation, (2) community engaged feedback and cognitive interviewing, (3) team-based iterative revision, (4) piloting. We applied this framework to iteratively adapt test instructions and stimuli by eliciting direct feedback from Spanish speakers with diverse cross-regional linguistic and cultural experiences relevant to the heterogeneous population of Spanish speakers in the U.S. This study presents results from the initial stages of this digital multicultural test adaptation of the Stricker Learning Span and Symbols Test for Spanish speakers, representing a critical initial step prior to future work that will focus on construct equivalence, clinical validation, normative data collection, and implementation in clinical and research settings. Using a mixed methods approach with virtual focus groups, we examined attitudes and perceptions toward the Mayo Test Drive platform, test paradigms, and test adaptation process, including perceived feasibility, accessibility, and linguistic and cultural relevance for Spanish-speaking Hispanic/Latino populations.

Methods

Mayo Test Drive-Español registry participants were recruited using snowball sampling via listservs, word-of-mouth, and direct outreach, including bilingual and monolingual Spanish speakers with diverse nationalities, cultural backgrounds, and experience with cognitive testing (e.g. test naïve, patients, psychometricians, interpreters, clinicians/healthcare staff, researchers). Participants had to be self-identified Spanish speaking (primary or early speakers) and at least 18 years old. Spanish speaking patients were also identified for recruitment by electronic medical record review (AJK) that had undergone cognitive testing within 6 months of enrollment and did not have dementia.

All recruitment, consent, questionnaires, focus group slides and other study materials were provided in both English and Spanish. All Spanish study materials were translated first by a professional service and then revised to ensure appropriate meaning by multiple multinational members of the study team unless previously published translated/adapted versions were available. Various existing scales of language and acculturation were reviewed in the development of the current study materials. Mayo Test Drive-Español registry participants consented via the recruitment email or orally to complete a web-based survey and to be contacted about future Mayo Test Drive research including the current focus group study. Focus group participants were recruited on a rolling basis over 2 months via email and/or phone and could not have dementia. Participant availability, preferred language, and nativity were prioritized in the scheduling and enrollment process. We partnered with the Mayo Clinic Center for Health Equity and Community Engagement Research program to develop our protocol and enhance recruitment. International Test Commission Guidelines for cultural/linguistic adaptation of neuropsychological tests were used in designing this protocol (Nguyen et al., 2024). Data was captured and stored via secure platforms including REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) hosted at Mayo Clinic and the Mayo Test Drive platform v1. This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board and completed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Mayo Test Drive-Español registry

The Mayo Test Drive-Español registry surveys included quantitative and open-ended question formats to characterize participant demographics (e.g. age, gender identity, sexuality, pronouns, education, race, marital status, occupation), ethnic/cultural backgrounds (e.g. country of origin, ethnicity, other countries resided in), study scheduling and communication preferences (e.g. preferred language of focus group, communication, and study documents, phone vs. email contact, availability for the focus groups), language history (e.g. other language proficiency, speaking English or Spanish from birth or early childhood), experience with cognitive testing, history of a neurocognitive disorder, and technology (e.g. comfort and access to use devices). Given that international participants were recruited, “Other” and open-ended response options were provided in attempts to account for cross-regional variation in social constructs (e.g. race, education). See supplemental materials for the full registry questionnaire.

Mayo Test Drive tests and adaptation

This study was designed in accordance with the neuropsychological application of the International Test Commission guidelines for test adaptations including preconditions and test development portions (Phases 1 through 4), with permissions obtained to use the Mayo Test Drive platform and to adapt the subtests (Nguyen et al., 2024). Given the heterogenous target population of Spanish speakers living in the U.S., we consulted content experts (see Test Development Guideline 4) as part of our research team and through our focus group participants.

The Mayo Test Drive platform was designed to increase accessibility to cognitive tests through use of a web-based platform with multi-device compatibility that is optimized for remote assessment. Individuals can access the test using a direct test link or QR code link that contains a study identification code. The platform and tests were designed to have brief text instructions on each page and simple touch or single-click responses. The feasibility and usability of Mayo Test Drive have been previously described in detail and has demonstrated that Mayo Test Drive is appropriate in older adults and clinical populations for self-administered and unsupervised cognitive testing (Patel et al., 2025; Stricker et al., 2025). The platform uses full data capture (time to completion, item by item responses/completion time, device used) and examinees are prompted about where the test was taken, interference (after each test and at the end) and administration concerns/details (e.g. if they used a stylus; see Patel et al. 2025 for details). Mayo Test Drive includes the Stricker Learning Span (SLS) to assess verbal memory (Stricker et al., 2024) and Symbols Test (SYM) to assess visual processing speed/executive functioning (Boots et al., 2024; Nicosia et al., 2023). See our previous publications for detailed descriptions of the platform, test paradigms, key variables, construct validation, clinical validation and normative data (Boots et al., 2024; Patel et al., 2025; Stricker et al., 2022, 2023,2024, 2025). All of the instructions and stimuli are presented on the screen (see Figure 1 for practice samples of Mayo Test Drive-Español).

The SLS is a computer adaptive, visual word list-learning and 4-choice recognition memory test (5 learning trials and delayed recognition), with 8 target words presented at Trial 1 and the difficulty (number of words) is altered for subsequent trials based on the number of correct items (see Stricker et al. 2023 JINS for details). There is a ceiling of 23 possible words (1 target and 3 foils in each of the 23 word bins) that make up a bank of 92 words. Word bins are matched on imagery, and later word bins have lower imagery ratings and high word frequency to increase recognition difficulty (Stricker et al., 2023). The 4 words in each bin and the relative placement of each bin (1–23) do not change across administrations; however, the target word chosen (vs. foils) from the 4 is randomly selected for each new test session to reduce practice effects, and the order in which the word list and recognition items are presented is randomized across each learning trial to reduce serial position effects. After each memory recognition trial, feedback is given on the number of correct items. SYM is a rapid paired symbol matching test (4 trials of 12 items). SYM involves matching/discrimination of paired symbols; participants identify which of two symbol pairs at the bottom of the screen matches one of three symbol pairs at the top of the screen.

In our efforts to maintain construct equivalence, we aimed to choose words for the SLS based on similar cognitive neuroscience principles as the original SLS including the use of very common words (high word frequency, early age of acquisition) that increase in difficulty over the course of the test (based on imagery), and consider unique and shared linguistic characteristics between English and Spanish to avoid creating an advantage based on degree of English acculturation/proficiency. The initial draft of the SLS-Spanish (SLS-S) involved (a) data driven selection of word bins and (b) professional translation of instructions used in the original empirically validated SLS paradigm. The English version of the SLS was coded for shared and unique bin properties (e.g. most common parts of speech, shared elements or themes) in attempts to select words with comparable difficulty while avoiding bias. First, we downloaded a bank of the highest frequency words and other linguistic property data (e.g. word frequency, parts of speech) from the EsPAL Spanish language corpus subtitle contextual diversity database (https://www.bcbl.eu/databases/espal/) (Duchon et al., 2013). We chose the corpus derived from Latin American film subtitles. EsPAL was also consulted through the multi-draft revision process. When imagery data was not available for words, other associated linguistic properties were considered (e.g. nouns are generally more imageable than conjunctions). Words were selected to avoid cognates or other obvious similarities between the two languages, to prevent giving bilinguals an advantage over monolinguals (Blumenfeld et al., 2016). For example, numerous words have identical spelling (e.g. hotel, actual, idea, doctor), are borrowed from the other language (e.g. cafetería/cafeteria), or are systemically derived from the same root language (e.g. acción/action, diario/diary, clásico, classic, exacto/exact, curioso/curious). Cross-regional compatibility was also prioritized. For example, we did not use word categories with known variations by country (e.g. fruits or vegetables, the plural you tense “vosotros”). Instructions were independently professionally translated prior to the team revisions.

Our research team was composed of clinician scientists (neuropsychologists, neurologist, psychiatrist) and a medical interpreter who practiced medicine outside of the U.S., including 5 native Spanish speakers originating from Spanish speaking countries in South America, Central America, and Europe; 3 s language learners with varied proficiency levels; and 2 monolingual English speakers. The research team reviewed and revised the draft of word bins/alternates and instructions, independently and then in a mock focus group format before focus groups were initiated. Subsequent iterations were created through a combination of feedback (participants and research team), consulting the EsPAL database, and other Spanish language resources (e.g. dictionary of the Royal Spanish Academy) throughout the study period and again during quality control.

Focus groups

Prior to the focus groups, participants completed additional Spanish/English REDCap surveys and were invited to complete the original English Mayo Test Drive if comfortable doing so. Acculturation was assessed formally through the Bidimensional Acculturation Scale using the original English and the Peragallo Spanish translation that uses a minor variation on linguistic proficiency scale prompts (Marin & Gamba, 1996; Peragallo et al., 2005) along with additional questions designed to understand linguistic history (e.g. questions about language acquisition, current activities/task-based language use). Focus groups were conducted via videoconferencing for approximately 1 h with weekday/evening, and weekend sessions. We aimed to recruit 4–7 participants per session to optimize group cohesion in a virtual environment. Bilingual Spanish/English clinician scientists (PA, NLA) facilitated the focus groups with a slide deck and semi-structured scripts. Facilitators were trained to optimize group cohesion and participation in order to elicit feedback on cultural and linguistic acceptability of Mayo Test Drive-Español components. During the sessions, a supportive co-facilitator (AJK, NHS) was present to manage time, technical difficulties, field questions, take attendance, send final survey links, and take notes.

During the sessions, names were altered (first name, code number) to help maintain privacy, with the option to participate with their cameras turned off. Verbal, symbolic (e.g. emoji), and written (via the chat function) feedback were encouraged. All focus groups included the following components: (1) introduction with an overview of the expectations and goals, (2) ice breaker activity reflecting on one’s cultural/linguistic history, (3) cognitive interviewing, (4) review of translated/adapted test instructions in the test formatting, (5) review of SLS-S stimuli (words, bin combinations, bin order), (6) debriefing survey. Facilitators conducted cognitive interviewing using prompts based on the COSMIN criteria for evaluating content validity for cultural adaptation of assessments that was based on expert consensus from 21 different countries (Terwee et al., 2018). Given that the COSMIN criteria assess patient-reported outcomes and do not address technological issues, additional cognitive interviewing and technology-related prompts were included to address concerns related to technology for participants and their perceptions of these concerns for family and others in their community (e.g. ability to make the task progress, other barriers to use). Scripts with prompts were available in both languages to use it flexibly based on participant preference and the natural flow of conversation (McKown et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2021). The protocol was designed to provoke personal reflection, increase group cohesion, and sharing of opinions, all relevant to the study aims. Mayo Test Drive screenshots and proposed Spanish instructions and words/bins were displayed in slide decks to elicit direct feedback. Focus group slides were revised based on the information gleaned from prior groups and the evolving needs of the study (e.g. word bin drafts, order of components 4&5). The final survey was administered at the end of the focus group via REDCap to assess attitudes about feasibility and acceptability, along with open ended prompts for additional feedback.

Data analysis

Informal qualitative analysis from focus groups was first discussed iteratively in research team meetings that included both facilitators present during the groups and key feedback displayed on slide decks. All iterative changes to instructions and stimuli were tracked. Focus group sessions were recorded and professionally transcribed (Landmark Associates Inc). Formal qualitative analysis was conducted by coding segmented lines of text based on broad and specific themes relevant to COSMIN criteria, and in line with the structure of data collection (e.g. cultural references, language properties, test specific). All of the transcriptions were coded by one rater and large segments were selected at random for double coding by a second rater (discrepancies were minimal and did not meaningfully change results). Both raters were present for the virtual sessions, completed quality control procedures, and finalized the adaptation. Group differences between registry participants that did or did not enroll in focus groups were examined using t-tests and Chi-square analyses. Responses to categorical multiple choice, continuous Likert scale-based survey questions, and word properties (from existing language corpuses) are reported using descriptive statistics.

Results

Mayo Test Drive-Español participants (total n = 85) that enrolled into the registry during the focus group recruitment period (n = 67) were invited to participate and a subset completed a focus group session (n = 39). Few participants (n = 4) only completed portions of the pre-focus group materials, but were not included in analyses as they were unable to attend their scheduled group/reschedule or had technical issues that limited their full participation. Mayo Test Drive-Español registry participants were natives of 17 countries/territories, with 90% born outside of the U.S., and 84% residing in the U.S. during the study (see Figure 2 for all cumulative countries of residence). 54% received formal education outside of the U.S. and Puerto Rico, and 81% received formal education in Spanish (42% both languages). Participants with graduate degrees were oversampled given our efforts to recruit people familiar with cognitive testing. Figure 3 shows the proportion of time participants lived in Spanish or English dominant contexts during their lifetime. Additional demographics, language/cultural history, and cognitive testing experience are reported in Table 1. Focus group participants did not differ by age, gender, Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, educational background or proportion of early/native English speakers from non-focus group participants (p-values > .05). The majority of focus group participants reported that they speak in Spanglish (74%) and code switch between languages during conversation (72%) in their normal communication. Most focus group sessions were conducted predominantly in Spanish (90%), and all sessions included some combination of spoken Spanish and English. Regarding technology use and access, the majority of focus group participants currently use smartphones (n = 37, 95%) and laptop computers (n = 34, 87%) for internet access and approximately half of participants currently use tablets (n = 17, 44%) or desktop computers (n = 20, 51%). All focus group participants (n = 39) reported having access to a web-based device to participate in a virtual study, use email to receive and send documents, and have used videoconferencing in the past for appointments, work, or social activities. One participant responded that they would need assistance to complete a study involving online surveys and videoconferencing.

Qualitative data analyses

Based on consensus made during a research team meeting, saturation (i.e. the lack of new ideas generated by the groups) was met after 10 sessions. Focus group sessions were transcribed in the language spoken (1,945 text segments were reviewed). First, lines of text were coded based on a priori terms relevant to test validation (e.g. construct, content), specific references to culture, country of origin, linguistic concepts, and the aspect of the test (paradigm, instructions, SLS bins, SYM stimuli). Second, text was coded based on major shared themes related to the construct (face validity, paradigm), relevance (cultural, linguistic), and comprehensibility. See Table 2 for themes, detailed descriptions, quotes, and adaptation examples. Data from open ended participant responses, focus group chat text, and field notes were also reviewed and included in the qualitative analysis.

Quantitative data analyses

Of the focus group participants, two did not attempt the English Mayo Test Drive, one started but did not complete the SLS, and 92% of focus group participants completed both SLS and SYM in one session. On a multiple-choice survey, 80% of participants endorsed that the self-administered test would be appropriate for home and clinic, 8% thought it was only appropriate for the clinic only, 5% thought it was only appropriate for administration at home, and 1 participant was unsure. Regarding the length of test administration time, 92% of participants thought it was appropriate and 8% were unsure. 95% of participants responded that the Spanish instructions would be easy to understand and 5% endorsed that others may have trouble understanding them. When provided a list of multiple response options, participants reported that the SLS measured memory (100%), attention/concentration (90%), thinking speed (62%), language (28%), multitasking (20%), problem solving (10%), “other” (8%), and that SYM measured thinking speed (90%), attention/concentration (80%), multitasking (36%), problem solving (28%), memory (36%), language (5%), “other” (5%).

Cultural adaptation

Throughout the study, iterative versions of the test (10 for SLS-S and SYM instructions and 8 for SLS-S word bins) were created. See Table 2 for specific examples of adaptations and corresponding feedback. The final word bin draft included alterations of 54 of the 92 original words. In comparing the 1st draft and final iteration, 38 words were unchanged, 7 word endings were changed (e.g. noun to verb, singular to plural), 8 words were moved to a different bin (including one with a word ending change), 2 added an accent, and 38 were replaced with an alternate word. Only 1 bin underwent no alterations. The Symbols Test stimuli were not altered. Neither paradigm was altered, though there was concern that timed tasks may not be culturally appropriate for all Spanish speakers. Select suggestions were not incorporated into the adaptation for various reasons including if the word had a low frequency rating (e.g. preciso), programming consistency between versions (adding exclamation points to a button), maintaining equivalence between English and Spanish versions (including audio), contradictory input from other participants, only identified by a single participant, and/or following committee member consensus. For example, two words were not familiar to 1–2 English dominant participants (e.g. dispositivo is device, desempeño is performance) but familiar to all others. All SLS-S words had high frequency ratings based on the EsPAL Latin American subtitle language corpus (zipf M = 5.70, SD = 0.66, range = 4.54–7.52; logn M = 2.70, SD = 0.66. range= 1.55–4.52). Subjective age of acquisition (data missing for 4 words) was in the pre-Kindergarten age on average (M = 4.48, SD = 1.2, range = 2–7 years; Alonso et al., 2015). Imagery ratings were not evaluated because data was only available for 42/92 words. See Figure 4 for a comparison of part of speech for words included in SLS English and SLS-S. Discussion

Our Mayo Test Drive-Español study incorporates the Mayo Test Drive model for developing digital cognitive measures with community engaged methodology for cultural/linguistic Spanish test adaptation. The current study adapted the SLS and SYM for Spanish speakers with methods that prioritize cultural equivalence and cross-regional linguistic content. In spite of our best efforts with a data driven approach, well-selected research team, and following other parts of current professional test interpretation guidelines, we ultimately revised 59% of the initially-drafted SLS-S words (e.g. moved, removed, switched, altered) using focus group feedback. Considering that this was between two closely-related languages that are in continual contact, even greater challenges can be expected between languages that are less closely related. Results support the use of broadening content-related input to include a larger pool of community members and stakeholders, particularly for populations for who language is heavily influenced by regional differences, multilingualism, and acculturation. Future piloting of Mayo Test Drive-Español will help determine feasibility, psychometric properties, construct validity and clinical utility in diverse Spanish speakers living in the U.S.

Feedback from our focus groups contributed to simplified and approachable instructions (SLS-S and SYM), alternative memory word items and order (SLS-S), and altered item bin ordering (SLS-S). Specifically, we used qualitative (e.g. focus groups with cognitive interviewing) and quantitative (e.g. survey responses, test completion rates) data collected virtually to iteratively revise the tests and elicit attitudes about use of the digital platform and measures in Spanish speaking populations. Each component of the Mayo Test Drive-Español focus groups was designed intentionally to reduce barriers to research, facilitate group cohesion, and increase comfort in self-expression. For example, we included virtual sessions at different days of the week/times of day, made materials/sessions available in Spanish and English, and renumeration that did not require U.S. citizenship (nationwide and international recruitment). The study strongly benefited from dynamic group-based cognitive, a common practice in patient-reported outcomes research that is rarely used in performance-based cognitive assessment (Kobayashi et al., 2021). Future work to statistically assess construct equivalence will help determine whether these feasible approaches are similarly as effective as rigorous, but costly and timely methods such as piloting an extensive bank of items (Mungas et al., 2004, 2011).

Following suit with previous cross-cultural memory test development (Blackmon et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2009), our goal of adapting, and not directly translating, the SLS word-bin stimuli was to develop a fair and accurate test in terms of the content, constructs, task difficulty, relevance and accessibility, while enhancing these test properties with Spanish language and sociocultural factors. Feedback on Spanish word properties (e.g. lexical, semantic, word length) and other cultural aspects of language (e.g. verb tense used in instructions, contextual nuances, tone) informed 8 iterative revisions. Qualitative feedback in the focus groups drew attention to test content that was missed by our research team, even concepts we intentionally tried to avoid. For example, we did not want to include words that could have a different meaning in Spanish vs. English, but overlooked the word “sin” (without in Spanish, immoral act in English) in the process of trialing various words for more challenging word bins. Our participants identified words and phrases in the instructions that were not appropriate cross-regionally (e.g. Anglicisms), despite undergoing professional translation and study team review. Word bins in the SLS-S changed significantly across iterations because of feedback related to linguistics, cross-cultural differences, and/or intuition (e.g. words that stood out, though it was unclear why). Major feedback involved making the instructions concise, universally and easily understood, and consistent, while keeping individuals with varying education levels and technological literacy.

Many words in the Spanish language have a different meaning based on the pronunciation (i.e. what vowel is emphasized), which is determined by an accent if it is read. To reduce potential bias that may be more salient in certain populations (e.g. dyslexia, low literacy/illiterate), high frequency words that convey an entirely different meaning with the presence of an accent were removed (el vs. él) from the SLS-S word bins. Words that could be easily misinterpreted as a conjugated verb were sometimes removed (nada, nothing vs. swims; como, how/like vs. eats) because participants felt it altered the difficulty of the word relative to the others in the bin. Other phonetic and visual properties that influenced bin cohesion included the number of phonemes and syllables. In addition to appearing and sounding longer, these words had a different cadence that made them (as target words) stand out more or less depending on the alternatives (3 foils). The final word bins became the most challenging to adapt and the team went through 5 (of 8) different revisions of the 23rd bin to come to a solution that satisfied participants and the team. These more challenging bins are designed to be more abstract (less imageable) and are only included during administration with higher performing individuals, due to the adaptive nature of the test. Finding sets of 4 words that are abstract, greater than 1 letter while also being short words, similar but not too close in spelling/meaning to other words in the bin, and able to stand alone without other words, all while meeting all other standards we had set, was difficult. These instances made the dynamic focus group feedback sessions very helpful for brainstorming.

Cognitive interviewing following the English Mayo Test Drive administration provided early insight into face validity, acceptability, and feasibility of the tests and platform. This early stage buy-in from stakeholders and community members with a range of exposure to/expertise in cognitive testing is important. While there are increasing efforts to use technology to increase patient access (Weiner et al., 2023; Weintraub et al., 2022), digital tests have rarely been adopted clinically (Collins & Riley, 2016). The English Mayo Test Drive has shown high completion rates in individuals with and without cognitive impairment in a research cohort (Patel et al., 2025). The SLS/SLS-S paradigm uses word familiarity (high frequency, low age of acquisition) to make the items simultaneously difficult and accessible (Pérez, 2007). Some participants recommended the use of audio or pictures to improve instructions. We felt this would introduce other technological challenges and likely alter the construct, but have taken this feedback into consideration for the development of tests specifically for low literacy populations. Future work will test feasibility in samples with fewer years of education, lower literacy levels, older generations/ages, moderate to severe cognitive impairment, and less technological literacy.

While this mixed methods study has many strengths, there are also limitations to consider. For example, given our rapid convenience sampling, our recruitment of older adults and individuals with fewer years of education was low. Future piloting offers the opportunity to incorporate different strategies for recruitment as well as community-based and clinical enrollment. As many research measures are not designed for cross-cultural use or recruitment of people living in different countries/regions, we learned that some traditional ways of collecting demographic data were unsuitable (e.g. reporting years of education). In this regard, categorical terms (e.g. secondary school) were more reliable. This can be further complicated in countries where historically school has been disrupted for familial duties/work, political unrest, and/or is not universally offered or mandated. We benefited from the combination of supplemental categorical and open-ended responses to capture missing or incongruent responses (e.g. typing in Race/Ethnicity as 52% of registry participants did not endorse the U.S. census-based categories). This data is informative for future studies as it is important to consider the history of race/ethnicity labels, the impact they have for participants self-identifying their cultural and ancestral backgrounds, and the specific purpose the categories serve in a particular line of research. We recommend that researchers recruiting multinational or immigrant populations consult with various cultural resources to define categories, offer open-ended response options, semi-structured interviewing, or adaptive survey response options. Another limitation is the lack of a validated quantitative survey to capture participant feedback on the properties of the measures. While the potential for confirmation bias is always present in this type of work, we attempted to mitigate this at every stage of the study by starting fresh with a Spanish language corpus-based word bank, encouraging open feedback (Spanish/English sessions, culturally focused rapport building, verbal and written feedback opportunities), and presenting participant driven revisions and alternative ideas to the focus groups. Further, one of two researchers that are very familiar with the English test/stimuli was available for focus group support, but not a primary facilitator of the groups. Importantly, as this was an iterative process, our participants were exposed to Mayo Test Drive-Español test drafts and slides, but were not yet able to pilot a Spanish version in the platform. Feedback may vary slightly when the test is presented fully in the platform within a different context, with individuals who have varying technological literacy, and lower literacy levels. Finally, as the test is presented visually and requires reading, it is not appropriate for individuals who are illiterate in its current form.

Our study demonstrates the importance of increased diligence in the translation and adaptation phase of developing digital tools with the goal of cross-cultural equivalence. In addition to our efforts to avoid introducing cultural bias, we centered community perspectives, embraced linguistic nuances, and appreciated cultural differences. The next stages of Mayo Test Drive-Español include piloting in monolingual and bilingual samples of Spanish speakers with and without cognitive impairment. Further research is needed to assess whether the adaptation can achieve our ultimate goal of creating a psychometrically equivalent and accessible tool without compromising construct validity or diagnostic sensitivity. Validation of the adaptation will include clinical and cross-cultural analyses (e.g. measurement invariance, item response theory) to determine whether the English and Spanish versions are equivalent for clinical settings and research harmonization (Arce Rentería et al., 2023).
