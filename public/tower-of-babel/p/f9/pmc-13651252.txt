Trends in mortality involving coexisting lung cancer and sepsis in the United States: a CDC WONDER analysis, 1999–2024
Authors: Rasheeda Shehroz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer and sepsis are major contributors to morbidity and mortality worldwide. Their coexistence represents a significant clinical challenge, as patients with lung cancer may be at increased risk of developing sepsis because of cancer-related immune dysfunction, treatment-related immunosuppression, and frequent healthcare exposure. Sepsis in these patients is associated with poor outcomes, increased health care utilization, and higher mortality rates.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of death certificates from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) WONDER database to evaluate mortality associated with the co-occurrence of lung cancer and sepsis among adults aged ≥ 45 years in the United States from 1999 to 2024. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) and annual percent changes (APCs) were calculated and stratified by year, sex, race/ethnicity, census region, state, and metropolitan status.ResultsAAMR increased from 1.6 to 2.0 per 100,000 population between 1999 and 2024. Among males, the AAMR increased from 2.3 in 1999 to 2.4 in 2024, while among females, it rose from 1.1 to 1.6 over the same period. Racial disparities were evident, with non-Hispanic Black or African American adults experiencing the highest AAMR (2.6). Regionally, the South had the highest mortality burden (AAMR 2.2), while Kentucky and Mississippi reported the highest state-level rates (3.98 and 3.14, respectively). Mortality rates were higher in non-metropolitan areas (AAMR 1.9) compared with metropolitan areas (1.7).ConclusionsMortality associated with coexisting lung cancer and sepsis has increased over time, with notable disparities across demographic and geographic groups. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health strategies and improved access to care to reduce mortality in high-risk populations.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04470-1.

Introduction

Sepsis remains a major cause of preventable mortality worldwide and a major driver of health care utilization, with substantial variation by age, sex, race, and region [1]. In the United States, sepsis-related mortality continues to impose a large public health burden, and recent CDC WONDER analyses show persistent demographic and geographic disparities, with higher mortality among men, non-Hispanic Black populations, and residents of the South [2, 3]. Among malignant diseases, lung cancer is especially important because it remains the most lethal cancer in the United States, accounting for an estimated 124,730 deaths in 2025, and most deaths occur in older adults [4, 5]. These two conditions overlap because patients with cancer are highly vulnerable to severe infection due to tumor-related immune dysfunction, treatment-related immunosuppression, invasive procedures, and repeated hospitalization [6, 7].

The coexistence of lung cancer and sepsis is clinically significant and epidemiologically reasonable. Previous studies have shown that sepsis-associated mortality is higher in patients with cancer than in those without cancer, and metastatic lung cancer has been identified among the malignancies with the highest sepsis-related death rates [6, 7]. Current evidence also suggests that lung cancer may be associated with increased sepsis occurrence and short-term mortality, reinforcing the need to examine this relationship using strong, population-based analytical methods [7]. However, many earlier studies may not accurately reflect true trends over time. CDC WONDER offers a more standardized platform for mortality surveillance and allows evaluation of long-term patterns across age, sex, race, and geography, making it appropriate for assessing the burden of sepsis among patients with lung cancer [3].

The risk of lung cancer and sepsis rises sharply with advancing age [4]. This overlap suggests that mortality differences may reflect not only disease biology but also comorbidity burden, treatment intensity, and access to timely critical care. Understanding these differences is essential because sepsis outcomes remain worse in vulnerable groups and because improving cancer survival may increase the number of patients living long enough to experience infectious complications [1, 6].

Therefore, investigating sepsis mortality among patients with lung cancer is important for refining prevention strategies, identifying high-risk subgroups, and informing oncology and critical care decision making. A CDC WONDER-based analysis can provide nationally relevant evidence on temporal trends and disparities, filling an important gap in the literature and supporting targeted interventions to reduce avoidable deaths [2, 3].

To our knowledge, this is among the first national CDC WONDER analyses evaluating long-term mortality trends involving coexisting lung cancer and sepsis through 2024 while simultaneously assessing demographic and geographic disparities across the United States.

This study aimed to evaluate temporal trends and demographic disparities in mortality associated with coexisting lung cancer and sepsis in the United States.

Methods

Patient eligibility and screening

We conducted a retrospective analysis using mortality data from the CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death database for the period 1999–2024, focusing on individuals aged ≥ 45 years in the United States [3]. Mortality records were examined for cases in which both lung cancer and sepsis were listed among the causes of death. The 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) was used to identify relevant cases. Lung cancer was identified using ICD-10 code C34 (malignant neoplasm of bronchus and lung), and sepsis was identified using ICD-10 codes A40–A41 (streptococcal sepsis and other sepsis). Data for some racial groups, including American Indian or Alaska Native populations, were unavailable for certain years in the CDC WONDER database and were excluded from analysis for those years.

Deaths were included only if both C34 and A40–A41 codes were present anywhere on the death certificate, either as underlying causes of death or contributing causes of death. Records without documentation of both conditions were excluded. Analyses were restricted to adults aged ≥ 45 years because deaths involving both lung cancer and sepsis were relatively uncommon in younger age groups, resulting in unstable mortality estimates and suppression of data in several CDC WONDER strata. Restricting analyses to adults aged ≥ 45 years improved estimate stability and interpretability. Because this study utilized publicly available, de-identified data, Institutional Review Board approval was not required.

Data abstraction

Data on deaths due to coexisting lung cancer and sepsis, including population size and geographic location, were obtained for the period January 1999 through December 2024. Mortality records were identified using the CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death database, with both lung cancer and sepsis listed anywhere on the death certificate as underlying and/or contributing causes of death. Demographic variables, including sex and race/ethnicity, as well as regional characteristics such as urban–rural classification and state-level data, were extracted for the same period.

Race/ethnicity categories included non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, NH American Indian or Alaska Native, and NH Asian or Pacific Islander. These classifications are consistent with those used in prior CDC WONDER analyses and are based on information reported on death certificates in accordance with the U.S. Office of Management and Budget guidelines.

The National Center for Health Statistics Urban–Rural Classification Scheme was applied to categorize counties into metropolitan (large metropolitan areas with populations ≥ 1 million and medium/small metropolitan areas with populations 50,000–999,999) and non-metropolitan areas (population < 50,000), based on the 2013 U.S. Census. Geographic regions were defined according to U.S. Census Bureau classifications as Northeast, Midwest, South, and West.

Data synthesis

The age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 population for deaths involving both lung cancer and sepsis were calculated by standardizing mortality counts to the 2000 U.S. standard population. AAMRs were stratified by year, sex, race/ethnicity, state, census region, and metropolitan status. These stratifications were feasible because the CDC WONDER database provides population estimates across demographic, geographic, and age-specific subgroups for each year analyzed. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for AAMRs were obtained directly from the database. State-level and metropolitan status analyses were restricted to 1999–2020 because these variables were not available in the CDC WONDER datasets for 2021–2024.

Temporal trends in age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were evaluated using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0; National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD). Joinpoint regression models were used to identify significant changes in mortality trends over time. A minimum of 0 and a maximum of 4 joinpoints were permitted. Model selection was performed using the Weighted Bayesian Information Criterion (Weighted BIC) method. Analyses incorporated heteroscedastic errors using standard errors provided with the age-adjusted mortality rates. Annual percent changes (APCs) and average annual percent changes (AAPCs) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using empirical quantile confidence intervals based on 5001 resamples. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05.

Use of artificial intelligence tools

A large language model, ChatGPT, was used to assist with language editing and improving clarity of the manuscript. The author reviewed and verified all content and takes full responsibility for the accuracy and integrity of the manuscript.

Results

Between 1999 and 2024, a total of 109,864 deaths were recorded among adults aged ≥ 45 years in whom both lung cancer and sepsis were listed as underlying or contributing causes (Supplementary Table 1). Data on place of death were available for 108,960 cases (Supplementary Table 2). Most deaths occurred in medical facilities (94.8%), followed by decedents’ homes (4.1%), hospices (3.1%), and nursing homes/long-term care facilities (2.2%).

Demographic trends in mortality

Adults aged ≥ 45 years with both lung cancer and sepsis had an overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) of 1.6 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.5–1.7) in 1999, which increased to 2.0 (95% CI: 1.9–2.0) in 2024. Temporal trend analysis demonstrated a significant increase throughout the study period from 1999 to 2024 (APC: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.09 to 1.45) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Age-stratified analysis demonstrated that older adults consistently experienced substantially higher mortality rates compared with middle-aged adults. Among middle-aged individuals, the AAMR increased modestly from 1.4 (95% CI: 1.3–1.5) in 1999 to 1.5 (95% CI: 1.4–1.5) in 2024. Temporal trend analysis showed a stable period from 1999 to 2013 (APC: 0.48; 95% CI: −0.84 to 1.04), followed by a significant increase between 2013 and 2016 (APC: 6.95; 95% CI: 2.31 to 9.08), and a subsequent decline from 2016 to 2024 (APC: −1.71; 95% CI: −3.36 to − 0.76). In contrast, older adults exhibited a higher and steadily increasing burden, with AAMR rising from 5.6 (95% CI: 5.4–5.9) in 1999 to 7.3 (95% CI: 7.1–7.5) in 2024. Mortality rates among older adults increased significantly throughout the study period (APC: 1.42; 95% CI: 1.25 to 1.60) (Supplementary Table 3).

Gender stratification

Over the study period, men consistently exhibited higher age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) than women. In 1999, the AAMR for men was 2.3 (95% CI: 2.2–2.4) compared with 1.1 (95% CI: 1.0–1.2) for women. By 2024, these rates were 2.4 (95% CI: 2.3–2.4) in men and 1.6 (95% CI: 1.5–1.6) in women (Supplementary Table 5).

Trend analysis showed that among men, AAMRs exhibited a non-significant increase from 1999 to 2013 (APC: 0.52; 95% CI: −0.92 to 1.19), followed by a significant increase from 2013 to 2016 (APC: 4.64; 95% CI: 2.61 to 5.72), and a significant decline from 2016 to 2024 (APC: −0.45; 95% CI: −1.12 to − 0.01) (Supplementary Table 4).

In contrast, among women, AAMRs increased significantly over the entire study period from 1999 to 2024 (APC: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.54 to 2.16) (Supplementary Table 4).

Overall, although mortality rates were consistently higher in men, temporal patterns differed by sex, with men demonstrating an initial stable period followed by a significant increase and subsequent decline, while women experienced a steady and significant increase throughout the study period.

Racial stratification

When stratified by race/ethnicity, age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were highest among non-Hispanic (NH) Black individuals, followed by NH White, NH Asian or Pacific Islander (API), NH American Indian or Alaska Native (AIAN), and Hispanic populations. In 2024, AAMRs were 2.6 (95% CI: 2.4–2.7) for NH Black or African American individuals, 1.9 (95% CI: 1.8–2.0) for NH Whites, 1.8 (95% CI: 1.6–2.0) for NH Asian or Pacific Islander individuals, 1.6 (95% CI: 1.0–2.5) for NH American Indian or Alaska Native individuals, and 1.1 (95% CI: 1.0–1.2) for Hispanics (Supplementary Table 6).

Over time, AAMRs among NH Black or African American individuals remained consistently elevated with moderate fluctuations. NH White individuals demonstrated a gradual increase followed by stabilization in later years. NH American Indian or Alaska Native rates showed variability throughout the study period, with fluctuations in later years. NH Asian or Pacific Islander and Hispanic populations consistently exhibited lower mortality rates throughout the study period.

Overall, significant racial and ethnic disparities persisted, with NH Black individuals experiencing the highest mortality burden (Fig. 2).

State-wise distribution

Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) varied substantially across states, ranging from 0.75 (95% CI: 0.51–1.01) in Utah to 3.98 (95% CI: 3.54–4.41) in Kentucky (Supplementary Table 7). States in the highest 90th percentile, including Kentucky, Mississippi, Alaska, Oklahoma, and Arkansas, exhibited markedly higher AAMRs compared with those in the lowest 10th percentile, such as Utah, Wyoming, Hawaii, New Mexico, and Oregon (Fig. 3).

Census region

Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were highest in the Southern region, followed by the Midwest, Northeast, and West. In 2024, AAMRs were 2.2 (95% CI: 2.2–2.3) in the South, 2.1 (95% CI: 2.0–2.2) in the Midwest, 1.7 (95% CI: 1.6–1.8) in the Northeast, and 1.5 (95% CI: 1.4–1.6) in the West (Supplementary Table 8). Over time, AAMRs demonstrated variable trends across regions, with an overall increase in recent years. The Southern region exhibited the highest mortality burden throughout most of the study period, while the Western region consistently maintained the lowest mortality rates (Fig. 4). Urbanization

Throughout the study period, age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) in non-metropolitan areas were comparable to those in metropolitan areas initially but became consistently higher in later years. In 1999, AAMRs were 1.6 (95% CI: 1.4–1.7) in non-metropolitan areas and 1.6 (95% CI: 1.6–1.7) in metropolitan areas, increasing to 2.5 (95% CI: 2.3–2.6) and 1.9 (95% CI: 1.8–1.9), respectively, by 2020 (Supplementary Table 9).

AAMRs increased in both groups over time, with a more pronounced rise in non-metropolitan areas, particularly after 2010. Overall, a widening urban–rural disparity was observed, with higher mortality in non-metropolitan populations in recent years (Fig. 5). Trends in mortality rates by individual disease type

Overall trends in lung cancer alone

The age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for lung cancer among adults aged ≥ 45 years decreased from 91.4 (95% CI: 91.0–91.9) in 1999 to 54.3 (95% CI: 54.0–54.6) in 2020 and further declined to 47.9 (95% CI: 47.7–48.2) in 2024 (Supplementary Table 10) [8, 9].

Overall trends in sepsis alone

The AAMR for sepsis among adults aged ≥ 45 years was 77.5 (95% CI: 77.1–77.9) in 1999 and 77.7 (95% CI: 77.4–78.0) in 2024. Temporal trend analysis demonstrated a stable mortality trend from 1999 to 2018 (APC: 0.11; 95% CI: −0.26 to 0.39), followed by a significant increase from 2018 to 2021 (APC: 8.04; 95% CI: 3.99 to 9.89). Thereafter, mortality rates declined significantly between 2021 and 2024 (APC: −7.57; 95% CI: −11.08 to − 5.05) (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Tables 4 and 10).

Trend analysis showed that lung cancer mortality declined significantly over time, whereas sepsis mortality remained relatively stable through 2018, increased sharply during 2018–2021, and subsequently declined after 2021 (Fig. 6A and B, Supplementary Tables 4 and 10).

Gender trends in lung cancer alone

The AAMR for lung cancer was higher in men compared to women (e.g., 127.3 vs. 66.0 in 1999). Between 1999 and 2018, AAMR values declined among men, with the most pronounced decrease observed from 2013 to 2018 (APC: −4.88; 95% CI: −5.77 to − 4.38). Women demonstrated a modest increase from 1999 to 2002 (APC: 0.80; 95% CI: −0.24 to 2.21), followed by a significant decline from 2002 onward, including a marked decrease between 2014 and 2018 (APC: −3.99; 95% CI: −4.73 to − 2.32). Mortality rates continued to decline through 2024 among both men and women. From 2021 to 2024, AAMR decreased significantly among men (APC: −4.58; 95% CI: −5.66 to − 3.95), while women also experienced a significant decline during the same period (APC: −2.08; 95% CI: −2.55 to − 1.31) (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables 4 and 10).

Gender trends in sepsis alone

Like lung cancer, AAMRs for sepsis were higher in men compared to women (e.g., 89.6 vs. 69.2 in 1999). From 1999 to 2018, mortality trends remained stable in both sexes, with non-significant APCs of 0.12 (95% CI: −0.27 to 0.44) among men and 0.10 (95% CI: −0.29 to 0.42) among women. Between 2018 and 2021, AAMRs increased significantly in both men (APC: 9.06; 95% CI: 4.73 to 10.99) and women (APC: 6.72; 95% CI: 3.06 to 8.35). Thereafter, mortality rates declined significantly from 2021 to 2024 among men (APC: −8.53; 95% CI: −11.95 to − 5.94) and women (APC: −6.48; 95% CI: −9.97 to − 4.12) (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables 4 and 10).

Racial trends in lung cancer alone

Racial analysis of lung cancer showed that NH Black individuals had the highest AAMR (159.7 in 1999), while NH Asian or Pacific Islander individuals had the lowest (57.9 in 1999). AAMRs declined significantly across most racial and ethnic groups over time. Among NH White individuals, AAMRs decreased significantly from 2005 to 2013 (APC: −2.08; 95% CI: −2.39 to − 1.80), followed by a more pronounced decline from 2013 to 2018 (APC: −3.89; 95% CI: −4.75 to − 3.41). NH Black individuals experienced a sustained decline across multiple periods, including a marked decrease from 2013 to 2018 (APC: −4.84; 95% CI: −5.85 to − 4.16). Hispanic individuals also demonstrated significant declines across several periods, with the steepest decrease observed between 2014 and 2018 (APC: −4.94; 95% CI: −6.64 to − 2.27). Similarly, NH Asian or Pacific Islander populations showed overall declining trends, with the most pronounced decrease occurring between 2012 and 2019 (APC: −3.58; 95% CI: −4.79 to − 2.98), although mortality rates increased modestly from 2019 to 2024 (APC: 2.96; 95% CI: 0.72 to 6.18). NH American Indian or Alaska Native populations demonstrated relatively stable mortality through 2010, followed by a significant decline from 2010 to 2024 (APC: −3.64; 95% CI: −4.25 to − 3.16) (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 4 and 11).

Racial trends in sepsis alone

Racial analysis of sepsis revealed that NH Black individuals consistently recorded the highest AAMRs (e.g., 159.7 in 1999), while NH Asian or Pacific Islander individuals had the lowest (e.g., 57.9 in 1999). Following generally stable or declining trends through 2018 in most groups, AAMR values increased significantly between 2018 and 2021 in several populations, including NH Black individuals (APC: 10.14; 95% CI: 4.77 to 12.44), NH White individuals (APC: 7.00; 95% CI: 2.91 to 8.93), Hispanic individuals (APC: 13.10; 95% CI: 6.39 to 16.27), and NH American Indian or Alaska Native individuals (APC: 9.15; 95% CI: 4.11 to 12.49). NH Asian or Pacific Islander individuals demonstrated the most pronounced increase during this period, with mortality rising significantly from 2019 to 2022 (APC: 24.18; 95% CI: 13.50 to 29.34). Following these increases, mortality rates declined significantly in all racial and ethnic groups after 2021 or 2022, including NH Black individuals (APC: −8.96; 95% CI: −12.92 to − 6.08), NH White individuals (APC: −6.18; 95% CI: −10.97 to − 3.37), Hispanic individuals (APC: −13.45; 95% CI: −18.33 to − 9.74), NH American Indian or Alaska Native individuals (APC: −10.12; 95% CI: −19.04 to − 5.24), and NH Asian or Pacific Islander individuals (APC: −13.31; 95% CI: −24.11 to − 0.13) (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables 4 and 12).

Discussion

Our current analysis demonstrates that mortality associated with the co-occurrence of lung cancer and sepsis increased during the study period, although the magnitude and pattern of change varied over time. This pattern reflects the increasing clinical burden of managing individuals with cancer complicated by severe infection. Patients with lung cancer may be particularly vulnerable to severe infection because of tumor-associated immune dysfunction, treatment-related immunosuppression, frequent healthcare exposure, and other clinical factors reported in prior studies. These factors increase susceptibility to severe infections. The increase in mortality observed in this study therefore highlights the complex interaction between malignancy and systemic infection. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that sepsis remains a major cause of death among patients with cancer and is associated with worse outcomes compared with individuals without malignancy [6]. The gradual increase in mortality over time may partly reflect improved recognition and documentation of sepsis, advances in diagnostic coding, and enhanced clinical reporting practices. In addition, an aging population, increasing numbers of individuals living with lung cancer, and prolonged survival resulting from advances in cancer treatment have likely expanded the population at risk for infectious complications. Although progress in cancer therapy has improved survival, the burden of infection-related mortality remains substantial, indicating that this population continues to be at elevated risk [2]. Interpretation of long-term mortality trends should consider evolving sepsis definitions and coding practices. Increased awareness of sepsis, implementation of sepsis quality-improvement initiatives, revisions in diagnostic criteria, including the introduction of Sepsis-3 definitions, and improvements in clinical documentation may have increased the likelihood that sepsis was recorded on death certificates over time. Consequently, part of the observed increase may reflect changes in ascertainment and reporting rather than a true increase in disease burden. Temporal trend analysis demonstrated an overall increase in mortality across the study period, with periods of accelerated growth followed by more recent stabilization and modest decline. These patterns may reflect changes in disease epidemiology, health care utilization, treatment approaches, and improvements in sepsis recognition and management. Nevertheless, the persistent mortality burden suggests that current advances have not been sufficient to eliminate the excess risk among individuals with underlying lung cancer.

Differences between men and women were also observed, with consistently higher mortality rates among men. This observation has been reported previously in both lung cancer and sepsis literature and may be attributable to a higher prevalence of risk factors such as smoking, occupational exposures, and comorbid conditions among men. Biological differences, including variations in immune responses and hormonal influences, may also contribute. The present findings are consistent with previous studies reporting similar sex-based disparities [1].

The racial and ethnic differences identified in this study underscore the importance of socioeconomic determinants of health in influencing outcomes. Mortality rates were highest among non-Hispanic Black individuals throughout much of the study period. These disparities may be related to differences in health care access, delays in diagnosis, variations in treatment, and broader socioeconomic factors. Insurance coverage, access to specialty care, and geographic availability of health care services continue to influence outcomes across racial and ethnic groups. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating persistent disparities in both cancer-related outcomes and sepsis mortality [4].

Geographic variation across states also suggests important inequities in health care delivery. States and regions with higher mortality rates may have reduced access to specialized oncology services, critical care resources, and timely emergency treatment. In addition to differences in health care infrastructure, shortages of health care professionals in rural and underserved communities may contribute to delays in diagnosis and management. Variations in public health policies, environmental exposures, population demographics, and underlying health status may further influence these patterns. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated substantial regional variation in mortality from both infectious and chronic diseases [10].

These findings suggest that management strategies should be more coordinated and targeted toward high-risk populations. Early recognition of sepsis among patients with lung cancer, strengthened infection prevention measures, and closer collaboration between oncology and critical care teams remain essential. Reducing mortality in this vulnerable population will also require improvements in health care access, policy-level interventions, and focused public health efforts aimed at addressing persistent demographic and geographic disparities.

Future directions

These findings emphasize the need for targeted strategies to address disparities in mortality related to lung cancer and sepsis. Efforts should focus on improving access to care, reducing demographic, geographic, and urban–rural disparities, and optimizing management of high-risk populations. Further research is needed to better understand the factors contributing to these mortality trends and to develop interventions aimed at reducing mortality and improving outcomes among individuals with coexisting lung cancer and sepsis.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on death certificates and ICD-10 coding may result in misclassification or underreporting of lung cancer and sepsis as causes of death. Because death certificates may contain multiple underlying and contributing causes of death, the presence of both lung cancer and sepsis on a death certificate does not establish that either condition directly caused death. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as mortality involving coexisting lung cancer and sepsis rather than mortality directly attributable to either condition.

Second, changes in diagnostic practices and increased recognition of sepsis over time may influence observed mortality trends independent of true disease burden. Third, the CDC WONDER database lacks detailed clinical information, including disease severity, lung cancer stage, source of infection in sepsis, treatment modalities, and comorbid conditions, which may confound mortality estimates.

Additionally, differentiation between specific sepsis syndromes or histological subtypes of lung cancer is not possible using this database, as these variables are not consistently captured in death certificate records. Important baseline characteristics, including smoking status, immunosuppression, cancer treatment history, socioeconomic factors, and other comorbidities, could not be assessed. This limitation applies to all studies utilizing this dataset. Furthermore, although mortality trends demonstrated substantial changes during the COVID-19 pandemic period, the direct contribution of SARS-CoV-2 infection to lung cancer- and sepsis-related mortality could not be quantified using the available data. Finally, death certificate data are subject to reporting variability across geographic regions and over time, which may influence observed differences in mortality rates.

Conclusions

Mortality among adults aged ≥ 45 years with coexisting lung cancer and sepsis demonstrated evolving trends between 1999 and 2024. Overall mortality increased over the study period, with periods of accelerated growth followed by recent stabilization and modest decline. Higher mortality rates were consistently observed among non-Hispanic Black individuals, males, residents of the Southern region, and individuals living in non-metropolitan areas.

These findings highlight persistent demographic and geographic disparities in lung cancer– and sepsis-related mortality and underscore the need for targeted public health interventions, improved access to care, and optimized management strategies to reduce mortality and improve outcomes in high-risk populations. These findings should be interpreted as mortality involving coexisting lung cancer and sepsis rather than evidence that sepsis directly caused the observed increase in mortality.
