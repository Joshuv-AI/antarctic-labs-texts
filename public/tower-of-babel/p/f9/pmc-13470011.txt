The BZR1-BLH1-PIF4 feedback module acts as a molecular amplifier for precise thermomorphogenesis in Arabidopsis
Authors: Wenqi Qin, Qi Yin, Ning Wang, Yige Pan, Sihan Meng, Genji Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global increases in the intensity and frequency of elevated temperatures is threatening ecosystem stability and crop yield. Understanding plant thermomorphogenesis is critical for developing climate-resilient crops, yet the underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified. Here, we identify the BEL1-LIKE HOMEODOMAIN transcription factor BLH1 as a critical negative regulator of thermomorphogenesis that modulates the key BRASSINAZOLE-RESISTANT 1 (BZR1)-PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) thermomorphogenic regulatory module. Overexpression of BLH1 or its homologs confers high-temperature (HT) insensitivity, whereas blh higher-order mutants exhibit HT hypersensitivity. BLH1 expression is directly repressed by BZR1 and is down-regulated by HT. We further demonstrate that BLH1 directly binds to the PIF4 promoter to repress its transcription and concurrently interacts with the PIF4 protein to inhibit its activity. Overexpression of BLH1 rescues the elongated hypocotyl phenotype in bzr1-1D or PIF4 overexpression plants. Our findings define a BZR1-BLH1-PIF4 regulatory axis that modulates the BZR1-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 positive feedback loop, ensuring a balanced thermomorphogenic response to HT.

Introduction

Global temperatures are rising at an unprecedented rate, posing a significant threat to global ecosystems and agricultural productivity1–3. As sessile organisms, plants have evolved sophisticated mechanisms to perceive temperature fluctuations and integrate these signals into their developmental programs, a process crucial for adaptation. Among these adaptive strategies, thermomorphogenesis refers to a suite of morphological changes triggered by elevated ambient temperatures, such as elongation of hypocotyls, petioles, and internodes, leaf hyponasty, and shifted flowering time4,5. The thermomorphogenesis are not merely passive responses but represent a vital survival strategy. For instance, the elongation of petioles and upward leaf movement (hyponasty) increases the spaces within the canopy and leaf distance from the soil surface. These morphological changes enhance air circulation and potentially reduce heat absorption, thereby facilitating leaf cooling through transpiration and helping plants acclimate to elevated ambient temperatures6,7. However, the adaptive value of thermomorphogenesis comes with physiological trade-offs. This reprogramming forcibly alters carbon source allocation within the plant8, often diverting resources away from storage organs or reproductive structures. Consequently, while promoting survival under elevated ambient temperatures, thermomorphogenesis can ultimately compromise crop yield and quality9. Therefore, understanding the molecular basis of thermomorphogenesis is paramount for developing climate-resilient crops that can balance the dual demands of thermal adaptation and agricultural productivity in a warming world.

Genetic analyses have identified key molecular players governing thermomorphogenesis, with the bHLH transcription factor PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) serving as a central regulatory hub. The essential role of PIF4 is evidenced by the pif4 mutants, which exhibit a near-complete abolition of thermomorphogenic responses under high-temperature (HT, 28 °C)10. The initiation of this signaling cascade involves thermosensory mechanisms. Phytochrome B (PhyB) functions as a primary thermosensor, with HT triggering its conversion from the active nuclear Pfr form to the inactive cytoplasmic Pr form, thereby relieving its light-dependent inhibition of PIF411–14. The accumulation and activity of PIF4 are precisely modulated by an intricate network that integrates environmental and internal cues. The circadian-regulated evening complex (EC), comprising EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), ELF4, and LUX ARRHYTHMO (LUX), represses PIF4 expression during the night. This repression is lifted under HT, partly through B-BOX DOMAIN PROTEIN 18 (BBX18)- and BBX23-mediated degradation of ELF315–19. Interestingly, ELF3 itself acts as a thermosensor via its prion-like domain20. Light signaling pathways further fine-tune PIF4 activity. Blue light receptor CRYPTOCHROME 1 (CRY1) and UV-B receptor UV-B RESISTANCE 8 (UVR8) antagonize PIF4 activity through direct interaction or by stabilizing repressors, such as LONG HYPOCOTYL IN FAR-RED 1 (HFR1), ensuring growth remains balanced with light conditions21–23. Moreover, PhyB inactivation alleviates BRASSINOSTEROID-INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2)-mediated phosphorylation and degradation of PIF424. The downstream basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5) directly represses PIF4 transcription and competes with PIF4 for target gene binding, while CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1)-mediated degradation of HY5 under HT enhances PIF4 stability4,25–27. Hormonal signals are also integrated. HT promotes the degradation of DELLA proteins, thus releasing their repression of the transcriptional activity of PIF428,29. Once stabilized and activated, PIF4 orchestrates thermomorphogenesis by directly activating a suite of genes that promote cell elongation. It potently induces auxin biosynthesis genes and signaling components, thereby elevating auxin levels and responsiveness30,31. The functional importance of this pathway is confirmed by the ability of SMALL AUXIN UP RNA 19 (SAUR19) overexpression to rescue the hypocotyl elongation defect in the pif4 mutant30. PIF4 also directly activates cell elongation-related genes, such as PACLOROBUTRAZOL RESISTANCE 1 (PRE1), LONGIFOLIA 1 (LNG1), and SPEECHLESS (SPCH), ultimately driving the morphological adaptations characteristic of thermomorphogenesis32–34.

While multiple pathways converge to suppress PIF4 and prevent excessive thermoresponse, few key positive regulators have been identified. The brassinosteroid-activated transcription factor BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1) acts as a critical co-regulator enhancing PIF4 function32. BZR1 functions within an important positive feedback loop. HT promotes nuclear accumulation of BZR1, enabling BZR1 to directly bind the PIF4 promoter and enhance its transcription, while also physically interacting with PIF4 protein to form a potent transcriptional activation complex32,35. The BZR1-PIF4 module co-regulates a vast set of target genes, including key auxin biosynthetic genes. The resulting auxin, in turn, promotes brassinosteroid biosynthesis, further reinforcing BZR1 activity and establishing a positive feedback circuit that robustly drives thermomorphogenic growth32,35,36.

BEL1-LIKE HOMEODOMAIN (BLH)/BELL transcription factors constitute a subfamily within the three-amino-acid-loop-extension (TALE) family proteins37,38. The genome of Arabidopsis thaliana encodes thirteen BLH/BELL members, which typically recognize and bind to the TGACAGG/CT motif in the promoters of their target genes39. BLH proteins contain a conserved SKY domain at the N-terminus, followed by a central BELL domain and a homeodomain (HD) that facilitates DNA binding39,40. The SKY and BELL domains together form the MID domain, mediating protein–protein interactions. The MID domain enables homodimerization as well as heterodimerization with other proteins, thereby expanding the functional versatility of BLH transcription factors41–43. Although BLH/BELL transcription factors are emerging as regulators of plant development and response to environmental cues38,43, their specific function in thermomorphogenesis has not been elucidated.

Previous studies from our group and others have demonstrated that CINCINNATA (CIN)-like TEOSINTE BRANCHED 1/CYCLOIDEA/PCF 5 (TCP5), TCP13, and TCP17 positively regulate PIF4 expression and further form complexes with PIF4 to reinforce its function44,45. Moreover, we identified the WRKY transcription factor ABNORMAL THERMOMORPHOGENESIS 1 (ABT1) as an interactor of TCP5, which fine-tunes PIF4 activity by disrupting the TCP5-PIF4 complex46. In this study, we show that BLH1 and its six close homologs function as critical negative regulators of thermomorphogenesis downstream of BZR1. The expression of BLH genes is directly repressed by BZR1. Overexpression of BLH1 or any of its homologs leads to shortened hypocotyls and/or reduced leaf hyponasty under HT, whereas the disruption of BLH genes in a quadruple mutant blh1 blh5 blh6 blh7 (blhQ) and a septuple mutant blh1 blh5 blh6 blh7 blh3 blh10 blh11 (blhS) causes elongated hypocotyls and enhanced leaf hyponasty under HT. Furthermore, we demonstrate that BLH1 directly represses PIF4 expression and physically interacts with PIF4 to inhibit its protein activity. Additionally, BLH1 disrupts the formation of the PIF4-TCP5 transcriptional activation complex. Our findings establish BLH1 as a key negative regulator of thermomorphogenesis and reveal a BZR1-BLH1-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 feedback loop that finely modulates plant responses to HT.

Results

BZR1 directly represses BLH1 and its homologs during plant thermomorphogenesis

BZR1 is a central regulator of plant thermomorphogenesis. Under HT, BZR1 accumulates in the nucleus, where it activates PIF4 and establishes a positive feedback loop to amplify thermomorphogenic responses32,35. To identify novel regulators of thermomorphogenesis, we analyzed genome-wide BZR1 binding sites using publicly available ChIP-seq data GSE23341647. The results revealed significant BZR1 enrichment in the promoter region of BLH1 (Fig. 1a). Phylogenetic analysis classified BLH1 within the TALE transcription factor superfamily, forming a distinct BELL clade together with BLH3, BLH5, BLH6, BLH7, BLH10, BLH11, ATH1, BLH8, BLH9, BEL1, BLH2, and BLH4 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Interestingly, BZR1 was enriched in the promoters of all these BLH genes with the exception of BLH8 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1b), suggesting that BLH genes may function downstream of BZR1-mediated thermomorphogenic signaling. To investigate the functional effects of BZR1 binding, we examined the expression levels of all the BELL clade genes in the dominant gain-of-function bzr1-1D mutant under both HT (28 °C) and normal temperature (20 °C) conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The results showed that the expression levels of BLH1, BLH3, BLH5, BLH6, and BLH10 were significantly decreased in bzr1-1D compared to WT controls at both 28 and 20 °C, whereas BEL1 expression was increased at 20 °C (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). At 28 °C, BLH7 were also markedly down-regulated in bzr1-1D, while BEL1 was up-regulated (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2c). The other BELL family genes showed no expression changes in bzr1-1D at both temperatures (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). To further evaluate BZR1-mediated transcriptional repression, we generated reporter constructs in which the firefly luciferase (LUC) gene was driven by the promoters of BLH1, BLH3, or BLH10. As a negative control, we used the promoter of BLH4, which belongs to a distinct subclade of the BELL gene family and is not repressed by BZR1 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Each construct also contained the Renilla luciferase (REN) gene under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter, which served as an internal normalization control. For effector constructs, we used 35S-BZR1 expressing BZR1 and 35S-GAL4BD expressing the GAL4 DNA-binding domain as a negative control. Transient expression assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts revealed that BZR1 significantly repressed the promoter activities of BLH1, BLH3, and BLH10 at both 28 and 20 °C, whereas no repression was observed for the negative control BLH4 promoter (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of BZR1 on the BLH1 and BLH3 promoters was significantly enhanced at 28 °C compared to 20 °C (Fig. 1c).

BZR1 is a central transcription factor in the brassinosteroid (BR) signaling pathway32,35. To investigate whether BLH1 genes are also regulated by BR signaling, we examined the effect of BR treatment on the transcript levels of BLH genes. Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis showed that treatment with 0.1 μM BL for 1 h or 6 h significantly down-regulated the expression of BHL1, BLH3, BLH5, BLH6, BLH7, BLH10, and BLH11 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To further investigate the expression patterns and transcriptional repression of BLH genes under HT, we cloned the promoter regions of all BLH genes, including a 2360-bp BLH1 promoter, a 3327-bp BLH3 promoter, a 2512-bp BLH5 promoter, a 2356-bp BLH6 promoter, a 2138-bp BLH7 promoter, a 1792-bp BLH10 promoter, and a 4155-bp BLH11 promoter. These promoters were used to drive the β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene to generate BLHxpro-GUS constructs. More than thirty independent transgenic lines were obtained for each construct, showing consistent GUS staining patterns. Representative stable lines were selected for detailed analysis. GUS histochemical assays revealed that BLH1, BLH3, BLH6, BLH7, and BLH11 were highly and ubiquitously expressed in 5-day-old or 6-day-old seedlings, while BLH10 showed predominant expression in hypocotyls and roots, and BLH5 was primarily expressed in shoot apical meristems with lower expression in hypocotyls (Fig. 1d–j and Supplementary Fig. 4a–g). Interestingly, HT treatment for 1 or 2 days significantly reduced the expression of BLH genes, particularly in the elongation zones, including hypocotyls and cotyledon petioles (Fig. 1d–q and Supplementary Fig. 4a–n). To validate the results, we further examined GUS activity in BLHxpro-GUS lines under both high and normal temperatures. The quantitative analysis confirmed that GUS activity in BLHxpro-GUS plants was significantly decreased after two days of HT treatment compared to that under normal temperature (Supplementary Fig. 4o). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed the rapid downregulation of BLH1 expression within 1–4 h of HT exposure (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Collectively, these results suggest that BZR1 directly binds to and represses the expression of these BLH genes, and BLHs may play redundant and critical roles in plant thermomorphogenesis.

Overexpression of BLH genes confers HT insensitivity during plant thermomorphogenesis

To investigate the redundant roles of BLH genes in plant thermomorphogenesis, we generated 35S-BLHx-GFP constructs by fusing the coding sequences of BLH1, BLH3, BLH5, BLH6, BLH7, BLH10, or BLH11 with the GFP reporter gene under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. Each construct was transformed into WT plants. Among the independent transgenic lines obtained, clear thermomorphogenic defects including inhibited petiole elongation, repressed hypocotyl elongation, and increased cotyledon angle under HT were observed in 32 out of 98 35S-BLH1-GFP lines, 23 out of 72 BLHpro-BLH1-GFP lines, 22 out of 68 35S-BLH3-GFP lines, 21 out of 55 35S-BLH5-GFP lines, 26 out of 67 35S-BLH6-GFP lines, 16 out of 47 35S-BLH7-GFP lines, 26 out of 54 35S-BLH10-GFP lines, and 15 out of 50 35S-BLH11-GFP lines. From these, two stable independent lines per construct were selected for subsequent detailed analysis. Western blot and RT-qPCR analysis confirmed successful expression of the respective BLH genes in all transgenic lines (Supplementary Fig. 6). Phenotypic characterization revealed that HT treatment induced characteristic thermomorphogenic responses in WT plants, including hypocotyl elongation and hyponastic cotyledon growth, as shown by the reduced angles between cotyledons. Two-way ANOVA and multiple comparisons for hypocotyl length and cotyledon angle revealed that under HT conditions, the stable independent transgenic lines overexpressing BLH1, BLH3, or BLH5 exhibited significantly shorter hypocotyls and significantly larger cotyledon angles than WT controls (Fig. 2a–d, i, j, and Supplementary Fig. 7a–f, k, l). These results indicate that BLH1, BLH3 and BLH5 suppress both hypocotyl elongation and hyponastic growth under HT. For BLH10, the stable independent overexpression lines (35S-BLH10-GFP L8 and L7) showed significantly reduced hypocotyl elongation compared to WT under HT (Fig. 2g, i, j, and Supplementary Fig. 7i, k, l). However, while the cotyledon angle of 35S-BLH10-GFP L8 was slightly but significantly larger than that of WT (Fig. 2g, i, j), the L7 line showed no significant difference (Supplementary Fig. 7i, k, l). These results suggest that BLH10 predominantly regulates hypocotyl elongation rather than cotyledon angle under HT. In contrast, the stable independent lines overexpressing BLH6, BLH7, or BLH11 displayed no significant differences in hypocotyl length relative to WT under HT, yet their cotyledon angles were significantly increased (Fig. 2e, f, h–j, and Supplementary Fig. 7g, h, j–l). These results indicate that BLH6, BLH7, and BLH11 specifically repress hyponastic growth in Arabidopsis seedlings under HT. Disruption of BLH genes enhances thermosensitivity

To validate the redundant functions of BLH genes in thermomorphogenesis, we generated multiple blh mutants using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. Two editing constructs were designed to target the two subclades, including either BLH1, BLH5, BLH6, and BLH7 or BLH3, BLH10, and BLH11 (Supplementary Fig. 9). Successful mutagenesis yielded a quadruple mutant blh1 blh5 blh6 blh7 (blhQ) and a triple mutant blh3 blh10 blh11 (blhT). In blhQ, frameshift mutations including a 1 bp insertion in BLH1, a 2 bp deletion in BLH5, a 370 bp deletion coupled with a 17 bp insertion in BLH6, and a 1 bp deletion in BLH7 resulted in premature translational termination for each gene. Similarly, blhT carried a 1 bp insertion and deletion in BLH3, a 1 bp insertion in BLH10, and a 1 bp deletion with a 1 bp insertion in BLH11, all leading to truncated proteins (Supplementary Fig. 9).

We then investigated thermomorphogenic responses in these mutants. Under HT, WT hypocotyls elongated significantly, while PIF4-overexpressing (35S-PIF4-FLAG) lines exhibited even greater elongation. In contrast, the pif4-2 loss-of-function mutant showed no significant response (Fig. 3a–c, f). Similarly, hyponastic cotyledon growth was pronounced in WT and even more in 35S-PIF4-FLAG under HT (Fig. 3a, b, g). To further reveal the redundancy of BLH genes, we generated a septuple mutant blh1 blh3 blh5 blh6 blh7 blh10 blh11 (blhS) via genetic crosses. Phenotypic and statistical analysis revealed that the hypocotyls, cotyledons and petioles of blhS were hyper-responsive to HT compared to WT (Fig. 3h–m and Supplementary Fig. 10). Interestingly, blhS produced fewer leaves than WT under HT (Supplementary Fig. 10g, j). These results indicated that BLH genes redundantly control plant thermomorphogenesis.

Our data indicate that BLH genes are directly repressed by BZR1, pivotal for BR signaling32,35 (Fig. 1). To investigate whether the function of BLHs affects BR signaling during thermomorphogenesis, we treated the septuple mutant blhS and BLH1-overexpressing lines, including two independent transgenic lines driven by either the 35S promoter or the native BLH1 promoter, with exogenous BR or the BR biosynthesis inhibitor brassinazole (BRZ). Under both normal and high temperatures, BL treatment promoted hypocotyl elongation in all genotypes, whereas BRZ treatment inhibited hypocotyl elongation (Supplementary Fig. 11). After BL treatment, the blhS mutants consistently produced longer hypocotyls than WT under HT, while BLH1-overexpressing lines exhibited shorter hypocotyls (Supplementary Fig. 11). These suggest that blhS mutants are more sensitive to BL treatment, whereas BLH1-overexpressing lines are less sensitive to BL. In contrast, following BRZ treatment, only blhS showed significantly longer hypocotyls than WT under HT (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating reduced sensitivity to BRZ inhibition. The hypocotyl lengths of BLH1-overexpressing lines under both temperatures, and of blhS under normal temperature, were comparable to WT (Supplementary Fig. 11), likely due to the already short hypocotyl baseline at normal temperature limiting further inhibition. These results suggest that BLHs act as important factors downstream of BZR1 during thermomorphogenesis.

We further generated BLH1pro-BLH1-GFP in which BLH1 fusion with GFP was driven by its own promoter. Complementation assays by transformation of BLH1pro-BLH1-GFP in blhQ and blhS rescued the hypersensitivity to HT during thermomorphogenesis (Fig. 3j–m), confirming the critical roles of BLH1 in this process.

BLH proteins modulate the transcriptional reprogramming during thermomorphogenesis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the negative regulation of BLH1 in plant thermomorphogenesis, we conducted comprehensive transcriptome analyses using the BLH1 overexpression line (35S-BLH1-GFP L1), the septuple mutant blhS, and WT controls under both normal (20 °C) and HT (28 °C) conditions. Principal component analysis confirmed the reproducibility among biological replicates in our RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data (Supplementary Fig. 12).

For the blhS mutant, 4022 DEGs were detected upon HT treatment, including 2035 up-regulated and 1987 down-regulated genes. To further identify the genes regulated by BLH1 under HT, we performed Venn diagram analysis of DEGs in different transcriptome comparisons (Fig. 4b). Given that hypocotyl elongation was strongly suppressed in the 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 line under HT, while WT seedlings exhibited pronounced HT-induced hypocotyl elongation, we mainly use their dataset to identify potential genes regulated by BLH1 under HT. First, we examined genes that were up-regulated in WT at 28 °C versus 20 °C and down-regulated in 35S-BLH1-GFP relative to WT at 28 °C. We further excluded DEGs between 35S-BLH1-GFP and WT at 20 °C, yielding 567 overlapping DEGs. We then analyzed genes that were down-regulated in WT at 28 °C versus 20 °C and up-regulated in 35S-BLH1-GFP relative to WT at 28 °C, after removing DEGs between 35S-BLH1-GFP and WT at 20 °C, identifying an additional 456 overlapping DEGs (Fig. 4b). In total, 1023 genes associated with thermomorphogenesis were obtained as potential BLH1-regulated genes under HT.

GO analysis of these genes down-regulated by BLH1 under HT revealed that these genes were enriched in pathways, such as: response to light intensity, response to auxin, response to red or far red light (Fig. 4c). Indeed, heatmap analysis showed that the expression levels of many key thermomorphogenesis-related genes, including PIF4, IAA19, YUC6, PRE1, PRE5, SAUR20-24, BBX18, and LNG2, were higher in blhS and lower in 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 compared with the WT during thermomorphogenesis (Fig. 4d). GO analysis of genes up-regulated by BLH1 under HT revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to ethylene response, red or far-red light signaling, and brassinosteroid response (Fig. 4e). Heatmap analysis further showed that several hormones- and light signaling-related genes including RAV1 (related to ABI3/VP1 1), WRKY46, WRKY70, RUP1 (repressor of UV-B photomorphogenesis 1), and BZS1/BBX20 were expressed at higher levels in the 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 and at lower levels in the blhS septuple mutant compared to WT under HT conditions (Fig. 4f). Six BLH1-mediated thermomorphogenesis genes including PIF4, IAA19, PRE1, PRE5, SAUR20, and SAUR23 were selected for validation by RT-qPCR. The results showed that the expression levels of these genes were higher in blhS than in the WT, but lower in 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 overexpression lines than in the WT after 4 h of HT treatment (Fig. 4g–l). Furthermore, under normal temperature, the expression levels of PIF4, IAA19, PRE1, SAUR20, and SAUR23 in 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 were also lower than those in the WT (Fig. 4g–l). These results demonstrate that BLHs regulate thermomorphogenesis by controlling the expression of PIF4 and its downstream genes.

BLH1 represses PIF4 function through transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation during thermomorphogenesis

To further investigate whether BLH1 regulates PIF4 post-transcriptionally, we first tested their physical interaction using yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays. The results showed that BLH1 directly interacts with PIF4 (Fig. 5c). Firefly luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments confirmed that BLH1 interacted with PIF4 in vivo (Fig. 5d, e). To examine the functional consequence of this interaction, we generated 35S-REN-PRE1pro-LUC and 35S-REN-IAA19pro-LUC reporters in which the promoter of PRE1 or IAA19, known as PIF4 direct targets, was used to drive the LUC gene, and REN was put under a CaMV 35S promoter as an internal control. The 35S-PIF4 or 35S-BLH1 effector construct were generated using a CaMV 35S promoter to drive PIF4 or BLH1. Transient expression assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts indicated that PIF4 strongly activated LUC reporter gene expression driven by the IAA19 or PRE1 promoter. Expression of BLH1 alone had no significant effect on LUC activity. However, when BLH1 was co-expressed with PIF4, the LUC activity was markedly reduced (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 14a). EMSA confirmed that PIF4 specifically bound to the G-box motif of IAA19 or PRE1 in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 14b), consistent with previous reports5,44. Co-incubation of PIF4 with BLH1 significantly attenuated this binding, whereas BLH1 alone showed no detectable binding to the G-box (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Moreover, sequence analysis revealed that neither the IAA19 nor the PRE1 promoter contains the 5′-TGACAGG/CT-3′ motif recognized by BELL subfamily proteins. Collectively, these results suggest that BLH1 does not compete with PIF4 for direct occupancy of target gene promoters. Rather, BLH1 likely interferes with the DNA-binding activity of PIF4 through physical interaction, thereby repressing PIF4-mediated transcriptional activation.

Previous studies have documented that BZR1 and TCP5 are well-established positive regulators of PIF4, both of which form a transcriptional activation complex with PIF432,35,44,45. To determine whether BLH1 regulates this complex, we tested the interaction between BLH1 and TCP5 or BZR1. Y2H showed that BLH1 interacted with TCP5 and its closed homologs, including TCP13 and TCP17, but not with BZR1 (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 15). LCI and Co-IP further confirmed that BLH1 was associated with TCP5 (Fig. 5h, i). To investigate whether BLH1 interferes with PIF4-TCP5 complex formation, we performed split-luciferase complementation assays in N. benthamiana leaves. Co-expression of PIF4-nLUC and TCP5-cLUC yielded high LUC activity, consistent with the previous results44. However, when 35S-BLH1-FLAG was co-expressed, LUC activity was significantly reduced (Fig. 5j, k), suggesting that BLH1 competitively disrupts PIF4-TCP5 complex formation. To further validate this result, we co-transformed the 35S-REN-PRE1pro-LUC reporter with different effector combinations in Arabidopsis protoplasts. As expected, PIF4 activated LUC expression, and TCP5 co-expression enhanced this activation as previously reported44. However, BLH1 co-expression abolished the transactivation activity of the PIF4-TCP5 complex (Fig. 5l). These findings suggest that BLH1 suppresses PIF4 activity also by disrupting its functional interaction with TCP5.

To test whether BLH1 counteracts PIF4 to mediate thermomorphogenesis, we generated 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 35S-PIF4-FLAG by genetic crosses. 35S-PIF4-FLAG displayed pronounced hypocotyl elongation and hyponastic cotyledons under HT, consistent with the previous reports. In contrast, the 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 35S-PIF4-FLAG double overexpression line displayed intermediate hypocotyl length and cotyledon angle relative to the two parental lines under both normal and high temperatures. These results indicate that BLH1 overexpression strongly suppresses PIF4-induced hypocotyl elongation and cotyledon angle reduction, while PIF4 overexpression significantly enhances the thermomorphogenic response of the 35S-BLH1-GFP L1 line (Fig. 6g–l and Supplementary Fig. 17). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis showed that the transcript levels of PIF4, IAA19, and PRE1 in these genetic backgrounds correlated well with the observed phenotypic changes under both temperature conditions (Supplementary Fig. 16d–f). Together, these genetic data provide evidence that BLH1 and PIF4 act antagonistically in the regulation of thermomorphogenesis.

To further investigate whether BLH1 inhibits the activity of TCP5-PIF4 complex, we introduced 35S-BLH1-GFP into the TCP5 overexpression plant 35S-TCP5-MYC. Two independent transgenic lines, 35S-TCP5-MYC 35S-BLH1-GFP L1/L3, were analyzed. Under HT, 35S-TCP5-MYC exhibited excessive hypocotyl elongation, while overexpression of BLH1 significantly rescued the excessive growth of hypocotyls (Fig. 6m–r). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the expression levels of PIF4, IAA19, and PRE1 in these genetic backgrounds were also consistent with the observed hypocotyl phenotypes under both temperature conditions (Supplementary Fig. 16g–i). These results indicate that BLH1 counteracts the TCP5 activity during thermomorphogenesis.

Collectively, our genetic analyses support the hierarchical relationship in which BZR1 modulates PIF4 activity by transcriptionally repressing BLH1 and its homologs, thereby modulating thermomorphogenic responses.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that BLH1 participates in the regulation of BZR1-PIF4-mediated thermomorphogenesis and acts as a critical repressor. Under HT, the expression of BLH family genes is directly suppressed by nuclear-localized BZR1 and is specifically down-regulated in hypocotyls and cotyledon petioles. Consistent with this repression, overexpression of BLH1 or any of its homologs conferred HT insensitivity, whereas loss-of-function mutants of BLH1 and its homologs (blhQ and blhS) exhibited elongated hypocotyls and enhanced hyponastic growth under HT. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that BLH1 modulates the expression of a set of thermomorphogenesis-related genes, including PIF4, YUC6, IAA19, PREs, and SAURs. Further molecular and genetic evidence indicated that BLH1 represses PIF4 transcription and physically interacts with PIF4 to inhibit its transactivation activity on downstream targets. Moreover, BLH1 disrupts the formation of the PIF4-TCP5 transcriptional activation complex, thereby attenuating PIF4 function. Based on these findings, we propose a model delineating the role of BLHs in thermomorphogenesis. HT promotes the nuclear accumulation of BZR135, which transcriptionally represses BLHs. This derepression relieves BLHs-mediated inhibition of PIF4 transcription and releases TCP5 to interact with PIF4, collectively enhancing PIF4 activity. The enhanced PIF4 function promotes auxin accumulation and upregulates auxin-related genes. Auxin, in turn, stimulates BR biosynthesis and reinforces BZR1 activity35, forming a positive feedback loop that amplifies thermomorphogenic growth (Fig. 7). Our results not only identify BLHs as a key regulator of thermomorphogenesis, but also position it downstream of BZR1 to reinforce the BZR1-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 circuit that ensures a robust response to elevated temperature.Fig. 7Working model of BLHs in forming the BZR1-BLHs-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 feedback loop during plant thermomorphogenesis.HT promotes the nuclear accumulation of BZR1, leading to enhanced repression of BLHs expression. This repression releases two key inhibitory effects of BLHs on PIF4 function. One is the transcriptional suppression of PIF4 by BLHs, and the other is the inhibition of the transactivation activity of the PIF4-TCP5 complex by BLHs at the protein level. The resulting enhancement of PIF4 activity promotes the expression of auxin-related genes (such as IAAs, PREs, YUCs, and SAURs), leading to auxin accumulation and further activation of BZR1 through BR biosynthesis and signaling. Together, these form a BZR1-BLHs-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 positive feedback loop that amplifies the thermomorphogenic response, ultimately driving rapid hypocotyl elongation and hyponastic cotyledon growth under HT.

One is the transcriptional suppression of PIF4 by BLHs, and the other is the inhibition of the transactivation activity of the PIF4-TCP5 complex by BLHs at the protein level. The resulting enhancement of PIF4 activity promotes the expression of auxin-related genes (such as IAAs, PREs, YUCs, and SAURs), leading to auxin accumulation and further activation of BZR1 through BR biosynthesis and signaling. Together, these form a BZR1-BLHs-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 positive feedback loop that amplifies the thermomorphogenic response, ultimately driving rapid hypocotyl elongation and hyponastic cotyledon growth under HT.

As sessile organisms, plants have evolved developmental plasticity to adjust their morphology and physiology in response to diverse environmental stimuli. Thermomorphogenesis represents a key morphological adaptation that enables plants to cope with elevated ambient temperatures48,49. Under HT conditions, plants balance growth and reproduction by fine-tuning their thermal responses, thereby optimizing energy allocation8,9,50. In Arabidopsis, this process is primarily coordinated by the transcription factor PIF4, which acts as a central hub integrating multiple endogenous and exogenous signals to ensure appropriate developmental adjustments under HT5,7,10,51. Consequently, the activity of PIF4 is tightly modulated by various regulators. Among these, BZR1 acts as a critical positive transcriptional regulator of temperature-induced PIF4 expression35. HT promotes PIF4 upregulation through BZR1, establishing a positive feedback amplification loop: BZR1-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1. Tight negative regulation of PIF4 at both transcriptional and protein levels is therefore essential to prevent unrestrained elongation driven by the amplification circuit. In this study, we identified a class of BLH transcription factors that function as direct negative regulators within this BZR1-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 feedback loop. We show that BZR1 directly represses BLH1 transcription, while BLH1 directly inhibits both PIF4 transcription and PIF4 protein activation. This hierarchical regulation enables BLH1 to fine-tune thermomorphogenesis under fluctuating temperatures. That is, under HT, BZR1 represses BLH1, thereby relieving its inhibition of PIF4 and promoting thermomorphogenesis; upon temperature decrease, reduced nuclear accumulation of BZR1 derepresses BLH1, which in turn suppresses the PIF4-auxin-BR cascade to curtail thermomorphogenesis. Thus, the BZR1-BLH1-PIF4-auxin-BR-BZR1 regulatory module adds a previously unrecognized layer of precise control over thermomorphogenesis, allowing plants to dynamically balance growth and energy allocation under varying temperature conditions.

BZR1 is a plant-specific transcription factor that transduces BR signaling into transcriptional responses, thereby regulating diverse physiological processes52,53. BZR1 directly modulates thousands of BR-responsive genes, acting as a transcriptional activator or repressor. One side, BZR1 activates the expression of growth-promoting genes (e.g., PRE1, IAA19, and SAUR-AC1) and BR-inactivating genes, such as BAS1, while on the other side, BZR1 represses BR biosynthetic genes like DWF4 and CPD54,55. The BR-response element (BRRE; 5′-CGTGC/TG-3′) was initially identified in the promoters of BR biosynthetic genes that are directly down-regulated by BZR156. BZR1 contains an ethylene response factor (ERF)-associated amphiphilic repression (EAR) motif, which is essential for recruiting the corepressor TOPLESS (TPL)/TPL-related proteins (TPRs)57. Recent studies indicate that BZR1 target genes with high-affinity binding sites are more likely to have TPL/TPRs recruited to their promoters, leading to transcriptional repression. For strong binding, the core motif requires at least an NN-BRRE sequence (5′-N1N2C3G4T5G6-3′), with C−1A0/T7G8 or C−1G0/C7G8 flanking bases being particularly favorable. This also explains the prevalence of BRREs in the promoters of BZR1-repressed genes58. When BZR1 acts as a transcriptional activator, it interacts with partner bHLH transcription factors, such as PIF4 and BIM132,59–61. However, the mechanism by which BZR1 cooperates with bHLH factors to strongly bind the promoters of BR-activated genes, most of which contain multiple E-box motifs (5′-C1A2N3N4T5G6-3′), remains elusive. This may involve the recruitment of specific coactivators and the exclusion of the TPL/TPR corepressors58. In this study, we identified a high-affinity BZR1 binding site (5′-C−1A0T1T2C3G4T5G6T7G8-3′) at the −33 bp position of the BLH1 promoter, along with a canonical BRRE site (5′-A−1A0C1A2C3G4T5G6C7A8-3′) at −400 bp. The findings suggest that BZR1 might bind tightly to the BLH1 promoter and recruit TPL/TPRs to repress BLH1 expression. Interestingly, some BLH1 overexpression lines exhibit a smaller compact rosette resembling that observed in BR-deficient mutants. Therefore, in addition to the repression of BLH1 during thermomorphogenesis, BZR1 may also suppress BLH1 during other developmental processes, highlighting a potential broader function of BLHs in plant morphogenesis.

Indeed, BELL/BLH family transcription factors are increasingly emerging as key regulators in a wide range of plant processes, including growth, development, and responses to environmental cues, such as light, salinity, temperature, humidity, and drought stress38,43. In Arabidopsis, BLH1 specifically modulates the high-irradiance response (HIR) of phytochrome A (PhyA). Under HIR conditions, the blh1 mutant exhibits a significantly smaller cotyledon angle compared to WT, suggesting BLH1 involvement in light-regulated morphological adjustments62. BLH1 also influences abiotic stress responses: the blh1 mutant shows reduced sensitivity to salt stress during germination and early seedling development, while overexpression of BLH1 leads to increased sensitivity to both ABA and salinity, accompanied by upregulation of ABA-responsive genes63. Beyond Arabidopsis, soybean GmBLH4 enhances seed tolerance to HT and high humidity64, while cotton GhBLH5-A05 promotes drought tolerance by activating GhRD20-A09 and GhDREB2C-D05 and interacting synergistically with GhKNAT6-A0365. In contrast, tomato SlBEL2 acts as a negative regulator of drought tolerance by repressing multiple drought-responsive genes66. Additionally, BELL/BLH members participate in auxin-mediated development. Overexpression of BREVIPEDICELLUS (BP) disrupts auxin distribution and impairs lateral root growth. BLH6, a potential BP interactor, mitigates the shoot and root developmental defects in BP overexpression lines when knocked out, indicating its role in auxin-dependent root development67. BLH6 also functions as a transcriptional repressor and, through physical interaction with KNAT7, enhances the repression of the REVOLUTA/interfascicular fiberless1 (REV/IFL1) gene, thereby co-regulating secondary cell wall biosynthesis68. Despite these multifaceted roles, the function of BELL/BLH transcription factors in thermomorphogenesis remains unexplored. In this study, we demonstrate that a subset of BLH genes are transcriptionally regulated by HT and act as repressors of PIF4 function at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, and thus regulate auxin biosynthesis and signaling during thermomorphogenesis. Our results are consistent with the previously established role of BLH6 as a transcriptional repressor and modulator of auxin function. Together, these findings support a possible model in which BLH proteins act as critical modulators of plant development and environmental responses by partnering with distinct factors to fine-tune hormone signaling pathways. Given their established roles in regulating auxin signaling, the potential functions of BELL/BLH transcription factors in processes like shoot branching, along with their conserved roles in crops, such as soybean, represent promising directions for future research.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used. The mutants, including blhT, blhQ, and blhS, were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing and genetic crosses. The pif4-2 (SALK_140393C) and bzr1-1D mutants were previously described by Han et al.44 and Wang et al.69, respectively. Seeds of WT and mutants were surface-sterilized by immersion in 75% (v/v) ethanol for 10 min, followed by two washes with 95% (v/v) ethanol. After air-drying under sterile conditions, the seeds were sown on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium. The plates were stratified at 4 °C for 3 days in darkness and then transferred to a growth chamber set at 20 °C under long-day photoperiods (16 h light/8 h dark). A light intensity of 120 μmol m−2 s−1 was provided using Philips F17T8/TL841 17-W fluorescent bulbs. Seedlings were later transplanted into soil and maintained under the same environmental regime.

Tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) seeds were directly sown in soil and grown in a greenhouse under conditions identical to those used for Arabidopsis.

Thermomorphogenic phenotype analysis

Thermomorphogenic phenotypes were assessed by measuring hypocotyl elongation and hyponastic growth (quantified as the cotyledon angle) in response to HT. For hypocotyl and cotyledon assays, 3-day-old seedlings grown at 20 °C were transferred to either 28 °C (HT) or 20 °C (control) incubators for 4 days before imaging. For petiole elongation analysis, 25-day-old plants initially grown at 20 °C were exposed to 28 °C or 20 °C conditions for 3 days, after which whole plants and individual leaves were photographed. Hypocotyl length, petiole length, and cotyledon angles were measured from the images using ImageJ software. All experiments included three independent biological replicates. Statistical significance between treatment groups was determined by one-way ANOVA, followed by post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.

Binary vector construction and plant transformation

The 35S-BLHx and 35S-BLHx-GFP constructs (BLHx represents BLH1, BLH3, BLH5, BLH6, BLH7, BLH10, or BLH11) were generated by cloning the corresponding coding sequences (with or without the native stop codon) into the pENTR™/TOPO® vector (Invitrogen). The CDS was amplified from cDNA using primer pairs BLHx-FC-F with either BLHx-FC-R (with stop codon) or BLHx-FC-R-ns (without stop codon). The resulting entry clones (pENTR-BLHx or pENTR-nsBLHx) were recombined via LR reaction with the destination vectors pK2GW7 or pB7FWG2 to produce the final binary vectors.

For BLHxpro-GUS constructs, promoter regions (ranging from 1792 bp to 4155 bp) of each gene were amplified from genomic DNA using primers BLHxpro-FC-F and BLHxpro-FC-R. The fragments were cloned into pENTR™/TOPO® to create pENTR-BLHxpro entry clones, which were then recombined with pB7GUSWG0 via LR reaction.

All constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 for subsequent Arabidopsis transformation via the floral dip method70 or for transient expression in tobacco leaves71. Primers are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from plant samples using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Code No. 9769) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase kit (Promega, Code No. M170A) in a 20 μL reaction volume. The resulting cDNA was diluted and used as a template for RT-qPCR with gene-specific primers (listed in Supplementary Data 1). The reactions were performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa) on a LightCycler 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Switzerland), as previously described72. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min; followed by 35–40 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. The relative quantification of gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method73, with ACT2 serving as the internal reference gene for normalization.

Histochemical staining and microscopy

For GUS staining, seedlings (untreated or heat-treated at 28 °C for 1–2 days) were fixed in ice-cold 90% acetone for 30 min, washed twice with phosphate buffer, and then incubated in GUS staining solution at 37 °C in the dark for 8 h. After clearing in 75% ethanol, samples were visualized under a Leica M205 FCA stereomicroscope.

Generation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutants

The blhT and blhQ were generated using a CRISPR/Cas9 system driven by an egg cell-specific promoter, as previously described74. Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting genomic sequences of BLHx were designed with the online tool CRISPR-P 2.0 (http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/cgi-bin/CRISPR2/CRISPR). To construct the blhT mutant, each pair of target oligonucleotides was annealed and cloned into the pAtU6 backbone to generate intermediate vectors carrying tandem sgRNA expression cassettes for each gene (pAtU6-BLH3-target1+2, pAtU6-BLH10-target1+2, and pAtU6-BLH11-target1+2). These cassettes were subsequently assembled into a single multigene tandem sgRNA vector (pAtU6-BLH3-target1+2-BLH10-target1+2-BLH11-target1+2) through multiple rounds of restriction enzyme digestion and ligation. For the blhQ mutant, a tandem sgRNA vector targeting BLH1, BLH5, BLH6, and BLH7 (pAtU6-BLH1-target1+2-BLH5-target1+2-BLH6-target1+2-BLH7-target1+2) was generated using the same strategy. In brief, annealed oligonucleotide pairs corresponding to each target site were sequentially cloned into the pAtU6-based intermediate vectors, which were then assembled into the final multigene construct via stepwise restriction-ligation reactions. The expression cassettes were then assembled into the pCAMBIA1300-UBQ10-Cas9-P2A-GFP binary vector via restriction enzyme digestion and ligation74. The final constructs were introduced into WT via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated floral dip transformation70. Genomic DNA was extracted from T1 transgenic plants, and the targeted regions were amplified by PCR with primers flanking the sgRNA sites (Supplementary Data 1). Mutations were verified by Sanger sequencing, and the resulting indel profiles were analyzed using the TIDE (tracking of indels by decomposition) algorithm.

After obtaining homozygous, Cas9-free blhT and blhQ mutants, the two lines were crossed to generate F1 progeny (blhT × blhQ). F1 plants were self-pollinated, and F2 seeds were collected. Based on the linkage analysis, the seven BLH genes segregate as three independent genetic units (three tightly linked genes on chromosome 1, three tightly linked genes on chromosome 2, and one gene on chromosome 4). The probability of obtaining a septuple homozygous mutant in F2 is about (1/4)3 = 1/64 ≈ 1.56%. To obtain at least one blhS septuple homozygous mutant with 95% confidence, a minimum of 191 F2 individuals is required. To ensure sufficient screening redundancy, a total of 1080 F2 seedlings were planted (10 growth trays × 18 pots per tray × 6 seedlings per pot). Under HT (28 °C for 3 days), approximately 40 individuals showing significantly elongated petioles were preliminarily selected for genotyping. Genotyping was first performed at three representative loci (BLH1 on chromosome 2, BLH3 on chromosome 1, and BLH6 on chromosome 4). Candidates confirmed to carry homozygous mutations at all three loci were then genotyped for the remaining four genes (BLH5, BLH7, BLH10, and BLH11). Following this screening strategy, six independent blhS septuple homozygous mutant lines were successfully isolated.

All primer sequences used for genotyping are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Yeast two-hybrid assays

Protein–protein interactions between BLH1 and PIF4, TCP5, TCP13, and TCP17 were investigated using a Y2H system. Bait vectors were generated by performing LR recombination between pENTR-BLH1 and the pDEST32 destination vector (Invitrogen). Prey vectors were similarly generated by recombining pENTR clones containing PIF4, TCP5, TCP13, or TCP17 with pDEST22. Bait and prey plasmids were co-transformed into the Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain AH109 using the lithium acetate method. An empty pDEST22 vector was co-transformed with each bait construct as a negative control. Transformed yeast cells were first selected on synthetic dropout (SD) medium lacking leucine and tryptophan (-Leu/-Trp) to confirm co-transformation. Protein–protein interactions were then assessed by plating the yeast on SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, and histidine (-Leu/-Trp/-His), supplemented with or without 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) to suppress basal HIS3 reporter expression.

Firefly luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay

Protein-protein interactions between BLH1 and PIF4 or TCP5 were tested in planta using the LCI assay in a transient tobacco (N. benthamiana) expression system. The binary vectors pCAMBIA1300-nLUC-GW and pCAMBIA1300-cLUC-GW were used to generate the fusion constructs. Specifically, the coding sequences for PIF4 and TCP5 were recombined with pCAMBIA1300-nLUC-GW to produce 35S-PIF4-nLUC and 35S-TCP5-nLUC, while the BLH1 and TCP5 sequence was recombined with pCAMBIA1300-cLUC-GW to yield 35S-cLUC-BLH1 and 35S-cLUC-TCP5. All constructs were individually transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Different combinations of bacterial cultures carrying these constructs were then co-infiltrated into tobacco leaves. The pair of 35S-GAL4BD-nLUC and 35S-cLUC was co-infiltrated as a negative control. After infiltration, plants were kept in darkness for 24 h and then transferred to a light cycle for 48 h. Prior to imaging, the infiltrated leaves were injected with 100 mM D-luciferin substrate and incubated in darkness for 10 min. Luciferase luminescence was captured using a low-light cooled charge-coupled device imaging system (NightOWL II LB 983, Berthold Technologies) controlled by indiGO software.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

Co-IP assays were performed to validate the protein–protein interactions between BLH1 and PIF4 or TCP5 in planta. We selected transgenic plants stably expressing the corresponding proteins: 35S-PIF4-FLAG, 35S-BLH1-GFP L1, 35S-PIF4-FLAG, 35S-TCP5-MYC, and 35S-TCP5-MYC 35S-BLH1-GFP L3. Total proteins were extracted from Arabidopsis seedlings using an ice-cold IP buffer (50 mM pH 6.8 Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 0.1% MG132, and 0.1% NP-40). The clarified lysates were incubated with either anti-MYC agarose beads (Sigma, Cat. No. E6654) or anti-GFP mAb-Magnetic Agarose (MBL, Cat. No. D153-10) for 3 h at 25 °C with gentle rotation. The beads were subsequently washed five times with IP buffer, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 4× SDS loading buffer at 100 °C for 10 min. The eluted proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, ISEQ00010). Immunoblotting was performed using the following primary antibodies: anti-MYC (ABclonal, Cat. No. AE070; diluted 1:5000), anti-GFP (BBI, Cat. No. D110008-0100; diluted 1:5000), and HRP-conjugated anti-FLAG (Sigma, Cat. No. A8592; diluted 1:10,000). A goat-anti-mouse HRP-conjugated antibody (CWbio, Cat. No. CW0102S; diluted 1:10,000) was used as the secondary antibody. Protein signals were visualized using chemiluminescence on a Tanon 5200 Multi imaging system.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) assays

ChIP-qPCR was performed as previously described75 to assess the in vivo binding of BLH1 to target genomic regions. Chromatin was isolated from 7-day-old seedlings of 35S-BLH1-GFP and 35S-GFP (negative control) grown at 20 °C. The sonicated chromatin extracts were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-GFP antibody (Abcam, Cat. No. ab290). Protein A-Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare, Cat. No. 17-1279-01) were used to capture the antibody-chromatin complexes. A portion of the sonicated chromatin, set aside prior to immunoprecipitation, served as the input control. After reversing the cross-links, the immunoprecipitated DNA was purified by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. The enriched DNA fragments were quantified by qPCR using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Data 1). The relative enrichment at each target locus was calculated as the percentage of input (IP/Input) and normalized to the ACT2 reference gene. The final enrichment in 35S-BLH1-GFP samples is presented relative to the background signal from the 35S-GFP control. Data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent biological replicates. Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided Student’s t test.

Transient dual-luciferase reporter assays

To investigate whether BLH1 represses the transactivation activity of PIF4, and whether BLH1 affects PIF4-mediated transactivation of its downstream target genes PRE1 and IAA19, a reporter construct containing the PIF4 promoter driving LUC expression was generated. The promoter region was amplified using primers PIF4pro-F and PIF4pro-R and cloned into pENTR™/TOPO® to create pENTR-PIF4pro. The final reporter construct, 35S-REN-PIF4pro-LUC, was produced via an LR reaction between pENTR-PIF4pro and pGreenII 0800-LUC-GW. The 35S-REN-IAA19pro-LUC and 35S-REN-PRE1pro-LUC were constructed using the same method described above. The effector plasmids 35S-GAL4BD, 35S-BLH1, 35S-TCP5, and 35S-PIF4 were generated by cloning the respective sequences into the pGreenII 62-SK vector using the FastClone method.

Arabidopsis protoplasts were isolated as previously described76. For the assay, protoplasts were co-transfected with 10 μg of plasmid DNA in different combinations. After transfection, the protoplasts were collected, resuspended in 1 mL of W5 solution, and incubated under mild light at room temperature for 14–16 h. Luminescence was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega) on a GloMax 20/20 luminometer. The LUC activity was normalized to the REN activity for each sample.

RNA-seq analysis

BLH1-overexpressing (35S-BLH1-GFP), blhS mutant and WT seedlings were grown at 20 °C for 6 days. A subset of the seedlings was then subjected to a 4 h HT treatment at 28 °C, while the remainder was maintained at 20 °C as a control. Total RNA was extracted from both treated and control seedlings using a Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China). RNA-seq libraries were prepared by Biomarker Technologies Corporation (Beijing, China) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end reads.

The raw sequencing data were processed and analyzed on the BMKCloud platform (international.biocloud.net). Reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana TAIR10 reference genome (www.arabidopsis.org). DEGs were identified using thresholds of a FDR < 0.01 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2.0. Venn diagrams were generated using Omicshare tools (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/) to compare DEGs across genotypes and conditions. Heatmaps were constructed with TBtools77 to visualize gene expression patterns. The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the genome sequence archive (GSA) of the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC), China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB), under BioProject accession number CRA029339.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism software (v.8.0). Data were analyzed via a two-sided Student’s t test (Microsoft Excel, for two-group comparisons) or one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (GraphPad Prism 8, for multi-group comparisons).
