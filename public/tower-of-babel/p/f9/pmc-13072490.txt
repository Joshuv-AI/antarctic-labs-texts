Treatment and prognosis of proximal humerus fracture in older adults: a retrospective cohort study using health insurance claims database
Authors: Akihiro Saitsu, Hayato Yamana, Yusuke Sasabuchi, Hiroki Matsui, Hideyuki Sasanuma, Tomohiro Matsumura, Yoshimitsu Wada, Takeshi Kanno, Hitoaki Okazaki, Takahide Kohro, Katsushi Takeshita, Hideo Yasunaga, Kazuhiko Kotani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe underlying general condition of older adults before proximal humerus fracture (PHF) and outcomes after PHF have not been described in detail. Furthermore, there is no consensus regarding the treatment of PHF in older adults requiring long-term care (LTC). This study assessed the LTC status and treatment in older adults with PHF and examined the changes in LTC status and death after PHF.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the medical and LTC insurance claims databases of a prefecture from 2014 to 2019. We included patients ≥ 65 years old with PHF and evaluated their LTC status and mortality, excluding those with an observation period of < 6 months before PHF. A survival time analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method.ResultsWe identified 887 eligible patients, including 452 (51%) in the surgical group and 435 (49%) in the non-surgical group. The proportions of patients requiring LTC were 42% and 52% in the surgical and non-surgical groups, respectively. In patients who did not require LTC, the proportion for surgery decreased with age, whereas in patients who required LTC, the proportion for surgery was highest in the 75- to 84-year-old group. The mortality rate was lower in the surgical group than in the non-surgical group among patients 75–84 years old who required LTC (log-rank p = 0.001; hazard ratio 0.16, 95% CI 0.04–0.56). In contrast, there was no significant association between treatment methods and mortality in the LTC status in the other age groups. The overall 1-year mortality after PHF was 7%, and an additional 20% of patients with PHF had worsening LTC 1 year after injury.ConclusionsAmong the older adults with PHF, 47% required LTC before the injury, and 26% had a worsening LTC status or died 1 year after PHF. The prognoses after surgery for PHF differed according to age and LTC status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07269-1.

Background

Proximal humeral fracture (PHF) is the third-most frequent fragility fracture among older adults after hip and wrist fractures [1]. The incidence of PHF has increased considerably over the past decade [2], with similar age-related trends and rising incidences reported worldwide [3–5]. Patients with PHF are generally regarded as frailer than those with wrist fractures but more robust than those with hip fractures [6]. Nevertheless, PHF causes considerable worsening of the general condition of older adults [7].

Previous studies have evaluated conditions before PHF and outcomes (e.g. institutionalization) after PHF [8, 9]. However, these studies have focused primarily on the physique, comorbidities, and mobility of older adults. Understanding the general condition of older adults requires additional perspectives, such as their cognitive function and adjustment to social life. However, data on the general condition of older adults before PHF, as well as their subsequent changes and death, are insufficient.

Treatment of PHF includes surgical treatments, such as open reduction and internal fixation, as well as arthroplasty, and non-surgical treatments, such as immobilization using an arm sling. Previous studies have reported an association between surgical and non-surgical treatments for PHF and mortality. However, whether or not treatment can help reduce mortality remains unclear [10–12]. Furthermore, studies on treatment modalities and long-term outcomes, such as the functional recovery, quality of life, and rates of reoperation or complications, have shown mixed results [12, 13]. Thus, there is a lack of consensus on the treatment for PHF, especially according to the age and condition of older adults.

In Japan, public long-term care (LTC) insurance provides services following standardized requirements that reflect the general condition of older adults. Thus, this study utilized a population-based database of medical and LTC insurance to evaluate the LTC status and treatment in older adults with PHF and examine the change in LTC status and deaths after PHF.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study included data recorded in an administrative database from June 2014 to February 2019 in Tochigi Prefecture. With around 30% of its population aged 65 or over, the Prefecture is one of Japan’s 47 prefectures with a demographic profile similar to the national average [14]. Approximately 700,000 residents are registered in these databases, which covers approximately 35% of the prefecture’s population [14].

The database includes information related to National Health Insurance and Late Elderly Medical Insurance [15, 16]. This database contains data on sex, birth date, the diagnosis (coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] in Japanese), prescriptions, and treatments, including surgery. Many variables, such as diagnoses, procedures, and LTC service use, are only recorded when present. When no corresponding event occurs, it is treated as non-occurrence.

The database also includes information on LTC Insurance. In principle, every Japanese person ≥ 40 years old is covered under LTC Insurance [15, 16]. Individuals ≥ 65 years are Category 1 insured persons, while people 40–64 years old with 16 predetermined specific diseases (Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, etc.) are Category 2 insured persons [17]. Individuals apply to their local municipality for LTC services, which are granted if they meet the certification criteria. The LTC certification is based on a standardised questionnaire assessing five areas: daily activities, physical function, cognitive function, adjustment to social life, and behavioural disorders. Additionally, the estimated time required for nine care-related activities, including grooming, bathing, eating, excretion, transfers, domestic assistance, instrumental activities of daily living, and behavioral problems related dementia, and medical services, was used to determine the appropriate LTC level. According to the total estimated LTC time per day, the level of LTC required is classified into seven stages: support level (SL) 1–2 and care needs level (CNL) 1–5 [16]. A previous study demonstrated a strong correlation between LTC levels and the Barthel Index in the Japanese LTC insurance system [18]. Individuals with SL1–2 or CNL1 have Barthel Index scores of approximately 85–95 (independence with minor help), those with CNL2–3 have scores of around 65–80 (partial dependency), and those with CNL4–5 have scores < 40 (full dependency) [18]. The correspondence between LTC levels and the Barthel Index is summarized in Additional File 1. In addition, the level of LTC and cognitive decline were found to be closely related in a previous study [19].

Residents of Tochigi Prefecture who are enrolled in the National Health Insurance (self-employed persons, retirees, and their dependents) or Late-stage Elderly Medical Insurance (all adults ≥ 75 years old) are registered in this database. Therefore, a large proportion of Tochigi Prefecture residents ≥ 65 years old was included. We linked the data in the medical and LTC services by using anonymized identification numbers assigned to each individual. Uniform health insurance applies to medical and LTC services under the Japanese system of universal health coverage through mandatory social insurance. Diagnoses and procedures were consistently recorded using standardized codes, such as ICD-10 and national procedure codes, thereby ensuring data completeness and reliability.

This population-based cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jichi Medical University (approval number: Clinical 22–202). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data. The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation and the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

We included patients diagnosed with PHF between June 2014 and February 2019 who underwent surgery or non-surgical treatment for PHF within 30 days of diagnosis. PHFs were identified using Japanese diagnosis codes under the ICD-10 diagnosis categories S42.1, S42.2, and S42.3 (a complete list provided in Additional File 2). Procedures were classified as surgery (open reduction and internal fixation or shoulder arthroplasty) or non-surgical treatment (closed reduction or immobilization such as sling use) according to national procedure codes (150019110, 150042610, 150047010,150352110, 150353210, 150050310, 150049410, 150049410, 150255910, 140040110, 150016610). Patients who had not been observed for at least 6 months before being diagnosed with PHF and those < 65 years old were excluded. When a patient received multiple surgical and non-surgical treatments for PHF during the study period, only the first episode was analyzed. All participants were followed until death or withdrawal from medical insurance.

Exposures and outcomes

The primary interest of this study was the effect of LTC needs before PHF on treatment and postoperative outcomes. Death within one year and LTC status one year after PHF were evaluated as outcomes. The following baseline characteristics were evaluated: sex, age, LTC needs before PHF, hospitalization [8], head trauma (ICD-10, S06) [8], femoral fracture (ICD-10, S72) [8], comorbidities based on the components of the Charlson Comorbidity Index using the algorithm by Quan et al. [20], and drugs based on the anatomical therapeutic chemical classification [21] within 6 months before PHF. The participants were grouped by age as follows: 65–74, 75–84, and ≥ 85 years old.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, whereas continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. First, the baseline characteristics of the surgical and non-surgical groups were evaluated. In addition, comorbid diseases were described according to age and requirement for LTC. The standardized mean difference was calculated to evaluate the balance in baseline characteristics between the groups, and < 10% was considered as balanced [22]. Next, the proportion of patients who received surgery and non-surgical treatment was evaluated in each age group between patients with and without preexisting LTC needs. The survival time until one year after injury was compared between the surgical and non-surgical groups using the Kaplan-Meier method, with stratification according to age and LTC needs. In addition, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated and plotted for all Kaplan–Meier survival curves. The log-rank test was used for intergroup comparisons. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CI were additionally calculated using univariable Cox models to complement the Kaplan–Meier analyses. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Finally, the survival and LTC status after one year were assessed in different treatment groups (surgical and non-surgical) and preexisting LTC needs (without LTC, with SL 1–2 or CNL 1, with CNL 2–3, and with CNL 4–5).

All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS software program, version 24.0 (Tokyo, Japan).

Results

A total of 887 patients ≥ 65 years old with PHF between June 2014 and February 2019 were selected from the database: 452 (51.0%) underwent surgery, and 435 (49.0%) received non-surgical treatment (Fig. 1). In the surgical group, 386 (85.4%) patients underwent open reduction and internal fixation, and 66 (14.6%) underwent arthroplasty.

Discussion

The present study of 887 older adults with PHF, using a database of medical and LTC insurance, found that 47% of patients were already in need of LTC before the injury and that 26% had a worsening care-needs level or had died one year after PHF. Differences in outcomes after PHF between surgical and non-surgical treatments varied according to age and LTC status. PHF surgery may be associated with reduced mortality rates in certain patient groups.

Our study is consistent with previous studies that have reported an association between functional impairment and death in older adults with PHF. A study of 8,888 older adults in Canada reported that older adults with functional limitations were at a high risk of fractures and that functional impairment may subsequently worsen their quality of life and activities of daily living [23]. The National Hospital Discharge in the United States Survey reported that 37% of patients admitted with PHF were discharged to facilities [8]. Age (≥ 75 years old) and comorbidities (e.g. dementia, obesity, congestive heart failure) were predictors of discharge to a facility, and comorbidities increased the risk of mortality after PHF [8]. A report from Finland also showed that PHF increased the risk of mortality, and the standardized mortality rate for patients with PHF was 2.2 (95% confidence interval: 1.8–2.6) [24]. In a Swedish study of 147,692 patients with PHF, the 1-year mortality rate after PHF was 8.5% [25], and another study reported that mortality after PHF in older adults was comparable to that of femoral neck fractures [26]. Although the results of the present study cannot be directly compared with those of other studies, it is evident that older adults with PHF generally have some level of functional limitations before the injury and that PHF increases the risk of death or worsened functional limitations.

In contrast to most countries, which often lack systematically linked information on formal LTC assessments, Japan’s universal LTC insurance system provides standardized, graded assessments of functional dependency. This structure allowed us to evaluate pre-fracture LTC needs as a determinant of treatment selection and postoperative outcomes [15]. The present study provides insights into the relationship between LTC needs and PHF outcomes in an aging society with a well-established LTC insurance system.

The present study showed that the surgical rate for PHF varied by patient age and whether or not they required LTC. Because postoperative complications increase with age [27], surgery may be avoided in older patients. Patients without pre-existing LTC need to follow this pattern. However, in patients with pre-existing LTC needs, the surgical rate was highest among patients aged 75–84 years. Some patients in this group may depend on walking aid devices and use their upper limbs to walk before injury [28]. Long-term immobilization of the upper limbs using conservative treatment may prevent them from walking. Thus, surgery may have been selected for early functional recovery in this group.

There is some controversy regarding treatment strategies for older adults with PHF. Our results of the survival time analysis comparing surgical and non-surgical treatments differed depending on age and the requirement for LTC. A previous randomized controlled trial reported no difference in mortality between surgical and non-surgical treatments [29]. This result might support our findings in patients 65–74 years old; indeed, the mean age of the patients was 66 years old in a randomized clinical trial. Notably, the present study found a significant difference between the treatment groups among those 75–84 years old who required LTC, the group with the highest surgical rate. A previous study reported that dependency due to long-term immobilization by conservative treatment was associated with an increased mortality rate in older adults [26]. Surgery helps to maintain upper-limb function, which is important for transfers, mobility with assistive devices, and independence in daily activities among older adults [26, 28]. Preservation of upper-extremity function through surgery may have contributed to better functional recovery and overall survival in this group of patients, particularly in those who rely on their upper limbs for walking. Nevertheless, confounding indications, such as surgeons opting for surgery for older adults with better health conditions, must be considered when interpreting the results of the present observational study. Finally, patients ≥ 85 years old who underwent surgery had fewer comorbidities than those who did not (Additional File 5). This finding implies that patients who underwent surgery generally had better conditions than those who did not. However, there was no marked difference in the survival time between the two groups. Surgery in very old adults is often accompanied by complications and is associated with a high risk of resurgery [30]. Therefore, surgery may have a negative impact on the mortality of such patients. Extra caution may be necessary when considering surgical treatment for patients 85 years old.

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty has recently been described as a new treatment strategy with good outcomes and low complication rates [12, 31]. However, arthroplasty accounted for only 14.6% of surgeries in our study. This low rate may be explained by the fact that reverse total shoulder arthroplasty was approved by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Welfare, and Labour in 2014 and that it had to be performed by certified surgeons; there may have been few surgeons certified for this method during the study period (2014–2019). Further research is necessary to examine changes after an increase in the number of cases treated using the new method.

The present study evaluated the LTC status before PHF, treatment method for PHF, and postoperative outcomes, including changes in the level of LTC needs. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate these factors. Furthermore, we used a large-scale, population-based database of medical and LTC insurance to conduct a longitudinal analysis. However, several limitations associated with the present study warrant mention. First, this was a retrospective observational study that used a health insurance claims database. We were unable to obtain data on the type and severity of PHF, both of which are important for determining the treatment strategy. In addition, there is insufficient data on the living conditions and economic status of patients. These factors may have influenced the treatment selection. Furthermore, this study did not examine post-operative conditions such as functional evaluation scores, the quality of life, and caregiver burden. These factors are also important when considering treatment strategies for elderly patients. It should also be noted that, as the database does not include older adults who remain enrolled in employment-based health insurance, selection bias cannot be ruled out, although this group is small in Japan. The variables used in this study may still be subject to measurement error, including miscoding, delayed updates of LTC certification levels and under-recording of comorbidities. This could have introduced non-differential misclassification. However, mandatory fields such as age, sex, dates of diagnosis, LTC levels and date of death contained no missing values. Second, the study cohort was limited to patients who received treatment for PHF, to enhance the reliability of the data. This may have excluded mild cases that did not require treatment, potentially influencing overall treatment distribution. Third, because there were few deaths within subgroups, multivariable regression analysis could not be performed. As a result, the survival analyses were limited to univariate methods, and it was not possible to adjust for confounding factors related to treatment selection. Fourth, this study was conducted in a single prefecture of Japan. The generalizability of our results to other regions that adopt different acute and long-term care systems may be limited; similar studies from other countries are needed.

Despite these limitations, however, our findings highlight the importance of considering functional status and individual characteristics when making treatment decisions for older adults with PHF. Further large-scale prospective studies with more detailed patient information would add to our findings to clarify the effects of different treatment strategies on outcomes and to optimize care for PHF.

Conclusions

This study examined the prognosis and treatment of PHF in older adults using a health insurance claims database. Approximately half of older adults with PHF were dependent on LTC before injury. One year after PHF, 26% had a worsened care-needs level or died. Treatment strategies and the prognosis differed according to age and LTC. Further studies are needed to elucidate appropriate treatment strategies for older patients with PHF under different conditions.
