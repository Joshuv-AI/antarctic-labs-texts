Thoracolumbar rachipagus parasitic twins with multiple congenital anomalies: A rare case report with review of literature
Authors: Namrata Bagchi, Poonam Sherwani, Kandikonda Pruthvi Raj, Nishant
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeteropagus, also known as parasitic twining, is the asymmetric twinning of conjoined identical or monozygotic twins. One severely deficient twin (the parasite) relies on the other twin's heart (the autosite) to survive. The circumstance only arises when parts of the body of the developed and delivered twin are joined to the undeveloped or underdeveloped twin. Very few case reports of this uncommon congenital defect have been published in the literature. Here, we describe a case of this atypical entity in a neonate in which the rudimentary pelvis and thigh of the parasite were attached to the autosite of the twin.Keywords: Autosite, Parasite, Rachipagus, Twin

Introduction

Greek terms rachi (spine) and pagus (fixed) are the source of the name "rachipagus," which refers to conjoined twins—linked dorsally in the spinal column, most likely connected by the vertebral arches, with the individual twins' distinct vertebral bodies on either side of a shared spinal canal. One occurrence for every 1 million live births is the estimate [1]. A survey of the literature finds that 27 cases have been documented to date [2].

Radiological imaging and identification of this entity is vital for multidisciplinary consultation teams and treatment, especially for neurosurgeons and Pediatric surgeons. Surgical techniques for separating conjoined twins can vary significantly in complexity based on the shared internal body parts and attachment points. For surgical separation planning, imaging using CT, MRI, angiography, or ultrasound is crucial [3]. The majority of instances that are publicized and reported are from developing nations. In another publication, prenatal sonography was used to identify a pygopagustetrapus parasitic twin successfully excised at 38 weeks of gestation [3]. Pygopagus are conjoined twins who are fused at the lumbosacral level and share a spinal canal. Tetrapus is one of the conjoined subtypes in which all 4 lower extremities are present.

Case report

An 8-month-old male patient presented to a tertiary hospital with complaints of abnormal limb-like soft tissue structures arising from the pelvis since birth. Parents give a history of nonconsanguineous marriage. Physical examination revealed a soft skin-covered mass with a bony component on the pelvic and lumbosacral regions of the child, which had a bony component within and extensions resembling lower limbs with rudimentary digits (fingers/ toes).

The child also had shortness of breath for 7 days, along with fever and productive cough for 20 days. Chest examination revealed bilateral wheezes and crepitations in the right lung. Laboratory examination showed normocytic normochromic anemia and raised leucocyte count, predominantly lymphocytosis, presumably due to an underlying lower respiratory tract illness. Liver function tests reveal borderline SGPT values and mildly raised ALP (∼458U/l). Echocardiography showed situs solitus, levocardia with mildly dilated left ventricle, and apical ventricular septal defect (VSD) approximately 3 mm in size with left to right shunting. Normal atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial concordance was observed with a left-sided aortic arch and no patent ductus or coarctation of the aorta was present. No pulmonary hypertension or abnormal pulmonary venous drainage was seen in the child; SVC and IVC were seen normally draining into the right atrium.

An infantogram taken at 6 months of age showed a dysmorphic pelvis with asymmetric obliquity on both sides (Figs. 1A and B). The right femoral head was displaced postero-superiorly, forming pseudoarthrosis with the superior part of the right iliac blade, and the right acetabular fossa was empty. The left hip joint was formed with well-defined articular margins. Abnormal radio-opaque, well-formed bilateral femur-like structures are seen adjacent to the true pelvis, with an overlying soft tissue opacity obscuring the shadows of the child's lower limbs. The rest of the visualized bones and upper limbs were normal. Chest radiograph showed patchy, granular radiopacities in bilateral upper lung zones. Contrast-enhanced CT scan(bone window) of the autosite pelvis confirmed the presence of an abnormal large extra soft tissue structure in the posterior aspect of the true pelvis of autosite with a pair of femoral heads, ischii, and pubic bones within it (Figs. 2A and B). A Transabdominal Ultrasound was done, which revealed the absence of a left kidney in the abdomen or pelvis, likely due to congenital agenesis. Neurosonogram revealed a dilated supratentorial ventricular system with increased anterior horn widths of 8mm on the right side and 7.8mm on the left and ventricular indices of 17.5 mm on the right and 17.4 mm on the left side (Fig. 3A). (Levene index or ventricular index is calculated by measuring the distance between the falx and the lateral wall of the anterior horn of the lateral ventricle in the coronal plane). Visualized posterior fossa appeared small with effaced retro cerebellar cisterns (Fig. 3B and C). There was also skin-covered swelling on the spine, for which an ultrasound spine was done. A large heterogeneous, predominantly hypoechoic lesion in the subcutaneous plane of the lumbosacral region was present, and it was communicating with the spinal canal through a defect in posterior elements (Fig. 3D). Closed spinal dysraphism was suspected, and findings were confirmed on subsequent body MRI. The whole spine MRI showed a low-lying spinal cord terminating at the S2 vertebral level. A defect of size ∼11 mm involving posterior elements in the lower lumbosacral spine was seen with herniation of the spinal cord and cauda equina nerve roots through the spina bifida, ending in a large hydro-syringomyelic cavity. The sac was covered by intact skin and subcutaneous tissue.

Autosite pelvis showed a short phallus with 2 normal testicular structures seen in 2 widely separated, noncommunicating hemiscrotal sacs. The left kidney was not visualized in the abdomen and pelvis, likely due to agenesis. An anal opening was seen at the right posterolateral aspect of the urethra in the perineum. The presence of abnormal soft tissue along with pelvic bones, which were fused to the pelvis of the autosite, was also confirmed by an MRI. The constellation of pelvic findings suggested a rachipagus parasitic twin with closed spinal dysraphism.

Discussion

Rachipagus parasitic twins are a rare anomaly. 24 occurrences in more than 125 years were discovered in Spencer's most thorough systematic review, published in 2001 [4]. More reports of this phenomenon have been worldwide throughout the last 10 years. More recently, in a case series by Zewdie et al. in 2020, 37 more rachipagus cases were discovered throughout the review, bringing the total to almost 70 [5]. Geographic distribution reveals that developing nations account for nearly all its occurrences. Only 2 instances came from Asia (16/37), while the other half came from Africa (19/37). Ethiopia, Pakistan, and India have the highest number of reports (9, 6, and 5 cases recorded from each country, respectively) [5].

The described parasites seem to have different morphologies and anatomy depending on the location. When the parasitic union occurs in the cervical or upper thoracic areas, an upper limb and its girdle bones are typically observed, but lower limbs and their girdle bones are more common when the union occurs in the dorsal and lumbar regions [2].

In parasitic or abortive twinning cases, organs and tissues from multiple organ systems are often found within the parasitic mass. Notably, whereas a lipomatous mass was found within the parasite in other published cases, Chadha et al. [2] reported the existence of a benign teratoma within the parasitic mass. This suggests that there is a narrow boundary separating fetal inclusions, conjoined twins, teratomata, abortive duplications, and more evolved types of parasitic twinning. The most commonly involved organ systems are the central nervous system and the vertebral column, often adjacent to the union site. It is interesting to note that significant anomalies affecting other organ systems are uncommon in the autosite. These appear to be more prevalent in cases involving substantial union when the lower genitourinary and alimentary tracts and the cloacal membrane were malformed in both patients.

In rachipagus lesions, it has already been observed that a lipoma frequently develops at the base of the parasite mass, often extending intraspinally [2]. There were multiple instances when the autosite contained a lipomeningomyelocele, subcutaneous lipoma, intradural lipoma, or intramedullary lipoma. There were patches of capillary hemangioma over the soft tissue mass in 1 case that was documented [6]. Spina bifida occulta is characterized by hemangiomatous lesions in the overlying skin, tiny appendages, and skin dimpling. Cartilaginous fragments and bone spicules are frequently observed in the lipomatous mass of patients with concealed spinal dysraphism [6].

Two cases with spina bifida and hemivertebrae were described by Sharma et al. [7] in 2012. Zhang et al. [8] in 2011 reported rachiphagus conjoined twinning in a female seventeen-year-old patient who also had a ventricular septal defect, diplomyelia, tethered cord, and spina bifida. In a case report by Samuel Sisay Hailu in 2022 from Ethiopia with findings interestingly like our case, there was thoracolumbar spina bifida and hemivertebra with lipomyelomeningocele and a parasitic twin with well-formed lower limbs and external genitalia [9]. No other anomalies in the autosite were diagnosed. In the case presented here, there was noncommunicating hydrocephalus, lumbosacral spina bifida, low-lying tethered spinal cord, sacral lipomyelomeningocele, left renal agenesis and a parasitic twin with well-formed lower limbs and rudimentary urinary bladder.

Conclusion

Since there are few occurrences of rachipagus parasitic twins, generalizations concerning them are challenging. Even though this is very uncommon, we think the knowledge about the function of sectional imaging will be beneficial to the multidisciplinary consulting team. It helps identify such an anomaly by offering a thorough morphological analysis of the location and size of the abnormal twinning, allowing for precise surgical excision of the parasite and subsequent repair of autosite abnormalities.
