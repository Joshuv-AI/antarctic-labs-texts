Protecting infants from RSV: cost-effectiveness of nirsevimab in a real-world study from Chile
Authors: Elena Priante, Eugenio Baraldi
Published: 2026

The recent availability of two highly effective prophylactic strategies, maternal vaccination during pregnancy (RSVpreF bivalent vaccine) and long-acting monoclonal antibodies (nirsevimab and clesrovimab) represents a historic opportunity to prevent respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections in infants.1 Although the encouraging results point toward the possibility of defeating RSV-related disease, it is still crucial to keep producing strong supporting evidence.

The main challenges that require attention include ensuring universal access to disease prevention and surveillance, assessing the comparative effectiveness of different approaches (monoclonal antibodies versus maternal vaccination), identifying the immunization programs best suited to each setting, monitoring breakthrough infections and the emergence of resistance, as well as evaluating the impact of preventive strategies on long-term respiratory health.

Regarding the first point, we have a historic chance to make a difference in infant mortality rates in low-income countries, which account for 97% of deaths in children under five.2,3 To ensure this opportunity is not missed, it is essential to guarantee equal access worldwide not only to new drugs but also to epidemiological surveillance tools. Programs such as the WHO-backed global RSV surveillance initiative represent key examples and should be strongly supported. Since its launch in 2016, significant advances have been made in RSV genomic sequencing capabilities with participation now spanning 25 countries. The most recently published findings indicate that although multiple RSV lineages co-circulate globally, the key antigenic sites of the F protein remain highly preserved.3

If on one hand the availability of multiple preventive options is a great advancement, on the other hand it places clinicians and stakeholders in the position of having to select among different strategies, thereby increasing decision-making complexity. A one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to be appropriate. As recently emphasized by Prosser and Hutton for nirsevimab, the cost-effectiveness evaluations may vary substantially across countries, reflecting differences in epidemiology, health-care systems, and willingness-to-pay thresholds.4 Policy decisions should be context-adapted considering local factors beyond modeled estimates, including implementation feasibility, budget impact and population attitude towards preventive treatments.

In this context the article “Health care utilization and cost implications of Chile's 2024 nirsevimab strategy for RSV prevention: a counterfactual analysis” published by Sauré and colleagues in this issue is valuable.5 The study aimed to evaluate the impact of the nationwide nirsevimab program in Chile, the first country in the Southern Hemisphere to implement a universal immunization strategy in 2024.6 The authors demonstrate a significant reduction in disease incidence and economic burden across all evaluated outcomes, including medical leave, outpatient consultations, and hospital bed utilization with a net economic benefit of USD 23·50 million.5 Although the results largely confirm previous evidence, they clearly demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of nirsevimab in a specific context, such as Chile. Furthermore, the manuscript offers added value by separately quantifying the impact in the “in” and “out of season” cohorts of children, an aspect that is still subject to discussion. The findings underscore the importance of including all newborns and infants entering their first RSV season, regardless of their date of birth. A limitation of the study is that it included a broader category of lower respiratory tract infections rather than focusing exclusively on RSV-related cases. Once again, this highlights the importance of robust epidemiological monitoring.

In addition, it is essential to focus on monitoring the development of resistance, as well as to gaining knowledge on the mechanisms that contribute to breakthrough infections. These events, even exceptional, appear to be influenced by time since immunization, host factors, and viral characteristics. Although recent studies have reported only sporadic cases of resistance following nirsevimab implementation,7,8 it remains essential to sustain and expand genomic surveillance. Similar events are biologically plausible with other preventive strategies, including maternal vaccination and clesrovimab. However, evidence remains limited for these approaches, largely reflecting the current lack of extensive real-world data.

Lastly, a consistent body of evidence shows that early-life RSV is associated with long-term respiratory sequelae, such as recurrent wheezing and asthma.9 The real-world NIRSE-GAL study conducted in Spain, demonstrated a reduction in hospitalizations that extends beyond the initial months following administration and persists across two consecutive RSV seasons. It remains unclear whether this sustained effect may be attributable to residual protective antibody levels or to the “lung-sparing” effect of avoiding damage from early severe RSV infection.10 Further evidence and longer follow-up will help to determine if the prevention or delay of RSV infection may represent an effective primary prevention strategy for recurrent wheezing and childhood asthma.

In conclusion, real-world evidence, exemplified by the study by Sauré and colleagues from Chile,5 plays a crucial role in validating clinical trial data and in defining the broader public health impact of novel preventive strategies. To ensure that the advancements on RSV prevention are widely accessible and sustainable over time, it is essential to support ongoing research in the field and promote real-time surveillance and data sharing.
