Green synthesis of pistachio shell-derived carbon nanofibers: synergistic photocatalytic activity in TiO₂ nanocomposites
Authors: Mohammadsaleh Noormandipour, Hassan Hashemipour, Hassan Ranjbar-Askari, Reza Ranjar-Karimi, Hamidreza Bagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanofibers represent an advanced class of fibrous materials characterized by diameters typically below 500 nm. Nanofibers can be prepared from various natural and synthetic polymers. A common method for fabricating nanofibers is electrospinning. When nanoparticles are present in the polymer solution, the electrospinning product can result in nanocomposite nanofibers. These nanomaterials have numerous industrial applications, particularly as adsorbents and catalysts. In this study, nanofibers were prepared from pistachio shell as the raw material through resinification, preparation of the polymer solution, electrospinning of the polymer solution, thermal stabilization, and carbonization. The effects of various factors, including solution injection rate, applied voltage, carbonization conditions, and polymer solution concentration were investigated. The produced nanofibers were evaluated using FE-SEM, FTIR, and UV-Vis analysis. The effects of polymer solution concentration (12–40 wt%), applied voltage (14–23 kV), and polymer injection rate (0.2–1.2 mL.h− 1) on the diameter of nanofibers were experimentally examined. In the next step, nanocomposite nanofibers with TiO2 nanoparticles, were prepared, and their ability to photocatalytically degrade methylene blue was measured. Finally, to evaluate the performance of the prepared nanocomposite, the photocatalytic degradation rate of methylene blue in aqueous solution was used. A removal percentage of 98.02 ± 1.2% (mean ± SD, n = 3) was achieved using 20 mg of nanocomposite, with an initial concentration of methylene blue at 50 mg.L− 1 and a reaction contact time of 60 min. Kinetic analysis indicated that the reaction followed the pseudo-first-order model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56826-6.

Introduction

Nanomaterials are generally defined as materials with at least one dimension below 100 nm; however, in the case of fibers, this size range is commonly extended to diameters below 500 nm1,2. The production of nanofibers has attracted significant attention due to their unique structural characteristics. Electrospun nanofibers typically exhibit extremely high specific surface areas (approximately 300 m².g− 1) and continuous lengths that may reach several kilometers, making them highly attractive for advanced functional applications. Naturally occurring fibers are continuous filaments3,4. In contrast, synthetic fibers such as rayon (derived from cellulose) and nylon represent early milestones in artificial fiber production. The development of synthetic polymers and polyesters has significantly expanded fiber applications while reducing reliance on natural fibers. Various spinning techniques, including wet, dry, and melt spinning, have been developed for fiber production from synthetic polymers5,6. However, at the nanoscale, electrospinning has emerged as one of the most versatile and widely used techniques. The characterization of nanofibers is essential for understanding their structure-property relationships and for evaluating their performance in specific applications7. Key properties such as morphology, molecular structure, and mechanical behavior determine nanofiber functionality, while application-dependent characteristics include porosity, air permeability, optical properties, electrical behavior, and chemical stability8–10.

Electrospinning is a well-established technique for fabricating micro- to nanofibers from polymer solutions or melts under a high electric field at ambient conditions. This method enables the production of nanofibers from a wide variety of materials, particularly from polymeric systems11. Both natural and synthetic polymers, provided they are soluble or melt-processable, can be electrospun into continuous nanofibers, and over one hundred different polymers have been successfully processed using this technique. Synthetic polymers such as polystyrene and polyvinyl chloride have been electrospun for environmental applications, while biodegradable polymers including polycaprolactone and polylactic acid have found extensive use in biomedical fields12. In addition, natural polymers (e.g., silk, chitosan, collagen, and gelatin) and functional polymers such as polyvinylidene fluoride have been electrospun for advanced applications including piezoelectric and pyroelectric devices13. Due to their interconnected, porous structures and high surface area, electrospun nanofibers are widely used in filtration, biomedical devices, nanosensors, protective clothing, and water treatment technologies14. The electrospinning process is governed by three primary groups of parameters: solution parameters (such as polymer concentration and viscosity), process parameters (including applied voltage, flow rate, and needle-to-collector distance), and environmental conditions15,16. Each of these factors directly influences fiber formation and diameter. For example, increasing polymer concentration raises solution viscosity, leading to larger fiber diameters, whereas dilute solutions tend to result in electrospraying rather than continuous fiber formation17. Similarly, higher applied voltages enhance electrostatic stretching and reduce fiber diameter, while excessive solution flow rates may hinder solvent evaporation and produce thicker fibers18.

Biomass is one of the most abundant and renewable sources of biopolymers and is defined as organic matter derived from photosynthesis19,20. Biomass sources include agricultural residues, animal waste, algae, and aquatic products, and their chemical composition varies widely depending on their origin21,22. Plant-based biomass typically consists of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, whereas other biomass sources may be rich in proteins or starches23,24. This compositional diversity enables biomass-derived materials to exhibit a wide range of chemical and physical properties, making them promising precursors for functional materials. In recent years, biomass-derived materials have gained attention as supports for catalysts and photocatalysts. Photocatalytic oxidation processes enable the degradation of organic pollutants into non-toxic products such as CO₂ and water under light irradiation. Among semiconductor photocatalysts, TiO2 is particularly attractive due to its high stability, low cost, and strong oxidative capability under ultraviolet irradiation19,20,23,24. TiO₂ nanoparticles have therefore been widely employed in photocatalytic degradation reactions. Understanding photocatalytic kinetics is crucial for evaluating catalyst efficiency. Reaction rates are influenced by parameters such as catalyst loading, irradiation intensity, and pollutant concentration25. Biomass, as renewable resource rich in organic carbon, serves as a fundamental feedstock for producing a wide range of high-value carbonaceous products26. Thermochemical processes such as pyrolysis, carbonization, and gasification convert the raw material into useful products27. The most prominent of these products is biochar, a stable carbon material that serves the dual functions of enhancing soil fertility and sequestering carbon. Other products include activated carbon, advanced carbon materials used in energy storage, and composite materials28. This relationship establishes a closed and nearly carbon-neutral cycle. Carbon dioxide previously absorbed by plants is stabilized in long-lived carbon products rather than being rapidly returned to the atmosphere. Consequently, this technology represents a key strategic approach within the circular economy, waste management, and greenhouse gas mitigation29,30. Owing to the presence of active functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, these materials can adsorb and retaining heavy metal ions (such as cadmium, lead, and chromium) as well as industrial dye molecules through adsorption, ion exchange, and complexation processes31–34. These resources such as walnut shells, sugarcane bagasse, and algae effectively remove these pollutants from aqueous environments. This method is considered a green and sustainable approach to water treatment32,34.

Natural or modified biomass are widely used as low-cost and efficient biosorbents for the treatment of industrial wastewater. These materials, owing to the presence of active functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl are capable of adsorbing heavy metal ions (e.g., lead, cadmium, and chromium) as well as industrial dye molecules via mechanisms including adsorption, ion exchange, and complexation35. Feed stocks such as pistachio shells, sugarcane bagasse, and algae can effectively remove these pollutants from aqueous environments. This approach is considered a green and sustainable technology to water treatment36.

Zi et al.37 have highlighted electrospinning as a preferred technique for nanofiber fabrication. In particular, TiO2, due to its high photocatalytic activity, finds widespread application in solar cell technology. TiO2 nanofibers are generally prepared by electrospinning organic solutions containing an alkoxide precursor and a carrier polymer under high voltage. Multiple studies have indicated that smaller nanofiber diameters can be achieved with lower solution viscosity, higher applied voltage, and lower injection rates.

Kuchi et al.38 synthesized polyvinylpyrrolidone/TiO2 nanofibers using the electrospinning technique. They fabricated nanofibers with varying polymer concentrations and needle diameter, subsequently investigating their various properties. Optical properties were assessed through absorption spectroscopy, revealing that changes in polymer concentration and needle diameter significantly influenced the viscosity and surface tension, demonstrably affecting the formation of polyvinylpyrrolidone/TiO₂ nanofibers.

Amini et al.39 investigated the electrospinning of varying concentrations of polyvinylidene fluoride/starch solutions, characterizing the resulting nanofibers using rheometric mechanical spectroscopy. Their findings demonstrated that increasing the starch content in the polyvinylidene fluoride solution enhanced the solution’s viscosity. Furthermore, the mechanical properties of the polyvinylidene fluoride improved in the presence of starch because of the inherent mechanical properties of the starch chains.

Amith et al.40 explored the development of a cost-effective electrospinning process for producing high-quality thin films and optimized parameters using polyvinylpyrrolidone solutions in dimethylformamide. Their results indicated that polymer concentration was an important factor in controlling fiber diameter and bead formation, although collector distance and voltage also influenced fiber diameter. SEM revealed that a polymer solution with a 10 wt% yielded a minimum fiber diameter of 25 nm, while a solution with 15 wt% produced fibers with a diameter of 135 nm.

Dai et al.41 synthesized a TiO2/lignin-derived carbon nanofiber (CNF) composite with high photocatalytic efficiency via electrospinning followed by heat treatment. SEM, FTIR, and EDS analysis revealed that TiO2 nanoparticles were uniformly and strongly deposited on the surface of the carbon fibers. The excellent hydrophobicity and high electrical conductivity of the carbon fibers imparted unique properties to the fiber composite. The degradation rate of the fiber nanocomposite reached 62.77% after 15 min and increased to 87.28% after 30 min under static conditions. A review of the literature reveals a clear need for further investigation into the utilization of agricultural wastes as a feedstock to mitigate the environmental impact of agricultural waste. To meet the environmental challenge of utilizing agricultural by-products, this study investigates the use of pistachio shells as a source of biomass. Although considerable progress has been made in electrospinning of nanofibers and in the use of TiO2 as a photocatalyst, a research gap remains in the development of sustainable materials. Currently, no established processes exist for converting large quantities of agricultural waste into functional, high-performance photocatalytic nanomaterials. Even though electrospinning of synthetic polymers has been well-characterized, the processing parameters and morphologies of electrospun nanofibers made from resin synthesized from pistachio shells, which is a chemically complex, heteroatom-rich biomass, have not been studied. Moreover, the synergistic effect of photocatalysis generated by a composite of TiO2 and carbon derived from pistachio waste products remains unexplored. Therefore, this research aims to establish an electrospinning process based on parameters specifically related to the preparation of nanofibers from a blend of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), and a resin derived from pistachio shells (a waste product of the food industry). The synthesis of these nanofibres will lead to the production of new TiO2 nanocomposite materials which will then be tested for photocatalytic efficiency. Even though we have developed new ways of converting agricultural waste into advanced materials, the use of this type of residual biomass to create electrospun photocatalytic nanofiber prototypes is still very limited. Agricultural wastes could serve as a sustainable source of materials for many developing regions where there is limited development in this sector and where biomass is abundantly available. For example, Kerman province, Iran, generates large amounts of pistachio shell waste each year; however, this agricultural waste has never been used, investigated, or developed as a direct electrospun precursor for producing carbon nanofibers and TiO2-based photocatalytic composites via electrospinning. Additionally, very little research has been published on the processing parameters and electrospinning behavior of the resulting nanofibers when using resin-based solutions created from this type of agricultural waste. The main goal of this research is to demonstrate a method for converting agricultural waste into effective agents for environmental remediation. Ultimately, this project will demonstrate a reproducible method for synthesizing a new type of bio-based nanofiber and provide a quantitative assessment of the photocatalytic activity of the resultant product, potentially making a significant contribution to the development of sustainable nanomaterials and advanced oxidation technologies.

Experimental procedure

Production of nanofibers using PVP polymer

To produce the resin, first 2.5 g of pistachio shell powder, 5 g of phenol, and 0.04 g of sulfuric acid were added to a two-necked flask equipped with a condenser and magnetic stirrer. The two-necked flask was placed in a paraffin bath on a hot plate, and the reaction was carried out at 90 °C for 90 min. After cooling the product to room temperature, 2 mL of formaldehyde was added, and the reaction proceeded at 90 °C for 150 min. After cooling to 40 °C, the mixture was neutralized with an aqueous solution of sodium hydroxide. Finally, the product was dried in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h, resulting in a brown solid resin. The obtained resin and the PVP were transferred to a beaker in a resin-to-PVP weight ratio of 2:3. Subsequently, dimethylformamide solvent and ethanol were added to the mixture at a volume ratio of 7:3 to achieve an overall solution concentration of between 20 and 40 wt%. Finally, the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h.

For electrospinning of the polymer solutions, a syringe with an internal diameter of 12 mm and an 18 G needle were used. The collector of the electrospinning apparatus was covered with aluminum foil with dimensions of 25 × 27 cm². The electrospinning conditions are given in Table 4. To produce CNFs, a 55 wt% polymer solution was prepared. After electrospinning according to the conditions outlined in Table 4, the electrospun fibers were thermally stabilized in a furnace at a 1.0 °C.min− 1 heating rate at 220 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, they were carbonized at 450 °C under an argon gas for 45 min with a heating rate of 6 °C.min− 1.

To prepare the nanocomposite solution, 0.22 g of TiO2 nanoparticles were added to a 20 wt% polymer solution prepared earlier. The flask opening was covered with parafilm, and the mixture was placed in an ultrasonic bath for 1 h, followed by attachment to a condenser and stirring in a paraffin oil bath at 60 °C for 6 h. Electrospinning of this polymer solution was conducted as described in the previous step.

Production of nanofibers using PVDF polymer

The resin was produced using phenol-formaldehyde, following the procedure outlined in the previous step. The resulting resin from the preceding stage was combined with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) polymer at a weight ratio of 2:3, along with acetone and dimethylformamide solvents at a volume ratio of 3:7, in a condenser-attached flask. The overall concentration of the solution was adjusted to 10, 12, and 15 wt%. This mixture was then stirred in a paraffin bath at 60 °C on a heater for 24 h. The preparation of nanocomposite the manner was described in the last section. To simplify, the synthesized nanofibers and nanocomposites were coded as follows: NF-PVP (synthesized nanofiber using PVP), NC-PVP (synthesized nanocomposite by PVP), NF-PVDF (synthesized nanofiber using PVDF) and NC-PVDF (synthesized nanocomposite using PVDF) and CNF (carbonized nanofibers using PVP).

Photocatalytic degradation of MB using TiO₂

TiO2 is a semiconductor photocatalyst that catalyzes oxidation and reduction reactions by absorbing light. It is used to decompose organic pollutants and bacteria in water and air51. To investigate the extent of photocatalytic degradation, precisely weighed nanocomposites derived from electrospun nanofibers after precise weighing were introduced into beakers. Each containing 10 mL of an aqueous MB solution with concentrations of 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 mg.L− 1, along with a magnetic stir bar. The experiments were conducted using varying masses of the nanocomposites: 13, 15, 17, 20, and 25 mg. The beakers were then sealed with Parafilm and incubated in a temperature-controlled water bath maintained at 40 °C. The solutions were exposed to a mercury vapor lamp for 15, 30, 45, 60, and 75 min. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. After separating the nanocomposites, the concentration of supernatant was determined using UV-Vis spectrophotometry at a wavelength of 660 nm. Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the data. For the determination of nanofiber diameters, at least 50 individual fibers from different regions of each FE-SEM image were measured. The average fiber diameter was then calculated. For the photocatalytic degradation experiments, the percentage removal, as mentioned, was calculated using Eq. 1. The mean values were computed from three independent replicates.

Results and discussion

The electrospinning parameters examined in this study, injection rate, polymer concentration, and applied voltage, significantly influence the morphology and diameter of the resulting nanofibers. The following sections present a systematic analysis of each parameter’s effect, followed by characterization of the TiO₂ nanocomposites and evaluation of their photocatalytic performance.

Effect of polymer solution concentration

To determine the morphology and assess the nanofiber diameter, samples were analyzed using FE-SEM imaging. Figures 2a-f present the electrospun fibers FE-SEM analysis of the electrospun fibers at different polymer and resin solution concentrations. As shown in Fig. 2a, electrospinning a solution with a concentration of 10 wt% polymer and resin did not yield fiber formation. The electrospinning of dilute polymer solutions is not feasible due to the insufficient overlap between polymer chains. In a dilute state, polymer molecules interact predominantly with solvent molecules. Consequently, electrospraying occurs instead of electrospinning in such dilute polymer solutions.

The FE-SEM analysis of the electrospun fibers produced from the 40 wt% polymer and resin solution (Fig. 2b) confirmed successful fiber synthesis, yielding average fiber diameter of approximately 2.6 ± 0.30 μm (mean ± SD, n = 50 fibers measured from different regions of the sample). Based on the findings from the 10 wt% and 40 wt% polymer solutions, further polymer solutions with varying weight percentages between these two values were prepared. Examination of the FE-SEM images in Figs. 2c-f indicated that increasing the polymer solution concentration led to increased interactions between polymer chains. This, in turn, resulted in greater chain overlap and, ultimately, an increase in fiber diameter (see Fig. 3).

Electrospinning cannot produce electrospun fibers (Fig. 2a) from 10 wt% PVP/resin solutions when the concentration is below the critical entanglement concentration, because there is not enough viscoelasticity present in the solution to support the formation of the electrospinning jet. Consequently, the solution will produce either electrosprayed droplets or beads rather than a continuous jet. As the concentration increases, the number of entanglements increases, which in turn increases the viscosity and elasticity of the solution, both of which resist excessive jet stretching. This results in larger and more uniform fibers from the electrospinning process. This principle applies to all electrospinning processes, regardless of the polymer system employed52.

The influence of voltage on nanofiber diameter, as determined by FE-SEM analysis, is illustrated in Fig. 4a and b. Consistent with expectations, this analysis reveals that increasing the applied voltage generates the electrostatic force necessary to initiate electrospinning, leading to a reduction in nanofiber diameter due to the enhanced stretching of the polymer solution. Furthermore, Fig. 5 shows the average nanofiber diameter as a function of the applied voltage.

The voltage applied creates an electrostatic field, inducing charge on the surface of a jet. According to electrohydrodynamic theory, applying a higher voltage voltage increases repulsion between the particles within a charged jet, increases the force exerted by charged particles, increases the acceleration of the jet and increases the frequency of whipping. Ultimately, a minimum average nanofiber diameter of 96 ± 7 nm (n = 50) was observed at a voltage of 23 kV, compared to 132 ± 9 nm at 14 kV under otherwise identical conditions (20 wt% solution, flow rate 0.5 mL.h− 1). Applying a higher voltage creates greater stretching and thinning of the material prior to solidification, thereby producing finer fibers53.

The volumetric flow rate of the polymer solution is determined by the injection rate. Under a fixed electric field, the volumetric flow rate that maintains a stable Taylor cone is considered the optimal flow rate. An increased volumetric flow rate of the polymer solution results in more polymer solution mass being delivered to the collector per unit of time. If the solvent’s evaporation rate remains unchanged, the additional polymer solution mass will not be completely evaporated before it reaches the collector and will therefore still retaining some solvent (higher residual solvent content or higher moisture content (i.e., retained solvent)). This results in two outcomes: (a) the formation of wet fibers, which are more prone to coalescence and exhibit lower tensile strength, and (b) a direct correlation between processing parameters and final fiber morphology, because fiber diameter is influenced by both coalescence and jet stretching54.

Resin/PVP/TiO2 nanocomposite

To evaluate the performance of the nanofibers, a TiO2 nanocomposite was fabricated from a polymer solution of resin and polyvinylpyrrolidone. The resulting nanocomposite was then characterized using FE-SEM analysis. Figures 8a and b show the FE-SEM images of the produced nanocomposite. This analysis shows that the grains present on the surface of the nanofibers correspond to TiO₂ nanoparticles, and the average diameter of the nanofibers is 112 ± 9 nm (mean ± SD, n = 50 fibers).

Fig. 8The FE-SEM image NC-PVP under the following conditions: 18 kV and the flow rate equal to 0.5 mL.h− 1 (a) low zoom and (b) high zoom.

The FE-SEM image NC-PVP under the following conditions: 18 kV and the flow rate equal to 0.5 mL.h− 1 (a) low zoom and (b) high zoom.

FE-SEM analysis (Figs. 8a, b) confirms the successful fabrication of a cohesive TiO₂-pistachio resin/PVP nanocomposite with well-integrated components. The TiO₂ nanoparticles appear as distinct, well-dispersed grains anchored on the nanofiber surface. This morphology is critical because it indicates effective integration without severe aggregation, which is essential for maximizing accessible photocatalytic surface area. The interfacial adhesion between the TiO₂ and the carbonaceous matrix is likely facilitated by polar functional groups (e.g., -C = O from PVP and phenolic -OH from the resin) that can coordinate with the TiO₂ surface, similar to interactions observed in polymer/TiO₂ composites by Kuchi et al.38. The composite diameter of 112 ± 9 nm results from the interplay of electrospinning parameters and the addition of TiO₂. The nanoparticles increase the electrical conductivity of the solution, which can enhance jet stretching in the presence of an electric field, potentially leading to finer fibers; however, they also increase the net solid content. The observed fiber diameter represents a balance of these effects, yielding a structure in which the TiO₂ is well supported on a conductive, high-surface-area carbon network derived from pistachio resin. This integrated architecture facilitates electron transfer and pollutant adsorption, prerequisites for enhanced photocatalytic activity, as demonstrated in similar carbon-TiO₂ fiber systems41.

The systematic increase in nanofiber diameter with PVDF/resin concentration (from 10 to 15 wt%, Figs. 9 and 10) is governed by fundamental polymer solution rheology. As concentration increases, the number of chain entanglements per polymer molecule rises, which significantly increasing the solution’s viscosity and elastic modulus. During electrospinning, the resulting higher viscoelastic force resists the electrostatic stretching forces acting on the jet, resulting in less thinning before solidification and thus larger fiber diameters. This aligns with established electrospinning models where fiber diameter, d, scales with solution concentration according to a power-law relationship55. Notably, a continuous, bead-free nanofiber morphology was achieved even at the lowest concentration (10 wt%, Fig. 9a). This suggests that the 10 wt% PVDF/resin/DMF-acetone solution is at or above the critical chain entanglement concentration for this specific blend. This critical concentration is lower than that observed for the PVP/resin system (20 wt%), highlighting the influence of the base polymer’s molecular characteristics (e.g., chain stiffness, polymer-solvent interaction parameter) and its specific interaction with the pistachio shell resin on the electrospinning window. Defining this processing window for a novel bioresin blend is a key practical contribution of this study.

To evaluate the performance of the nanofibers, TiO2 nanocomposites were fabricated using a polymer solution of resin and polyvinylidene fluoride. Figure 11a and b show the FE-SEM results. TiO2 nanoparticles are visible on the surface of the nanofibers, and the average diameter of the produced nanocomposite fibers is 48 ± 4 nm (mean ± SD, n = 50 fibers).

Fig. 11The FE-SEM image NC-PVDF under the following conditions: 18 kV and the flow rate equal to 0.5 mL.h− 1 (a) low zoom and (b) high zoom.

The FE-SEM image NC-PVDF under the following conditions: 18 kV and the flow rate equal to 0.5 mL.h− 1 (a) low zoom and (b) high zoom.

The NC-PVDF samples (Fig. 11a and b) exhibited a remarkably small mean diameter (48 ± 6 nm), with TiO₂ nanoparticles uniformly dispersed throughout the nanofiber matrix, whereas the NC-PVP samples (112 ± 9 nm) had a much larger mean diameter. The smaller mean diameter of the NC-PVDF samples is likely due to the unique characteristics of the PVDF polymer matrix, such as its high dielectric constant, which can enhance the electrostatic field in the electrospinning process by boosting the charge density; therefore, increasing the likelihood of instability in the electrospinning jet and causes further stretching of the electrospinning the jet. Thus, smaller diameter fibers are created, yielding a reduction that is significantly higher than Frenot and Chronakis56, leading to an approximately 15-fold difference in the mean diameter of the two different nanocomposites. Second, the thermal behavior of PVDF during the stabilization and carbonization steps may differ from that of PVP, potentially leading to a higher degree of volumetric shrinkage or decomposition, further reducing the final fiber diameter. The effective dispersion of TiO₂ on these ultra-fine fibers is crucial. The nanoscale diameter and the resulting high surface-to-volume ratio maximize the exposure of both the TiO₂ active sites and the adsorbent carbon surface derived from the pistachio shell-derived carbon precursor. This creates an optimal architecture for photocatalysis: the carbon framework adsorbs and concentrates MB molecules, while the intimately connected, nanoscale TiO₂ particles generate oxidative radicals upon irradiation. This adsorption and degradation and degrade mechanism on a high-surface-area platform is a recognized strategy for enhancing photocatalytic efficiency, as demonstrated in composite systems where a carbonaceous matrix boosts TiO₂ activity57. Our results demonstrate that this advantageous architecture can be successfully achieved using a pistachio shell waste precursor. The inverse relationship between initial MB concentration and removal percentage is classic in adsorption-mediated photocatalysis. At higher concentrations, the active sites become saturated with MB molecules, which limiting the adsorption of hydroxyl ions and oxygen necessary for generating reactive oxygen species (●OH, O2●-). The fit to pseudo-first-order kinetics (Langmuir-Hinshelwood model) suggests the reaction rate is proportional to the surface coverage of MB. This is typical when the pollutant concentration is relatively low compared to adsorption capacity and the oxidative intermediates do not strongly compete for active sites.

The exceptional photocatalytic degradation of MB achieved by the NC-PVDF nanocomposite (98.02% ± 1.2% removal) can be attributed to a synergistic mechanism involving both the TiO₂ nanoparticles and the pistachio shell-derived carbon nanofiber matrix. The multi-step process is described as follows61,62:

1- Photon adsorption and charge carrier generation:

Upon irradiation with UV light, the TiO₂ nanoparticles (Eg ≈ 3.2 eV for anatase) are photoexcited. Electrons (e−) are promoted from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), generating positively charged holes (h+) in the VB.

5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Ti{O}_{2}+hv\:\to\:\:Ti{O}_{2}({e}_{CB}^{-}+{h}_{VB}^{+})$$\end{document}

2- Charge separation and migration (key synergistic role of the carbon support):

This is the critical step enhanced by our nanocomposite design. The conductive network of pistachio shell-derived CNFs acts as an efficient electron acceptor and mediator. Photo-generated electrons in the TiO2 and CB are rapidly transferred to the CNF matrix. This suppresses the direct recombination of electron-hole pairs, which is a major factor limiting the efficiency of pure TiO2 photocatalysis. The holes remain on the TiO2 surface.

3- Formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS):

The separated charges react with surface-adsorbed species to generate strong oxidizing agents.

- The holes (h+) in the TiO₂ VB can directly oxidize adsorbed MB molecules or react with surface hydroxyl groups (OH-) or adsorbed water molecules to produce hydroxyl radicals (●OH):6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{VB}^{+}+{OH}^{-}\left(or\:{H}_{2}O\right)\to\:\:{\bullet}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{H}$$\end{document}

4- Degradation and mineralization of MB:

The generated ROS, particularly the highly non-selective and potent ●OH radical, attacks the chromophoric structure of the adsorbed MB molecules. This radical cleaves the aromatic rings and the conjugated system responsible for the dye’s blue color, leading to the formation of smaller organic intermediates (e.g., sulfoxides, aromatic amines) and ultimately to complete mineralization into CO2, H2O, SO42−, NO3−, and NH4+ ions.

The enhanced efficiency factors of the synthesized nanocomposite are as follows:

1- Adsorption-enrichment effect:

The high surface area and porous structure of the electrospun CNF membrane (supported by FE-SEM, Figs. 8 and 11), provides abundant sites for the preconcentration and adsorption of MB molecules from the solution onto the catalyst surface prior to irradiation. This proximity brings the pollutant in close contact with the active TiO₂ sites and the ROS generated in situ, significantly increasing the degradation rate.

2- Dopant effects from the pistachio shell:

The inherent heteroatoms (N, O, S) from the pistachio shell, which are now incorporated into the CNF matrix, can create defect sites and modify the electronic structure. This can further enhance electron mobility and provide active sites for O2 activation, enhancing the catalytic cycle.

3- Membrane platform advantage:

The electrospun nanocomposite forms a cohesive, reusable membrane, preventing nanoparticle leaching and facilitating catalyst recovery, which is an important practical advantage over powder-based catalysts.

Hence, the outstanding photocatalytic performance of the pistachio shell-derived CNF/TiO₂ nanocomposite is attributed to a combined adsorption-concentration-degradation mechanism, where the carbon scaffold serves a triple function: (i) as an electron sink to enhance charge separation, (ii) as an adsorbent to enrich pollutant concentration at the reactive interface, and (iii) as a stable, conductive support facilitating the regeneration of surface-active sites.

Performance of pistachio shell-derived carbon nanofibers: A comparison

The nanocomposite developed from pistachio shells has demonstrated excellent photocatalytic capability (98.02 ± 1.2% MB degradation), which offers significant opportunities for commercial use. The high performance of this agricultural waste-derived nanocomposite provides a time- and cost-effective approach for producing functional catalytic materials. The ability to create immobilized catalyst systems such as fixed-bed or flow-through reactors from the electrospun nanofiber membranes represents a major advantage of using these materials in practical catalysts applications, as these systems are easier to implement by eliminating the challenges of post-treatment filtration, reducing the potential for nanoparticles leaching, and outperforming powder catalysts. The ability to scale the performance and applicability of the nanocomposite created from pistachio shells under moderate temperature and time (40 °C, 60 min) further strengthens the argument for the feasibility of implementing this nanocomposite in practice. This study provides a sound foundation for the design and development of modular wastewater treatment systems for waters containing reactive dyes. However, further work is needed to evaluate the long-term stability of the catalyst in actual wastewater matrices to ascertain its commercial viability. The carbon nanofiber matrix formed from pistachio shell waste has a large surface area and conductive pathways that provide a means to collect electrons, which in turn reduce the likelihood of charge recombination in TiO₂. This method of removing pollutants works by concentrating them near the surface of TiO₂ through adsorption, enabling more rapid degradation than previously possible. The combination of high efficiency and low-temperature requirements makes this technology suitable for energy-efficient applications. While the catalyst may not currently possess the highest value in photocatalytic efficiency, it remains a strong candidate when considering risks and benefits related to photocatalytic activity, waste valorization, material costs, and technical design of reactors. Additionally, the competitive advantages related to the nanocomposite made from pistachio shells include high photocatalytic efficiency and the ability to valorize waste and simplify catalyst handling. The comparison between the performance of our synthesized pistachio shell-derived carbon nanofiber/TiO₂ nanocomposite against other recently reported composite materials (Table 1) shows that our nanocomposite can remove 98.02 ± 1.2% of MB under a 250 W mercury vapor lamp (UV light) under the following optimal conditions: 20 mg catalyst, 50 mg.L− 1 of MB, at 60 min, in 40 °C.

Evaluation of recycling performance

A critical metric for assessing the practical viability and economic feasibility of an adsorbent or photocatalyst for wastewater treatment is its stability and reusability. To this end, the reusability of the synthesized pistachio shell-derived carbon nanofiber/TiO₂ nanocomposite was systematically evaluated over multiple consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles. Following the initial photocatalytic degradation experiment, the spent NC-PVDF membrane was retrieved from the solution via simple filtration. The dye-laden nanocomposite was then subjected to a desorption process to regenerate its active sites. This was achieved by immersing the material in 20 mL of a 0.1 M HCl solution and stirring at ambient temperature for 120 min. The acidic medium protonates the adsorbent surface, effectively reversing the electrostatic interactions responsible for the initial adsorption of the cationic MB dye and facilitating its release into the desorption medium. Subsequently, the regenerated nanocomposite was thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove any residual acid and loosely bound dye molecules, followed by drying in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The dried material was weighed and reused in the next photocatalytic degradation cycle under identical experimental parameters. The reusability of the NC-PVDF nanocomposite over five successive cycles is illustrated in Fig. 18. The composite demonstrates exceptional regenerative capability, maintaining a high level of photocatalytic activity. The marginal and gradual decrease in performance can be attributed to several unavoidable factors common to cyclic adsorption-desorption processes. First, a minimal physical mass loss of the nanocomposite occurs during the recovery, washing, and handling stages between cycles. Second, while the acidic desorption is effective, it may not achieve 100% dye removal; a small fraction of dye molecules or their degradation intermediates may be strongly chemisorbed or may become trapped within the porous carbon matrix, leading to cumulative partial blockage of active sites. Third, repeated exposure to acidic conditions and thermal drying might induce subtle alterations in the surface chemistry of the interfacial bonding between the TiO₂ nanoparticles and the carbon nanofiber support. Despite these factors, the retention of over 88% activity after five cycles underscores the remarkable structural integrity and chemical stability of the bio-derived composite. In conclusion, the excellent recycling performance of the pistachio shell-derived NC-PVDF nanocomposite validates its potential for repeated use in the remediation of dye-contaminated water. The effective regeneration protocol using dilute acid, coupled with the composite’s inherent stability, significantly enhances its cost-effectiveness and environmental sustainability. This robust reusability, resulting from both its green synthesis approach and rational design, positions this material as a promising candidate for scalable, practical photocatalytic wastewater treatment systems.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrated the production of nanofibers from polymer solutions containing polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), which were blended with a resin derived from pistachio shell agricultural waste. The subsequent synthesis of TiO₂ nanocomposites and evaluation of their photocatalytic properties revealed several key findings. FE-SEM imaging revealed that increasing the polymer solution concentration and resin content enhances the overlap between polymer chains, thereby increasing viscosity. Consequently, higher concentrations lead to increased fiber diameter, with a minimum average nanofiber diameter of 107 ± 8 nm achieved for a 20 wt% solution of polyvinylpyrrolidone polymer with resin, and a minimum average nanofiber diameter of 48 ± 6 nm obtained for a 10 wt% solution of polyvinylidene fluoride polymer with resin. In dilute polymer solutions, electrospinning fails to occur due to insufficient polymer chain overlap. Increasing the applied voltage enhances the stretching of the polymer solution, resulting in a reduction in nanofiber diameter. Ultimately, a minimum average nanofiber diameter of 96 ± 7 nm is observed at a voltage of 23 kV. Higher solution injection rates reduce the drying time of the nanofibers and reduce solvent evaporation, thereby increasing the nanofiber diameter. Consequently, a minimum average nanofiber diameter of 92 ± 10 nm was achieved at an injection rate of 0.2 mL.h− 1. Carbonization of the fibers eliminated residual moisture and solvent, leading to a reduction in fiber diameter. Fibers with an initial diameter of 2.60 ± 0.30 μm were transformed into nanofibers with an average diameter of 99 ± 13 nm after stabilization heat treatment at 220 °C (heating rate: 1.0 °C·min⁻¹) followed by carbonization at 450 °C (heating rate: 6 °C·min⁻¹) under an argon atmosphere. Finally, the performance of the produced nanocomposite was evaluated by examining the photocatalytic degradation of an aqueous methylene blue solution at 40 °C. This experiment revealed that the optimal amount of nanocomposite produced was 20 mg, the ideal contact time was 60 min, and the highest removal rate (98.02 ± 1.2%; mean ± SD, n = 3) was observed for a MB solution concentration of 50 mg.L− 1. The photocatalytic degradation reaction follows a pseudo-first-order kinetic model, based on the correlation coefficients.
