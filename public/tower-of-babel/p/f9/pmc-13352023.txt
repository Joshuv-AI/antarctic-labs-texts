CART-immune-related adverse events after BCMA CAR-T therapy
Authors: Luca Paruzzo, Matthew Ho, Julia Han Noll, Alfred L. Garfall, Adam D. Cohen, Joseph A. Fraietta, Marco Ruella
Published: 2026

Main text

B cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy (BCMA CAR-T) is now a standard of care for patients with relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) after first-line therapy. Two BCMA CAR-T products are currently approved in the United States: idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel) and ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel). Both therapies induce deep and durable responses in heavily pretreated patients, with cilta-cel associated with longer progression-free survival compared with ide-cel.1

With broader BCMA CAR-T use and longer follow-up available, a unique spectrum of delayed toxicities has become apparent. These events are distinct from the acute toxicities classically associated with CAR-T therapy, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), immune effector cell-associated (IEC) hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like syndrome, and immune effector cell-associated haematotoxicity (ICAHT). These delayed complications have heterogeneous clinical presentations, but they are all characterized by the hyperactivation of the immune system leading to CART-mediated tissue damage, and therefore, they are defined as CART-immune-related adverse events (CirAE). CirAE include delayed neurologic complications such as Parkinsonism and cranial nerve palsies, observed in approximately 10% of cilta-cel patients, as well as immune effector cell associated enterocolitis (IEC-enterocolitis), occurring in approximately 2% of patients.2,3 Despite a growing number of isolated reports,2,4,5,6,7 the clinical spectrum, risk factors, and mechanistic underpinnings of these delayed toxicities remain poorly understood, limiting our ability to identify high-risk patients or develop preemptive strategies. Alongside broader ongoing efforts to characterize, understand, and prevent BCMA CAR-T-associated toxicities, our recently published study in Nature Medicine provides a comprehensive clinical description of these events and shows that, despite clinical heterogeneity, they share a common biological basis and risk profile.8 While any specific toxicity is uncommon in isolation, collectively these delayed toxicities are more common than previously appreciated and are associated with significant morbidity and mortality; these toxicities might therefore represent a barrier to the widespread diffusion of CAR-T in earlier lines and in the community setting.

We studied a cohort of heavily pretreated patients (median 5 prior lines) receiving commercial BCMA CAR-T therapy (125 cilta-cel and 73 ide-cel). CirAE occurred significantly more frequently with cilta-cel than with ide-cel (20% vs. 2.7%) and involved four main organ systems: neurologic, gastrointestinal, rheumatologic, and pulmonary. Among cilta-cel recipients, the most common CirAE were facial nerve palsy (7.2%), Parkinsonism (4.8%), and IEC-enterocolitis (5.6%). Immune-mediated pneumonitis was the only CirAE observed among ide-cel-treated patients (n = 2; 2.7%). CirAE occurred at a median of four weeks after CAR-T infusion, highlighting their delayed onset relative to CRS or ICANS. Time to CirAE onset varied depending on the organ system involved. Rheumatologic and neurologic CirAE occurred early, with a median onset of 27 days after infusion. In contrast, IEC-enterocolitis and pneumonitis had a delayed onset, occurring at a median of 3.5 months and 1 year, respectively. Importantly, we observed a trend toward inferior overall survival among CirAE patients, driven by significantly higher 1-year non-relapse mortality (NRM): 17% vs. 3%. This excess mortality was primarily due to fatal infections, likely linked to the prolonged and high-intensity immunosuppression, including corticosteroids required to manage CirAE (Figure 1). In line with prior reports, the development of ICANS and elevated peak lymphocyte counts were identified as independent predictors of CirAE in our study.4,7 Although the precise threshold predictive of CirAE varies across reports, most studies converge on a lymphocyte peak count of approximately 2.5–5 ×103/μL, suggesting a potential cutoff for early dysfunctional hyperexpansion associated with subsequent CirAE. A novel finding of our study was the link between an apheresis CD4:CD8 ratio >1 and increased CirAE risk. Additionally, patients who developed CirAE had higher CD4:CD8 ratios at multiple time points from apheresis through 6 months post-infusion, suggesting that the T cell phenotype of the apheresis product may influence the infused CAR-T product and predispose patients to delayed toxicities. Interestingly, although disease burden was not associated with neurologic CirAE risk in our cohort, emerging data7 suggest that response to bridging therapy may reduce the incidence of CirAE. This highlights the need to define optimal bridging strategies that enhance CAR-T efficacy while improving the safety profile.7

Because CirAE occurred almost exclusively with cilta-cel, we investigated whether construct-specific differences might contribute by using Jurkat triple-reporter cells transduced with ide-cel or cilta-cel CAR constructs. In line with the findings from Atanackovic et al.,6 cilta-cel CAR demonstrated higher tonic signaling, reflected by increased baseline NFAT and NF-κB activity and greater metabolic output, consistent with a more pre-activated state even in the absence of antigen. In contrast, ide-cel CAR T cells exhibited greater inducible activation after antigen stimulation, with more robust increases in signaling activity and metabolic responses. Together, these data suggest that cilta-cel CAR-T cells may exist in a more tonic, metabolically engaged state that could predispose to immune-mediated toxicity. Additionally, delayed CirAE have not been reported with anitocabtagene autoleucel, a BCMA CAR-T product using a D-domain binder, raising the possibility that CAR binder architecture may influence CirAE risk.9

To directly investigate the cellular mechanisms underlying CirAE, we performed immune profiling of peripheral blood, enterocolitis biopsies, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples collected during CirAE. Immunohistochemistry of intestinal biopsies from patients with enterocolitis revealed dense infiltration by CD4+ CAR-T cells colocalizing with BCMA-expressing cells, suggesting that IEC-enterocolitis may represent an on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Our findings confirm and extend prior reports demonstrating CAR T cell infiltration in the lamina propria in IEC-enterocolitis; notably, previous studies have also described clonal restriction in a subset of patients, suggesting a potential contributory role in disease onset.10

Similarly, proteomics and single-cell RNA sequencing of paired peripheral blood and CSF samples from patients with neuro-CirAE demonstrated a chemokine-driven enrichment of CD4+ CAR-T cells within the CSF, including a population characterized by interferon-stimulated, resident memory-like transcriptional features. These CD4+ CAR-T cells expressed cytotoxic effector genes and showed strong enrichment for leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity pathways (Figure 1). The potential role of effector-like CD4+ CAR-T cells in the pathogenesis of neuro-CirAE is further supported by prior CSF profiling studies reported in the literature.6 Whether neuro-CirAE represents an on-target, off-tumor toxicity or a Claudin-9 directed off-target toxicity was not directly evaluated in our study, with conflicting reports in the literature.5,9 More rigorous validation of central nervous system BCMA expression in patients with IEC-parkinsonism, including orthogonal assessment using multiple anti-BCMA antibody clones alongside RNA in situ hybridization and appropriate control tissues, will be essential to answer this question more definitively.

Finally, proteomic profiling of longitudinal serum samples identified early inflammatory signatures preceding CirAE. Before lymphodepletion, patients who developed CirAE showed higher levels of cytokines implicated in autoimmune disease, including EGF, CCL4, and CCL5, and by day 7 after infusion exhibited increased levels of cytokines associated with ICANS and systemic inflammation (GM-CSF, IL-6, IL-5, IL-13, and CXCL10). Altogether, these data support an early inflammatory window potentially amenable to preemptive intervention. Dexamethasone is currently under investigation as a preemptive strategy in patients with high lymphocyte counts; however, recent evidence suggests that it may be insufficient to prevent neuro-CirAEs.11 Further studies are needed to establish the optimal preemptive approach and to assess whether the CD4:CD8 ratio at apheresis, alongside the lymphocyte count kinetics, may help refine patient selection. Together, our findings identify CD4+ CAR-T cells as key mediators of CAR-T cell-associated immune-related adverse events following BCMA CAR-T therapy. These results provide mechanistic insight into a newly recognized spectrum of delayed toxicities and highlight potential strategies to anticipate and mitigate risk.

As CAR-T therapies continue to achieve durable remissions and extend patient survival, understanding and preventing delayed immune-mediated toxicities will become increasingly important for optimizing long-term outcomes.
