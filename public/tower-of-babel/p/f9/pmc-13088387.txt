Preoperative geriatric nutritional risk index is a reliable tool for predicting postoperative adjacent segment disease among elderly patients with degenerative lumbar diseases: a case control study
Authors: Zhuojie Xiao, Ce Zhu, Qian Chen, Dun Luo, Yongdi Wang, Youwei Ai, Hong Ding, Lei Wang, Chunguang Zhou, Ganjun Feng, Yueming Song, Limin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdjacent segment disease (ASD) is a frequent complication following spinal fusion surgery, particularly prevalent in elderly patients. The nutritional status of elderly patients is also closely related to postoperative outcomes. However, the relationship between nutritional status and the incidence of ASD after spinal surgery remains unknown. This research uniquely evaluates the predictive value of the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) concerning the development of postoperative ASD in elderly individuals (aged 65 years and above) who underwent transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) for degenerative lumbar disorders.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted to assess the outcomes of TLIF in elderly patients with lumbar degenerative disease between 2016 and 2022. Based on the presence or absence of ASD, patients were categorized into the ASD group and the non-ASD group. Demographic, radiographic, and laboratory data were collected. The GNRI was calculated using the baseline serum albumin level and body weight. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were employed to identify potential preoperative risk factors for ASD. Additionally, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was utilized to determine the optimal cutoff values of risk factors for predicting ASD.ResultsA total of 216 patients (aged 69.67 ± 3.46 years) were included in the analysis, of whom 39(18.1%) developed ASD. The mean GNRI was 102.91 ± 7.42 in the non-ASD group and 95.01 ± 7.95 in the ASD group (p๤0.001). On multivariate logistic regression, GNRI was an independent factor in the prediction of ASD following TLIF, and susceptibility to ASD increases significantly with low GNRI (OR 0.88; 95%CI: 0.81 ~ 0.97; p = 0.007). On the receiver operating characteristic curve, the area under the curve (AUC) for GNRI was 0.802 (95%CI: 0.718 ~ 0.887), significantly higher than that of Alb (AUC = 0.675, 95%CI: 0.582 ~ 0.767, p = 0.007) and BMI (AUC = 0.616, 95%CI: 0.521 ~ 0.710, p = 0.022). The cutoff value for GNRI according to the Youden index was 97.05 (sensitivity: 77.4%, specificity: 69.2%).ConclusionOur study revealed a significant correlation between lower GNRI scores and the occurrence of ASD following TLIF. Evaluating GNRI prior to TLIF could serve as an effective strategy for predicting ASD risk in elderly patients with degenerative lumbar conditions, thereby facilitating more informed clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) is a commonly performed surgical procedure for treating lumbar degenerative diseases [1]. Nonetheless, spinal fusions may elevate the strain on the neighboring, untreated segments, leading to the frequent complication of adjacent segment degeneration during long-term follow-up [2]. Initially, adjacent segment degeneration manifests as radiographic changes and later progresses to clinical symptoms, known as adjacent segment disease (ASD) [3]. Previous research has indicated that the incidence of ASD can be as high as 30.3% [3]. ASD causes low back pain and radicular pain, and is a leading reason for revision surgery following lumbar fusion. Elderly patients are particularly vulnerable to ASD due to reduced hydration and elasticity of the intervertebral discs, as well as diminished strength in ligaments, joints, and muscles, which compromise spinal stability and exacerbate postoperative stress on adjacent segments. Therefore, conducting a comprehensive pre-operative risk assessment is essential to reduce the likelihood of this complication in elderly patients.

In recent years, research has identified malnutrition as a crucial risk factor for adverse outcomes following orthopedic surgery [4–6]. Elderly individuals are particularly susceptible to malnutrition due to diminished organ functional reserves and an imbalance between nutrient intake and expenditure [7]. Thus, it is imperative to consider the nutritional status of elderly patients in the preoperative evaluation. The GNRI is a straightforward and reliable tool for evaluating the nutritional status of this population [8]. As reported, GNRI has been proven to be effective in predicting adverse medical events associated with spinal surgery [9]. A recent study has also linked preoperative malnutrition to an increased incidence of cervical kyphosis following laminoplasty [10]. Nevertheless, as far as we know, the relationship between preoperative GNRI and ASD in elderly patients undergoing lumbar fusion has not yet been explored.

This study aimed to investigate whether preoperative GNRI can serve as a predictor for ASD in elderly patients undergoing lumbar fusion. Our goal is to identify a concise and effective predictive tool for forecasting postoperative ASD, which could enhance preoperative interventions and support clinical decision-making to improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Patient

This study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital. The requirement for informed consent was waived, as all patients had previously consented to the use of their anonymized medical data for research and publication purposes.

We enrolled a consecutive cohort of patients at our medical center who underwent Transforaminal Lumbar Interbody Fusion (TLIF) for degenerative spine diseases from 2016 to 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients undergoing single-level TLIF for lumbar degenerative diseases; (2) fusion segment at L4/5 or L5/S1; (3) age ≥ 65 years; (4) well-documented preoperative, postoperative, and follow-up medical records. Single-level TLIF was selected to minimize surgical heterogeneity and reduce the potential confounding effects associated with multi-level procedures. The L4/5 and L5/S1 fusion segments were chosen because they are the most frequently involved segments in degenerative lumbar diseases requiring surgical intervention [11]. Additionally, we selected patients aged ≥ 65 years as this threshold has been widely adopted in previous studies investigating GNRI in elderly populations [9, 10, 12]. Postoperative follow-up visits were routinely scheduled at 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and annually thereafter. During each follow-up, clinical symptoms and radiographic examinations were performed to identify the occurrence of ASD. Exclusion criteria included the presence of spinal infection, tumor, trauma, or a history of prior spinal fusion surgery.

GNRI

To assess the impact of preoperative nutrition on ASD, we utilized the GNRI, an objective and concise tool for nutritional assessment in elderly patients. The GNRI, which combines serum albumin levels and body weight, has demonstrated effectiveness in predicting complications following spinal surgery [9, 10, 12]. It is calculated using the formula: GNRI = 14.89×serum albumin (g/dL) + 41.7×(body weight/ideal body weight) [8]. The ideal body weight is determined using the Lorentz formula: for men, it is height − 100 - (height − 150) / 4; for women, it is height − 100 - (height − 150) / 2.5 [8].

ASD

Patients who met at least one of the following criteria were diagnosed with radiographic ASD: (1) disc height loss of๥3 mm; (2) increase of posterior angulation by๥5°; (3) progression of spondylolisthesis by๥3 mm between the pre-and postoperative standing radiographs. In this study, ASD referred to radiographic ASD accompanied by corresponding clinical symptoms of low back pain and radicular pain in the lower extremities.

Measured data

We extracted baseline characteristics from medical records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), GNRI, smoking and alcohol assumption, diagnosis, fusion segment, Pfirrmann’s grade of the proximal adjacent disc of surgery, Pfirrmann’s grade of the distal adjacent disc of surgery, and the comorbidities suffered. We also collected the last serologic indicators before surgery as follows: blood glucose, serum albumin, triglyceride, total cholesterol, red blood cells, hemoglobin, white blood cells, and lymphocytes. Additionally, we gathered surgery and hospital information such as estimated blood loss, operative time, and length of stay. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at the final follow-up, including the visual analog scale (VAS) for back and leg pain, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and R 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Figures were generated by GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Patients were classified into the ASD group and the non-ASD group. Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent t-test and presented as mean±standard deviation. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher exact test and presented as the number of cases (percentage of total). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were employed to evaluate independent risk factors for ASD, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The predictive performance of the candidate factors for ASD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with areas under the curve (AUC) calculated. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden index. The DeLong’s test was used to compare the statistical differences in the AUCs of the candidate factors. Statistical significance was set at p-values < 0.05.

Results

A total of 216 patients who met the study criteria were included. They were divided into the ASD group and the non-ASD group, with 39 patients (18.1%) in the ASD group. As detailed in Table 1, patients in the ASD group had a significantly lower Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) compared to those in the non-ASD group (95.01 ± 7.95 vs. 102.91 ± 7.42, p < 0.001). Additionally, the ASD group had a significantly higher body mass index (BMI) than the non-ASD group (24.18 ± 2.47 vs. 23.01 ± 2.75, p = 0.015). The Pfirrmann’s grade at both the proximal (p < 0.001) and distal (p < 0.001) adjacent segments was higher in the ASD group compared to the non-ASD group. Other baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the two groups.

We performed logistic regression analysis to evaluate preoperative risk factors for ASD after single-level TLIF (Table 4). Factors with a p-value๤0.1 and factors reported as potential contributors previously were included in the multivariate logistic regression. In univariate logistic regression, GNRI (OR = 0.86, 95%CI: 0.81 ~ 0.91, p๤0.001), Pfirrmann’s grade at the proximal adjacent segment (OR = 2.43, 95%CI:1.68 ~ 3.51, p๤0.001) and Pfirrmann’s grade at the proximal adjacent segment (OR = 3.22, 95%CI:1.61 ~ 6.45, p๤0.001) were identified as potential risk factors for ASD. On top of that, we conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis. Because the variables included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis should be mutually independent, we excluded BMI and Alb, as GNRI was derived from these factors. Only GNRI was considered a significant preoperative risk factor for ASD (OR = 0.89, 95%CI: 0.81 ~ 0.97, p = 0.006). Additionally, we evaluated the predictive value of GNRI, Alb, and BMI for ASD using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The AUC for GNRI was 0.802 (95%CI: 0.718 ~ 0.887) with a cut-off value of 97.05 determined by the Youden index (sensitivity: 77.4%, specificity:69.2%). The AUC for Alb was 0.675 (95% CI: 0.582–0.767), with a cutoff value of 4.08 (sensitivity: 48.0%, specificity: 82.1%). The AUC for BMI was 0.616 (95%CI: 0.521 ~ 0.710), with a cutoff value of 23.87 (sensitivity: 66.1%, specificity: 53.8%). The ROC curve of GNRI was significantly different from that of Alb (p = 0.007) and BMI (p = 0.022) according to DeLong’s test (Fig. 1).

Discussion

ASD is a prevalent complication following lumbar fusion, often presenting with back pain and radiculopathy, and potentially requiring revision surgery [13]. Research indicated that the incidence of ASD after TLIF ranged from 5.2% to 18.5% [14]. In our study, 39 patients (18.1%) developed ASD post-TLIF. The relatively high incidence was considered acceptable in the elderly population, as the adaptive capacity of the spine and intervertebral discs diminishes with advancing age, rendering elderly individuals more vulnerable to the development of ASD [15]. Therefore, accurate assessment and prediction of ASD risk in elderly patients are crucial for enhancing patient outcomes and optimizing treatment strategies.

It has been reported that advanced age, gender, increased BMI, osteoporosis, and preoperative intervertebral disc degeneration of adjacent segments are risk factors for ASD [16–18]. The preoperative degree of adjacent segment degeneration is considered one of the most critical factors contributing to the development of ASD following lumbar fusion surgery [19, 20]. In our study, we observed a higher proportion of advanced degeneration in adjacent segment discs in the ASD group. However, disc imaging grades did not reach statistical significance in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. This may be due to the fact that elderly patients inherently have more advanced disc degeneration, which could have diminished the impact of this factor. Additionally, some studies have suggested that female gender, postmenopausal status, and osteoporosis contribute to the development of ASD following lumbar surgery [17, 21]. We still found a higher proportion of female gender and a lower incidence of osteoporosis in the ASD group, despite the lack of statistical significance. This may be attributed to the fact that all included patients were elderly individuals over 65 years old, which differed from the cohorts in some previous studies. Besides, no significant differences were observed between the ASD and non-ASD groups in terms of smoking, alcohol consumption, diagnosis, surgery segments, comorbidities, estimated blood loss, surgical duration, or length of hospital stay, as detailed in Tables 1 and 2. These factors were well-balanced between the two groups.

Previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between malnutrition and worse outcomes in elderly patients [22, 23]. Factors such as serum albumin, height, and weight significantly influence the nutritional status of older individuals. Serum albumin, a laboratory marker commonly measured during preoperative evaluations, reflects both nutritional status and the inflammatory state of the body and is associated with the severity of various diseases [24]. Preoperative hypoalbuminemia has been closely linked to an increased risk of postoperative infection and mortality in spinal surgeries [12, 25]. In our study, we also found serum albumin levels were lower in the ASD group (Table 2). Body weight is another critical factor affecting lumbar degeneration, as it increases the mechanical load on the lumbar spine and impairs the shock-absorbing capacity of the intervertebral discs [26]. Consistent with previous research, we found that the ASD group had a higher BMI [27–29].

GNRI is a novel and comprehensive measure that integrates a patient’s height, weight, and albumin level to provide a brief and accurate assessment of nutritional status [8]. Previous research has validated GNRI’s effectiveness in predicting surgical complications following spinal surgery. For instance, Acarbas et al. reported an AUC of 0.926 for GNRI in predicting perioperative adverse medical events in elderly patients undergoing spinal procedures [9]. Additionally, Mendiratta et al. identified severe malnutrition, as determined by GNRI, as an independent risk factor for mortality and sepsis in elderly patients undergoing posterior cervical decompression and fusion [12]. Our findings indicated that GNRI was an independent risk factor for ASD following single-level TLIF among elderly patients. A lower GNRI score suggested an increased probability of ASD development (OR 0.88; 95%CI: 0.81 ~ 0.97; p = 0.007). Notably, the predictive validity of GNRI, reflected by an AUC of 0.802, was significantly superior compared to serum albumin (AUC = 0.675, p = 0.007) and BMI (AUC = 0.616, p = 0.022) according to DeLong’s test(Fig. 1). This result confirms that GNRI, as a composite nutritional assessment index combining both serum albumin and a ratio of body weight to ideal body weight, provides a more comprehensive and accurate prediction of postoperative ASD risk than either marker alone. The superior predictive performance of GNRI may be attributed to its integrated nature: serum albumin reflects protein nutritional status and systemic inflammatory status [24], while the body weight to ideal body weight ratio represents energy reserves and overall nutritional adequacy [30]. By integrating these two complementary parameters, GNRI offers a more holistic and sensitive assessment of a patient’s physiological and nutritional condition, which may explain its superior discriminative ability in identifying patients at higher risk for postoperative complications such as ASD. Furthermore, the cutoff value of GNRI for predicting ASD was 97.05, which closely aligned with the previously reported cutoff value of 98 for GNRI risk stratification [8]. Therefore, we speculate that GNRI may indirectly influence the risk of ASD through two primary mechanisms. First, an increased protein intake in the diet can enhance intestinal calcium absorption, suppress parathyroid hormone secretion, and inhibit bone resorption [31, 32]. A low GNRI reflects poor protein nutritional status, which may contribute to decreased bone quality. Second, malnutrition associated with a low GNRI can lead to reduced muscle protein synthesis, resulting in paraspinal muscle atrophy and decreased muscle function [33]. This compromises spinal stability and increases biomechanical stress on adjacent segments following lumbar fusion, thereby contributing to the development of ASD.

Regarding patient-reported outcomes, those in the ASD group reported more severe lower back and leg pain compared to the non-ASD group; however, the severity did not require immediate surgical intervention. We opted for conservative treatment for all patients in the ASD group, resulting in pain relief for each individual. None of the patients in the ASD group required revision surgery, and all are currently under follow-up.

Although this study offers valuable insights, it has several limitations. First, conducting a prospective multicenter study with a larger sample size is recommended to address confounding factors and enhance the stability and generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the follow-up period in this study is relatively short, and the exact onset time of ASD could not be accurately captured, as ASD was identified based on clinical and radiographic findings at scheduled follow-up visits. Radiological parameters were not evaluated in this study, which needs to be addressed in future research. Therefore, we will extend patient follow-up to more comprehensively evaluate the predictive effect of preoperative GNRI on the development of postoperative ASD in the future, aiming to provide more robust and reliable conclusions. Additionally, future studies should combine bone mineral density assessment and paraspinal muscle quality evaluation to further investigate the profound relationship between GNRI and ASD.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a GNRI score below 97.05 is an independent risk factor for postoperative ASD following single-level TLIF in elderly patients. Preoperative GNRI proves to be a potentially valuable tool for predicting the risk of postoperative ASD in this patient population, which may considerably enhance risk assessment and management in clinical practice.
