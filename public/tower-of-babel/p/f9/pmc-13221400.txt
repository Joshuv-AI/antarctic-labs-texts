The FICUS cluster randomized controlled trial of a family support intervention in adult intensive care units: mental health and family functioning outcomes
Authors: Marco Riguzzi, Marie-Madlen Jeitziner, Michael Rufer, Stefanie von Felten, Lotte Verweij, Saskia Oesch, Simone Sutter, Judith Safford, Monique Seraina Wenzler, JoEllen Welter, Jan Wiegand, Dirk Wiechmann, Christoph von Dach, Peter Steiger, Esther Siegrist, Naira Ruch, Urs Pietsch, Michaela Moser, Paola Massarotto, Fabienne Lussmann, Yvonne Keller, Johanna Elisabeth Hoffmann, Benjamin Hertler, Antje Heise, Alexander Dullenkopf, Hanna Burkhalter, Philipp Karl Bühler, Christoph Brunner, Damian Brülhart, Ursula Betschart, Bettina Bergmann-Kipfer, Miodrag Filipovic, Rahel Naef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo examine the effect of a multicomponent, nurse-led, interprofessional family support intervention on family functioning and mental health in adult intensive care units (ICUs).MethodsA cluster-randomized controlled trial in 16 Swiss ICUs compared an intervention—a family care pathway with specially trained ICU family nurses engaging and liaising with families, giving psychoeducational and relationship-focused care, and providing structured, interprofessional communication—to usual care. Family members of patients with an expected ICU stay of ≥ 48 h and a high risk of death, serious impairment, or prolonged mechanical ventilation participated. Outcomes included family functioning, resilience, life satisfaction, quality of life, distress, anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress, assessed at patient ICU admission, discharge, 3-, 6-, and 12 months post-ICU, and analyzed by linear mixed effects models.ResultsAlmost half (43%) of the invited family members participated (885; May 2022 to January 2024). Follow-ups were completed on time by 736 (83.2%), 665 (75.1%), 643 (71.6%), and 593 (67.0%), respectively. Between 609 and 613 were included in the analysis. Family member characteristics were comparable at baseline, yet patient mechanical ventilation (60.3 vs. 49.5%) and ICU death (19.9 vs. 13.2%) were higher in the intervention than the control arm. There was no significant difference between the study arms for any of the outcomes. Type of relationship, prior ICU experience, and patient mechanical ventilation were associated with some of the outcomes. Mental health systematically improved post-ICU.ConclusionNo evidence of a significant improvement in family functioning or mental health was found within the first year after ICU treatment. Due to limitations, the results of our study have to be interpreted cautiously and in a hypothesis-generating manner.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov NCT05280691.Visual abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00134-026-08425-2.

Take-home message

A multicomponent, nurse-led, interprofessional family support intervention, which increased quality of family care outcomes (satisfaction, quality of family-clinician communication, cognitive/emotional support), showed no evidence of improving family functioning or mental health in family members of critically ill adults. Patient mechanical ventilation, being a spouse/partner, and previous ICU experience were associated with many of the investigated outcomes, but not with intervention effectiveness.

Introduction

Family members of adults admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) for critical illness or trauma experience unexpected challenges and high burden, some with devastating consequences for family life [1, 2]. They are at risk of adverse mental health outcomes, termed Postintensive Care Syndrome-Family (PICS-F), which is a constellation of symptoms such as anxiety, depression, posttraumatic stress, or prolonged grief [3, 4], affecting 20–40% of family members of critically ill adults [3, 5]. Besides personal risk factors, such as preexisting mental illness and female gender, care-related risk factors are dissatisfaction with care and poor communication [3, 5, 6]. Interventions to increase the quality of care and communication to the family and to mitigate psychological distress and adverse mental health outcomes have therefore been called for, with ICU nurses playing a central role [7].

Prior nurse-facilitated family-focused interventions, using decision-making support and structured communication strategies, have primarily targeted surrogate decision-makers of persons at high risk of dying in the ICU [8–14], without clear benefit regarding PICS-F. At six months post-ICU, one trial identified a decrease in prolonged grief disorder [10], one decreased depressive symptoms [9], and another one reduced posttraumatic stress [14]. Only two trials have co-investigated communication quality, with contradictory results [8, 12]. Cost benefits of family-focused interventions have been identified [15, 16].

We tested the nurse-led family support intervention (FSI) targeting families of critically ill adults during and after patients’ ICU stay with a cluster-randomized design to avoid contamination (FICUS trial) [17]. The intervention uses a family systems care approach and combines engaging and liaising practices, family assessment, psychoeducation, relationship-focused support, and an interprofessional communication structure [17]. The FICUS trial identified a small improvement in family satisfaction (primary outcome) and a strong increase in quality of communication and emotional/cognitive support to families [18]. A further objective of the FICUS trial was to test whether the intervention improves family functioning and individual family member well-being while reducing mental health symptoms over the first year post-ICU.

Methods

Study design

The cluster-randomized controlled FICUS trial included 16 Swiss adult ICUs (clusters) with a patient and public engagement strategy [17]. The trial was registered with clinicaltrials.gov (04.03.2022) and approved by all appropriate Swiss cantonal ethics committees (No. 2021–02300). Family members were enrolled from May 2022 to January 2024. Informed consent was obtained before study start by a nurse or intensivist. Reporting follows the appropriate CONSORT statements.

Participants

Family members of critically ill patients with an expected length of ICU stay (LoS-ICU) ≥ 48 h and either a high risk of death or long-lasting functional impairment or prolonged mechanical ventilation were eligible if they were a significant other, 18 years or older, cognitively able to participate, and able to complete German-language measures. Refusal of general patient consent for the use of routine clinical data for research led to exclusion. ICUs had to operate ≥ 8 beds and admit ≥ 300 patients per year (LoS-ICU ≥ 48 h). Only one member per family was included in the trial.

Randomization

ICUs were randomized 1:1 to intervention or control using minimization stratified by certification of the ICU (academic/major versus other teaching hospital) and site (a total of 12 hospitals, one with two and one with four study ICUs) [19]. Clinicians, data assessors, and family members were not masked.

Intervention and control

The FSI, described in detail elsewhere [17, 18], is based on a family systems care approach and best practice recommendations for family-centered ICU care, targeting all members of the family [20]. It aims to improve the quality of interaction and communication between families and the ICU team. Specifically, it seeks to reduce family burden, strengthen family illness management and well-being, and mitigate the impact of the critical illness on family members’ mental health. Three intervention elements are combined and delivered along a structured family care pathway, starting with patient ICU admission and continuing into the post-ICU phase, with at least one contact around four weeks post-ICU. Designated ICU nurses delivered the pathway in close collaboration with the interprofessional ICU team. A minimum of five intervention sessions reflecting all three elements was pre-specified. Two to three interested ICU-certified nurses per ICU assumed the family nurse role and received a five-day training in family systems care and the protocolized intervention activities, followed by monthly case conferences and two refresher days [17, 21]. Further implementation activities were tailored to each ICU based on an assessment of barriers and facilitators [21]. This included, for example, nurse and physician leadership support, ICU team information, champions, an implementation support person, and external facilitation by the research team through regular meetings and observations.

The intervention was introduced in addition to and compared to non-standardized usual care as established before trial start (defined as family care practices offered by nurses, intensivists, and others, including visitation, information provision, and communication) [17].

Outcomes

Family functioning, well-being, and mental health symptoms were assessed at patient ICU admission (+4 days), patient ICU discharge or death (−1 day/+4 weeks), and 3 (−14 days/+4 weeks), 6 (−14 days/+6 weeks), and 12 months (−14 days/+6 weeks) after discharge [19] using German versions of psychometrically validated instruments that were completed by one member of the family. The Family Assessment Device - General Functioning Scale (FAD-GF, range 1–4, with 4 representing low functioning) and Brief Resilience Scale (BRS, score range 1–5, 5 representing high resilience) were used to assess family functioning and resilience. Individual family members’ well-being was obtained through the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS-5, score range 5–35, 35 representing high satisfaction), WHO-5 Well-being Index (WHO-5, score range 0–100, 100 representing high psychological well-being), and a visual analog scale (QoL-VAS, score range 0–100, 100 representing high general QoL). Mental health symptoms, namely distress, depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress, were measured using the Distress Thermometer (DT, score range 0–100, 100 representing high distress), Hospital Depression and Anxiety Scale (HADS, score range 0–21, 21 representing worse symptoms), and Impact of Event Scale-6 (IES-6, score range 0–4, 4 representing worse symptoms). These were secondary outcomes of the FICUS trial [17] (eTable 1).

Data collection

Family members were screened for eligibility, informed about the intervention versus usual care and data collection processes, and enrolled by ICU clinicians or research nurses, using a video, flyer, and detailed study information package. Data was collected using REDCap©. After completion of informed consent, participants completed the (1) outcome measures, (2) demographic form, and (3) questions on ICU survivor functional status, support need, or death (post-ICU only) either online through a personalized survey link or by paper–pencil questionnaire, which was entered into REDCap© by research staff. At least one reminder occurred per phone, complemented by email or letter. Patient demographic and health data at ICU admission and discharge were extracted from clinical records. Intervention characteristics and fidelity were recorded in structured logs.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done using R [22] by linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with a random intercept per cluster and a random intercept per family member (nested within clusters) to account for the dependence of participants from the same center and for repeated measurements from the same study participant. The serial autocorrelation of residuals was modelled using a first-order autoregressive correlation structure. The models included the treatment (intervention vs. control), the baseline outcome measurement (if returned within 4 days of ICU admission), the visit (factor with visit T3 as the reference group), and the visit × treatment interaction to assess whether the treatment effect changed over time. In addition, we included the covariates patient age, type of admission (unplanned or planned), mechanical ventilation (yes/no), SAPS-2 and SOFA scores at admission, the type of family relationship (spouse/partner, child, or other), and the family members’ previous ICU experience, which we report as the main model (see [19] for the originally planned statistical analysis). The six-month follow-up was chosen as the reference category for follow-up visits in the models with interaction. One outcome, FAD-GF, was log-transformed to better meet normality assumptions. In addition, the LMMs were fitted (1) using multiple imputation of missing data (and pooling of results according to Rubin’s rules) and (2) without the visit × treatment interaction to estimate an average intervention effect.

Results

Participants

From 12th May 2022 to 1st February 2024, 3181 family members were screened, and 885 participated (42.7% of those invited), with 473 in the control and 412 in the intervention arm (eTable 2). Loss to follow-up was comparable between study arms and ranged between 6.3 to 9.7% per timepoint (Fig. 1). Intervention characteristics and fidelity

The intervention was delivered by 22 ICU-certified family nurses with a median work experience of 19 years (IQR, 2.5–29.0). Most interventionists (n = 18) were ICU team members and involved in patient care. A few, known to the ICU team, worked in another hospital unit. Interventions were delivered by family nurses to a single (53.5%) or several family members, and 22.2% of interventions were team-delivered. Fidelity consistency to the pathway was achieved by 23.1% (n = 95) of cases, representing a minimum of five intervention sessions covering all three intervention components within the pre-defined timeframe (Table 1). The total intervention duration per family (fidelity dose) amounted to 250 min at the median, which equals 25.0 min (IQR = 18.7) per patient LoS-ICU. Fidelity frequency had a median of 11 contacts overall, or 1.1 (IQR = 0.7) per patient LoS-ICU [18]. Details are reported in eTables 3–4.

Intervention effectiveness at 6 months

No significant difference between the control and intervention arms was observed for the family functioning, resilience, life satisfaction, quality of life, anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress outcomes (Table 3, significant results with p < 0.05 are shown in bold). Associations between covariates and family functioning and mental health outcomes

Resilience was higher in family members with prior ICU experience as a patient (+0.17, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.32, p = 0.035) (Table 3). Life satisfaction was lower for spouses/partners of patients compared to all other relationships (point estimate −1.64 to −1.10, p ≤ 0.011). Quality of life (WHO-5) scores were lower in the case of mechanical ventilation at ICU admission (−4.45, 95% CI −7.55 to −1.35, p = 0.005) and for spouses/partners (vs. parents of adult patients; −4.39, 95% CI −8.73 to −0.05, p = 0.047), and they increased with patient age (+0.11 per year, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.21, p = 0.031). QoL-VAS scores were lower for spouses/partners (vs. parents of adult patients; −4.19, 95% CI −7.50 to −0.88, p = 0.013).

Anxiety was higher in the case of mechanical ventilation at ICU admission (+0.56, 95% CI 0.05 to 1.07, p = 0.031) and for spouses/partners (vs. the family member being an adult child of the patient; +0.70, 95% CI 0.12 to 1.28, p = 0.018). Depression was higher in spouses/partners compared to all other relationships (point estimate +0.86 to +1.13, p ≤ 0.015) and after prior ICU experience as a patient (+1.09, 95% CI 0.13 to 2.04, p = 0.026).

Discussion

In this cluster-randomized trial with 885 family members of critically ill adults, we examined a nurse-led family care pathway with engagement and liaison, support, and interprofessional communication as core elements [18]. The intervention did not show improvements in family functioning or mental health—the secondary outcomes of the FICUS trial [17, 18]—within the first year post-ICU. Our results align with previous trials on nurse-led or facilitated, family-focused interventions, which mostly failed to demonstrate effects on mental health outcomes up to six months post-ICU [8, 11, 12], with two exceptions [9, 13], in comparison to non-standardized, usual care. Life satisfaction was lower in the intervention arm in the multiple imputation model. A similar observation has been made in the Four Supports trial, which found higher posttraumatic stress in the intervention arm [8]. In our study, differences in patient mechanical ventilation and death between study arms may explain slightly less favorable outcomes in the intervention arm, as evidenced in the sensitivity analyses, but these differences were not associated with the intervention’s effectiveness.

As previously shown, psychological sequelae declined during the first six months [3, 13], indicating a stabilization and adaptation process independent of the intervention. Some patient and family-related characteristics were associated with several outcomes, but not with intervention effectiveness. First, mechanical ventilation increased family member anxiety and decreased quality of life, which has been previously noted [23, 24], and is not surprising given that it is indicative of severe illness, life-sustaining treatment, and communication challenges. Second, family members with prior experience as an ICU patient had both higher resilience and higher depression, suggesting that prior ICU exposure may support their role as family members [25], but also adds to their mental burden. Third, spouses/partners had higher anxiety and depression and lower satisfaction and quality of life than other family members. Prior research has similarly demonstrated that the effectiveness of coping responses varies with the relationship to the patient [26]. These differences imply that family-focused interventions should be adaptive to the individual patient, family, and relationship contexts.

FICUS, together with the recently published, mono-center Caregiver Pathway Intervention study [13, 14], is the first trial of a nurse-led, family-focused intervention in ICUs to collect one-year follow-up data and to assess quality of life, life satisfaction, family functioning, and resilience [18]. The intervention is rooted in a family systems approach, which has only been used in a post-ICU nurse-led intervention [27, 28], with favorable effects on family functioning at one year [27]. In a qualitative evaluation with families, we observed a high need for post-ICU care [29], and it may therefore be important to increase intensity and duration of follow-up to families, which has also been suggested elsewhere [8]. Post-ICU care delivered by the same family nurse who is part of the ICU team to ensure continuity of care may be particularly important because family members of critically ill patients are especially vulnerable due to either bereavement or persistent illness with caregiving responsibilities, both resulting in an ongoing need for support [4, 30, 31]. One review highlights the importance of bereavement follow-up [32], including information on grief together with the identification and referral of those at risk for prolonged grief to specialist support [33, 34], which was part of the intervention, albeit limited to the first four to 12 weeks post-ICU. Families appreciated having a trusted and familiar person to turn to, even after patient ICU discharge, as evidenced in a qualitative sub-study [29], together with proactive, skilled communication and emotional, behavioral, and practical support [18], which are important implications for nursing practice. Further research is needed to clarify the optimal timing, content, and delivery of such interventions along the patient trajectories, including along the post-ICU continuum [2, 4, 35]. As previously suggested, specialist mental health support may need to complement preventive, nurse-led, collaborative care by the ICU team to families affected by critical illness and its consequences [2, 8, 11]. Also, in end-of-life situations, a physician-driven communication strategy has been found to reduce prolonged grief disorder and grief intensity [10] and may therefore be of value. Despite a failure of nurse-led or -facilitated interventions to consistently produce a measurable impact on PICS-F, the FICUS trial [18], together with the PARTNER [12] and the Caregiver Pathway Intervention trials [13, 14], provides convincing evidence that nurse-led family care pathways with emotional, behavioral, cognitive, and practical support by designated nurses who are part of the ICU team, combined with structured, interprofessional communication, are of benefit for families.

Limitations

This study has several limitations [18]. First, our study included a small number of clusters, and there were differences between the control and intervention arms in ICU characteristics, patient-related baseline characteristics, and patient outcome (higher mortality in the intervention arm despite comparable SAPS-2 scores). To minimize the danger of confounding, we therefore controlled for several patient and family member characteristics, including illness severity (mechanical ventilation, SOFA and SAPS-2). However, there may be unmeasured confounders at the ICU or patient level which we were not able to adjust for, such as patient case mix, quality of patient care, treatment guidelines, or organizational structures and processes. Causal interpretations of the trial results may therefore be limited. Second, the large proportion of missing outcome data, particularly at baseline, was handled by multiple imputation. The underlying assumption of missingness at random, which also underlies the mixed-effects models, cannot be verified. Exploration of missingness per cluster revealed different levels and patterns over time between ICUs, but not between study arms. Nevertheless, non-random missingness may distort the results. Third, while fidelity to each prespecified intervention element was moderate to high, combined fidelity to all prespecified time points in the family care pathway was low. Deviations from the predefined timeline and minimal dose may mean that many families did not get a sufficient intervention dose. Fourth, while attrition rates were comparable between study arms and acceptable overall, the analysis included about only 70% of the sample because of the late return of the baseline questionnaire that served as a covariate. Fifth, post-ICU patient illness trajectories may have influenced family functioning and mental health, which we were unable to assess with this trial. Sixth, socially disadvantaged and non-German-speaking families and families with a high burden may be underrepresented in our sample.

Conclusion

In conclusion, no evidence was found that the multicomponent, nurse-led, interprofessional FSI improves short- or long-term family functioning or well-being or mitigates PICS-F in families of critically ill adults in the Swiss healthcare context. Our findings are consistent with those reported in other randomized, clinical trials of nurse-led or -facilitated family care pathways. Due to the limitations, the results of our study have to be interpreted cautiously and in a hypothesis-generating manner. Overall, this evolving body of research suggests that these nurse-led interventions, while effective in improving the quality of family care and communication and economically feasible, may not translate into reduced mental health burden post-ICU.
