Automated MRI-based brain volumetric analysis in women with polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Nisa Baspinar, Sultan Salk, Aysenur Ersoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePolycystic ovary syndrome is associated with complex endocrine and metabolic disturbances that may influence brain structure. This study aimed to evaluate global and regional brain volumes in women with polycystic ovary syndrome using automated magnetic resonance imaging–based volumetric analysis with age- and sex-adjusted normative percentiles, and to investigate their associations with hormonal and metabolic parameters.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 48 women with polycystic ovary syndrome who underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging. Automated volumetric analysis was performed on high-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted images using Quantib® Brain automated volumetry software. Total brain volume, lobar volumes, hippocampal volumes, cerebellar volume, and cerebrospinal fluid volume were quantified and normalized to intracranial volume. Normative percentile values were used instead of a separate healthy control group. Volumetric measures were compared according to insulin resistance status and luteinizing hormone/follicle-stimulating hormone ratio, and multivariable regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of brain volume measures.ResultsGlobal and lobar brain volumes did not differ significantly according to insulin resistance, total testosterone, or dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate levels. Cerebrospinal fluid volume was higher in insulin-resistant individuals; however, this association did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons. In contrast, hippocampal volumes differed significantly according to the luteinizing hormone/follicle-stimulating hormone ratio. Women with an LH/FSH ratio ≥ 2 exhibited lower total hippocampal volume, predominantly driven by reduced left hippocampal volume (p = 0.009).ConclusionAutomated magnetic resonance imaging–based volumetric analysis demonstrated largely preserved global and lobar brain volumes in women with polycystic ovary syndrome. However, an elevated luteinizing hormone/follicle-stimulating hormone ratio was selectively associated with reduced hippocampal volume, suggesting region-specific neuroanatomical vulnerability potentially related to gonadotropin dysregulation.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine disorders affecting women of reproductive age and is characterized by hyperandrogenism, oligo-anovulation, and polycystic ovarian morphology, frequently accompanied by insulin resistance, obesity, and chronic low-grade inflammation [1–4]. Beyond its reproductive and metabolic manifestations, PCOS is increasingly recognized as a systemic condition with potential effects on the central nervous system (CNS), including structural and functional brain alterations suggested by recent neuroimaging studies in women with PCOS [5]. However, the nature and extent of structural brain alterations associated with PCOS remain incompletely understood.

In the general population, metabolic disturbances such as obesity, insulin resistance, and impaired glucose metabolism have been consistently associated with alterations in brain morphology. Large-scale volumetric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have demonstrated associations between increased body mass index and reduced global and regional gray matter volumes, particularly involving the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and temporal lobes—regions implicated in cognition, memory, and metabolic regulation [1–4]. Similarly, patients with type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome exhibit both global and regional brain volume reductions, suggesting that chronic metabolic stress may contribute to neuroanatomical vulnerability [6, 7]. Recent neuroimaging studies have further shown that metabolic risk factors such as central obesity and insulin resistance are associated with structural brain changes and increased risk of cognitive decline [8]. Given the high prevalence of metabolic abnormalities in PCOS, it is plausible that similar brain structural patterns may be observed in this population.

Neuroimaging studies specifically focusing on women with PCOS remain limited and methodologically heterogeneous. Structural MRI investigations have yielded mixed results, with some studies reporting reduced total brain or regional gray matter volumes—particularly in obese PCOS cohorts—while others have found largely preserved global brain morphology [9]. Functional neuroimaging studies have provided complementary evidence of altered cerebral glucose metabolism and abnormal activation patterns in PCOS, especially within frontal and limbic networks involved in appetite regulation and cognitive processing [10]. Together, these findings suggest that potential CNS alterations in PCOS are likely to be subtle and region-specific rather than diffuse. Several studies have also suggested that metabolic status, particularly obesity and insulin resistance, may partially modulate neuroimaging findings in women with PCOS [11].

PCOS is also characterized by complex endocrine disturbances, including altered gonadotropin secretion patterns and hyperandrogenism. Although circulating hormones and metabolic signals are known to interact with central neuroendocrine pathways, direct and consistent associations between specific hormonal parameters and regional brain volumetric measures in women with PCOS have not been firmly established. Importantly, existing neuroimaging studies in this field are generally small, cross-sectional, and exploratory in nature, limiting robust mechanistic interpretation.

A major methodological challenge in PCOS neuroimaging research is the frequent reliance on external healthy control groups. Differences in demographic characteristics, scanner hardware, acquisition protocols, and image processing pipelines may introduce substantial variability, thereby complicating cross-study comparisons. Recent advances in automated MRI-based volumetric analysis allow the generation of age- and sex-adjusted normative percentile values derived from large reference datasets. Automated brain volumetry tools are increasingly used in clinical neuroimaging to provide standardized quantification of regional brain volumes across populations [12]. This approach enables individual and group-level brain volumes to be contextualized within standardized normative distributions, providing an alternative framework for descriptive and exploratory analyses without direct reliance on locally acquired control cohorts.

Accordingly, this exploratory study aimed to characterize global and regional brain volumes in women with PCOS using fully automated MRI-based volumetry and age- and sex-adjusted normative percentiles. The primary objective was to contextualize brain volumes within a standardized normative framework, while a secondary objective was to explore associations between regional brain volume percentiles and selected endocrine and metabolic parameters commonly altered in PCOS. This approach provides a transparent, standardized description of brain structural characteristics and generates hypotheses for future longitudinal studies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study included women diagnosed with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) who underwent brain MRI at the Department of Radiology, Sivas Cumhuriyet University, between January 2023 and January 2025. PCOS diagnosis was established according to the Rotterdam criteria, requiring the presence of at least two of the following features: oligo- or anovulation, clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound [13–15].

Women aged 18–45 years with complete hormonal laboratory data and high-quality 3D T1-weighted MRI scans suitable for automated volumetric analysis were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included neurological or psychiatric disorders, history of intracranial surgery or trauma, congenital brain anomalies, space-occupying lesions, demyelinating disease, inadequate image quality, missing volumetric data, or use of medications known to affect hormonal status. After applying these criteria, a total of 48 women were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Demographic and clinical variables, including age and body mass index (BMI), were retrieved from medical records when available.

Hormonal and metabolic assessment

Venous blood samples were collected during the early follicular phase of the menstrual cycle (days 2–5). Serum levels of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), estradiol (E2), total testosterone (TTES), dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), prolactin (PRL), fasting glucose, and fasting insulin were obtained from hospital laboratory records. Hormonal analyses were performed as part of routine clinical evaluation using standardized automated immunoassays at the institutional laboratory. For descriptive purposes, TTES and DHEAS levels were interpreted according to the upper limits of the institutional laboratory reference ranges; however, all hormonal parameters were analyzed as continuous variables.

Insulin resistance was calculated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) formula: HOMA-IR = (fasting glucose × fasting insulin) / 405 [16].Values > 2.5 were considered indicative of insulin resistance, consistent with commonly used thresholds in PCOS research [16, 17]. The LH/FSH ratio was calculated for each participant, and a ratio ≥ 2 was used to define relative gonadotropin imbalance for exploratory subgroup analyses rather than diagnostic classification [13, 14].

MRI acquisition

MRI examinations were performed using a 1.5-Tesla scanner (Magnetom Aera, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 20-channel head coil. Volumetric analysis was based on high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE sequences with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) 1600 ms; echo time (TE) 2.45 ms; slice thickness 1 mm; matrix size 256 × 256; field of view 256 mm.

Automated volumetric analysis

Automated volumetric measurements were obtained using Quantib® Brain software (Quantib BV, Rotterdam, The Netherlands). The software performs fully automated segmentation of brain structures using a multi-atlas approach with probabilistic label propagation, in which manually annotated atlas images are non-rigidly registered to each subject scan and probabilistic tissue labels are derived through label fusion—an approach conceptually comparable to the non-rigid registration–based lobar segmentation strategy validated in large population-based neuroimaging cohorts such as the Rotterdam Scan Study [18]. Hippocampal segmentation is performed using atlas-guided label propagation combined with graph-cut optimization.

The normative reference dataset used for percentile generation is derived from a large multicenter population spanning ages 18–90 years, with normative curves fitted using age- and sex-stratified regression models. As all participants in the present study were female, the sex-adjustment component was inherently fixed to the female normative reference stratum and therefore did not contribute additional variance adjustment beyond age correction in this cohort. This represents a standard output of the software pipeline designed for mixed-sex clinical applications.

Total brain volume, intracranial volume (ICV), lobar volumes (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital), bilateral hippocampal volumes, cerebellar volume, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) volume were quantified. All volumetric measures were normalized to intracranial volume (ICV) and expressed as age- and sex-adjusted normative percentile values derived from the Quantib® Brain reference database. These percentile values were used for subsequent statistical analyses to contextualize individual brain volumes relative to an age- and sex-matched reference population rather than to perform direct case–control comparisons.

Segmentation outputs were visually inspected for quality control. Scans with motion artifacts or segmentation errors were excluded. Representative examples of automated segmentation and percentile output reports are shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test, and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Group comparisons according to HOMA-IR categories (< 2.5 vs. ≥2.5) and LH/FSH ratio (< 2 vs. ≥2) were performed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data distribution.

Associations between brain volumetric percentile values and hormonal or metabolic parameters were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, given the exploratory nature of the study and the non-normal distribution of several variables. The variables included in the correlation analyses were age, LH, FSH, estradiol, TTES, DHEAS, prolactin, fasting glucose, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR.

To identify independent predictors of regional brain volumes, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed including age, hormonal parameters, and metabolic variables as potential explanatory factors.

A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because multiple volumetric parameters were examined, Bonferroni correction was applied to control for potential inflation of type I error. For analyses involving 20 comparisons, the adjusted significance threshold was set at p < 0.0025.

Comparison according to HOMA-IR categories

Comparison of volumetric MRI parameters between participants with HOMA-IR < 2.5 and ≥ 2.5 showed no statistically significant differences in global or regional brain volumes. Total brain volume was similar between groups (1151.29 ± 113.11 cm³ vs. 1117.54 ± 110.70 cm³; p = 0.302). Frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobe volumes likewise did not differ significantly across HOMA-IR categories (all p > 0.05).

In the regression analysis, HOMA-IR emerged as the strongest predictor of total brain volume; however, this association did not reach statistical significance (β = − 0.303, p = 0.068). In the multivariable linear regression model, none of the hormonal parameters—including age, FSH, LH, estradiol, TTES, DHEAS, and prolactin—showed a significant association with total frontal lobe volume (all p > 0.05). HOMA-IR was the only variable independently associated with frontal lobe volume (β = − 0.346, p = 0.038), corresponding to an approximate decrease of 6.3 cm³ per unit increase in HOMA-IR.

Although FSH showed a similar negative trend, none of the hormonal parameters—including LH, estradiol, TTES, DHEAS, and prolactin—demonstrated a significant independent association with total brain volume.

The CSF volume was higher in the insulin-resistant group compared with the non–insulin-resistant group (232.58 ± 36.80 cm³ vs. 212.00 ± 23.17 cm³; uncorrected p = 0.025); however, this difference did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction. After applying Bonferroni correction for 20 comparisons (adjusted significance threshold: p < 0.0025), none of the associations between HOMA-IR categories and brain volumetric parameters remained statistically significant.

Hippocampal and cerebellar volumes did not differ significantly between HOMA-IR groups (all p > 0.05). These findings are summarized in Table 3.

Comparison according to the LH/FSH ratio

Most volumetric measures did not differ significantly between participants with LH/FSH < 2 and ≥ 2, including global brain volume, lobar matter volumes, and cerebellar volume (all p > 0.05).

In contrast, hippocampal measurements revealed a significant group difference according to the LH/FSH ratio. Participants with an LH/FSH ratio ≥ 2 showed lower total hippocampal volume (5.10 ± 0.90 cm³ vs. 5.73 ± 0.68 cm³; p = 0.025), driven predominantly by reduced left hippocampal volume (p = 0.009). Right hippocampal volume showed a non-significant trend (p = 0.074).

In the multiple regression model, LH was the only significant predictor of total hippocampal volume (β = − 0.406, p = 0.025), indicating that higher LH levels were associated with reduced hippocampal volume.

Comparison according to total testosterone and DHEAS

There were no significant differences in global or regional brain volumes between participants with normal versus elevated TTES levels (all p > 0.05). Likewise, volumetric measures did not significantly differ according to DHEAS categories (all p > 0.05). Accordingly, these parameters were not retained in the main comparison table.

Discussion

By integrating fully automated MRI-based volumetric analysis with concurrent endocrine and metabolic profiling, the present study provides a standardized and exploratory assessment of global and regional brain volumes in women with PCOS. Using age- and sex-adjusted normative percentile values rather than a locally recruited control group, this approach aimed to contextualize brain structural measures within a reference framework and to generate hypotheses regarding potential neuroendocrine correlates of PCOS-related brain alterations. Age- and sex-adjusted normative percentile values derived from large reference datasets provide a standardized framework for interpreting individual brain volumes relative to expected population distributions. This approach reduces variability related to locally recruited control cohorts and scanner differences. However, normative comparisons may be less sensitive than direct case–control designs in detecting subtle regional effects, and therefore small volumetric differences should be interpreted cautiously.

A principal finding of this study was the absence of significant differences in global and lobar brain volumes across categories defined by insulin resistance, androgen status, or LH/FSH ratio. This observation is consistent with previous structural MRI studies suggesting that overall brain morphology is largely preserved in women with PCOS, particularly in non-obese or mixed cohorts [9, 10]. Saydam et al. demonstrated that reductions in total brain and gray matter volumes were observed predominantly in obese women with PCOS, whereas lean women showed preserved global brain volumes, highlighting the potential influence of metabolic status rather than PCOS itself on gross brain structure [9]. Functional neuroimaging studies further suggest that PCOS-related neural alterations may manifest at metabolic or functional levels rather than as overt volumetric changes [5, 10]. Collectively, these findings support the notion that central nervous system alterations in PCOS are likely to be subtle and region-specific rather than diffuse.

An exploratory association between insulin resistance and increased CSF volume was observed in our cohort, although this finding did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons. In non-PCOS populations, insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism have been associated with reduced brain parenchymal volume and relative expansion of CSF spaces, potentially reflecting early metabolic stress on brain tissue [6, 7, 19]. Kullmann et al. proposed that central insulin resistance may represent a mechanistic link between metabolic dysfunction and brain vulnerability [20]. While the present findings should be interpreted cautiously, the observed trend remains biologically plausible and warrants further investigation in larger, adequately powered cohorts. Emerging evidence from longitudinal neuroimaging studies suggests that metabolic dysregulation and vascular risk factors may accelerate structural brain changes over time, highlighting the importance of prospective imaging approaches to better understand the temporal dynamics of brain alterations [21, 22].

The most notable region-specific finding of this study was the association between an elevated LH/FSH ratio and reduced hippocampal volume. Women with an LH/FSH ratio ≥ 2 demonstrated smaller total and left hippocampal volumes compared with those with lower ratios. Clinical and experimental evidence suggests that modulation of luteinizing hormone signaling may influence hippocampal-dependent cognitive processes, and suppression of LH secretion has been associated with improvements in cognitive performance in non-PCOS populations [23]. Recent studies have also highlighted potential links between gonadotropin signaling and hippocampal structure and function, supporting the hypothesis that hormonal dysregulation may influence limbic brain regions. Within the context of PCOS—characterized by chronically elevated LH secretion—our findings raise the hypothesis that sustained gonadotropin imbalance may be associated with hippocampal structural vulnerability. Importantly, this interpretation remains exploratory and does not imply causality. Asymmetric hippocampal vulnerability has been reported in previous neuroimaging studies. For example, Shu et al. [24]. demonstrated that left hippocampal volume reduction was more pronounced than right-sided changes in patients with post-traumatic stress disorder. These findings suggest that the hippocampus may exhibit lateralized susceptibility to stress-related or neuroendocrine influences, which could partly explain the predominance of left-sided hippocampal alterations observed in our cohort.

Despite well-established hyperandrogenism in PCOS, no significant differences in brain volumetric measures were observed across TTES or DHEAS categories. This finding aligns with previous neuroimaging studies reporting preserved global brain volumes in women with PCOS despite elevated androgen levels [7, 8], suggesting that androgen-related neural effects may preferentially involve functional, metabolic, or microstructural pathways rather than gross anatomical changes detectable by volumetric MRI.

The relatively large variability observed in hormonal and metabolic parameters likely reflects the well-recognized clinical and endocrine heterogeneity of PCOS, which encompasses a broad spectrum of metabolic and hormonal phenotypes and is strongly associated with insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia, both of which play central roles in its pathophysiology [25].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The modest sample size may have limited the ability to detect small-to-moderate effects, particularly after correction for multiple comparisons. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and single-time-point hormonal measurements may not fully reflect long-term endocrine exposure. In addition, the use of normative percentile values, while offering a standardized reference framework, is not intended to replace well-matched case–control designs but rather to complement existing methodologies in exploratory settings. Furthermore, the automated volumetric approach used in this study focuses primarily on global and major regional brain structures and therefore may be less sensitive than higher-resolution parcellation or atlas-based segmentation techniques in detecting subtle alterations within smaller subcortical regions. Consequently, more fine-grained regional effects related to endocrine or metabolic factors may not have been fully captured. Future studies using higher-resolution parcellation approaches and advanced neuroimaging techniques may help to further characterize region-specific brain alterations in women with PCOS. In addition, due to the retrospective design of the study, detailed clinical variables such as BMI, specific Rotterdam phenotype classification, and duration of PCOS symptoms were not consistently available in the medical records. Nevertheless, the use of automated segmentation, rigorous quality control, and standardized statistical analyses strengthens the internal consistency of the present findings.

Conclusion

In this study, comprehensive automated volumetric MRI analysis demonstrated that global and lobar brain volumes are largely preserved in women with polycystic ovary syndrome, despite substantial metabolic and endocrine heterogeneity. However, a selective and clinically relevant association was identified between elevated LH/FSH ratio and reduced hippocampal volume—particularly involving the left hippocampus—suggesting region-specific neuroanatomical vulnerability rather than diffuse brain involvement in PCOS.

The observed trend toward increased CSF volume in insulin-resistant individuals, although not sustained after correction for multiple comparisons, supports the hypothesis that metabolic dysfunction may contribute to early compartmental brain alterations before overt parenchymal volume loss becomes apparent.

Taken together, these findings indicate that PCOS-related brain changes are subtle, hormonally mediated, and region-specific, with gonadotropin dynamics—particularly LH excess—emerging as a potential neuroendocrine determinant of hippocampal structure. Future longitudinal studies integrating neurocognitive assessment, endocrine profiling, and advanced neuroimaging techniques are warranted to clarify whether hippocampal alterations precede, accompany, or follow endocrine dysregulation in PCOS.
