A qualitative exploration of clinicians’ experiences and perceptions of a well-being leadership development training program
Authors: Kristi L. Roybal, Tricia T. James, Laura L. Chun, Karina M. Squire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare worker burnout is a national challenge, with implications for healthcare worker well-being and mental health, the quality of patient care, and healthcare costs. The Clinician Wellness Council (CWC), a 15-month well-being leadership development training program, was designed and implemented to provide practicing clinicians (“CWC members”) across several clinical groups within the Providence health system with skills, coaching, and community to address the local drivers of their clinical groups’ burnout. The purpose of this study was to explore CWC members’ experiences with and perceptions of the impact of this program on their well-being leadership development, including their knowledge, insight, and mindset toward addressing workplace burnout.MethodsWe conducted four virtual focus groups with a total of 21 Providence clinicians who recently completed the well-being leadership development training program. Focus groups were analyzed using a rapid qualitative analysis method.ResultsWe generated three themes in our analysis: transformed well-being mindsets, guiding well-being leadership principles, and cultivating factors for well-being leader development.ConclusionsIn-depth insights from our analysis suggest a positive impact on participating clinicians. They shared a transformational well-being leadership development experience that included mindset shifts and the emergence of guiding well-being leadership principles. They also reported a reduction in burnout and increased confidence in their capacity to effect change. Participating clinicians highlighted health system financial commitment, relationships and sense of community, and knowledge and skill-building as the three structural program components that cultivated their development as well-being leaders. Study findings can inform other health systems seeking to design and implement a similar training program to support clinician well-being leadership development and address local drivers of clinician burnout.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-15058-0.

Background

Healthcare worker burnout is a nationally recognized issue that was declared a public health crisis by the U.S. Surgeon General in 2022 [1]. Though healthcare worker burnout was an issue before the COVID-19 pandemic [2, 3], national burnout rates increased during the pandemic as clinicians, nurses, and other frontline healthcare workers operated under extraordinarily stressful conditions [4–6]. Healthcare worker burnout rates remain alarmingly high, with a reported prevalence of nearly 50% for physicians and 45% for nurses [2, 4, 6–9].

Burnout is characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and diminished feelings of personal accomplishment [10]. The adverse consequences of clinician and nurse burnout are well established and include increased rates of depression, substance use, suicidal ideation, and suicide [11–17]. Evidence also suggests that clinician and nurse burnout negatively impacts the quality of patient care, decreases patient satisfaction, and leads to increased medical errors [18–21]. Healthcare costs increase as burnout creates higher rates of turnover and reduced clinical hours worked [18, 22, 23].

Historically, well-being interventions have primarily targeted individuals [19, 24, 25]. More recently, approaches to address burnout have shifted as evidence indicates that organizational factors, including high workloads, burdensome and inefficient work processes, lack of organizational support, and loss of control and meaning in the workplace, are the largest drivers of burnout and that organizational changes are more effective at reducing burnout than targeting individual factors [19, 24, 26]. While healthcare organizations’ commitment to organizational changes is critical for promoting healthcare worker well-being, there is growing acknowledgement that drivers of burnout vary at the clinical group level [27–29]. Therefore, organizational efforts to reduce burnout and promote well-being will require clinical group-level interventions that respond to local challenges, needs, and opportunities.

Building on the success of a pilot intervention focused on well-being leadership development and clinical group burnout among one group of hospitalists at the Providence Portland Medical Center during the COVID-19 pandemic [30], the Clinician Wellness Council (CWC) was developed. The CWC is a 15-month well-being leadership development training program designed to provide practicing clinicians across differing specialties and locations within the Providence footprint with skills, coaching, and community to reduce their clinical groups’ burnout through tailored well-being interventions. This interventional approach is grounded in both transformational and distributed leadership theories, which together emphasize the role of frontline clinical leaders in influencing their clinical groups’ culture and workplace experience [31, 32]. By intentionally expanding leadership capacity across our large, complex health system, the CWC seeks to address local drivers of burnout and strengthen well-being culture across the organization. The program design also recognizes that employment in large healthcare organizations can deteriorate clinicians’ autonomy and perceived ability to influence their workplace. Therefore, the program centers Covey’s model of spheres of concern, influence, and control to foster mindset shifts and transformational leadership behaviors among participating clinicians (“CWC members”) [33].

The CWC was designed with a goal of recruiting clinicians (MD/DO, APP) who do not hold formal leadership positions within Providence. CWC members are asked to identify an intervention group, comprising teammates from their clinical group, to focus their leadership efforts. Intervention groups are not limited to clinicians. They can reflect the multidisciplinarity of CWC members’ clinical groups.

James et al. describe in-depth how the first cohort of CWC members was recruited from the Providence Portland Medical Center and Providence Central Division [34]. All medical staff from both employed and affiliate groups at Providence Portland Medical Center were invited to participate through email. Department leaders were sent direct invitations as well to ensure representation across departments. An additional 10 participants from the Providence Central Division were recruited through direct email outreach to Chief Medical Officers, extending representation and potential program impact to rural areas and 2 additional states in the Providence footprint.

The first cohort of CWC members attended a full day in-person kick-off education event, monthly educational sessions and small group sessions for 15 months, and a wrap-up event. Educational sessions focused on leadership topics, including effective communication, gathering high yield information, effective meeting facilitation practices, effective advocacy, navigating conflict, and understanding spheres of control, influence, and concern (see Additional file 1 for a complete list of educational topics). Educational sessions were held at two different times each month to encourage full participation. Small group sessions, held once monthly, created opportunities for peer support and coaching, with CWC members grouped by clinical setting: outpatient, hospitalists, medical subspecialists, and hospital-based specialties. Members focused on identifying workplace stressors unique to their own clinical group and developing and implementing interventions within their sphere of control to address those stressors. We provide a complete list of clinical groups represented in Additional file 2. Participation was voluntary and each CWC member was reimbursed $1000/month for the program duration for working an average of 2 h per week on program-related activities, with the exception of one member who already had FTE dedicated to well-being leadership. Funding was obtained from a grant from the Providence Portland Medical Foundation for participating clinicians from the Providence Portland Medical Center and a combination of system and local funds for participating clinicians from the Providence Central Division. CWC members’ clinical expectations were not decreased during the program.

Conducted parallel to a quantitative evaluation of the effectiveness of the first CWC cohort in decreasing clinical group burnout [34], the aim of this qualitative study was to explore CWC members’ experiences with and perceptions of the impact of program on their well-being leadership development, including their knowledge, insight, and mindset toward addressing workplace burnout. Given the complexities of leadership development, qualitative inquiry was well-suited to gaining a rich, in-depth understanding of CWC members’ leadership development experiences and elucidating possible mechanisms of program impact.

Methods

Design

We conducted this qualitative study between November 2024 and February 2025 with the first cohort of Providence clinicians who had recently completed the well-being leadership development training program (CWC). This study was approved by the Providence Health and Systems Institutional Review Board (STUDY2023000731). We followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guidelines [35].

Study team and reflexivity

The study team included: (1) the Director of the Providence Medical Group Department of Clinician Experience and Principal Investigator of the CWC evaluation (T.J.); (2) the department’s Clinical Program Manager (L.C.); (3) a Providence APRN working in primary care who participated in the well-being leadership development training program (K.S.); and (4) a Ph.D.-level qualitative researcher from the Providence Health Research Accelerator with no preexisting relationships with study participants (K.R.). K.R. led the qualitative study design, consulting T.J. and L.C. for input on recruitment materials, discussion guide development, and focus group scheduling to ensure alignment with the study purpose. K.R. facilitated all focus groups and was supported by a research associate with notetaking. This research associate left her role at Providence prior to data analysis, therefore K.R. analyzed the data independently. K.R.’s positionality as an independent researcher with no relationship to the CWC enhanced the trustworthiness of this study, mitigating the possibility of social desirability bias and confirmation bias during data collection and analysis. Additionally, her methodological training and expertise positioned her to engage in reflexive practices throughout the study to further strengthen the credibility of findings. K.R. wrote reflexive analytic memos throughout each step of data collection and analysis to critically reflect on how her positionality may have influenced focus group facilitation and the analytic insights generated in the study.

Participants

We purposively recruited the first cohort of CWC members (n = 21), which included 20 physicians (MD/DO) and one nurse practitioner, to participate in focus groups scheduled during existing small group sessions in November and December 2024 for each of the four clinician groups represented on the CWC: outpatient (n = 5), hospitalists (n = 6), medical subspecialists (n = 5), and hospital-based specialties (n = 5). Focus groups provided a pragmatic approach to data collection given they did not require additional scheduling or time commitment demands for CWC members beyond their existing participation in small group sessions. Additionally, because each of these clinician groups participated in sessions together throughout the course of the 15-month program, they had built trust and played a role in shaping each other’s experiences as CWC members. Thus, we decided to use these existing small groups in anticipation that participants would feel more comfortable sharing their experiences than they would if they participated with CWC members from other clinician groups. There was no other planned small group discussion topic for these sessions.

The Principal Investigator (T.J.) emailed CWC members an invitation to participate, along with an information sheet that described the purpose of the study and CWC members’ research protections. CWC members were informed that K.R. would facilitate all focus groups, that their participation was voluntary, and that they had the right to decline participation altogether or request a one-on-one interview with K.R. if they preferred that to a focus group during their small group session. All 21 CWC members participated in their scheduled focus group.

Data collection

K.R. facilitated four 60-minute focus groups using Microsoft Teams in November and December 2024. A research associate observed, took notes, and provided logistical support during the focus groups. At the beginning of each focus group, K.R. reviewed required elements of informed consent and encouraged participants to maintain confidentiality by not discussing information shared in the focus group with anyone other than small group members. K.R. asked all CWC members to consent verbally prior to beginning each focus group. Focus groups were audio recorded and transcribed using Microsoft Teams. K.R. used a semi-structured discussion guide (Additional file 3) designed to explore CWC members’ motivations for participating in the well-being leadership development training program, their perceptions of the impact of the program on themselves and their clinical group, and their long-term vision for building and sustaining a culture of wellness in their clinical group. K.R. developed the discussion guide in close consultation with T.J. and L.C. to ensure alignment with the study purpose. After each focus group, the research associate cleaned her notes and debriefed with K.R. to discuss general impressions, observations, and initial insights, and the quality of data collection and notetaking to adjust procedures as appropriate. The initial debrief yielded a more comprehensive notetaking process to ensure we accurately captured the context and content for each focus group to support data analysis.

Data analysis

Prior to the start of data analysis, K.R. reviewed and cleaned focus group transcripts for readability and accuracy. K.R. employed a rapid qualitative analysis method, which included the use of transcript summaries, a matrix analysis, and thematic synthesis [36]. The study team selected this method to ensure a systematic analysis grounded in both analytic rigor and efficiency [37–39], with a goal of presenting timely, actionable findings to health system and philanthropic stakeholders. The rapid analysis was completed in January and February 2025.

K.R. first developed a Microsoft Word transcript summary template using focus group discussion domains from the discussion guide (Additional file 4). K.R. then concisely summarized each focus group using this template. She used a bulleted list under each domain to capture main points discussed in each focus group and included some brief quotations along with line number references to raw data in the transcript. Including these cross-references was helpful for returning to the transcripts to validate the focus group summaries, and clarify or expand the summaries as needed to accurately capture main points [40]. During the transcript summary step, K.R. documented thoughts, questions, and preliminary patterns in reflexive analytic memos, though she aimed to minimize analytic inference during this step.

After completing transcript summaries, K.R. used Microsoft Excel to create a matrix where she copy and pasted transcript summary content by domain (columns) and focus group (rows) to facilitate analysis. At this step, K.R. iteratively and analytically reviewed the data down columns and across rows, returning to focus group transcripts and notes frequently to ensure interpretations were grounded in the data and extract illustrative quotes. Several returns to the focus group transcripts did yield additional summary content and illustrative quotes for inclusion in the matrix, which supported more nuanced and comprehensive context for a rigorous analysis. K.R. documented preliminary themes and their dimensions in reflexive analytic memos and she refined the themes iteratively through cross comparison with matrix content, the raw data, and analytic debriefs with K.R., L.C., and K.S. The team held two analytic debriefs during the analysis phase to review and refine preliminary themes. This analytic process continued until the point of saturation, specifically when further data analysis generated no new themes. There was one additional team debrief during manuscript preparation, as the analysis and synthesis of thematic findings continued during that phase. As a CWC member who participated in a focus group, K.S. provided reflections on the thematic findings that strengthened analytic interpretations, the write-up and visualization of themes, and the overall credibility of the findings.

Results

Thematic findings

This paper presents the three themes generated in our qualitative analysis: transformed well-being mindsets, guiding well-being leadership principles, and cultivating factors for well-being leader development. We describe each theme below with illustrative quotes. We also provide an infographic that visually synthesizes our thematic results with cultivating factors for well-being leader development at the foundation of CWC members’ transformative leadership development experiences (Fig. 1).

Transformed well-being mindsets

CWC members shared positive transformations in their mindset toward well-being and addressing burnout as a result of their participation in the well-being leadership development training program.

Feeling a sense of control.

They emphasized a shift in their sense of control. One member reflected on their mindset before participation on the CWC: “It was us individuals up against a big system…this feeling of hopelessness that there was nothing you can do to really make a difference” [Hospitalists Focus Group]. They then shared their mindset shift to a confidence that “there were things we could do and there were ways that we can talk to make long-term changes” [Hospitalists Focus Group]. CWC members indicated that this specific mindset shift released them from the responsibility of fixing other’s problems. One member shared that the program “really helped me let go of a lot of things that were not mine that I was struggling to get control over” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group], while another indicated that this shift supported their ability to constructively hold others accountable for their own well-being. Members discussed the importance of responding to burnout from their sphere of control. While CWC members indicated that large scale changes like compensation and contracts are important for addressing burnout and are pressing concerns for clinicians, they also acknowledged that such changes were not in their spheres of control. They encouraged asking oneself “what exactly can I do and how can I bring some relief?” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group],” a vital leadership practice for being “in touch with your abilities and your limitations” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group] when responding to burnout.

From hopelessness to possibility.

CWC members also expressed a mindset shift from hopelessness to possibility at work. With a perspective that change is possible, members reported having a “new lease” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group] as clinicians with increased “sustainability at work” [Medical Subspecialists Focus Group] and shifts in their “feelings about work” [Outpatient Focus Group]. The scale of change for impact. With a mindset of possibility for change and capacity to continue in their work, members also indicated a transformation in their perceptions around the scale of change. Prior to program participation, members expressed experiences of overwhelm with the scale of burnout, unsure of how to play a role in addressing it. One member shared their mindset shift around beliefs that “small things can make a difference,” noting that “if something we do positively impacts just one of my colleagues, that is enough” [Hospitalists Focus Group]. Another member stated that they had “adopted more of a perspective of wellness for myself and my immediate colleagues,” shifting away from “trying to solve the big problems” [Medical Subspecialists Focus Group].

The process of achieving well-being.

In addition to a transformed perspective on the scale of impact, members indicated a different perspective on the process of achieving well-being. One member asserted that well-being is not a “one-time fix” [Medical Subspecialists Focus Group], but rather a slow, long, and ongoing process. Another highlighted the importance of embracing a “yes, and” mindset to address burnout [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group]. Rather than responding to an idea or intervention with “no or just hell no” [Medical Subspecialists Focus Group], responding with “yes, and” supports the possibility of change and creates pathways for a variety of impactful solutions. The core strength of the “yes, and” mindset is “getting a different perspective on how to think about some of these things” [Medical Subspecialists Focus Group], which builds the foundation for “taking a different approach” to addressing burnout [Medical Subspecialists Focus Group].

A shared mission for well-being.

CWC members underscored the impact of the program on reducing their sense of isolation. They recognized they were not alone in their burnout or efforts to address it. One member simply said, “I am not alone in this” [Hospitalists Focus Group]. Another stated that they “felt very alone in this work before” participating in the program, noting that “after being a part of this group, just because it was a shared struggle, or I would say a shared mission, it made it easy” to move forward [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group]. As members described a transformation from struggling alone to sharing a mission, they emphasized that they are “building a community that can respond” [Medical Subspecialists Focus Group] to their clinical groups’ burnout.

Identifying as a well-being leader.

CWC members also described changes to their perceptions of themselves as leaders. While some expressed lacking leadership skills and language prior to participating, this program was able to support members in their development and identification as leaders. One member said that the program allowed them to consider themselves a leader and provided support on “how to grow and embrace that” [Hospitalists Focus Group]. Another member shared that the program “gave me more confidence in my voice as a leader” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group]. This transformation was accompanied by the shift in perspective on their leadership behavior, specifically showing up to address burnout by “leaning into difficulty in a different way” [Subspecialists Focus Group]. As well-being leaders, they have to “be brave enough to go in that room and have that conversation” [Hospitalists Focus Group].

Understanding clinical group culture.

Members also indicated a shift in their perspective on the state of their clinical groups and clinical group cultures, a change that increased their awareness of how to connect with their clinical groups around burnout and interventions. They acknowledged that some clinicians may not be in a space to engage in well-being initiatives, citing burnout, time constraints, and lack of trust in the system as factors that shaped their clinical groups’ engagement. CWC members noted that the personal nature of well-being means interventions will resonate with some and not others in their clinical groups. They also suggested the challenge of defining well-being and interventional success given the range of experiences and perceptions of well-being across distinct clinical groups.

Guiding well-being leadership principles

CWC members reflected on seven core guiding leadership principles to advance clinician well-being they surfaced through their program experiences. Members emphasized that “wellness is local” [Subspecialists Focus Group]. Members strongly expressed that “top-down” [Outpatient Focus Group] initiatives can feel burdensome and disconnected from their local realities. In a large, complex health system, “the actual work has to be local” [Subspecialists Focus Group] and “not just the administration” [Outpatient Focus Group]. One member discussed the malleability of the CWC, which allowed them to “adapt it to what I thought my group needed” [Subspecialists Focus Group]. Maintaining a grassroots approach to well-being initiatives was cited as a critical element of sustaining a culture of well-being in their clinical group, as it will ensure the local context and relevant interventional targets drive well-being forward.

Surfacing and discussing burnout was another leadership principle highlighted by CWC members. As one member stated, “Make burnout and its factors real so that those can be worked on personally and professionally” [Hospitalists Focus Group]. CWC members shared that the well-being leadership development training program’s cultivation of spaces to have these discussions out front and out loud allowed them to demonstrate to their clinical groups that there was system-level acknowledgement of burnout and that “there’s a group that’s working towards [well-being]” [Subspecialists Focus Group]. Participants indicated that broaching these conversations requires a sense of community among clinical group clinicians. Without a sense of community, clinicians “don’t really want to engage” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group]. Members emphasized sense of community as a key principle in well-being leadership. They asserted that though they need to act within their sphere of control, intentionally taking steps to center and build community among clinicians in their clinical group is foundational for the impact of local well-being initiatives.

CWC members also outlined the importance of the approach and framework used to guide well-being initiatives among clinical groups. One member highlighted differences in impact when “problem solving from the core of wellness, as opposed to the core of efficiency” [Outpatient Focus Group]. With a core of well-being undergirding the CWC, members and their clinical groups “were able to share and problem solve together” without top-down constraints of efficiency and scalability [Subspecialists Focus Group]. Another related principle shared by CWC members is the necessity to consider intergenerational differences among clinicians to drive interventions forward. They maintained that these differences shape how clinicians view and experience burnout and well-being and thus should be central to clinical group discussions when identifying possible interventional targets. One member highlighted the impact of centering intergenerational differences among their clinical group, stating that “the older generation allowed a process to be implemented that had essentially been stonewalled three times previous” [Outpatient Focus Group]. Because that clinical group was open to discussion, some of the younger clinicians were able to express why that process was valuable to them, which in turn “allowed some of the older generation to decide that it was maybe OK to move forward” [Outpatient Focus Group]. CWC members affirmed the positive impact of inviting all perspectives, which ultimately strengthened problem solving to advance effective well-being interventions.

As discussed earlier, CWC members reported a mindset shift in their perceptions of the scale of impact necessary to demonstrate success in their well-being interventions. The general consensus was that small changes can make a difference and are therefore valuable targets for change. This mindset shift was the foundation for another well-being leadership principle set forth by CWC members: “an N of 1 matters” [Outpatient Focus Group]. When one member discussed the potential cost ramifications of burnout and the loss of a clinician from the health system, they emphasized the value of acting on this principle. Well-being efforts that keep just one clinician “longer, happier, not just more productive as an individual but more productive as a person” can “help pull other people along” [Outpatient Focus Group] as they work toward building a sustainable culture of well-being.

Finally, CWC members shared the well-being leadership principle of mindful self-compassion. Engaging in well-being work requires a “love of vulnerability” [Subspecialist Focus Group] to show up authentically and dig into the “mushy” and “messy” [Hospitalist Focus Group] depths of burnout. It also requires mindfulness to discern the time and place for addressing problems. One member shared that as a clinician they are “trained to know the answer or get to the bottom of the answer” [Hospitalist Focus Group]. Another discussed a goal achievement mentality, stating that clinicians “do want to achieve goals and our objectives, you know, at a faster pace and it has to happen now” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group]. While members maintained these are important for clinical practice, they also indicated that they may not serve them in their work as well-being leaders. Instead, they emphasized the centering of vulnerability, the willingness to deeply engage in well-being work from a place of “curiosity and humility” [Hospitalists Focus Group], and the strength to embrace self-compassion, to set aside the harsh self-directed judgment, along their well-being leadership journey.

Cultivating factors for well-being leader development

CWC members discussed three structural factors of the training program they perceived as foundational for their transformative leadership development experiences: (1) health system financial commitment to well-being; (2) relationships and sense of community; and (3) knowledge and skill-building.

Health system financial commitment to wellness.

CWC members’ shared that an essential factor for program success was financial commitment from health system leadership. Dedicated funding to support CWC members’ engagement in the program “gave value to the time” [Outpatient Focus Group] and a “feeling of recognition that [the program] was important” [Outpatient Focus Group]. Members noted the importance of “real commitment from our leaders” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group] to advance well-being long-term. They indicated that this commitment needs to include funding to cover well-being leaders’ time and discretionary funds to design and implement well-being interventions. One member suggested funding a department with support staff, statisticians, and grant writers “to actually do the work long-term and be impactful” [Hospitalists Focus Group]. Another member emphasized that the overall cost for the well-being leadership development training program “is small compared to what could happen” [Outpatient Focus Group] with sustained and potentially deepening clinician burnout.

CWC members also surfaced tensions that can arise from system support of local well-being initiatives. One member shared their concern about the implications of system-level leadership funding clinical group-level well-being efforts: “I always worry that if they’re providing the funding, then somehow they think that it has to be done their way and it takes away that autonomy” [Subspecialists Focus Group]. While acknowledging program successes and the importance of health system financial commitment to well-being, another member emphasized their concern about the power of the ”bigger system at play” that influences well-being leaders’ “ability to change things in a sustained way” [Outpatient Focus Group]. They specifically commented on the dynamics of an ever-changing health system, persistence of external stressors on the system, and tightening financial context, and how those shape a feeling of fragility in their well-being leadership.

Relationships and sense of community.

Members indicated that their small groups created a sense of community and provided safe spaces for members to be vulnerable. The “sense of togetherness” [Hospitalists Focus Group] and “shared struggle” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group] fostered a culture of vulnerability and disclosure where members could discuss their challenges and their goals. Members also noted the role of small groups in creating a generative space where they could brainstorm ideas and “hash out” [Outpatient Focus Group] practical solutions together before introducing them to their clinical group.

Knowledge and skill-building.

CWC members described the positive impacts of didactic, small group, and coaching components of the program on their knowledge and skill development as well-being leaders. One CWC member shared:[The program] brought some expertise in to lay a foundation where I feel like before a lot of wellness work had just been everybody trying to figure it out on their own, independently, without any support or knowledge base. In addition to providing a structured foundation for leadership development, some CWC members reported that the knowledge and skills gained through participation gave them “the tools to stay in the game” [Outpatient Focus Group]. Others commented on the impact of developing skills to have “difficult conversations” [Hospital-Based Specialties Focus Group] and “speak [the leadership] language” [Hospitalists Focus Group] with their clinical group and health system leadership. Members shared that prior to their participation in the program, they lacked the skills to give real feedback or vent the “dirty laundry” [Hospitalists Focus Group], skills not emphasized in their medical training but critical to address stressors head on.

Discussion

In this qualitative study, we explored CWC members’ experiences with and perceptions of the impact of the 15-month well-being leadership development training program they completed. Our thematic findings suggest positive and transformational program outcomes for the first cohort of CWC members’ well-being leadership development, rooted in three cultivating factors: health system financial commitment, relationships and sense of community, and knowledge and skill-building.

CWC members emphasized shifts in their mindset toward clinician well-being and addressing burnout as a core outcome of their participation in this training program. These included a shift from hopelessness to confidence in their capacity to effect change within their clinical group and the recognition of the need to address burnout from their own spheres of control. Members also indicated that their own hopelessness was transformed through program participation. Now able to view change as possible, CWC members shared a perceived decrease in their own burnout and perceived increase in their capacity to keep going at work.

CWC members also discussed the importance of applying the yes/and framework in well-being leadership. Yes/and has its roots in improvisational theater and encourages collaborative and generative brainstorming that can yield creative solutions that may otherwise be missed or dismissed with a yes/but mindset. Misch argues that yes/and can help teams “bring to fruition a shared vision” [41]. Previous research found a positive impact of improvisational techniques on psychological well-being, including self-efficacy [42]. The yes/and framework, as a core pillar of the CWC training program’s design, may be an important driver of CWC members’ perceived decrease in burnout and perceived increase in their confidence to effect change at the clinical group level.

Participation in the training program also shifted members’ feelings of isolation. Because of their program experiences, they felt a sense of connection around a shared mission to respond to clinical group burnout. Prior research indicates that loneliness is harmful to occupational well-being in health care settings [43], while feeling connected to peers and being a part of a community can reduce burnout [44–46]. Additionally, members reported changed perspectives on their clinical groups’ culture and the current state of clinical group burnout, leading to increased awareness of how to build connection with their respective clinical groups. This awareness, in turn, may help CWC members, as well-being leaders, set reasonable expectations for intervention design, implementation, and outcomes.

Through their participation in the training program, CWC members surfaced well-being leadership principles that will guide their ongoing well-being efforts with their clinical groups. Consistent with current research and recommendations for health care systems’ approach to burnout, members reinforced the importance of clinical group-level well-being initiatives that are responsive to clinicians’ local practice environment [27–29, 47].

Members also cited the importance of having a space to surface and discuss burnout with their program peers and clinical groups. Both pervasive and normative, employee silence in healthcare is associated with burnout and sub-optimal care delivery [47, 48]. As a system approach, intentionally cultivating well-being leaders and leadership spaces that facilitate honest conversations about burnout may increase psychological safety and support an organizational well-being culture responsive to clinicians’ experiences and needs [49, 50].

CWC members were clear that a sense of community is foundational to engaging in deep and vulnerable conversations around burnout and well-being. Prior studies support this finding, as physicians with low reported burnout cited building and nourishing connection and community among co-workers as core drivers of their thriving and happiness [51–54]. CWC members shared that clinical group spaces grounded in the value of clinician well-being, as opposed to the value of organizational efficiency, enabled their clinical groups to collaboratively problem solve to address clinical group burnout, a finding aligned with previous research that demonstrated the effectiveness of wellness-centered leadership in reducing physician and APP burnout [50]. Swensen and Shanafelt also argue that leadership approaches to burnout that primarily focus on boosting productivity run the risk of engendering value misalignment between leadership and clinicians, which may in turn deepen burnout and its consequences [53].

Current evidence highlights the positive impact of compassionate leadership on employee well-being and burnout and organizational outcomes [55–57]. One driver of compassionate leadership development is the practice of self-compassion, or “compassion directed inward, relating to oneself as the focus of care and consideration when faced with the experience of difficulty [55, 56, 58]. CWC members reported the importance of self-compassion as well-being leaders, though they also discussed the need to mindfully embrace it as it diverges from how they are trained as clinicians—to know the answer or get to the bottom of it efficiently. Montgomery and Lainidi argue that this healthcare professional ethic can lead to “pathological altruism,” which in turn can lead to adverse long-term consequences, including clinician burnout and mental health conditions [47]. As a part of the training program, CWC members’ participated in coaching with the Principal Investigator (T.J.), who is certified in thought-based individual and group coaching. Coaching is cited in the literature as an effective strategy to support the development of self-compassion and improve clinician well-being through the reduction of burnout and improvements in clinician self-valuation [58–61]. Our findings contribute to this evidence and suggest that coaching is an essential well-being leadership development training program component.

Finally, CWC members’ identified three structural training program factors that cultivated their growth as well-being leaders. They first discussed the training program’s focus on knowledge and skill building, facilitated through structured educational sessions, supportive small group sessions, and coaching sessions. These program components were designed from our organization’s pilot well-being leadership development training program outcomes, which indicated that training leadership behaviors was an effective interventional target [30]. This finding also aligns with previous research that demonstrated the effectiveness of a health system wellness-centered leadership training program, which included a formal leadership development curriculum, in reducing clinician burnout and enhancing job satisfaction [50]. The researchers argue that ongoing, dedicated opportunities for leaders to learn and gain skills in wellness-centered leadership are important for maintaining organizational accountability to clinician well-being.

As discussed earlier, CWC members underscored the importance of connection and a sense of community in driving well-being. Not only did CWC members indicate this as foundational to supporting their work with their own clinical groups, but they also shared how critical building a sense of community was for their leadership development journey together [51–54]. Having a safe space where developing leaders could engage authentically and with vulnerability was viewed as constructive for reducing isolation, fostering a shared mission, and generating and driving well-being solutions forward. This finding suggests that well-being leadership development training programs may play an important role in increasing psychological safety and self-compassion for developing leaders.

Given growing acknowledgement that addressing healthcare worker burnout is multi-faceted, intervention frameworks call for organizational infrastructure to support clinical group-level approaches that respond to local drivers of burnout and well-being [47, 53, 62]. CWC members affirmed this need for organizational infrastructure, specifically financial commitment from health system leadership to support their participation in the training program through protected time and provide resources for clinical group-level intervention design and implementation [30]. This specific training program was relatively inexpensive, yet CWC members reported a perceived reduction in their own burnout and perceived increase in their confidence to effect and sustain meaningful change at the clinical group-level. Our findings underscore the potential of expanding well-being leadership at the local level and leveraging a bottom-up approach for designing interventions to reduce healthcare worker burnout. However, our findings also highlight the imperative for system-level support for these initiatives that preserves local autonomy and fosters collaborative partnership between system and local leaders.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. We recognize that CWC members’ motivations for participating and the organizational context of this well-being leadership development training program may limit the transferability of study findings. While a strength that all CWC members who completed the program participated in focus groups, they were approached to voluntarily participate because of their known openness to engage in process improvement initiatives [34]. Therefore, our findings reflect the experiences of a highly motivated group of clinicians who received organizational support for their participation, and are not representative of the perspectives of clinicians who did not complete the program, opted not to participate, or were not approached to participate. The CWC was also a funded program with dedicated leadership for program design, implementation, and evaluation. Other health systems seeking to do similar work will necessarily have different organizational contexts that shape their program design, implementation, and outcomes. To address these limitations, we provided a comprehensive overview of the CWC and participating clinicians, and rich descriptions of study findings for others to assess transferability to their specific contexts. Our findings also represent CWC members’ immediate post-program perceptions; therefore, we cannot make claims about the long-term sustainability of the leadership development outcomes reported by participants.

Though author K.S., a CWC member who participated in a focus group, reviewed and provided input on study findings during manuscript development, member checks with all participating clinicians were not feasible for this study, as we employed a rapid analysis to ensure we could present timely, actionable findings to health system and philanthropic stakeholders. We recognize this limitation. We also note that a trained qualitative researcher (K.R.) with no preexisting relationship with participating clinicians provided scientific leadership on this study. K.R. conducted data collection and analysis independently of CWC program leadership and support staff and implemented analytic practices to enhance the rigor of the analysis, which together bolster the trustworthiness of our findings.

Conclusions

Findings from this qualitative exploration suggest a positive impact of the well-being leadership development training program on the first cohort of participating clinicians, who reported a transformational well-being leadership development experience that included mindset shifts and the emergence of guiding well-being leadership principles. They also reported a perceived reduction in their own burnout and increased confidence in their capacity to effect change within their clinical groups. Participating clinicians underscored three essential structural components that cultivated their development as well-being leaders. The first, health system financial commitment, was emphasized as a critical precondition for participation, signaling organizational value of this grassroots clinician well-being initiative. In addition to this cultivating factor, CWC members’ indicated that relationships and a sense of community and knowledge and skill-building were foundational to their growth as well-being leaders.

Healthcare worker burnout remains a national challenge with implications for well-being and mental health, the quality of patient care, and healthcare costs tied to turnover and reduced work time. Addressing burnout has shifted from individual-level interventions to organizational-level interventions in response to growing evidence that organizational factors are the biggest drivers of burnout. Findings from our study support this paradigm shift, suggesting that the empowerment of frontline clinicians with leadership skills through a health system-funded, relationship-based well-being leadership development training program is an effective interventional approach to address clinical group-level burnout.

The study team presented our qualitative findings, along with findings from the parallel quantitative evaluation of the effectiveness of the first CWC cohort in decreasing clinical group burnout [34], to Providence leadership and philanthropic stakeholders. The demonstrated success of this well-being leadership development program was a key driver of an organizational commitment to enhance clinician well-being at Providence. A second CWC cohort launched in August 2025 to train 32 more well-being leaders and equip them with leadership skills to address clinical group-level burnout with their teams. The Department of Clinician Experience, a first of its kind, was also created with ongoing financial support and the infrastructure to implement the well-being leadership development training program (CWC), conduct regular clinician well-being assessments, offer evidence-based clinician wellness programs, and disseminate effective well-being strategies. Though our qualitative findings must be interpreted with the study’s context and limitations in mind, they provide an adaptable blueprint for other health systems seeking to develop a similar intervention that addresses clinical group-level burnout and enhances healthcare worker well-being.
