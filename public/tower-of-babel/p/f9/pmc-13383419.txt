Undermining older adults’ autonomy impairs physical health: longitudinal evidence and psychological mechanisms
Authors: Zizhuo Chen, Yuhao Liu, Shufei Yin, Xin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesOlder adults' autonomy is frequently undermined by well-intentioned but paternalistic caregiving, representing a pervasive form of benevolent ageism. This research examines the long-term physical health consequences of such autonomy restrictions and investigates whether the endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes serves as an underlying psychological mechanism.MethodsStudy 1 analyzed 10-year longitudinal data (2008–2018) from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey using a progressive analytic strategy: a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (1a, N = 4,288), panel data regression with instrumental variables (1b, N = 3,909), and Cox proportional hazard regression (1c, N = 12,053). Study 2 (N = 154) experimentally primed older adults with either undermined or preserved autonomy to assess the causal impact on stereotype endorsement and subsequent cognitive and physical performance.FindingsAfter controlling for baseline health and covariates, Study 1 demonstrated that undermined autonomy independently predicted poorer self-rated health and increased mortality risk over time. Study 2 revealed that exposure to autonomy-undermining cues heightened older adults' endorsement of infantilizing stereotypes, which in turn impaired their physical performance.ConclusionsUndermined autonomy poses a significant independent threat to older adults' physical health and longevity. This detrimental effect is partly driven by the internalization of negative stereotypes regarding older adults' competence. Findings underscore the critical need to protect older adults' autonomy and challenge the infantilizing attitudes that often rationalize paternalistic practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07701-6.

Introduction

Autonomy, the capacity for self-governance and decision-making, is a fundamental human need that persists across the lifespan, as posited by Self-Determination Theory [20]. For older adults, however, maintaining autonomy is challenged not only by age-related functional decline but also by well-intentioned yet paternalistic caregiving and benevolent ageist behaviors [12, 45, 48]. For example, family members and healthcare providers often restrict older adults' decision-making, ranging from financial and medical decisions to daily routines, under the guise of protection [8, 23]. In many cases, older adults are excluded from care planning or shielded from distressing news due to assumptions about their fragility [12, 49]. Indeed, such autonomy restrictions appear prevalent: according to the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Surveys, 19.5% of older adults could seldom or never make their own decisions and 29.1% lacked decision-making power over any expenditure [64], while 32.6% of older adults in a German survey reported experiencing paternalistic treatment [10].

However, paternalistic help that undermines older adults’ autonomy, although often framed as protective, may have adverse consequences for both mental and physical health. While existing research has established the association between restricted autonomy and poor mental health outcomes among older adults [3, 30, 42], evidence regarding physical health remains relatively limited. The classic work of Langer and Rodin [35] showed that restricting autonomy in institutional settings increased mortality risk. However, large-scale longitudinal evidence generalizing these findings to community-dwelling older adults is lacking.

Self-Determination Theory provides a theoretical basis that undermined autonomy may impair older adults’ health. According to SDT, deprivation of autonomy, a basic psychological need, is associated with impairment in well-being and health [20, 45]. This process may be especially important in later life. Unlike children (i.e., under the age of 18, who are not yet legally recognized as autonomous decision-makers in most cultures), older adults are fully developed agents with established life experiences and rights. Thus, autonomy-undermining in later life therefore represents the withdrawal of previously held self-governance and personal control, which may cause adverse mental and physical effects [52, 56].

Stereotype Embodiment Theory [37] and Age-Based Stereotype Threat [34] further suggest a psychological mechanism through which autonomy-undermining experiences may translate into functional decline. According to SET, repeated exposure to autonomy-undermining cues can lead older adults to internalize negative age-related stereotypes, especially infantilization stereotypes that portray older adults as too childlike or irrational to manage their own affairs [36, 41, 46, 53]. This is because such infantilization stereotypes usually serve as an implicit justification for paternalistic behaviors that undermine older adults' autonomy. Moreover, consistent with the phenomenon of ABST, once these stereotypes are activated and internalized, they may compromise older adults’ physical and cognitive functioning through psychological and behavioral pathways [38, 65].

Therefore, the present research examines the longitudinal impact of undermined autonomy on older adults' physical health and mortality risk, as suggested by SDT. It further investigates whether the endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes mediates this relationship in accordance with SET and ABST.

Undermined autonomy impaired physical health in older adults

The relationship between autonomy and health is likely bidirectional. While declining health may constrain older adults’ independence [44, 51], restrictions on autonomy driven by helpers' convenience or age-based assumptions rather than necessity may also act as an independent stressor [12, 15]. From the perspective of SDT, autonomy-undermining may reduce well-being and health-related functioning [20, 45]. Thus, autonomy-undermining treatment may not only reflect poor health, but also contribute to subsequent health declines. This expectation is consistent with the classic findings from Langer and Rodin [35], which showed that autonomy-supportive interventions in nursing homes improved health and survival, whereas control groups receiving unsolicited assistance experienced decline. Theoretically, undermined autonomy may precipitate physical decline through several interrelated pathways, including reduced self-efficacy, lower engagement in daily and physical activities, and diminished social participation, all of which are important for maintaining functional health in later life [2, 11, 17, 31, 59].

Moreover, research has consistently linked autonomy to older adults' mental well-being, including negative attitudes toward aging, lower self-efficacy, higher levels of depression, reduced life satisfaction, and increased suicidal ideation [3, 14, 30, 42, 59]. These psychological outcomes are further associated with poorer physical health and increased mortality risk [24, 54, 65].

Thus, Self-Determination Theory and the relevant evidence together support the prediction that undermined autonomy may prospectively predict poorer physical health and higher mortality risk. However, existing evidence remains limited with small institutional sample (e.g., [35]). Therefore, the present research used large-scale longitudinal data to examine the causal relationship between autonomy and physical health among older adults.

Endorsing age stereotypes of infantilization as an underlying mechanism

As discussed above, undermined autonomy may impair physical health partly through psychological pathways, including negative attitudes toward aging. Stereotype Embodiment Theory provides a theoretical basis for this mechanism: age-related cues in the social environment can be internalized by older adults and subsequently influence their functioning [37]. Autonomy-undermining treatment may serve as such a cue because it usually holds the assumption that older adults are unable to make decisions or manage their own affairs. Thus, based on SET, we expected that exposure to autonomy-undermining cues would increase older adults’ endorsement of negative age stereotypes.

Among different negative age stereotypes, infantilization is especially relevant to autonomy. Infantilizing stereotypes portray older adults as childlike, irrational, or lacking the maturity and competence required for self-governance [28, 41, 53]. These beliefs directly justify paternalistic restrictions on older adults’ decision-making and motivate autonomy-undermining behaviors. According to SET, when older adults are treated as "children" who must be managed by "parental" figures, they receive a situational cue regarding their presumed infantilizing characteristics and may in turn perceive themselves as infantilized.

Age-Based Stereotype Threat further explains how these stereotypes may affect performance. Prior research shows that situational cues activating negative age stereotypes can impair older adults’ cognitive and physical functioning [5, 6, 47, 60]. Therefore, we expected that autonomy-undermining cues would increase endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes, which would in turn predict poorer cognitive and physical performance.

The present research

The present research conducted two complementary studies to examine whether undermined autonomy predicts poorer physical health among older adults and to test a potential psychological mechanism. Guided by Self-Determination Theory, Study 1 utilized 10-year longitudinal data from a national survey of Chinese older adults, employing a progressive analytic strategy: Study 1a aimed to establish longitudinal associations between autonomy and health. Study 1b further disentangled the causality with an instrumental variable approach. Study 1c extended the outcome from self-rated health to mortality risk. Study 1: physical health outcomes of undermined autonomy in older adults

To examine the long-term impact of undermined autonomy on older adults' physical health, Study 1 utilized 10-year longitudinal data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS, 2008–2018; [13, 64]). As we introduced above, we employed a progressive analytic strategy comprising three complementary approaches, which allows us to move beyond simple associations and establish more rigorous evidence for the causal impact of undermined autonomy on physical health.

Study 1a: undermined autonomy and self-rated health

Study 1a employed a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) to establish bidirectional within-person associations between autonomy and health.

Participants

Participants who responded to all three survey waves in 2008, 2011, and 2014 (N = 5,245) were included. After excluding 957 cases with missing data on autonomy or health status, the final analytical sample comprised 4,288 participants (Mage = 77.37, SD = 9.15; 50.7% female at the 2008 baseline). Excluded participants were older and reported poorer health and lower autonomy (see Table S1a), and these variables were controlled in the following analysis.

Measurements

Subjective autonomy

Participants reported their perceived autonomy using the item "How often can you make decisions for yourself in your daily life?" on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always). This item is a standard component of the CLHLS questionnaire and has strong face validity. It directly captures the core of individual's overall subjective sense of self-governance in everyday life, as conceptualized in Self-Determination Theory [20], without specifying a particular agent who may restrict autonomy. In the present data, as expected, subjective autonomy also showed positive correlations with financial autonomy (r = 0.425, p < 0.001, see Study 1b for details), supporting its convergent validity.

Self-rated health

Participants' physical health was assessed using the item "How do you feel about your physical health?" on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very bad, 5 = excellent).

Covariates

Age, gender, and socioeconomic status (SES) were included as covariates based on their established associations with autonomy and health in the aging literature [39, 62, 65]. Subjective SES was assessed by asking participants to rate their economic status compared to other local people on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = very rich).

Analysis

To examine the longitudinal bidirectional relationship between autonomy and health at the within-person level, we employed a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM; [27]). Unlike traditional cross-lagged panel models, RI-CLPM partitions variance into stable between-person differences and within-person fluctuations, thereby providing a more accurate estimate of the temporal associations between variables. The analysis assumed consistent slopes across observations.

Results

The RI-CLPM demonstrated acceptable model fit (CFI = 0.909, TLI = 0.824, RMSEA = 0.049). Results indicated that lower subjective autonomy at a previous time point significantly predicted worse self-rated health at the subsequent observation (B = 0.031, 95% CI [0.005, 0.056], p = 0.018), after controlling for age, gender, and SES. Conversely, poorer self-rated health also predicted lower subjective autonomy at the following observation (B = 0.073, 95% CI [0.029, 0.116], p = 0.001). These findings suggest a bidirectional relationship: undermined autonomy has a detrimental impact on physical health over time, and declining health also constrains autonomy (Fig. 1). Study 1b: investigating the causal direction by panel data regression with instrumental variables (IV)

Given the bidirectional associations observed in Study 1a, Study 1b employed panel data regression with instrumental variables (IV) to more rigorously investigate the causal relations between autonomy and health.

Participants

After excluding cases with missing values on any key variables across all waves, a total of 3,909 participants (Mage = 76.80, SD = 8.86; 49.2% female at the 2008 baseline) were included in the final analysis.

Measures

Subjective autonomy, self-rated health, and time-variant covariates (age, SES) were measured identically to Study 1a. Additional measures were included:

Financial autonomy

Participants reported their autonomy in family economic decisions on a 5-point scale: 1 = "I could make no decisions for any expenses in my family," 2 = "I could only decide my own expenses," 3 = "I could make decisions for some unimportant expenses in my family," 4 = "I could to some extent make decisions for important family expenses," and 5 = "I made almost all economic decisions in my family." This item is also a standard component of the CLHLS questionnaire and has been used before [39]. Unlike the subjective autonomy measure, which captures older adults' general perceived self-governance, financial autonomy reflects a practical, domain-specific dimension of decision-making power within the family. As detailed below, this measure served as an instrumental variable in the analysis.

Self-rated changes in health from last year

Participants assessed changes in their physical health relative to the previous year using a single item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = much worse, 5 = much better).

Life satisfaction (covariate) was assessed with a single item ("How do you feel about your current life?") on a 5-point scale (1 = very poor to 5 = very good), with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. Although life satisfaction may be conceptually linked to both autonomy and health, its inclusion provides a more conservative test of the autonomy-health relationship. Importantly, sensitivity analyses excluding life satisfaction yielded substantively identical results, suggesting that the findings are robust (the same applies to Study 1c).

Activities of daily living (ADL, covariates) were assessed among six basic activities: bathing, dressing, toileting, indoor transferring, continence, and feeding [33]. Participants rated each activity on a 3-point scale (1 = complete dependence, 2 = partial assistance required, 3 = independent), and responses were averaged, with higher scores indicating greater functional independence. ADL was included as a covariate to control for functional capacity as a supplement to the subjective measure of health status (the same applies to Study 1c).

Analysis

We employed double fixed-effects panel data regression models with instrumental variables. Panel data methods make full use of multi-wave observations and provide more reliable estimates by controlling for unobserved individual heterogeneity that remains constant over time (e.g., gender, educational background). Incorporating exogenous instrumental variables further addresses endogeneity concerns, enabling clearer causal inference. Two models were estimated: Model A tested whether subjective autonomy predicted self-rated health, and Model B tested the reverse direction. All analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0.

Results

Study 1c: investigating the mortality risk of undermined autonomy using cox regression

Having established that undermined autonomy predicts poorer self-rated health (H1a), Study 1c then extended the self-rated health outcome to objective mortality risk using Cox proportional hazard regression (H1b).

Participants

The 2008 baseline wave of the CLHLS included 16,954 participants. After excluding 4,901 cases that were loss with unknown survival status during the 2011–2018 follow-up period, the final sample included 12,053 participants (Mage = 87.87, SD = 11.36; 57.6% female at the 2008 baseline), of whom 9,613 were recorded as deceased by 2018. The mean survival duration was 55.42 months. Compared to the included sample, excluded participants were on average younger and reported better SES and higher autonomy at baseline (see Table S1b for details).

Measures

Subjective autonomy

Measured identically to Studies 1a and 1b.

Financial autonomy

Measured identically to Study 1b. In addition to serving as an instrumental variable in Study 1b, financial autonomy was included here as a separate predictor of mortality. Unlike the subjective autonomy measure, which is a strict Likert-type scale, financial autonomy reflects ordered but unequal categories of decision-making power. It was therefore treated as a categorical variable in the Cox regression, which accommodates non-interval scaling more appropriately. Moreover, including financial autonomy alongside subjective autonomy provides complementary evidence, capturing a practical, observable dimension of autonomy beyond older adults' subjective perceptions.

Covariates

Age, gender, self-rated health, self-rated changes in health from last year, subjective SES, life satisfaction, and activities of daily living (ADL) were included as covariates. Notably, by controlling for both self-rated health and health changes, this analysis examines the effect of autonomy on mortality above and beyond older adults' current health status.

Analysis

A Cox proportional hazard regression model was employed to compare mortality rates among older adults with varying levels of subjective and financial autonomy. Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis confirmed that the proportional hazard assumption was satisfied for both autonomy measures.

Brief discussion of study 1

Through a progressive analytic strategy, Study 1 provides converging evidence supporting H1a and H1b: undermined autonomy predicts poorer self-rated health (1a, 1b) and increased mortality risk (1c) among older adults. Importantly, the results demonstrated that autonomy independently predicted health, suggesting that the autonomy reduction observed cannot be fully attributed to health limitations alone, consistent with the influence of external factors such as paternalistic treatment. Study 2 therefore directly examines this external pathway experimentally.

Study 2: endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes as mechanism between undermined autonomy and physical performance

Study 1 established the causal relationship between undermined autonomy and poorer physical health as well as increased mortality risk. Building on these findings, Study 2 further investigated the psychological mechanism with experiment. Specifically, we tested whether older adults primed with autonomy-undermining scenarios would endorse higher levels of infantilizing age stereotypes, and whether such endorsement would in turn impair their cognitive and physical performance.

Method

Participants

A priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1) indicated a required sample of 146 participants to detect a medium effect size (d = 0.25) with 85% power at α = 0.05. We recruited 159 older adults from local communities. After excluding participants for illiteracy, hearing impairment, or invariant responding, the final sample consisted of 154 individuals (aged 61–89 years, Mage = 70.30, SD = 4.88; 57.1% female).

Procedure

Participants were invited to the laboratory and randomly assigned to one of two conditions. In the autonomy-undermining condition, participants read a passage depicting older adults as often having to rely on their children or others to make decisions. In the autonomy-preserving condition (control), participants read a passage highlighting older adults' independence and wisdom (both two priming passages are presented in the Supplementary Materials). Following the priming task, participants completed the measures described below. Upon completion, all participants were fully debriefed by reading a passage that emphasized the strengths and autonomy of older adults.

Measures

Manipulation check

Participants rated the extent to which they believed older adults should rely on others on a 10-point scale.

Endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes

To our knowledge, no established scale exists to directly measure older adults' endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes. We therefore developed items drawing partly on Haslam's [28] conceptualization of animalistic dehumanization, which characterizes targets as childlike, irrational, and coarse, attributes that most closely align with the core features of the infantilizing age stereotype [41, 53].

Six items were developed, with each pair corresponding to one of three infantilizing attributes: childishness (e.g., "Compared to younger adults, older adults are less able to manage their emotions and desires"), coarseness (e.g., "The coarse speech and behaviors of older adults can sometimes be embarrassing"), and irrationality (e.g., "Compared to younger people, older individuals are sometimes too irrational to distinguish right from wrong"). All items were phrased to assess older adults' perceptions of their own age group relative to younger people, thereby capturing endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes at the group level rather than evaluations of specific individuals. Participants rated their agreement on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater endorsement (Cronbach's α = 0.77)1.

The complete item list is available in the Supplementary Materials (Table S2).

Cognitive performance

A word recognition task was used to assess memory. Participants were shown a list of 10 words, completed a brief numerical distractor task, and were then shown a new list of 20 words (10 original, 10 new). Performance was indexed by the rate of correct hits (correctly recognizing original words) and correct rejections (correctly identifying new words as not previously presented).

Physical performance

Two tasks assessed physical ability. Handgrip strength was measured twice for each hand, and the highest value (in kilograms) was recorded. In a reaction-time task, participants tapped a screen as quickly as possible when it changed from red to green, and response time was recorded in milliseconds.

Covariates

Age, gender, self-rated health, and life satisfaction were included as covariates in all analyses.

Results

Manipulation check

Participants in the autonomy-undermining condition perceived older adults as significantly more dependent on others than those in the control condition (t(152) = 11.0, p < 0.001), indicating that the manipulation was effective.

Endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes

An ANCOVA controlling for age, gender, self-rated health, and life satisfaction revealed that participants in the autonomy-undermining condition reported significantly higher endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes than those in the control condition (F(1, 152) = 10.70, p = 0.001, partial η2 = 0.067). However, no significant direct effects of condition on cognitive or physical performance were observed (see Table S3). Despite this, partial correlations controlling for covariates indicated that higher endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes was significantly associated with weaker grip strength (r = −0.214, p = 0.009), longer reaction time (r = 0.204, p = 0.013), and higher correct hit rate in the memory task (r = 0.210, p = 0.010), suggesting that the effect of undermined autonomy on performance may operate indirectly through stereotype endorsement.

Mediation analyses confirmed this pattern. After controlling for covariates, the autonomy-undermining prime elevated endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes, which in turn predicted weaker grip strength (indirect effect: standardized β = −0.108, 95% CI [−0.245, −0.013]; Fig. 2a), longer reaction time (indirect effect: standardized β = 0.107, 95% CI [0.017, 0.219]; Fig. 2b), and lower correct rejection rates (indirect effect: standardized β = −0.089, 95% CI [−0.222, −0.002]; Fig. 2c). Brief discussion of study 2

Building on Study 1's evidence, Study 2 further provides experimental evidence that autonomy-undermining experiences indeed elevate older adults' endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes (H2a), which in turn impairs their physical functioning (H2b). Although the priming manipulation did not produce direct effects on performance, the manipulation check confirmed its effectiveness, and the significant indirect effects through stereotype endorsement suggest that the detrimental impact of undermined autonomy operates through a subtle psychological pathway.

General discussion

The present research reveals that undermined autonomy, a manifestation of benevolent and paternalistic ageism, poses a significant threat to older adults' physical health. Consistent with Self-Determination Theory, Study 1 established that undermined autonomy independently predicts poorer self-rated health and increased mortality risk using 10-year longitudinal data, extending the classic but small-scale institutional findings from Langer and Rodin [35]. Study 2 further revealed a psychological mechanism underlying this effect. As suggested by Stereotype Embodiment Theory [37], autonomy-undermining experiences led older adults to endorse infantilizing age stereotypes about their own age group, which in turn impaired their physical and cognitive performance aligning with the Age-Based Stereotype Threat framework [34].

Undermined autonomy and physical health

Grounded in Self-Determination Theory [20], our findings reinforce the proposition that the deprivation of autonomy may result in negative health outcomes. Extending SDT to the domain of late-life physical health, our results align with and extend the seminal work of Langer and Rodin [35] by showing, in a larger longitudinal sample, that autonomy-undermining experiences are associated with poorer self-rated health and increased mortality risk. This effect likely operates through converging pathways. Psychologically, restricted autonomy may foster diminished self-efficacy and negative aging self-perceptions [14, 29, 59], both serve as predictors of health decline [38, 54]. Behaviorally, when others take over daily tasks and decisions, older adults lose opportunities to engage in physical and cognitive activities that are essential for maintaining functional capacity [17, 22, 32]. Socially, paternalistic treatment may narrow older adults' scope of social participation and connections [11, 19]. These pathways are particularly salient in such context of unnecessary paternalistic help [15], where such restrictions stem from helpers' stereotypes rather than the older adults' actual functional limitations. Future research should employ multiple mediation models within longitudinal designs to simultaneously examine these pathways and their sequential relationships.

The findings regarding financial autonomy further indicate a concrete, practical illustration of these dynamics. Financial autonomy is a domain of autonomy that is deeply embedded in everyday family life. Exclusion from financial decisions not only erodes psychological agency but also restricts the essential resources and opportunities for social participation and interaction. This deprivation carries tangible survival costs: our data revealed a 37.8% higher mortality risk for those lacking decision-making power, highlighting the severe physiological consequences when autonomy is restricted in everyday life domains.

Endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes as mechanism

Drawing on the Stereotype Embodiment Theory [37] and Age-Based Stereotype Threat framework [34], our research identifies the endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes as a key psychological mechanism underlying the health consequences of undermined autonomy as revealed in Study 1. Existing frameworks such as benevolent ageism and the pity and patronizing behaviors described by the BIAS map capture several paternalistic behavioral phenomena directed at older adults [12, 18]. However, they do not focus on the underlying cognitive basis, that is, what perceptions about older adults foster such behaviors. Infantilization overlaps conceptually with the "warm but incompetent" profile in the Stereotype Content Model [25], but whereas the SCM characterizes older adults' incompetence broadly in terms of age-related decline, infantilization specifically targets the denial of their status as mature and autonomous agents. This denial of maturity and autonomous agency also shares certain features with animalistic dehumanization. However, animalistic dehumanization encompasses a broader set of attributes (e.g., lack of culture or civility) and remains rarely studied in aging population [9, 28]. The present findings further demonstrated that, compared with this broader framework, infantilization maps more directly to paternalistic behaviors commonly observed in aging contexts, such as shielding older adults from bad news, offering unsolicited help based on presumed incompetence, or preempting their financial and legal decisions [15, 16, 57, 63]. Although well-intentioned, these behaviors convey the implicit message that older adults are not mature enough to manage their own affairs, thereby systematically undermining their autonomy.

Our findings also provide experimental support for a pathway linking autonomy-undermining cues with stereotype embodiment (SET) and stereotype-threat (ABST) processes. According to SET, older adults exposed to such autonomy-undermining treatment may internalize these distinct stereotypes, coming to view their own age group as childlike or irrational. Once internalized, these stereotypes may impair physical and cognitive performances through the processes described by ABST. By addressing the relationship of stereotype embodiment and its threats in a single experiment, our findings suggest a potentially negative reinforcement loop: as older adults internalize infantilizing views and their performance declines, the observed impairment may appear to validate the helpers' initial stereotypes, inviting further paternalistic restrictions on autonomy.

Practical implications

These findings carry practical implications at multiple levels. At the individual and family level, caregivers should be guided to distinguish between necessary assistance and paternalistic overreach [4, 43]. Concrete strategies include using less elderspeak, consulting older adults about their preferences before making decisions, involving them in financial and healthcare planning, and regularly assessing what they can and wish to do independently.

At the institutional level, healthcare facilities and settings should implement training programs to reduce infantilizing practices such as unsolicited assistance, and exclusion from care planning [46, 55]. Autonomy-supportive care models such as the Safety-Autonomy Grid [40] should be adopted to help balance protection with independence.

At the policy level, given our finding that financial autonomy is strongly linked to mortality risk, policies safeguarding older adults' financial decision-making rights warrant particular attention. More broadly, anti-ageism initiatives should explicitly target benevolent ageism alongside hostile discrimination. Unlike overtly negative stereotypes, infantilizing attitudes are often disguised as care and concern, making them harder to recognize and resist [21, 36]. Interventions promoting healthy aging must therefore go beyond addressing blatant discrimination to challenge the subtle beliefs underpinning paternalistic practices.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be addressed. First, certain measurements used in this research have constraints. The autonomy measures in Study 1 relied on single items, which cannot fully capture the multidimensional nature of autonomy, although convergent results across two distinct indicators strengthen confidence in these findings. Additionally, this item does not specify the agent restricting the older adult's autonomy (e.g., family members, healthcare providers, or policies), since different agents may have differential effects on older adults' well-being. Future research should employ context-specific measures to disentangle their influences. The measure of infantilizing age stereotype endorsement in Study 2, while grounded in established theoretical dimensions and aligned with the ageism literature, was newly developed and has not undergone formal psychometric validation. Future research should develop validated, multi-item measures for both constructs.

Second, the excluded participants with missing data were older, less healthy, and had lower autonomy at baseline than the remaining sample in Study 1a. This systematic difference indicates that our findings may not fully generalize to the entire older population, particularly the most vulnerable subgroups with advanced age and greater functional dependence. Autonomy restrictions among these excluded individuals may be primarily driven by physical necessity and care-related constraints, rather than the social and psychological factors examined in the present study. Future research focusing on frail and care-dependent older adults is needed to determine whether the social and psychological pathways identified here also operate in these populations. Additionally, the present research did not include comparisons with other age groups. Future research incorporating cross-age designs (e.g., children vs. older adults) would help to further clarify the developmental characteristics of paternalism and infantilization in later life.

Third, participants in Study 2 read general vignettes rather than recalling personal experiences, which may have limited the strength of the priming manipulation and partly explain the small effect sizes and absence of direct effects on performance. To enhance ecological validity, future studies should employ behavioral manipulations that directly restrict participants' own autonomy during experimental tasks and in daily settings [1], and would benefit from replication in larger samples.

Finally, the cultural context of our sample should be considered. In Chinese society, strong norms of filial piety [58] may render paternalistic help from family members more acceptable or even expected. Older adults in such contexts may be more willing to accept assistance from their children, even when it compromises their autonomy. Moreover, autonomy in collectivist contexts may be better understood as "relational autonomy", referring to having a meaningful voice within one's social network rather than individual independence per se [26]. From this perspective, autonomy is undermined not when older adults receive help, but when their preferences are neglected or they are excluded from decisions. Nevertheless, even within this cultural context, undermined autonomy was still associated with adverse health outcomes, suggesting the robustness of autonomy as a fundamental need. Future research should replicate these findings in Western and other cultural contexts [50, 61].

Conclusions

The present research demonstrates that undermined autonomy poses a significant and independent threat to older adults' physical health and longevity. We further suggest that the endorsement of infantilizing age stereotypes serves as the psychological mechanism. Theoretically, the present research offers an integrated perspective linking Self-Determination Theory, Stereotype Embodiment Theory, and Age-Based Stereotype Threat: undermined autonomy may foster the embodiment of infantilizing age stereotypes, which in turn contributes to impaired physical and cognitive functioning. This integrated perspective underscores the importance of protecting older adults' autonomy from such subtle, paternalistic ageism. Such protection requires not only supporting their daily decision-making in activities, finances, and healthcare, but also actively recognizing and challenging the infantilizing attitudes that give rise to paternalistic practices. Above all, respecting older adults' autonomy is not merely a matter of dignity; it is a direct investment in their long-term health and well-being.
