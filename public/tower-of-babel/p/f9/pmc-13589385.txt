Sensory impairment, activities of daily living, and multimorbidity in middle-aged and older adults: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Chunjie Huang, Sichang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo examine the longitudinal associations between sensory impairment and multimorbidity among middle-aged and older adults in China, and to explore whether activities of daily living (ADL) mediate these associations.MethodsData were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including the baseline wave in 2011 and follow-up waves in 2015 and 2018. This study was designed as a longitudinal cohort study. For repeated-measures generalized estimating equations (GEE) analysis, 11,542 participants with complete multimorbidity data across three waves were included. For longitudinal analyses of incident multimorbidity, 7,248 participants free of multimorbidity at baseline and successfully followed in both 2015 and 2018 were analyzed, accounting for 74.8% of the 9,696 participants without multimorbidity at baseline. Sensory impairment was classified into four categories using self-reported vision and hearing function: no sensory impairment (NSI), self-reported visual impairment (VI), self-reported hearing impairment (HI), and self-reported dual sensory impairment (DSI). VI was operationally defined as reporting “poor” distance or near vision, and HI as reporting “poor” hearing. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of ≥ 2 physician-diagnosed chronic conditions. Repeated-measures logistic regression using GEE was used to examine the association between baseline sensory impairment and multimorbidity across survey waves. Fixed-time logistic regression models were further used to examine incident multimorbidity at follow-up. Multinomial logistic regression was conducted as a sensitivity analysis, and mediation analysis was conducted to examine the potential role of ADL.ResultsIn the repeated-measures GEE analysis, VI, HI, and DSI were each associated with higher odds of multimorbidity across survey waves after adjustment for covariates and survey wave (VI: OR = 1.538, 95% CI: 1.426–1.658; HI: OR = 1.421, 95% CI: 1.231–1.641; DSI: OR = 2.115, 95% CI: 1.888–2.370). In fixed-time longitudinal analyses, all impairment types predicted incident multimorbidity over 2011–2015 (VI: OR = 1.381, 95% CI: 1.172–1.627; HI: OR = 1.380, 95% CI: 1.017–1.872; DSI: OR = 1.412, 95% CI: 1.063–1.875). Over the longer 2011–2018 follow-up, only DSI remained significantly associated with incident multimorbidity (OR = 1.449, 95% CI: 1.116–1.881). Multinomial logistic regression further showed a graded association between sensory impairment and chronic disease burden, with the strongest associations observed for DSI. Mediation analysis indicated that ADL partially mediated the association between DSI and incident multimorbidity, accounting for 14.20% of the total effect.ConclusionsSensory impairment is significantly associated with multimorbidity among middle-aged and older Chinese adults, with DSI showing the most persistent long-term effect. ADL decline may represent a potential indirect pathway linking DSI to subsequent multimorbidity. These findings highlight the importance of integrated sensory screening and functional maintenance interventions, particularly for individuals with DSI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28537-0.

Introduction

One of the challenges brought by aging is health issues, with multimorbidity being particularly prominent [30]. Multimorbidity is typically defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic diseases in an individual [20]. Commonly co-occurring chronic conditions include cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, arthritis, chronic respiratory diseases, and mental health disorders [50]. A study in the United States found that 77.3% of individuals aged 65 and above suffer from two or more chronic diseases [35]. In a study involving older Chinese adults, 42.4% were found to have multimorbidity, with higher prevalence among women and urban residents [51]. The prevalence of multimorbidity increases with age, from 10% in those aged 0–19 to 78% in those over 80(van den [42]. Multimorbidity is a major cause of disease burden for patients, caregivers, and society as a whole [4], and is associated with increased risk of mortality, decline in physical function, higher risk of disability, and reduced quality of life [21].

Multimorbidity is a complex clinical phenomenon with diverse and intertwined causes, involving factors ranging from genetics to lifestyle [24]. Notably, recent studies have identified sensory impairments as a significant risk factor for multimorbidity [49]. Sensory impairments, common in aging, include visual impairment (VI), hearing impairment (HI), and dual sensory impairment (DSI). It is estimated that at least 2.2 billion people globally have vision impairment, of whom at least 1 billion have vision impairment that could have been prevented or remains unaddressed,most people with vision impairment and blindness are over the age of 50 years [46]. Approximately 1.57 billion people worldwide suffer from HI, with those over 50 accounting for 62.1% of this group. By 2050, the number of people with HI is expected to increase to 2.45 billion [18]. Studies show that the prevalence of DSI among individuals aged 55 and older is 6%, increasing to 26.8% among those aged 80 and above [36]. Existing studies have shown that sensory impairments are closely linked to a variety of chronic diseases. For instance, HI is associated with a higher incidence of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cognitive impairments, stroke, arthritis, and cancer [31]. The presence of HI along with other conditions such as frailty can further deteriorate functional status [6]. Moreover, among older adults with VI, the prevalence of 15 common chronic diseases is significantly higher compared to those without VI [17]. Research has also found that the combination of visual and auditory impairments significantly affects the functional independence of older adults, especially when cardiovascular diseases and hypertension are present, exacerbating the impact [25]. In Ireland, studies have shown that DSI significantly increases the risk of poor self-assessed health, limited activities of daily living (ADL), feelings of loneliness, and multimorbidity among older adults [9].

To understand the impact of sensory impairments on middle-aged and older adults' multimorbidity, it is important to explore potential explanatory pathways. From a biological perspective, poor vision can limit the ability to perform daily activities [28], increase the risk of falls [34], and adversely affect medication management [29], all of which may exacerbate other chronic conditions. HI can lead to communication difficulties and reduced social interactions, promoting a sedentary lifestyle that subsequently increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases and diabetes [1]. Psychologically, VI is often associated with a higher incidence of depression and anxiety, which can further hinder the management of other health conditions [22]. Similarly, HI can lead to communication barriers, increasing the risk of cognitive decline. These factors may interact and intensify the degree of multimorbidity [15]. According to a study in China, the coexistence of DSI and multimorbidity significantly increases limitations in ADL and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) among middle-aged and older adults [45]. This risk is particularly pronounced when DSI coexists with multimorbidity patterns affecting vital organs such as the liver and kidneys.

According to recent literature reviews, studies conducted in the past decade have increasingly revealed a potential link between sensory impairments and chronic diseases. However, most research has focused on the impact of a single sensory impairment on a specific chronic disease [31], or the relationship between a single sensory impairment and multimorbidity [49], with few studies exploring the connection between combinations of different sensory impairments and multimorbidity. Building on this foundation, our study will use data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to examine how various sensory impairments, including VI, HI, and DSI, correlate with multimorbidity among middle-aged and older adults, and will preliminarily analyze the potential mechanisms involved. This study proposes the following hypotheses: Significant correlations exist between sensory impairments (including VI, HI, and DSI) and multimorbidity among middle-aged and older adults. The ability to perform daily activities may mediate these relationships.

Materials and methods

Study population and data source

This study used data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative longitudinal survey of community-dwelling Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. CHARLS collects information on sociodemographic characteristics, health conditions, and functional status through standardized face-to-face interviews. We used the baseline wave in 2011 and two follow-up waves in 2015 and 2018 to examine the associations between baseline sensory impairment and multimorbidity across survey waves and the risk of incident multimorbidity during follow-up.

The merged 2011 baseline dataset included 17,705 respondents. We excluded respondents younger than 45 years or with missing age information (n = 421; 367 were aged 22–44 years and 54 had missing age), and those with missing data on sex (n = 12), hukou status (n = 11), education (n = 24), marital status (n = 1), smoking status (n = 117), drinking status (n = 8), or sensory impairment measures (n = 1,377). The remaining 15,734 participants constituted the baseline analytical sample, used for the descriptive and multinomial sensitivity analyses.

From this baseline sample, we derived two longitudinal samples (Fig. 1). For the repeated-measures analyses across survey waves, we constructed a three-wave balanced panel by excluding 2,602 and 1,590 participants without valid multimorbidity data in 2015 and 2018, respectively, leaving 11,542 participants with complete multimorbidity information at all three waves (34,626 person-wave observations). For the incidence analyses, we instead excluded 6,038 participants with multimorbidity at baseline to obtain an at-risk cohort of 9,696, and after linking to the 2015 and 2018 waves by unique respondent identifiers excluded a further 1,536 and 912 participants without valid follow-up, yielding an incident-multimorbidity sample of 7,248 followed in both waves. Because follow-up was incomplete, 4,192 of the 15,734 baseline participants (26.6%) were not retained in the three-wave panel. To assess attrition bias, we compared baseline characteristics between retained and non-retained participants (Table S1); those lost to follow-up were on average older and more likely to be urban, more educated, unmarried, and current smokers, and to have a higher baseline disease burden, whereas differences in the distribution of sensory impairment were small.

Self-reported sensory impairments

Sensory impairments were assessed using self-reported items from the CHARLS questionnaire at baseline. Visual function was evaluated using two questions on distance and near vision. Distance vision was assessed by asking: “How good is your eyesight for seeing things at a distance, like recognizing a friend from across the street? (with glasses or corrective lenses if you wear them)?” (DA033). Near vision was assessed by asking: “How good is your eyesight for seeing things up close, like reading ordinary newspaper print? (with glasses or corrective lenses if you wear them)?” (DA034). Hearing function was assessed by a global self-reported hearing question: “Is your hearing … (with a hearing aid if you normally use it and without if you normally don’t)?” (DA039). For each item, response options were excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor.

In this study, VI was operationally defined using self-reported visual function rather than clinical visual acuity or visual-field measurements. Participants who reported “poor” vision on either the distance-vision item (DA033) or the near-vision item (DA034) were classified as having VI. HI was defined as reporting “poor” hearing on DA039. Dual sensory impairment (DSI) was defined as meeting criteria for both VI and HI. Participants were categorized into four mutually exclusive groups: no sensory impairment (NSI), self-reported VI only, self-reported HI only, and self-reported DSI.

All sensory impairment variables were derived from baseline assessments and treated as the exposures in subsequent analyses. These items assess sensory function in respondents’ usual condition (with corrective lenses/hearing aids if typically used), but CHARLS does not allow us to quantify device use.

Multimorbidity

Multimorbidity was defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions (≥ 2) in the same individual. In CHARLS, participants were asked whether a doctor had ever diagnosed them with any of a set of chronic diseases. In this study, multimorbidity was constructed based on the following 14 physician-diagnosed chronic conditions: hypertension; dyslipidemia; diabetes; malignant tumors; chronic lung diseases; liver disease; heart disease; stroke; kidney disease; stomach or other digestive system disease; emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems; memory-related disorders; arthritis or rheumatism; and asthma.

We summed the number of these conditions reported by each participant and created a binary indicator of multimorbidity: participants reporting ≥ 2 conditions were classified as having multimorbidity, whereas those reporting 0–1 condition were classified as not having multimorbidity. For the repeated-measures analysis, multimorbidity status measured at each survey wave was treated as the binary outcome. For fixed-time longitudinal incidence analyses, we focused on incident multimorbidity by excluding participants with multimorbidity at baseline and defining incident multimorbidity as meeting the ≥ 2-condition criterion at follow-up among those free of multimorbidity at baseline.

Activities of daily living

ADL were assessed using a 12-item functional measure in CHARLS, including six basic activities of daily living (BADL; dressing, bathing, eating, getting in/out of bed, using the toilet, and continence) and six instrumental activities of daily living (IADL; doing household chores, shopping, using the telephone, cooking, managing finances, and taking medications). For each item, respondents rated the degree of difficulty on a four-point scale: (1) no difficulty, (2) some difficulty but can still do it alone, (3) difficulty and need help, and (4) unable to do it alone. Item scores were summed to create a continuous ADL difficulty score ranging from 12 to 48, with higher scores indicating greater difficulty in daily functioning (i.e., poorer functional ability).

Covariates

The demographic variables include age, sex (female, male), educational level (elementary school and below, junior high school, high school, college and above), hukou status (rural, urban), and marital status (unmarried, married). Health-related variables include smoking (no, yes) and drinking (no, yes).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by sensory impairment status (NSI, VI only, HI only, and DSI). Continuous variables were described as mean (standard deviation, SD) and categorical variables as number (percentage).

To account for the repeated-measures structure of the CHARLS data, we used repeated-measures logistic regression based on generalized estimating equations (GEE) to examine the association between baseline sensory impairment status and multimorbidity across survey waves. Multimorbidity status measured at each survey wave was treated as the binary outcome, and baseline sensory impairment status was treated as the exposure.

In addition to the repeated-measures GEE model, we conducted fixed-time incidence analyses among participants without multimorbidity at baseline. Incident multimorbidity at follow-up was modeled as the outcome, and baseline sensory impairment was modeled as the exposure. Separate binary logistic regression models were fitted for the 2011–2015 and 2011–2018 follow-up periods.

To explore potential heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex and by hukou status using the 2011–2018 incidence cohort. In addition, we formally tested effect modification by fitting pooled models that included interaction terms between sensory impairment category and sex, and between sensory impairment category and hukou; P values for interaction were obtained from joint Wald tests of all interaction terms.

Mediation analyses were conducted within a counterfactual framework. Sensory impairment status in 2011 was specified as the exposure (X), ADL score in 2015 as the mediator (M; continuous), and incident multimorbidity in 2018 as the outcome (Y; binary). The mediator model was estimated using linear regression and the outcome model using logistic regression, adjusting for baseline covariates. We assumed no exposure–mediator interaction (nointer). We reported the controlled direct effect (CDE), natural indirect effect (NIE), total effect (TE), and the proportion mediated when the total effect was statistically significant. For key significant effects, we additionally quantified robustness to unmeasured confounding using E-values.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the main findings. We re-estimated the main repeated-measures GEE model and fixed-time incidence models using individual-level sampling weights and cluster-robust standard errors at the community level. We also conducted multinomial logistic regression using baseline chronic disease burden categories, with no chronic condition as the reference category.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2.

The prevalence of multimorbidity at baseline differed significantly across groups. Participants with VI, HI, and especially DSI showed a higher prevalence of multimorbidity compared with those without sensory impairment. Specifically, multimorbidity was present in 32.4% of participants without impairment, 45.2% of those with VI, 43.6% of those with HI, and 55.6% of those with DSI.

Participants with sensory impairment tended to be older than those without impairment, with mean age increasing from 58.0 years in the NSI group to 59.9 years in the VI group, 63.4 years in the HI group, and 64.8 years in the DSI group. Significant differences were also observed across impairment groups in sociodemographic and health-related characteristics, including sex, education level, hukou status, marital status, smoking, and drinking status. Overall, the DSI group exhibited the most disadvantaged profile, characterized by older age, lower educational attainment, and a higher proportion residing in rural areas.

In the 2011–2015 analysis, compared with participants with NSI, those with VI at baseline had significantly higher odds of developing multimorbidity at follow-up (OR = 1.381, 95% CI: 1.172–1.627; p < 0.001). Baseline HI was also significantly associated with incident multimorbidity (OR = 1.380, 95% CI: 1.017–1.872; p = 0.039). Baseline DSI showed a significant association as well (OR = 1.412, 95% CI: 1.063–1.875; p = 0.017).

In the 2011–2018 analysis, baseline DSI remained significantly associated with a higher risk of incident multimorbidity (OR = 1.449, 95% CI: 1.116–1.881; p = 0.005). In contrast, baseline VI (OR = 1.051, 95% CI: 0.894–1.236; p = 0.545) and HI (OR = 0.833, 95% CI: 0.595–1.165; p = 0.286) were not statistically significant over the longer follow-up period. The confidence intervals for VI and HI included the null value, indicating statistical uncertainty regarding their long-term associations with incident multimorbidity.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

Using the 2011–2018 incidence cohort, we assessed whether the fixed-time longitudinal association between baseline sensory impairment and incident multimorbidity differed by sex or hukou status. We first conducted covariate-adjusted stratified analyses by sex and by hukou using the same model specification as in the main longitudinal model; the detailed stratified results are reported in the Supplementary Materials (Table S3 and Table S4). In the sex-stratified models, baseline DSI was associated with higher odds of incident multimorbidity among women (OR = 1.616, 95% CI: 1.141–2.290), whereas the corresponding association among men was not statistically significant (OR = 1.264, 95% CI: 0.850–1.878). In the hukou-stratified models, baseline DSI remained statistically significant in the rural subgroup (OR = 1.428, 95% CI: 1.081–1.887), while the estimate in the urban subgroup was imprecise and not statistically significant (OR = 1.614, 95% CI: 0.761–3.425). To formally test effect modification, we fitted pooled models including interaction terms between sensory impairment category and sex, and between sensory impairment category and hukou, and performed joint Wald tests for the interaction terms. The interaction tests were not statistically significant for sex (χ2(3) = 2.21, P = 0.529) or for hukou (χ2(3) = 0.75, P = 0.862), indicating no statistical evidence that the association between baseline sensory impairment and incident multimorbidity over 2011–2018 varied by sex or hukou status. Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses addressing the CHARLS sampling design and alternative operational definitions of multimorbidity.

First, to account for the complex survey nature of CHARLS, we re-estimated the repeated-measures GEE model (Sect. 3.2) and the fixed-time longitudinal incidence models (Sect. 3.3) using individual sampling weights and cluster-robust standard errors at the community level. Specifically, we fitted weighted models with pweight and computed robust variance estimates clustered by community identifier.

Second, to examine whether the associations were robust across different levels of chronic disease burden, we conducted a multinomial logistic regression analysis using baseline chronic disease burden categories. The outcome was categorized as no chronic condition, one chronic condition, two chronic conditions, and three or more chronic conditions, with no chronic condition as the reference outcome category. Relative risk ratios (RRRs) and 95% CIs were reported for VI, HI, and DSI, using NSI as the reference exposure group.

Across these specifications, the overall pattern of results was consistent with the primary analyses. Notably, in the multinomial logistic regression analysis, with no chronic condition as the reference outcome category, VI, HI, and DSI were all associated with higher relative risks of greater chronic disease burden, and the magnitude of these associations increased as the number of chronic conditions increased. Detailed results are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S5–S7).

Discussion

This study utilized nationally representative data from CHARLS to investigate the longitudinal association between sensory impairments and multimorbidity among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Our findings indicate that self-reported VI, HI, and DSI were significantly associated with higher odds of multimorbidity in the repeated-measures GEE analysis, with self-reported DSI showing the strongest association. In fixed-time incidence analyses, all three impairment types predicted incident multimorbidity over the 4-year follow-up. However, over the longer 7-year follow-up, only self-reported DSI remained a significant predictor of incident multimorbidity. Furthermore, our mediation analysis suggested that ADL may serve as a potential pathway in the association between self-reported sensory impairment and incident multimorbidity, explaining approximately 14.2% of the total effect for DSI.

Our repeated-measures GEE analysis showed robust associations between baseline self-reported sensory impairment and multimorbidity across survey waves, and the multinomial analysis further supported a graded relationship, with self-reported VI, HI, and especially DSI associated with progressively higher chronic disease burden. These findings are broadly consistent with prior evidence across countries and study designs. Both VI and HI have been independently linked to a greater multimorbidity burden in cohorts from China, the United States, and Europe [10, 11, 38, 49], while DSI tends to exert an amplified or "additive" effect, often remaining significant after multivariable adjustment even when associations for single impairments attenuate [13, 47]. The evidence is not entirely uniform, however. Bidirectional relationships are plausible, as a heavier chronic disease burden may itself coincide with more frequent vision problems [26], and associations for single impairments tend to weaken when the outcome is narrowly defined (e.g., cardiometabolic multimorbidity only) [47]. These inconsistencies likely stem mainly from heterogeneity in outcome definitions and in the strictness of sensory-impairment classification across studies.

Compared with previous studies, our longitudinal analysis yields several novel findings. Specifically, over the 4-year follow-up period, all types of self-reported sensory impairment predicted incident multimorbidity; however, over the longer 7-year follow-up, only self-reported DSI remained a significant predictor. Several mechanisms may help explain this temporal divergence. First, the sensory compensation hypothesis posits that individuals with a single sensory impairment may develop effective adaptive strategies over time [16]. Over a longer time horizon, these individuals can rely on the remaining intact sense to compensate for the deficit—for instance, people with HI may use lip-reading [5], whereas those with VI may rely more on auditory cues [27]—or they may adopt assistive devices such as hearing aids or glasses, thereby attenuating the long-term risk of adverse health outcomes. In contrast, DSI deprives individuals of both major sensory inputs, leaving little room for compensatory buffering and making the accumulation of health deficits more persistent and difficult to mitigate [7, 37]. Second, the synergistic burden of DSI may generate a higher cumulative allostatic load than single impairments. A growing body of evidence indicates that DSI is closely associated with more severe functional limitations, social isolation, and depressive symptoms [2, 8], all of which are well-established antecedents of chronic disease onset and progression [48]. Importantly, these psychosocial pathways often require a longer “incubation period” to manifest as somatic multimorbidity [33].

Our mediation analysis suggested that reduced ADL may represent one plausible pathway linking sensory impairment to multimorbidity. This finding is highly consistent with the Disablement Process Model [43], suggesting that sensory impairment, as an upstream disadvantage, may be associated with subsequent functional decline that in turn relates to the accumulation of chronic diseases. This potential pathway may be particularly relevant among older adults with DSI, as concurrent deficits in vision and hearing substantially compromise their ability to perform everyday tasks safely and independently. As a result, individuals with DSI may shift from active engagement to activity avoidance, becoming more sedentary and physically inactive [44]. In this sense, DSI can be viewed as an early-life disadvantage that contributes to declining daily functioning [39], which may subsequently increase the risk of chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes. Moreover, longitudinal evidence indicates that DSI is associated with life-space constriction, which restricts older adults’ mobility and activity levels over time, further reinforcing functional deterioration [19, 40]. From this perspective, reduced ADL may not only reflect lower physical participation but could also be linked to downstream physiological changes, including metabolic dysfunction and sarcopenia [23], which may be associated with greater susceptibility to multimorbidity [14, 32]. A growing body of research supports the protective role of physical activity in both the prevention and management of multimorbidity. Regular moderate-to-vigorous physical activity significantly lowers the risk of multimorbidity, and maintaining a higher level of daily activity helps slow the onset and progression of multiple chronic conditions [3, 12]. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that ADL explained only part of the total effect (14.2%), indicating that DSI may affect multimorbidity through additional direct or indirect potential pathways, such as increased cognitive load, psychological distress (e.g., depression and loneliness), or shared neurodegenerative pathology—pathways that warrant further investigation.

Based on our findings, we suggest that vision and hearing screenings be integrated into routine chronic disease management for older adults, as sensory impairments serve as significant early indicators of multimorbidity risk. Special priority should be given to individuals with DSI, whose long-term risk of multimorbidity is most persistent; therefore, policy support for sensory aids (e.g., glasses and hearing aids) is crucial to mitigate this trajectory. Furthermore, given that ADL significantly mediate the relationship between DSI and multimorbidity, public health interventions should extend beyond clinical care to focus on "functional maintenance"—such as community rehabilitation and home modifications—to preserve independence and break the cycle of decline. Finally, targeted resource allocation is necessary to support rural and lower-educated populations who are disproportionately affected by the compounding burdens of sensory and chronic conditions.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, sensory impairments were assessed using self-reported vision and hearing questions rather than clinical visual acuity, visual-field, or audiometric measurements. Therefore, VI, HI, and DSI in this study should be interpreted as self-reported sensory impairment under respondents’ usual conditions rather than clinically diagnosed impairment. Because the CHARLS questions assess sensory function with corrective lenses or hearing aids if typically used, these measures do not allow us to distinguish uncorrected impairment from irreversible sensory loss or to quantify assistive device use, which may have introduced classification bias. Second, participants with missing baseline sensory-function measures were excluded from the analysis. Although complete-case analysis ensured consistent exposure classification, the relatively large number of missing sensory-function responses may have introduced selection bias if non-response was related to participants’ health status. Third, although our mediation analysis highlighted the role of ADL, the development of multimorbidity likely involves a more complex and multidimensional causal chain, including mental health, social participation, physical activity, nutritional status, and access to health care. Therefore, the identified mediation via ADL should be viewed as a partial and probable pathway rather than an exhaustive mechanistic explanation. Fourth, analytical sample was also subject to incomplete follow-up. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, participants lost to follow-up differed from those retained—a pattern consistent with the selective attrition commonly seen in aging cohorts. Because those with more severe sensory impairment and multimorbidity were somewhat more likely to drop out, this non-random attrition may have biased the observed associations, most plausibly toward the null.
