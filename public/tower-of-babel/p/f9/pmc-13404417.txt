Efficacy of high-flow nasal cannula versus conventional face mask oxygen therapy on ipsilateral cerebral haemodynamics during the immediate post-extubation phase following carotid endarterectomy: protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Zice Liu, Yi An, Lixia Li, Zhongjia Li, Chuanyu Liang, Pei Wang, Ke Cui, Manke Luo, Shuning Zhang, Xue Li, Shuai Ruan, Tianlong Wang, Qinghai Liu, Lei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (CHS) is a severe complication following carotid endarterectomy (CEA). During emergence from general anaesthesia, residual anaesthetics induce mild respiratory depression and carbon dioxide accumulation, exacerbating hyperperfusion risks via hypercapnia-induced cerebral vasodilation. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) effectively reduces excessively elevated pressure of arterial carbon dioxide via multiple mechanisms including anatomical dead space washout. This trial aims to evaluate the effects of HFNC versus conventional mask oxygen therapy on ipsilateral cerebral haemodynamics during the immediate post-extubation phase in patients undergoing CEA.Methods and analysisThis single-centre, randomised controlled trial will allocate 66 adult patients scheduled for elective CEA in a 1:1 ratio to receive either HFNC or a conventional oxygen mask for 10 min immediately following tracheal extubation. The primary outcome is the ipsilateral relative middle cerebral artery blood flow velocity (rMCAv) at 10 minutes post-extubation. Secondary outcomes encompass continuous high-frequency trajectories of ipsilateral rMCAv and invasive arterial blood pressure, their haemodynamic variability (coefficient of variation) and hyperperfusion burden (area under the curve), absolute changes in arterial blood gases (ABG) and clinical outcomes evaluated up to hospital discharge (including abnormal ipsilateral cerebral hyperperfusion, postoperative headache, CHS, incidence of postoperative acute ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack and length of stay). Longitudinal high-frequency data will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models while pre-intervention and post-intervention ABG variations will be evaluated via analysis of covariance.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol (V.2.0, 30 June 2025) complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (LYS[2025]-NO.147-002). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. Results will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500105199.Keywords: Randomized Controlled Trial, ULTRASONOGRAPHY, Hemodynamics, Stroke, ANAESTHETICS

Introduction

Internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis is a major aetiology of ischaemic stroke.1–3 Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) significantly mitigates the risk of recurrent stroke in patients with moderate-to-severe extracranial ICA stenosis.4 However, perioperative cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (CHS) remains a critical complication. CHS occurs when cerebral blood flow acutely exceeds the autoregulatory capacity of the chronically hypoperfused vascular bed, potentially resulting in ipsilateral headache, cerebral oedema, intracranial haemorrhage and focal neurological deficits.5–8

Cerebral vascular tone is highly sensitive to partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2).9 Following extubation, residual anaesthetic effects and surgical stress frequently compromise ventilatory drive, predisposing patients to CO2 accumulation. Hypercapnia-induced cerebral vasodilation subsequently destabilises cerebral haemodynamics and amplifies reperfusion injury risks.10 11 Conventional face masks, while routinely utilised to maintain oxygenation, are insufficient in preventing CO2 rebreathing during episodes of respiratory depression.12–15

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy delivers heated, humidified gas at flow rates up to 80–100 L/min. A fundamental physiological advantage of HFNC is the continuous washout of the nasopharyngeal anatomical dead space, effectively attenuating CO2 rebreathing and reducing PaCO2.16–20 We hypothesise that the immediate application of HFNC following extubation in CEA patients enhances CO2 clearance, blunts hypercapnia-induced cerebral vasodilation and minimises haemodynamic oscillations. This trial is designed to compare the efficacy of HFNC versus conventional face mask oxygen therapy in stabilising cerebral haemodynamics, optimising PaCO2 and reducing the hyperperfusion burden in the post-anaesthesia emergence phase.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This single-centre, randomised controlled trial will be conducted at Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University from 2025 to 2026. The protocol is developed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines. Trial status

This study has been reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, on 28 May 2025, and has been registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry website (registration date: 30 June 2025). The trial is currently at the stage of patient recruitment and data collection. Participant recruitment officially began on 9 September 2025, and the study is expected to be completed by 9 September 2026.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Scheduled for elective CEA under general anaesthesia; (2) age≥18 years; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I-III; (4) intact cognitive function and ability to comply with the study protocol; (5) unilateral ICA stenosis ≥50% confirmed by preoperative transcranial Doppler (TCD); (6) feasibility of intraoperative middle cerebral artery (MCA) monitoring via TCD; (7) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (1) History of nasopharyngeal deformity or prior surgery; (2) preoperative arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis indicating baseline respiratory failure or CO2 retention (PaCO2 ≥45 mm Hg); (3) absolute contraindications to HFNC (eg, acute nasopharyngitis, complete upper airway obstruction, basilar skull or nasal bone fractures).

Withdrawal and termination criteria

Participants will be withdrawn from the trial under the following conditions: (1) Voluntary withdrawal of informed consent; (2) occurrence of severe perioperative complications necessitating immediate protocol deviation (eg, massive haemorrhage, cardiac arrest); (3) extreme postoperative agitation or complete loss of the TCD acoustic window precluding data acquisition. The trial will be prematurely terminated if: (1) Unacceptable rates of severe adverse events (AEs) are directly attributed to the intervention; (2) the Institutional Review Board mandates cessation due to safety concerns.

Randomisation and blinding

Eligible participants will be allocated (1:1 ratio) to either the HFNC group or the Mask group via a computer-generated random number sequence. Allocation concealment will be strictly maintained using opaque, sealed and sequentially numbered envelopes prepared by an independent researcher not involved in clinical management. Blinding of the anaesthetists and participants is not feasible due to the visibly distinct nature of the devices. To control for detection bias, primary data analysis will be executed by an independent statistician blinded to group assignments.

Interventions and contingency plan

The designated intervention will commence for a duration of 10 min immediately after extubation.

HFNC group: Pure oxygen (fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) set to 100% at the air-oxygen blender) administered via HFNC with weight-adjusted flow rates (40 L/min for <50 kg; 50 L/min for 50–70 kg; 60 L/min for >70 kg).

Mask group: Pure oxygen (O2 flowmeter connected to the wall source) administered via a conventional face mask with weight-adjusted flow rates (8 L/min for <50 kg; 10 L/min for 50–70 kg; 12 L/min for >70 kg).

The specific weight-adjusted flow rates for both the HFNC and conventional face mask groups (eg, HFNC flow rates structured at 40, 50 and 60 L/min according to body weight tiers) were determined based on standard clinical titration protocols. These flow rates are designed to exceed the patient’s peak inspiratory flow during the awake emergence phase, thereby optimising anatomical dead-space washout and ensuring precise oxygen delivery while maintaining maximum patient tolerance. Furthermore, the use of ‘pure oxygen’ in this protocol refers exclusively to the FiO2 set to 100% at the source gas blender or wall flowmeter, acknowledging that the actual alveolar oxygen concentration achieved will vary, particularly in the face mask group due to room air entrainment.

To strictly standardise the emergence phase and minimise potential performance bias secondary to varied levels of residual anaesthetic-induced respiratory depression, a structured verbal cueing protocol will be uniformly implemented across both study arms. Immediately on extubation and the concurrent initiation of the assigned oxygen therapy (pure source oxygen for both groups initially), the attending anaesthetist will provide a standardised verbal prompt to breathe at fixed 15 s intervals throughout the entire 10-minute intervention period.

During the intervention, patients in the HFNC group will be closely monitored for device-related complications. If a patient exhibits severe intolerance or specified complications (eg, severe nasopharyngeal discomfort, gastric distension, mucosal bleeding, severe agitation or other unanticipated events), HFNC will be immediately discontinued and the patient will be converted to conventional face mask oxygen therapy. Such instances will be documented as treatment discontinuations due to AEs, and the data will be analysed strictly under the intention-to-treat (ITT) framework to preserve randomisation integrity.

Preoperative preparation and monitoring

Patients in both groups will strictly adhere to standard preoperative fasting guidelines and will not receive any premedication. On arrival at the operating room, peripheral intravenous and radial arterial access will be established. Routine intraoperative monitoring will be continuously applied, including heart rate (HR), continuous invasive radial arterial blood pressure, pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2), tidal volume, peak airway pressure and the Bispectral Index (BIS).

Anaesthesia induction and maintenance

All patients will undergo a strictly standardised general anaesthesia protocol. Following 5 min of pre-oxygenation with pure source oxygen, anaesthesia induction will be achieved using etomidate (0.1–0.2 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3 µg/kg) and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). Then endotracheal intubation will be performed. Anaesthesia will be maintained with continuous intravenous infusions of propofol and remifentanil. Norepinephrine will be administered via continuous infusion as clinically indicated to closely maintain haemodynamic stability and meet surgical requirements throughout the procedure.

Intraoperative ventilation strategy

Baseline mechanical ventilation for all patients will be delivered using the volume-controlled ventilation mode. The ventilatory parameters will be set as follows: tidal volume at 6–8 mL/kg, FiO2 at 0.50, and an inspiration-to-expiration ratio of 1:2. During the surgery, the respiratory rate will be continuously titrated to maintain the end-tidal carbon dioxide between 35 and 45 mm Hg.

Extubation criteria

On completion of the surgery, the infusions of propofol and remifentanil will be immediately discontinued. The endotracheal tube will be removed, and the allocated oxygen therapy (based on randomisation) will be initiated, only when the patient fulfils all of the following explicit criteria:

Recovery of adequate spontaneous breathing.

Recovery of adequate spontaneous breathing.

Full recovery of consciousness (defined as a BIS >90 sustained for at least 10 s, spontaneous eye opening to verbal command and the ability to follow instructions).

Regarding the collection of respiratory and metabolic parameters, the initial trial registration included transcutaneous PaCO2 monitoring. However, strict data quality control during the initial enrolment phase revealed significant signal instability and a high rate of data artefact with the transcutaneous sensors in this specific post-extubation setting. To ensure the rigorous quantification of ventilatory efficacy and data integrity, a methodological adaptation was implemented to use ABG analyses—the clinical gold standard. Specifically, ABG samples will be drawn at exactly two predefined time points: immediately prior to the initiation of the designated oxygen therapy and 10 min after its commencement. Because obtaining these samples via the pre-existing radial arterial cannula is part of the standard perioperative care for CEA patients at our institution, this adaptation captures highly accurate physiological metrics without introducing additional invasive procedures or clinical risks. Furthermore, to provide a precise and objective cardiovascular risk stratification for this specific surgical cohort, the Revised Cardiac Risk Index will be calculated and documented for all enrolled participants during the baseline clinical data acquisition phase.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Ipsilateral relative middle cerebral artery blood flow velocity (rMCAv) at the conclusion of the 10-minute allocated oxygen intervention (which commences immediately post-extubation).

Definition of baseline: Following anaesthesia induction, the patient’s blood pressure will be pharmacologically stabilised to their preoperative baseline (defined as the average of blood pressure measurements taken on days 0, 1 and 2 preoperatively). Once stabilised, the MCA mean velocity (MV) measured via the temporal window is designated as 100% (baseline). Subsequent real-time MCA MV values are expressed as a relative percentage of this baseline, defined as rMCAv.

Data management and monitoring

All clinical and physiological data will be recorded in standardised electronic case report forms (eCRFs) using a secure clinical data management system in compliance with data privacy regulations. To ensure data integrity and accuracy, data entered into the eCRFs will be routinely cross-checked against original source documents by an independent monitoring team through Source Data Verification. Any identified discrepancies will be managed through a formal query process and resolved by a designated senior investigator. Following complete query resolution and rigorous data cleaning, the final database will be officially locked to prevent further alterations prior to statistical analysis. All original study files and records will be securely archived for a minimum of 5 years after trial completion.

Adverse event reporting

An AE is defined as any untoward medical occurrence during the study period, regardless of its causal relationship to the assigned oxygen therapy. All AEs will be comprehensively documented in the eCRFs, detailing their nature, onset, duration, severity and subsequent management. Any serious adverse events (SAEs)—such as those that are life-threatening or prolong hospitalisation—will be managed immediately to ensure participant safety. The principal investigator will be notified promptly, and all SAEs will be reported to the Institutional Review Board within 24 hours of identification. All AEs will be actively followed-up until complete resolution or clinical stabilisation.

Sample size calculation

Due to a paucity of literature examining HFNC effects on immediate post-extubation cerebral haemodynamics following CEA, a preliminary pilot study was conducted at our institution (unpublished data). Pilot data indicated that the rMCAv at the end of the 10-minute oxygen intervention was 130.8%±34.5% in the HFNC group and 168.8%±39.9% in the Mask group. Based on a mean difference of 38.0% and a pooled SD of 37.3%, sample size was calculated utilising G*Power software. To achieve 95% statistical power at a two-sided significance level of α=0.05, 26 patients per group were required. Adjusting for an anticipated 20% data attrition rate, the final target sample size was established at 66 patients (33 per group).

Statistical analysis and handling of missing data

Statistical analyses will be performed using R software (V.4.5.2). All statistical tests will be two-sided, and a p value<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Normality of continuous variables will be assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Baseline characteristics will be compared using independent t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or χ2 tests as appropriate. For categorical secondary outcomes evaluated up to hospital discharge (abnormal ipsilateral cerebral hyperperfusion, headache, CHS, complications), comparisons will be conducted using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Length of postoperative hospital stay and calculated metrics (CV and AUC) will be compared using independent t-tests or the Mann-Whitney U test depending on data distribution. Post-intervention PaCO2 and PaO2 variations will be evaluated using analysis of covariance with baseline ABG values incorporated as covariates.

For the primary efficacy analysis, the high-frequency longitudinal data (acquired every 6 s) will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models (LMM). The model incorporates group, time and their interaction (Group×Time) as fixed effects, and subject as a random effect to account for within-subject correlation. An appropriate covariance structure (eg, first-order autoregressive, AR(1)) will be explored to best model the correlation of repeated measures over time.

To illustrate the primary analytical framework and our clinical hypothesis, an illustrative conceptual model of the longitudinal rMCAv dynamics was constructed (figure 2). We hypothesise that, compared with conventional mask oxygenation, HFNC will significantly attenuate both the initial surge and the subsequent oscillatory amplitude of rMCAv following extubation, thereby yielding a stabilised cerebrovascular trajectory characterised by lower CV and AUC. This hypothesised divergence over the 10-minute acute phase conceptually represents the Group×Time interaction that the aforementioned LMM framework is powered to detect.

This primary analysis will strictly adhere to the ITT principle, encompassing all randomised participants, inclusive of those crossing over to conventional mask therapy due to complications.

For baseline characteristics and discrete clinical endpoints, missing data will be assessed before analysis. If the proportion of missing data is less than 5%, a complete-case analysis will be used. However, if the missing data rate exceeds 5%, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) will be implemented to generate multiple complete datasets, assuming the data are missing at random. Regarding the high-frequency longitudinal measurements (rMCAv and haemodynamics), missing data are anticipated primarily as transient technical artefacts. The LMM, using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, intrinsically accommodates these randomly missing intermittent data points without ad hoc imputation. Nevertheless, a strict quality control threshold will be applied: if a participant has >20% missing longitudinal data points (ie, >2 min of the 10-minute intervention phase) due to continuous probe dislocation or equipment failure, their data will be excluded from the specific dynamic trajectory analysis to prevent model distortion.

Discussion

The emergence and extubation phase of general anaesthesia is characterised by profound sympathetic arousal, often leading to drastic haemodynamic fluctuations.21 22 For patients undergoing CEA, this transition period is particularly perilous. Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion often leaves these patients with impaired cerebral autoregulation, rendering them highly vulnerable to abrupt changes in blood pressure, arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide levels.23–26 To our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to evaluate the acute cerebrovascular protective effects of HFNC in the immediate post-extubation window of CEA patients, using continuous, high-frequency TCD quantification. By bridging airway management with neuro-monitoring, this study aims to determine whether the physiological benefits of HFNC—specifically, optimised oxygenation and dead-space CO2 washout—translate into stabilised cerebral haemodynamics.

A notable methodological strength of this trial is the integration of high-resolution, continuous data acquisition with an advanced statistical framework. Traditional perioperative studies typically rely on intermittent, discrete physiological measurements, which fail to capture the transient yet clinically significant cerebrovascular instability triggered by extubation. The primary outcome is specifically defined within the 10-minute post-extubation window to capture the most acute phase of emergence-induced hypercapnia, while simultaneously mitigating the risk of continuous TCD signal degradation caused by movement artefacts in fully awake patients. By recording rMCAv and systemic haemodynamics every 6 s and quantifying the dynamic volatility through CV and AUC, our design provides a highly detailed assessment of cerebrovascular haemodynamics during respiratory transitions. Furthermore, employing LMM with a first-order AR covariance structure allows for the optimal handling of this dense longitudinal dataset (100 data points per patient). Coupled with strict thresholding for missing data and MICE for baseline variables, this analytical strategy rigorously defends the ITT principle while resisting distortion from transient technical artefacts.

We acknowledge several inherent limitations in the study design. First, the visibly distinct nature of HFNC and conventional oxygen masks precludes the blinding of the attending anaesthetists and the patients. However, the risk of detection bias is heavily mitigated by our reliance on objective, device-generated primary endpoints (TCD and monitor metrics). Furthermore, the data transcription and subsequent statistical analyses will be conducted by independent researchers blinded to the group allocation. Second, as a single-centre trial with a calculated sample size of 66 patients, the external validity of our findings may be restricted. Nevertheless, this single-centre approach ensures strict, standardised adherence to anaesthetic management and TCD probe positioning, significantly minimising interoperator variability and strengthening internal validity. Furthermore, while early treatment crossover may occur due to device intolerance, our reliance on the ITT framework, reinforced by the intrinsic capacity of LMM to accommodate unbalanced longitudinal data without ad hoc imputation, robustly preserves the statistical integrity of the primary analysis.

Finally, regarding the potential pulmonary complications associated with oxygen therapy, we acknowledge that the routine administration of pure oxygen (source FiO2 100%) during anaesthesia induction and the immediate post-extubation phase may theoretically increase the risk of absorption atelectasis in non-critically ill populations.27 28 However, for our specific cohort of elderly patients with severe cerebrovascular disease, maximising the safe apnoea time to prevent transient hypoxaemia or hypercapnia-induced haemodynamic destabilisation remains the paramount clinical priority. To actively mitigate potential pulmonary risks, our institutional protocol strictly mandates routine lung recruitment manoeuvres following intubation and prior to extubation. Furthermore, preliminary data from our pilot study demonstrated no clinically detectable postoperative atelectasis, with all patients maintaining stable SpO2 and PaO2 levels within normal ranges in their ABG analyses. Therefore, our standardised use of pure oxygen safely adheres to the established institutional protocols for neurovascular anaesthesia.

Ultimately, this trial will provide high-quality evidence regarding the prophylactic utility of HFNC in neurosurgical anaesthesia. If our hypothesis is confirmed, HFNC could serve as an adjunctive non-pharmacological neuroprotective strategy rather than solely a respiratory support modality. Stabilising cerebral haemodynamics during the critical extubation window could represent a paradigm shift in the perioperative management of CEA, potentially reducing the incidence of postoperative neurological complications such as hyperperfusion syndrome.
