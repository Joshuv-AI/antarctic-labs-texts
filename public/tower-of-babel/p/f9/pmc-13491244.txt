Cretaceous moss–cyanobacteria association reveals trophic interactions in polar forests of Antarctica
Authors: Zane Walker, Alexander C Bippus, Ari Iglesias, Selena Y Smith, Brian A Atkinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Through symbiotic associations with microbes, mosses play many important ecological roles in terrestrial ecosystems, such as facilitating trophic interactions and nitrogen cycling. However, little is known about interactions between mosses and microbes in past ecosystems. Here, we characterize a permineralized moss with epiphytic cyanobacteria and tubular hyphae from the Cretaceous of Antarctica.The fossil was identified in a calcium carbonate concretion from the Beta member of the Santa Marta Formation Cretaceous (Campanian) from James Ross Island. The fossil was studied using acetate peels and light microscopy.The fossil moss is assignable to Bryopsida. Cyanobacteria trichomes with heterocyst‐like cells are present in leaf axils and the perichaetium. In some leaf axils, epiphytic fungi are preserved. The apical perichaetium contains several archegonia; one contains an egg cell while another contains a possible embryo.Fossil epiphytic cyanobacteria demonstrate that moss associations were part of Antarctic polar forests during the Cretaceous. Recognizing their importance in modern ecosystems, moss–cyanobacteria associations likely contributed bioavailable nitrogen to the nutrient cycle of ancient polar forests, which would benefit organisms in different trophic levels. This work reveals long‐term stasis in commensal relationships between cyanobacteria and mosses and provides a minimum age for these relationships. Keywords: archegonium, bryosphere, fungi, Nostocales, symbiosis

Introduction

Consisting of over 12 000 species (Goffinet & Buck, 2004; Christenhusz & Byng, 2016; Dong et al., 2025) and inhabiting every continent, mosses play key roles in many ecologically processes. One such role is the formation of the bryosphere, which is the nexus of living and dead moss tissue that hosts diverse microbial inhabitants including prokaryotes, fungi, and microfauna (Lindo & Gonzalez, 2010). Microbial interactions hosted within the bryosphere support other trophic levels that create an essential link between the nutrient cycle of the bryosphere and the local ecosystem (Gerson, 1969; Seastedt, 1984; Merrifield & Ingham, 1998; Lindo & Gonzalez, 2010; Wardle, 2013). These microbial communities often include nitrogen‐fixing nostocalean cyanobacteria, which can have a profound influence on nutrient cycles in local ecosystems and the broader biosphere (Solheim & Zielke, 2002; Turetsky, 2003; Lindo & Gonzalez, 2010). Moss–cyanobacteria associations, in particular, have been studied in several different ecosystems (Menge & Hedin, 2009; Jean et al., 2012; Deane‐Coe, 2015; Page & Flannery, 2018; Calabria et al., 2020), especially in (but not limited to) boreal forests, Arctic tundra, and Antarctic habitats (DeLuca et al., 2002; Pandey et al., 2004; Zielke et al., 2005; Alvarenga et al., 2024). Importantly, a significant amount of ecosystem nitrogen input can be derived from moss–cyanobacteria associations in boreal ecosystems (Turetsky et al., 2012; Alvarenga & Rousk, 2022). In these high‐latitude ecosystems, epiphytic cyanobacteria on mosses may produce up to half of the bioavailable nitrogen (an excess of 2 kg N ha−1 yr−1), significantly contributing to global nitrogen cycles (Dodds et al., 1995; DeLuca et al., 2002; Solheim & Zielke, 2002; Zielke et al., 2002, 2005; Rousk, 2025).

Despite their ubiquitous nature and ecological importance in modern ecosystems, fossil evidence for moss–cyanobacteria symbiotic associations has been lacking. This hinders our understanding of the role that mosses and the inhabitants of the bryosphere played in nutrient cycling within ancient ecosystems. Unlike other bryophytes, mosses (with the exception of Sphagnales) host epiphytic cyanobacteria rather than endophytic cyanobacteria as seen in liverworts and hornworts (Adams & Duggan, 2008). Recognizing that moss epiphytes are susceptible to depositional abrasion, they are likely to have low preservational potential. Furthermore, fossil bryophytes are undersampled and understudied relative to tracheophytes. However, there are a few reports of structurally and anatomically preserved (permineralized) mosses with fungi from the Early Cretaceous Apple Bay locality (Shelton, 2015; Tomescu, 2016). These fossilized moss–fungi associations suggest that the recovery and analysis of permineralized mosses have much potential for revealing important aspects of the bryosphere and its inhabitants including nutrient cycling and trophic interactions.

Herein, we characterize an exceptionally preserved, permineralized moss gametophyte with epiphytic cyanobacteria and a fungus from the Late Cretaceous of Antarctica. Cellular preservation in this fossil allows for the characterization of several epiphytic cyanobacteria filaments on the moss in addition to an epiphytic fungus. These prokaryote, fungal, and plant fossils reveal the presence of a crucially important ecological symbiosis and trophic interaction in ancient Antarctica shortly after the initial phylogenetic and ecological diversification of flowering plants (Benton et al., 2022).

Materials and Methods

The specimen described in this study comes from a single marine carbonate concretion collected from the Santa Marta Formation (c. 80 Ma) on James Ross Island, Antarctica, near Brandy Bay and Baculites Hill, 63°52′35″S, 57°54′00″W. Using data from magnetostratigraphy and biostratigraphy, this locality has been determined to be early‐to‐middle Campanian in age (Olivero, 2012; Milanese et al., 2020), which was later confirmed in 2024 field expedition by the authors (AI, SM, and BA). The Beta Member of the Santa Marta Formation preserves a rich assemblage of marine invertebrates, marine vertebrates, and plants including bryophytes, ferns, gymnosperms, and angiosperms (Scasso et al., 1991; Hayes et al., 2006; Reguero & Gasparini, 2006; Olivero, 2007; Iglesias, 2016; Silva et al., 2025).

Fossil preparation

The permineralized moss gametophyte was identified in a single concretion (UWBM 116051) that was collected by Peter Ward (University of Washington). The concretion was sectioned into slabs using a water‐cooled diamond‐edged rock saw. The fossil moss was identified on the bottom face of slab B. The cellulose acetate peel technique was performed (Joy et al., 1956) using 5% hydrochloric acid to etch the concretion surface for c. 15 s. Cellulose acetate peels were mounted on microscope slides with xylene‐soluble Eukitt Mounting Medium (O. Kindler Gmbh, Bobingen, Germany). Specimens were photographed using a DS‐Fi3 Nikon camera mounted to a Nikon Eclipse LV100ND microscope (Nikon Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and processed using Adobe Photoshop and Lightroom (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA). A total of 28 slides were made: UWBM 116051 B bot 370α, 380α, 406α, 422α, 425α, 431α, 436α, 437α, 438α, 440α, 441α, 442α, 443α, 444α, 445α, 446α, 447α, 448α, 449α, 450α, 453α, 454α, 455α, 462α, 470α, 479α, 481α, 488α. Slides are housed at the Paleobotany collections at the Burke Museum (UWBM) of the University of Washington.

Leaf lamina

Leaves loosely sheathing around the stem in transverse section lack plications (Figs 2e,f,h, S1b,c). Leaves for the basal‐most half portion of leaf and range 602–885 μm in width ultimately tapering to a narrower free limb in the distal half of the leaf measuring 630 μm in width widest points and occasionally becoming reflexed (Figs 1a arrows, 4c). The broadly tapering width of leaves examined through oblique sections indicates a lanceolate leaf shape. Leaves range in length from 1.1 to 3.1 mm in length and may vary dramatically due to abrasion of apical regions of leaves (Figs 1a, 4c). Near the point of attachment, leaves clasp around stem and overlap one another (Figs 1a, 2e,f,h, S1b,c). Cells of the lamina at the apical and mid‐regions of the leaf in peridermal view are rectangular and elongate measuring 16–18 μm wide and 65–71 μm long (Fig. 2b). Cells at the basal or alar region of the leaf are difficult to characterize due to the plane of section; however, their morphology seems to vary little from more distal leaf cells (Fig. S1b). Transverse sections of apical and mid‐regions of leaves reveal a slightly inflated marginal cell (Fig. 2c arrowheads,e arrowhead,f arrow). The lamina is unistratose with rounded cells in transverse section, which are 16–17 μm thick and 12–15 μm in width (Fig. 2c,e,f,h).

Costa

The costa protrudes outward toward the abaxial side of the lamina in cross section and only slightly protrudes on the adaxial side (Fig. 2a,c,e,f,h). The costa is 70–80 μm wide and 45–55 μm thick at mid‐sections of leaves (Fig. 2c,e,f,h). In all sections and leaves, costa appears percurrent and associated with some laminal cells (Figs 2a, 4c). Costal anatomy is simple and consists of a single layer of abaxial and adaxial epidermal cells (Fig. 2f arrowheads) that surround several poorly preserved thin‐walled cells in the center of the costa (Fig. 2c arrow). Epidermal cells of the costa are similar in size to the laminal cells and measure 20 μm wide and 10 μm thick (Fig. 2c,e,f arrowheads,h).

Stem

The single unbranched stem (Fig. 1a) has a central conducting strand of dark, elongate, thin‐walled cells that are incompletely preserved and measure 14.5–19.0 μm in width (Fig. 2d red asterisk). A cortical layer that lacks clear cellular preservation surrounds the central conducting strand (Fig. 2d). At the widest basal sections, the stem measures 326–339 μm in diameter with the central conducting strand measuring 107–164 μm in diameter (Fig. 1a). Epidermal cells of the stem are rectangular in longitudinal section and measure 61.1–153.5 μm in length and 14.6–22.2 μm in width (Fig. 2i). Multicellular smooth rhizoids arise from leaf axils at basal regions of the stem and measure at least 430 μm in length (Fig. 2d arrowheads,g arrowheads). Rhizoid cells are smooth walled and have oblique cross walls (Fig. 2g). Cells measure 12.5 μm in width and 88.0–156.0 μm in length (Fig. 2g).

Perichaetium

The perichaetium consists of apical perichaetial leaves, most of which are longer than more basally positioned leaves measuring 3455–3527 μm in length (Fig. 1a). These perichaetial leaves surround exclusively female reproductive structures and paraphyses (Figs 1a,b, 3a–c).

Paraphyses

Paraphyses are uniseriate and filamentous, measuring 260–335 μm in length and 26 μm in width (Figs 1b, 3a). Paraphyses are 5–7 cells in length with terminal cells that are smooth walled with a bulbus, blunt tip (Figs 1b, 3a,c) or appear tapered near the apex (Fig. 1b).

Archegonia

At least three archegonia with varying degrees of preservation and development have been identified within the perichaetium (Fig. 3a–c). Fig. 3(a) reveals a single archegonium surrounded by paraphyses. Near the base of this archegonium and immediately below neck cells, a well‐preserved egg is present (Fig. 3b arrow) with a distinct darker‐colored nucleus (Fig. 3b arrowhead). The egg is 22 μm in length and 11–12 μm in width with the nucleus measuring 5 μm in diameter (Fig. 3b). The neck cells of this archegonium are 16–17 μm in length and 17–18 μm in width (Fig. 3b). Canal cells are incompletely preserved or absent (Fig. 3b). We use prepared sections of an archegonium of extant Mnium (Mniaceae; Fig. 3d) as a comparison that allows us to confidently interpret the fossil archegonium as containing a preserved egg cell with nucleus.

A significantly larger archegonium also occurs in the perichaetium (Fig. 3c). The neck cells (Fig. 3c arrowheads) are 20–25 μm in length and 20 μm in width. The cells of the venter and below the neck cells are indistinguishable. The region below the neck cells is much larger than the previously described archegonia at 296 μm in length and 80 μm in width. This larger archegonium also contains amorphous tissues, which are a notably different color and less completely preserved than surrounding archegonium (Fig. 3c). The much larger size of this archegonium leads us to conclude that before preservation, the egg of the archegonium was fertilized as nonfertilized archegonia undergo no further development (Shaw & Goffinet, 2000). Areas of incomplete preservation within the archegonium are consistent with the interpretation that these tissues represent rapidly dividing thin‐walled cells of the embryo and/or archegonial cells that develop into the calyptra. Unfortunately, incomplete preservation precludes more detailed description of such structures.

Epiphytic cyanobacteria

Cyanobacterial trichomes occur in at least five separate leaf axils and the perichaetium (Fig. 1b–g). Two cyanobacteria morphotypes co‐occur in the leaf axils; one morphotype is recognized by trichomes with spherical cells (Fig. 1c,d,g arrowhead), while the other morphotype has trichomes with cylindrical cells (Fig. 1e,f). This variation in cell morphology may indicate the presence of two co‐occurring species, as is often the case with extant moss–cyanobacteria symbioses (Gentili et al., 2005; Zielke et al., 2005; Holland‐Moritz et al., 2021). We follow the terminology of Komárek & Johansen (2015) when describing cyanobacteria morphology.

Phylum: Cyanobacteria

Order: Nostocales Borzi

Family: Nostocaceae C.A. Agardh ex Kirchner

Morphotype 1

Description

Seven trichomes of this morphotype have been identified as epiphytes on the single stem of K. cyanifera. These trichomes are uniseriate, unbranched, and isopolar, consisting of 9–11 spherical cells (Fig. 1b–g arrowhead). Trichomes are 40–67 μm in length and lack sheaths. There are two cell types within the trichomes of this morphotype, one of which is characteristic of vegetative cells that have a diameter of 5.7–7.2 μm and a cell wall thickness of 0.4–0.5 μm. In addition to the vegetative cells, there are cells that we interpret as heterocytes. With a diameter of 7.5–7.8 μm, the heterocytes are slightly larger than the vegetative cells. The cell walls of the heterocytes are distinct from those of vegetative cells by having an ‘inner layer’ with a fairly bright amber color and a dark ‘out layer’ (Fig. 1d), suggesting that they are comprised of a different composition than the walls of vegetative cells. Furthermore, the cell walls of the heterocytes are thicker (0.46–0.49 μm thick) than the cell walls of most vegetative cells.

Within the perichaetium, there is a shorter trichome of six spherical cells (Fig. 1b arrowhead). The trichome is 33 μm in length, and cells are 6.4–6.6 μm in diameter. The terminal cell of this trichome is tapering with a conical shape. The position of this trichome and the tapered morphology of the apical cell is diagnostic of hormogonia (Fig. S1a). It is worth noting that tapered cells of hormogonia develop into heterocytes after successful colonization (Campbell & Meeks, 1989). Motile hormogonia of extant symbiotic cyanobacteria are composed of shorter trichomes, with cells that may morphologically differ than the original trichome, and often colonize the apical regions of moss (Solheim & Zielke, 2002; Bay et al., 2013; Risser, 2023). The spherical shape of the intercalary cells leads us to assign this hormogonium to Morphotype 1 (see the Discussion section).

Order: Nostocales

Family: Nostocaceae

Morphotype 2

Description

Trichomes of this morphotype are isopolar, uniseriate, and consist of cylindrical or barrel‐shaped cells (Fig. 1e,f). Sheaths are absent. The length of trichomes ranges from 45 to 226 μm and consist of 10–20 cells (Fig. 1e,f). For one of the trichomes of this morphotype, the cell size decreases from one end (presumably the base) to the terminal end (Fig. 1g arrow). Additionally, one trichome of this morphotype appears to be truly branched with T‐type branching (Fig. 1e). Trichomes have differentiated cells including vegetative cells and intercalary heterocytes. Vegetative cells are typically cylindrical, whereas heterocytes have a more spherical shape (Fig. 1f arrowhead). These heterocytes are intercalary and occur singly along the trichome and have a diameter of up to 8.4–8.5 μm while the vegetative cells have a diameter of 6.67–7.31 μm. Heterocytes in these filaments have thicker cell walls at 0.61–0.68 μm, compared to the thinner, 0.35‐ to 0.41‐μm‐thick cell walls of the vegetative cells. Cell walls of the heterocytes have an ‘inner layer’ that is a bright amber color, which is lacking in the vegetative cells.

Fungal association

Epiphyllous fungal hyphae are present in a single leaf axil of K. cyanifera near the mid to basal region of the gametophyte (Fig. 4a arrowheads). A paradermal section of the leaf and stem reveals septate tubular hyphae preserved in the leaf axil and on an abaxial basal leaf surface (Fig. 4a,b). Individual fungal cells range in size from 2.5–4.4 μm in width and 6.9–15.3 μm in length (Fig. 4a). Hyphal strands measure 44 μm to at least 119 μm in length (Fig. 4a).

Discussion

Comparison of K. cyanifera to extant and fossil taxa

Gametophytic traits preserved in K. cyanifera resemble the range of variation observed within Bryopsida. Without sporophyte characters, however, a more precise taxonomic placement of Kimmerera within Bryopsida is difficult. Homoplasious traits and general lack of defining gametophyte characters among Bryopsida make it difficult to assign Kimmerera to a specific order or family with confidence. Splachnaceae (Splachnales), Bryales, and Funariales appear most similar to K. cyanifera. For instance, these clades include species with a similar costal anatomy to the fossil (Krupa, 1984; Lüth, 2019; Lin et al., 2021). In addition, members of these clades have lamina that are often entire margined, unistratose, and comprised of rounded or rectangular‐shaped cells in transverse section (Flora of North America Editorial Committee, 2007, 2014). Leaves in these groups often form a broad semicircle in transverse section, which weakly clasps the stem and overlaps with more apically attached leaves (Flora of North America Editorial Committee, 2007, 2014; Lüth, 2019). Furthermore, these three clades include species known to form an association with cyanobacteria (e.g. Brasell et al., 1986; Solheim & Zielke, 2002; McCuaig et al., 2015).

While the vegetative anatomy of Splachnaceae generally conforms to K. cyanifera (as discussed earlier), the reproductive anatomy between these two taxa differs. Lateral archegonia develop in the leaf axils in most genera within Splachnaceae, and genera with terminal archegonia lack abundant paraphyses (Chopra, 1974). However, it is plausible that a stem group member of the family had terminal gametangia rather than axillary.

The reproductive anatomy of Funariales is similar to Kimmerera as some species within this order produce terminal archegonia with paraphyses (Crum, 2001). However, most Funariales have a diminutive stem, with only a few leaves arranged in a rosette often densely arranged near the apical region of the stem (Flora of North America Editorial Committee, 2007). This is unlike Kimmerera, which has leaves more or less evenly distributed along the stem.

Bryales includes hundreds of extant moss species and extensive morphological diversity (Shaw & Goffinet, 2000; Flora of North America Editorial Committee, 2014). All traits of Kimmerera fit within the range of variation in Bryales. However, several characters typical of many families in Bryales differ from the fossil. For instance, unlike our fossil, many Bryales such as Bryaceae, Mniaceae, and Cinclidiaceae have leaves with a border of thin and elongate cells (Flora of North America Editorial Committee, 2007; Lüth, 2019). The reproductive biology of many bryalean families, such as Mniaceae, is also similar to the Antarctic fossil, with terminally produced archegonia and abundant paraphyses (Fig. 1a,b). Further diagnostic characters such as mature sporophyte structure, additional morphological features of the laminal cells, and asexual structures are not preserved in the fossil, which prevents us from assigning Kimmerera to a specific family within this order with confidence.

Overall, the vegetative and reproductive characters preserved in Kimmerera overlap with Bryales, Funariales, and Splachnaceae. It is possible that Kimmerera could represent an extinct higher clade, but until more diagnostic characters can be recognized, we refrain from assigning this fossil moss to a specific order or family within Bryopsida. Despite this, the preservation of Kimmerera allows for the observation of enough features to describe it as a new genus rather than assigning it to a form taxon such as Muscites. The approach of creating a new genus has been used to categorize other incompletely preserved (but distinct) permineralized gametophytes (e.g. Bippus et al., 2019; Walker et al., 2024).

While other Cretaceous mosses have been described from James Ross Island, only the external traits are described for the majority of these reported specimens (Kadlecová, 2020). Without internal anatomy, it is difficult to compare these taxa. Permineralized moss gametophytes described from the Cretaceous of Antarctica include Jamesrossia plicata Z. Walker, Stockey, Rothwell, Atkinson, S.Y. Smith & Iglesias (Walker et al., 2024), which was identified in the same slab as Kimmerera. However, Jamesrossia was assigned to Rhabdoweisiaceae (Dicranales). The vegetative gametophytic anatomy of Kimmerera differs from Jamesrossia in several significant ways. For instance, unlike Kimmerera, leaves of Jamesrossia are plicate in cross section, have recurved, bistratose margins, and have a well‐defined D‐shaped costa with two median guide cells (Walker et al., 2024). Likewise, Livingstonites gabrielae was also described from the Antarctic Peninsula but differed in anatomy from Kimmerera as the costal anatomy is more complex (Vera, 2011).

Microbial epiphytes on K. cyanifera

The modern bryosphere is a crucial detrital system in which living and dead moss tissue form habitats for diverse communities of microflora and microfauna. This system can have a significant impact on nutrient cycling and trophic interactions in a wide array of ecosystems. Moss and cyanobacteria symbiotic associations are a key example of this in which epiphytic Nostocales fix atmospheric nitrogen into a bioavailable form for the moss host, which later enters the broader ecosystem. While this widespread symbiotic association is ecologically important today, before this work, it had not been identified in the fossil record, hindering our understanding of the role of the bryosphere in ancient ecosystems.

Nostocalean cyanobacteria of K. cyanifera

We identify the epiphytic cyanobacteria on K. cyanifera as Nostocales, and more specifically Nostocaceae. Nostocales are among the most common diazotrophs that develop symbiotic associations with mosses. These prokaryotes are filamentous and have nitrogen‐fixing heterocytes, a structure not present in any other known microbe. Heterocytes can be identified by their distinctive cell walls, which are thicker than those of vegetative cells. Among Nostocales, Nostocaceae are among the most common cyanobacteria found in symbiotic association with mosses (Warshan et al., 2016; Klarenberg et al., 2022; Rousk, 2022). These bacteria are characterized by uniseriate trichomes or filaments that may or may not branch, comprising cells that are spherical or barrel‐shaped to cylindrical (Komárek et al., 2014).

Nostocaceae colonize bryophytes using hormogonia (Meeks et al., 2002; Bay et al., 2013; Álvarez et al., 2023). These motile trichomes are typically shorter than vegetative trichomes, lack heterocytes, and are often comprised of differently shaped and/or sized cells. Furthermore, the end cells of the hormogonia are often tapered, giving the cell a conical appearance (Risser, 2023). Hormogonia often travel to the apex of moss gametophytes in which they can establish themselves in areas of fresh growth (Solheim & Zielke, 2002; Bay et al., 2013; Warshan et al., 2017). Colonization by cyanobacteria via hormogonia is higher for gametophytes with sporophytes, possibly as a response to increased nitrogen requirements of sporophytes (Bay et al., 2013), and may provide a further explanation for why both established and motile cyanobacteria occur on K. cyanifera. To initiate cyanobacteria colonization, mosses typically release chemicals (e.g. nitric oxide) that trigger nearby cyanobacteria to develop motile hormogonia (Warshan et al., 2017). Via chemotaxis, hormogonia travel toward the soon to be moss host and begin colonizing the surface of the gametophyte with developing vegetative trichomes, occupying axils of leaves or upper surfaces of leaves (Bay et al., 2013). Once the hormogonia establish themselves on a host and return to a phase of vegetative growth, the tapered end cells eventually differentiate into heterocytes (Campbell & Meeks, 1989).

Based on the previously mentioned traits, the morphology of the epiphytic trichomes on K. cyanifera is consistent with Nostocales, more specifically Nostocaceae. The trichomes are uniseriate, comprise spheroidal or barrel‐shaped cells, and rarely branch. Furthermore, the trichomes possess intercalary heterocytes with thickened cell walls that differ in color (and likely composition) than the vegetative cells. Among the population of epiphytic trichomes on K. cyanifera, there is a hormogonium‐like trichome at the apex of the gametophyte. This trichome is composed of fewer cells relative to the other trichomes positioned lower on the gametophyte and is terminated by a conical or tapering cell. While the cells of the hormogonium‐like trichome are not markedly smaller than the cells of vegetative trichomes, they potentially represent cells that have transitioned into a vegetative phase from a motile phase while the tapering cell has yet to differentiate into a heterocyte (Rippka et al., 1979). The position of the vegetative trichomes and the hormogonium‐like trichome on the gametophyte of K. cyanifera is exactly where epiphytic symbiotic cyanobacteria are found in extant mosses. Overall, due to the morphology, including the presence of heterocytes, and the position of the epiphytic trichomes, we assign these microbes to Nostocaceae, the most common diazotrophs that form symbiotic associations with modern mosses.

Comparison between cyanobacteria of K. cyanifera and moniliform fungi in the fossil record

Fungi with moniliform hyphae superficially resemble cyanobacteria. These fungi, often belonging to Ascomycota, colonize the surfaces of bryophytes and tracheophytes (Döbbeler, 1997; Rosa et al., 2020) and have been identified in the fossil record (Shelton, 2015; Harper et al., 2017; Nunes et al., 2021). While moniliform hyphae are comprised of spheroidal to barrel‐shaped cells, several important traits allow us to distinguish these fungi from the epiphytic cyanobacteria on K. cyanifera.

Fossil moniliform hyphae are often highly branched (Harper et al., 2017; Nunes et al., 2021), whereas the cyanobacterial trichomes of K. cyanifera rarely branch. Furthermore, moniliform hyphae often form dense masses, or subicula, that cover the exterior surfaces of the plant (Shelton, 2015; Nunes et al., 2021), as commonly occurs in extant sooty molds (Chomnunti et al., 2014). The overall position and relative low density of cyanobacterial trichomes in the leaf axils of K. cyanifera are most consistent with extant moss–cyanobacteria symbiotic associations. Cells of moniliform hyphae often have pore‐like detachment scars or denticle‐like detachment points of conidia (Nunes et al., 2021), which are lacking in the cells of the cyanobacteria filaments described in this study. Moniliform hyphae often have tubular hyphae attached at one end (Shelton, 2015; Harper et al., 2017), a trait not present in the cyanobacteria of this study. Furthermore, many monoliform hyphae have porous or micro‐echinate cell wall ornamentation, which is absent in the cells of the trichomes associated with K. cyanifera. Lastly, the presence of differentiated cells interpreted as heterocytes is inconsistent with fungal morphology and biology.

Tubular hyphae of an epiphytic moss on K. cyanifera

A diversity of fungal taxa can be found on mosses ranging from saprotrophs, necrotrophs, biotrophs, or commensals (Döbbeler, 2002; Davey & Currah, 2006; Döbbeler & Hertel, 2013). The exceptionally preserved and permineralized assemblage from the Early Cretaceous Apple Bay locality (Vancouver Island, Canada) contains several fungi reported as bryophilous and are interpreted as biotrophs, necrotrophs, and potential saprotrophs (Shelton, 2015; Tomescu, 2016). Tubular hyphae that appear to belong to a commensal bryophilous fungus have been identified from the same Apple Bay locality (Tomescu, 2016). Beyond these examples, however, reports of fungi on bryophytes in the fossil record are lacking.

The tubular septate hyphae on the lower surface of the K. cyanifera leaf are not connected or attached to the markedly larger cyanobacterial trichomes, appearing more similar to that of hyphae produced by bryophilous fungal genera such as Epibryon (Döbbeler, 1997). However, the fossil tubular hyphae have few systematically informative characters for precise taxonomic identification. Most noncommensal bryophilous fungi are endophytic (Davey & Currah, 2006), whereas the fossil fungus in this study is epiphytic. Leaf and stem tissue show no clear signs of decomposition or necrosis as to be expected in some saprotrophic or necrotrophic fungal interactions (Davey & Currah, 2006). The presence of ephemeral structures on K. cyanifera, such as archegonia with an egg nucleus, may suggest that the fossil moss was relatively healthy and not in a prolonged state of decay or significant infection before deposition and fossilization. The depositional and fossilization residence time of K. cyanifera was likely rapid, and shorter than the time it would take the egg to decay (Tomescu, 2016). This further suggests that the fungi inhabiting the surface of K. cyanifera may have been commensal, obtaining nutrients, such as bioavailable nitrogen, from leachates derived from the host. It is also possible that the fungus was a dormant pathogen or parasite.

The bryosphere of cretaceous Antarctica

The specimen of K. cyanifera is a component of the bryosphere that existed in Antarctica during the Campanian, an ecological transitional time when angiosperms began to replace gymnosperms as the most diverse and common canopy forming plants in Antarctica (Cantrill & Poole, 2005; Pujana et al., 2018; Barreda et al., 2019; Benton et al., 2022). K. cyanifera and its epiphytic Nostocales and fungi provide insights into nutrient cycling and trophic interactions in a high‐latitude transitional flora.

Relative to nonreproducing gametophytes, mosses experience elevated rates of hormogonia colonization during sexual reproductive and sporophyte development phases, which are N demanding (Rydgren & Økland, 2002; Bay et al., 2013). The gametophyte of K. cyanifera described in this work is in a reproductive phase with an embryonic sporophyte developing within an enlarged archegonium. The prevalence of epiphytic Nostocales on Kimmerera, especially in the perichaetium, would have likely provided much needed nitrogen for the moss to develop a sporophyte (Rydgren & Økland, 2002), suggesting that nutrients may have been limiting in its environment. Kimmerera cyanifera and its microbial epiphytes are direct evidence that mosses formed commensal to symbiotic associations with N‐fixing cyanobacteria in polar ecosystems in Antarctica during the Campanian.

Mosses, with their epiphytic cyanobacteria, may have promoted the increase of bioavailable N accumulation in soils, helping the diverse Campanian polar West Antarctica forests thrive. Bioavailable N leaches from mosses during the flush and release of nutrients associated with desiccation and wetting cycles in modern ecosystems (Carleton & Read, 1991; Wilson & Coxson, 1999; Lindo & Gonzalez, 2010). This symbiotic association would have facilitated trophic interactions. N‐saturated leachates would have benefited microflora inhabiting this ancient bryosphere, such as the epiphytic fungus living on K. cyanifera. Overall, our results highlight the capacity of mosses and the broader bryosphere for hosting cyanobacteria and fungi during a critical ecological transition interval in potentially resource limited ecosystems of Antarctica.

Conclusion

This fossil reveals meaningful commensal to symbiotic interactions among moss, cyanobacteria, and fungi during the Late Cretaceous, a critical ecological transition interval. While bryophyte–cyanobacteria symbioses likely predate the Cretaceous, there has been no fossil evidence for these associations. Kimmerera cyanifera and its epiphytic cyanobacteria provide a minimum age of 77 Ma for these commensal relationships, indicating that there was long‐term stasis for moss and cyanobacteria associations. Furthermore, this fossil reveals that reproductive moss gametophytes have been able to attract hormogonia since the Late Cretaceous and probably much earlier.

The exceptional preservation of K. cyanifera demonstrates that the assemblages of permineralized plants in Cretaceous deposits of James Ross Island have much to offer in understanding the composition and ecology of ancient polar forests of Antarctica. The presence of the moss–cyanobacteria symbiosis demonstrates the bryosphere had the potential to facilitate the production of bioavailable N to Late Cretaceous ecosystems that were undergoing dramatic changes in vegetational structure as angiosperms diversified. As more fossil plants, especially bryophytes, are described from James Ross Island, eventual reconstruction of unique Cretaceous terrestrial ecosystems in exceptional detail continues to rapidly progress.
