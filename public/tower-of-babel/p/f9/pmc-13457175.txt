Reprogramming the tumor microenvironment by rational immune gene therapy: From in vivo CRISPRa screening to combination design
Authors: Yufan Zhao, Quanjun Yang
Published: 2026

Main text

The past decade of cancer immunotherapy has revealed a paradox: while the immune system possesses extraordinary anti-tumor potential, most solid tumors remain refractory to treatment due to a profoundly suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Efforts to overcome this barrier have largely relied on single-axis interventions, such as checkpoint blockade or isolated cytokine therapies, yet durable responses remain limited. In this context, the recent study by Zhang et al. in Cancer Discovery introduces a conceptually important advance: rather than beginning with a preselected cytokine or checkpoint target, they built an in vivo CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) platform to interrogate immune-regulatory nodes directly within the TME, leveraging the resulting signal map to rationally assemble combination gene therapies.1

The broader importance of this study is not simply that it identifies a potent gene combination but that it reframes the TME as a tractable design space for therapeutic bioengineering. Rather than pursuing another isolated immune target, Zhang et al. adopt a combinatorial logic that reflects the biological realities of solid tumors, where immune exclusion, stromal confinement, and metabolic suppression often coexist. The authors organized their screening library around antigen presentation, proliferation, costimulatory molecules, and migration—an APCM framework that mirrors the fundamental requirements for productive anti-tumor immunity. Through iterative in vivo screening and subtractive validation, the study converged on a minimal yet highly effective combination consisting of TNFSF9 (4-1BBL), IFNG, and IL12B, collectively termed 4II.1

Importantly, this APCM-guided strategy represents a distinct approach when viewed alongside other emerging TME reprogramming modalities. For example, TGF-β blockade primarily seeks to overcome stromal exclusion and improve immune infiltration, whereas metabolic reprogramming strategies attempt to restore T cell function by reversing hypoxia, lactate accumulation, and nutrient deprivation within tumors. In contrast, the 4II combination functions as an active and multi-pronged immune-conditioning platform that simultaneously enhances antigen presentation, inflammatory signaling, and T cell costimulation. Thus, rather than competing with current immunotherapies, the APCM strategy may serve as a biologically complementary platform capable of sensitizing tumors to immune checkpoint inhibitors or adoptive cell therapies.

Biologically, the final combination is compelling because it addresses three distinct barriers at once. IFNG can promote antigen presentation and inflammatory activation; TNFSF9/4-1BBL provides a potent costimulatory cue that supports T cell expansion and persistence; and IL12B, by supporting IL-12/IL-23 family signaling, reinforces inflammatory polarization and cytotoxicity. The authors’ data show that adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated expression of these factors increases total CD45+ immune infiltration, CD8+ T cell abundance, IFN-γ-producing CD8+ cells, and tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells, significantly outperforming single-gene delivery. This is a meaningful design principle: in solid tumors, durable immune control usually requires simultaneous enhancement of trafficking, activation, and persistence, not isolated stimulation of one axis.2

This study also notes an enrichment of tissue-resident memory T cells within the remodeled TME, which is particularly timely. Recent work in Immunity and related reviews has reinforced the idea that tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM)-like populations are enriched in responsive tumors and may help sustain local immune surveillance and checkpoint responsiveness. In the APCM framework, the phenotype of increased TRM-like CD8+ cells is not merely incidental; it may represent a part of the mechanism underlying durable tumor control. A commentary on this paper should therefore highlight that the study is not only about “more T cells” but about engineering a TME that can retain and maintain the right T cells in the right place.3

The translational utility of this study is further strengthened by its synergy with adoptive cell therapy (ACT). Solid tumors remain difficult for CAR-T or TCR-T therapy because trafficking, infiltration, and local persistence are all constrained by the suppressive TME. By preconditioning tumors with AAV-4II, the authors show improved synergy with CAR-T and TCR-T therapies in vivo, supporting a modular therapeutic concept in which immune gene delivery is used as a local “soil preparation” strategy before adoptive cell infusion. This resonates with broader literature on solid tumor CAR-T limitations and the growing consensus that success will require not only better receptors but better tumor ecosystems.4

The use of AAV as the delivery backbone is both pragmatic and provocative. Pragmatic, because AAV is clinically familiar and can support local intratumoral gene delivery; provocative, because the study implicitly reframes gene therapy from a cell-intrinsic repair tool into a spatial immunology platform. The paper also shows that the concept is not dependent on CRISPRa alone: an open reading frame (ORF)-based delivery mode reproduced anti-tumor activity, suggesting that the real advance is the discovery of the combination itself, not the exclusive use of a single gene-expression technology. That is an important translational point because manufacturability and regulatory simplicity will matter as much as biological potency when moving toward the clinic.1

Despite these major advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, intratumoral delivery is inherently constrained by lesion accessibility and may therefore be more suitable for localized tumors than disseminated metastatic disease. Reaching visceral metastases will likely require improved systemic tropism or alternative delivery platforms. Although systemic AAV serotypes continue to evolve, their clinical application remains challenged by tissue selectivity, pre-existing immunity, and insufficient tumor-specific accumulation. Alternative vectors, such as oncolytic viruses, may improve intratumoral amplification and spatial selectivity but introduce additional concerns related to antiviral immunity, vector control, and dosing complexity. Second, different immune-cold tumor architectures may respond differently to the 4II strategy. APCM-guided immune activation may effectively initiate anti-tumor immunity in immune-desert tumors with poor T cell priming. However, in immune-excluded tumors characterized by dense stromal barriers and vascular dysfunction, cytokine signaling alone may be insufficient to overcome physical constraints on immune infiltration. In such cases, combination strategies involving stromal-targeting or anti-TGF-β therapies may be required. Third, cytokines such as IL-12 and IFN-γ are highly potent but potentially toxic molecules. Systemic leakage could induce severe inflammatory toxicity, hepatotoxicity, or broader cytokine-associated adverse effects if expression is not tightly controlled. Thus, future translational efforts will likely require spatially restricted or inducible expression systems to balance efficacy and safety. Another important conceptual point concerns the distinction between the discovery and therapeutic platforms. CRISPRa primarily functions as a discovery engine for identifying synergistic immune programs in vivo, whereas ORF-based expression systems represent the clinically actionable implementation layer. This distinction clarifies that the principal innovation of the study lies not in permanent genome editing itself but in the identification of biologically effective combinatorial payloads that can subsequently be delivered through more translationally feasible systems.5

The broader significance of this paper lies in its methodological message. Instead of asking which single immune factor is superior, the authors ask which combinations are naturally synergistic inside the TME when tested in situ. That question is likely to define the next phase of immunoengineering. Similar logic is already emerging in neighboring fields: personalized CRISPR knockin strategies have begun to optimize tumor-local cytokine delivery, while genome-wide CRISPR screens in CAR-T cells are identifying intracellular determinants of fitness and exhaustion resistance. Together, these studies suggest that the future of solid tumor immunotherapy will be neither purely cell based nor purely gene based but combinatorial, spatially informed, and mechanistically engineered.

In summary, Zhang and colleagues have done more than identify a useful cytokine cocktail. They have demonstrated a scalable route to discover immune gene therapy combinations by reading the TME as a screenable biological system. The resulting 4II strategy is attractive because it is rational, modular, and mechanistically grounded: it strengthens antigen presentation, supports costimulation, recruits and sustains effector T cells, and appears capable of improving ACT in otherwise resistant solid tumors.
