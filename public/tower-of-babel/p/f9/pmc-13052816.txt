Retinal vascular phenotyping for early detection of coronary artery disease: quantitative assessment and diagnostic modelling
Authors: Zhenyan Wu, Xue Jiang, Yu Xin, Jian Liu, Saiguang Ling, Caixia Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the association between quantitative retinal vascular parameters and coronary artery disease (CAD) and to evaluate the efficacy of a retinal phenotype-based diagnostic model as a non-invasive tool for early CAD screening.DesignA retrospective cross-sectional study.SettingA single-centre study conducted at the Cardiovascular Center of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, China, between January and October 2024.Participants417 patients with suspected angina undergoing their first coronary angiography (CAG) were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were age >18 years and high-quality fundus photography within 24 hours pre-CAG. Major exclusions were prior coronary interventions, severe systemic/valvular heart diseases and ocular conditions impairing retinal vascular visualisation.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the association between quantitative retinal vascular parameters and the presence of CAD (defined as ≥50% stenosis). Secondary outcomes included the diagnostic performance area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of three predictive models: one based on quantitative retinal vascular parameters alone, one based on traditional risk factors and a combined model integrating both retinal and clinical variables.ResultsThis study enrolled 417 patients undergoing initial CAG. Compared with non-CAD controls (n=190), patients with CAD (n=227) had higher prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidaemia and diabetes, along with elevated levels of fasting blood glucose, lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), triglyceride (TG) and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) (all p<0.05). Quantitative fundus analysis revealed that multiple retinal vascular parameters were independently associated with CAD after multivariable adjustment, including fractal dimension (FD), vessel density (VD) and specific zonal measures of vessel diameter and tortuosity (all p<0.05). Multivariable logistic regression incorporating both fundus and clinical variables identified the following independent predictors of CAD: a decrease in FD (OR=0.26, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.41, p<0.01), reduced optic disc long-to-short axis ratio (OR=0.04, 95% CI 0.004 to 0.46, p=0.01) and optic disc-to-macula distance (OR=0.91, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.97, p<0.01), male sex, dyslipidaemia and elevated levels of Lp(a), TG, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and HbA1c (all p<0.05). The final diagnostic model achieved an AUROC of 0.802 (95% CI 0.76 to 0.845), with a sensitivity of 0.797 and a specificity of 0.679 at the optimal cut-off. Internal validation via bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) confirmed the robustness of the identified predictors.ConclusionOur findings, derived from an artificial intelligence-based fully automated quantitative retinal vascular parameters measurement method, revealed that multiple quantitative fundus parameters—including FD, VD and other morphological parameters were significantly associated with CAD risk. The CAD diagnostic model we developed demonstrates strong performance and high interpretability, making it suitable for early CAD screening and diagnosis.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading and growing cause of global morbidity and mortality, imposing a significant burden on both public health and the economy. According to statistics, the number of people suffering from CVD surged from 271 million in 1990 to 523 million in 2019, while annual CVD-related deaths increased from 12.1 million to 18.6 million during the same period. This rapid escalation is particularly prominent in countries undergoing rapid urbanisation and population ageing, such as China.1 Currently, invasive coronary angiography (ICA) remains the gold standard for diagnosing coronary artery disease (CAD). While ICA exhibits high specificity and sensitivity in confirming CAD and assessing its severity, its technical complexity, procedural intricacy and invasive nature make it unsuitable as a screening tool for CAD.2 Against this backdrop, there is an urgent need for a non-invasive and cost-effective diagnostic screening tool to facilitate early detection and risk stratification of CAD.

Recent advances in retinal imaging technology have underscored the potential of retinal vasculature as a window into systemic vascular health, offering a unique opportunity to explore its correlation with CAD. The retinal microvasculature is the only vascular tissue in the human body that can be directly visualised in vivo, and it shares key anatomical and physiological similarities with coronary arteries.3 Both vascular beds are susceptible to atherosclerotic pathological processes, such as endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, under the influence of shared genetic phenotypes and environmental factors, thus providing a basis for investigating the comorbidity of retinal and CADs. These shared characteristics position retinal microvascular changes as ideal surrogate biomarkers for CAD. Numerous studies have already demonstrated associations between retinal microvascular abnormalities and various cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes4 and hypertension.5 Furthermore, multiple high-quality studies have elucidated a significant correlation between retinal vascular alterations and CAD. For instance, Wang et al6 were among the first to explore the relationship between retinal artery/vein diameters and CAD prognosis, finding that narrower retinal arteries and wider retinal veins significantly increased the risk of CAD-related mortality. McGeechan et al7 later confirmed in a systematic review and meta-analysis that retinal venous widening and arterial narrowing were associated with increased CAD risk in women. More recently, Wang et al8 used optical coherence tomography (OCT) angiography to compare retinal and choroidal vascular changes between patients with CAD and healthy controls, observing that patients with CAD exhibited significantly reduced retinal thickness and vessel density (VD) in multiple regions. Additionally, decreased retinal VD, reduced blood flow and diminished choroidal vascularity were closely associated with stenosis in major and branch coronary arteries. These findings collectively highlight the potential of quantitative retinal biomarkers as non-invasive diagnostic and prognostic tools for CAD.

The recent rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly influenced the development of scientific research and clinical medicine. AI’s ability to learn from, compute and analyse large-scale medical datasets has given rise to numerous intelligent medical research applications. As a key branch of this field, intelligent medical image processing not only enables the extraction of potential imaging biomarkers from retinal vascular images but also facilitates the construction of AI-based predictive models for CAD. These models can predict both traditional CAD risk factors and CAD itself.9 For instance, Zhang et al10 developed a neural network model based on 1222 colour fundus photographs (CFP), which demonstrated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of 0.766, 0.880 and 0.703 for predicting hypertension, hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia, respectively. Similarly, Rim et al3 established a deep learning-based coronary artery calcium scoring system for predicting cardiovascular events, achieving an AUC of 0.74 (95% CI 0.73 to 0.75). This system effectively stratified risk by tertiles, showing good performance in predicting 5-year cardiovascular events. However, deep learning is constrained by its ‘black box’ nature; its processing logic is difficult to interpret, and the key mechanisms by which models use features for decision-making remain unclear, thus failing to fundamentally identify definitive influencing factors for CAD diagnosis.

In summary, given the epidemiological characteristics of CAD, there is an urgent need to develop an early, rapid, non-invasive, efficient and clinically practical diagnostic model. CFP offers a non-invasive, readily accessible and cost-effective modality. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated a robust association between quantitative retinal microvascular features and CAD, thereby prompting increasing interest in fundus-based screening strategies. Nevertheless, existing CAD diagnostic models derived from fundus photographs have predominantly employed deep-learning frameworks. Although these algorithms achieve commendable discriminative performance, their ‘black-box’ nature precludes physiologically interpretable explanations, markedly limiting external validation and clinical translation. To explore the potential of retinal microvascular characteristics as interpretable biomarkers for CAD and to develop a diagnostic model that balances high performance with clinical explainability, we leveraged the prospective cardiovascular database of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University. We enrolled consecutive patients undergoing first-time coronary angiography (CAG). Comprehensive clinical metadata and quantitative retinal vascular parameters extracted from high-resolution fundus images were subsequently analysed. In this study, we first delineated the specific retinal microvascular alterations associated with CAD and then developed and validated a novel, explainable diagnostic model for incident CAD.

Patients and methods

Study population

This study retrospectively analysed 492 patients who were admitted to the Cardiovascular Center of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, from January to October 2024 due to suspected angina symptoms and underwent CAG for the first time. The study cohort primarily comprised individuals of Han Chinese ethnicity, reflecting the local demographic. The remaining participants belonged to various other ethnic groups within China. Due to the highly heterogeneous and fragmented distribution of these minority groups within our sample, further subdivision was not statistically meaningful for the primary aims of this study. The study population was divided into two groups based on the results of coronary angiography during hospitalisation: the CAD group and the non-CAD group. CAD was defined as the presence of at least one vessel (≥2 mm) with a stenosis degree of ≥50% on CAG during hospitalisation. The non-CAD group included patients with a maximum stenosis degree of <50% on CAG during hospitalisation. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age above 18 years; (2) undergoing CAG during hospitalisation; (3) undergoing fundus photography (CFP) centred on the macula in both eyes within 24 hours before CAG; and (4) voluntary provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with a history of severe valvular heart disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy or congenital heart disease; (2) patients with acute or chronic infectious diseases, autoimmune diseases or chronic respiratory diseases; (3) patients with a history of chronic renal failure, hepatitis, cancer or steroid treatment; (4) patients with ophthalmic diseases affecting the retinal vessels and optic disc structure, such as retinal artery or vein occlusion, retinal arteriolar macroaneurysm, glaucoma or congenital optic disc anomalies; (5) patients with severe opacification of the refractive media affecting the quality of fundus photography; (6) patients with incomplete clinical data; (7) patients who had undergone ophthalmic surgery within the past 6 months; and (8) patients who had previously undergone CAG, percutaneous transluminal coronary balloon angioplasty, coronary stent implantation, coronary drug-eluting balloon angioplasty or coronary artery bypass grafting. The screening flowchart is shown in figure 1. Prior to the main study, a pilot study was conducted with 61 participants to estimate the effect size for sample size calculation. The results indicated a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d=0.641) in a key retinal vascular parameter between the two groups. An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power V.3.1.9.7. For an independent two-sample t-test with an effect size of 0.641, a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two tailed), and a desired statistical power of 0.95, the analysis determined that a total sample size of 146 would be required. (The pilot study referenced here has not been previously published.) Our final study enrolled 417 participants, which substantially exceeded this requirement and ensured adequate power to detect effects of clinical significance.

Collection of baseline data

The baseline data of the patients were collected, including personal basic information (sex, age), medical history and personal history (history of hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol consumption, etc). The fasting blood glucose (FBG) and lipid results within 24 hours of admission were collected, including FBG, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), triglyceride (TG), Total Cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c). The results of CAG were collected, with the Gensini score used to indicate the severity of CAD. The Gensini score is an index for measuring the severity of CAD.11 The coronary artery tree is divided into 16 segments, and each segment is scored based on its functional importance (the left main trunk is scored as 5, and the most distal segment is scored as 0.5). Each degree of stenosis (0%, 1–49%, 50–74%, 75–99% or 100%) is assigned a score (0, 1, 2, 3, 4) for the percentage reduction in lumen diameter, and the weighted calculation is performed.12 For all included participants, data for these key analytical fields were complete, as they were mandatory for study inclusion.

Fundus photography and quantitative analysis of retinal vascular morphology

In this study, 45° CFP was performed using a non-mydriatic fundus camera (SYSEYE Kestrel 3100 m). The CFP was primarily captured in the right eye, centred on the macula. If the right eye was uncooperative or the CFP results were suboptimal, the left eye CFP results were used as a substitute. A single-field imaging method was employed, with the midpoint of the line connecting the macula and optic disc as the centre of the imaging field, covering at least a 60° retinal area. Patients did not require mydriasis, and the results were immediately available after imaging. On completion of CFP acquisition, all images were systematically screened by two senior ophthalmologists specialising in fundus diseases to identify any ocular pathologies listed in the exclusion criteria. One ophthalmologist performed the initial assessment, and the other conducted a secondary review to verify adherence to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. To assess potential bias introduced by selecting one eye per participant, we compared the proportion of right eyes between the CAD and non-CAD groups using a χ2 test. No significant difference was found (CAD group: 84.58% right eyes; non-CAD group: 82.63% right eyes; p=0.59), indicating balanced laterality across groups, consistent with methodologies employed in prior retinal studies.1315 The CFP images were analysed using the EVisionAI fundus image analysis system (EVision Technology (Beijing), Beijing, China) to quantify the morphological features of retinal vessels.16 This system is based on a human vision simulation mechanism and integrates advanced AI image processing techniques such as computer vision and deep learning. For the pre-processing of colour CFP images, operations such as region of interest extraction, noise reduction, normalisation and enhancement were performed to remove non-fundus structures, minimise interimage variability and enhance the clarity of fundus feature edges.17 Subsequently, a deep target detection network was combined with computer vision edge detection algorithms to achieve precise segmentation of the optic disc and cup.18 For the segmentation of peripapillary atrophy (PPA) and vessel identification, the software used a deep learning segmentation network model, ResNet 101-UNet, which segmented the images based on the colour, brightness, texture, interconnections and topological relationships of retinal vessels to distinguish between arterial and venous vessels.19 Based on the segmentation results, morphological parameters including fractal dimension (FD), VD, mean branch angle (MBA), mean blood vessel diameter (MBVD), mean arterial diameter (MAD), mean venous diameter (MVD) and arteriovenous ratio (AVR) were obtained, providing a comprehensive digital description of the CFP and its structures. The accuracy of feature recognition in the quantitative analysis of the fundus was verified by measuring the segmentation and extraction precision of the optic disc, optic cup, PPA and vessels. This system aims to enhance our understanding of fundus abnormalities through AI-based image processing and to provide intelligent assessment and detection of related fundus diseases. Additionally, it allows for objective and meticulous monitoring of fundus structural changes or disease progression. The retinal vascular quantification was divided into the following regions: (1) 0.5–1.0 papillary diameter (PD) from the optic disc centre, 1.0–1.5 PD, 1.5–2.0 PD and 2.0–2.5 PD; (2) within the superior, temporal, inferior and nasal quadrants of the optic disc; (3) within 3 and 5 mm of the macular fovea. The model accuracy and specificity were both over 0.9.16 This AI-based framework leveraged an automated pipeline to facilitate objective monitoring and intelligent evaluation of abnormalities. Statistical methods

Data organisation and statistical analysis were conducted using SPSS V.26.0. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean±SD, and comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were expressed as median (IQR) (M(P25, P75)), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups. Categorical data were presented as percentages (%) and compared using the χ2 test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed on the fundus data to explore the association between fundus variables and CAD after adjusting for multiple factors. Three models were used for adjustment: model 1 adjusted for age and sex; model 2 further adjusted for hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes and smoking history; model 3 additionally adjusted for FBG, Lp(a), TG, LDL-c, HDL-c and HbA1c. ORs and their 95% CIs were calculated. Indicators with significant associations (p<0.05) in model 3 were further analysed in subgroup analyses based on age (≥65 years), sex and the presence of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia or smoking history to explore the associations with CAD in different subgroups. Four different models were established in the subgroup analyses: model 1 with no adjustment; model 2 adjusted for age and sex; model 3 further adjusted for hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes and smoking history; model 4 with additional adjustment for FBG, Lp(a), TG, LDL-c, HDL-c and HbA1c. Finally, three diagnostic models were established: one based on fundus data alone, one based on traditional risk factors alone and one combining fundus data and traditional risk factors. The diagnostic performance of these models for CAD was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. To assess the stability and internal validity of the optimal diagnostic model, particularly given the sample size constraints, we employed the Bootstrap resampling technique. A total of 1000 bootstrap samples were generated with replacement from the original dataset. We calculated the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% CI for the regression coefficients of the predictors. A predictor was considered statistically robust if its BCa 95% CI did not cross zero. This procedure was performed to ensure that the selected features represented true associations rather than chance patterns inherent in the training data. A two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with a significance level of α=0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. As this study was a retrospective analysis of existing clinical and imaging data, patient involvement in the study design was not feasible.

Results

General data

Among the 492 participants, after excluding those with poor-quality images, incomplete clinical data, CFP not centred on the macula and those who had undergone ophthalmic surgery within the past 6 months, a total of 417 patients’ CFPs were included in this study. Of these, 349 were right-eye CFP and 78 were left-eye CFP. After CAG, 227 patients were diagnosed with CAD, with a mean age of 63.39 years and 114 (50.22%) were male; 190 patients were diagnosed with non-CAD, with a mean age of 60.61 years and 83 (43.68%) were male. The CAD group had significantly higher mean age, history of hypertension, dyslipidaemia and diabetes compared with the non-CAD group, with statistically significant differences. The CAD group also had higher levels of FBG, Lp(a), TG and HbA1c and lower levels of HDL-c compared with the non-CAD group, all of which were statistically significant (p<0.05). For the quantitative analysis of retinal vascular morphology, the CAD group had significantly higher values for MBVD and all its subgroup diameters, MVD and its subgroup diameters (1.5–2.0 PD, 2.0–2.5 PD), and the minimum distance between the optic disc and cup compared with the non-CAD group. In contrast, the CAD group had significantly lower values for VD, FD, AVR, optic disc axis ratio, vertical cup diameter and horizontal cup-to-disc ratio compared with the non-CAD group, all with statistically significant differences (p<0.05). Other variables showed differences but were not statistically significant (p>0.05) (see table 1). Subgroup analysis of fundus quantitative data

After multivariate correction analysis of the fundus quantitative data, it was found that VD, FD, MBVD and its subgroup vessel diameter (0.5–1.0 PD, 1.0–1.5 PD), MVD and its subgroup (1.5–2.0 PD), AVT, AVC and its subgroup curvature (1.5–2.0 PD) were correlated with the presence of CAD. The above 10 fundus retinal vascular variables were stratified by age, sex and medical history to explore the correlation between different subgroups and the risk of CAD onset. A total of four different models were established. Model 1 was unadjusted, model 2 was adjusted for age and sex, model 3 was further adjusted for hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes and smoking history and model 4 was further adjusted for FBG, Lp(a), TG, LDL-c, HDL-c and HbA1c.

After multistep adjustment in the fully adjusted model (model 4), we assessed the associations between various retinal vascular parameters and CAD across key clinical subgroups (sex, age, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, smoking and alcohol consumption). The findings are comprehensively detailed in table 3 with a summary of the principal patterns presented below. FD and VD demonstrated statistically significant associations with CAD (p<0.05) across all examined subgroups. However, no significant interaction effects were observed between these variables and the subgroups, indicating that the strength of these associations was consistent regardless of the patients’ clinical characteristics. In contrast, other parameters, including MBVD, MBVD in specific peripapillary zones (0.5–1.0 PD and 1.0–1.5 PD), AVT, AVC, AVC (1.5–2.0 PD), MVD and MVD (1.5–2.0 PD), exhibited significant associations only within specific subgroups. Diagnostic performance and evaluation of CAD models

Based on the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was calculated for each model. Model 1 was based on all fundus results, model 2 was based on significant indicators from general information and traditional risk factors and model 3 was a combination of model 1 and model 2. The ROC curves showed that the AUROC for model 1 was 0.694 (95% CI 0.644 to 0.744, p<0.01), for model 2 was 0.748 (95% CI 0.701 to 0.795, p<0.01) and for model 3 was 0.802 (95% CI 0.76 to 0.845, p<0.01). The diagnostic performance of each model for CAD was as follows: for model 1, the cut-off value was 0.532, with a sensitivity of 0.612, specificity of 0.642 and Youden’s index of 0.254. For model 2, the cut-off value was 0.50, with a sensitivity of 0.74, specificity of 0.668 and Youden’s index of 0.408. For model 3, the cut-off value was 0.465, with a sensitivity of 0.797, specificity of 0.679 and Youden’s index of 0.476. Among the three models, model 3 demonstrated the best performance. Internal validation using bootstrap resampling (n=1000) confirmed the robustness of the established diagnostic model (table 6). The majority of the included predictors, specifically sex, dyslipidaemia, TG, HbA1c, FD and length axis ratio of optic disc, demonstrated high stability, with their BCa 95% CIs strictly excluding zero. Although LDL-c showed a borderline confidence interval (BCa 95% CI −0.012 to 0.651) in the internal validation, it was retained in the final equation due to its well-established pathophysiological significance in CAD. Overall, the bootstrap analysis indicated that the model’s predictive performance is stable and not driven by overfitting.Therefore, the CAD diagnostic model established in this study is as follows:

Discussion

In this study, we analysed retinal microvascular parameters from 417 patients undergoing their first CAG using CFP. After extensive multivariate adjustments, we found that FD, optic disc axis ratio and the distance from the optic disc to the macula were significantly associated with the risk of CAD. These results highlight the potential of retinal microvascular parameters as predictive markers for CAD and suggest the feasibility of developing a diagnostic model based on retinal fundus images. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between CAD and retinal vascular geometric parameters in a population undergoing first-time CAG and to establish a corresponding diagnostic model.

Previous research has established connections between retinal microvascular characteristics, retinal diseases and CVDs,68 with the anatomical and functional similarities between cardiac and ocular vasculature being central to this relationship.21 The link between retinal microvascular features and CVD is emerging as a new research focus. While traditional methods required mydriasis for detailed retinal imaging, recent advancements have shown that non-mydriatic fundus cameras perform equivalently to mydriatic ones.22 23 Although existing software for retinal vascular analysis enables objective and quantitative measurements, it often requires manual input and adjustment, leading to longer processing times and potential errors. In contrast, our study employed a fully automated approach based on retinal vascular segmentation, leveraging deep learning and computer vision technologies to extract detailed vascular information from CFP, including MBA, FD and MBVD, thereby providing precise and efficient measurements.19

In this study, we made the following observations:

First, FD as a significant marker: FD was significantly lower in the CAD group compared with the non-CAD group. This association persisted across various populations after single-variable, multivariate logistic regression analyses and subgroup analyses, confirming FD as a significant risk factor for CAD. The FD of the vessels refers to the complexity of retinal vascular branching within a certain region, reflecting the effectiveness of complex structures occupying space and serving as a measure of vascular irregularity.24 Liew et al25 investigated the relationship between retinal vascular FD and CAD mortality and suggested that this association is similar to the relationship between retinal vascular changes and CAD.6 After adjusting for age, blood pressure and other risk factors, subjects with suboptimal FD (lowest and highest quartiles) had a 50% higher 14-year CAD mortality rate compared with those with optimal FD (middle quartiles). In subjects under 70 years of age, suboptimal FD was associated with nearly double the CAD mortality rate (HR=1.89; 95% CI 1.25 to 2.84). FD was considered a novel method for quantifying microvascular branching and could independently predict 14-year CAD mortality. However, in subjects over 70 years of age, the association between suboptimal FD and CAD mortality was weaker, likely due to the greater contribution of macrovascular disease.25 Wang et al15 used semiautomated software to obtain a series of quantitative retinal vascular geometric measurements from retinal photographs and found that lower FD of retinal vessels was associated with greater severity and extent of CAD. Zekavat et al26 found that, after adjusting for age, sex, smoking status and race, lower FD (defined as more than 2 SD below the mean) was associated with a higher risk of mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes (HR=1.83, 95% CI 1.46 to 2.3, p<0.01) or hypertension (HR=1.29, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.62, p=0.03). They subsequently expanded their research, using deep learning algorithm results to guide phenome-wide association studies, genome-wide association studies and Mendelian randomisation analyses, and identified several new potential therapeutic targets. Fu et al2 followed up 57 947 patients from the UK Biobank database for a median of 11.0 years and found that, in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, FD was closely related to the risk of CAD (HR=0.80, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.98, p=0.033). In our study, using quantitative retinal analysis results, FD remained in the final model after adjusting for traditional CAD risk factors and other meaningful variables, indicating its significant influence on CAD risk. Moreover, the results of subgroup analyses also showed that FD could demonstrate its association with CAD in a broader CAD population, and more studies will be conducted in different populations in the future.

We found that the axis ratio of the optic disc and the distance from the optic disc to the macula were associated with the presence of CAD. We speculate that this association may be related to the following aspects: First, Chen et al27 showed that the tilt and rotation of the optic disc are related to the distribution of superficial VD and radial peripapillary capillary VD. Previous studies have shown that retinal VD in patients with CAD is significantly lower than that in healthy controls (p<0.05),8 which is to some extent associated with the risk of CAD. Second, previous studies have shown that variables related to changes in the optic disc axis, such as optic disc tilt28 and axial elongation29 of the eye, are associated with the severity of myopia. The occurrence of myopia is related to age and BMI, with higher BMI corresponding to an increased risk of myopia (OR=1.26, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.51),29 which in turn causes changes in the optic disc axis. This indirectly relates the optic disc axis ratio to the risk of CAD. Dong et al30 found in their study using OCT/OCT angiography that retinal thinning and reduced VD are associated with the presence of CAD. The alterations in retinal VD observed in patients with CAD may be associated with the vascular distribution within the optic disc to the macula region. Variations in the distance from the optic disc to the macula could influence VD in this area, subsequently affecting blood perfusion and tissue nutrition in the macula. This, in turn, may lead to changes in retinal and choroidal thickness, which are linked to the presence of CAD. Summarising previous studies, we hypothesise that changes in the optic disc axis and the distance from the optic disc to the macula may impact retinal vascular haemodynamics. This impact is related to alterations in the length, calibre and curvature of retinal vessels. When blood flow resistance increases, it affects blood perfusion in the retina and choroid. These haemodynamic changes may be related to the pathogenesis of CAD. However, it should be noted that both the optic disc axis ratio and the distance from the optic disc to the macula should be calculated and assessed within a reasonable range. Due to the limited supporting literature, a direct relationship between these two parameters and CAD cannot be clearly established from previous studies. Therefore, more related studies are needed in the future to confirm their association with the pathogenesis of CAD.

Second, patients with CAD had significantly lower VD compared with those without CAD. Additionally, MBVD and MVD were significantly higher in patients with CAD than in patients with non-CAD. After multivariate adjustment, these variables remained associated with the presence of CAD, which is consistent with previous studies. Zekavat et al26 found that, after adjusting for age, sex, smoking status and race, a 2 SD decrease in VD was associated with an increased risk of unexpected death (HR=1.83, 95% CI 1.39 to 2.42, p<0.01). Hannappe et al31 reported that patients hospitalised for acute coronary syndrome had significantly lower retinal VD compared with those without cardiovascular risk (p=0.004). Wang et al8 found that, in the outer retina and choriocapillaris layer, retinal VD was significantly lower in patients with CAD compared with healthy controls (p<0.05). They also demonstrated that stenosis in the left main coronary artery, proximal left anterior descending artery, proximal left circumflex artery and proximal right coronary artery was significantly associated with reduced retinal and choriocapillaris VD. These studies confirm a clear association between VD and the risk of CAD, and our findings are consistent with previous results. Fu et al2 showed that, in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, larger venous diameter was associated with an increased risk of CAD (HR=0.78, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.98, p=0.034). Wang et al6 reported that increased retinal venous diameter (RR: 1.24, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.52, p<0.05) significantly increased the risk of CAD mortality, particularly in individuals aged 43–69 years (RR: 1.41, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.89). McGeechan et al7 conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis and found that, in women, retinal venous dilation (per 20 µm increase) was independently associated with an increased risk of CAD, with an adjusted risk ratio of 1.16 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.26). In men, however, changes in retinal vessel calibre were not significantly associated with CAD risk, with a risk ratio for venous dilation of only 1.02 (95% CI 0.94 to 1.10). In our subgroup analysis, MVD was weakly associated with women after adjusting for age (OR=1.03, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.05, p=0.036), but this association disappeared after multivariate adjustment. However, MVD between 1.5–2.0 PD was weakly associated with women after adjusting for age and traditional risk factors such as medical history (OR=1.03, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.06, p=0.034). This suggests that further stratification is needed in future studies to more clearly assess the association between fundus quantitative data and CAD risk.

It should be noted that, in our multivariate analysis, MBVD and subgroups (0.5–1.0 PD, 1.0–1.5 PD) were associated with the presence of CAD, but no studies have yet reported a correlation between mean vessel diameter and CAD presence. We speculate that this association may be related to the significant increase in venous diameter, but there is currently no direct evidence to support this link, and further studies are needed to confirm it. Additionally, in our study, we did not find a statistically significant association between MAD, AVT and AVC, which have been reported in previous studies. This may be due to several reasons: First, our study population consisted of patients undergoing CAG for the first time, which differs from the populations included in previous studies32 (eg, patients admitted for angina symptoms vs the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study). Second, compared with previous studies, our study population may have differences in race33 (Asian participants vs black and white participants) and age32 (mean age 63.39 vs 59), which could affect the sensitivity of the above indicators. Admittedly, we did find that, in multivariate analysis using individual fundus indicators, AVT (OR=0.859, 95% CI 0.746 to 0.99), AVC (OR=0.837, 95% CI 0.739 to 0.947) and AVC between 1.5 and 2.0 PD (OR=0.902, 95% CI 0.831 to 0.98, p=0.014) were significantly associated with CAD presence after adjustment, which is not contradictory to previous findings. Wang et al15 found that retinal vascular tortuosity was associated with the severity of CAD. Veluchamy et al34 conducted a meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies of quantitative retinal vascular traits using semiautomated image analysis of digital retinal photographs from two cohorts and identified a novel genome-wide significant locus for retinal venular tortuosity and retinal arteriolar tortuosity, confirming that genetic determinants of retinal vascular tortuosity are also associated with multiple CVDs. Although no studies have yet explicitly described a direct link between arterial tortuosity, venous tortuosity and overall vascular tortuosity with CAD, we can speculate that vascular tortuosity may be a potential predictor of CVD. Additionally, in the subgroup analyses, variables such as FD and VD exhibited significant statistical differences across multiple subgroups, including sex, age and medical history (p<0.05). However, the interactions between these variables were not statistically significant (p>0.05). These findings indicate that the clinical associations of FD and VD are widely consistent, and their predictive values are not significantly influenced by sex, age or common cardiovascular risk factors. It should be noted that the lack of statistical significance in the interactions within the subgroup analyses may be related to the relatively small differences in effect sizes between groups. For example, in the case of FD, the OR values for males and females are close (0.33 vs 0.38). This only suggests that the trend of the effect of FD is consistent across subgroups and does not imply that there is no difference between them in the subgroup analyses. Our study identifies and supports the potential application value of these variables in a broad patient population. However, further validation of their predictive stability is needed in larger sample sizes or prospective studies.

In contrast to established models such as the Framingham Risk Score, which focus on long-term risk prediction,35 36 the present study was designed to develop a diagnostic tool for the contemporaneous identification of CAD. Recent research has increasingly explored the direct application of non-invasive retinal imaging for CAD diagnosis. Early work, such as that by McGeechan et al,32 demonstrated that integrating retinal vascular features with clinical variables could enhance model performance. Subsequently, the introduction of deep learning has further unlocked the potential of fundus images, with models like the deep-learning retinal coronary artery calcification (CAC) score (RetiCAC) model by Rim et al3 and the hybrid model by Lee et al37 demonstrating commendable discriminative power (AUROC 0.742–0.781). Notably, Ding et al38 achieved exceptional performance (AUC up to 0.885) in a Chinese population using a pure deep-learning model. However, the ‘black-box’ nature of such deep learning approaches, where model decisions are based on features that are not quantitatively explicit, limits clinical interpretability and, consequently, physician trust and adoption. Against this backdrop, the principal strength of our study lies in the development of a diagnostic model that successfully balances high performance with strong interpretability. Leveraging a logistic regression framework, we integrated readily obtainable clinical variables with automatically quantified retinal morphological phenotypes. Our final model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability in the validation set, achieving an AUROC of 0.802—a performance that surpasses models derived from coronary artery calcium scoring combined with traditional risk factors (AUROC=0.794)39 and those based on stress myocardial perfusion imaging (AUROC=0.795).40 This performance is notably superior to the logistic regression model by Zhang et al41 (AUROC=0.71), which integrated clinical features with retinal vascular parameters but was potentially limited by the use of a Gensini score≥4 as the diagnostic criterion for CAD, a threshold not universally recognised as the gold standard for defining the disease. Critically, our approach entirely circumvents the radiation exposure and risk of contrast-induced nephropathy associated with these established techniques, offering a superior safety profile. The clinical variables incorporated into our model, including sex, dyslipidaemia, TG, LDL-c and HbA1c, are well-established in their diagnostic significance for CAD, consistent with the known pathophysiology of coronary lesions. Furthermore, we incorporated Lp(a) as a key component, aligning our model with emerging evidence that positions Lp(a) as a novel therapeutic target in atherosclerotic CVD prevention. Crucially, unlike high-performing yet opaque deep learning models,37 38 our approach offers inherent interpretability. The model provides transparent, quantifiable insights into the contribution of each feature. For instance, it explicitly indicates that for every one-unit increase in LDL-c, the probability of CAD increases by 37.9% (OR=1.379, 95% CI 1.004 to 1.894). This clarity allows clinicians to trace the model’s decision-making logic, which aligns seamlessly with clinical reasoning and validates the risk factors commonly focused on in practice. Finally, the inclusion of quantitative fundus data, such as FD and optic disc morphology, served to enhance the model’s overall performance, thereby validating the findings of previous research while also introducing novel retinal biomarkers for CAD. This strategy of thoughtfully integrating retinal vasculature findings with clinical features represents a valuable balance between diagnostic innovation and clinical interpretability.

The participants in this study were recruited from a tertiary cardiovascular centre, representing a well-defined cohort of patients with suspected angina referred for diagnostic CAG. Based on the quantification of retinal morphological features and precise segmentation by our fundus image analysis system, we demonstrated that these retinal vascular parameters can serve as novel risk indicators for CAD within this clinically relevant population. Our diagnostic model, which integrates these findings, demonstrates good performance and represents a safe, non-invasive and cost-effective method to identify CAD. Therefore, early observation of the retinal microvasculature may help identify individuals at increased risk, providing a valuable opportunity for the early detection and timely management of CAD. While this enhances the immediate clinical relevance of our findings for similar specialist settings, their generalisability to community-based or asymptomatic populations warrants further validation in future multicentre studies.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, we employed computer-aided intelligent measurement of vascular characteristics to explore the relationship between retinal vascular features and CAD based on numerical indicators. Compared with previous computer-aided detection of retinal vessels, the most significant innovation of this study is the ability to automatically detect and segment retinal vessels, followed by the automatic calculation of vascular feature parameters. This fully automated process saves considerable time, reduces labour costs and avoids subjective errors introduced by manual or machine-assisted manual measurement. Second, the non-mydriatic fundus camera used in this study is highly efficient, time-saving and provides a better patient experience, thereby improving efficiency and reducing the risk of conflicts between medical staff and patients. Third, based on the general information of patients with CAD at their first visit and the quantification of retinal morphological features, this study explored risk indicators for CAD. By excluding patients previously diagnosed with CAD, myocardial infarction and those who had undergone coronary interventions, we minimised the impact of medications and therapeutic interventions on the morphology of the coronary arteries and retinal features. Finally, we performed detailed regional segmentation of the retinal vascular system, which is rarely achieved in previous related studies. By subdividing the retinal fundus areas to identify more accurate candidate biomarkers, we aimed to facilitate the early identification of CAD.

Admittedly, our study also has some limitations. First, the inclusion and exclusion criteria, specifically the requirement for CAG and macula-centred CFP, led to the exclusion of many ineligible patients, which may have caused selection bias. Second, this study used 45° photographs of the central fundus. These photographs cover a limited area, which may restrict the measurement of peripheral vessels. Moreover, while our automated quantitative phenotyping reduces subjective grading variability, it may not fully capture or classify all rare congenital or pathological vascular variants. This could lead to potential residual confounding and misattribution of observed changes to the target condition. In the zonal analysis, we only performed annular zones without further subdividing them into quadrants, which may have overlooked some meaningful results. Additionally, to minimise costs, the study only included blood glucose and lipid test results and did not incorporate results from complete blood counts, coagulation profiles or inflammatory cytokines, which may limit its application in diagnostic models. Finally, our study is a single-centre, cross-sectional, retrospective study with a relatively small sample size; future research should employ prospective, multicentre designs with even more granular retinal assessment to account for these vascular variants and further validate our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, we conducted a study examining the correlation between prevalent CAD in suspected populations and retinal vascular parameters, using a deep learning-based vessel segmentation model to achieve fully automated quantitative measurement from CFP. Based on these findings, we established a diagnostic model for diagnosed CAD. In this study, a reduction in the FD of retinal microvasculature was clearly associated with an increased risk of CAD in patients who had not previously undergone CAG. Additionally, we found that the axis ratio of the optic disc and the distance from the optic disc to the macula were associated with CAD risk. Future investigations should increasingly focus on leveraging models based on retinal microvascular parameters to explore the screening and early diagnosis of CAD.
