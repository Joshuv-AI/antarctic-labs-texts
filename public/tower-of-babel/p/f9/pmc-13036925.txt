The ‘amplifier’ of fertility risk perception in a social network environment: the chain-mediated effects of body image comparison and negative physical self
Authors: Xuemeng Zhang, Xiumei Xiao, Keer Wang, Yizhou Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
According to the World Population Prospects 2022, fertility rates are projected to continue declining in most regions globally. Research has shown that the use of social network sites (SNSs) has been linked to the perception of fertility risk and reduces fertility intentions. One of the most significant transformations women experience due to childbearing is a change in body shape, often resulting in increased body dissatisfaction. Consequently, this study aimed to explore the effect of SNS use on perceived fertility risk and to uncover the potential role of SNS body comparisons and negative physical self-perceptions in this context. Our findings suggest that the intensity of SNS use positively is positively associated with fertility risk perception, a relationship mediated by SNS body comparisons and negative physical self-perceptions (fatness/thinness/facial appearance). The results indicate that women of childbearing age, when exposed to a persistent online environment of sexually objectifying information, become more concerned about external evaluations of themselves. This concern leads them to make social comparisons, resulting in negative physical self-perceptions about fatness versus thinness or appearance, and stimulating perceptions of childbearing risk. Therefore, future efforts should focus on creating a healthier social network environment for women, promoting a positive body image and increasing their willingness to have children.

Introduction

Social Network Sites (SNS) have significantly altered individuals’ lifestyles and their interactions with other people. Users often spend considerable time on these platforms, and this engagement may substantially affect their physical and mental health [1, 2]. Prior research shows that SNS use is associated with lower self-esteem [3], more depressive symptoms [4, 5], and body image concerns such as body dissatisfaction and body shame [6–10]. Women in particular tend to internalize thin ideals [11, 12] and pregnancy can amplify these challenges [13]. Although the effects of SNSs on body image are well established, much less is known about their implications for fertility-related perceptions. Recent studies indicate that SNS use can shape fertility intentions [14–17].

The United Nations Population Division’s World Population Prospects 2022 predicts that fertility rates will peak in most regions during the latter half of the 21st century and then decline, leading to global population reduction (World Population Prospects - Population Division - United Nations https://population.un.org/wpp/). Emerging research indicates that SNSs amplify women’s awareness of fertility risks and shape fertility intentions [14–18]. Fertility risk itself is a complex construct, defined by scholars like Zhu as encompassing health and economic risks [19], and by Beck as also including familial relationship and career risks [20]. Therefore, we define fertility risk as the range of potential challenges individuals face during childbearing, which includes health risks, career risks, familial relationship risks, and economic risks. Risk perception, defined as individuals’ attitudes, beliefs, judgments, and psychological responses to specific risks, is shaped by both psychological and sociocultural factors [21]. While prior studies have linked SNS use to women’s negative self-perceptions, the connections among SNS use, body image, and fertility risk perception remain underexplored.

The literature has primarily concentrated on outcomes like body dissatisfaction, eating behaviors, and mental health [7, 8, 11]. Our study advances this line of inquiry by extending the framework to a novel and body-salient outcome—fertility risk perception. Specifically, we examine the previously unexplored pathway from negative physical self to fertility risk perception, thereby linking social media psychology with fertility-related research, a connection that has received limited attention so far.

We propose this pathway is built upon established mechanisms. Body image, or an individual’s perceptions and thoughts about their body [22], is frequently influenced by social comparison processes [23, 24], which research confirms mediate the relationship between SNS use and negative body image [6, 25]. SNS platforms, saturated with idealized beauty standards [26], foster upward comparisons that are closely linked with body dissatisfaction [7, 8, 11, 27–30]. These pressures are particularly noteworthy in women of childbearing age. From a sociocultural perspective [31, 32], the physical changes of pregnancy and postpartum exacerbate body image concerns [33, 34]. These concerns often focus on body shape over function [35], can rise postpartum [36], are linked to prenatal anxiety and depression [13], and are prevalent given weight stigma [37]. SNSs reinforce these thin-ideals postpartum [38], which can increase image anxiety [39] even as body-positive content exists [40]. This process is often mediated by appearance-based social comparisons [39, 41]. Therefore, this study examines the association between SNS usage intensity and women’s perceived fertility risk, focusing on the mediating role of negative physical self (NPS), which includes concerns about general appearance, thinness, and fatness [42].

In Beck’s risk society theory, late-modern social media renders risks increasingly manufactured and individualized [43]. In the fertility domain, social media makes pregnancy and postpartum costs highly visible—circulating idealized body norms and stories about recovery, career penalties, and relational strain. Under these appearance-centered norms, anticipated postpartum bodily change becomes a personal risk cue that requires self-management. Consequently, upward appearance comparisons heighten negative physical self (NPS), which channels attention to fertility-specific vulnerabilities (health, work, family), elevating perceived fertility risk even when objective hazards are limited. Specifically, digital media act as a powerful engine for this individualization through three specific processes. First, algorithmic personalization tailors content feeds to a user’s existing anxieties, creating an echo chamber (i.e., an environment where similar viewpoints are constantly reinforced) where stories of difficult postpartum recoveries and body image struggles appear not as diverse experiences, but as a probable personal future. Second, the quantification and comparison culture of SNS (e.g., ‘likes’, follower counts, ‘bounce-back’ progress photos) transforms the natural process of pregnancy and recovery into a high-stakes individual performance to be judged publicly. Third, the constant and curated visibility of postpartum bodies on these platforms transforms what was once a relatively private experience into a public spectacle. This elevates the physical body to the central stage of the postpartum narrative, often overshadowing other aspects of motherhood and intensifying the perceived risks associated with any physical changes. In this digitally mediated environment, societal pressures are no longer abstract; they are delivered as a personalized, daily regimen of self-monitoring and comparison, making fertility a matter of profound individual risk management.

Based on this framework, we hypothesize that women of childbearing age, after exposure to SNS content, engage in body comparisons with others, which may heighten body dissatisfaction and increase fertility risk perception, such as fears that body changes could affect social life, work, or health. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate this specific chain mediation, providing insight into women’s childbearing fears and offering effective strategies for addressing low fertility trends.

Methods

Participants

We enlisted 450 women of childbearing age to participate in an online survey disseminated via popular Chinese social media platforms such as Weibo, WeChat, and Xiaohongshu. The questionnaire was created and administered using the Wenjuanxing platform, and participants were recruited through publicly posted announcements on these platforms. This approach ensured that participants had actual experience using social media, rather than relying on self-reported data. Those who completed the survey received a small monetary compensation in the form of a 5 RMB digital red-envelope reward. During the data collection phase, 62 surveys were discarded. In the initial screening, 42 invalid responses were excluded based on the control question (‘From which direction does the sun rise?’). In the secondary screening, 20 surveys were removed due to participants providing completely contradictory answers to identical items, resulting in a final count of 378 valid responses. Minors younger than 18 years of age, illiterate subjects were not included in the study. The age distribution of the participants was as follows: 21 (5.6%) were under 20 years old, 237 (62.7%) were between 20 and 30, 100 (26.5%) were between 30 and 40, 16 (4.2%) were between 40 and 50, and 4 (1.1%) were over 50. Although these four participants slightly exceeded the standard upper limit for childbearing age (typically 49), they were retained in the final analysis due to their small number (1.1%) and the negligible impact on the overall model. In terms of educational attainment, 48 (12.7%) had a high school diploma or less, 100 (26.5%) had an associate degree, 219 (57.9%) had a bachelor’s degree, and 11 (2.9%) had a postgraduate degree. Furthermore, 204 (54%) of the participants had no childbirth experience, 108 (28.6%) had experienced childbirth once, and 66 (17.4%) had given birth two or more times. The study was approved by the Chongqing Normal University Human Ethics Committee (CNU-PSY-202407-013).

Measures

Social networking site usage intensity

We based the SNS Usage Intensity Scale on the Facebook Intensity of Use Scale developed by Ellison, Steinfield, and Lampe [44], with Facebook being the representative social networking site. Drawing from a revised version by Zhao [45], we used six items (e.g., “Using social networking sites is part of my daily life”) to measure the intensity of individuals’ use of these sites. Participants responded to these six items on a five-point scale, ranging from 1 (very inconsistent) to 5 (very consistent). Mean scores were calculated, with higher scores indicating a greater intensity of SNS usage. The Cronbach’s α for this study was 0.801, demonstrating a high level of internal consistency.

Negative physical self

The Negative Physical Self Scale (NPSS) [45] was used to evaluate an individual’s perception of their negative physical attributes and negative emotional experiences. We used three dimensions of the NPSS: appearance concern (sample item: “My appearance depresses me”), fatness concern (sample item: “I have tried a variety of ways to reduce my own weight”), and thinness concern (sample item: “I think I look skinny to others”). The three dimensions of the NPSS comprises 32 items, each rated on a five-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 4, indicating a spectrum from “totally inconsistent” to “totally consistent.” A higher score signifies a greater level of negative cognition about one’s body. The Cronbach’s α for this study was 0.976.

Social networking site body comparisons

We used the Body Comparison Scale on SNSs, developed by Fardouly and Vartanian [28], to measure participants’ propensity to compare their body image with others on these platforms. This scale comprises three items, each rated on a five-point scale, where 1 signifies “strongly disagree” and 5 denotes “strongly agree” (e.g., “When using social networking sites, I sometimes compare my body type to other Internet users”). Higher scores on this scale suggest a greater frequency of body comparisons with others on SNSs. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.865, indicating a high level of internal consistency.

Self-objectification

The degree of self-objectification was measured using the Self-Objectification Questionnaire [46]. Participants were instructed to rank 10 body-related words, five of which pertained to internal body characteristics, and five to external body characteristics. The self-objectification score was then calculated by subtracting the sum of the rankings for the internal characteristic words from the sum of the rankings for the external characteristic words. A higher score indicated a greater level of self-objectification. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the questionnaire was 0.731.

Fertility risk perception

We used the Fertility Risk Perception Scale [47] to measure participants’ perception of fertility risk (Table 1). Participants were asked to respond to six items on a five-point scale, with 1 indicating “very unlikely” and 5 indicating “very likely.” A higher score on this scale signified a greater perception of fertility risk. The Cronbach’s α for this scale was 0.881.

Procedure

The host university’s ethics committee granted approval and consent for the study. Recruitment details were disseminated through prominent Chinese social networking platforms, such as Weibo, WeChat, and Xiaohongshu. Participants, who remained anonymous, completed online questionnaires on these platforms. The questionnaires included demographic information, and information from the SNS usage intensity scale, NPSS scale, scale for body comparisons on SNSs, fertility risk perception scale, and self-objectification scale. Participants gave their consent for all data collected.

Testing for the mediation effects

To examine the effects of SNS usage intensity on perceived fertility risk and the mediating effects of SNS body comparison and NPS, we used Model 6 of the PROCESS macro (Hayes, 2013). Given that most research has identified body dissatisfaction as primarily concerned with appearance, fatness, and thinness [7, 8, 11], we analyzed only the three dimensions of NPS (fatness concern, thinness concern, facial appearance concern) as mediating variables in this study. In addition to self-objectification, covariates also included demographic variables such as age, education level, and childbirth experience. When controlling for these variables, SNS usage intensity was significantly associated with fertility risk perception, with β = 0.483, p < 0.001, R2 = 0.177, F(5,372) = 7.748, p < 0.001. Specifically, the inclusion of self-objectification and demographic variables as covariates did not alter the significance of the main mediation pathways, indicating that the proposed mechanism captures unique variance in fertility risk perception beyond the influence of these factors.

The path coefficient results, using SNS body comparison and fatness-NPS as the chain mediating variables, are shown in Fig. 1A. The regression equation between the intensity of SNS use and the perception of fertility risk was significant, with R2 = 0.318, F(7,370) = 24.605, p < 0.001. The mediation effect was tested using the bootstrap sampling method, which revealed a significant total indirect effect of 0.14, SE = 0.033, 95% CI = [0.078,0.208]. Specifically, there was a significant indirect path from the intensity of SNS use to the perception of fertility risk through SNS body comparison (indirect effect = 0.058, SE = 0.021, 95% CI = [0.021,0.104]), and another significant indirect path through fatness concern (indirect effect = 0.064, SE = 0.025, 95% CI = [0.015,0.116]). Additionally, the indirect path from the intensity of SNS use to the perception of fertility risk through both SNS body comparison and fatness concern was also significant (indirect effect = 0.019, SE = 0.008, 95% CI = [0.006,0.036]).

The path coefficient results, using SNS body comparison and thinness-NPS as the chain mediating variables, are shown in Fig. 1B. The regression model showed that the intensity of SNS use was significantly associated with the perception of fertility risk, with R2 = 0.295, F(7,370) = 22.167, p < 0.001. The results show a significant total indirect effect of 0.163, with SE = 0.032, 95% CI = [0.104,0.228]. Specifically, a significant indirect path was found from the intensity of SNS use to the perception of fertility risk through SNS body comparison (indirect effect = 0.059, SE = 0.022, 95% CI = [0.022,0.106]), and another significant indirect path through thinness concern (indirect effect = 0.086, SE = 0.025, 95% CI = [0.042,0.139]). Additionally, the indirect path from the intensity of SNS use to the perception of fertility risk through both SNS body comparison and thinness concern was also significant (indirect effect = 0.018, SE = 0.008, 95% CI = [0.006,0.035]). The standardized indirect effects are close to a medium magnitude according to Cohen’s benchmarks [48], suggesting that SNS use, body comparison, and negative physical self together explain a meaningful proportion of the variance in perception of fertility risk.

The path coefficient results, using SNS body comparison and facial appearance-NPS as chain mediating variables, are shown in Fig. 1C. The regression model showed that the intensity of SNS use was significantly associated with the perception of fertility risk, with R2 = 0.297, F(7,370) = 22.313, p < 0.001. The results show that the total indirect effect was 0.145 and significant (SE = 0.031, 95% CI = [0.088,0.210]). Specifically, there was a significant indirect association between SNS use intensity and the perception of fertility risk through SNS body comparison (indirect effect = 0.054, SE = 0.021, 95% CI = [0.017,0.101]), and another significant indirect path through Facial Appearance Concern (indirect effect = 0.068, SE = 0.023, 95% CI = [0.024,0.115]). Additionally, the indirect path from the intensity of SNS use to the perception of fertility risk through SNS body comparisons and thinness concern was also significant (indirect effect = 0.023, SE = 0.008, 95% CI = [0.010,0.042]). The standardized indirect effects are close to a medium magnitude according to Cohen’s benchmarks [48], indicating that the pathway involving SNS use, body comparisons, and negative physical self explains a meaningful proportion of variance in perception of fertility risk.

Testing for the alternative mediation effects

To explore the possibility of a reverse causal relationship between SNS use intensity and fertility risk perception, we conducted an additional exploratory analysis. We tested a reverse chain mediation model in which fertility risk perception was the independent variable (X), SNS body comparisons was the first mediator (M1), each of the three dimensions of negative physical self (NPS) was the second mediator (M2), and SNS usage intensity was the dependent variable (Y). The results showed a clear lack of support for this reverse model, particularly for the key hypothesized chain mediation pathway (X → M1 → M2 → Y). Specifically, when the ‘fatness’ dimension was M2, both the total indirect effect (β = 0.044, 95% CI [-0.003, 0.089]) and the specific chain mediation path (Ind3: β = -0.001, 95% CI [-0.005, 0.004]) were non-significant. Although the total indirect effects were significant when ‘thinness’ (β = 0.003, 95% CI [-0.002, 0.012]) and ‘appearance’ (β = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.005, 0.009]) dimensions served as M2, the crucial chain mediation path (Ind3) remained non-significant for both ‘thinness’ (β = 0.003, 95% CI [-0.002, 0.012]) and ‘appearance’ (β = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.005, 0.009]). Overall, given that the specific chain mediation pathway (X → M1 → M2 → Y) failed to reach statistical significance for any of the NPS dimensions, these findings suggest that the alternative model in which fertility risk perception precedes SNS use intensity is not supported by the data. This provides additional evidentiary support for the directionality of our proposed model.

Additional analysis of demographic variables

To explore the potential impact of demographic characteristics on the outcome variable, we examined differences in fertility risk perception based on childbirth experience and employment status.

First, the results indicated a significant difference based on childbirth experience, t (376) = 3.28, p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.34. Specifically, nulliparous women (M = 23.25, SD = 5.30) reported significantly higher levels of fertility risk perception compared to women who had given birth (M = 21.58, SD = 4.51).

Second, a significant difference was also found based on employment status, t (376) = 2.20, p = 0.028, Cohen’s d = 0.28. Students (M = 23.57, SD = 5.23) perceived significantly higher fertility risks compared to working women (M = 22.19, SD = 4.93).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to uncover how the intensity of SNS use relates to fertility risk perception, viewed from the perspective of negative perceptions of the physical self. By identifying the sequential mediating role of body image comparisons and NPS, this study extends well-established SNS–body image mechanisms into the domain of fertility psychology, providing a conceptual bridge between these fields and offering insights into why negative physical self may shape women’s reproductive decision-making. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first to demonstrate that negative physical self is not only linked to traditional outcomes such as body dissatisfaction and mental health concerns, but also associated with perceived fertility risk. The study also contributes to explaining the psychological mechanisms underlying women’s low fertility intentions and fear of childbirth in contemporary society.

Aligning with prior research, our findings confirm that intense SNS use is linked to higher NPS and body dissatisfaction [7, 10, 11, 39]. SNS acts as a modern cultural conduit for appearance pressures, merging peer and sociocultural factors [32]. On these platforms, peer interactions surrounding body ideals often reinforce thin-ideal internalization [49–51], a process explained by social comparison theory [26]. Indeed, studies, including our own, confirm that social comparison and the internalization of ideals mediate the association between SNS use and negative self-perceptions [7, 11]. These findings highlight the need for public health interventions to promote positive body messages within the SNS environment [52].

Our research further suggests that SNSs are strongly associated with the development of NPS, and more importantly, we discovered a correlation between the frequency of high-intensity SNS use and an increased perception of fertility risk. This is because SNSs have become a vital source of health information for women [12]. SNSs are filled with negative news, such as the financial burden of childbearing, the adverse effects of employment, the increased strain on health, and significant changes to body shape, among other things. Specifically, pregnant women’s use of SNS can result in a variety of mental health problems and unhealthy behaviors, including postpartum depression, restrictive eating habits, and body dissatisfaction [12, 36, 39, 53]. Childbirth brings about substantial physical changes in women, and pregnant women often struggle to accept their altered body image. The societal expectations to remain “slim” and “beautiful” also add pressure during pregnancy.

Our mediation model proposes a correlational pathway in which the intensity of SNS use is associated with higher levels of perceived fertility risk through SNS body image comparisons, as well as the fatness/thinness/facial appearance-NPS. While previous studies have found that SNS use can negatively affect the body image and emotions of pregnant and postpartum women [39–41], our results suggest that unhealthy messages from SNS that idealize women not only lead to negative self-perceptions, but also contribute to perceptions of fertility risk.

We adopt Embodied Cognition and the Affect Heuristic as interpretive lenses to understand the robust association between NPS and fertility risk perception. From an embodied cognition perspective, NPS functions not merely as an abstract self-evaluation but as a chronic ‘felt sense’ of bodily dissatisfaction [54]. Unlike general health risks, fertility risks are inherently physical and experienced through the body. Consequently, this abstract ‘risk’ is appraised through the lens of one’s pre-existing negative bodily experience. This embodied appraisal is likely translated into risk judgment via the Affect Heuristic [55]. Because the prospect of bodily change during pregnancy activates the potent negative affect (e.g., anxiety, dread) associated with NPS, this intense negative feeling serves as a rapid, intuitive cue. Individuals may unconsciously rely on this negative affect to conclude that fertility represents a high-magnitude and unmanageable personal threat, regardless of objective medical hazards.

This mechanism provides the deep, affective and embodied grounding for key precursors to reproductive decision-making, which can be further conceptualized within the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [56]. The TPB posits that behavioral intentions are shaped by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Our model explains how these components are constructed in the digital age. Specifically, a high NPS, as a chronic ‘felt sense’ of bodily dissatisfaction, represents a deeply rooted negative attitude toward one’s own body. Given that reproduction is a profoundly bodily experience, this prior negative evaluation provides a powerful psychological basis for the formation of a negative attitude toward childbirth. Simultaneously, the idealized ‘perfect mother’ narratives prevalent on SNS act as a potent subjective norm. Furthermore, the embodied and affect-laden fertility risk perception, which concretizes abstract risks into specific, ‘unmanageable’ personal threats, directly undermines perceived behavioral control. In particular, this perception fosters a belief that one cannot manage the inevitable physical changes of pregnancy and postpartum recovery. Thus, by linking SNS use to the embodied and affective roots of these TPB precursors, our model offers a nuanced understanding of how the digital environment shapes the psychological context surrounding reproductive decisions (see Fig. 2).

Our model captures the specific digital-psychological pathway among these many determinants, indicative of a meaningful theoretical signal. Its practical significance also becomes clear when considering the pervasive nature of social media. SNS use is a near-universal daily activity for young women, meaning even a moderate psychological effect, when multiplied across millions of users, can exert a substantial aggregate influence at the population level. In the context of societal concerns over declining birth rates, this pathway from routine online behavior to heightened fertility risk perception is not trivial. It suggests that the digital environment is a meaningful factor contributing to the overall context in which reproductive decisions are made, highlighting that the interventions we propose are not merely addressing a minor issue but a psychologically significant and widespread phenomenon.

It is worth noting that we also considered and tested the possibility of a reverse causal pathway, namely whether fertility risk perception might, in turn, drive SNS engagement. The results of our exploratory reverse mediation analysis did not support our hypothesized core chain pathway (Fertility Risk Perception → SNS Body Comparisons → NPS → SNS Use Intensity), as this path (Ind3) was non-significant across all NPS dimensions. Although the data indicated that a simpler indirect path (Ind1: Fertility Risk Perception → SNS Body Comparisons → SNS Use Intensity) was significant, this does not support the specific reverse chain involving NPS. The significance of the Ind1 path might suggest that women with higher perceived fertility risk do engage in more SNS body comparisons—perhaps as a way to seek information about postpartum recovery or gauge related social norms—and this comparison behavior is, in turn, associated with higher SNS use intensity. This potential information-seeking or comparison behavior could align with social support theory’s view that individuals seek resources online when facing stressors [57]. However, the crucial finding remains that the specific reverse chain pathway involving NPS (Ind3) lacks empirical support. Therefore, these results lend further credence to the directionality of our primary proposed model (i.e., SNS use is associated with fertility risk perception), although the precise causal relationship between these variables warrants further investigation through future cross-sectional and longitudinal research.

This study’s findings offer a fresh perspective on the low fertility intentions and “fear of childbirth” prevalent among modern young women. Our results further revealed that nulliparous women and students reported significantly higher fertility risk perception compared to mothers and working women, respectively. This finding implies that for women who have not yet given birth or fully entered the workforce, the perception of fertility risk may be amplified by the ‘fear of the unknown’ regarding both physical changes and career interruptions [58, 59]. In contrast, women with actual childbirth and work experience may hold more moderated, realistic views of these risks.

The pervasiveness of female objectifying content in SNSs exerts constant pressure on women to look a certain way. The study’s results suggest the need to promote healthy and positive messages for women across various SNS platforms, in conjunction with comprehensive reproductive health support. In practice, this may involve three specific measures. First, encouraging the dissemination of scientifically grounded knowledge on postpartum recovery to provide women with accurate and reliable guidance. Second, sharing positive cases of women’s physical and psychological adjustment after childbirth to offer relatable and supportive role models. Third, recommending beneficial activities such as Pilates or other moderate exercises that can improve body image and enhance reproductive well-being. Beyond these individual-level strategies, broader policy-relevant measures are essential. Collaboration with health authorities and maternal health programs can integrate accurate reproductive health information and fertility education into both offline services and online platforms. Partnerships with SNS providers may also regulate the circulation of harmful or stigmatizing fertility- and body-related content while amplifying positive and supportive narratives. Importantly, such interventions must be contextually sensitive, particularly when navigating the intersection of pro-natalist social norms and digital pressures. Here, the broader pro-natalist social environment often intersects with pervasive digital body surveillance, specifically the pressure for rapid postpartum body ‘bounce-back’. This paradox intensifies the individualization of risk, placing the burden on women to balance the societal expectation to reproduce with the digital-cultural demand for physical permanence. Therefore, multi-level approaches are essential to foster healthier digital environments and strengthen women’s reproductive well-being by connecting these personal experiences with broader social and policy frameworks.

However, this study has certain limitations that warrant improvement in future research. First, by recruiting via public SNS announcements, we effectively reached our target demographic of childbearing-age women with SNS experience (89.2% aged 20–40). But this approach resulted in a sample that is substantially more educated (87.3% college degree or higher) than the national benchmark (15.5%) [60]. Nevertheless, this demographic likely represents the population most susceptible to digital beauty pressures. Drawing on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, aesthetic concerns often become salient only after basic subsistence needs are met [61]; thus, highly educated urban women likely face intensified pressure to balance appearance and career compared to those prioritizing economic stability. Consequently, while our findings offer insights into this specific group, generalizations to women with lower digital literacy or socioeconomic status should be made with caution. Additionally, while our sample included women with childbirth experience, we did not specifically target pregnant women or those in the immediate postpartum period, a demographic directly experiencing fertility-related body changes. Future research should explicitly include these groups to enhance generalizability. Second, the study concentrated on women’s perceived risk of childbirth, but did not examine women’s attitudes toward childbirth, childbirth decisions, and childbirth intentions. Future research should investigate these aspects more thoroughly to understand the reasons for low childbirth rates. Furthermore, future research should move beyond the current main-effect model to explore potential moderating variables. For instance, examining whether childbirth experience (primiparous vs. multiparous) moderates the impact of NPS on fertility risk perception could provide deeper insights into how these mechanisms vary across different subgroups of women. Third, because the present study used a cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be firmly established. It is important to note that while mediation analysis was used, this statistical method inherently implies a directional pathway that the current data cannot definitively support. Therefore, the proposed mediation model should be interpreted as a plausible theoretical framework that outlines potential correlational relationships, rather than a confirmed causal chain. To further clarify the causal ordering between SNS use intensity and fertility risk perception, future studies could adopt a longitudinal cross-lagged panel design. This approach would allow examination of reciprocal effects over time and separation of within-person dynamics from stable between-person differences. By tracking participants across multiple waves and establishing measurement invariance, the model can directly compare the predictive strength and directionality of each path, thereby providing stronger evidence for or against reverse causality. Fourth, future studies could refine the measurement of SNS usage. While our study used SNS use intensity as a broad measure, future research could differentiate between platform types (e.g., Weibo vs. Xiaohongshu) and content types, such as appearance-related, body-related, and fertility-related content. This would provide more precise insights into how different content affects fertility risk perception. Developing specific scales for these types of content would help clarify their impact on fertility-related outcomes. Fifth, the study also relied exclusively on self-report questionnaires for both predictor and outcome variables, which raises the possibility of common method variance. Specifically, the Negative Physical Self Scale yielded a high Cronbach’s α of 0.976. While indicative of internal consistency, such a high value in a lengthy scale may also reflect item redundancy or participant response bias. Future research could address these limitations by incorporating objective behavioral data, such as logged SNS metrics (e.g., daily screen time duration, login frequency, or specific interaction counts), alongside experimental paradigms (e.g., Approach-Avoidance Tasks), which can provide more objective indicators of individuals’ automatic motivational tendencies when processing body-related or fertility-related information. Such multi-method approaches would help mitigate potential biases and yield stronger evidence regarding the mechanisms under investigation. Lastly, future research should conduct a series of empirical studies to examine the effects of the dissemination of information on SNSs related to body image, appearance, and fertility on perceptions of fertility risk, fertility decisions, and fertility intentions.

Conclusion

This study has determined that the degree of SNS usage is significantly associated with perceived fertility risk, with this correlation mediated by SNS body comparisons and negative self-perceptions concerning thinness, fatness, and facial appearance. These findings call for actionable interventions. For public health communication and media literacy, this includes promoting scientifically grounded knowledge on postpartum recovery and amplifying positive, diverse narratives of motherhood to counter idealized content. At the fertility policy level, partnerships between health authorities and SNS providers are essential to regulate harmful body-related content and foster healthier digital environments. Crucially, these interventions must address the societal paradox where state messaging encouraging childbirth intersects with intense digital body surveillance, thereby connecting the personal, psychological burdens of fertility risk to the broader social contexts that shape them.
