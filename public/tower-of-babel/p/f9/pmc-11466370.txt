Self‐reported sleep duration and quality and cardiovascular diseases among middle‐aged and older Chinese: A 7‐year longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Zhe Lv, Yuqiang Ji, Chao Li, Zhao Zhao, Wanru Jia, Jingjing Hou, Hong Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of death worldwide, and several studies have attempted to identify its risk factors. This study aimed to investigate the association between sleep duration and sleep quality, and the 7‐year incidence of CVD among middle‐aged and older Chinese individuals. A total of 6682 participants aged 45–90 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study database were included in this study. The authors estimated sleep duration and quality based on self‐reported data of night sleep hours and disturbance symptoms, and examined the associations between them and the composite outcome of CVD using logistic regression models. A total of 1692 participants (25.32%) reported new CVD events during follow‐up. Short sleep duration (< 6 h/night) was significantly associated with a higher risk of CVD in all three models (p < .05). However, this was not observed for long sleep duration (> 8 h/night). Additionally, participants with mild sleep disturbance in all three models, and severe sleep disturbance in Models 2 and 3 had a significantly higher risk of CVD (p < .05). After stratification by age and daytime napping, we still found a significant association between short sleep duration and CVD in individuals aged 45–59 years, and between sleep disturbance and CVD in non‐nappers (p < .05). However, these associations were not significant in individuals aged ≥60 years or in nappers (p > .05). In conclusion, short sleep duration and sleep disturbance are both associated with an increased risk of CVD in middle‐aged and older Chinese individuals.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has been identified as a leading cause of death among non‐communicable diseases worldwide. 1 In China, the annual number of deaths due to CVD increased from 2.51 million to 3.97 million between 1990 and 2016, and the age‐standardized prevalence of CVD has risen significantly by 14.7% overall. 2 With such a serious background, the indirect and direct costs of CVD pose a threat to ongoing poverty reduction initiatives in China. It is also estimated to be an obstacle to the implementation of the Healthy China 2030 Plan. 3 As an aging society emerges, it is crucial to consider the risks associated with the development of CVD, especially in middle‐aged and older adults, and identify which factors are modifiable and which are not. Traditional risk factors for CVD include cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and modified lipid metabolism. 4 , 5 A recent study found that despite some lifestyle indicators shifting in a beneficial direction, substantial gaps still exist between guideline‐recommended targets and current levels of these indicators. 6

After decades of socioeconomic progress, marked by a high‐intensity work pace and life pressure among Chinese adults, sleep problems are starting to receive the attention they deserve. 7 Recently, the recognition of CVD‐specific risk factors, in addition to the traditional risks, such as variable sleep symptom profiles, and sleep‐improved outcomes with guideline‐directed medical therapy had become important in improving the outlook for CVD health and quality of life. 8 Accumulating evidence suggests that sleep disorders and sleep deprivation are associated with the deterioration of human body functioning and increased cardiovascular risks. 9 , 10 Additionally, sleep duration has shown a curvilinear (U‐shaped) relationship with all‐cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, indicating that the lowest risk is observed among those obtaining 7−8 h of sleep per night. 11 There is increasing evidence suggesting that optimal sleep duration is critical for cardiovascular health. However, limited research has explored the association between sleep duration, sleep quality, and cardiovascular outcomes based on long‐term longitudinal cohort studies in China.

This prospective cohort study aimed to explore the association between CVD and sleep among middle‐aged and older Chinese individuals. This study aimed to examine whether alterations in health‐related habitual behaviors related to sleep affect the occurrence of CVD. We hypothesized that short sleep duration, sleep disturbances, and lack of daytime napping are independent risk factors that negatively affect the occurrence of CVD.

METHODS

Setting and participants

The data used in this study were based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), conducted between 2011 and 2018. Previous studies have described the study design in detail. 12 , 13 To obtain a nationally representative dataset, the survey included 150 county‐ and 450 village‐level units. The participants were required to complete a standardized questionnaire, undergo physical measurements, and provide venous blood samples. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before they completed the survey. The CHARLS was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University (No. IRB00001052‐11014).

The details of the participant selection are illustrated in Figure 1. Participants who were sampled but did not respond to the baseline survey, as well as those with individual non‐responses or extreme values for the main questionnaire, were screened. At baseline, 17 708 participants were recruited for CHARLS. Of these, 366 participants were excluded because they were < 45 years or > 90 years, and 57 were excluded because of incomplete age information. Additionally, 6008 participants diagnosed with CVD (including hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart problems, and stroke) or cancer were excluded. Another 2380 were excluded for implausible sleep duration (<3 or > 13 h/night) and nonresponse to sleep questions. Subsequently, 9954 participants were included at baseline. Until 2018, 1968 participants with incomplete CVD information, and 1304 participants with extreme body mass index (BMI) values (<17 or > 35 kg/m2), and incomplete weight and height measurements were excluded. After data preprocessing, 6682 participants were included in the analysis of the association between sleep and CVD. Furthermore, after excluding 118 participants with abnormal blood pressure, 6564 participants comprised a subpopulation for comparison of non‐blood biomarkers. Additionally, 3191 participants who failed to provide complete blood test information were excluded, and 3491 participants comprised a subpopulation for comparison of blood biomarkers.

Assessment of covariates

In the baseline questionnaire for CHARLS, basic information about the participants, including date of birth, sex, marital status, educational level, and residence, was collected. In the health status and functioning sections, the participants’ lifestyle and chronic disease histories were also collected. Education was divided into four levels: illiteracy, primary, middle and high school, and college or above. The frequency of drinking (alcohol consumption) was divided into three groups: never, less than once a month, and more than once a month. The chronic disease history was assessed using the following questions: “Have you been diagnosed with any of the conditions listed below (read one by one by a doctor)? (1) hypertension; (2) dyslipidemia (elevation of low‐density lipoprotein, triglycerides [TGs], and total cholesterol, or a low high‐density lipoprotein level); (3) diabetes or high blood sugar; (4) cancer or malignant tumor (excluding minor skin cancers); (5) chronic lung diseases, such as chronic bronchitis, emphysema (excluding tumors or cancer); (6) liver disease (except fatty liver, tumors, and cancer); (7) heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems; (8) stroke; (9) kidney disease (except for tumor or cancer); (10) stomach or other digestive disease (except for tumor or cancer); (11) emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems; (12) memory‐related disease; (13) arthritis or rheumatism; (14) asthma.” The response options were “Yes” or “No.”

Assessment of sleep

A self‐reported question on sleep duration was: “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)?” Respondents provided answers in hourly increments. According to previous research, 14 a sleep duration of 6−8 h/night is considered optimal for the majority of the population, and we divided the participants into three groups (<6, 6−8, and > 8 h/night). Similarly, information on daytime naps was collected through the question: “During the past month, how long did you take a nap after lunch?” Since a previous study indicated that naps as short as 10 min could produce significant benefits, 15 respondents were categorized as non‐nappers (< 10 min) and nappers (≥10 min) in this study. Furthermore, the levels of sleep disturbance were reported by the participants based on the question: “How many days a week did you have trouble falling asleep, frequently nighttime awakenings, and earlier waking?” The response options included rarely or none of the time (<1 day), a little of the time (1−2 days), occasionally or moderate amount of the time (3−4 days), and most or all of the time (5−7 days). These responses corresponded to none, mild, moderate, or severe symptoms.

Ascertainment of outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of CVD (including hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, other heart problems, and stroke) in 2018. These were defined based on the chronic disease questions: “Have you been diagnosed with hypertension by a doctor?”, “Have you been diagnosed with dyslipidemia (elevation of low‐density lipoprotein, triglycerides, and total cholesterol, or a low high‐density lipoprotein level) by a doctor?”, “Have you been diagnosed with heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems by a doctor?”, and “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?”. Participants were defined as having CVD if they answered “Yes” to any question.

Physical measurement

In this study, the interviewer responsible for visiting a county or district also carried the medical examination equipment to the home of the interviewee for a physical examination (including blood pressure, weight, and height). The physical examination measurements and methods were almost identical. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Considering that antihypertensive and lipid‐lowering therapy addresses only half of the CVD risk in patients with BMI > 30 kg/m2, this analysis excluded participants with extreme BMI values. 16 Blood pressure (systolic and diastolic) and pulse were measured three times after participants rested for 5 min. The current analysis considered the average values of blood pressure and pulse rate from the three readings.

Blood biomarkers test

Of the total 17 708 participants in CHARLS, 11 847 (response rate: 67%) completed the blood tests. Venous blood samples were collected and assayed for c‐reactive protein, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, TGs, glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, uric acid, and cystatin C. Additionally, complete blood count analysis, which included hemoglobin, hematocrit, white blood cell count, platelet count, and mean corpuscular volume, was performed at the local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention laboratories.

Statistical analysis

In this study, continuous variables were presented as means (standard deviations, SDs) and medians (interquartile ranges) according to whether the data were normally or non‐normally distributed. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). Restricted by the data on physical biomarkers and blood samples, after omitting a small number of extreme values, the biomarker and blood test results were compared among the subpopulations. ANOVA (for means), Kruskal–Wallis (for medians), and chi‐square analysis (for frequencies) were performed to detect statistical differences between different sleep duration groups. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the associations among sleep duration, sleep disturbance, and CVD based on the three models. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, residence, educational level, and marital status. Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, residence, educational level, marital status, chronic lung disease, and arthritis or rheumatism. The estimates of the strengths of associations were demonstrated by the odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). A p‐value < .05 (two‐sided test) was considered statistically significant in this study. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0) and R software (version 3.5.1).

Association between CVD and sleep

After a follow‐up period of > 6 years, 1692 of 6682 (25.32%) participants developed CVD.

The effects of nocturnal sleep duration on CVD are shown in Table 2. Model 1 (OR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.03−1.33], p < .05) showed that the risk of CVD in the <6 h/night group was significantly higher than that in the 6−8 h/night group, with similar results in Models 2 (OR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.03−1.34], p < .05) and 3 (OR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.01−1.31], p < .05). However, the results of Models 1, 2, and 3 demonstrated no significant difference in the incidence of CVD between the >8 h/night group and 6−8 h/night group. After stratification by age and daytime nap, the associations between the <6 h/night group and CVD remained significant in the three models among participants aged 45−59 years, but were attenuated and without any significant differences among those aged 60−90 years, non‐nappers, or nappers.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cohort study, we observed that both short sleep duration (< 6 h/night) and sleep disturbance independently increased the risk of CVD among middle‐aged and older Chinese individuals during a 7‐year follow‐up period. Our results suggest that the public should attach great importance to improving sleep to prevent CVDs.

Consistent with previous studies, 17 , 18 , 19 the present study identified a significant association between short sleep duration and CVD. The current study confirmed additive interactions between short sleep duration and poor sleep quality, which affect the risk of CVD. After stratification by age, this study found a significant association between short sleep duration and CVD in participants aged 45−59 years, but not in those aged ≥60 years. This replicated the null findings in the geriatric population of several former cohort studies. 20 , 21 Therefore, maintaining a healthy sleep duration is extremely necessary for the most vulnerable group (45−59 years), who suffer from high stress in daily life and work. Notably, the effect of long sleep duration on CVD was not statistically significant. In contrast with a former study, long sleep duration (> 9 h) was observed to be independently associated with an increased risk of incident CVD in participants aged ≥50 years, even after adjusting for potential confoundings. 22 This discrepancy warrants further exploration. This might be due to the small number of participants (only 559/6682, 8.37%) with a night sleep duration of > 8 h in this study. However, we noted that data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys also did not confirm that any health indicator was associated with long sleep duration. 23 Consequently, more studies are needed to explore the reasons for these inconsistencies.

Additionally, daytime naps did not alter the relationship between night sleep duration and CVD. An umbrella review indicated that the associations of daytime napping with CVD and mortality in most meta‐analyses were mainly supported by weak or suggestive evidence. 24 Conversely, a two‐sample Mendelian Randomization analysis suggested that daytime napping was causally associated with an increased risk of heart failure, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, cardiac arrhythmia, and coronary atherosclerosis. 25 Recent research has observed a significant association between the presence of metabolic syndrome and daily naps. 26 Therefore, the role of daytime naps in CVD development still needs further investigation to provide robust evidence.

Sleep disturbances are associated with impaired sleep quality and/or abnormal sleep duration. This study found that mild and severe symptoms of sleep disturbance were significantly associated with the incidence of CVD in middle‐aged and older participants. Furthermore, the association with CVD was more significant among daytime non‐nappers. However, when the participants were stratified by age, these associations were attenuated. Our study highlights the role of sleep disturbance among participants who do not take daytime naps, suggesting that daytime naps may alleviate disturbance symptoms. Based on the current evidence, identifying and addressing sleep disorders should be part of both primary and secondary CVD prevention. CVD and sleep are “bedfellows” as sleep disorders, including sleep‐disordered breathing, parasomnias, sleep‐related movement disorders, insomnia, and hypersomnia, are intimately intertwined with comorbid cardiovascular conditions and increased risk of stroke. 27 Sleep disturbance is a modifiable risk factor for both physical and mental health. 28 Thus, more research is needed to determine what interventions are appropriate to slow the progression of CVD for people suffering from disturbed sleep. This study has shown that nocturnal sleep duration, sleep disturbance, and daytime naps seem to be related to each other, and further evidence suggests that participants with mild sleep disturbance in the 6−8 h/night group and participants with moderate sleep disturbance in the < 6 h/night group are key risk populations for CVD. While moderate sleep disturbance in the 6−8 h group did not significantly cause an increase in CVD, its overall trend was consistent with mild sleep disturbance. This might be due to the small sample size and short follow‐up period in this study. These insights into the regulation of sleep balance may provide new insights for understanding and treating CVD.

The potential effects of short sleep duration on cardiovascular health have been explored in previous studies. According to the analysis of the sub‐cohort participants in this study, diastolic blood pressure and some blood biomarkers were significantly different among the three sleep duration groups. Consistent with previous results, blood pressure followed a diurnal pattern with fluctuations during rest and sleep under physiological conditions. 29 Simultaneously, these results confirmed that sleep duration is associated with serum cholesterol in middle‐aged and older Chinese individuals. Compared to the 6−8 h/night group, higher total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and cystatin C levels were observed in the shorter sleep period group. This was consistent with previous findings that prolonged sleep deprivation modifies inflammatory and cholesterol pathways at the level of gene expression and serum lipoproteins, inducing changes toward a potentially higher risk for cardiometabolic diseases. 30 Additionally, we speculated that cystatin C might be associated with a significant increase in the relative risk of developing CVD, as well as increased mortality, requiring early identification and aggressive treatment. 31 This is because cystatin C is not only a cause of gout but also may play a role in developing chronic kidney disease, a common underlying disease process for developing CVD. 32 , 33 Important directions for the future should include public education on the unique profile of sleep disturbance on CVD, as well as the reciprocal links between sleep and inflammatory biology. 34

This study had some limitations. Although the CHARLS has abundant data resources and provides sufficient information for analysis, it also raises the challenge of data cleaning. To ensure the reliability and accuracy of conclusions, we excluded participants with missing data or abnormal values that might affect the quality of the data according to previous research. 35 Additionally, since sleep data only covers the previous month, it cannot reflect the longer‐term sleep patterns. The main indicators, including sleep duration, disturbance symptoms, and CVD incidence, were reported using subjective questions, which may have caused recall bias. Furthermore, owing to the inability of the CHARLS database to collect physical and blood biomarkers for all participants, the potential effect mechanism of sleep on CVD was analyzed in a small population. Our study was based on participants aged 45−90 years from China; therefore, these findings should be circumspectly applied to other populations or countries.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated that both short nocturnal sleep duration and sleep disturbance significantly affect the incidence of CVD. In the future, great importance should be attached to prolonging sleep duration and improving sleep quality in middle‐aged and older populations, as well as introducing interventions to target sleep‐disturbing factors.
