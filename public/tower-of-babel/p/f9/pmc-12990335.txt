Enhancing tenderness and flavor of microwave-dried shrimp through ultrasonic-assisted papain pretreatment: Effects and mechanisms
Authors: Wenwen Luo, Danyang Song, Zhongwei Peng, Jiao Li, Jianan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Litopenaeus vannamei, Microwave drying, Tenderness, Ultrasound-assisted papain, Flavor

Introduction

Litopenaeus vannamei is one of the three widely farmed shrimp species globally and a crustacean that belongs to the order Decapoda and family Penaeidae [1]. Drying is a common method used in shrimp processing. By reducing moisture content, inactivating microorganisms, and inhibiting the action of endogenous enzymes, it prolongs the shelf life of shrimp while also contributing to the development of a distinctive flavor [2]. Among drying techniques, microwave drying utilizes the dielectric heating effect. Under the influence of the microwave electric field, polar molecules, such as water molecules within the food oscillate rapidly, generating heat through intermolecular friction, which enables rapid and uniform heating [3]. For meat processing, microwave drying offers advantages including quick heating, high drying efficiency, and rich flavor [4]. However, high-intensity microwave drying induces substantial moisture loss, leading to physicochemical changes including protein cross-linking and aggregation and lipid peroxidation [5]. These changes reduce digestibility and simultaneously increase muscle toughness, rendering the product unsuitable for elderly individuals and children with limited chewing ability, thereby restricting its market potential and sales range. With the increasing demand for balanced diets and specialized foods, exploring effective strategies to tenderize dried shrimp meat is important. Nevertheless, compared with extensive studies on raw meats like beef and pork tenderization, research on dried shrimp remains scarce [6], [7].

Meat tenderizing technologies could be broadly categorized into three approaches: physical intervention (including mechanical tumbling, ultrasonic vibration, etc.), chemical regulation (including injection of calcium salts, phosphates, etc.) and biological catalysis [8]. Because of the risk of chemical residues and cross-contamination, chemical tenderization is less favored by consumers. Among physical methods, ultrasonic waves can preserve the integrity of the meat while offering advantages including high efficiency, safety, environmental friendliness, and affordability. Unlike mechanical tumbling, which ruins the products' natural condition, ultrasonic vibration has been extensively applied in raw meat tenderization research. Liu et al. [9] and Zou et al. [10] observed a significant decrease in shear force after ultrasound treatment in grass carp and goose breast, respectively. Biological tenderization utilizes enzymes such as bromelain, papain, and trypsin to cleave peptide chains in proteins to facilitate the breakdown of muscle fibers. Papain and bromelain are natural proteases extracted from plants [11], [12]. Due to their broad applicability and affordability, they are commonly used in the tenderization of raw meat. However, most studies have examined their application in fresh or minimally processed meat, where their impact is more pronounced. Their effectiveness in improving the characteristics of dried meat products is less understood and appears to be limited [13]. Thus, it is essential to investigate the synergistic effects of different tenderizing methods and the underlying mechanisms.

Furthermore, the high-intensity heating and dehydration during microwave drying lead to more complex compositional changes compared to mild processing. After microwave drying, the flavor profile is significantly altered from its raw state by Maillard reaction, lipid oxidation, and other processes [2]. Zhang et al. [14] discovered that ultrasonic treatment could accelerate the oxidation of free fatty acids (FFAs) and showed great potential for flavor enhancement when investigating the effect of ultrasonic-assisted frying on the flavor characteristics of fried meatballs. Therefore, while tenderizing dried shrimp, it is important to consider not just texture and mouthfeel but also flavor alterations caused by variations in protein, fat, moisture, and other ingredients.

Currently, there is limited research focused on the tenderization of low-moisture Litopenaeus vannamei undergoing microwave drying. Additionally, the synergistic mechanisms of combined tenderization treatments on the texture and flavor of shrimp meat have not been thoroughly analyzed. Therefore, this study examined the effects of ultrasonic-assisted papain pretreatment on the tenderness and flavor of microwave-dried shrimp. Furthermore, the synergistic mechanism was elucidated through the perspectives of protein physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, and correlation analysis.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Litopenaeus vannamei (22.0±1.0 g) was purchased from Shangcheng Farmers' Market (Qingdao, China). Papain (200,000 U/g) was obtained from Nanning Pangbo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. 5×SDS-PAGE protein loading buffer and protein electrophoresis gel preparation kit were obtained from Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The 3-color pre-dyed protein Marker (14-250 kDa) was obtained from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Other chemicals, including NaCl, KBr, and KCl were all analytical grade or chemical grade.

Sample preparation

According to the previous studies [15], [16], the pretreatment conditions were determined through pre-experiments while ensuring the activity of papain. The shrimp were randomly assigned to 5 groups as follows: (1) PS: no pretreatment; (2) NS: soaked in normal saline; (3) PAP: immersed in 1% papain solution (containing 0.9% NaCl); (4) UT: ultrasonic treatment (120 W, containing 0.9% NaCl); (5) UAP: ultrasonic treatment combined with 1% papain solution (containing 0.9% NaCl). All pretreatment conditions were conducted at 50°C for 45 min. Then, the shrimp were boiled in brine (1.5% NaCl, shrimp-to-water mass ratio of 1:3) at 100°C for 1 min. After draining, they were dried at a constant microwave power output of 539 W. Drying was terminated when the moisture content reached 20% (w.b.) [4].

Shear force

Shear force was determined with a food physical property analyzer (TMS-TOUCH, FTC, USA) equipped with the single-blade shear mode [17]. The first and second abdominal segments were taken and sheared perpendicular to the fibers of the dried shrimp. Measurements were performed on a minimum of six replicates per group.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

Sample preparation was performed following the method described by Bai et al. [18] with appropriate modifications. The samples were cut into small cubes (5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm) and fixed by immersion in a glutaraldehyde solution (2.5%) for over 2 h. Subsequently, the meat was rinsed with PBS (0.1 M, pH = 7.2) for 15 min, which repeated four times. It was then dehydrated sequentially with 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol solutions for 10 min each. After freeze-drying, the samples were sputter-coated with gold using an ion sputtering instrument to observe the degree of muscle fiber fragmentation.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS)

Measurements were performed using a malondialdehyde (MDA) content assay kit (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.), with values expressed as nmol/g meat:

MDA (nmol/g meat) = 53.763 × ΔA ÷ W (1)

Where, ΔA and W represented the difference in absorbance between each sample at 532 nm and 600 nm, and the sample mass, respectively.

E-Tongue analysis

Taste characteristics of dried Litopenaeus vannamei were evaluated using an E-Tongue (SA402B Plus-EX, Insent, Japan). Samples (3.00 g) were homogenized with ultrapure water (1:10, w/v) and then centrifuged at 4°C (9000 ×g, 30 min). The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane to determine the taste of samples [19].

HS-GC-IMS analysis

Volatile compounds from shrimp samples (1.50 g) in 20 mL headspace vials were analyzed by HS-GC-IMS (FlavorSpec®, G.A.S., Germany). The vials were incubated at 60°C for 20 min with agitation (500 rpm) to equilibrate the headspace. Subsequently, 500 μL of the headspace gas was injected via a heated syringe (80°C) [19]. Compound separation was achieved using an FS-SE-15-CB capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm) maintained at 40°C.

Free amino acids (FAA) and taste activity values (TAV)

The FAA content was determined using an automatic amino acid analyzer (PF system, L-8900, Hitachi, Japan) following a previously described method [20]. TAV was calculated using the following formula:

TAV = C / T (2)

Where, C and T represented the concentration and threshold of taste compounds respectively, mg/100 g.

Extraction of Litopenaeus vannamei proteins (LVPs)

LVPs were extracted according to the pH-shift method described by Duppeti et al. [21] and Ma et al. [22]. Briefly, shrimp meat was mixed with distilled water at a 1:6 (w/v) ratio and homogenized (10,000 ×g). The homogenate was then adjusted to pH 11.0 using NaOH (2 M). The homogenate was shaken on ice for 2 h, followed by centrifugation at 4°C (9000 rpm, 20 min). The resulting supernatant was adjusted to pH 5.0 with HCl (2 N), agitated on ice for 30 min, and then centrifuged again under the same conditions (9000 rpm, 20 min, 4°C). The protein pellet was suspended in water and the pH was brought to 7.0. The protein suspension was stirred overnight at 4°C, and then centrifuged at 8000 rpm (10 min, 4°C). The LVPs concentration was determined using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method and adjusted to an appropriate concentration for indicator analysis.

Total sulfhydryl content and surface hydrophobicity

The total sulfhydryl content of the LVPs (1 mg/mL) was measured using a micro total mercapto assay kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Following the method described by Liu et al. [9], LVPs were diluted to 2 mg/mL. The surface hydrophobicity was determined by calculating the amount of bromophenol blue (BPB) bound:

BPB bound (μg) = 200 × (A1 – A0) /A0 (3)

Where, A0 and A1 represented the absorbance values of the control group and the sample at 595 nm, respectively.

Structure of LVPs

Determination of fluorescence spectroscopy

The tertiary structure of LVPs (1 mg/mL) was analyzed using the fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600, Hitachi, Japan). The excitation wavelength was 280 nm, the slit width was 5 nm, while conducting scanning measurements at the emission wavelength of 300-500 nm [23].

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum

The secondary structures of protein were determined using FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [24]. The freeze-dried LVPs powder was thoroughly mixed with dried KBr and pressed into transparent tablets. During the test, 64 scans were conducted at a resolution of 4 cm-1. The OMNIC software was used for fitting to perform baseline correction, automatic smoothing and deconvolution of the spectra within the range of 1600-1700 cm-1, in order to analyze the relative contents of different types of secondary structures.

SDS-PAGE

The LVPs (1 mg/mL) were mixed with 5× protein loading buffer and boiled for 10 min. Subsequently, 8 μL of protein Maker and 12 μL of samples were added to the gel respectively. After staining in the Coomassie Brilliant Blue (R-250) rapid staining solution for 1.5 h, the destaining solution was added until the background on the gel was completely removed [25].

Statistical analysis

Except where indicated, all experiment analyses were conducted with three independent replicates. Each experimental group included at least n = 3 independent shrimp (i.e., from different individuals), and data were reported as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were performed using SPSS (version 16.0, SPSS Inc.). Principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation were carried out on the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform. Partial least squares regression (PLS-R) modeling and flavor fingerprinting were performed using Unscrambler (v9.7, CAMO Software, Norway) and VOCal (v0.4.03), respectively, whereas Origin 2021 produced the other visualizations.

Results and Discussion

Shear force

Shear force was a key indicator for assessing the tenderness of meat and its products. Lower shear force values corresponded to a higher degree of meat tenderness [26]. All pretreatment groups exhibited significant reductions in shear force (p < 0.05). The shear force of NS was marginally lower than that of PS, possibly due to the tenderizing effect of low-concentration NaCl. This finding was consistent with the results reported by Deng et al. [27] and Liu et al. [9]. The shear force values of PAP, UT, and UAP decreased by 28.59%, 24.51%, and 40.35%, respectively (p < 0.05; Fig. 1a). During the microwave drying process, rapid moisture transfer mechanism caused the myofibrillar protein network in Litopenaeus vannamei to contract and denature [17]. Consequently, the increased shear resistance and tensile strength of the myofibrils resulted in a significant increase in the hardness of the dried prawns compared with the raw material, making them tougher to chew. Papain was a commonly used food-grade proteolytic enzyme derived from papaya. Its tenderizing effect might be attributed to its capacity to efficiently degrade myofibrillar proteins and connective tissue [13]. According to Fig. 1a, UT (47.58±1.31 N) showed a shear force value that was comparable to that of the PAP (45.01±1.27 N). The cavitation effect induced by ultrasound disrupted and fragmented the spatial structure of shrimp muscle fibers. Furthermore, the ultrasound treatment promoted the release of the endogenous protein hydrolysis system in the prawns [28], [29]. It was noteworthy that UAP exhibited more uniform texture improvement traits in addition to synergistic tenderization effect. The combined application of papain and ultrasound during the pretreatment stage accelerated mass transfer process, enabling papain to penetrate beyond the surface layer of the shrimp [30]. Consequently, the shear force of UAP decreased considerably (37.60±1.23 N, p < 0.05) due to the enhanced enzyme accessibility within the meat and the synergistic impact of the combined treatment. Thus, UAP established optimal muscle and moisture migration conditions for the subsequent microwave drying process.

TBARS

Microwave heating promoted the attack of free metal ions on cell membranes, which induced lipid peroxidation and led to the formation of MDA. MDA reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) under high temperature and acidic conditions to form red-colored TBARS adduct, which could be used to characterize the degree of lipid oxidation in shrimp [31]. According to the experimental findings, papain pretreatment groups (PAP and UAP) significantly inhibited lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1b, p< 0.05). This finding was consistent with the results reported by Garg & Mendiratta [32] in their study on pork tenderization. This effect was likely related to the antioxidant properties of bioactive peptides and free amino acids generated during enzymatic hydrolysis in the pretreatment stage. These hydrolysis products, derived from the pretreatment soaking step, could exert antioxidant effects during microwave heating by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and interrupting the chain reactions of lipid oxidation [33]. The TBARS values for UT (147.92±2.64 nmol/g) and NS (143.41±2.29 nmol/g) were also lower than those for PS (200.11±4.47 nmol/g, p < 0.05), with no significant difference between UT and NS (p > 0.05). This might be attributed to the redistribution of water and NaCl during pretreatment. Furthermore, the reduced moisture content of the pretreated samples, a requisite for manufacturing dried shrimp, might have facilitated interactions between aldehydes (like MDA) and amino groups under prolonged microwave heating. This likely contributed to reduced TBARS levels in the pretreated groups while simultaneously promoting the formation of pyrolytic flavor compounds such as pyrroles and pyrazines [34].

E-Tongue analysis

The taste profiles of microwave-dried Litopenaeus vannamei subjected to different pretreatments were analyzed using an E-tongue. The assessed taste attributes included bitterness, saltiness, umami, astringency, and richness. The results demonstrated that the dried shrimp lacked discernible sourness and astringency, whereas scores for attributes such as richness, umami, and saltiness were relatively high (Fig. 1c, e). It was evident from PCA results that PC1 contributed 64.40%, PC2 contributed 30.22%, and the overall contribution was 94.62% (Fig. 1d). This indicated that the E-tongue measurements were able to accurately reflect the overall taste profile [19]. Furthermore, the variations in taste components among the pretreatment groups were small. PS was distinctly separated from the others, indicating notable taste differences. In contrast, NS, PAP, UT, and UAP showed overlapping distributions, with only slight separation among them (Fig. 1d).

Microwave drying significantly altered the taste profile of the shrimp compared with the raw material. As shown in Fig. 1c, the bitterness intensity of the dried shrimp from all pretreatment groups was comparatively minimal. The saltiness intensity was significantly enhanced in UT and UAP (p < 0.05). This was likely because the cavitation and mechanical effects of ultrasound during pretreatment created micro-channels within the muscle fibers, facilitating salt penetration. Additionally, the mass transfer of NaCl was enhanced by fluid dynamics phenomena, such as micro-jets and micro-stirring produced by ultrasonic waves in salt solutions [9]. Consequently, ultrasonic treatment accelerated the diffusion of sodium chloride into the muscle tissue, thereby increasing the salt content and enhancing the perceived saltiness (p < 0.05) [35]. Guo et al. [36] also reported that the NaCl content in pork was highest at an ultrasound frequency of 26.8 kHz, which concurrently improved its tenderness. Furthermore, ultrasound might promote protein degradation, releasing small peptides that synergistically enhanced saltiness perception in the presence of sodium ions [37]. In contrast to PS, the richness decreased in all pretreatment groups (Fig. 1c, p < 0.05). This reduction was likely due to the leaching of water-soluble taste components such as amino acids, during the soaking step [38]. In summary, while the various tenderizing pretreatments had a limited overall impact on the taste profile, all dried shrimp products exhibited a pronounced umami characteristic.

SEM

Fig. 2 demonstrated the impact of different pretreatment methods on the microstructure of microwave-dried Litopenaeus vannamei muscle. The microscopic structures of PS and NS appeared relatively intact. The muscle fibers were densely arranged with minimal gaps between the bundles, and the intercellular matrix was also densely distributed, in both groups. Prior studies have established a strong correlation between the degree of muscle fiber disruption and meat tenderness [18], [39]. The shrimp fibers shrank and aggregated after being microwave-dried, which greatly increased their stiffness [40]. In contrast, pretreatment with papain or ultrasound (PAP, UT, and UAP) resulted in a visible increase in the gaps between muscle fiber bundles compared with the control groups (PS and NS). These microstructural fragmentation observations aligned with the findings of Ribeiro et al. [41] regarding papain treatment on beef bread and Guo et al. [42] regarding ultrasound treatment on pork. This demonstrated that both papain and ultrasonic treatment could effectively disrupt the structural integrity of myofibrils. In particular, UAP exhibited the most slackened and fragmented fiber arrangement. This synergistic effect was likely attributable to the combined action of ultrasound-induced cavitation and papain-mediated proteolysis, which disrupted the connective tissue and loosened the muscle structure [43]. These structural changes ultimately contributed to the improved tenderness of the dried shrimp meat.

HS-GC-IMS analysis

Microwave drying generated the unique and appealing aroma profile of the shrimp, a process driven by multiple reactions including the Maillard reaction and the oxidative decomposition of lipids and proteins. HS-GC-IMS was commonly employed to analyze trace volatile compounds and differentiate flavor profiles, leveraging the high separation efficiency of GC and the superior sensitivity of IMS [44]. It was possible to effectively distinguish the substances closely related to the flavor of dried shrimp by comparing the topographic plots and PCA (Fig. 3a, b), and to evaluate the flavor similarity of different samples [19]. Although different pretreatments endowed the samples with distinct characteristics, significant overlaps were observed in their flavor compounds (Fig. 3c). In terms of overall distribution, the data points for PS and NS formed a tight cluster, indicating highly similar volatile flavor profiles (Fig. 3a).

PS: no pretreatment; NS: soaked in normal saline; PAP: immersed in 1% papain solution (containing 0.9% NaCl); UT: ultrasonic treatment (120 W, containing 0.9% NaCl); UAP: ultrasonic treatment combined with 1% papain solution (containing 0.9% NaCl).

A total of 43 volatile compounds were identified across all five pretreatment groups of microwave-dried Litopenaeus vannamei, which were categorized into aldehydes (6), ketones (9), alcohols (9), esters (6), and other compounds (13). The concentrations of several volatile compounds, including butanal, 3-methylbutyl acetate, and pentanal, were significantly higher in PS than in the PAP, UT, and UAP (Fig. 3c). These substances primarily originated from lipid oxidation and decomposition, which was consistent with the results of the aforementioned lipid oxidation degree [45]. Aldehydes and esters contributed to the basic flavor of dried shrimp with their subtle grassy, fruity, and mildly oily aromas [19]. However, compared with the pretreated groups, the overall flavor composition of PS still exhibited a less complex characteristic, and the aroma intensity was relatively low.

In contrast, the concentrations of numerous compounds, such as 1-pentanol, 2-pentanone, propanol, 1-penten-3-one, butyl acetate, ethyl pyrazine, and 2-octanone were elevated in the PAP, UT, and UAP groups. This increase was likely attributable to the oxidation and degradation of myofibrillar proteins induced by ultrasonic cavitation and papain proteolysis during pretreatment, which generated peptides and free amino acids [23], [46], [47]. It was beneficial for Litopenaeus vannamei to undergo reactions with carbonyl compounds, free fatty acids, etc. during the microwave heating stage, intensifying the Maillard reaction and imparting intense roasted, nutty, and sweet aromas to the dried shrimp [48]. Concurrently, the increased levels of certain aldehydes and ketones also further enriched the overall aroma complexity. Although the odor detection threshold of ketones (1-104 ppb) was generally higher than that of aldehydes (0.1-103 ppb), ketones still played a significant role in the overall flavor of dried shrimp [49], [2]. They usually offered unique aroma and fruity fragrance, and had a certain antioxidant capacity [2], [50]. UAP contained the highest abundance and diversity of volatile compounds, which demonstrated the synergistic effect of the combined papain and ultrasound pretreatment. Consequently, UAP developed a more focused, intense, and harmonious aroma, characterized by distinct roasted, smoky, and matured seafood notes.

FAA and TAV

FAA are important component of proteins and have a significant impact on the meat taste [51]. This study characterized the FAA composition of microwave-dried shrimp subjected to different pretreatments. The results showed that the TAV value of glutamic acid (Glu) was consistently greater than 1 in all groups (Table 2). Umami was a crucial factor in enhancing the palatability of food, and the presence of Glu assisted in the presentation of umami in dried shrimp [52]. Amino acids served as crucial precursors in Maillard reaction, playing a vital role in the formation of volatile flavor compounds and the development of browning in dried shrimp. Microwave heating accelerated the oxidative degradation of proteins and peptides, thereby promoting the formation of FAA [53]. It was noteworthy that glycine (Gly) and serine (Ser) could generate pleasant caramel and baked flavors through their participation in Maillard reaction [54]. Compared with PS, the combined content of Gly and Ser in NS, PAP, UT, and UAP decreased by 9.43%, 13.61%, 17.47%, and 28.13%, respectively (p < 0.05, Table 1), which was consistent with the flavor analysis results. This results indicated that UAP pretreatment not only improved tenderness but also promoted protein degradation and the conversion of Maillard reaction precursors. Ultrasound and papain pretreatment altered the structure of myofibrillar proteins and might have broken them down into small peptide. Consequently, these pretreatment process significantly promoted the extent of protein degradation and the participation of the resulting compounds in the Maillard reaction during subsequent microwave heating.

FAA are among the key factors directly influencing the taste of dried shrimp. Sweet free amino acids (SFAA) were the most abundant category in the dried shrimp products, followed by bitter free amino acids (BFAA) and umami free amino acids (UFAA) (Fig. 4a-c). This distribution was consistent with findings reported by Shi et al. [53] in dried shrimp. Different pretreatment methods resulted in varying degrees of change in UFAA (Glu; aspartic acid, Asp) content (115.93±0.99 mg/100 g ∼ 159.53±0.46 mg/100 g). Furthermore, the soaking step in the pretreatments significantly reduced the levels of SFAA such as Ser, Gly and proline (Pro) (p < 0.05, Fig. 4b). This reduction was primarily attributed to the consumption of amino acids in Maillard reaction during heating, as well as potential leaching losses during pretreatment [55]. All samples contained the eight essential amino acids (EAA) required by humans, but cysteine was not detected in any sample. Papain treatment (PAP and UAP) significantly promoted the production of EAA in dried shrimp and reduced their loss rate to a certain extent (p < 0.05, Fig. 4d). The UAP group had the highest total EAA content (361.27±22.96 mg/100 g), indicating that UAP products possessed superior nutritional quality in terms of essential amino acid provision [56]. In summary, ultrasound-assisted papain pretreatment modified the content and composition of FAA in dried shrimp and facilitated their participation in Maillard reaction, thereby enhancing the flavor and nutritional value of shrimp meat.

Physicochemical properties of LVPs

Analysis of total sulfhydryl

The primary components of LVPs had been identified as myofibrillar proteins (MPs) [57]. During microwave drying, rapid moisture evaporation caused muscle proteins to undergo aggregation, cross-linking, denaturation, and degradation, while the muscle fibers contracted, ultimately leading to increased toughness [58]. The total sulfhydryl content of proteins represented the sum of sulfhydryl groups exposed on the protein surface and those embedded within the molecular structure. Fig. 5a described the changes in total sulfhydryl content of samples following different pretreatments. Compared with PS, LVPs treated with ultrasound and papain (PAP, UT, UAP) exhibited increased sulfhydryl content (p < 0.05), with increases of 17.79%, 26.28%, and 23.86%, respectively. There was no significant difference between UT and UAP (p > 0.05). In studies on thawing and freeze-thaw cycles, sulfhydryl content had been widely used as an indicator of protein oxidation, which was associated with diminished freshness and the development of off-odors [59], [60], [61]. However, in the context of microwave drying, the increase in sulfhydryl content induced by ultrasonic and papain pretreatment was attributed to complex kinetic changes between protein oxidation and the unfolding of the LVPs spatial structure, with the latter being the predominant factor [9]. It was worth noting that the sulfhydryl content of NS also increased significantly by 9.71% compared with PS. The primary reason for the change was that low ionic strength NaCl (0.9%) promoted partial unfolding and denaturation of LVPs in heating environment exceeding 100°C, thereby exposing sulfhydryl sites. Liu et al. [62] also observed the same pattern when investigating the effect of ionic strength during the thermal processing of fish fillets. As seen in Fig. 5a, ultrasound pretreatments (UT: 25.44±0.35 nmol/mg prot; UAP: 24.95±0.91 nmol/mg prot) significantly increased the total sulfhydryl levels, which was likely caused by ultrasound-induced unfolding of the protein molecules. Changes in protein spatial conformation triggered subunit dissociation, disulfide bond breakage, and exposure of internal active sulfhydryl, culminating in a prominent increase in the total sulfhydryl content [63]. Pan et al. [7] investigated the effects of ultrasound on dry-cured pork and found that as ultrasound duration increased, the level of sulfhydryl exposure further increased.

Surface hydrophobicity

Hydrophobic interactions between the side chains of nonpolar amino acid residues were a major stabilizing force in the tertiary structure of proteins. Therefore, surface hydrophobicity could be employed to assess the degree of unfolding of protein structures [64]. In this study, the extent of surface hydrophobicity was determined by measuring the binding capacity of LVPs to BPB. Different from oxidative changes at lower temperatures, intense microwave heating markedly accelerated protein aggregation. This aggregation often caused hydrophobic side chains to cluster internally, which increased the hardness of the dried shrimp and reduced surface hydrophobicity [65]. As shown in Fig. 5b, the surface hydrophobicity of NS changed slightly. In contrast, the surface hydrophobicity of the ultrasound and papain pretreatment groups was significantly increased compared with PS (p < 0.05), with UAP (95.53±6.27 μg) showing the most pronounced increase. On the one hand, the cavitation effect generated by microbubbles could be utilized to disrupt intermolecular hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions. This promoted the exposure of hydrophobic aromatic amino groups and facilitated the disassembly and restructuring of LVPs aggregates [9], [63]. Simultaneously, enzymatic hydrolysis unfolded the protein chains [66]. The synergistic effect of ultrasound and papain ultimately resulted in the greatest increase in surface hydrophobicity of UAP (17.42%) compared with PS, while PAP and UT increased by 5.43% and 12.20%, respectively. This trend was consistent with the changes observed in total sulfhydryl content. In particular, surface hydrophobicity had been proven to play an important role in ketone binding. Its increase helped maintain the flavor of dried shrimp and prevent volatilization [67].

Structure of LVPs

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Fluorescence spectroscopy probed changes in the microenvironment of aromatic amino acids residues embedded in proteins and changes in intrinsic fluorescence intensity [24]. These measurements characterized the effects of different pretreatments on the tertiary structure of LVPs. As shown in Fig. 6a, PAP, UT, and UAP marginally increased the maximum emission wavelength (λmax) of LVPs, although the change was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The redshift of λmax suggested an increase in the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding tryptophan, indicating the unfolding of the LVPs tertiary structure and the exposure of these residues to the polar environment [23]. The maximum fluorescence intensity varied across the different pretreatment groups. Compared with PS, there was no significant change in fluorescence intensity in NS (p > 0.05), whereas it increased significantly in the PAP, UT, and UAP groups (p < 0.05, Fig. 6a). Among these, UAP exhibited the most pronounced increase in fluorescence intensity, which was consistent with the observed increases in surface hydrophobicity and total sulfhydryl content. It was potentially attributed to the cavitation and mechanical effects of ultrasound, which could promote the unfolding of the tertiary structure of proteins, leading to the exposure of tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine residues [11]. Simultaneously, papain was able to effectively degrade LVPs, which also promoted peptide chain unfolding and structural loosening [68]. In the UAP group, the synergistic effect of ultrasound and papain induced extensive unfolding of the tertiary structure and a pronounced loosening of the molecular conformation. It could be concluded that PAP/UT could effectively alter the tertiary or quaternary structure of LVPs, causing sulfhydryl and hydrophobic groups to be exposed. This structural loosening of LVPs was manifested macroscopically as reduced shear resistance, which corresponded to the improved tenderness measured in the texture analysis.

FTIR

FTIR spectroscopy was a widely used technique for investigating the chemical composition and conformational structure of proteins, which was equipped to detect changes in protein secondary structure. Due to the presence of C=O and NH- bonds involved in hydrogen bonding, the amide I band (1600-1700 cm-1) was quite sensitive to changes in protein secondary structure [65], [69]. It was typically used to analyze changes in the coiling and folding of protein secondary structures. Following intense microwave drying, the proportion of α-helix in LVPs decreased significantly [65]. The FTIR spectra of PS, NS, PAP, UT, and UAP were presented in Fig. 6b. The tenderization pretreatments had a profound effect on the secondary conformation of LVPs. The α-helix content decreased in all pretreated samples, while the random coil and β-turn contents increased relatively. Specifically, the combined content of β-sheet and α-helix decreased by 2.52%, 12.45%, and 15.73% in the PAP, UT, and UAP groups, respectively (p < 0.05, Fig. 6c). The decline in α-helix content indicated a weakening of hydrogen bonds, which promoted the unfolding of the protein backbone and increased the exposure of hydrophobic groups. This interpretation was associated with the surface hydrophobicity results [70]. Conversely, the combined content of random coil and β-turn increased by 3.77%, 18.64%, and 23.55% in PAP, UT, and UAP respectively (p < 0.05, Fig. 6c).The cavitation effect of ultrasound accelerated this process. Amiri et al. [66] reported that this structural transformation became more pronounced with prolonged ultrasound exposure times. Similarly, papain hydrolysis could also promote the unfolding of LVPs chains and the breaking of hydrogen bonds, although its effect appeared to be more limited to the transition from α-helix to β-sheet. The synergistic action of ultrasound and papain in UAP drove the conversion of ordered structures into more disordered conformations. It ultimately caused the secondary structure of proteins to become more flexible and less ordered, and the muscle fiber network to loosen. The overall shift from a rigid, ordered state to a more flexible and open conformation stretched the muscle fibers, which ultimately contributed to the improved tenderness of the dried shrimp [23].

SDS-PAGE

The primary structure of LVPs determined their two-dimensional and three-dimensional structures and ultimately influenced the functional state of muscle proteins [71]. Fig. 7 presented the SDS-PAGE patterns of proteins extracted from microwave-dried shrimp subjected to different pretreatments. All treatment groups exhibited numerous bands ranging from 10 to 250 kDa, with the darker bands corresponding to actin (46-48 kDa), tropomyosin (35-39 kDa), and myosin light chain (MLC, 19-24 kDa), respectively [22]. Myosin heavy chain (MHC), C-protein, and other macromolecular proteins underwent noticeable degradation after prolonged microwave heating, consistent with the findings of Gao et al. [72]. The overall banding patterns of the NS, PAP, UT, and UAP groups showed no major differences from that of the PS group. It indicated that the pretreatments did not cause extensive proteolysis which would significantly alter the primary structure of the major myofibrillar proteins. The intensity of MLC bands appeared slightly greater in UAP, which were usually considered to be the enzymatic degradation products of MHC [65]. In contrast, the individual PAP and UT treatments had a more limited impact on the protein electrophoretic profile. The synergy in UAP could be attributed to two main factors: the complementary disruptive effects of ultrasound and papain on myofibrillar architecture, and ultra-sonication boosted enzyme penetration and intracellular enzyme activity [12]. Therefore, UAP accelerated the degradation of larger proteins, potentially leading to an accumulation of intermediate-sized fragments or a relative increase in the abundance of stable proteins like MLC. Higher levels of protein degradation were also widely associated with improved meat tenderness, as the structural integrity and toughness of muscle fibers and connective tissue were weakened [73], [74]. Furthermore, microwave drying participated in and influenced alterations to the amino acid and peptide composition of prawns, thereby fostering the formation of baking flavors such as pyrazines.

Correlation analysis

The relationships among various indicators of microwave-dried shrimp after ultrasound-assisted papain pretreatment were established through cross-validation using Pearson correlation and PLS-R analysis (Fig. 8a, b). As shown in Fig. 8b, principal components PC1 and PC2 collectively explained 82% of the total variance, indicating the model possessed good fit and high reliability [75]. The results showed that the shear force of prawns was extremely significantly positively correlated with α-helix (p < 0.01). Conversely, it was significantly negatively correlated with fluorescence intensity, total sulfhydryl content, random coil, β-turn, and surface hydrophobicity (p < 0.01, p < 0.05). It was suggested that the synergistic effect of papain and ultrasound promoted the loosening of the LVPs structure, which in turn disrupted the meat fiber integrity and led to reduced shear force. The research conducted by Wang et al. [76] also supported this conclusion and found that there might be a positive correlation between tertiary structure and ultrasound treatment time. Similarly, TBARS showed a highly significant positive correlation with shear force (p < 0.01). On the one hand, it might be related to the antioxidant peptides produced during the UAP pretreatment stage. On the other hand, a higher degree of lipid oxidation could regulate calcium-activated enzyme activity and had a certain negative impact on the tenderness development of raw meat [77]. Regarding flavor development, 2-pentanone was highly significantly positively correlated with 2-ethylpyrazine (p < 0.01), but highly significantly negatively correlated with nonanal (p < 0.01), a compound primarily derived from lipid oxidation. Concurrently, acetic acid exhibited a significant positive correlation with 2-pentanone and β-turn (p < 0.05). The transformation of flavor compounds ultimately promoted the formation of UAP roasted nutty, and sweet flavor. The richness of the product was positively correlated with bitterness (p < 0.05), and showed significant positive correlations with Arg, Pro, and α-helix (p < 0.01, p < 0.05). It exhibited significant negative correlations with total sulfhydryl and random coil (p < 0.01, p < 0.05). In summary, the structural changes in LVPs caused by UAP and microwave heating not only improved tenderness but also effectively modulated the flavor profile and amino acid composition without compromising umami perception [78].

Conclusion

This study evaluated the improvement of tenderness and flavor of microwave-dried Litopenaeus vannamei through UAP. UAP integrated the advantages of papain and ultrasound, with ultrasound accelerating enzyme penetration, leading to more uniform tenderization. PAP, UT and UAP promoted the participation of FAA such as Gly and Ser in the Maillard reaction during subsequent microwave heating, which led to the increased formation of pyrazines and 2-pentanone, contributing to more intense roasted and caramel flavor notes. All pretreated samples maintained a desirable umami taste. Furthermore, UAP induced the unfolding of the tertiary protein structure and facilitated the conversion of α-helix to β-turn and random coils. Concurrently, the degradation of LVPs primary structures reduced the shear resistance of dried shrimp while concurrently enhancing flavor. In conclusion, integrated analysis of LVPs structural changes and multivariate correlation analysis demonstrated that UAP was an effective method for synergistically improving the tenderness and flavor of microwave-dried shrimp. We will conduct further research to determine the optimal process conditions such as the ultrasonic parameters and enzyme treatment temperature of microwave-dried shrimp.
