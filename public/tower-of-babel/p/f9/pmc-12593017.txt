Effect of Pyrolysis Temperature and Atmosphere on the Pyrolysis Characteristics of Cold-Rolled Oily Sludge with a High Oil Content
Authors: Mengtao Fan, Xiaoyan Li, Chi Jia, Liqiu Feng, Mengmeng Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An innovative pyrolysis strategy has been proposed for oil sludge in which the flue gas with a low oxygen content from the cement kiln tail is used as the protective atmosphere. However, the impact of cement kiln flue gas on the pyrolysis of oil sludge remains unclear and needs intensive study. Taking the cold-rolled oily sludge with a high oil content as the object, the effects of pyrolysis temperature (400–600 °C) and atmospheres (N2, CO2, 64% N2/36%CO2, and 64%N2/30%CO2/6%O2) on the pyrolysis behaviors were investigated. GC, GC-MS, and SARA analyses were adopted to evaluate gas composition and oil properties besides the pyrolysis product yields. Under N2 atmosphere, as the pyrolysis temperature increases within the scope involved, the yield of pyrolysis gas gradually increases, while the yield of pyrolysis oil increases first and then decreases. When the pyrolysis temperature ranges from 500 to 550 °C, the pyrolysis oil yield exceeds 60% and the oil recovery rate reaches over 85%. The yield and quality of pyrolysis products achieve the optimal balance at 550 °C. Among the four atmospheres, the CO2 atmosphere results in the lowest yield of pyrolysis oil and the highest yield of pyrolysis gas. The simulated atmosphere of 64% N2/30% CO2/6% O2 witnesses the maximum release amounts of H2 and CO during the pyrolysis process. Preliminary results indicate that it is feasible to utilize the flue gas from the cement kiln tail for the direct pyrolysis of oily sludge.

Introduction

Approximately 60 million tons of oily sludge (OS) is generated annually by the global petroleum industry. Oily sludge is composed of petroleum hydrocarbons, water, and mineral impurities, containing diverse contaminants such as organic pollutants, toxic metals, and bacteria. Improper disposal may lead to adverse environmental impacts. Oily sludge with a high oil content exhibits significant potential for resource recovery.

Pyrolysis technology has emerged as an efficient and environmentally friendly approach for OS treatment, characterized by minimized secondary pollution and high recovery rates of oil/gas products. The pyrolysis process induces the cracking of long-chain alkanes and unstable O/N-containing compounds in OS, generating olefins, alicyclic hydrocarbons, aromatic compounds, and more stable heteroatomic compounds. The resultant products are composed of pyrolysis oil, pyrolysis gas, and pyrolysis residue. He et al. conducted pyrolysis experiments on three types of oily sludges at 700 °C and compared the properties of pyrolysis oil with those of oil extracted via Soxhlet extraction. The results showed that pyrolysis promoted the cracking of long-chain alkanes, accompanied by conjugation, cyclization, and aromatization reactions. Notably, the pyrolysis oil exhibited lower viscosity and higher calorific value than the raw OS.

Pyrolysis of OS is influenced by parameters including temperature, duration, heating rate, atmosphere, and OS characteristics, among which temperature exhibits the greatest impact. , Oil recovery rate of 70–84% was achieved through pyrolysis at 460–550 °C under nitrogen atmosphere by Schmidt et al.

Previous research on pyrolysis atmospheres has predominantly focused on a single inert atmosphere. The influence of N2 atmosphere has been extensively studied while CO2 atmosphere exerts differential effects on the pyrolysis of oily sludge. Gong et al. investigated the effects of different heating rates and atmospheres (N2 and CO2 atmospheres) on the pyrolysis of OS and results showed that fast pyrolysis under CO2 atmosphere enhanced the conversion of macromolecules into short-chain methyl compounds, increasing the yield of CH4. Under CO2 atmosphere, the primary mass loss occurred at 300–500 °C, accompanied by hydrocarbon cracking and gas production. Above 400 °C, CO2 facilitated thermal decomposition and dehydrogenation of volatiles, producing more H2 and light hydrocarbons while generating CO. The CO formation occurred through homogeneous reactions between CO2 and volatiles, with CO2 serving as the exclusive oxygen donor. In the case of organic solid waste decomposition in a CO2 atmosphere, the tars produced will react further with CO2 to significantly increase the pyrolysis gas yield. Furthermore, CO2 inhibits the formation of harmful substances such as dioxins and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) during the pyrolysis process.

Cement industry exhibits outstanding advantages in the collaborative disposal of municipal sludge, household waste, and hazardous wastes. Given that the CO2 concentration in cement kiln tail gas can reach up to 30% or higher, an innovative strategy has been proposed to replace traditional inert atmospheres with cement industrial flue gas for OS pyrolysis. The abovementioned method combining low-temperature pyrolysis of oil sludge and fuel substitution for cement production has a potential application value. Specifically, on the one hand, the pyrolysis products of oil sludge are used as fuel substitutes in the cement industry, with pyrolysis oil and gas being sent to the rotary kiln and precalciner, respectively. On the other hand, the flue gas with low oxygen content from the preheater tower of the cement kiln is used as a protective atmosphere during the oil sludge pyrolysis process. The challenge lies in understanding how the flue gas affects pyrolysis processes when it is used as the reaction atmosphere, in addition to fundamental studies on OS pyrolysis behavior. Currently, there are no published reports on the use of cement kiln tail gas as the pyrolysis atmosphere for oil sludge.

This work investigated the pyrolytic characteristics of cold-rolled oily sludge (CROS), focusing on the influence of the pyrolysis temperature and atmosphere on the pyrolysis behavior. Four atmospheres covered pure N2, pure CO2, 64% N2/36% CO2 mixture, and simulated cement kiln flue gas (64% N2/30% CO2/6% O2) to make comparisons step by step. The quality evolution of pyrolysis oil and gas obtained was discussed in detail.

Experimental Setup and Methods

The experimental apparatus (Figure ) consisted of a gas delivery system, a tubular electric furnace, a temperature controller, a quartz reactor, a condensation unit, and a gas collection device. Prior to experiments, CROS was dried at 105 °C for 2 h. Approximately 4 g of dried CROS was loaded into a quartz boat and placed in the isothermal zone of the reactor, with temperature monitored via a thermocouple at the reactor bottom. A fixed flow rate of 200 mL/min was used to blow high-purity gas into the quartz tube to ensure a predetermined atmosphere. The furnace was heated to 400–600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, and the sample was maintained at a target temperature for 20 min. Gas purging continued until the setup was cooled to room temperature after the pyrolysis process. Gaseous products were condensed into oil in a U-shaped condenser, while noncondensable gases were dried and collected in gas bags. Pyrolytic residue was collected from the quartz boat after cooling.

Schematic diagram of the pyrolysis experimental apparatus.

Experimental Condition

The pyrolysis experiments were conducted in two groups. The first group employed a N2 atmosphere at temperatures of 400 °C (400-N2), 450 °C (450-N2), 500 °C (500-N2), 550 °C (550-N2), and 600 °C (600-N2). The second group was carried out under different atmospheres: CO2 (500-CO2), 64% N2/36%CO2 (500-C/N), and 64% N2/30% CO2/6% O2 (500-C/N/O) at a fixed temperature of 500 °C.

Methods of Analysis

The oil, moisture, and solid contents of CROS were determined by the azeotropic distillation method using petroleum ether (boiling range: 60–90 °C) as the solvent. Elemental analysis of CROS was conducted with an Elementar Unicube analyzer (EA). SARA analysis was conducted on the pyrolysis oil and CROSO (CROS-derived oil from azeotropic distillation). The four components of SARA include saturate, aromatic hydrocarbons, resin, and asphaltene, and their content distribution determines the quality of the recovered oil. The column chromatography was employed in the SARA analysis for the CROSO and pyrolyzed oil samples. Asphaltenes were precipitated with n-hexane and filtered, followed by sequential elution of the filtrate using organic solvents with increasing polarity to separate saturates, aromatics, and resins. Mass fractions were determined gravimetrically after solvent evaporation.

The yields of pyrolysis products were calculated according to eqs –3. 1 Yoil=MoilMCROS×100% 2 YRes=MResMCROS×100% 3 Ygas=100%−Yoil−YRes

Y oil, Y Res, and Y gas refer to the oil yield, residue yield, and gas yield, where M CROS, M oil, and M Res correspond to the mass (g) of CROS, pyrolysis oil, and residue.

GC-MS (Agilent 7890A GC-5975C MS) was utilized to characterize the main compounds and the carbon number distribution in the pyrolysis oil. GC (Agilent 7890B) was used to analyze volume fractions of gas components, with FID quantifying organic gases (CH4, C2H4, C2H6, C3H6, C3H8 (C2–C3)) and TCD detecting inorganic species (H2, CO2, O2, N2, CO).

Results and Discussion

Effect of Temperature on the Yield and Characteristics of Pyrolysis Products

Figure illustrates the influence of pyrolysis temperature on the yields of CROS pyrolysis products under a N2 atmosphere. The yield of pyrolysis gas exhibited an increasing trend, while the residue yield decreased progressively with rising temperature. The oil yield initially increased to a maximum at 500 °C (62.56%) before declining, corresponding to an oil recovery efficiency of 87.74% based on the original oil content of 71.3% in CROS. Notably, residue yields of 53.62% and 31.49% were observed at 400 and 450 °C, which were significantly higher than the solid content of 25.50% obtained through azeotropic distillation, indicating incomplete pyrolysis. When the temperature reached 500 °C, the residue yield decreased to 25.38% and stabilized at 25.02% within the temperature range of 550–600 °C. Meanwhile, the gas yield increased from 12.06% to 23.68% from 500 to 600 °C. Previous studies demonstrated that oil-phase formation occurred at 200–500 °C, while secondary cracking of macromolecular volatiles generated light oil and small-molecule noncondensable gases above this temperature range. , Optimal pyrolysis performance was achieved at 500–550 °C with an oil recovery rate of 85.89–87.74% when the oil recovery rate was preferentially considered as the evaluation index.

Product yields at different pyrolysis temperatures under a N2 atmosphere.

Figure shows the influence of the pyrolysis temperature on the carbon atom distribution and organic compounds in pyrolysis oil products. The organic compounds were categorized as hydrocarbons, ketones, esters, and other substances (furans and oxygen-containing heterocyclic compounds). As can be seen in Figure a, the organic matter in pyrolysis oil was predominantly composed of heavy oil fractions (C ≥ 20) with a relative content of 57.21% at 400 °C. As the pyrolysis temperature increased from 400 to 550 °C, the long-chain hydrocarbons (C ≥ 20) reduced, while the contents of C ≤ 10 and C10–15 fractions increased to 10.12% and 44.56%, respectively. When the pyrolysis temperature was further increased from 550 to 600 °C, the compounds with carbon chains longer than 15 (C > 15) increased. The reason was that high temperatures induced the primary cracking of heavy hydrocarbons via the cleavage of weak bonds (e.g., C–C side chains), forming long-chain alkane intermediates.

Effect of pyrolysis temperature on the carbon number distribution (a) and main organic components (b) of pyrolysis oil products.

As shown in Figure b, the proportion of hydrocarbon substances generally exceeded 50%, and the contents of ketone substances were between 15% and 20%. At 550 °C, the proportions of hydrocarbon and ketone substances in the pyrolysis oil reached their peak values, which were 71.84% and 17.91%, respectively, indicating a good quality of the pyrolysis oil. However, as the pyrolysis temperature further rose to 600 °C, the proportion of hydrocarbon substances dropped significantly to a minimum of 48.34%, while the proportion of other substances increased significantly. The results reveal that pyrolysis enhances short-chain hydrocarbon formation and increased temperature promotes ring opening of cyclic compounds to form alkanes, which subsequently crack into smaller molecules. This process is accompanied by the decomposition of long-chain organics and components (e.g., resins, asphaltenes) into saturated hydrocarbons. Besides, the observed decrease in ester contents from 9.85% at 450 °C to 1.74% at 500 °C suggests thermal conversion of esters to hydrocarbons via decarboxylation or decarbonylation pathways. Rapid volatilization and carrier gas removal of small-molecule products inhibit deep cracking, preserving these intermediates, , which leads to the decreased hydrocarbon proportion and increased other substances.

Figure gives the photos of SARA fractions from CROSO and pyrolysis oil dissolved in chloroform at mass-proportional volume ratios (quantitative data are shown in Table ). SARA contents revealed that the saturated hydrocarbons constituted 56.06% in the pyrolysis oil at 550 °C, significantly higher than 44.81% in CROSO. Notably, the asphaltene content decreased sharply from 15.33% in CROSO to 1.50% in the pyrolysis oil at 550 °C, with slight increases in aromatic hydrocarbons and reductions in resin contents observed. It was worth mentioning that asphaltene content exhibited a significant increase at 600 °C (Figure ). This phenomenon is generally attributed to incomplete cracking of macromolecules at elevated temperatures, when bond cleavage triggers secondary reactions such as cyclization and conjugation. The observed increase in asphaltenes may result from the condensation of low-carbon-chain molecules into high-molecular-weight compounds coupled with intensified dehydrogenation, condensation, and polymerization reactions.

The release amounts of H2, CO, CH4, and C2–C4 in pyrolysis gas at different pyrolysis temperatures are shown in Figure a. The compositions of pyrolysis gases are presented in Figure b. The volume fractions of gas components were quantitatively determined using gas chromatography (GC) analysis after calibration with standard curves and normalization of peak areas. H2 and CO have been identified as critical factors for improving gas quality. As the temperature increased, the total gas release amount initially rose and then fell, reaching a maximum of 194.55 mL/g at 550 °C. Specifically, H2 and CO amounts reached their maxima of 53.82 and 48.2 mL/g at 550 °C. The release amount of CH4 varied insignificantly. The release amounts of C2–C4 increased rapidly before 550 °C and then declined slightly at 600 °C. Between 450 and 550 °C, enhanced C–C bond cleavage and dehydrogenation in C2–C4 converted these compounds into H2 and CO, reducing their proportion. At 600 °C, the slight increase in C2–C4 content resulted from the cracking of previously refractory macromolecules, meanwhile C2–C4 generation rate surpassed consumption by secondary reactions. Optimal gas quality was achieved at 550 °C with H2 and CO proportions accounting for 27.24% and 25.76%, respectively.

Figure illustrates the multistep pathways for pyrolysis gas generation. The increase in the CO content originated from the reaction of CO2 (a pyrolysis product of oxygen-containing functional groups in CROS) with coke and water vapor at elevated temperatures, generating CO and H2. The rise in H2 content was driven by two pathways, the first was the break of C–H bonds releasing H atoms, followed by cyclization and aromatization of olefins to form H2; the other was the secondary aromatization reactions of small molecules generated from pyrolysis under high temperatures. The pyrolysis gas was predominantly composed of alkanes, which could undergo dehydrogenation to form olefins, alkynes, and H2.

Effect of Atmosphere on the Yield and Characteristics of Pyrolysis Products

The influence of the atmosphere on the pyrolysis product yield at 500 °C is depicted in Figure . The residue yield showed slight variation under different atmospheres. Compared with N2 atmosphere, a lower oil yield (55.00%) and a higher gas yield (18.50%) were achieved under CO2 atmosphere, attributable to the reaction between CO2 and cracking products (specifically olefins and free radicals) generated from long-chain alkane cracking, which produced CO and new free radicals. Under a 64%N2/36%CO2 mixed atmosphere, the dilution effect of nitrogen attenuated the promoting effect of CO2 on pyrolysis gas generation. Under a 64% N2/36% CO2/6% O2 atmosphere, in addition to the positive effect of CO2 on pyrolysis gas production, O2 underwent oxidative reactions with specific components in CROS. These reactions promoted the cleavage of macromolecular hydrocarbons into small-molecule gaseous products through oxidative cleavage mechanisms, while simultaneously consuming oil-phase components within CROS. This dual effect diminished the availability of oil-forming precursors, ultimately leading to a relatively lower yield of pyrolysis oil.

Product yields under different pyrolysis atmospheres at 500 °C.

Figure displays the effect of the pyrolysis atmosphere on the main organic components in pyrolysis oil products. The atmospheric conditions exhibited limited impact on the primary organic components of pyrolysis oil, keeping hydrocarbon contents above 70% under all conditions. Among these, a 64%N2/36%CO2 atmosphere achieved the highest hydrocarbon proportion at 72.57%, with alcohols, esters, and aldehydes remaining relatively stable at concentrations below 5%. Figure reveals SARA results of pyrolysis oil obtained from different atmospheres, with detailed quantitative data also summarized in Table . As shown in Table , the aromatic hydrocarbon content in pyrolysis oil increased to 31.82%, 29.52%, and 30.46% under CO2, 64%N2/36%CO2, and 64%N2/30%CO2/6%O2 atmospheres, respectively, which were higher than that of 26.90% under N2 atmosphere. Notably, all values exceeded the aromatic content (26.65%) in CROSO. This result could be attributed to reaction pathways involving dehydrogenation, deoxygenation, cyclization, and polycondensation processes during aromatic hydrocarbon generation. , The results suggest that CO2-containing atmospheres could alter the redox environment, potentially promoting the cyclization of aliphatic hydrocarbons and converting them into aromatic hydrocarbons.

Figure a illustrates the release amounts of H2, CO, CH4, and C2–C4 gas under different pyrolysis atmospheres. Figure b shows the gas composition. Due to the large amount of N2 and CO2 in the pyrolysis gas brought by the carrier gas, data processing involved normalization after excluding these components. Compared with N2 atmosphere, the C2–C4 amount decreased to 26.29 mL/g, while H2 and CO amounts increased to 37.73 and 25.19 mL/g under a CO2 atmosphere. This enhancement derives from the dual role of CO2: acting as the oxygen donor for CO formation through the carbon gasification reaction and promoting dehydrogenation of volatile organic compounds to generate H2. The intermediate H2 and CO amounts were observed under a 64%N2/36%CO2 atmosphere due to the dilution effect of N2. It was worth noting that the release of H2 and CO reached their peaks (54.39 and 47.19 mL/g) under the stimulated atmosphere of 64% N2/30% CO2/6% O2, while the release amount of CH4 and C2–C4 gases was highest under N2 atmosphere. It was obvious that the introduction of CO2 changed the distribution of gas products, significantly reducing C2–C4 and CH4 release in pyrolysis gas, while increasing H2 and CO release. The reason for these results is the carbon gasification reaction between CO2 and pyrolysis char at elevated temperatures, producing CO and H2, as well as the interaction of CO2 with carboxylic acids and esters derived from CROS pyrolysis processes, which leads to additional CO formation. It is worth noting that a 64%N2/30%CO2/6%O2 atmosphere achieved the optimal gas quality with the highest H2 proportion of 39.50% and a CO proportion of 34.09%, indicating that limited oxygen supply effectively enhanced partial oxidation reactions. These findings are consistent with Kim’s research. , The feasibility of cement kiln flue gas (64%N2/30%CO2/6%O2) as the pyrolysis carrier gas has been preliminarily verified.

Employing CO2 as the reaction medium has been proved to improve the thermal efficiency and suppress the formation of harmful chemical compounds during the pyrolysis of biomass and municipal solid wastes. , With the help of CO2, CO generation is also enhanced from thermal cracking of volatile organic carbons (VOCs) or the direct reaction between VOCs and CO2. During this process, CO2 acts as an additional carbon and oxygen provider, contributing to the significant increase in CO production due to the gasification of CO2 with carbon and the increasing total gas yield. Additionally, oxidative pyrolysis, introducing a determined amount of oxygen into the pyrolysis process, has been adopted to make the pyrolysis process autothermal. Oxygen plays a key role in controlling the gas fractions. The increase in hydrogen production can be attributed to the presence of oxygen, which plays a crucial role in promoting the cleavage of C–H bonds, resulting in the formation of hydrogen. The flue gas from the cement kiln tail contains CO2 (approximately 30–36%) and a certain amount of oxygen (2–6%). Utilizing the cement kiln flue gas for pyrolysis offers a viable solution to enhance carbon utilization and facilitate gas production.

Conclusion

The impact of pyrolysis temperature and atmosphere on both yields and physicochemical properties of pyrolysis products from cold-rolling oil sludge was investigated. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:1.Under nitrogen atmosphere, the yield and quality of pyrolysis products achieve the optimal balance at a pyrolysis temperature of 550 °C. The yields of pyrolysis oil and pyrolysis gas are 61.24% and 13.74%, respectively. The proportions of H2 and CO in the pyrolysis gas are 27.67% and 24.77%. The short-chain substances (C ≤ 15) in the pyrolysis oil account for 54.68%, and hydrocarbons account for 71.84%. Compared with the oil extracted by azeotropic distillation from oily sludge, SARA analysis shows that the saturated hydrocarbon content of the pyrolysis oil increases by 11.25%, and the asphaltene content decreases by 13.83%, significantly improving the quality of the oil product.2.Compared with N2 atmosphere, CO2 atmosphere reduces the yield of pyrolysis oil but increases that of pyrolysis gas. More importantly, the introduction of CO2 promotes the production of H2 and CO in the pyrolysis gas and improves the pyrolysis gas quality. The pyrolysis performance under the atmosphere of 64% N2/36% CO2 falls between those under N2 and CO2 atmosphere. The pyrolysis of oily sludge under the simulated atmosphere of 64% N2/30% CO2/6% O2 witnesses the maximum release amounts of H2 and CO, along with the highest H2 proportion of 39.50% and CO proportion of 34.09%.3.Under 64% N2/30% CO2/6% O2 mixed atmosphere, CO2 promotes the formation of CO and H2 and facilitates the conversion of certain paraffins into aromatic hydrocarbons; while a small amount of O2 promotes partial oxidation reactions, thereby enhancing CO production.

Future Research Outlook

To realize the application of low-oxygen flue gas in the pyrolysis of oily sludge and promote fuel substitution in the cement industry, further research that needs to be conducted includes the following:1.The synergistic effect between pyrolysis atmosphere and catalysts is very important to reduce pyrolysis temperature and directionally regulate the product distribution. Efficient catalysts for oil sludge pyrolysis should be developed.2.In addition to the components mentioned above, the flue gas from the cement kiln tail also contains SO2 and NO2. More attention should be paid to the pollutant migration and control during the pyrolysis process.3.Based on solid waste disposal in the cement industry, the copyrolysis behavior and mechanism of oil sludge and other solid wastes (such as waste textiles, plastics, and tires) should be studied. The goal of increasing the recovery rate and improving the quality of pyrolysis oil and gas can be achieved through the complementary action of raw materials.
