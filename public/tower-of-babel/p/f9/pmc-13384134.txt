Implementing and evaluating Adaptive Mentorship Networks to strengthen primary care in Canada: protocol for a mixed-methods implementation science study
Authors: Arun Radhakrishnan, L Jayne Beselt, Alesha C King, Zachary van Allen, Douglas Archibald, Jerry Maniate, Samuel Hickcox, Cristian Rangel, Joshua A Rash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionCanada’s health workforce crisis is placing sustained pressure on primary care, particularly in the management of chronic pain, mental health conditions and substance use disorders. Mentoring is a compelling response to workforce, educational and well-being challenges in healthcare, and Adaptive Mentorship Networks (AMNs) have emerged in Canada as an interprofessional model to strengthen provider capacity, psychological safety and compassionate care. Despite widespread interest and demonstrable effectiveness, the determinants that influence successful implementation, spread, adaptation and sustainability of AMNs remain insufficiently understood.Methods and analysisThis 3-year mixed-methods protocol will evaluate implementation of 11 AMNs that are established or in development across Canada. The study will focus on two complementary levels: (1) implementation of AMNs within healthcare ecosystems; and (2) capabilities, opportunities and motivations that contribute to adaptive mentorship between mentors and mentees. The overarching approach to understand the determinants of successful implementation and behavioural influences on adaptive mentorship uses implementation science and systems thinking methodologies to support a pan-Canadian Learning Health System. Work will proceed in three phases. Phase I will explore determinants of implementation (phase Ia) and behavioural influences on adaptive mentorship (phase Ib) through document review, interviews, systems mapping and readiness assessment. Phase II will use a Learning Alliance to identify, select and operationalise fit-for-purpose implementation strategies. Phase III will co-develop AMN-specific logic models and evaluate the feasibility, acceptability and initial utility of selected strategies. The study will be guided by the Active Implementation Framework, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs, the Capabilities, Opportunities and Motivation for Behaviour model, the Theoretical Domains Framework and the Health Equity Implementation Framework. Outputs from phase Ia will provide a compendium of determinants critical for the implementation of mentorship networks within healthcare arranged by stage of implementation, and the activities that current networks have taken to influence such determinants. Outputs from phase Ib will identify the capabilities, opportunities and motivations deemed critical for facilitating engagement in the act of clinical mentorship. Phase II will identify a contextualised understanding of strategies that are optimally suited to influence critical determinants of implementing AMNs and engaging in clinical mentorship. Our outputs from phases I and II will be pilot tested with each AMN and inform the development of site-specific logic models and a cross-site implementation blueprint that will be evaluated in phase III. This protocol advances a systems-informed approach to implementing AMNs across diverse Canadian health systems, with implications for mentorship-enabled workforce development.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been obtained from the Bruyère Health Research Ethics Board (M16-25-001 and M16-25-002) and the Newfoundland and Labrador Health Research Ethics Board (Ref# 2025.047). Participants provide informed consent prior to participating in study interviews acknowledging potential risks and benefits to participation. Findings will be mobilised through integrated knowledge mobilisation with participating AMNs, members of the Learning Alliance, policy and practice partners, conference presentations, publications, AI-enabled knowledge sharing tools and plain-language resources.Keywords: Implementation Science, Primary Care, Chronic Pain, MENTAL HEALTH

Introduction

Background and rationale

The Canadian health workforce crisis is a multifaceted issue with several interconnected challenges. Key issues include a shortage of healthcare professionals, geographic disparities, an ageing workforce, poor optimisation and coordination of roles, increasing complexity of chronic health conditions, outdated healthcare service delivery models and threats to the well-being of practitioners.1,3 The primary care crisis in Canada is an important part of the broader health workforce crisis.4 5 Primary care is especially affected by the increased complexity of health conditions and by inadequate levels of interprofessional collaboration. The inadequacy of collaboration and system coordination is particularly apparent in the management of people living with mental health conditions, substance use disorders and chronic pain.6,8 With an ageing population, it is expected that the demands of complexity, limited resources and poor access will add to burnout and attrition of the workforce through moral distress, diminishing psychological safety and poor well-being.2 6 National and regional efforts to increase interprofessional team-based care are intended to address some of the issues within the primary care and larger health workforce crisis.5 9

Addressing the health workforce and primary care crisis requires government policy changes, investment, education and training, improved working conditions and continuing supports for clinicians managing complex chronic conditions.1 3 5 9 Mentoring is viewed as a compelling solution that can address multiple issues related to recruitment, retention, professional development and the well-being of healthcare professionals.10,13 The importance of mentorship is also emphasised through its endorsement within health professional training and competency frameworks.14 15 Adaptive mentoring, the model used in this study, is characterised by mentorship that adapts to mentee needs, compassionate provider communities and bidirectional value for mentors and mentees.16 17

Adaptive Mentorship Networks (AMNs) have been used in Canada at the national and regional levels to build capacity and improve healthcare provider well-being.18,21 At a national level, the AMN intervention is being used to support the development of interprofessional team-based primary care that is equitable and accessible. At a provincial level, clinically focused AMNs have been implemented to build the capacity of primary care providers to manage complexity associated with chronic pain, mental health and substance use disorders through engagement in interprofessional mentoring.18,21 Participation in these voluntary networks involves recruiting mentees through health professional associations, academic institutions and health authorities, matching mentees to mentors in similar geographic regions, and supporting one-to-one, small-group and peer mentoring through virtual and in-person interactions.18,21 Small group meetings and shared platforms foster a sense of community that resembles a community of practice.18 22 Currently eight active AMNs include 42 mentors and 650 mentees across multiple provinces and operating in a variety of organisational types (eg, research institutes, health authorities).

Recent cross-network survey findings from AMN mentees in British Columbia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador and Prince Edward Island further suggest that these networks operate in settings marked by substantial barriers to specialty access, non-pharmacological treatment coverage and care coordination.23 Evaluations of AMNs have demonstrated improvements in knowledge, confidence, care focused on compassion, interprofessional collaboration, participant resiliency and professional satisfaction.18,21 Despite widespread interest and demonstrable effectiveness, we do not yet have a scientific understanding of the determinants that influence successful implementation of AMNs in a manner that facilitates delivery of effective adaptive mentorship.18,21 This protocol seeks to fill that gap.

Methods and analysis

Study design and overarching research approach

Our work will focus on two complementary levels: (1) implementation of AMNs within healthcare systems and (2) understanding the capabilities, opportunities and motivations that contribute to adaptive mentorship between mentors and mentees across participating AMNs. This study will be conducted in three phases over 3 years.

The overarching scientific approach uses implementation science and systems thinking methodologies to support a Learning Health System. The process of implementation will be understood in alignment with the stages identified in the Active Implementation Framework (AIF).27 Development and evaluation of implementation interventions will be conducted using a four-step procedure outlined by French et al28: (1) identify what needs to be done differently for implementation to be successful; (2) use theoretical frameworks from implementation science to understand which barriers and enablers need to be addressed; (3) select and tailor fit-for-purpose implementation strategies that have a high likelihood of overcoming modifiable barriers and enablers; and (4) measure the impact of the implementation strategies on implementation outcomes.

At this time, data collection and analysis for phase Ia and Ib have been completed (July 2025–January 2026). The Learning Alliance has been convened (February 2026–June 2026), and survey input from Learning Alliance participants is currently being collected (completion expected by July 2026). Phase III will be initiated in fall 2026, and study completion is anticipated in spring 2028.

Factors influencing sustainability will be considered throughout stages of implementation, in alignment with the Consolidated Framework for Sustainability Constructs.29 An intersectional health equity lens will be applied to all implementation plans and activities in accordance with published recommendations and the Health Equity Implementation Framework (HEIF).30 31 Implementation of the AMNs in the exploration and planning stage will be informed by implementation of the AMNs at the spreading and scaling stage, which will be informed by learnings from the well-established networks. This approach respects the level of readiness of each AMN for implementation and allows for a process akin to a Learning Health System.

Setting and participating AMNs

Our work will be conducted in partnership with development efforts of 11 AMNs across Canada, including clinically focused AMNs and one AMN that is focused on health equity. For the purposes of this protocol, these include networks in the exploration and planning stage, spreading and scaling stage, sustainability stage and those that were decommissioned after reaching sustainable operations. Ontario is represented as an AMN currently in the exploration and planning stage, as well as a second AMN that was decommissioned after reaching sustainable operations. Intervention: AMNs

AMNs have been used in different regions in Canada for over 20 years.18,21 Adaptive mentoring is characterised by three concepts: (1) mentorship that adapts to the learning needs of mentees, (2) building compassionate provider communities and (3) bidirectional value for mentors and mentees.16,21 Adaptability of mentoring allows for customisation of how mentors and mentees engage to fit the learning needs of the mentee. Adaptation can include different environments (eg, in-person, virtual, synchronous), forms of mentoring (eg, one-to-one, group, peer), purposes and duration (eg, discussing single episodes of care vs longitudinal).16 17

Compassionate provider communities are developed by mentors and mentees engaging in a safe space that allows for supportive and non-judgmental conversations around clinical challenges. These interactions enable sharing of knowledge and support for the mentee in overcoming barriers to practical implementation of clinical best practices. They go beyond translating knowledge by addressing the stress, fears and anxieties mentees face in providing complex care, and by creating spaces that are psychologically safe for participants.18,21 This augments mentees’ clinical resiliency and professional satisfaction. Mentors also benefit through increased knowledge, improved resiliency and professional satisfaction.1618,21

Participation in these voluntary networks involves recruiting mentees through communication channels of health professional associations, academic institutions and health authorities. Mentees are matched to mentors or mentor teams in similar geographic regions and connect with mentors as needed. The mentees connected to a mentor team form a small group, and these groups meet regularly for didactic teaching and discussions that can be clinical. Small-group meetings provide opportunities for group and peer mentoring and foster a sense of community that resembles a community of practice.18 22

Patient and public involvement

Persons with lived and living experience and patient-stakeholders are embedded within the study team and the Learning Alliance. They will contribute to priority setting, interpretation of findings, selection and operationalisation of implementation strategies, and dissemination activities.

Phase I: exploration phase

The aims of the first phase of our research are to (1) explore the process of implementing AMNs with a focus on mapping the determinants of successful implementation and understanding contextual adaptations; (2) determine the modifiable enablers and barriers to implementing AMNs within healthcare ecosystems that can be targeted through fit-for-purpose implementation strategies and (3) map the capabilities, opportunities and motivations among mentors and mentees to engage in adaptive mentoring. Such an understanding will offer a metric to gauge the impact of program-level implementation strategies and support future research efforts to enhance adaptive mentorship.

Phase Ia: identifying the key determinants of successful implementation of AMNs

Design

We will leverage more than 14 years of experience in the implementation of AMNs to identify determinants of successful implementation using a retrospective observational study that includes document review and interviews conducted with key informants.

Data sources and measures

Data sources include interviews and documentation review. Interview guides will be developed in alignment with the CFIR and associated Interview Guide Tool.32 33 Particular attention will be paid to characteristics of the innovation, outer setting and inner setting. Review of documents pertaining to development and implementation of AMNs will be completed using reports, meeting minutes, field notes, project charters, documented workflows and existing evaluations. Special attention will be paid to understanding: (1) sustainability of AMNs among well-established networks; (2) organisational and leadership drivers among networks that are in the spreading and scaling stage and (3) readiness to enact AMNs among networks that are in the exploration/preparation stage. Readiness will be conceptualised using the R=MC² model, where readiness is a function of motivation, general capacities and innovation-specific capacities.34 Where feasible, readiness will be quantified using an established readiness measure.35

Recruitment

We will recruit a purposeful sample of AMN administration and leadership (eg, managers, directors and staff) who were present during planning, delivery or network refinement, or who will be present during development and implementation of respective AMNs, to participate in interviews. AMN leadership and staff will be recruited through targeted communications.

Procedures

Interviews will be conducted in English or French with each participating AMN to explore development and implementation of the AMN, or readiness to develop an AMN. An understanding of the implementation process will be explored within each stage of implementation.27 Data synthesised from interviews and document reviews will be triangulated to map the implementation process, understand the role of implementation teams, specify data-driven processes (eg, plan–do–study–act) and identify key implementation drivers.

Phase Ib: developing a behavioural understanding of the capabilities, opportunities and motivations to engage in effective adaptive mentorship

Design

Qualitative.

Data sources and measures

Interviews will be conducted with mentors and mentees from across participating AMNs to develop a behavioural understanding of adaptive mentorship and delineate potentially modifiable enablers and barriers. The COM-B model will guide conversations towards the essential ingredients important to support behaviour change for adaptive mentorship.24 The COM-B posits that behaviour change is influenced by capability, opportunity and motivation. The TDF will then be applied to support conceptualisation of anticipated barriers and enablers.25 The TDF is a synthesis of theories and psychological constructs that can be mapped under the three components of the COM-B model.25

Recruitment

We will recruit a purposive sample of mentors and mentees from across participating AMNs to ensure representation across geographic region, health profession, familiarity with adaptive mentoring, stage of career and work setting. Mentors and mentees will be recruited through targeted communications.

Procedures

Semi-structured interview guides will be developed to explore capabilities, opportunities and motivations necessary to enact adaptive mentorship, and beliefs about factors anticipated to impact delivery or receipt of adaptive mentoring. The number of interviews will vary depending on staffing structure and the richness and breadth of data collected. Interviews will be conducted face-to-face or online using video conferencing software. Interviews will be recorded and facilitated in English or French by a research team member trained in qualitative methods and experienced with the theoretical frameworks.

Phase Ic: developing a Learning Alliance

We will use Learning Alliance methodology to facilitate the collaborative development and context-sensitive application of implementation strategies developed in phase II.36 37 A Learning Alliance is a diverse network of stakeholders committed to improving knowledge on a specific topic and seeking to further systems change.36 Learning Alliances can effectively support capacity-building, document and disseminate innovative practices and foster contextualised understandings of and responses to crises that cannot be solved by a single stakeholder group, system or perspective.36 37 Originally developed for knowledge mobilisation work in rural areas, Learning Alliances are distinguished from advisory committees in that they actively work together to deliberate, share information, solve problems and accomplish specific tasks.36

In this study, the Learning Alliance’s work will be facilitated in English and French through a series of formal processes and collaborative engagement in shared tasks mapped to objectives 1 and 2 completed during phase II. At the outset of the project, Learning Alliance members will have an introductory virtual workshop in which they reflect on the appropriate application of AMNs and identify meaningful, practical priorities that should drive implementation within healthcare contexts. Members will include mentees and mentors from each AMN, leadership across participating AMNs, members of advisory committees, clinicians and policymakers, government officials, and persons with lived and living experience and patient-stakeholders. We anticipate that the Learning Alliance will be composed of more than 30 members.

Phase II: developing fit-for-purpose implementation strategies

Aim

The aim of this phase is to co-design fit-for-purpose implementation strategies to overcome barriers and leverage enablers to implement, spread and scale AMNs at the system level. This phase will involve the identification, selection and operationalisation of strategies to support implementation of AMNs.

Design

First, the research team will map barriers and enablers identified within each AMN to evidence-informed implementation strategies. Next, the research team will work with the Learning Alliance to select a suite of implementation strategies to overcome potentially modifiable barriers and leverage enablers. Strategies will be prioritised based on (1) results of systems mapping; (2) the degree to which they address common factors anticipated to impact implementation across AMNs and (3) potential impact on the capabilities, opportunities and motivations to engage in adaptive mentoring. Next, the research team and Learning Alliance will work together to operationalise the selected strategies, develop procedures and resources necessary to enact those strategies, and draft guidance in English and French for addressing anticipated contextual factors.

Procedures

Identification of relevant strategies

Barriers and enablers to spreading and scaling AMNs identified during phase Ia will be classified according to CFIR constructs and mapped to the strategies recommended by the ERIC taxonomy, using the CFIR-ERIC implementation strategy matching tool.38 39 This tool was developed through expert consensus to produce descriptions of implementation barriers based on CFIR constructs and rank implementation strategies best suited to addressing those barriers. Use of this tool will facilitate the identification of strategies to address characteristics of systems and procedures anticipated to impact spread and scale of AMNs. Strategy identification will be conducted by members of the study team trained in use of the TDF, CFIR and strategy selection tools.

Capabilities, opportunities and motivations that facilitate or hinder engagement in adaptive mentorship identified during phase Ib will be mapped onto intervention functions using the behaviour change wheel by members of the research team.40 This understanding will be used to identify behaviour change techniques and mechanisms41 that will be optimally linked using the Theory and Technique Tool.42 Behaviour change techniques that optimally operate on mechanisms of action for each identified barrier and facilitator will be selected as a plausible technique to bring forward for consideration of the Learning Alliance.

Selection and operationalisation of strategies

The research team will use findings from phase Ia and systems mapping to inform the Learning Alliance while selecting a suite of strategies to be applied to overcome barriers and leverage enablers anticipated to impact the spread and scale of AMNs. Results from phase Ib will inform the Learning Alliance while selecting behaviour change techniques that operate on mechanisms of action that can be leveraged to augment barriers and facilitators to engagement in adaptive mentorship. Selection of behaviour change techniques will occur in a multi-round consensus-driven process.

Selection of strategies and behaviour change techniques will occur in a multi-round consensus-driven process akin to a Delphi panel that will be held virtually.43 44 The first round will consist primarily of broad, open-ended questions for the expert panel. Items for which consensus is achieved will be deemed agreed on and removed from subsequent rounds, while items for which consensus has not yet been achieved will be re-evaluated, with edits based on comments, moving to increasingly closed-ended questions in subsequent rounds.43 44 We will examine the results of each round in detail, including comments on wording or missing elements, and share them with the Learning Alliance to ensure a breadth of interpretations. Consensus on quantitative questions will be determined using common Delphi standards (eg, 70–80% agreement). Materials will be developed by the research team with feedback from the Learning Alliance to support delivery of strategies at each site.

Phase III: supporting implementation and evaluation

Aim

The aims of this phase are to develop AMN-specific logic models to support implementation, and to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability and initial utility of implementation strategies.

Design

We will work with partnering AMNs to prioritise implementation strategies from phase II through the process of co-developing implementation logic models that map determinants to strategies, mechanisms and outcomes.45 We will provide support in English and French for partnering AMNs to operationalise and enact their logic models. Finally, we will adopt a mixed-methods approach46 to triangulate the acceptability and feasibility of implementation strategies within each AMN, and build an understanding of fidelity to, and adaptation of, the implementation strategies applied.

Acceptability will be defined as the extent to which implementation strategies are considered appropriate, based on anticipated or experienced cognitive and emotional responses and understood through the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability, investigated through interviews and questionnaires.47 48 Feasibility will be defined as the degree to which implementation strategies can be effectively carried out within the AMNs given context and will be operationalised through interviews guided by Proctor’s framework and the feasibility of implementation measure.49 50 Fidelity and adoption will be defined as the extent to which selected implementation strategies were adopted as planned in the logic model, and how those strategies were adapted through pilot testing.51 Our team will design a survey guided by the National Institutes of Health Behaviour Change framework with Likert scales and open-ended items.51 Finally, interviews will be conducted with mentors and mentees to understand the impact of implementation strategies on identified capabilities, opportunities and motivations to enact adaptive mentorship.

Procedures

A research associate with experience in developing implementation logic models will facilitate development of a logic model with AMNs in the process of spreading and scaling and AMNs in the exploration/preparation stage using a standard template. This work will be informed by systems maps.

After piloting implementation at each partnering AMN for a period of 6 months, we will invite AMN administration and leadership to participate in an evaluation of acceptability and feasibility of enacting implementation strategies selected within their logic model to address barriers and leverage facilitators identified through CFIR mapping in phase II. This work will be performed through surveys and interviews with special attention paid to fidelity and adaptation of the implementation strategies applied. Mentors and mentees will be invited to participate in interviews to understand the impact of the implementation strategies on capabilities, opportunities and motivations to engage in adaptive mentorship.

Phases I–III: ongoing work

Systems mapping

We will use a systems-thinking approach to map (1) AMN structures and offerings; (2) the interconnectedness and feedback loops between AMN structures and offerings; (3) connectedness of AMNs to external organisations and related supports and (4) gaps in AMN offerings and supports.52 Information from systems maps will be fed back to the Learning Alliance to inform prioritisation of implementation strategy selection during phase II and to inform a systems-level theory of change when developing logic models during phase III.

Document analysis using data sources identified in phase Ia will inform development of interview guides. Questions and prompts included in the qualitative guides will identify components, relationships and goals of AMNs. Participants recruited in phase Ia and phase Ib will have the opportunity to continue participation in systems-mapping interviews or workshops.

Semi-structured interviews will be conducted in English or French to understand the structure, operation and impact of AMN systems. In parallel, we will hold systems-mapping workshops with relevant stakeholders to co-create a system map of partnering AMNs, including stock-and-flow and causal loop diagrams. The diverse perspectives represented in interviews and workshops will be converged into a comprehensive system map depicting each AMN and their interrelations. We will observe AMN operations and activity delivery throughout data collection to uncover real-time interactions, dependencies and emergent behaviours. Observational data will complement insights obtained from document analysis and interviews and will be used to refine the AMN system diagrams.

AMN mentor and mentee evaluation

The clinical AMNs in British Columbia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island have developed and harmonised a longitudinal evaluation for mentors and mentees that will be leveraged in the proposed work. In brief, mentors and mentees complete surveys every 6 months to (1) document engagement in adaptive mentorship activities and (2) track change over time in knowledge of best practices, confidence to enact learnings and delivery of compassionate clinical care. Evaluations were developed using a competency-based approach14 and include outcomes of interest to this project, such as burnout.

Sampling and sample size

Sampling will be purposive across phases. For phase Ia, we will recruit AMN administration and leadership who can speak to planning, delivery and refinement. For phase Ib, we will recruit mentors and mentees to ensure representation across geographic region, health profession, familiarity with adaptive mentoring, stage of career and work setting. For systems mapping and phase III evaluation work, purposive sampling will continue to ensure adequate representation across language, geography, profession and equity-deserving communities.

Across 11 AMNs or AMN development efforts, we anticipate recruiting approximately 2–3 administration or leadership participants per AMN for phase Ia, 3–5 mentors or mentees per AMN for phase Ib, and a purposive subset of leadership, mentors and mentees for phase III interviews, in addition to surveys in active networks. The Learning Alliance will include more than 30 members. These targets may be adjusted based on staffing structures, network maturity and the richness of data collected.

Approach to analysis

Multiple approaches to qualitative synthesis were selected to align with the goals of the various phases. To create a rich contextual understanding of AMN implementation, thematic analysis will be used to organise and categorise patterns within the data, using an inductive, constant comparison, descriptive approach.53 Pairs of reviewers will read transcripts to familiarise themselves with the data, create codes and note patterns. Potential themes will be discussed and transcripts reread to refine themes in an iterative process. Codebooks will be iteratively developed and refined to support consistency in analysis.54 Final themes will be agreed upon through consensus meetings with the team.

When synthesising barriers and facilitators, statements about barriers and facilitators will be assigned to a relevant TDF domain, or set of domains, using the process described by Cane et al55 The process evaluation of implementation strategies will examine acceptability, feasibility, fidelity, adoption and adaptation through qualitative coding of interviews, matrices and triangulation with surveys and document review. When mapping the implementation process, data synthesised from interviews will be triangulated with field notes and document review and mapped onto CFIR to understand determinants in a manner that aligns with AIF stages.27 32

Descriptive statistics will be generated and compared against normative data where appropriate to understand implementation determinants measured through surveys (eg, readiness, acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility and trialability of the innovation). Data from fidelity checklists will be abstracted for evaluation of fidelity and adaptation. In phase II, consensus statistics will be generated for each strategy considered by the Delphi process.43 44 These data will be fed back to implementation teams in an iterative improvement process.

Data pertaining to three levels of AMN function will be fed back to implementation teams and AMN leadership to form a developmental evaluation of implementation strategies and their impacts on mentors and mentees: (1) impact on the function of the AMN (eg, acceptability, feasibility and fidelity); (2) effect on capabilities, opportunities and motivations of mentors and mentees to engage in adaptive mentorship and (3) impact on outcomes of interest to mentees within the AMN, including resilience, psychological safety, interprofessional practice and compassionate care.26

Ethics and dissemination

Study protocols were approved by the Bruyère Health Research Ethics Board (M16-25-001 and M16-25-002) and the Newfoundland and Labrador Health Research Ethics Board (Ref# 2025.047). All participants will provide informed consent; confidentiality and data security procedures will comply with institutional and national standards.

Our study will use integrated knowledge mobilisation (iKM) targeting three audiences, including (1) AMNs in our study by focusing on integrating results from Phase III into their programme implementation plans; (2) engaging the novel AMNs in ON, SK and QC and (3) working with the Centre for Effective Practice as one of the largest primary care knowledge translation organisations in Canada to mobilise our findings. We will also engage with Health Canada, the Association of Faculties of Medicine of Canada, Ontario Medical Association, Can-Change to employ the study outputs to support novel health system innovations they are funding or undertaking. The Learning Alliance will be a key knowledge mobilisation conduit throughout the project, including liaising with the Evidence Support and Knowledge Mobilization (ESKM) Hub to mobilise rapid response research.

The end of grant knowledge translation will involve knowledge synthesis and dissemination. We will create tools that adapt knowledge to specific audiences (including sex and gender considerations) and local context. We will leverage our partnerships to provide reports and presentations as analyses are completed. We will support knowledge diffusion through conference presentations, open access journal publication and non-peer reviewed reporting. Emerging approaches to integrated knowledge translation, including governed artificial intelligence tools that support traceable, audience-responsive synthesis, may also inform how project outputs are adapted and mobilised across partner contexts.56 Plain language summaries and educational materials related to our implementation strategies will be developed and disseminated to our partners and patients.
