Artificial polysaccharide-binding protein mediates co-assembly of nanodrug and probiotics against drug-resistant infectious enteritis
Authors: Ying Sun, Jiayue Yang, Zirun Zhao, Suke Liu, Mingchun Li, Qilin Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intestinal drug-resistant pathogens, e.g., Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Tm) and enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (E. coli), frequently cause life-threatening infectious enteritis. Probiotic-based therapy is a promising way to eliminate drug-resistant pathogens for treatment of infectious enteritis, but its colonizing and therapeutic efficacy after oral administration are limited. Here, we developed a facile therapeutic agent to treat infectious enteritis by co-assembly of the peptide nanodrug melittin-loaded MSN grafted by polysaccharide-binding protein (MMPB) with the famous probiotic bacteria Lactobacillus plantarum (Lac) and Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis (Bif). The nanodrug was composed of the antimicrobial peptide melittin and mesoporous silica nanoparticles exposing the artificial polysaccharide-binding protein. Owing to presence of the artificial protein on the MMPB surface, the nanodrug strongly bound and cross-linked the probiotic cells, forming the Lac+Bif+MMPB co-assembly. During co-incubation with the kanamycin-resistant E. coli strain (Ecka), the co-assembly strongly reduced the viability of Ecka, leading to the increase in the ratio of probiotic to Ecka from 1.6 to 9.2. After oral administration of the co-assembly to the mice pre-colonized by Ecka, Lac+Bif+MMPB almost eliminated the kanamycin-resistant gene in the intestine, and led to 2–3-fold higher levels of the probiotic cells than the nanodrug MMPB or the combined probiotics Lac+Bif. More importantly, in the mice suffering from enteritis caused by drug-resistant S. Tm, the co-assembly remarkably recovered the mouse body weight, reduced intestine colonization of S. Tm cells, and decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in both serum and colons. This study realized the synthetic biology technique-mediated abiotic/biotic co-assembly for efficient treating infectious enteritis induced by drug-resistant pathogens.

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance of intestine pathogens represents a formidable challenge in modern medicine, characterized by the ability of these pathogens to withstand antibiotics that effectively treated infections before [[1], [2]]. This phenomenon has emerged primarily due to the extensive use of antibiotics, resulting in a significant escalation in the prevalence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. This trend has become a profound concern within the realms of clinical practice, necessitating urgent attention and innovative solutions [[3], [4], [5]]. The advent of multidrug-resistant bacteria exacerbates this issue, imposing a severe threat to public health on a global scale [[6], [7], [8]]. The World Health Organization’s global surveillance of antimicrobial resistance reveals a disturbing prevalence of antibiotic resistance in approximately 500,000 patients across 22 countries. These intestinal strains have demonstrated the capability to acquire resistance genes from other bacterial species and, in turn, transmit these genes, thus contributing to the burgeoning repository of antibiotic resistance genes [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Especially, the pervasive nature of antibiotic resistance in intestinal bacteria stands as a pivotal challenge confronting both human and animal health sectors globally, underscoring the need for its recognition as a critical public health issue [13].

Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Tm) and enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (E. coli) are the most frequently identified antibiotic-resistant species causing intestinal infectious diseases [[14], [15], [16]]. For example, in mammalian hosts, drug-resistant S. Tm colonizes the intestinal tract, eliciting pronounced inflammatory responses that result in acute non-typhoidal salmonellosis, a condition aggravated by its antibiotic resistance [[17], [18]]. The emergence and proliferation of these resistant bacteria critically impede the efficacy of traditional bacterial infection treatments [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Consequently, the ongoing quest for novel strategies to eradicate drug-resistant bacteria in the intestinal environment is of paramount importance [25,26]. In this context, probiotics, recognized for their beneficial effects on intestinal health, are emerging as a highly promising solution.

Probiotics are defined as living microbes that, if given in sufficient doses, are beneficial to the health of the host [27]. These microbes generally grow and reproduce at 37 °C, have high tolerance to acid and bile, and can survive under adverse conditions of the human digestive system [28]. Common types of probiotics include Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and probiotic yeasts [29,30]. Among them, Lactobacillus plantarum (Lac) and Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis (Bif) are representative Gram-positive bacterial probiotics [31]. These probiotics exert positive effect on human health, such as inhibition of harmful and pathogenic bacteria, regulation of immune response, supplementation of essential nutrients, and improvement of gastrointestinal mucosal functions [[32], [33], [34]]. Extensive research has indicated their activity in suppression of cancer progression, prevention of neurological disorders, and alleviation of depression symptoms [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. However, these probiotics are highly sensitive to harsh environments, which drastically compromised their survival and intestine retention [[43], [44], [45]]. Therefore, it is of significance to promote the survival and colonization capacity of these probiotics in the gastrointestinal tract. Generally, formulations such as liquids, capsules, and pills have been widely used to protect the vitality of oral probiotics [[28], [46]], but these formulations are still insufficient to maintain a high survival rate of probiotics in the gastrointestinal tract. As an alternative way, nanotechnology is becoming a emerging strategy to regulate probiotic functionality and improve their therapeutic effects [[47], [48], [49], [50], [51]].

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) have the advantages of higher stability, easier preparation and modification compared to other nanocarriers (such as liposomes), and are becoming one of the most powerful drug delivery platforms [[52], [53], [54], [55]]. The gatekeeper system of MSNs plays a key role in enabling targeted drug release while preventing premature leakage. These gatekeepers can be removed in response to specific internal or external stimuli, such as pH, redox conditions, temperature, or biomolecules, thereby achieving precise and controlled drug delivery in the body [[56], [57], [58], [59], [60], [61]]. Recently, MSNs were used for preparation of melittin (MEL) nanodrugs, which exhibited high antimicrobial activity, intelligent stimulus responsiveness and good biocompatibility [58,62].

Nowadays, it remains a great challenge to efficiently treat drug-resistant pathogen-induced infectious enteritis via traditional antibiotic and probiotic strategies. To address this issue, we aimed to establish a targeted co-assembly mechanism between probiotics and antimicrobial peptide-based nanodrugs. For this purpose, we introduced the carbohydrate-binding domain (CBD) of CttA from Ruminococcus flavefaciens, which is known to bind alginate-like polysaccharides (PS) with the high affinity and the excellent stability under physiological conditions. Compared to other carbohydrate-binding proteins, CttA exhibits broader recognition toward PS and has been successfully applied in PS labeling and tracking [63,64]. These properties make CttA an ideal module for mediating precise interaction between synthetic PS and bioactive molecules. In this study, the CttA domain was engineered into a fusion protein termed the polysaccharide-binding protein (PB), and used as a key mediator for constructing an anti-enteritis platform based on the abiotic/biotic co-assembly of probiotics and MEL-loaded nanodrugs (Scheme 1). The platform is MEL-loaded plus PB-grafted MSN (MMPB), together with the probiotics Lac and Bif. Herein, the MMPB nanodrug was prepared by loading the antimicrobial peptide MEL into the MSNs, followed by covalently PB grafting on the MSN surface to avoid premature release of the peptide drug. The PB protein exposed on the nanodrug surface further recognized PS on the surface of the probiotic cells, facilitating the co-assembly to form MMPB+Lac+Bif. After oral administration to the mice with enteritis induced by either drug-resistant E. coli or S. Tm, the co-assembly may persistently colonize in the gastrointestinal tract, efficiently eradicating the drug-resistant pathogens and alleviating enteritis. This study provides a synthetic biology-assisted co-assembly strategy to enhance intestine retention of probiotics and nanodrugs against infectious enteritis.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the MMPB+Lac+Bif co-assembly and its effect on alleviation of enteritis caused by drug-resistant pathogens. Materials and methods

Materials

Hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide, sodium trifluoroacetate, ammonium nitrate, and (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Antimicrobial peptide MEL (GIGAVLKVLTTGLPALISWIKRKRQQ) was prepared by chemical synthesis method in China Peptides Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Anti-CD3, anti-CD4, anti-CD8, and anti-IL-17A antibodies all purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA).

Synthesis of MSNs

To synthesize the large-pore monodisperse MSNs, 760 mg hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide, 1,134 mg sodium trifluoroacetate and 136 mg triethylamine were added into 50 ml distilled water, and dissolved under magnetically stirring at 83 °C for 30 min. Tetraethyl orthosilicate (4 ml) were then added into the mixture, followed by stirring for further 20 min. The MSNs were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and re-suspended in 50 ml ethanol containing 1 g ammonium nitrate. The surfactants in the MSN pores were then removed by heating the suspensions with stirring at 75 °C for 24 h. After centrifugation, the pellets were further washed twice with distilled water and twice with ethanol, and then re-suspended in 50 ml ethanol. (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (2 ml) were added into the nanoparticle suspension. The mixture was heated with constant shaking at 80 °C for 12 h, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and washing twice by ethanol. After dried in a freezing vacuum dryer (10 N, SCIENTZ, China), the amino group-exposed MSNs were obtained for further MEL loading and PB grafting.

Preparation of the artificial protein PB

The PB was designed by fusing the CBD of Ruminococcus flavefaciens CttA (CBDCttA) with the green fluorescence protein (GFP) (Fig. 1A, Table S1). The PB-encoding gene was designed via the E. coli optimized codons and de novo synthesized. This gene was cloned into pET-28a, obtaining the expression plasmid pET-PB. The plasmid was transformed into the E. coli BL21 strain. The PB protein was expressed in the transformed strain under the induction of isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside, and purified by using nickel columns. Synthesis of artificial protein-grafted MSNs

The amino group-exposed large-pore MSNs as described above were further used for protein grafting via the cross-linking agent glutaraldehyde. Briefly, 20 mg MSNs were suspended in 10 ml distilled water, and then 50 µl of 10% (v/w) glutaraldehyde were mixed. The mixture was magnetically stirred for 12 h, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min and washing twice by distilled water. The pellet was re-suspended in 10 ml distilled water, and then 1 ml PB or GFP solution (4 mg/ml, dissolved in distilled water) were added. The mixture was further stirred at 4 °C for 12 h, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The PB-grafted MSN (MPB) or GFP-grafted MSN (MG) was finally obtained after pellet washing twice by distilled water and drying with a freezing vacuum drier (Scientz, China).

For MEL loading and protein grafting to the MSNs, 5 mg MEL were dissolved in 5 ml distilled water. The MEL solution was then mixed with 5 ml MSN suspension (prepared in distilled water, 4 mg/ml). The mixture was gently shaken at 4 °C for 24 h, and then 1 ml PB or GFP solution (5 mg/ml, dissolved in distilled water) and 50 µl of 10% (v/w) glutaraldehyde were added. The mixture was further stirred at 4 °C for 12 h, and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The MMPB or MEL-loaded plus GFP-grafted MSN (MMG) was finally obtained by pellet washing and freezing vacuum drying.

The morphology of MSN, MMG and MMPB was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (TecnaiG2 F-20, FEI). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping of MSN and MMPB was performed by TEM (JEM 2100F, JEOL, Japan). Size distribution and Zeta potentials of the nanoparticles in distilled water were analyzed by a Zeta-potential analyzer (ZetaPLAS, Brookhaven, USA). The characterization of the MMPB co-assembly was mainly performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN MIRA LMS, Czech).

MEL-loading/releasing assays

To evaluate the MEL-loading capacity of MSN, MMG and MMPB nanoparticles, 500 µl of each nanoparticle suspension (prepared in PBS at 5 mg/ml) were mixed with 500 µl FITC-labeled MEL (FITC-MEL) solution (also prepared in PBS at 5 mg/ml). The mixtures were gently shaken for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, for the MMPB group, 50 µl PB protein (5 mg/ml) was added, while for the MSN and MMG groups, 50 µl phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added instead to serve as a control. All mixtures were further incubated with shaking for 2 h, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The pellets were then washed twice with PBS to remove unbound FITC-MEL, yielding the MEL-loaded nanoparticles [62].

The concentrations of unbound FITC-MEL in the supernatants and wash solutions were quantified using a fluorescent microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). The MEL-loading capacity (%) was calculated using the following formula:Loadingcapacity(%)=FITC-MELinitial−FITC-MELsupertant−FITC-MELwashesNanoparticlemass×100%

Culture medium and conditions

Two kinds of probiotics, i.e., Lac (Bio-82092) and Bif (Bio-121729), were obtained from Biobw, China. The drug-resistant E. coli strain Ecka was constructed by transforming the kanamycin-resistant gene-carrying plasmid pET28a-mCherry (Dingguo, China) into the E. coli BL21 strain. The kanamycin-resistant S. Tm was constructed by transforming the plasmid pGFPuv (Dingguo, China) into the S. Tm strain SL134 (bio-110527, Biobw, China) by the electro-transformation method. The probiotics Lac and Bif were cultured on the solid MRS medium in an anaerobic incubator at 37 °C for activation, followed by culturing in the liquid MRS medium at 37 °C. The Ecka and S. Tm strains were activated in the LB solid medium at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by culturing in liquid LB medium supplemented with 50 mg/l kanamycin to the logarithmic phase of growth for further use.

Cell culture

The RAW264.7 and MCCEpic-SV40 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (TransGen, Beijing, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) (Gibco, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Once the cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were sub-cultured and expanded for subsequent assays.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay kits (Tianjin Simubiotech). In brief, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and adhered for 12 h. After adherence, the cells were exposed to different concentrations of MEL or MMPB for another 12 h. After the treatment, 10 µl CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was then recorded using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group.

Flow cytometry

After treatment, the spleen, cecum and colon tissues of the mice were collected and processed into single-cell suspensions through enzymatic digestion. The resulting cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended. Cell suspensions were diluted at a ratio of 1:1000 and stained with fluorescence dye-conjugated antibodies, including anti-CD3, anti-CD4, anti-CD8, and anti-IL-17A antibodies. The cells were incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min, followed washed twice by PBS. Finally, the cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry. The gating strategies were described in the Supplementary Methods. For ROS measurement, the treated cells were stained by 10 mg/l DCFH-DA for 30 min and washed twice by PBS. Fluorescence intensity of the cells was then measured by flow cytometry. The FlowJo software (v10.8) was used to analyze the data, with mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) normalized to untreated controls.

Animal experiments

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nankai University (Approve number 2024-SYDWLL-000297). The pathogen-free, 6-week-old female ICR mice (∼20 g per mouse) were used for constructing the infectious enteritis model. These mice were randomly assigned into four groups, with each group containing four mice. The mice were housed under controlled conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle. They had unrestricted access to water and food throughout the study.

Model construction and probiotic treatment

To establish the colonization model of the resistant bacteria, the drug-resistant Ecka was inoculated into LB liquid medium and cultured in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 12–16 h, and then the cultures were adjusted to 1.6 × 109 CFU/ml. Each mouse orally received 200 µl of the bacterial suspensions. On Day 4 after the colonization of the resistant bacteria, the mice were divided into four groups, i.e., the CK group (the control group, each mouse orally receiving 200 µl -physiological saline), the Lac and Bif group (Lac+Bif, each mouse orally receiving 200 µl of Lac at a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/ml and Bif at the equal concentration), the MMPB group (each mouse orally receiving 200 µl MMPB at the concentration of 100 mg/l), and the Lac+Bif+MMPB group (each mouse orally receiving 200 µl of the Lac+Bif+MMPB mixture containing MMPB at the concentration of 100 mg/l, Lac and Bif both at a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/ml). The agents were orally administrated into the mice for 3 d with one time per day. The mice were then anesthetized and euthanized on Day 7. Their large intestine, small intestine, and feces were dissected for qPCR assays, bacterial number detection and histopathological observation.

For constructing the mouse enteritis model, each healthy mouse was orally administrated with 250 µl ampicillin (Amp, 100 mg/ml) and 200 µl ampicillin-resistant S. Tm (1 × 108 CFU/ml),which had been cultured in LB liquid medium at 30°C with shaking for 12–16 h. On Day 4 after administration, the mice showed severe weight loss, mental depression and reduced activity. These mice were divided by four groups and administrated by the corresponding agents as described above, i.e., the CK group, the Lac+Bif group, the MMPB group, and the Lac+Bif+MMPB group. The body weights of the mice were recorded in each day. The mice were anesthetized and euthanized on Day 10. The intestine tissues and feces were sampled for further analysis.

In vivo imaging

The 6-week-old female ICR mice (∼20 g per mouse) were randomly allocated to four experimental groups (n=6/group) receiving: Ecka, Lac+Bif, Ecka+Lac+Bif+FITC-labeled MMPB and Lac+Bif+FITC-labeled MMPB, with the doses consistent with those specified in Section 2.10. At 1, 4, 8 and 24 h post-administration, the gastrointestines were excised, rinsed with ice-cold PBS, and imaged immediately using an animal imaging system (IVIS Lumina Il, Xenogen, USA) with GFP filters (excitation wavelength at 465 nm). Exposure times (8–10 s) were dynamically adjusted to prevent signal saturation.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

The total DNA of the large intestine, small intestine and feces samples was extracted by using the DNA extraction kit (Beijing Biomarker Technologies, China), and the relative content of Lac and Bif was measured. The kanamycin resistance gene (KanaR), the β-glucuronidase-encoding gene uidA of E. coli, the 16S rRNA gene of Lac and the atpD gene sequence of Bif were detected by the corresponding detection primers (Table S2) with a qPCR detector (Realplex2, Eppendorf, USA). These genes were used to reflect the abundance of the drug-resistant gene, Lac and Bif. To evaluate the expression levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, and TGF-β in the colon tissues, the total RNA of the mouse colon was extracted with the RNA extraction kit (Shanghai Promega Biological Products Co., Ltd. America). The cDNA was then prepared by using the reverse transcription kit (TransGen Biotech, China). The expression levels of the inflammatory factors were then detected with the corresponding detection primers (Table S2) by the qPCR detector.

Microbiome analysis

The contents of large intestine in the mice after 5 d of the treatment with saline (Control), probiotics, MMPB or the assembly were sampled for microbiome analysis. Briefly, total DNA was extracted from each sample using the TGuide S96 Magnetic Bead Extraction Kit. The 16S rDNA libraries were then constructed by amplifying the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene from the samples with the primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The obtained rDNA libraries of each sample were sequenced by the Biomarker Technologies Corporation, China, and the data were analyzed by the online software (http://www.biocluod.net). PCoA of the microbiome composition, Shannon index, Simpson index and abundance of the bacterial genera were analyzed as described previously [65]. The analysis of NMDS, Chao 1 index, and relative abundance of the bacterial groups was performed.

Colony forming unit (CFU) assays

To detect the viable bacterial numbers in the samples, the mouse large intestine, small intestine and feces of the resistant bacteria colonization model were ground with a glass homogenizer. The obtained homogenates were diluted with distilled water, and added on LB solid medium containing kanamycin (Kana), followed by culturing in an incubator at 37 °C for counting resistant E. coli colonies. The cecal contents of mice in the enteritis model were mixed with shaking and coated on LB plates containing Amp and placed at 30 °C for culturing, and the colonies were counted after growth was completed.

To evaluate the effect of MSN and MMPB on probiotic survival, the fresh Bif and Lac cells were adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/ml and diluted with the ten-fold gradient into fresh MRS medium containing either MSN or MMPB. The final concentration of MSN or MMPB was maintained at 4 mg/l. The CFUs were determined after different time points of nanoparticle treatments. After incubation, aliquots were serially diluted with sterile PBS and plated on MRS agar plates. The plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h, and CFUs were counted to assess bacterial viability. Survival was expressed as CFU/ml, and all experiments were performed in triplicate.

Histopathological evaluation

At study endpoints, mice were euthanized, and gastrointestinal tissues were collected. The colon was photographed in situ and its length from the cecum to anus was precisely measured using ImageJ software (NIH version 1.53 g). Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h, then processed through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95% and 100%) and embedded in paraffin blocks. Serial sections of 8 µm thickness were cut for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and Alcian blue staining. For immunofluorescence studies, all samples were immediately frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned at 10 µm thickness using a cryostat. Sections were immune-stained for 30 min with anti-mouse TNF-α (1:1000, Biolegend, China) and anti-mouse IL-6 (1:1000, Biolegend, China) primary antibodies, followed by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:500, Solarbio, China) nuclear counterstaining. All stained sections were imaged using a Leica DM3000 brightfield microscope for histological evaluation and an Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope for fluorescence detection. Histopathological scores of the colon tissue were performed to assess the severity of inflammation and tissue damage. The scoring system is introduced in Table S3.

Extraction and purification of LacPs and BifPs

The PS (LacPs from Lac and BifPs from Bif) were extracted using the ultrasonic-assisted extraction method, as described by Michela Ferrari et al. [66], with minor modifications. Briefly, bacterial cultures were grown in MRS broth at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions for 48 h. After centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 15 min, the cell pellets were resuspended in distilled water and subjected to ultrasonic treatment (ultrasonic frequency: 20 kHz, 30 min, 4 °C). The suspension was then centrifuged to remove cell debris, and the supernatant containing the PS was concentrated. The crude PS were purified by ethanol precipitation, followed by dialysis (MW: 8,000 Da) against distilled water for 48 h, and then lyophilized to obtain purified LacPs and BifPs.

MMPB, MMG or PB was co-incubated with FITC-labeled β-glucan or probiotic cells (Lac or Bif) in PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The mixtures were gently shaken at 37 °C for 1 h to allow sufficient binding. The used concentrations were demonstrated as follows: MSN-based nanoparticles at 100 µg/ml, β-glucan at 10–100 µg/ml, and probiotic cells at 1 × 109 CFU/ml. After incubation, unbound components were removed by centrifugation, and the binding efficiency was evaluated by fluorescence quantification or staining.

Stability assays of MMPB and the co-assembly

To assess the stability of MMPB, 1 ml of the nanoparticles (100 mg/l) were co-incubated with 1 ml simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 2, 2 h) and 1 ml simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8, 4 h) or 1 ml live Ecka bacteria (1 × 109 CFU/ml). At specified time points (1 h, 2 h and 4 h), the supernatants were used for size distribution assays by the Zeta-potential analyzer. The resistance of the probiotics to SGF was evaluated by co-incubating 1 ml of Lac+Bif, Lac+Bif+MSN and Lac+Bif+MMPB, and 1 ml SGF for different times. The CFUs of the probiotics were then determined by MRS plates. The stability of the Lac+Bif+MMPB co-assembly was detected by mixing 1 ml of Lac+Bif+MMPB and 1 ml SGF or 1 ml Ecka suspension. After co-incubation for 1 h, 2 h and 4 h, the numbers of viable probiotics in the supernatants were determined by CFU assays.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as the means ± standard deviations (SD). The difference between two groups was analyzed by the Student’s test, and the multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test, respectively. All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The difference between two groups was considered statistically significant for ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001. All data are compared with control group.

Results and discussion

Artificial PB strongly binds PS and probiotic cells

To realize the co-assembly of nanoparticles and probiotic cells, the artificial PB was designed, which contains the N-terminus CBD of CBDCttA and the C-terminus GFP (Fig. 1A). The capacity of PB or GFP to bind β-glucan was evaluated by grafting the protein onto MSN, followed by co-incubation of the protein-grafted MSN (MPB or MG) with FITC-labeled β-glucan. As revealed by fluorescence intensity quantification of the supernatants (Fig. 1B), MPB exhibited much higher capacity to adsorb glucan than the control MG. For instance, MPB could adsorb 98% glucan at the glucan concentration of 10 mg/l, while MG only adsorbed 14% glucan. Even at the β-glucan concentration of 100 mg/l, MPB also adsorbed much higher levels of β-glucan than MG (62% versus 3%, Fig. 1B). Consistently, upon co-incubation of the nanoparticles with the probiotic-derived PS (LacPs or BifPs), MPB adsorbed 78% LacPs, 81% BifPs and 88% LacPs+BifPs, while MG adsorbed the probiotic PS less than 5% (Fig. 1C). This implied that PB has a high affinity to glucan and probiotic PS.

The capacity of PB to bind probiotic cells was further evaluated by co-incubation of the cells with the proteins. After 1 h of incubation, the control GFP protein showed negligible binding to either Lac and Bif cells, with the binding ratio less than 0.022%. In contrast, the PB protein bound 30% of Lac cells, and 47.6% of Bif cells. More strikingly, this protein bound 98.3% of the mixed Lac+Bif cells (Fig. 1D). Statistical analysis further confirmed that PB bound significantly higher levels of Lac+Bif cells than that of Lac or Bif cells alone (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these results confirmed that the artificial PB protein strongly bind the PS-exposing Lac and Bif cells, while the control GFP failed to bind the probiotic cells (Fig. 1F).

MMPB induces probiotic assembly and facilitates probiotic survival

For inducing assembly of probiotic cells, the PB protein and the control GFP were then grafted onto the large-pore MSNs with a capacity to carry high-molecular-weight cargos [62], obtaining MMPB and MMG, respectively. Protein quantification revealed that the loading efficiency of the nanoparticles was 5.5% for MMPB and 5.3% for MMG, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and TEM revealed that the synthesized MMG and MMPB exhibited particle sizes ranging from 200–250 nm, with the pore sizes between 20–30 nm (Fig. 2A&2B). Moreover, both of nanoparticles contained the uniformly distributed elements, including O, Si, C and N. As compared to MMG, MMPB showed higher content of N (10.2% versus 6.5%, Fig. 2C), which may be attributed to the higher molecular weight of PB than that of GFP. Owing to the strong capacity of PB to bind the probiotic cells, the PB-grafted nanoparticles MMPB were supposed to also be capable of binding the cells. To evaluate the surface charge and colloidal stability of the nanoparticles, we investigated the effect of Ecka and SGF/SIF on PB degradation. As shown in Fig. S2, PB was significantly degraded by Ecka rather than by SGF and SIF, indicating that only live Ecka produce specific enzymes to trigger PB degradation. This supported a pathogen-specific release mechanism of MMPB, and suggested its potential to survive the gastrointestinal environment and achieve targeted drug release.

The Zeta potentials of MMPB and its co-assembled forms were then measured. As shown in Fig. S3A, MMPB exhibited a slightly negative surface charge with a Zeta potential around −5 mV, indicating its limited electrostatic stability in aqueous environments. Upon co-assembly with Lac and Bif, the Zeta potential shifted toward the positive direction (approximately +5 mV, Fig. S3B), suggesting successful surface modification and charge neutralization by the probiotic-derived PS.

We next assessed the hydrodynamic size distribution of MMPB by dynamic light scattering (DLS) assays. The nanoparticles exhibited a monodisperse size distribution centered around 200–300 nm (Fig. S3C), which is favorable for gastrointestinal delivery. After sequentially treated by SGF and SIF, the sizes of MMPB remained unchanged (Fig. S3D), indicating that the MMPB structure maintained good physical stability under harsh digestive conditions. Moreover, as revealed by CFU assays, the Lac+Bif+MMPB co-assembly retained significantly higher viability after SGF/SIF exposure compared to the free bacteria and the Lac+Bif+MSN complex (Fig. S4A). In addition, while SGF did not lead to the release of probiotic cells from the co-assembly, Ecka triggered a substantial probiotic release (Fig. S4B-S4D). Collectively, these findings suggest that the co-assembly effectively protected the probiotics from gastrointestinal digestion, and could respond to the attack of drug-resistant bacteria by releasing the probiotics.

Comprehensive in vivo toxicity studies were further performed to confirm the biosafety of MMPB and the co-assembly. We conducted acute toxicity studies in healthy mice. Mice were orally administered once daily with Lac+Bif, MMPB, or the Lac+Bif+MMPB co-assembly for 3 d. Discussion

The increasing prevalence of drug-resistant bacteria in the intestinal tract poses a significant and growing threat to global health. Traditional antibiotic treatments have become less effective in eradicating these pathogens. This inefficacy underscores the urgent need for innovative strategies to combat drug-resistant infections. Probiotics have emerged as a promising alternative due to their multifaceted health benefits and potential to inhibit drug-resistant bacteria. However, their clinical application has been hindered by poor colonization efficiency in the gastrointestinal tract following oral administration, which limits their therapeutic potential. To address these limitations, nanotechnology has been explored to enhance the delivery and efficacy of probiotics. Among various nanocarriers, MSNs have gained attention due to their unique properties, such as high surface area, tunable pore size, and the ability to be functionalized for targeted delivery [67,68]. For instance, MSN-encapsulated Bifidobacterium has been shown to enhance bacterial stability and therapeutic efficacy in neurodegenerative disease models [69]. Moreover, the MSN layer attached to Bifidobacterium infantis could enhance the survival rate of the probiotics when they are exposed to SGF [70]. These characteristics render MSNs an ideal platform for improving the stability, colonization, and therapeutic effects of probiotics.

In this study, we developed MSNs for co-delivering probiotics and MEL to target drug-resistant E. coli. Based on our previous work, MSNs were chosen for their negatively charged surfaces, enabling efficient MEL loading to form MMG [62,71]. However, probiotic viability in MMG was limited to approximately 30%. To address this, we modified MMG with a PB, creating MMPB. PB enabled controlled MEL release and enhanced probiotic adhesion and stability. In vitro tests showed MMPB increased probiotic survival to ∼90%, demonstrating its potential as an effective delivery platform.

Furthermore, the co-precipitation rate of the MMPB-probiotic complex was significantly higher than that of free probiotics, indicating improved intestinal colonization. These findings align with previous studies. For instance, Song et al. demonstrated that multifunctional spore shell nanomaterials (CN) could enhance probiotic colonization by forming CN-coated probiotics [50]. Zhu et al. showed that the modified two-dimensional H-silene nanomaterials improved the adaptability of probiotics to varying gastrointestinal microenvironments [72]. Additionally, Razavi et al. emphasized the potential of nanomaterials as encapsulation agents to enhance the targeted delivery of live probiotics to the intestine [73]. Our findings align with these studies, highlight the promise of nanotechnology in enhancing probiotic therapy.

This synergistic effect of the probiotics and MMPB may be attributed to the complementary properties of Lac and Bif, which modulate distinct yet cooperative immune and metabolic pathways to enhance host health. Lac exhibits strong adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells [74], while Bif species contribute to mucosal protection and anti-inflammatory effects [75]. Their expression of diverse surface adhesion proteins, such as mucus-binding and S-layer proteins, may target different mucin receptors, increasing available binding sites and improving colonization stability [76,77]. Meanwhile, MMPB encapsulation enhances probiotic survival by shielding them from gastric acid and bile salts. MSNs serve as protective barriers or controlled-release carriers, improving microenvironmental stability and mucosal retention under gastrointestinal conditions [69].

In vitro antibacterial assays demonstrated that MMPB-probiotic co-assembly exhibited superior inhibitory effects against drug-resistant bacteria compared to free probiotics or MMG alone. The enhanced therapeutic efficacy was further validated in a mouse model challenged with kanamycin-resistant E. coli. In the large intestine, the co-assembly group showed the lowest relative abundance of drug-resistant bacteria and the highest level of probiotic colonization. Similar trends were observed in the small intestine, indicating that this strategy effectively reduces drug-resistant bacterial populations while promoting probiotic colonization. Fecal analysis, an important indicator of intestinal health, further supported these findings [78,79]. Fecal qPCR analysis indicated that the MMPB-probiotic co-assembly not only rapidly eliminates drug-resistant bacteria but also prolongs the intestinal residence of probiotics. The possible mechanism is that the MMPB sensitively responds to the attack of drug-resistant bacteria, leading to the release of both MEL and the probiotics. On the one hand, the released MEL binds to the drug-resistant bacteria and disrupt their cell wall and plasma membrane. On the other hand, the released active probiotics adhered to the intestine mucosa, further inhibiting the colonization and growth of drug-resistant bacteria. Moreover, this co-assembly modulates the intestinal microenvironment by reducing local inflammation, improving epithelial barrier integrity, and restoring microbial balance, as demonstrated by reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and improved tight junction protein expression in the infected mice.

Through establishment of a Salmonella-induced murine enteritis model, we demonstrated that probiotic monotherapy (Lac+Bif) failed to achieve complete resolution of intestinal inflammation, as evidenced by colon tissue staining results and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels at the endpoint. These results are consistent with the observations of Buddhasiri et al., who reported that Limosilactobacillus reuteri KUB-AC5 could alleviate but not cure Salmonella-induced enteritis [18]. However, in our study, MMPB-probiotic co-assembly demonstrated the ability to effectively treat drug-resistant Salmonella-induced enteritis, individual nanomaterials or probiotics provided only partial relief. Moreover, after administration of the co-assembly, the probiotic gene levels were still detected after 36 h, indicating that the co-assembly extends the duration of probiotic colonization. This underscores the synergistic benefits of combining nanomaterials with probiotics for enhanced therapeutic outcomes.

The superior performance of MMPB in treating drug-resistant bacterial infections can be attributed to the unique role of PB. The hydrolysis of PB is influenced by the activity of drug-resistant bacteria, enabling the targeted release of MEL at the site of infection. This controlled release not only mitigates the adverse effects of the intestinal environment on probiotics but also enhances their survival and colonization. The Lac+Bif+MMPB co-assembly combats intestinal pathogens by promoting probiotic adhesion and competitive exclusion. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium offer complementary binding through distinct surface proteins, while MMPB enhances mucosal retention. This co-assembly also improves the intestinal microenvironment by reducing inflammation, strengthening epithelial barrier function, and restoring microbial balance, as evidenced by decreased CD3+CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and increased tight junction protein expression in infected mice. Overall, the integration of nanotechnology with probiotics represents a significant advancement in addressing the limitations of traditional probiotic delivery systems. By improving probiotic stability, colonization, and therapeutic efficacy, nanomaterials such as MMPB offer a promising solution for combating drug-resistant bacterial infections. Although the probiotic colonization and therapeutic effects of the MMPB system were confirmed in murine models, the long-term effects of repeated administration and the fate of the nanomaterial in vivo remain to be evaluated through extended pharmacokinetic and safety studies.

The strong affinity of MMPB for β-glucan highlights its potential as a versatile biomaterial in diverse biotechnological applications. This specific binding capability could be harnessed in environmental remediation systems, where selective adhesion to PS-rich biofilms or microbial surfaces is essential for stabilizing functional consortia. Additionally, the interaction between MMPB and β-glucan may support the development of biofilm-based bioreactors, controlled release platforms for biocatalysts, or microbial immobilization strategies in wastewater treatment, soil restoration and bioconversion processes. These features underscore the broader applicability of the MMPB system beyond biomedical settings, particularly in industrial and ecological biotechnology.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully constructed a biotic/abiotic hybrid co-assembly platform for efficient elimination of drug-resistant bacteria and treatment of infection-related enteritis. The co-assembly of dual probiotics is mediated by the biocompatible MEL nanodrug, which integrates MEL-loading MSNs and the surface-grafted artificial PB. This innovative system not only enables the targeted delivery of both MEL and probiotics to the site of infection, but also remodels the intestinal microbiome and immune response. Consequently, the co-assembly achieves the controlled release of MEL, the cooperation of the antimicrobial peptide and probiotics to eradicate drug-resistant bacterial cells, and the alleviation of intestinal inflammation. Our findings highlight the potential of nanosystems in enhancing therapeutic efficacy, particularly through the co-assembly of probiotics with functional nanomaterials. This approach not only improves the precision of drug delivery, but also significantly enhances anti-infection efficiency. Further studies will focus on optimizing the design and functionality of these co-assemblies, as well as on evaluating their long-term safety and efficacy for clinical applications, particularly in the field of oral probiotic therapies. Upon further development, the MMPB system could provide a more effective and precise treatment approach for a range of microbiome-related conditions, paving the way for its clinical translation.
