User experience and feasibility of CVD risk models: a study on clinician and patient expectations, and implementation in primary healthcare with P-CARDIAC pilot study in Hong Kong
Authors: Celine S L Chui, Celia Jiaxi Lin, Natalie Tsie, Marco Lee, Ashley Ching Yau Kwok, Judy Lee, Bonnie Leung, Meyone Yuen Tung Ng, Alston Conrad Chiu, Emmanuel Chun Ka Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA newly developed machine-learning-driven cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk prediction tool, the Personalised CARdiovascular DIsease risk Assessment for Chinese (P-CARDIAC), tailored for the Hong Kong population, has demonstrated superior predictive performance compared with existing risk prediction tool. This study aims to explore the acceptability and expectations of P-CARDIAC among clinicians and the general public, and to assess its feasibility within pharmacist-led services in local primary healthcare systems.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted to investigate the awareness and expectations of risk prediction tools among clinicians and the general public. A pragmatic pilot study was carried out to implement P-CARDIAC in pharmacist-led services at local primary healthcare centres. Data analysis included descriptive statistics, univariate regression modelling and reliability assessments of validated measurement scales.ResultsFor the cross-sectional study, a total of 113 of the general public and 17 clinicians responded to the questionnaire. The general public demonstrated low awareness but high health-seeking behaviour related to CVD risk prediction tools. Clinicians, especially cardiologists, reported limited experience with such tools due to unavailability and limited user-friendliness. In the pilot study, 15 participants engaged with pharmacist-led services incorporating P-CARDIAC. They expressed positive perceptions of the tool, believing that it could support better medication adherence and disease management. The pharmacist-led services incorporating P-CARDIAC were well-received by participants.ConclusionP-CARDIAC demonstrates promise as a local risk prediction tool to enhance cardiovascular care in Hong Kong. The study underscores the importance of improving awareness and adoption of such CVD risk tools.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including coronary heart disease (CHD) and ischaemic stroke, remain the leading cause of global disease burden. Despite a decline in age-standardised CVD mortality over the past three decades,1 the number of diagnosed heart disease cases in Hong Kong (HK) increased by 21% from 2009 to 2021.2 The risk of recurrent cardiovascular events within 5 years among those with established acute coronary syndrome can reach 31.5%.3 As the population ages, more individuals will live with long-term cardiovascular conditions, increasing healthcare and indirect costs and straining HK’s healthcare system. HK is currently reforming its healthcare system to emphasise health promotion and disease prevention in primary care.4 Implementing a standardised risk stratification score using Electronic Health Records (EHR) and digital platforms could streamline referrals across primary, secondary and tertiary care levels.

Previous research compared machine-learning (ML)-based models with common CVD risk prediction models and the conventional statistical models.5 It indicated ML models were better in performance than others with EHR utilisation in real-world evidence. Our team developed Personalised Cardiovascular Disease risk Assessment for Chinese (P-CARDIAC), a ML-based CVD risk prediction tool estimating 10-year recurrent CV event risk in individuals with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), considering over 120 risk factors and medication use. Tailored for the HK population, P-CARDIAC outperforms Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) Risk Score for Secondary Prevention (TRS 2°P) and Secondary Manifestations of Arterial Disease (SMART2).6 P-CARDIAC predicts the 10-year risk of recurrent CVD for high-risk individuals in integrating dynamic medication effect and multiple risk. It is one of the first ML-driven models with EHR utilisation in a clinical setting among HK population, and we attempt to alleviate the healthcare burden by adopting P-CARDIAC as a risk stratification tool. As a new tool intended for real-world use, we aim to assess user expectations during its initial implementation phase.

HK’s healthcare workforce is projected to face a clinician and nurse shortfall by 2030, but pharmacist numbers are expected to remain stable.7 The expansion of pharmacist-led services in primary care offers an opportunity to improve chronic disease management.8 9 Particularly, pharmacist-led services were favourable for the patients with CVD with complex healthcare needs.10 We plan to leverage pharmacists and these services to pilot P-CARDIAC’s feasibility and risk management in practice.

Method

Study design

This study consisted of two parts which addressed the two objectives. Part 1 was a cross-sectional survey (7 February–7 July 2022) that explored the acceptability and expectations of clinicians and the public regarding the existing CVD risk prediction tools. Conducted anonymously via Qualtrics using convenience sampling, two tailored surveys were developed: one for the general public (online supplemental file 1) and one for clinicians (online supplemental file 2). The surveys included 26 and 20 questions, respectively, covering demographics, internet usage, awareness and use of CVD risk prediction tools and expectations. Surveys were originally in English and translated into Traditional Chinese by two researchers independently (NT and CJL).

For part 2, we conducted a pragmatic pilot study to explore the feasibility of implementing P-CARDIAC in pharmacist-led services in the HK primary healthcare. Patients were recruited from the cardiology specialty out-patient clinic (SOPC) of Queen Mary Hospital (QMH) from 1 September 2022 to 31 December 2023. Eligible patients were screened and seen by clinicians. Written consent was obtained to collect patients’ EHR from the Hospital Authority (HA) Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS) and Electronic Health Record Sharing System (EHRSS). EHRSS is an EHR system with data from HA and other sources such as private general practitioners or other health centres. The latest health parameters, including age, sex, disease history, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and relevant data were extracted for P-CARDIAC score estimation.

Participants were referred to collaborating primary healthcare centres (A-Lively Community Pharmacy managed by Aberdeen Kai-fong Welfare Association (AKA) or Central Western District Health Centre/Express (DHC/E)) for pharmacist-led services, depending on their choice and home location, with some visited at home by pharmacists and volunteers. The P-CARDIAC score was provided beforehand for counselling during health checks (blood pressure, medication review, adherence assessment). A follow-up occurred around 2 months later via phone or home visit, with updates to the P-CARDIAC score based on the latest EHR data. Perceptions and satisfaction with P-CARDIAC and pharmacist-led services were assessed using questionnaires. Participants additionally completed the Beliefs about Medicines Questionnaire-General (BMQ-General-12), the Perceived Sensitivity to Medicines Scale (PSM-5) and the Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5) at their first visit. Service providers also completed feedback questionnaires. Each questionnaire took 5–10 min; unreachable or withdrawing participants were classified as lost to follow-up. A summary of questionnaires is given in online supplemental appendix table 1.

Study population

For part 1, we recruited the general public through convenient sampling and distribution of survey link through patient groups such as Care For Your Heart. Participants aged 18 years or above and able to comprehend English or Traditional Chinese were eligible for the study. Clinicians with any specialties who were currently practicing in HK were eligible and were invited via WhatsApp (an instant messaging tool) by convenient sampling to complete the survey.

For part 2, participants aged 18 years or above and with prior CVD and who lived around the collaborating primary healthcare centres are eligible for inclusion. Participants over 80 years old with dementia or a history of psychiatric diseases were not included in this study as they are less likely to benefit from the medication education.

Data analysis

For part 1, all survey data were exported from Qualtrics. Incomplete responses and submissions after the recruitment period were excluded. For duplicate submissions from the same IP address, only the most recent completed response was included to avoid duplication. To assess whether stronger beliefs in CVD risk prediction tools and current health conditions (including self-reported chronic or medical conditions or CVD history) influenced health-seeking behaviour based on CVD risk scores, we conducted a univariate regression analysis.

For part 2, we described participants’ and service providers’ perceptions of P-CARDIAC and CVD risk prediction tools before the pharmacist-led service, along with feedback, satisfaction, medication perceptions and adherence after the service. Cronbach’s alpha assessed the reliability and validity of these measures, with values above 0.6 considered acceptable.11–14 The means with SD for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables were described. Additionally, we compared 1-year to 10-year P-CARDIAC scores using both basic (15 risk factors) and full (120 risk factors) models between the first and second visits. Changes in cardiovascular risk factors—low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total cholesterol, fasting glucose, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP)—were also examined between these visits.

A significance level of 5% was used for all analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel, SPSS V.26 or R. Data cleaning and analysis were independently cross-checked by two researchers to ensure accuracy.

Internet use and knowledge of CVD risk prediction tools

In the general population, 89.4% sought health info online monthly, and 89.4% preferred Traditional Chinese methods for health information. Only 33.6% had heard of CVD risk prediction tools, and just 12.3% had used one (online supplemental appendix table 2). Website and healthcare professional recommendations were common sources, at 16.8% and 14.1%, respectively. Among users, 62% were concerned about their health. Among clinicians, 76.5% had used CVD risk prediction tools before, but only 23.5% had communicated CVD risk to patients (online supplemental appendix table 3). Main barriers included unavailability at work (29.1%), perceived inaccuracy (16.7%), lack of user-friendliness (16.7%), difficulty explaining results (16.7%) and low generalisability to HK populations (12.5%).

Expectation of the CVD risk prediction model

Most of the general population preferred a mobile app (71.7%) in Traditional Chinese (89.4%) and trusted the model’s evidence from a reputable HK university (97.4%). About 81.4% and 68.1% would consult clinicians if they had a high 3-year or 5-year CVD risk score, respectively. Half wanted to know their risk within 2 years (online supplemental appendix table 2). Clinicians preferred tools supporting automated variable input (82.4%) with options for manual adjustment (88.2%). Most favoured risk estimates over 1–5 years (58.8%) and desired explanations, recommendations and suggestions (94.1%) (online supplemental appendix table 3).

Participants were given a short introduction to a CVD risk prediction tool and a hypothetical new risk prediction model. We found that those who trusted the new model were more likely to consult doctors if they received high-risk results at 3, 5 or 10 years (OR 1.97; 95% CI 1.05 to 3.70; OR 1.97; 95% CI 1.15 to 3.38; OR 1.64; 95% CI 1.00 to 2.69). Those aware of the CVD risk prediction tools were more likely to seek consultation if high risk was identified at 3 years (OR 5.07; 95% CI 1.11 to 23.14) (table 2).

Perception on P-CARDIAC

Most (46.67%) participants had not heard of P-CARDIAC; however, 81.81% of those who responded (n=11) believed knowing their CVD risk would help manage their condition, and 72.73% thought it could improve medication habits (online supplemental appendix table 4). Half of the pharmacists and volunteers agreed that P-CARDIAC scores are important for medication education and could enhance adherence (online supplemental appendix table 5).

Feedback and satisfaction of using pharmacist-led services and P-CARDIAC score

Over half of the participants who completed the study (n=9) reported that P-CARDIAC scores were ‘helpful’ or ‘very helpful’ for managing health and medication compliance. Most (88.9%) believed pharmacist-led services contributed to reducing CVD risk (online supplemental appendix table 6). Eighty per cent of pharmacists and volunteers found P-CARDIAC helpful for demonstrating medication adherence, with 80% feeling their assessments closely matched the tool’s results. Sixty per cent indicated the scores could influence their assessments and 80% believed the tool was helpful for preventive strategies, with 60% rating it as ‘very helpful’. Overall, feedback was positive, supporting P-CARDIAC’s role in medication management and patient care (online supplemental appendix table 7).

Medication perception and adherence

Most participants (87%) scored above the midpoint on the BMQ, indicating they viewed medication as necessary for their health (mean: 17.87, SD: 2.03). One participant was excluded from the PSM analysis due to missing data. The mean PSM score was 10.7 (SD: 2.8). Regarding self-reported adherence assessed with MARS-5, scores ranged from 15 to 25, with a median of 24; forgetting medication was the most common reason for non-adherence (online supplemental appendix table 8). Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.62 for BMQ, 0.76 for PSM-5 and 0.81 for MARS-5, indicating acceptable reliability.

P-CARDIAC scores and risk factor changes after pharmacist-led service

The average 1-year P-CARDIAC scores using the basic model were 0.71 at both visits; for the full model, scores were 0.70 at the first and 0.72 at the second visit. Since scores were only calculated for the DHC/E subgroup at both visits, only data from this group are reported. The mean scores for both models are shown in figure 3. Figure 4 illustrates a more significant change in P-CARDIAC scores for the full model compared to the basic model. The average change of P-CARDIAC score between first and second visit in both basic and full model was significant (basic: p value <0.001 (95% CI 0.27 to 0.39); full: p value <0.001 (95% CI 0.82 to 1.29)). All participants maintained optimal management of common risk factors within normal ranges. No statistically significant differences (p values range from 0.53 to 1.00; 95% CIs range from 0.52 to 0.95) were observed in cardiovascular laboratory results between baseline and follow-up visit (table 4).

Discussion

This study identified that awareness of CVD risk prediction tool is low among general population. While clinicians, particularly cardiologists, know of these said tools, they have limited experience using them to communicate with patients, possibly due to lack of standardised tools in the current healthcare setting and limited access to the tool at workplace. Part 2 of the study demonstrated that the locally developed CVD risk prediction tool, P-CARDIAC, can be implemented in the clinical setting with favourable feedback from both the healthcare providers and the patients.

In part 1, we found that the general public’s health awareness is high; however, their knowledge of CVD risk prediction tools remains very low. Although the majority of participants regularly seek health information, most have never heard of any risk prediction tools. Interestingly, among the few who have used a risk prediction tool, their awareness of health was the main motivation. We also observed that the general public is more likely to seek further medical support if they have high predicted CVD risk scores, with a shorter-term risk prediction correlating with a higher willingness to consult doctors. Predicted CVD risk seems to encourage individuals to seek professional medical advice for early prevention, especially since HK people are highly concerned about their health status. This aligns with the idea that personalised risk presentation, like the P-CARDIAC score, makes individuals more likely to take proactive steps towards preventing CVD and prioritising their cardiovascular health.15

Additionally, our pragmatic pilot study demonstrated that P-CARDIAC’s user-friendly scoring presentation is generally well-received by both participants and health service providers. It enhances patients’ understanding of their CVD risk and facilitates better management of their cardiovascular health and medication adherence. The findings also suggest that P-CARDIAC acts as a helpful tool, enabling patients to effectively grasp their risk levels and take early preventive actions while our participants received high risk in a short term. Furthermore, our cross-sectional survey reported that 97.4% of participants trust the CVD risk prediction model if it is evidence-based and developed by a reputable local university. Our data indicate that trust in these tools is a key factor influencing healthcare-seeking behaviour. We are confident that P-CARDIAC, developed by local researchers to meet local healthcare needs, will be well-received by the public and will support enhanced cardiovascular risk prevention efforts.

Although many cardiologists have used some risk prediction tools before, only 23.5% have ever communicated risk results to patients using such tools. Several barriers hinder implementation in real-world settings. First, there are no standardised CVD risk prediction tools available within the HA or the Department of Health. Second, most existing tools lack user-friendly interfaces, making it difficult to explain results to patients. Third, some clinicians expressed concerns about trusting the predicted results, citing low generalisability of current tools to HK populations. P-CARDIAC was developed using local EHR data, specifically tailored for seamless integration into the HK healthcare system. Our prior publications6 have shown P-CARDIAC’s excellent performance compared with existing risk scores, which support clinician confidence given its alignment with local healthcare context.

The pragmatic pilot study further demonstrated that pharmacist-led services are well received among our participants. The common CVD risk factors, such as SBP/DBP and LDL-C, remained stable between the first and second visits. The absolute change between the two visits, in both basic and full model, was statistically significant, showing that P-CARDIAC was sensitive in detecting any change in risk factors for real-time risk prediction. The full model of P-CARDIAC was responsive to changes in risk factors within relatively brief follow-up period (average follow-up of 72 days), indicating its potential capability for monitoring cardiovascular risk over time. This sensitivity is attributed to the robust design of P-CARDIAC, which is designed to be compatible with the structured EHR data, allowing integration into existing health record platforms. As it underscores its potential utility in real-world healthcare settings, its sensitivity allows for timely interventions and adjustments in patient care. We expect P-CARDIAC could also streamline clinical workflows by extracting key information for risk estimation and providing concise summaries of patients’ medical histories. Integrating P-CARDIAC into clinical management systems has the potential to increase efficiency, reduce errors related to unstructured clinical notes and support the development of tailored treatment plans.

Our research also demonstrated that P-CARDIAC is applicable for use by various healthcare professionals, including pharmacists. In the follow-up, pharmacists identified some prescription issues, such as minor adverse events and unclear medication instructions. These issues were addressed through discussions between pharmacists and cardiologists before patients’ next SOPC visit. Given the long waiting times for specialist consultations in HK’s public hospitals, pharmacist-led consultations using the risk prediction model during waiting periods can identify and rectify medication issues early. We believe that equipping pharmacists with the skills to effectively use P-CARDIAC could elevate the quality of pharmacist-led services, thereby ensuring better outcomes and prevention for patients with CVD. We also anticipate that implementing P-CARDIAC could serve as a valuable risk stratification tool to assist clinicians in triaging low-risk cases to pharmacist-led services, easing the demand for specialist consultations. Our proposed referral framework showed the potential about how P-CARDIAC can support primary healthcare development and foster greater integration within HK’s healthcare system.

Limitation

This study introduced several limitations. The survey question was mainly designed objectively by our research team since no current study was applicable for this new development risk model. We conducted an internal validation by different parties to ensure the survey was well designed. The use of convenience sampling may limit the generalisability of our results, as the participants may not fully represent the broader population. We plan to use an implementation science framework, such as RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance), in our future study to support the feasibility of the P-CARDIAC implementation. The follow-up period was relatively short for the pragmatic pilot study, with an average of 72 days in the DHC/E subgroup and 22.8 days in the AKA subgroup. This limited timeframe may not capture the long-term impact of pharmacist-led services on CVD outcomes or medication compliance. Although the lost to follow-up rate is acceptable in the pilot study, we should strengthen the pharmacist-led services training and the connections with participants with better and more comprehensive services in the primary care in the future. Besides, due to the limited sample size and non-random sampling in both survey and pilot phases, the findings may not be generalisable to the broader population. Further research should explore the integration with control group and additional components, such as lifestyle consultation, dietary guidance in pharmacist-led service and objective feedback questions to quantify the implementation of P-CARDIAC in different healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study provides insight into user perceptions on existing CVD risk prediction tools and supports the design and preliminary evaluation of P-CARDIAC. The pragmatic pilot study demonstrated the feasibility and potential benefits of integrating P-CARDIAC into pharmacist-led services in the HK primary healthcare system. There is a notable opportunity for the implementation of P-CARDIAC in the current healthcare system, as it has been tailored for the local healthcare EHR system. We anticipate that this model will be well-received and widely accepted within the healthcare community. While the preliminary findings are positive, further research is needed to refine implementation strategies of P-CARDIAC, map out the relevant interventions and evaluate its long-term impact on patient outcomes. By addressing current limitations and building on the strengths of this study, we can design and conduct a more optimised and user-friendly P-CARDIAC interface and pharmacist-led services for cardiovascular risk prevention and management.
