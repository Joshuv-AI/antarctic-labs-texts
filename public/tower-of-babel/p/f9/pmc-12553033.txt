Sex and Age Differences in the Severity of Dizziness Complaints in Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV)
Authors: Emre Gürses, Esma Akış, İsa Tuncay Batuk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the relationship between clinical features, BPPV subtypes, and dizziness complaints in patients with BPPV to identify factors contributing to dizziness distress.MethodsThis study included 129 patients diagnosed with BPPV. Demographic and clinical features of the patients were determined. The degree of dizziness was assessed using the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS).ResultsFemale patients experienced longer BPPV durations and higher dizziness‐related distress. Recurrent BPPV was more common in females (46.5%), with higher physical distress reported in these patients. Cupulolithiasis was associated with significantly higher emotional and functional DHI scores. There was no correlation between age and dizziness severity, but a positive correlation was found between age and BPPV duration in patients without recurrence.ConclusionDizziness severity is influenced by sex, BPPV subtype, and recurrence. Females and patients with cupulolithiasis experience greater dizziness distress, particularly in functional and emotional domains.

Introduction

Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV) is the most common peripheral vestibular end‐organ disease associated with nystagmus and vertigo during changes in head position [1]. When otoconia become dislodged from the utricle, they can enter the semicircular canals and disrupt the normal flow of endolymph. This disturbance most often occurs in the posterior semicircular canal. This process induces vertigo and nystagmus [2]. Liberated otoconia may float freely within the canal (canalithiasis) or adhere to the cupula (cupulolithiasis) [3]. The sizes and movement patterns of the otoconia and their interactions with the canal walls and other particles cause nystagmus to occur in different forms and intensities [4]. It is commonly believed that the displacement of the cupula's volume correlates directly with the perceived rotation rate and subsequent eye movements [5]. Although there has been significant progress in understanding the various BPPV subtypes and their related nystagmus patterns, there is still a lack of comprehensive knowledge regarding the impact of these subtypes on dizziness and how this is manifested in patient‐reported symptoms.

Various canalith repositioning (CRP) maneuvers have been developed depending on the location of the liberated otoconia. The results of meta‐analysis studies indicate that CRP is a safe and effective treatment for BPPV [6, 7]. Even after successful repositioning maneuvers, some patients describe residual nonspecific symptoms, such as feeling light‐headed without positional vertigo. Increased postural instability that persists for several weeks after treatment of BPPV has been extensively demonstrated by posturography studies [8]. Dizziness in BPPV has been associated with the length of vertigo episodes prior to treatment. Therefore, it is important to also take into account other factors such as patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and comorbid conditions [9].

The literature on BPPV predominantly emphasizes conditions related to vertigo complaints, including the type of BPPV, treatment options, and the nature of nystagmus. However, there are only a limited number of studies addressing the complaint of dizziness in BPPV patients. Mi Jo Kim et al. [10] found that the Dizziness Handicap Inventory scores of lateral canal BPPV patients were higher than those in the anterior and posterior canals. It has been reported that the reason for this situation may be that the 30° angular location of the lateral canal may cause dizziness in an upright position. Camilla Martens et al. [11] identified a correlation between higher DHI scores and both lateral canal BPPV and female gender. It has been reported that lateral canal BPPV patients tend to experience a longer duration of symptoms compared to those with other canal involvements, which could contribute to this association.

BPPV can be treated successfully to a large extent with correct assessment and appropriate maneuvers. Severe dizziness attacks are greatly reduced after treatment. BPPV inherently involves both relapse and spontaneous or treatment‐induced recovery. Despite successful therapeutic maneuvers, patients may continue to experience dizziness. To investigate the patient‐related factors contributing to persistent dizziness, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between dizziness complaints in BPPV patients and the clinical features of BPPV. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the relationship between clinical features, BPPV subtypes, and dizziness complaints in patients with BPPV. Secondary objectives included examining sex and age differences in dizziness severity and BPPV duration, comparing dizziness severity between BPPV subtypes, evaluating the role of recurrence history, and assessing the clinical relevance of dizziness distress using minimum clinically important difference thresholds.

Material and Methods

Participants

This study was a cross‐sectional observational study conducted at Hacettepe University. The study received approval from the Ethics Board for Non‐Interventional Clinical Research at Hacettepe University under reference number GO 21/532. All individual participants involved in the study provided informed consent. Patients referred with a history that suggested they might have BPPV following the initial ENT examination were assessed for potential inclusion in the study. Participants were excluded if they met the following criteria: (a) History of accompanied central or peripheral vestibular disorders, (b) Different patterns of nystagmus suggesting more than one type of BPPV, known as mixed‐type BPPV, (c) Presence of significant neurological, psychiatric, or musculoskeletal conditions that could affect balance or dizziness perception.

Diagnosis

Their eligibility was determined by confirming active BPPV according to internationally recognized diagnostic criteria following an ENT consultation [12]. The diagnosis of BPPV relied on a blend of patient history, subjective symptoms, and objective findings in the Frenzel glasses or VNG eye records. Paroxysmal torsional up‐beating nystagmus with the torsional component directed toward the undermost ear during the modified Dix‐Hallpike maneuver for Posterior‐Canal BPPV (PC‐BPPV) was noted. In the same maneuver, a vertical down‐beating nystagmus with a torsional component and fatigability was considered indicative of anterior canal BPPV (Ant‐BPPV). Horizontal canal BPPV (HC‐BPPV) was distinguished by either paroxysmal geotropic (toward the undermost ear) or apogeotropic (away from the undermost ear) nystagmus observed in a head roll test position. Canalolithiasis or cupulolithiasis subtypes were inferred based on the nystagmus patterns observed during positional testing. Specifically, canalolithiasis was identified by nystagmus with a brief latency, transient duration, and fatigability; whereas cupulolithiasis was suspected when nystagmus appeared immediately, was persistent (lasting > 1 min), and showed no fatigability. These distinctions are consistent with widely accepted clinical criteria described by Fife et al. [13] This study excluded other atypical nystagmus due to potential confounding factors, such as central non‐fatiguing or non‐paroxysmal nystagmus. Patients with symptoms and a history of BPPV but no objective, recordable nystagmus (sub‐clinical BPPV) on the first evaluation were also excluded from the study.

Questionnaires

This study used a variety of scales and forms to collect patient‐reported symptoms. The demographic information form questioned for age, gender, a history of BPPV recurrence, the time from symptom onset to the first clinical appointment, and any concurrent medical conditions.

The degree of dizziness was assessed using the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS). The DHI, developed by Jacobson and Newman in 1990, consists of 25 items categorized into three dimensions: physical, functional, and emotional [14]. It comprises questions addressing patients' physical, functional, and emotional well‐being. Each question offers three response options: “yes” (4 points), “sometimes” (2 points), or “no” (0 points). The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with scores interpreted as indicating no handicap (0–14 points), mild handicap (16–34 points), moderate handicap (36–52 points), and severe handicap (54 points and above).

The Turkish version of the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) was used in the current study [15]. As reported in the Turkish validation study, the Cronbach's alpha for the total score was 0.92, indicating excellent internal consistency. The Cronbach's alpha values for the subscales were 0.83 for the emotional, 0.88 for the functional, and 0.67 for the physical subscale. Test–retest reliability coefficients for the 25 items ranged from 0.310 to 0.917.

The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was used to assess the patient's current perception of dizziness severity at the time of clinical evaluation, during the active BPPV episode, and prior to any repositioning maneuvers. The scale consisted of a 100 mm horizontal line labeled “no dizziness” on the left and “unbearable dizziness” on the right. Patients were instructed to mark a point on the line that best reflected their present dizziness severity. The score was determined by measuring the distance in millimeters from the left end of the line to the point indicated by the patient. The VAS was designed to capture the overall severity of the patient's subjective dizziness experience, including sensations such as spinning, imbalance, or lightheadedness. Our study employed a one‐question VAS to assess the present severity of dizziness. “Please indicate your present dizziness severity on the 10 cm line below.”

Statistical Analyses

The IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 23, was utilized for statistical analyses (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). To ascertain if the variables were regularly distributed, analytic (Kolmogorov–Smirnov) and visual (histograms, probability plots) methods were used. Standard deviations and means were employed in the presentation of descriptive analyses. Comparisons of BPPV subtypes and the presence of recurrence with dizziness severity were analyzed using independent samples t‐tests, except for BPPV duration, which was analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlation with BPPV duration, age, and severity of dizziness was analyzed using the Pearson Correlation test. A Type‐I error level of 5% was applied overall to determine statistical significance. To control for the risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to adjust p‐values, with a false discovery rate set at 5%. Effect sizes for t‐tests were calculated using Cohen's d, based on the formula: (M 1 − M 2)/√((SD1 2 + SD2 2)/2). For the Mann–Whitney U test, effect sizes were calculated using the formula r = Z/√N.

Results

Demographic Features and Subjective Dizziness Complaints

Eighty‐six females (mean age: 45.42 ± 1.24 years) and forty‐three males (mean age: 46.98 ± 1.86 years) were compared in terms of age, BPPV duration, DHI subscale and total scores, and VAS scores.

No statistically significant differences were found in age [t(127) = −0.708, p = 0.48] or VAS scores (female mean: 4.4 ± 2.9; male mean: 3.6 ± 3.0) [t(127) = 1.46, p = 0.14].

Although females demonstrated higher scores than males in DHI total (female mean: 53.4 ± 22.3; male mean: 43.9 ± 21.1) [t(127) = 2.31, p = 0.02, Cohen's d = −0.43], DHI physical (female mean: 17.7 ± 5.8; male mean: 15.5 ± 5.9) [t(118) = 2.01, p = 0.04, Cohen's d = 0.38], DHI emotional (female mean: 14.94 ± 9.3; male mean: 12.4 ± 9.9) [t(118) = 1.40, p = 0.16, Cohen's d = 0.27], and DHI functional (female mean: 20.48 ± 10.3; male mean: 16.3 ± 9.8) [t(118) = 2.25, p = 0.03, Cohen's d = 0.41] scores, these differences did not remain statistically significant after Benjamini‐Hochberg correction (adjusted p > 0.05).

However, in the female group, the duration of BPPV was significantly longer compared to the male group (Z = −2.27; p = 0.006), with a small effect size (r = 0.20). The correlation between age and subjective dizziness complaints was evaluated separately in patients with and without BPPV recurrence. Age was not correlated with subjective dizziness complaints in patients with and without BPPV recurrence. However, there was a statistically significant, small‐to‐moderate positive correlation between age and BPPV duration in patients without BPPV recurrence (r = 0.305, p = 0.008). In bilateral BPPV patients, there was a strong, statistically significant negative correlation between DHI physical scores and age (r = −0.73, p = 0.03).

Clinical Features and Subjective Dizziness Complaints

Patients with and without a previous BPPV experience were compared regarding dizziness distress. The results showed that the physical subscale of DHI was statistically higher in patients with recurrent BPPV (p = 0.03). However, there were no statistical differences in VAS, DHI emotional, functional, and total scores (p > 0.05). The involved side of BPPV was not found to be statistically related to the dizziness distress in terms of VAS (p = 0.66), DHI total (p = 0.93), DHI emotional (p = 0.84), DHI functional (p = 0.74), and DHI physical (p = 0.27).

The subtypes of BPPV were analyzed as canalolithiasis and cupulolithiasis. Among the patients, 105 (81.4%) were diagnosed with canalolithiasis and 24 (18.6%) with cupulolithiasis. Although patients with cupulolithiasis showed higher mean scores across all dizziness distress measures compared to those with canalolithiasis, statistical analysis indicated that the differences were not statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted p > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study explored the relationship between dizziness complaints and clinical features in patients with BPPV. Dizziness distress was evaluated by using the DHI. Although DHI is a self‐reported subjective method, numerous studies have shown that it is a valuable tool for predicting BPPV [5, 16]. Additionally, VAS was used to assess the perception of vertigo and dizziness, which is another widely used method for evaluating BPPV patients [11, 17, 18].

In our study, the number of female patients was nearly double that of male patients, with females experiencing a longer duration of BPPV and overall higher dizziness‐related distress. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified sex differences in the severity of dizziness and vertigo symptoms. The literature indicates that females are more frequently associated with vestibular vertigo [19, 20], and a higher prevalence of BPPV has been reported in women [21, 22]. Moreover, a meta‐analysis provided strong evidence that female sex is a risk factor for the occurrence of BPPV [23].

Higher functional subscale scores observed in women suggest that dizziness more severely impacts daily activities. Psychological and hormonal factors have been discussed in previous literature as potential contributors to such sex‐related differences, although these factors were not directly measured in the present study. In particular, hormonal changes related to the menstrual cycle or oral contraceptive use have been associated with an increased risk of vestibular dysfunction [19, 20]. Furthermore, estrogen deficiency during the postmenopausal period has been associated with an increased occurrence and recurrence of BPPV [24].

Estrogen is known to regulate calcium metabolism and is thought to play a critical role in maintaining the stability of otoconia within the inner ear. Animal studies have demonstrated that bilaterally ovariectomized osteoporotic rats exhibit larger otoconia with reduced density compared to controls, supporting the hypothesis that estrogen deficiency leads to structural otoconia degeneration [25]. Such degeneration may increase the susceptibility to otoconial dislodgment and, consequently, the risk of BPPV.

In addition to hormonal influences, anatomical differences in the peripheral vestibular system may also contribute to sex‐related differences in dizziness severity. Specifically, the width of the peripheral vestibular structures in the anteroposterior plane has been found to be significantly smaller in women than in men [26]. A smaller vestibular apparatus may alter endolymph dynamics or otoconial interactions within the semicircular canals, potentially increasing the risk of vestibular dysfunction. Taken together, the combination of hormonal influences on otoconia metabolism and anatomical differences in the vestibular system may help explain the higher incidence, longer duration, and greater dizziness‐related distress observed in female patients with BPPV.

Although no correlation was found between age and dizziness complaints, our study revealed a significant positive correlation between age and the duration of BPPV in patients without BPPV recurrence. This may be because of age‐related deterioration of sensory and motor mechanisms [27]. In the elderly, factors such as difficulty in obtaining an accurate medical history, restricted mobility, and complications with diagnostic and treatment maneuvers due to vascular and orthopedic issues may contribute to delays in BPPV diagnosis, resulting in prolonged symptoms [28]. Interestingly, in patients with bilateral BPPV, a strong negative correlation was observed between DHI physical scores and age, indicating that younger patients report more severe physical dizziness symptoms. While one possible explanation could be differences in daily activity levels, physical activity was not measured in the present study, and therefore this interpretation remains speculative.

Recurrent BPPV is reported to be relatively common among BPPV cases, with a higher incidence in females [23]. Our results are consistent with these studies, showing BPPV recurrence rates of 46.5% in female and 34.9% in male patients. These patients with recurrent BPPV exhibited greater vulnerability in the physical aspects of dizziness, as indicated by higher scores on the DHI physical subscale. This observation suggests that recurrent episodes might lead to a heightened physical perception of dizziness. This finding is consistent with previous literature that has reported persistent postural instability and physical discomfort even after successful canalith repositioning maneuvers in recurrent BPPV cases [29]. On the other hand, our study found no significant relationship between BPPV recurrence and dizziness severity, as measured by the VAS and DHI emotional, functional, and total scores.

The different pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these subtypes of BPPV may contribute to varying levels of dizziness distress. Cupulolithiasis, which involves otoconia adhering to the cupula, is characterized by a longer duration of vertigo and is more resistant to repositioning maneuvers. Although statistically significant differences were initially observed in the DHI Emotional and Functional subscale scores between canalolithiasis and cupulolithiasis groups, these did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons.

In interpreting our findings, it is important to distinguish between statistical significance and clinical relevance. The mean difference in DHI Total scores between canalolithiasis and cupulolithiasis patients (9.4 points) did not exceed the established MCID threshold of 11–18 points [14], indicating limited clinical importance despite statistical significance. By contrast, the differences observed in the DHI Emotional and Functional subscales (5.1 points each) approached or slightly exceeded the MCID range of 4–5 points, suggesting potential clinical relevance even though statistical significance was lost after correction for multiple comparisons. Similarly, the VAS difference of 1.0 point met the lower threshold of clinical importance (1.0–1.5 points) [30]. Taken together, these findings emphasize that some observed effects, although modest and not always statistically robust, may still have practical significance in patient care and warrant consideration in clinical decision‐making. This is likely why patients with cupulolithiasis report higher distress levels in this present study, especially on the emotional and functional subscales of the DHI, compared to those with canalolithiasis. Previous studies have also noted that patients with cupulolithiasis often require more repositioning maneuvers [31] and experience longer recovery periods [32], which could contribute to increased dizziness distress.

Posterior canal BPPV is the most prevalent subtype [33]. In our study, posterior canal BPPV constituted 90.6% of cases, with a higher incidence in the right ear compared to the left. This predominance is likely attributed to the anatomical structure of the posterior canal, which increases its susceptibility to otoconia displacement. Additionally, the higher occurrence of BPPV in the right ear may be influenced by factors such as head position during sleep and daily activities.

This study has some limitations, including its observational and cross‐sectional design, which precludes establishing causal relationships between clinical factors and dizziness severity. Additionally, the reliance on self‐reported measures for dizziness severity may introduce bias. Future research should aim to include larger and more diverse populations and use objective measures of vestibular function, such as Computerized Dynamic Posturography, to validate self‐reported data. Furthermore, longitudinal studies are needed to provide deeper insights into the long‐term impact of BPPV subtypes and recurrences on dizziness and overall quality of life. Future studies may also benefit from using regression analyses, which would allow for adjustment of covariates and provide more precise estimates of the independent contribution of each factor to dizziness severity, including psychological symptoms such as anxiety and depression. A post hoc power analysis was conducted based on the observed effect size (d = 0.37), the sample size (N = 129), and a significance level of α = 0.05. The achieved statistical power was approximately 62%, indicating that the study may have been underpowered to reliably detect small‐to‐moderate effects. In addition, the unequal sex distribution, with nearly twice as many female participants as males, may have limited the ability to detect sex‐specific differences and restricts the generalizability of the findings to male patients. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts are warranted to strengthen external validity.

The clinical implications of these findings are extensive. Firstly, healthcare providers should recognize the potential for increased dizziness severity in female patients and those with recurrent BPPV. For these groups, tailored management strategies, such as more frequent follow‐ups and targeted vestibular rehabilitation, may prove beneficial. Secondly, understanding how BPPV subtypes affect dizziness distress can inform the development of specific therapeutic approaches that address the unique challenges associated with cupulolithiasis. For example, customized vestibular rehabilitation programs designed to meet the specific needs of patients with cupulolithiasis could potentially alleviate their symptoms more effectively.

In conclusion, our study highlights the significance of considering demographic and clinical characteristics when evaluating and treating dizziness in BPPV patients. Customizing treatment strategies to fit individual patient profiles can improve the effectiveness of BPPV management and patient outcomes. Incorporating patient‐specific factors into treatment protocols marks an important advancement toward personalized medicine in the management of vestibular disorders.
