Development of injectable cuttlebone derived nanohydroxyapatite hydrogel for osteoblast cell encapsulation
Authors: Premjit Arpornmaeklong, Akapong Kongjaroen, Surisa Kangwarnwiboon, Onkanya Vajatieng, Komsan Apinyauppatham, Supakorn Boonyuen, Sarute Ummartyotin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study focused on creating bioactive, injectable hydrogels for bone regeneration, utilizing cuttlebone derived nanohydroxyapatite (CB-nHA) and quercetin (QT), a natural flavonoid, to enhance their capacity. It explored how different ratios of CB-nHA and chitosan/collagen, along with QT, in calcium carbonate (CaCO3-QT) microcapsules influenced the injectability and osteoconductive properties of thermosensitive chitosan/collagen hydrogels. The hydrogels were prepared with varying concentrations of CB-nHA (0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % w/v) and chitosan/collagen ratios (7:1 and 14:1 w/w). Their microstructure, mechanical, and physical properties, rheological and injectability tests were examined to identify optimal hydrogel's formulations. Additionally, cytotoxicity and osteoconductivity of the hydrogel were evaluated using encapsulated human fetal osteoblasts (hFOB) obtained from ATCC (USA). It was found that higher concentrations of CB-nHA and chitosan reduced pore size and degradation while increasing complex modulus and injection force. The CaCO3-QT promoted cell growth by effectively storing and releasing QT. The optimal formulation, a 5 % CB-nHA and 1 % CaCO3-QT hydrogel with a 7:1 chitosan/collagen ratio, was injectable and exhibited a pore size of 116 ± 47 μm. The hydrogel encapsulated cells demonstrated high viability and extensive intercellular connections, with ALP activity levels significantly higher than those in the groups with 0 % CB-nHA and CaCO3 alone (p < 0.05). In conclusion, the CB-nHA and CaCO3-QT enhanced the osteoconductive property of the injectable hydrogel. A thermosensitive hydrogel composed of 5 % CB-nHA, 1 % CaCO3-QT, and a 7:1 ratio of chitosan to collagen is a bioactive and injectable carrier for osteoblasts and QT to enhance bone regeneration.

Introduction

The demand for alveolar bone augmentation is increasing alongside the rising need for dental implant prostheses in an aging society [1]. This shift highlights the limitations of autogenous bone grafting, primarily due to the decreased availability of autologous bone and the diminished capacity for bone regeneration in older individuals [1,2]. Therefore, developing biomimetic bone substitutes using biomaterials—such as chitosan/collagen composites and calcium phosphates, including hydroxyapatite—becomes essential for enhancing bone regeneration and repair. These materials mimic the natural bone matrix, promoting cell adhesion and growth whil supporting osteogenic differentiation [[3], [4], [5]]. Additionally, a thermosensitive hydrogel should be injectable to facilitate minimally invasive surgery for bone augmentation, potentially reducing surgical-related complications and improving access for medically compromised patients, particularly the aging population [6].

Agricultural waste, often seen as a byproduct of food production, can be repurposed for sustainable development. Cuttlebone can be transformed into cuttlebone derived nanohydroxyapatite particles (CB-nHA) [7,8], which offer a promising alternative to synthetic and allogenic calcium phosphate bone grafting materials (Kangkan et al., 2020; Tangsuksant et al., 2023). Cuttlebone, derived from squid backbones, is a marine waste rich in calcium carbonate (CaCO3) and trace elements, historically used in medicine. It is a viable source for producing calcium phosphate particles, including hydroxyapatite (HA), β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), and biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) for biomedical applications [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. When heated, calcium carbonate converts to calcium oxide (CaO), which, through a hydrothermal process, becomes CB-nHA [8]. These nanoparticles, sized between 30 and 150 nm, have a chemical structure similar to bone HA. They are non-cytotoxic and promote the osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of dental pulp stem cells [7]. Thus, CB-nHA is a natural and cost-effective option for bone regeneration.

Chitosan and collagen, natural biopolymers that resemble the bone matrix, are both biocompatible and biodegradable. Chitosan is derived from crustacean shells, while collagen provides structural support in tissues. When combined into a hydrogel, these materials form a three-dimensional network that retains water and promotes cellular activity, supporting cell adhesion, growth, and osteogenic differentiation [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. The chitosan/collagen hydrogel is highly adaptable and has gained interest as a bone substitute, facilitating minimally invasive surgeries and delivering bioactive molecules and stem cells to challenging areas [15,16]. However, its low mechanical strength and rapid degradation limit its applications [[16], [17], [18]]. Efforts to enhance its properties include incorporating additional natural polymers [14,19,20] and calcium phosphate materials, which improve mechanical strength and osteoconductive properties [5,9,21,22]. The thermosensitive chitosan/collagen hydrogel can also deliver natural flavonoids to promote bone formation and can be customized for optimal mechanical strength and porosity [[11], [12], [13],16]. Incorporating bioactive molecules, such as BMP-2 and flavonoids, enhances the effectiveness of these hydrogels [4,12,22], promoting the growth of both local and transplanted cells and improving bone integration [4,23]. This bioactive property makes them a promising solution for alveolar bone augmentation.

The CaCO3-chitosan/collagen hydrogel is a biocompatible material that is well-suited for biomedical applications. It is non-cytotoxic and causes minimal tissue reactions when implanted subcutaneously in mice. This hydrogel also serves as a storage and delivery vehicle for natural antioxidants, such as quercetin, an abundant flavonoid found in fruits and vegetables. This characteristic helps reduce oxidative stress and promotes osteogenic differentiation [24,25]. Quercetin has antioxidative properties and can enhance the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells [26]. The interaction between the negative charges of hydrophobic flavonoids and the positive charges of chitosan in the chitosan/collagen composite matrix facilitates the retention and controlled release of these flavonoids, which stimulates the growth and osteogenic differentiation of encapsulated mesenchymal stem cells [22,25]. Additionally, the presence of CaCO3 microcapsules within the thermosensitive nHA-chitosan/collagen hydrogel enhances both its structural stability and strength. The Ca2+ and CO32− ions from CaCO3 can form ionic interactions with the amine groups of chitosan, thereby stabilizing the composite structure. As a biocompatible material, CaCO3 is commonly used in biomedical applications [24,27,28]. Calcium-rich molecules derived from CaCO3 also promote the mineralization of the chitosan/collagen matrix, which improves its mechanical properties and suitability for applications such as wound healing and bone tissue engineering [24,29,30]. Moreover, the physical entrapment of quercetin's hydrophobic molecules within the porous surfaces of the microcapsules, combined with their physical bonding to chitosan, helps prolong the release of flavonoids from CaCO3 [27,31].

Based on these considerations, we incorporated nanohydroxyapatite from a natural source (CB-nHA), used calcium carbonate microcapsules as carriers for the controlled release of quercetin, a natural antioxidant (CaCO3-QT) and varied chitosan/collagen weight ratios to improve the porous structure, strength, injectability and bioactive property of the thermosensitive nHA-CaCO3-β glycerophosphate chitosan/collagen hydrogel for controlled drug release and stem cell encapsulation to promote bone regeneration. The incorporation of CB-nHA and CaCO3-QT into a thermosensitive CaCO3-chitosan/collagen hydrogel underscores its potential as a multifunctional biomaterial for bone tissue engineering applications. This approach promotes the use of biosynthesis and sustainable biomaterials.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of cuttlebone derived nanohydroxyapatite and transmission electron microscopy analyses

The cuttlebone derived nanohydroxyapatite (CB-nHA) used in this study was sourced from the same batch as in our previous research [7]. Calcium oxide (CaO) was obtained from cuttlefish (Sepiida) in southern Thailand, dissolved in phosphoric acid (H3PO4), and precipitated with ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH) at pH 9. The resulting white sediment was rinsed with deionized water, vacuum filtered, dried at 90 °C, and calcined at 700 °C for 4 h.

Characterization of the CB-nHA particles included scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive spectrophotometry (EDS), physicochemical analyses, and dynamic light scattering (DLS) for size measurement. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed rod-like particles averaging 30 nm, while SEM showed small particles forming a porous structure, resulting in a larger particle size of 304.73 ± 4.19 nm (DLS analysis). X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed the crystalline nature of the CB-nHA, and EDS analysis provided a calcium-to-phosphorus (Ca/P) ratio of 1.44 with trace elements. The CB-nHA particles were found to be non-cytotoxic and supportive of osteogenic activity [7].

Preparation of calcium carbonate microcapsules and quercetin encapsulation and release

Preparation of calcium carbonate microcapsules

Microcapsules of pristine calcium carbonate (CaCO3) were synthesized through the direct precipitation of sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) (Cat No. 223530) and calcium chloride (CaCl2) (Cat No. C4901) at a controlled temperature of 40 °C. To obtain CaCO3 with (CaCO3-QT) as well as without quercetin (CaCO3 alone), 100 μL of a 100,000 μg/mL quercetin (Cat No, 337951) in DMSO (Cat No. D2650) or DMSO was added dropwise to 200 mL of 0.33 M Na2CO3, while stirring at 50 rpm for a duration of 30 min. Subsequently, a solution of 100 mL of 0.33 M CaCl2 was introduced rapidly into the Na2CO3 solution under continuous stirring at 750 rpm for 30 s. Following this step, the suspension was allowed to settle at ambient temperature (25 °C) for 30 min. The supernatant was carefully collected, and the resulting white precipitate underwent washing twice with double-distilled water and ethyl alcohol (Cat No. 493546), utilizing centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min for each wash. During the washing process, the supernatant from both the sediment and the initial wash was retained for subsequent analysis of unbound quercetin (UQT). The washed slurry was kept overnight at −80 °C and subsequently freeze-dried at −40 °C for a period of 24 h. The final microcapsules were sterilized under ultraviolet light for 30 min prior to their application in cell culture (All chemicals used are sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, USA.) [24,32].

Encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity estimation

The methodologies employed for assessing encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading capacity (LC) were adapted from the works of Adrar et al. and Yousefi et al. [33,34]. The measurement of UQT was conducted using the supernatants and the first wash water, with the total fluid volume recorded (V0). The concentrations of UQT were determined from a prepared standard curve of quercetin at an absorption wavelength of 375 nm, utilizing a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vantaa, Finland).

Following this, the total UQT was calculated based on the concentrations recorded and the total volume (V0). The total encapsulation amount (EQT) was derived by subtracting UQT from the total quercetin amount (TQT) integrated into the solutions, which was set at 200 μg. The weights of the lyophilized CaCO3-QT microcapsules were diligently recorded. Finally, the percentages of EE and LC were calculated from three sets of precipitation using the following formulas (Liu et al., 2022 - Food Science Technology, 153):- ∗∗Encapsulation Efficiency (EE%)∗∗ = [(Total quercetin (TQT) – Unbound quercetin (UQT)) / TQT] ∗ 100- ∗∗Loading Capacity (LC%)∗∗ = [(TQT – UQT) / Total CaCO3-QT] ∗ 100

The release profile of quercetin from the calcium carbonate microcapsules

The 30 % (w/v) CaCO3 microcapsules containing quercetin (CaCO3-QT) were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (0.3 g CaCO3 in 1 mL PBS) and incubated at 37 °C for 14 days. Samples of the incubated solutions were collected at 1, 3, 24, and 72 h, as well as on days 7 and 14, and replaced with fresh PBS. The optical density (OD) of the incubated PBS was then measured at 375 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Finland). The measured OD values were interpolated from a standard QT curve in PBS (μg/mL), allowing for the determination of the cumulative amount of quercetin released from the microcapsules (RQT). Additionally, the total amount of encapsulated quercetin (EQT) was calculated based on the loading capacity percentage (LC%) of the CaCO3-QT, which was determined to be 0.44 %. Specifically, in 300 mg of CaCO3-QT, there was 1.32 mg of quercetin. The functional release percentage of the cumulative amount of quercetin (QT) was quantified using the following formula:Fractional release (%) = (cumulative amount of RQT / total amount of EQT) ∗ 100.

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the fractional release percentages were plotted against time [34].

Preparation of thermosensitive cuttlebone derived nanohydroxyapatite-calcium carbonate microcapsule-chitosan/collagen hydrogel

The thermosensitive cuttlebone derived nanohydroxyapatite (CB-nHA) combined with 1 % (w/v) calcium carbonate microcapsules (CaCO3) and 10 % (w/v) β-glycerophosphate (βGP)-chitosan/collagen hydrogels were prepared as per previous reports [[22], [24]]. The study groups were categorized into three sets based on: (1). Chitosan/collagen ratios of 7:1 and 14:1 (w/w), (2). Percentages of CB-nHA content in the hydrogels (0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % w/v), and (3). The presence of CaCO3 microcapsules with and without quercetin (CaCO3 and CaCO3-QT).

In brief, 3 mg/mL of Type I bovine collagen (Cat No. 804592) was gradually mixed into a 2 % chitosan solution (medium molecular weight, Cat No. 448877, Sigma-Aldrich). in 0.1 M acetic acid (Cat No. 1.00058, Merck, Germany). The ratios of chitosan to collagen were 1.05:1 and 2.1:1 (v/v) for the 7:1 and 14:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen composite matrix, respectively. Next, mixtures of 0–15 % CB-nHA and 1 % CaCO3 along with 56 % (w/v) β glycerophosphate (βGP) (βGP disodium salt hydrate, Cat No. G9422, Sigma-Aldrich) were added dropwise to the chitosan/collagen matrix to achieve the designated concentrations of each component according to the study groups. The preparation was conducted on ice with slow stirring to ensure a homogeneous distribution of the hydrogel components. It is important to note that all chemical powders were disinfected under UV light for 30 min prior to use, and the preparation took place in a Level 1 biosafety hood. The hydrogels, at a volume of 0.8 mL each, were injected into Teflon molds (10 × 10 mm) or into a 6-well cell culture plate (2 mL per well). They were then incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. After incubation, the hydrogels were removed from the molds for subsequent tests on compressive stress, swelling, and degradation. For cell encapsulation, the hydrogels were mixed with cells on ice and then incubated in a humidified environment with 5 % CO2.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses

The FTIR and XRD analyses were performed to examine the physicochemical properties of the CaCO3 microcapsules with and without quercetin (CaCO3 alone and CaCO3-QT). The pulverized microcapsules and their components, sodium carbonate, calcium chloride, and quercetin, were prepared for XRD analysis and then mixed with KBr, after which they were pressed into pellets for FTIR analysis. The FTIR analysis was performed 64 times in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 for each sample using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Scientific) to examine the presence of functional groups and intermolecular interaction of QT and CaCO3. The XRD measurements were conducted using a diffractometer (Bruker AXS Model D8 Advance, Germany) to characterize the crystallinity of the samples. The diffraction angle ranged from 5° to 80° at 40 kV, with a step size of 0.02° and a step time of 0.2 s [22].

Hydrogel characterization

The properties of (0–15 %) CB-nHA – 1 % CaCO3 – 10 % bGP – Chitosan/collagen hydrogel, referred to as "Hydrogel," were characterized. The specific chitosan/collagen ratios and percentages of CB-nHA and CaCO3, both with and without quercetin (QT), will be detailed according to the corresponding study groups.

pH levels and gelation time

The pH levels of 3 mL of hydrogels were measured using a pH meter (Orion 2 Star, Thermo Scientific, USA), in the solution phase at 4 °C and in the gel phase at room temperature (RT). To evaluate the mobility of the hydrogel and determine its setting time, 1 mL of the hydrogel, stored at 4 °C, was placed in a 5 mL tall bottle and incubated in a 37 °C water bath. Every 3 min, the bottle was tilted at a 45-degree angle to assess the hydrogel's mobility and observe any changes in color. A setting time was recorded when no mobility was observed [22].

Microstructure analysis

The morphology and microstructure of CaCO3 microcapsules, both with and without QT (CaCO3 alone and CaCO3-QT), as well as freeze-dried hydrogels with CaCO3-QT, were examined. Three millimeter disks of the freeze-dried hydrogels were prepared by freeze-fracturing the sponges at liquid nitrogen temperature and then cutting the sponges into 3 mm thick cylindrical disks. Following this, the freeze-dried CaCO3 microcapsules and the disks were sputter-coated with gold and analyzed under a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM) (JSM 7800F, JEOL, Japan). The diameter of the microcapsules and the pore size of the hydrogels were measured from three randomly selected SEM images at magnifications of x1000 and x500, respectively, using NIS-Element AR 4.30.01 software on a Nikon Inverted Microscope Systems ECLIPSE Ti (Nikon, Japan) [22].

Rheological test

The Hydrogels, comprising 0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % CB-nHA - 1 % CaCO3-QT - 7:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen, were tested for their rheological properties. Shear rheological measurements were performed using a stress-controlled rheometer (Kinexus Ultra Plus, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) equipped with a serrated parallel plate (25 mm in diameter and a 1 mm gap size). Prior to measurement, samples were equilibrated at 25 °C for 5 min. Afterward, the samples were pre-sheared at 0.1 s−1 for 30 s and then equilibrated for 30 s at 25 °C to remove the shear history. Shear viscosity was then determined over the shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1. Additionally, the viscoelastic properties of the samples were studied using small-amplitude oscillatory shear (SAOS) measurements. The strain amplitude was optimized to ensure that measurements were performed in the linear viscoelastic region (LVR), so that the elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) were independent of the strain amplitude. Then, frequency sweep (FS) tests were conducted over the range of 0.1–30 rad/s at a strain amplitude of 0.5 % (within the linear viscoelastic range, LVR). Triplicate measurements were performed using separate samples, and all tests were conducted at 25 °C [24,35].

Injectability test

To investigate effects of CB-nHA on the injectability of the Hydrogel, the injection force required to inject the 0 %, 5 %, 10 % and 15 % CB-nHA - 1 % CaCO3-QT - 7:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen hydrogels was measured. The hydrogels were loaded into 3 ml disposable syringes, 2.5 ml/syringe connected to 18 G needle (1.2 mm in diameter x 1 inch) (Nipro, Thailand) and kept at 4 °C and calibrated at 25 °C for 5 min before being held vertically under a transparent acrylic 50.8 mm diameter cylinder probe (TA25/1000) of a benchtop texture analyzer (TexturePro CT V1.9 Build 35, Brookfield Engineering Labs, MA, USA) for the injection. After that, the hydrogel was uniaxially pushed out from the syringe connected to 18 G needle to 40 % of the total height of the 2.5 ml hydrogel in 3 ml syringe, at a loading rate of 0.3 mm/s with a 0.05 N loading force. The measurement began at an initial value of zero and continued for 90 s. The required injection force was recorded as a force-time graph [35].

Mechanical test

The compressive strength of the hydrogels, specifically those containing 0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % CB-nHA, with 1 % CaCO3 and a chitosan/collagen ratio of 7:1 and 14:1 (w/w), was measured. This resulted in a total of eight groups for testing. The measurements were conducted under unconfined conditions using a benchtop texture analyzer (TexturePro CT V1.9 Build 35, Brookfield Engineering Labs, USA). Cylindrical hydrogels, measuring 10 × 10 mm, were uniaxially compressed with a 50.8 mm diameter transparent acrylic cylinder probe (TA25/1000) at a loading rate of 1 mm/s and a loading force of 5.0 g. The peak stress at a strain of 30 % was recorded as the compressive strength of the hydrogel, expressed in Pascals (P) [18].

Swelling and degradation tests

The swelling degrees and degradation rates of the hydrogels, containing 0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % CB-nHA with 1 % CaCO3, and chitosan/collagen ratios of 7:1 and 14:1 (w/w), were assessed, resulting in a total of eight groups. The cylindrical hydrogels, also sized 10 × 10 mm, were briefly washed with deionized (DI) water, blot-dried using filter paper (Whatman Grade 4, GE Lifesciences, USA), and weighed (W0). Following this, the hydrogels were immersed in 5 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 24 h for the swelling test and in 10,000 μg/mL lysozyme (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 24 h, as well as at intervals of 3, 7, 11, and 14 days at 37 °C. At each time point, the hydrogels were again washed with DI water, blot-dried, and weighed (W1). The percentage of weight gained was calculated using the formula: \[(W1 – W0)/W0 \times 100\], which indicates the swelling degree. For the degradation test, the percentage of weight loss was determined using the formula: [(W0 - W1)/W0 ∗ 100] [18].

Cell culture

Cell expansion and seeding

The human fetal osteoblast cell line (hFOB 1.19) (Cat No. CRL-11372, ATCC, USA) was cultured in a growth medium for cell expansion and an osteogenic medium to stimulate osteogenic differentiation. Cells at passage numbers 4 to 7 were used. For cell expansion, the cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells/cm2 in T75 cell culture flasks (Cat No. 430641U, Corning Life Sciences, USA) and passaged when they reached 80 % confluence. For osteogenic differentiation investigations, the cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells/cm2 on the designated cell culture plates. They were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2, and the culture medium was changed every 2–3 days. The growth medium consisted of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM/F12) in a 1:1 ratio, containing HEPES and l-glutamine (Cat No. 15–090 CM, Corning Life Sciences), supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 % (v/v) antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco). The osteogenic medium was prepared by supplementing the growth medium with 50 μg/mL l-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate sesquimagnesium salt hydrate (Cat No. A8960), 10 mM sodium β-glycerophosphate (Cat NoG9422), and 100 nM dexamethasone (Cat No. D4902, Sigma-Aldrich) [12,24].

Hydrogel cell encapsulation

Human FOB cells (1 x 10∧6 cells) were mixed on ice with 1 ml of a 5 % hydrogel, composed of CB-nHA in a 7:1 (w/w) ratio of chitosan to collagen, along with 1 % (w/v) CaCO3 or CaCO3-QT, depending on the study group. The encapsulated cells were then injected into a 48-well cell culture plate at a volume of 300 μL per well and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5 % CO2 atmosphere for 15 min to facilitate gelation. Following this, 0.5 ml of growth medium was added to each well, and the cells were cultured for 18 h. After this incubation period, the culture medium was replaced with either growth or osteogenic media, depending on the designated investigations [18].

Cell cytotoxicity test

Preparing pre-incubated media

For the culture mediums pre-incubated with 2 % (w/v) CaCO3 alone and CaCO3-QT microcapsules, 600 mg of microcapsules were incubated with 30 ml of the phenol-red-free culture medium for 72 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5 % CO2 incubator. To prepare the hydrogel pre-incubated media, 5 % CB-nHA (1 % chitosan/collagen, 7:1 w/w) was prepared with 1 % (w/v) CaCO3, and both CaCO3 and CaCO3-QT hydrogels were added to a 6-well plate at a volume of 2 ml per well. These were incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow for complete gelation. Afterward, the hydrogels were incubated with 2 ml of phenol-red-free DMEM/F12 culture medium (Cat No.16405, Corning Life Science) for 1, 3, and 5 days. At each time point, the media were collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent cell analysis. Before use, the pre-incubated medium was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were then collected and supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % antimycotic-antibiotic (Gibco) to create growth media for cell culture [11].

Cell viability assay and live/dead cell staining assays

For the cell viability assay, hFOB cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 10∧3 cells per well in a 96-well cell culture plate and cultured in the pre-incubated growth medium according to the study groups for 72 h. After this incubation, the medium was replaced with 20 % (w/v) Aqueous One Solution (CellTiter 96™ Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay, Promega, USA) diluted in phenol red-free DMEM/F12 medium for 2 h. The optical density (OD) was then measured at an absorbance of 490 nm (OD-sample) using a microplate reader (Varioskan). The percentage of cell viability was calculated relative to a control group in regular growth medium (OD-control) using the following formula:% Cell Viability = (OD-sample / OD-control) × 100 [11]

Live/dead cell staining assay

A live/dead cell staining assay was performed on the hydrogel-encapsulated cells. These cells were cultured in a growth medium for 14 days, washed with PBS, and then immersed in phenol red-free DMEM/F12. Following this, the LIVE/DEAD cell viability assays (LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were conducted according to the manufacturer's instructions. The staining was then examined using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (A1 HD25/A1R HD25 confocal microscope, Nikon) [11].

Osteogenic differentiation parameters

Protein lysis and alkaline phosphatase assays

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity levels were measured from total protein content solutions. The hydrogel-encapsulated cells were cultured in osteogenic medium for 14 days. Following this period, the cells were washed with PBS and immersed in 1 % Triton-X™ in PBS (Cat No. X100, Sigma-Aldrich). They were then minced into small pieces and stored overnight at −20 °C. After thawing the hydrogels, they were incubated on ice for 1 h, followed by vigorous vortexing and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The total protein content in the supernatants was measured using the RC DC™ Protein Assay Kit II (Cat No. 5000122, Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). The ALP activity was assessed using the Alkaline Phosphatase Yellow (ρ-nitrophenylphosphate, pNPP) Liquid Substrate System for ELISA (Cat No. P7998, Sigma-Aldrich), following the manufacturer's instructions. The ALP activity in each sample was then normalized to its protein content and reported as nM p-nitrophenol/mg total protein [14].

Alizarin red staining to determine mineralization

The effects of calcium carbonate-quercetin (CaCO3-QT) release on the in vitro mineralization capacity of human fetal osteoblasts (hFOB) were evaluated using alizarin red staining. The hFOB cells were plated at a density of 5 x 10∧3 cells/cm2 and cultured in pre-incubated osteogenic medium for 14 days. Afterward, the treated cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (Cat No. P6148, Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min, washed again with deionized (DI) water, and then incubated in 2 % (w/v) Alizarin Red S (Cat No. A5533, Sigma-Aldrich) in DI water at pH 4.2 for 15 min at room temperature. The staining was subsequently eluted using 10 % (w/v) cetylpyridinium (Cat No. C0732, Sigma-Aldrich) in DI water for 10 min. Finally, the optical density of the elutions was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan) [6].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), with sample sizes ranging from 3 to 5 at each investigation time (n = 3–5). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism Nine software for Mac (version 9.4.1, GraphPad Software Inc., USA). For multiple-group comparisons, a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Tukey's multiple comparison test were employed. A t-test was utilized for two-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Properties of the cuttlebone derived nano hydroxyapatite-calcium carbonate microcapsule-chitosan/collagen hydrogels

Properties and microstructure of the hydrogels

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed porous structures within the hydrogels, which were influenced by the ratios of chitosan to collagen and the concentrations of CB-nHA. In the groups with a 0 % concentration of CB-nHA, the 7:1 and 14:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen ratios exhibited the largest pore sizes, measuring 279 ± 71 μm and 317 ± 49 μm, respectively (Fig. 1A and E). A higher chitosan ratio contributed to a more defined porous structure (Fig. 1F–D). Conversely, increased concentrations of CB-nHA led to a reduction in both pore size and interconnectivity, particularly noted in the 14:1 chitosan/collagen groups (Fig. 1A–H). In the 14:1 chitosan/collagen group, the pore sizes for samples with 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % CB-nHA were significantly smaller than those in the 7:1 chitosan/collagen group. However, there were no significant differences in pore size among the 5 %–15 % CB-nHA in the 7:1 chitosan/collagen group (p > 0.05). Specifically, the average pore sizes for the 7:1 chitosan/collagen groups with 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % CB-nHA were 116 ± 46 μm, 87 ± 29 μm, and 62 ± 16 μm (p > 0.05), respectively. In contrast, the average pore sizes for the 14:1 chitosan/collagen groups were 43.7 ± 9.2 μm, 32.6 ± 8.1 μm, and 20.3 ± 5.5 μm (p < 0.05), respectively (Fig. 1I). Swelling degree

Following a 24-h incubation period in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 37 °C, the hydrogel showed minimal weight gain. The swelling indices for the formulations containing 0 % and 5 % calcium phosphate nanohydroxyapatite (CB-nHA) in both the 7:1 and 14:1 chitosan/collagen matrices were statistically similar (p > 0.05), yielding an average weight increase of 4.8 ± 1.8 %. In contrast, the weight gains for the groups with 10 % and 15 % CB-nHA were significantly lower than those observed in the 0 % and 5 % groups (p < 0.05). While the 10 % and 15 % CB-nHA groups did not show significant differences between each other, it was noted that the weight gain in the 14:1 chitosan/collagen formulation was significantly lower than the 7:1 formulation (Fig. 2B).

Degradation degree

The weight loss percentages observed in chitosan/collagen hydrogels demonstrated a significant reduction with increasing concentrations of CB-nHA, particularly noted in the 14:1 chitosan/collagen ratio (p < 0.05). On days 11 and 14, the degradation levels in the 0 % CB-nHA groups were markedly higher compared to the other experimental groups (p < 0.05), with the exception of the 5 % CB-nHA in the 7:1 chitosan/collagen cohort, which did not show significant differences (p > 0.05). In the 7:1 chitosan/collagen group, the degradation rate for the 5 % CB-nHA between days 7 and 14 was significantly elevated, surpassing those of the 10 % and 15 % CB-nHA groups (p < 0.05), yet it was not significantly different from the 0 % CB-nHA group (p > 0.05). Conversely, in the 14:1 chitosan/collagen group, degradation rates for the 5 %–15 % CB-nHA treatments were statistically similar (p > 0.05) and consistently lower than the 0 % CB-nHA over the 14-day incubation period. Average weight loss percentages recorded for the 0 % and 5 % CB-nHA, and 10 % and 15 % CB-nHA in the 7:1 chitosan/collagen group were 37.1 ± 3.9 % and 30.8 ± 7.6 % respectively, compared to 11.0 ± 8.9 % and 5.1 ± 1.9 % for the higher concentrations. For the 14:1 grouping, the averages noted for 0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % CB-nHA were 33.7 ± 6.3 %, 17.2 ± 6.2 %, 14.5 ± 7.1 %, and 1.6 ± 8.4 %, respectively (p > 0.05), as illustrated in Fig. 2C and D.

Rheological characterization of hydrogels

Fig. 5 represents a rheological analysis of CB-nHA-hydrogels containing varying concentrations (0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % w/w) of CB-nHA. The viscoelastic and flow behaviors observed here are critical parameters in evaluating the mechanical performance, injectability, and stability of biomaterial systems. Fig. 5A shows the strain sweep test in terms of storage (G′) and loss (G″) modulus as a function of shear strain. All samples exhibited a linear viscoelastic region (LVR) at low strain (<10 %), where both moduli remained constant. This behavior indicates structural integrity under physiologically relevant low-strain conditions. Within the LVR, the samples with 0–10 % CB-nHA demonstrated similar G′ and G″ values, suggesting comparable initial network stiffness. Whereas the 15 % CB-nHA sample exhibited a significant increase in both moduli, indicative of CB-nHA-enhanced elasticity due to a denser or more entangled polymer network. However, upon increasing strain, the 15 % CB-nHA sample displayed an earlier G′/G″ crossover at approximately 100 % strain and a rapid drop in modulus, indicating a more brittle structure with limited strain tolerance. This behavior may be unfavorable in applications requiring flexibility, such as injectable or deformable hydrogels. In comparison, the 0–10 % CB-nHA samples showed delayed crossover points (∼700 % strain), indicating higher ductility and greater tolerance to deformation. Fig. 5B shows the frequency sweep results in terms of G′ and G″ as a function of angular frequency (ω). All samples demonstrated solid-like behavior (G′ > G″) across the examined frequency range. Interestingly, the 5 % and 10 % CB-nHA samples demonstrated a reduction in G′ relative to the 0 % CB-nHA, while G″ remained essentially unchanged. This reduction in elastic response, without a corresponding shift in viscous behavior, suggests a possible disruption or dilution of the matrix structure at intermediate CB-nHA concentrations. Discussion

This study presents a significant advancement by integrating nanohydroxyapatite derived from natural cuttlebone (CB-nHA) and quercetin-loaded calcium carbonate microcapsules (CaCO3-QT) into a thermosensitive hydrogel formulated with chitosan and collagen. We optimized the weight ratios of chitosan and collagen to enhance the mechanical strength and injectability of this bioactive hydrogel, which is essential for promoting osteoblastic differentiation and improving the encapsulation efficiency of osteogenic cells. Furthermore, this research explores the applicability of the previously characterized CB-nHA in hydrogel formulations. Our group has fully characterized the CB-nHA particles used in this study, which are also reported in Tangsuksant et al. (2023) [7]. We evaluated the influence of CB-nHA and the chitosan/collagen ratio on the structural and mechanical properties of a thermosensitive hydrogel containing 0–5 % CB-nHA, 1 % CaCO3-QT, and β-glycerophosphate (βGP)-chitosan/collagen. Additionally, the co-precipitation of quercetin with a CaCO3 matrix (CaCO3-QT) enhances the storage and controlled release of quercetin in the hydrogel, facilitating the delivery of natural antioxidants that promote the growth and differentiation of encapsulated cells for stem cell delivery. The focus was on its injectability and osteoconductive properties.

The relationships among the components of the hydrogel and related biological signals have been demonstrated to amplify cellular processes essential for bone regeneration, thereby improving the hydrogel's regenerative capacity [4,23]. By utilizing a direct precipitation method, CaCO3 microcapsules were successfully employed to encapsulate and subsequently release quercetin (QT), an unstable hydrophobic antioxidant flavonoid, thereby stimulating osteoblast activity within the hydrogel [40,41]. Quercetin promotes bone formation and blood vessel development in osteoporotic conditions by activating the ERK, p38, and AKT signaling pathways, enhancing the expression of factors crucial for bone regeneration [42]. It also activates the estrogen receptor, boosting the bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) pathway and increasing osteogenic markers like osterix (OSX), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), and osteopontin (OPN) [43].

Notably, a chitosan/collagen ratio of 14:1 (w/w) along with a 15 % (w/v) CB-nHA concentration significantly increased both the mechanical properties and the elastic modulus of the CaCO3-chitosan/collagen hydrogel. This specific formulation yielded a hydrogel with markedly low porosity, reduced swelling, and slower degradation rates, which presented challenges to its injectability. That is because the interaction between nanohydroxyapatite (nHA, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2) and the CaCO3-chitosan/collagen complex, both rich in Ca2+ ions, encourages the precipitation of CaCO3 and facilitates ionic bonding between the phosphate (PO43−) groups of CB-nHA and the amine (NH4+) groups of chitosan [22,44].

As previous research indicated that the development of a thermosensitive β-glycerophosphate chitosan/collagen hydrogel is facilitated through three primary interactions: intermolecular hydrophobic forces, electrostatic attractions, and the establishment of both intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding [16,22], a result, increasing concentrations of CB-nHA alongside greater chitosan/collagen ratios lead to a reduction in pore size and connectivity within the hydrogel and the enhanced mechanical strength [22,44,45]. Furthermore, this study corroborates earlier findings that a higher chitosan ratio is instrumental in refining the porous structure, enhancing mechanical strength, and moderating the release kinetics of quercetin [11,12]. The incorporation of the CB-nHA contributes to the stabilization of the composite structure. The enhanced affinity between the amine groups of chitosan and the negatively charged surfaces of hydroxyapatite, alongside the potential for ionic bonding with calcium ions and hydrogen bonding among the hydroxyl (-OH) groups in both chitosan and hydroxyapatite, reinforces the overall integrity of the composite [22,24,44]. The effects of CB-nHA on the hydrogel's properties align closely with those observed for synthetic nano-calcium phosphate particles, such as bTCP and nHA, highlighting the efficacy of integrating CB-nHA for optimizing hydrogel performance in bone regeneration [22,24,46]. Specifically, a 3 % concentration of bTCP has been found to increase the mechanical strength of thermosensitive chitosan/collagen hydrogels while simultaneously reducing their pore size, swelling capacity, and degradation rate [22]. Notably, the compressive strength exhibited by the 0 %–15 % CB-nHA-1 % CaCO3-7:1 and 14: 1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen hydrogels surpassed that documented for the thermosensitive bTCP-2:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen formulations in earlier research. The mechanical strength of materials previously reported varied notably, with values ranging from 0.07 ± 0.01 kPa for the 0 % bTCP group to 0.72 ± 0.03 kPa for the 3 % bTCP group [22]. In this current study, the average mechanical strength of hydrogels with 0 % CB-nHA and those containing 5–15 % CB-nHA—employing chitosan/collagen ratios of 7:1 and 14:1, respectively—was recorded at 9.2 ± 1.0 kPa and 9.4 ± 1.5 kPa. This enhancement can be attributed to the elevated chitosan/collagen ratios applied in our study and the incorporation of CaCO3-QT microcapsules, which likely increased the density of intermolecular interactions within the hydrogel. However, it is important to note that the mechanical strength of these hydrogels remains substantially lower than that of synthetic polymers and chemically cross-linked hydrogels, which typically exhibit strengths ranging from 3.4 to 6.5 MPa [47,48]; and compare even less favorably with the compressive strength of human cancellous bone, estimated at approximately 4–50 MPa [49]. This relatively low compressive strength may pose limitations for the application of these hydrogels in addressing certain skeletal defects, such as the 2–3 wall alveolar ridge defects characterized by Seibert in 1983 [50], necessitating additional support from guided bone regeneration membranes, especially in the context of peri-implant bone defects [51,52].

The current study contrasts with previous findings [53], indicating that increasing the concentration of CB-nHA to 5 % and 10 % does not significantly enhance the mechanical strength or viscoelastic properties of the thermosensitive CaCO3-QT-7:1 w/w chitosan/collagen hydrogel. This lack of enhancement can be attributed to the predominant role of CaCO3-chitosan/collagen interactions in stabilizing the polymer network, thereby affecting the distribution of CB-nHA particles [24]. At these lower concentrations, CB-nHA may disrupt the polymer matrix, leading to a deterioration in both mechanical strength and complex viscosity of the hydrogels [54,55]. In contrast, elevating the concentration of CB-nHA to 15 % (w/v) increases the density of physical cross-links [53] sufficiently to enhance the interactions between CaCO3 and the chitosan/collagen matrix, resulting in a hydrogel characterized by rigidity but brittleness, indicated by elevated storage and loss moduli, alongside a reduced shear viscosity at higher shear rates.

Moreover, the incorporation of CB-nHA reduced the swelling and degradation rates of the hydrogel, corroborating prior studies [53,56]. The hydrogel undergoes degradation primarily through hydrolysis and enzymatic digestion, which disrupts intermolecular bonding and facilitates the release of its constituents [22,57]. Consistently, degradation in both 7:1 and 14:1 chitosan/collagen hydrogels decreased in the 10 % and 15 % CB-nHA groups. In comparison, the 5 % CB-nHA hydrogel exhibited minimal impact on degradation, suggesting reduced intermolecular forces compared to the higher concentration groups. This observation aligns with the pronounced swelling behavior seen in the 5 % CB-nHA-1 % CaCO3-7:1 chitosan/collagen hydrogel. Furthermore, the low degradation rate of CB-nHA particles likely contributes to the decreased degradation rates observed in the 10 % and 15 % CB-nHA formulations [9,19].

The internal bonding enhancements and structural stability of the hydrogel can lead to a reduction in its flexibility and swelling capacity. Consequently, this results in a non-swelling hydrogel that maintains its volume and dimensions upon exposure to aqueous environments, such as PBS, while exhibiting limited water absorption [58]. The benefits of a non-swell hydrogel include enhanced mechanical strength and volume stability, which are critical for promoting cell adhesion, differentiation, and matrix deposition. Moreover, this characteristic aids in the modulated release of bioactive molecules [58,59], making it particularly suitable for personalized 3D printing applications in bone grafting [3,60]. The hydrogel's non-expansive nature ensures safety around vital structures, such as nerve foramina, mitigating the risk of tissue compression [59].

Furthermore, the physical adsorption of chitosan onto CB-nHA and CaCO3 resulted in a well-organized layer-by-layer architecture, which could enhance the stability of the composite material and facilitate the distribution of CB-nHA within the three-dimensional porous framework [24,31]. These hybrid composites closely resemble the natural mineral composition of bone, thereby enhancing osteoconductivity and cellular attachment of the hydrogel [61]. Moreover, the small dimensions of the pristine CaCO3 microcapsules and the nanoparticles of CB-nHA facilitate the hydrogel's function as a bioactive matrix for the storage and controlled release of bioactive molecules [28,62,63]. Through a direct precipitation process, CaCO3 microcapsules could effectively encapsulate and release QT, a hydrophobic and unstable antioxidant flavonoid that promotes osteoblast activity [40]. This precipitation mechanism potentially traps QT within the matrix and masks the detection of QT functional groups in the CaCO3-QT, with only the QT on the outer porous surfaces being rapidly released [28,64].

Among the various formulations evaluated, the 7:1 chitosan/collagen hydrogel was selected for further rheological and injectability assessments. The selection was based on its porous architecture and the kinetics of swelling and degradation. The rheological analysis revealed a gel-like behavior in the 0–15 % CB-nHA-1 % CaCO3-QT-7:1 chitosan/collagen hydrogels. These thermosensitive hydrogels exhibited injectable characteristics that are influenced by their rheological properties, including viscosity, storage modulus, and loss modulus. For clinical application, the hydrogel must exhibit shear-thinning behavior while requiring an injection force that preserves the viability of encapsulated cells and meets the standards for clinical use [19,35]. The influence of CB-nHA concentration on formulation performance was significant. While formulations with low to intermediate CB-nHA levels (5–10 %) demonstrated reduced structural integrity, a critical concentration of 15 % CB-nHA notably improved network stiffness, viscoelasticity, and viscosity. Despite these mechanical advantages, the 5 % and 10 % CB-nHA formulations were preferred for further investigation due to their favorable shear viscosity behaviors and injection performance. The 15 % CB-nHA formulation exhibited a drop-like, discontinuous flow profile during injection, which is suboptimal for applications necessitating smooth delivery. In contrast, the 5 % and 10 % CB-nHA samples displayed a uniform, continuous flow during the injection process. This behavior aligns with strain sweep data, indicating a brittle profile for the 15 % CB-nHA formulation, characterized by lower strain tolerance and an earlier crossover point of G′/G″. Furthermore, the injectability testing confirmed that the hydrogels were indeed injectable at lower injection forces, as inferred from the rheological data; however, the high viscoelasticity of the 15 % CB-nHA formulation necessitated a higher, rather than plateau, injection force [19,35].

The integration of 5 % (w/v) CB-nHA into a 1 % (w/v) CaCO3-QT-7:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen matrix significantly enhanced the hydrogel's porosity and overall physical properties. The hydrogel maintained a porous structure with high interconnectivity, exhibiting an average pore size of 116 ± 47 μm, which is suitable for facilitating bone regeneration [65]. It also demonstrated superior swelling and degradation compared to other formulations, indicating the importance of balancing concentration and structural integrity in hydrogel design for biomedical applications. Therefore, the 5 % (w/v) CB-nHA within a 7:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen matrix was selected for cell encapsulation studies. This formulation was demonstrated to be noncytotoxic and suitable for cell encapsulation, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation. The effects of CB-nHA and CaCO3-QT on alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels, a key marker of osteoblastic differentiation, highlighted the hydrogel's potential in supporting osteogenic differentiation and co-delivering quercetin to the encapsulated cells.

Conclusion

This study utilized agricultural waste-derived bone substitutes, specifically CB-nHA, in combination with the natural flavonoid quercetin, to serve as alternative bioactive materials aimed at enhancing the hydrogel's injectability and osteoconductive properties. The findings demonstrated that CB-nHA and CaCO3-QT significantly enhanced the properties and bioactivity of a thermosensitive chitosan/collagen hydrogel, supporting bone regeneration. The 5 % (w/v) CB-nHA-1 % CaCO3-QT-7:1 (w/w) chitosan/collagen hydrogel showed excellent potential as a carrier for flavonoid delivery and osteoblast encapsulation in treating skeletal defects due to its injectability, swelling, and degradation behaviors, as well as dimensional stability. This hydrogel is an appropriate bone substitute for well-defined bone defects. It is safe for use near critical nerve exits. Future research may investigate its potential for bone regeneration in skeletal defects using animal models.
