Serum Magnesium Levels as a Prognostic Marker in Emergency Department Admissions: A Landmark Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Keita Hattori, Nobuhiro Nishibori, Kazuhiro Furuhashi, Masaki Okazaki, Nobuhide Endo, Shoichi Maruyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEarly identification of serious hypermagnesemia in emergency department (ED) patients is challenging due to limited clinical information and unknown baseline factors. Our institution implemented routine serum magnesium (Mg) testing for all ED patients to prevent missed diagnoses of fatal hypermagnesemia. We evaluated whether these measurements improved short-term prognostic assessment by associating with 28-day all-cause mortality.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 43,100 adult ED admissions at a tertiary center in Japan from January 2017 to December 2019; 12,580 met the eligibility criteria. Patients were stratified according to Mg levels: hypomagnesemia (<1.6 mg/dL; n = 433), normomagnesemia (1.6-2.4 mg/dL; n = 10,722), and hypermagnesemia (≥2.5 mg/dL; n = 1425). The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Multivariable analyses were adjusted for age, sex, admission diagnosis, admission to the intensive care unit, albumin, C-reactive protein (CRP), and other laboratory covariates. Restricted cubic spline analysis was used to assess nonlinear associations.ResultsThe model adjusted for patients’ characteristics showed higher mortality rates in hypomagnesemia and hypermagnesemia. Spline analysis initially revealed a U-shaped relationship between Mg levels and 28-day mortality. However, the risk associated with hypomagnesemia was attenuated after additional adjustments for CRP and albumin levels, whereas hypermagnesemia remained independently associated with 28-day mortality (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.73; 95% CI 1.51-2.00). This association remained consistent after stratification by renal function or admission diagnosis.ConclusionSystematic Mg testing in the ED may facilitate the early identification of patients at imminent risk. Routine Mg assessment could help clinicians recognize high-risk patients in acute care.Keywords: hypermagnesemia, hypomagnesemia, mortality, emergency department, restricted cubic spline analysis

The Bottom Line

This study asked whether measuring blood magnesium levels during an emergency visit can help identify patients at short-term risk of death. We studied more than 12,000 adults and found that higher magnesium levels were strongly linked to death within 28 days, even after accounting for age, kidney function, and other clinical factors. In contrast, low magnesium levels were not an independent risk once factors such as inflammation, malnutrition, and severe illness were considered. Mildly elevated magnesium levels were not harmless; they signaled a real danger. Measuring magnesium at the time of emergency care may help doctors recognize patients who are sicker than they appear.

Introduction

Background

Detecting hidden severe illness in the emergency department (ED) is challenging because clinicians must make rapid decisions with incomplete information.1 Although patient status is assessed by systematically reviewing laboratory data, the risk of delayed or missed diagnosis persists when parameters are not included in the initial screening.2 Serum magnesium (Mg), a vital intracellular cation essential for neuromuscular and cardiovascular function, is often excluded from standard panels under-recognized in routine care.3,4 In an aging society with increasing polypharmacy, the widespread use of diuretics and Mg-containing laxatives increases the prevalence of both hypo- and hypermagnesemia.5,6 Consequently, Mg abnormalities may be more commonly encountered in the ED than generally assumed, especially among older adults with complex medication profiles and unrecognized comorbidities.7

Importance

Despite its biological relevance, the prognostic implications of Mg abnormalities in acute care remains unclear, partly owing to reliance on categorical cutoff values or unexplored potential nonlinear risk patterns.8, 9, 10, 11 Whether Mg abnormalities independently influence prognosis or reflect underlying illnesses remains unknown. Therefore, clarifying these associations is particularly crucial in emergency settings, where clinical decisions must be made with limited information.

Goals of This Investigation

Our institution implements routine Mg testing during all ED visits to prevent missing clinically important Mg derangements. Beyond signaling an overt electrolyte imbalance, Mg abnormalities may mirror occult renal deterioration, unrecognized malnutrition, or excessive ingestion of Mg-containing medications; however, these possibilities have not been systematically evaluated. In particular, whether low Mg levels confer independent risks or reflect frailty or nutritional deficiency remains unclear. Accordingly, we investigated the associations between Mg levels treated as a continuous variable and 28-day mortality in a consecutive cohort of patients admitted to the ED. Particular attention was paid to whether seemingly “mild” deviations in Mg confer excess risk. Clarifying these relationships may establish routine Mg measurements as a safeguard against severe hypermagnesemia and as a practical tool for early risk stratification in acute care.

methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients who presented to the ED of the Anjo Kosei Hospital in Japan. The registration period was from January 2017 to December 2019, and the observation period ended in February 2024. The ED of this hospital manages approximately 30,000 visits annually, of which about 20% to 25% result in hospital admission. Mg measurements were performed as a part of routine clinical practice in compliance with insurance-covered medical care regulations; therefore, informed consent was not required. Furthermore, the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to data anonymity. Informed consent was obtained in the form of an opt-out on the website, and those who did not provide informed consent were excluded. Study approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of Nagoya University and Anjo Kosei Hospital (approval no. 2023-0389 and 2023-96). This cohort study was conducted under the health insurance system of Japan in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, Ethical Guidelines on Clinical Studies of the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare of Japan, and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Selection of Participants

The current study enrolled patients aged >18 years who were admitted to the hospital after an ED visit. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) those who were not admitted, (2) those for whom Mg data could not be obtained, and (3) those who died on the first and second sick day because they included cardiopulmonary arrest on arrival or return of spontaneous circulation. Each consultation was considered a single visit. Only the first ED visit was included if multiple visits occurred on the same day.

Measurements and Data Collection

Patient information was extracted from electronic medical records. Data from patients in the ED included age, sex, admission diagnosis, outcome (return home, hospital admission, or referral to another hospital), laboratory data in the ED, admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), length of hospital stay, death date, date of last visit, and administered medicine. Admission diagnoses were classified into infection, trauma, cancer, major adverse cardiovascular events (MAVE), and other causes. The sample collected concurrently with the Mg sample was used if more than one blood sample was obtained. Medication information was obtained from data collected by hospital pharmacists from patients or their families.

Outcomes

The study outcome was all-cause mortality over 28 days after hospitalization.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables are expressed as medians (IQR), and categorical variables are expressed as numbers (%). Skewness and kurtosis tests were performed to assess the normality of the data, and skewed data (serum C-reactive protein [CRP]) underwent natural logarithmic transformation (ln) to achieve a normal distribution in the multivariable analysis. Age and laboratory parameters were treated as continuous variables, whereas sex and ICU admission were included as binary categorical variables. Patients were categorized into hypomagnesemia, normomagnesemia, and hypermagnesemia groups. The cutoff value for Mg levels was 1.5 and 2.5 mg/dL. For comparisons between the Mg groups, continuous variables were subjected to the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were subjected to Fisher’s exact test.

We estimated the association between baseline Mg levels and all-cause mortality over 28 days using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analysis. For survival analysis, the Kaplan–Meier method and univariable and multivariable adjusted Cox regression analyses were performed. In the multivariable analysis, the following variables with a missing data rate of <20% and no evidence of multicollinearity were included as adjustment variables; sex, age, admission diagnosis, admission to the ICU, and laboratory data (aspartate aminotransferase, lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], creatine kinase [CK], serum albumin [Alb], estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], sodium, potassium, calcium, white blood cell count [WBC], hemoglobin [Hb], platelet count [Plt], and ln-CRP). The at-risk period began upon admission and ended at death, discharge, or loss to follow-up. Some covariates were missing, including 24% for medication, 5% for albumin, 4% for blood count, and <1% for other laboratory data. Because complete case analysis may lead to reduced statistical power and potential bias when missingness is not completely random, we applied multiple imputation using chained equations. Missingness was assumed to be missing at random, and the imputation model included all covariates and the outcome variable. Therefore, multiple imputations by chained equations were conducted to address the presence of missing data. We generated 100 imputed datasets and combined the estimates of multivariable analysis using Rubin’s rules.

The nonlinear association between baseline Mg levels and overall mortality was evaluated using the restricted cubic spline (RCS) function. In the RCS analysis, we truncated the participants at the first and 99th percentiles of Mg levels (1.0 and 3.3 mg/dL) and set three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the Mg values. We calculated the hazard ratio adjusted for sex, age, admission diagnosis, and admission to the ICU in Model 1; for sex, age, admission diagnosis, admission to the ICU, Alb, and ln-CRP in Model 2; and for sex, age, admission diagnosis, admission to the ICU, Alb, ln-CRP, AST, LDH, CK, eGFR, sodium, potassium, calcium, WBC, Hb, and Plt in Model 3. The associations of hypermagnesemia (≥ or <2.5 mg/dL) and all-cause mortality over 28 days were examined using stratified analyses in the subgroups of renal function (eGFR category; ≥60, <60 and ≥30, or <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), and admission diagnosis (infection, trauma, cancer, MAVE, and others). Additionally, Wald’s test was performed to assess interactions.

Statistical significance was set at P <.05 (two-tailed). All statistical analyses were conducted using the Stata statistical software, V.15.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Baseline Patient Characteristics

A total of 104,033 patients admitted to the ED during the observation period were screened, and 43,100 patients had blood tests performed. Of these, 30,520 were excluded based on the study’s criteria. Although 1,048 patients had missing data, multiple imputation was performed, resulting in 12,580 patients included in the final analysis. Among these, 433 (3.4%) had hypomagnesemia, 10,722 (85.2%) had normomagnesemia, and 1425 (11.3%) had hypermagnesemia (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the three groups are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 75 years (range, 63–83 years), and 57.5% of the patients were male. The median length of hospital stay was 12 days (IQR: 7–21 days) in the hypomagnesemia group, 10 days (IQR: 6–21 days) in the normomagnesemia group, and 14 days (IQR: 6–26 days) in the hypermagnesemia group. The 28-day mortality rates were 11.8% (51/433), 7.6% (814/10,722), and 18.8% (268/1425), respectively. The mean Mg level was 2.1 mg/dL (1.9–2.2). Patients with hypermagnesemia were older and had higher CRP levels and poorer renal function than those in the normomagnesemia group. In contrast, patients with hypomagnesemia exhibit lower albumin levels and reduced blood cell counts. Regarding admission diagnoses, patients with hypomagnesemia tended to have a higher prevalence of infections and a lower prevalence of MAVE, whereas patients with hypermagnesemia tended to have a higher prevalence of cancer. Both the hypermagnesemia and hypomagnesemia groups also had longer hospital stays and higher CRP levels than the normomagnesemia group.

Logistic Regression Analysis

In univariable analysis, both hypomagnesemia (odds ratio [OR] 1.63, 95% CI 1.20-2.20, P <.001) and hypermagnesemia (OR 2.82, 95% CI 2.42-3.28, P <.001) were significantly associated with increased 28-day mortality compared with normomagnesemia. However, in the multivariable model, hypomagnesemia was not associated with increased risk (adjusted OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.72-1.43), whereas hypermagnesemia remained independently associated with higher mortality (adjusted OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.59-2.34, Table 2).

Survival Analysis

Fifty-one deaths occurred in the hypomagnesemia group, 814 in the normomagnesemia group, and 268 in the hypermagnesemia group. The Kaplan–Meier survival curves for 28-day mortality indicated significantly lower survival in the hypermagnesemia and hypomagnesemia groups than in the normomagnesemia group (Fig. 2). Using Cox proportional hazards models (Table 3), we analyzed time-to-event data with incremental adjustments for demographic and laboratory covariates. Although both hypo- and hypermagnesemia initially showed elevated mortality rates, further adjustments for Alb level, CRP level, and other factors revealed that only hypermagnesemia remained significant.

Nonlinear Relationships Between Continuous Mg Values and Mortality

The RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped association between mortality and Mg levels after adjusting for sex, age, admission diagnosis, admission to the ICU (Fig. 3A). However, after adjusting for Alb and CRP levels, no significant increase in mortality was observed in the hypomagnesemia group (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, hypermagnesemia remained significantly associated with increased mortality, even after adjusting for additional confounding factors (Fig. 3C).

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, obtaining precise information is challenging owing to the ED setting in which data were collected, and where many patients present with urgent needs. The absence of detailed medical history, primary illnesses, and medication history records limited our ability to conduct analyses that incorporated these factors. Although disease severity and comorbid conditions were adjusted for using ICU admission status and admission diagnoses, respectively, this approach does not account for patients’ functional status or the coexistence and complexity of individual comorbidities. In ED settings, detailed background information is often lacking, our findings suggest that Mg levels may serve as useful indicators associated with short-term outcomes in hospitalized patients. Second, the precise status of Mg supplementation remains unclear as medication histories were available for only approximately three–quarters of the patients, and information on over-the-counter medications and supplements was particularly lacking among critically ill patients—those admitted to the ICU or who died within 28 days—making it difficult to accurately assess both disease severity and the actual status of magnesium supplementation. Third, our study analyzed Mg levels at the time of admission without accounting for subsequent changes; therefore, the effects of therapeutic interventions on hypermagnesemia warrant further investigation. Similarly, the standardized eGFR levels may have been overestimated in frail older patients owing to a lack of information on body size. Furthermore, because this was a single-center study conducted at a tertiary ED in Japan, the external validity of our findings may be limited, and caution is warranted when extrapolating results to other populations or health care settings. Because this was a retrospective observational study, causal relationships between Mg levels and mortality cannot be established, and residual confounding may remain despite multivariable adjustment. In addition, although multiple imputation was used to address missing data, the possibility of bias related to the assumptions underlying the imputation process cannot be completely excluded.

Discussion

This large retrospective cohort study of patients admitted to the ED found that hypermagnesemia was consistently associated with elevated short-term mortality, even after adjusting for potential confounders, such as renal function and electrolytes. In contrast, although hypomagnesemia initially appeared to be associated with increased mortality, its effect was no longer significant when Alb and CRP levels were considered. This finding suggests that the prognostic effect of hypomagnesemia is largely mediated by factors related to inflammatory status, nutritional status, or general illness severity, whereas hypermagnesemia is an independent risk factor. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the largest cohort studies on Mg worldwide and is particularly valuable as it is the first to perform a nonlinear multivariable analysis using continuous values.

In the present study, a substantially larger cohort of ED admissions was examined, compared with many earlier investigations of hypomagnesemia.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Previous studies have reported conflicting findings regarding the prognostic contribution of hypomagnesemia; however, this expanded sample size enhances the statistical power and increases the credibility of our findings; specifically, hypomagnesemia initially appeared to be associated with higher mortality but lost significance after adjusting for Alb and CRP. Furthermore, by using RCS analysis rather than relying solely on categorical cutoff points, we discerned subtle, nonlinear relationships and showed that the apparent risk of hypomagnesemia is not attributable to magnesium deficiency itself but reflects broader clinical conditions—including inflammation, nutritional status, and overall disease severity—that frequently coexist in the acute care setting. These results support the notion that hypomagnesemia often serves as a surrogate marker of acute systemic illness rather than an isolated prognostic factor,12 highlighting the importance of assessing inflammatory and nutritional status as a part of comprehensive clinical evaluation rather than routine Mg evaluation alone. Although previous studies have implicated hypomagnesemia, often coupled with hypokalemia, in serious arrhythmic events,13, 14, 15 our results suggest that malnutrition and general clinical deterioration may be decisive factors for short-term mortality.

We demonstrated that hypermagnesemia remained an independent risk factor for short-term mortality even after adjusting for potential confounding factors such as renal function. Notably, Mg levels of 2.5 mg/dL or higher, which is often regarded as only “mildly” elevated, were associated with increased mortality, emphasizing the importance of values that could otherwise be overlooked in clinical practice. This observation is consistent with previous large-scale studies, which also demonstrated significantly elevated mortality even with modest hypermagnesemia after multivariable adjustment.16 Moreover, a renal function–stratified analysis revealed a significantly higher mortality rate in patients with hypermagnesemia irrespective of kidney function status, suggesting that factors beyond renal impairment alone could be involved. Hypermagnesemia can also result from non-renal causes, such as excessive intake of Mg-containing medications or gastrointestinal dysfunction, even in the absence of overt renal failure.16, 17, 18 Although statistical testing showed no significant differences in eGFR across Mg categories, the lower median eGFR observed in the hypermagnesemia group indicates clinically meaningful renal vulnerability. Thus, rather than negating the role of renal function, our findings suggest that hypermagnesemia may capture a composite risk state in which impaired renal clearance coexists with systemic physiological deterioration. In contrast, no clear trend emerged to indicate that mortality associated with hypermagnesemia increased with worsening renal function. This absence may stem from the single time point of measurement in the ED, which could fail to capture progressive changes such as acute kidney injury. Baseline renal function and prior clinical trajectories are often unknown in the ED, leaving the possibility that elevated Mg levels reflect ongoing renal deterioration or multiple organ failure.19, 20, 21 Consequently, elevated Mg levels may not merely reflect declining kidney function, but rather a broader state of organ dysfunction and physiologic instability; thus, even “mild” elevations warrant close attention. In subgroup analyses stratified by admission diagnosis, hypermagnesemia was associated with increased short-term mortality across most diagnostic categories. Although this association was not statistically significant among trauma patients, the number of trauma cases with hypermagnesemia was small, resulting in wide CIs. Importantly, no significant interaction was observed between admission diagnosis and hypermagnesemia. Our findings indicate that hypermagnesemia during ED visits could be a marker of potential renal deterioration, multiorgan failure, or underlying organ vulnerability. Hence, in patients presenting with hypermagnesemia, early assessment of renal function, medication history (especially Mg-containing preparations), and overall clinical status is vital, along with close follow-up and re-evaluation of therapeutic strategies. Therefore, measuring Mg levels in the ED is particularly useful for short-term prognostic assessments and may help detect subtle changes in renal function that could otherwise go unnoticed. However, the extent to which routine measurement benefits all patients remains uncertain, and its broader impact on clinical workflows, including the potential for prolonged the ED length of stay, warrants further evaluation.

In summary, in this large retrospective cohort of ED admissions, hypermagnesemia was consistently and independently associated with 28-day mortality, whereas the excess risk observed with hypomagnesemia appeared to reflect coexisting inflammation, nutritional impairment, and broader systemic illness, rather than a direct effect of magnesium deficiency. The finding that even moderate Mg elevations (≥2.5 mg/dL) are associated with higher mortality supports routine Mg testing in acutely ill patients, especially when medication histories are incomplete. Although causality cannot be confirmed in this observational study, systematic Mg measurement can reveal otherwise hidden renal vulnerability and physiological deterioration, and may enable timely targeted care for patients at short-term risk.
