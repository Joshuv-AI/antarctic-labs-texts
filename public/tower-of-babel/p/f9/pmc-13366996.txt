Use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in diabetes management in Africa: opportunities, challenges, and future prospects
Authors: M A Olamoyegun, O D Olamoyegun, A T Akinlade, O O Saliu, T A Adeyemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus represents a growing public health crisis across Africa, with prevalence rates increasing due to urbanization, lifestyle transitions, and epidemiological transition. Despite numerous technological advances in diabetes management, including continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), access to these innovations remains limited across the continent.ObjectiveThis narrative review examines the current state, opportunities, challenges, and prospects of CGM technology in African diabetes care, with emphasis on the socioeconomic, infrastructural, and cultural factors influencing implementation.MethodsA comprehensive review of peer-reviewed literature, clinical trials, and implementation studies from African contexts was conducted, incorporating evidence on CGM technology, diabetes epidemiology, healthcare systems, and policy frameworks across African nations.ResultsCGM technology offers substantial benefits for glycaemic control, complication reduction, and patient empowerment. However, widespread adoption in Africa faces significant barriers including prohibitive costs, limited healthcare infrastructure, supply chain challenges, inadequate healthcare workforce training, and sociocultural factors. Emerging clinical trials in South Africa, Kenya, and Malawi demonstrate feasibility and clinical benefits, though large-scale implementation remains constrained.ConclusionsWhile CGM holds important potential at transforming African diabetes care, successful integration requires multisectoral collaboration, innovative financing mechanisms, capacity building, and feasible implementation strategies. Policy frameworks that support equitable access, regulatory harmonization, and sustainable scale-up models are essential for realizing CGM’s full potential across the continent.Keywords: Continuous glucose monitoring, Diabetes management, Africa, Healthcare technology

Introduction

Overview of diabetes epidemiology in Africa

Diabetes mellitus has emerged as one of the most devastating non-communicable diseases that is facing Africa in this 21st century as its increasing prevalence is contributing to high morbidity and mortality. The rise in prevalence is attributed to rapid urbanization, changing dietary patterns, reduced physical activity, genetic predisposition and increasing obesity rates [1]. The International Diabetes Federation estimated 25 million of Africans to be currently living with diabetes [2] with variation in the projected prevalence by 2045 between Middle East and North Africa (MENA) and sub-Saharan Africa. Whereas this prevalence has been projected to increase by 87% in the MENA region by 2045 (prevalence was 16.2% as at 2024), the number will increase by 142% in the sub-Saharan Africa [2]. There is substantial heterogeneity in the prevalence of diabetes across African nations, with estimates ranging from < 1% in some rural populations to > 15% in urban areas of South Africa, Kenya, and North African countries [3]. The heterogeneity of the prevalence may be due to varying socioeconomic (rapid urbanization concentrates populations in cities where Western dietary patterns, sedentary lifestyles, and obesity) and genetic factors across different ethnic groups in Africa [4]. Compared to many other high-income regions of the world, Africa has disproportionately high rates of undiagnosed or delayed diabetes diagnosis which is usually associated with complications, and diabetes-related mortality [5]. This disparity is related to significant inequities in access to healthcare, diagnostic capacity, and availability of essential diabetes medications and technologies. Within Africa continent itself, disparities are present as relatively high-income countries generally have better healthcare infrastructures and resources compared to low- and middle-income countries, where access to essential diabetes care technologies remain limited or unavailable, inadequate facilities, and severe shortages of trained healthcare professionals prevail [4]. This disparity also reflects in the availability and affordability of diabetes care resources. Whereas insulin, glucose monitoring devices, and diabetes education programs have become standard in high-income nations, many African countries lack comprehensive access. The socioeconomic diversity across African countries further complicates uniform diabetes care delivery, often resulting in urban versus rural disparity where rural populations experience greater barriers to effective disease management [6]. Even within each nation, urban populations generally have better access to healthcare facilities and diagnostic services, while rural communities face significant barriers including geographic isolation, limited transportation infrastructure, and scarcity of specialized diabetes care and personnel [3].These disparities result in a vicious cycle: poor glycaemic control leads to severe, complicated and multiple complications, which create difficult-to-afford health expenditures for families and overwhelmingly already strained healthcare systems [7]. Due to socioeconomic diversity across African nations, creating or rather prescribing a uniform diabetes care delivery across the continent becomes complex and challenging.

This paucity of diabetes-specific resources is associated with suboptimal disease management and increased rates of complications such as cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, and diabetic retinopathy in the continent. The consequences of these inequalities are significant. The high rates of undiagnosed diabetes, increased diabetes-related mortality, and limited access to modern diabetes management tools are some of the factors that are exacerbating the problem. The need to address these pressing issues demands innovative strategies to enhance diabetes management across Africa, of which continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) emerges as a pivotal technology with transformative potential [7].

Diabetes management landscape in Africa

Healthcare infrastructure across Africa remains inadequate to address the diabetes crisis. Many countries lack sufficient primary healthcare facilities equipped for diabetes diagnosis and management. Essential diagnostics including glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) testing, lipid panels, and urine microalbumin assays are unavailable in numerous rural and even some urban centres [4]. Specialist diabetes care is concentrated in tertiary urban hospitals, creating access barriers for rural populations who constitute majority populations in many African nations [8]. The shortages in the professional healthcare workforce is one of the other factors compounding infrastructure limitations. The sub-Saharan Africa sub-continent has the lowest physician density globally, with many countries having < 1 physician per 10,000 population [4]. For diabetes specialists (endocrinologists and diabetologists), these are even exceptionally scarce, with some countries having single or double digits for entire nations. In Nigeria with a population of more than 230 million people, there are less than 200 diabetes experts [9]. Nurses and other allied health professionals face similar shortages, and diabetes educator roles remain largely unavailable or undeveloped across much of the continent [4].

Monitoring of blood glucose is an important component of diabetes management as it helps to assess blood glucose levels, determine adequacy of prescribed diets and medications, and determine overall glycaemic control. Various means are being used to assess glycaemic control including use of glucose meter and continuous glucose monitoring systems (CGM). Whereas, glucose meters are readily available and accessible in most parts of Africa, however, access to CGM technologies represent an important challenge across Africa.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) technology: principles and advances

Principles and mechanisms of CGM systems

The CGM devices comprise three main components: a subcutaneous sensor inserted just beneath the skin, a transmitter attached to the sensor that wirelessly sends glucose data, and a receiver or smartphone application required to display real-time glucose readings, trends, and alerts [10, 11]. Continuous glucose monitoring systems operate through enzymatic electrochemical sensors that measure glucose concentrations in interstitial fluid beneath the skin. This provides a close surrogate measure of blood glucose [12] The sensor is based on glucose oxidase or glucose dehydrogenase enzymes (similar to mechanism in glucose meter [12]). This catalyse is glucose oxidation and generates hydrogen peroxide or electrons that are proportional to glucose concentration. These electrochemical signals are detected by the sensor electrode, which is converted to electrical current, and processed through algorithms to estimate corresponding glucose values [13]. These sensors typically remain functional for 7–14 days before the need for a replacement, though newer implantable sensors can function for 90–180 days [14]. Sensor calibration is an important step that often requires fingerstick blood glucose readings to ensure accuracy, although newer generation sensors have minimized this need.

The CGM systems are categorized into three types based on operational characteristics:

Real-time CGM (rt-CGM)

This continuously transmits glucose measurements to receivers and provide immediate feedback and programmable alerts for hypo- and hyperglycaemia. This real-time awareness enables proactive intervention to prevent glycaemic excursions [15].

Intermittently scanned CGM (is-CGM)

This is also called flash glucose monitoring (FGM). This requires users to scan sensors with readers or smartphones to obtain current glucose values and 8-hour retrospective data. Although this lacks continuous transmission and real-time alerts yet is-CGM provides a simpler technology at lower cost [16].

Implantable CGM sensors are placed subcutaneously through minor surgical procedures and can function for extended periods (90–180 days). These systems reduce insertion frequency and may improve patient adherence, though they require specialized insertion procedures [15].

Compared to the earlier version, modern CGM systems have innovated features with improvements in sensor lifespan, miniaturization, user comfort, and connectivity, including Bluetooth- and smartphone-enabled integration. This detailed and advanced sensing and reporting feature capability makes CGM an essential tool in modern diabetes care, as it offers benefits that traditional blood glucose monitoring (BGM) and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) cannot provide alone [17].

CGM metrics and their clinical significance

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides numerous novel metrics that enhance diabetes management beyond traditional measures like HbA1c, fasting- or casual glycaemic levels. The most clinically significant CGM metrics include:

Time in Range (TIR)

The percentage of time that glucose remains within 70–180 mg/dL (3.9–10.0 mmol/L). International consensus recommends TIR > 70% for most adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. This TIR has been shown to correlate strongly with HbA1c and predicts microvascular complications risk [18]. The higher TIR levels are associated with fewer hypoglycaemic and hyperglycaemic events, thereby indicating better glycaemic control.

Time Below Range (TBR)

The percentage of time spent in hypoglycaemia, classified as Level 1 (< 70 mg/dL) and Level 2 (< 54 mg/dL). The recommended targets are < 4% and < 1% respectively for most adults. This TBR provides a more comprehensive hypoglycaemia assessment than conventional methods [13].

Time Above Range (TAR)

This is the percentage of time spent in hyperglycaemia (> 180 mg/dL for Level 1 and > 250 mg/dL and Level 2). The recommended targets are < 25% and < 5% respectively for most adults [13].

Coefficient of Variation (CV)

This is a measure of glycaemic variability calculated as standard deviation divided by mean glucose. The CV target threshold of ≤ 36% recommended indicates stable glucose control. A high glycaemic variability is associated with increased complications risk independent of mean glucose levels [8].

Glucose Management Indicator (GMI)

is an estimate of HbA1c derived from CGM data calculated from mean CGM glucose values. The GMI enables frequent assessment of glycaemic control trends without laboratory blood tests [19].

These metrics facilitate individualized diabetes management by identifying specific glucose patterns requiring intervention, such as nocturnal hypoglycaemia, postprandial hyperglycaemia, or excessive glycaemic variability [20]. These also enable more precise tailoring of diabetes therapy, which allow clinicians to optimize treatments beyond single-point HbA1c values [21].

Pregnancy-specific GMI metrics have also been developed to improve glycaemic management in diabetic pregnancies, showing the increasing scope and utility of CGM-derived parameters in diverse patient populations [22]. The adoption of these metrics in clinical practice underscores the significant role CGM plays in real-time adjustment and long-term monitoring (Table 1).

Methodology

This narrative review was conducted using a structured and transparent literature search strategy informed by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) framework. Although narrative in nature, a systematic search approach was used to ensure comprehensive identification, selection, and synthesis of relevant evidence on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) use in Africa focusing on its various opportunities for its use, the challenges and the future direction. A comprehensive search strategy was employed to identify relevant studies published in peer-reviewed journals and academic databases. The search included terms related to CGM, diabetes mellitus, Africa, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with Boolean operators used to refine the search. Key databases, such as PubMed, African Journals Online (AJOL), Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, were searched for eligible articles. Manual searches for reference lists of relevant articles and key diabetes guidelines were also performed to identify additional eligible studies. Grey literature, including conference proceedings, policy briefs, and reports from ministries of health and diabetes associations across Africa, was reviewed where relevant. Boolean operators (“AND”, “OR”) and truncation symbols (*) were used appropriately. The search was adapted for each database (Table 2).

Results

Monitoring of blood glucose level remains significantly low among many patients in Africa despite being fundamental to diabetes management. Many patients lack glucometers, and those who own devices often cannot afford sufficient test strips for recommended frequency of monitoring. The point-of-care HbA1c testing is not readily available outside major urban centres, requiring laboratory testing that may be unavailable or unaffordable [23].

Advanced diabetes technologies including insulin pumps and CGM devices are virtually absent from public healthcare systems and accessible only to wealthy individuals who can afford private healthcare in Nigeria as in most countries in Africa. Even in relatively affluent countries like Egypt, Morocco and South Africa, CGM and pump therapy uptake remain largely limited to private beneficiaries on insurance [3]. The limited use of these technologies is mainly due to cost as the cheapest CGM systems cost of $2,000–6,000 annually exceed per capita gross national income in many African countries [24]. The cost of such devices, including sensors and disposables, remains prohibitively high for most patients, and healthcare systems often lack comprehensive policies for reimbursement or subsidies, making these technologies inaccessible [25]. Governmental support and policy frameworks to improve access to these technologies are limited and inconsistently implemented within the continent. Although some countries provide partial subsidies or reimbursement for insulin but less so for modern technologies like CGM or insulin pumps, inhibiting widespread adoption [25]. Also, infrastructure challenges including device supply chains and maintenance logistics further complicate service delivery.

This review identified five (5) clinical studies conducted in five (5) African countries (Uganda [26], Kenya [26], Malawi [27, 28], Rwanda [29] and Tunisia [30]) that evaluated the use of CGM in diabetes care. The studies which are from East Africa and North Africa countries, comprised feasibility study, retrospective and prospective studies. Emerging African evidence demonstrated measurable glycaemic benefits of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). In a randomized feasibility trial in rural Malawi, time-in-range (70–180 mg/dL) increased from 30.8% in week 1 to 38.7% by week 10, while CGM users achieved approximately 1.1% lower HbA1c compared with usual care [27]. In Rwanda, the CAPT1D (Continuous Glucose Monitoring Among Patients with Type 1 Diabetes) prospective study reported HbA1c reductions of 2.8% at 6 months and 3.2% at 12 months, accompanied by improved TIR and absence of severe hypoglycaemia during follow-up [29]. These findings suggest that CGM can improve glycaemic control and reduce hypoglycaemia risk in African diabetes populations.

Discussion

Evidence from African settings indicates that the economic implications of CGM adoption are strongly influenced by device price relative to conventional self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG). Healthcare financing structures predominantly rely on out-of-pocket payments creating catastrophic health expenditures for families managing chronic diseases like diabetes and SMBG remains the standard as CGM costs are significantly higher. A multi-country survey in Kenya and South Africa reported that patients considered US$19–26 per month an affordable price for CGM, whereas the actual market cost is approximately US$120 per month, representing a major barrier to adoption [31]. However, improved glycaemic control and reductions in acute complications may offset costs over time. For example, the Continuous Glucose Monitoring Among Patients with Type 1 Diabetes (CAPT1D) study in Rwanda demonstrated sustained CGM use (> 80% sensor adherence) with improved glycaemic outcomes and reduced diabetes-related hospitalizations, suggesting potential downstream healthcare cost savings compared with conventional SMBG-based monitoring. In Nigeria, more than 95% of costs of treatment are borne by patients as less than 5% of the populace are on the insurance scheme [32–34]. The national health insurance scheme exists in some countries but typically provide limited coverage for diabetes care, particularly for medications, diagnostics, and technologies beyond basic care [4]. Where insurance exists, coverage often excludes CGM devices, insulin pumps, and even some newer medications. The economic burden of diabetes extends beyond direct medical costs. Productivity losses from diabetes-related disability, premature mortality, and caregiver time represent substantial indirect costs [35]. These economic impacts worsen poverty cycles, as families exhaust savings and borrow money to finance diabetes care, potentially impoverishing entire households [36]. Cost-effectiveness analyses from high-income countries have demonstrated long-term savings from intensive glucose management and complication prevention [25]. However, these analyses may not apply to African situations where healthcare system structures, baseline complication rates, and cost structures differ substantially. African-specific economic evaluations are largely absent from the literature, limiting evidence-based policy development [4].

Opportunities presented by CGM use in Africa

Potential for improved glycaemic control and reduction of complications

CGM technology offers a transformative opportunity to improve glycaemic control among people with diabetes in Africa. Evidence from global studies illustrates its effectiveness in reducing hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia, and glycaemic variability which are the important determinants of diabetic complications. In-hospital trials have demonstrated that CGM-guided insulin titration can significantly increase time-in-range (TIR) and reduce durations of hyperglycaemia, demonstrating its clinical utility [37]. For African populations who are experiencing high rates of diabetes complications, improved glycaemic control through CGM could substantially reduce microvascular and macrovascular complications. The relationship between glycaemic control and complications is well-established: each 1% HbA1c reduction decreases microvascular complications risk by approximately 35–40% [38]. Given that many African patients present with poor baseline glycaemic control and advanced complications, the absolute risk reduction achievable through CGM could be substantial [3]. Hypoglycaemia represents a particularly significant concern in resource-limited settings where emergency medical services are limited and hypoglycaemia treatment may be [39]. The ability of CGM to detect and alert users to impending hypoglycaemia before symptoms occur could prevent severe hypoglycaemic events and associated morbidity and mortality. Studies have demonstrated that CGM use reduces severe hypoglycaemia frequency by 50–70% [21]. Recent evidence suggests that CGM benefits extend to type 2 diabetes populations who are not using insulin. This represents most African patients with diabetes where at least 80% are on oral anti-diabetic medications alone [40]. The MOBILE study demonstrated that CGM use among non-insulin-treated type 2 diabetes patients improved HbA1c more than blood glucose monitoring, with additional benefits including increased physical activity and improved dietary choices informed by glucose [41].

Empowerment, engagement and quality of life of patients with diabetes

Beyond glycaemic metrics, CGM technology offers substantial potential for patient empowerment and improved quality of life. These benefits are particularly relevant in African situations where diabetes education and support services are limited. The visual representation of glucose patterns may enhance understanding for patients with limited health literacy, making abstract concepts like postprandial hyperglycaemia tangible and actionable [32]. For adolescents and young adults, CGM will help facilitate greater self-responsibility and control over diabetes management. This will foster a sense of normalcy and reducing the psychological burden associated with its management [42]. The real-time glucose feedback will enable patients to observe immediate consequences of food choices, physical activity, and medication timing, which will help facilitate behavioural modification and improve self-management skill [32]. Studies have shown that CGM users experience reduced anxiety about hypoglycaemia, greater confidence in diabetes self-management, and improved sense of control over their [33]. Patient-reported outcomes from CGM studies consistently demonstrate improved diabetes-related quality of life, treatment satisfaction, and reduced diabetes distress [43]. These psychosocial benefits complement clinical outcomes and may help improve long-term treatment adherence and sustained engagement with diabetes self-management [44].

Integration with digital health and telemedicine in resource-limited settings

The rapidly expanding mobile technology infrastructure in Africa presents unique opportunities for digital health interventions in diabetes management. Mobile phone penetration has increased significantly across the continent, with smartphone ownership growing even in rural areas. This is the same in some African countries including Nigeria where mobile phone ownership is high among people living with diabetes [45]. This digital connectivity will enable CGM integration with mobile health platforms for remote monitoring, automated diabetes education through smartphone applications, and telemedicine consultations for rural and underserved populations [23].

Challenges to CGM implementation in Africa

Financial barriers and cost-effectiveness concerns

The financial burden of CGM devices remains a significant barrier to their widespread adoption in Africa. The initial costs of CGM systems and sensors, combined with recurring expenses, are often prohibitive for both individuals and healthcare systems in low- and middle-income contexts [23]. In the absence of comprehensive insurance frameworks or government subsidies, out-of-pocket expenditures will hinder many from accessing these technologies. Economic evaluations reveal that despite demonstrated cost-effectiveness in high-income countries by preventing complications and reducing hospitalizations, these benefits are difficult to achieve without initial capital support. Hence, it is imperative to consider both direct and indirect costs when assessing CGM implementation feasibility as financial constraints will remain a key limiting factor in technology adoption across many African nations.

Infrastructure, supply chain, and technical support limitations

CGM deployment requires reliable supply chains for sensors and components, regular technical support, and an adequately trained healthcare personnel capable of interpreting data and managing devices. In many parts of Africa, such infrastructure is largely unavailable. Interruptions in supply or lack of maintenance services will retard consistent technology use, reduce clinical benefits and patient confidence [23]. Healthcare providers often lack formal training in CGM interpretation and clinical use, limiting the integration of this technology into routine care. Survey data from African diabetes clinicians reveal low confidence in CGM data interpretation and limited understanding of CGM-derived metrics like time-in-range, coefficient of variation, and ambulatory glucose profiles [46]. Without adequate training, healthcare providers cannot effectively use CGM data to optimize diabetes management, limiting the clinical benefit of technology. This absence of structured education and continuous professional development in CGM technology will hinders effective implementation [47]. Paediatric diabetes cares are particularly faced with severe healthcare limitations as only few African countries have paediatric endocrinologists, and general paediatricians often have limited diabetes training [24, 39]. Yet children and adolescents are specially benefit from CGM technology particularly for glycaemic control, hypoglycaemia prevention, and reducing family burden [48].

Internet connectivity, while improving, remains inconsistent across many of Africa countries. CGM systems increasingly rely on cloud-based data storage and smartphone applications requiring internet access. Rural areas with limited connectivity cannot fully utilize these features, limiting CGM functionality and remote monitoring capabilities [49].

Paediatric diabetes in Nigeria remains under-reported but clinically significant, with most cases involving Type 1 diabetes diagnosed during adolescence. Epidemiological data reveal substantial regional variability and late presentation, often complicated by diabetic ketoacidosis. The integration of Continuous Glucose Monitoring technologies offers a transformative opportunity for improving paediatric diabetes outcomes. Structured implementation models such as spanning hospital-based care, school support systems, telemedicine platforms, and community health integration are essential for effective CGM adoption in children and adolescents, particularly in resource-limited healthcare settings [50].

Sociocultural and behavioural barriers

Device acceptance and sustained use are influenced by many factors including cultural, psychosocial, and behavioural factors. Stigma related to diabetes and medical device visibility can discourage adolescents and adults from adopting CGM. Concerns over privacy, body image, and device management, including device adherence and discomfort, have been shown to affect uptake [44]. In African cultures where medical device visibility may conflict with body image norms or cultural clothing practices, acceptability may be reduced. Moreover, limited diabetes knowledge and poor health literacy among individuals with diabetes may restrict understanding of CGM benefits and appropriate usage. Additionally, some people in Africa have belief that diseases, including diabetes, are caused by gods, who must be appeased for healing to occur. This cultural beliefs about disease (diabetes) causation and appropriate treatment may influence CGM acceptability. Patients who attribute diabetes to spiritual causes may downplay or outrightly decline the use of technological interventions [45]. There is perception in Africa that women generally have less financial capability compared to men. Hence, women may have less control over household financial resources and healthcare decision-making, potentially limiting their ability to access technology even when available [51]. Psychosocial factors that are related to diabetes management, worsened by cultural beliefs and social determinants, have been shown to negatively impact CGM adoption and self-management efficacy [35, 51]. Hence, Patient-centred education to address these barriers is required and essential to improve acceptance and optimize clinical outcomes.

Regulatory and policy framework gaps

Across African countries, regulatory protocols and pathways for approval of medical devices vary widely, with many lacking streamlined processes or procedures for novel technologies like CGM. These regulatory difficulties usually postpone or delay market entry for years after devices become available in other continents [39]. Even where regulatory approval exists, devices may not reach the market due to limited commercial interest from manufacturers as companies may perceive African markets as insufficiently profitable given limited purchasing power, creating access barriers despite regulatory approval. In addition, policy frameworks for technology coverage and reimbursement are largely absent in most countries. National health insurance schemes, where they exist, typically do not cover CGM devices. Private insurance coverage is variable and often inadequate, leaving even insured patients with substantial out-of-pocket costs [52]. Harmonization efforts through regional bodies like the African Medicines Regulatory Harmonization initiative can help with standardization and approval. Standard treatment guidelines in many African countries do not address advanced diabetes technologies, limiting their integration into public healthcare systems.

Current clinical and implementation studies in Africa

The evidence base for CGM use in Africa is characterized by a small number of clinical trials and observational studies. Limited studies conducted in South Africa and Kenya only evaluated the impact of continuous and periodic CGM use in comparison with standard glucose monitoring in people with type 1 diabetes. These trials focussed on glycaemic control improvements, safety, quality of life, and economic evaluations [39]. In addition, feasibility trials, such as those in rural Malawi, assessed the real-world possibility and clinical outcomes of introducing CGM in low-resource, rural settings with limited healthcare personnel. Early results emphasized the importance of training, device reliability, and patient acceptability in these environments [23]. Additionally, in-hospital CGM studies across some African countries showed CGM could be important to optimize inpatient diabetes management. Studies from Nigeria, South Africa, and Kenya showed that CGM-guided insulin titration significantly increased percentage of time-in-range of glucose levels and reduced hyperglycaemic excursions compared to standard point-of-care glucose [37]. Inpatient CGM use also improved hypoglycaemia detection, identified asymptomatic hypoglycaemic episodes that standard monitoring misses. Given that severe hypoglycaemia in hospitalized patients increases mortality risk, improved hypoglycaemia detection through CGM could reduce inpatient complications and mortality [37].

Gaps in African CGM research

Despite emerging evidence, significant knowledge gaps exist regarding CGM use in African populations. Large-scale, multi-centre, long-term studies are scarce, limiting definite conclusions about effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability across diverse African settings. Most existing studies are small pilot or feasibility trials rather than definitive effectiveness trials. Additionally, research on type 2 diabetes populations which constitute 90–95% of Africans with diabetes, is particularly limited. Most studies on CGM use are focused on type 1 diabetes [3]. Evidence on the benefits of CGM use on non-insulin-treated patients with type 2 diabetes in African settings is almost absent. Also, research in paediatric CGM use in Africa is limited despite that children represent a substantial population of diabetes. The unique challenges of diabetes management in paediatric age group, including school environments, parental involvement, and developmental considerations, will require specific investigation in the Africa settings [52]. Studies on health economics regarding CGM benefits, including African-specific cost-effectiveness analyses accounting for local cost structures, healthcare systems, and willingness-to-pay thresholds are largely absent. Without such data, policy makers will lack evidence for informed resource allocation decisions for CGM implementation in African counties.

Data privacy, security, and ethical use of CGM data

CGM devices as digital tools generate massive sensitive health data of individual patient with diabetes that are transmitted wirelessly and often stored on cloud platforms. This raises the issues of data privacy, security, and ethical management of these data. In many African countries, legal frameworks for health data protection are underdeveloped, creating risks of unauthorized access, data breaches, or misuse of personal health information [49]. International data transfer regulations pose additional challenges. CGM data transmitted to cloud servers in other countries may be subjected to data protection laws of foreign jurisdictions which may be potentially conflicting with local regulations or patient expectations. Hence, establishing clear guidelines and robust cybersecurity measures will be essential to safeguard patient trust and comply with regulations as CGM integration progresses [48]. Consent for data sharing requires particular attention. Patients should understand how their CGM data will be used, stored, and shared, with clear opt-in mechanisms for research or secondary uses. Given varying digital literacy levels, consent processes must ensure genuine understanding [9].

Future directions: prospects and innovations in CGM for Africa

Emerging technologies: non-invasive and implantable sensors

Technological innovation in diabetes management continues to enhance CGM devices towards less invasive, more comfortable, and longer-lasting sensors. Implantable CGM sensors, such as those with 90- to 180-day wear cycles, have demonstrated stable accuracy over multiple insertion-removal cycles, thereby reducing patient burden and improving wear adherence [14]. These non-invasive glucose monitoring technologies under development could eliminate sensor insertion requirements entirely. Optical methods including Raman spectroscopy, near-infrared spectroscopy, and impedance spectroscopy had showed promise in research settings, though their clinical applicability and cost-effectiveness require further validation [53]. If successful, such technologies could improve acceptability and reduce costs.

Miniaturization and improved sensor materials has continued to enhance users’ comfort and discreteness. Smaller, thinner sensors which are less visible, more comfortable, and potentially reduce stigma and improving adherence are increasingly available [21]. In addition, integration with fashionable accessories or concealment strategies may further enhance acceptability among patients. CGM alternatives of reduced cost designed specifically for low-resource settings represent another innovation pathway. Simplified devices which prioritize essential functions while minimizing cost could expand accessibility. However, quality standards must be maintained to ensure safety and efficacy [52].

Artificial intelligence and integrated decision support systems

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) with CGM platforms offers sophisticated decision support systems for personalized diabetes management. AI algorithms, including machine learning algorithms that can analyse complex and numerous glucose data and patterns, predict hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia hours in advance, and provide automated insulin dosing recommendations and tailored lifestyle modifications are future possibilities [54]. Predictive algorithms for hypoglycaemia prevention are particularly valuable and essential. Studies have demonstrated that AI systems can predict hypoglycaemia 30–60 min in advance with high sensitivity and specificity, enabling preventive carbohydrate intake or insulin reduction [55]. For African patients with limited access to emergency hypoglycaemia treatment, such prevention capabilities could be lifes-aving. The integration of CGM data with other health metrics including physical activity, heart rate, sleep patterns, and dietary intake will create comprehensive digital phenotypes which will enable more precise, personalized diabetes management. Smartphone sensors and wearable devices available to facilitate this multi-dimensional data integration is essential [54].These possibilities will enhance therapeutic precision and reduce the cognitive burden on patients and clinicians.

Capacity building and sustainable models for CGM scale-up

Systematic healthcare workforce capacity building is essential for sustainable CGM implementation. Sustainable CGM scale-up necessitates comprehensive capacity-building interventions targeting healthcare providers and patients. Multi-level training programs that target physicians, nurses, diabetes educators, community health workers, and allied health professionals are essential. Curricula should cover CGM principles, device operation, data interpretation, insulin adjustment, troubleshooting, patient education and clinical decision-making will empower clinicians, particularly in paediatric and rural healthcare settings where experience is currently limited [39].Train-the-trainer models can efficiently scale training by developing master trainers who subsequently train others. Regional training centres could serve as hubs for capacity building, gradually expanding trained workforce across countries [52]. Online and distance learning platforms are important and cost-effective media which offer scalable training solutions. E-learning modules, webinars, and virtual communities of practice will enable continuing education without travel costs and time away from clinical duties. Open-access educational resources specifically designed for African peculiarities are needed [48] Mentorship and ongoing support systems should accompany initial training. Virtual mentorship networks that connect less experienced providers with CGM experts could facilitate knowledge transfer [8]. In addition, structured education which covers CGM use, data interpretation, and self-management actions that is culturally adapted, linguistically appropriate, and literacy sensitive is important. Peer educator models where experienced CGM users support new users will help [55].

Implementation of public-private partnerships can facilitate supply chain development, ensure consistent access to consumables, and provide technical support services in view of dwindling government financing capability. Engaging communities through culturally sensitive education and awareness campaigns will mitigate sociocultural barriers and foster acceptance [8]. Also, coordinated multisectoral efforts are key to establishing robust infrastructure and including CGM within national diabetes care programs, thereby driving sustained improvements in diabetes management in Africa [56].

Telemedicine and remote monitoring models

Africa generally has limited diabetes specialists to care for the high population of people with diabetes in the region and the few available mostly practised in tertiary health institutions located in cities with little or none in rural and remote places. Hence, telemedicine models which leverage CGM remote monitoring capabilities could significantly expand specialists access across Africa. Patients in rural areas could receive expert diabetes management guidance without traveling to distant urban centres. Studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of telemedicine for diabetes management, with outcomes comparable to in-person care [57]. Hub-and-spoke models could connect district and peripheral hospitals with tertiary centres, enabling consultative support for complex and difficult-to-manage cases. Specialists could review CGM data remotely, provide management recommendations, and conduct virtual consultations, thereby extending their reach beyond their immediate geographic areas [49].

Task-sharing models with community health workers that integrate CGM remote monitoring offer potential for scaling and improving diabetes care to remote areas. Trained community health workers could insert sensors, provide basic troubleshooting, deliver diabetes education, and facilitate remote monitoring by distant providers. Such models could leverage existing community-based care infrastructure while introducing advanced technology [58]. Additionally, mobile health platforms could facilitate automated data transmission, algorithm-driven insights, and patient-provider communication. SMS-based systems which are readily accessible on basic (flip-flop/non-internet-enabled) phones could extend benefits to populations without smartphones, ensuring digital inclusion [59].

Sustainable implementation models

Implementation of sustainable CGM program in Africa requires carefully designed models that is adaptable to the realities of the healthcare system. Phased approaches which begin with pilot programs in selected sites, followed by gradual expansion based on lessons learned, will mitigate risks and allow iterative refinement. Targeting high-risk populations for initial implementation that enable limited resources to be deployed to where benefits will yield the greatest results is important. These priority populations might include children with type 1 diabetes, pregnant women with diabetes, individuals with hypoglycaemia unawareness, and patients with recurrent severe hypoglycaemia [12]. Additionally, integration with existing diabetes care programs and chronic disease management systems ensures CGM implementation strengthens rather than fragments healthcare delivery. CGM should be embedded within comprehensive diabetes care including medication access, complication screening, and multidisciplinary support [4].

Recommendations for policy makers and health systems

Developing national guidelines and clinical pathways incorporating CGM

To realize the full benefits of CGM, African countries should develop and implement national clinical guidelines that are tailored to local healthcare capacities and patient needs. These guidelines must address CGM indications, usage protocols, data interpretation standards, and integration with insulin therapy, adapted to the available resources [18]. The inclusion of CGM in essential diabetes care packages will formalize its role within health systems and promote standardized use.

Conclusion

Continuous glucose monitoring technology holds significant promise to revolutionize diabetes management in Africa by improving glycaemic control, reduce complications, and empowering patients. Early clinical trials and real-world studies indicate substantial benefits; however, widespread adoption is limited by financial, infrastructural, and sociocultural challenges [18]. Addressing these constraints through targeted interventions offers a path to integrate CGM into African diabetes care paradigms effectively [52]. Successful CGM implementation demands multisectoral collaboration involving governments, healthcare systems, industry, non-governmental organizations, and communities. Coordinated efforts will facilitate regulatory harmonization, financing solutions, infrastructure development, and culturally sensitive education necessary for sustainable impact. Urgent efforts are needed to generate local evidence on CGM’s clinical effectiveness and cost-benefit profiles in African contexts, supporting evidence-based policy and clinical guidance [45]. Innovations in device technology, digital health integration, and capacity building must be prioritized for scalable solutions to diabetes management challenges [4]. Ultimately, embracing CGM technology in Africa has the potential to significantly improve the quality of life and health outcomes for millions of people living with diabetes, contributing significantly to the continent’s broader non-communicable disease control goals.
