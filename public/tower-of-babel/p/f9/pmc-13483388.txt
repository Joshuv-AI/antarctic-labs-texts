High-fidelity super-resolution microscopy datasets spanning multispectral to hyperspectral domains via diffractive optics
Authors: Ning Xu, Cilong Zhang, Yuegang Fu, Qiaofeng Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paired datasets are critical for advancing data-driven microscopy but remain scarce for spectral imaging. Here, we present a comprehensive super-resolution dataset bridging multispectral and hyperspectral domains, generated via diffractive optics-based structured illumination. Unlike synthetic datasets derived from degradation models, these high-fidelity images are derived from physics-based modulation, ensuring accurate representation of biological structures. The collection is organized into five distinct records: (1) a 4-channel multispectral calibration dataset resolving ~96 nm details; (2) a 3-channel multispectral biological dataset of labeled organelles; (3) two spatial super-resolution datasets of cellular filaments reconstructed via pattern-illuminated Fourier ptychography and analytical phase-shifting to minimize artifacts; and (4) a novel hyperspectral dataset (503–689 nm, 6 nm interval, comprising 63 distinct groups) of bovine pulmonary artery endothelial cells acquired using a compact lattice SIM system. We provide paired diffraction-limited widefield (WF) and reconstructed super-resolution (SR) images, alongside system point spread functions. This dataset serves as a rigorous benchmark for developing image restoration, spectral unmixing, and cross-modality deep learning algorithms, facilitating the extraction of nanoscale insights from standard optical setups.

Background & Summary

Fluorescence microscopy has long been an indispensable tool in biological research, yet the fundamental diffraction limit restricts the observation of subcellular structures smaller than approximately 200 nm1–3. While super-resolution (SR) techniques such as structured illumination microscopy (SIM) and point-scanning super-resolution microscopy have successfully overcome this barrier4–6, they often entail complex, bulky hardware and require precise optical alignment. Recently, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a transformative approach to democratize super-resolution, enabling the restoration of high-fidelity images from standard widefield or low-signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) inputs7–9. However, the success of these data-driven algorithms is heavily contingent on the availability of high-quality, paired datasets—specifically, aligned pairs of diffraction-limited widefield (WF) and reconstructed super-resolution (SR) images.

Currently, the development of computational microscopy is hindered by a scarcity of such publicly available benchmarks. Many existing studies rely on synthetic degradation (e.g., mathematically blurring high-resolution images) to generate training inputs, a method that fails to capture the complex, spatially variant point spread functions (PSFs) and Poisson-Gaussian noise inherent to real optical systems10–13. Furthermore, most open microscopy datasets are monochromatic, lacking the spectral dimensionality required to train algorithms for multispectral or hyperspectral analysis14,15.

To bridge this gap, we present a comprehensive benchmark resource: High-fidelity Super-resolution Microscopy Datasets Spanning Multispectral to Hyperspectral Domains. In contrast to prevalent datasets that predominantly rely on synthetic degradation models, our ground truth images are experimentally acquired via diffractive optics-based structured illumination systems. This physics-driven paradigm guarantees structural veracity, ensuring that high-frequency details correspond to genuine biological features rather than the hallucination artifacts frequently observed in purely computational enhancement methods.

To maximize usability, the collection is formally organized into five distinct records generated by two complementary optical architectures. First, to facilitate multicolor image restoration, we provide a multispectral spot array scanning dataset acquired using Mechanical-scan-free multiColor Super-resolution Microscopy (MCoSM) with super-resolution spot array illumination (Fig. 1a)16. This includes a 4-channel (405, 488, 561, and 647 nm) calibration dataset of fluorescent beads resolving ~96 nm details (Record 1), alongside a 3-channel (405, 561, and 647 nm) multispectral dataset of labeled subcellular structures in BPAE cells (Record 4). Second, focusing on hardware robustness, we release a compact lattice SIM dataset generated by a miniaturized diffractive optical element (DOE)-based system (Fig. 1b)17,18. Reconstructed via the noise-robust pattern-illuminated Fourier ptychography (pi-FP) algorithm19, these subsets (Records 2 and 3) serve as a testbed for algorithm generalization across different illumination modalities. Finally, we introduce a hyperspectral cell dataset of Bovine Pulmonary Artery Endothelial (BPAE) cells (Record 5). Spanning 503–689 nm with a fine 6 nm interval, this subset provides a unique data cube that captures the detailed spectral signatures of subcellular structures20,21. By providing these physically paired datasets, experimentally generated through a rigorous optical and analytical reconstruction pipeline (Fig. 1c), we aim to standardize the evaluation of next-generation AI models. Ultimately, this benchmark is specifically designed to facilitate robust algorithm development for super-resolution reconstruction22, spectral unmixing, and cross-modality image translation23–25, accelerating the extraction of nanoscale insights from standard optical setups.

While the optical hardware design and proof-of-concept reconstruction algorithms for these diffractive systems were previously reported16–18, the underlying comprehensive, physically paired datasets—comprising thousands of multi-channel and hyperspectral raw 16-bit frames—have never been publicly released. This Data Descriptor represents the first formal curation and publication of these raw datasets specifically benchmarked for computational microscopy.

Mechanical-scan-free multiColor Super-resolution Microscopy (MCoSM)

The multispectral dataset was acquired using the MCoSM system described in our previous work16. Briefly, the illumination path incorporates a phase-only spatial light modulator (SLM) to generate a multifocal spot array (Fig. 1a)26. By loading pre-calculated phase holograms onto the SLM, the spot array is electronically shifted to scan the entire Field of View (FoV) without mechanical movement, ensuring high temporal stability during multicolor acquisition. Four excitation lasers (405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, and 647 nm) were coupled into the system to excite different fluorophores. The foundational principle of diffractive spot array generation was adapted from our earlier study27,28. During multi-channel acquisition, the system operates in a sequential wavelength-scanning mode. The SLM completes a full spatial scanning cycle of the spot array for one specific wavelength before the system switches to the next excitation laser, thereby ensuring temporal stability.

Compact lattice SIM

The lattice SIM and hyperspectral datasets were acquired using a miniaturized structured illumination microscope detailed in17,18. This system utilizes a custom-designed DOE to generate stable, high-frequency 2D lattice patterns or sinusoidal fringes (Fig. 1b). The DOE-based configuration significantly reduces the system footprint and eliminates phase drift common in interferometric setups29–32. For the hyperspectral acquisition, the detection path was modified to include an electronically tunable spectral scanning module. This enables precise sequential intensity recording from 503 nm to 689 nm with a fixed 6 nm step size, tightly synchronized with the camera exposure.

Sample preparation

To ensure the dataset is representative of typical biological applications, we prepared both calibration standards and biological specimens.

Calibration standards

To quantify the system resolution and calibrate the Point Spread Function (PSF), we imaged carboxylate-modified microspheres (100 nm and 200 nm diameter, TetraSpeck™, ThermoFisher). These beads provided point-source references for estimating the resolution limit of both the MCoSM and compact lattice SIM16,17.

Biological specimens

To evaluate the system’s performance on continuous biological structures, we utilized diverse cell lines. A549 (human lung adenocarcinoma) and HeLa (human cervical cancer) cells lines were kindly provided by Dr. Mingjun Jiang from the School of Life Sciences, Peking University. Cells were cultured in F-12K Medium (for A549) or DMEM (for HeLa), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

For imaging the continuous filamentous networks (Records 2 and 3), cells were seeded on coverslips and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 minutes. Cells were then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour. To label the actin filaments, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor™ 488 Phalloidin (Invitrogen) prepared in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) for 1 hour in the dark at room temperature, according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. After staining, cells were washed twice with HBSS. Finally, approximately 3 drops (~100 μL) of ProLong™ Glass Antifade Mountant (P36982, Invitrogen) were applied directly onto the specimen. The mounted samples were placed on a flat, dry surface for 48 hours at room temperature in the dark prior to imaging.

For the multispectral and hyperspectral datasets (Records 4 and 5), we used prepared slides of Bovine Pulmonary Artery Endothelial (BPAE) cells (Invitrogen, FluoCells™ Prepared Slide #1). These samples contain rich multi-channel information, labeled with DAPI (nucleus), Alexa Fluor 488 (actin filaments), and MitoTracker Red CMXRos (mitochondria), making them ideal for validating multicolor and spectral unmixing capabilities.

Multispectral benchmark generation (MCoSM)

For the multispectral dataset (Fig. 1a), the super-resolution ground truths (GT) were reconstructed from the raw spot-scanning sequences using the shift-and-add principle16. To further recover high-frequency spatial information and suppress optical blur, the reconstructed images underwent deconvolution using the Richardson-Lucy algorithm (10 iterations) with an experimentally characterized point spread function (PSF). Crucially, to generate the corresponding diffraction-limited widefield inputs, we temporally averaged the raw scanning frames. Because the discrete spot array is digitally shifted to uniformly cover the entire FOV over one complete scanning cycle, the temporal integration of these structured raw frames physically equates to uniform widefield illumination. This methodology ensures intrinsic spatiotemporal alignment between the widefield and super-resolution images, thereby eliminating the registration errors and geometric distortions that commonly afflict data acquired via dual-camera configurations.

Lattice SIM cube generation

For datasets acquired via the Compact Lattice SIM system (Fig. 1b), ground truth reconstruction was governed by the pattern-illuminated Fourier Ptychography (pi-FP) algorithm19. In contrast to conventional Wiener filtering, which is susceptible to reconstruction artifacts under varying signal-to-noise ratios, pi-FP iteratively retrieves high-frequency components with superior robustness and artifact suppression.

Super-resolution reconstruction for compact lattice SIM

To generate the super-resolution ground truths for datasets acquired via the Compact Lattice SIM system, we employed reconstruction strategies tailored to the specific acquisition modes. For the datasets utilizing standard fringe illumination (Record 3), the super-resolution target images (designated as SIM.tif) were generated using a rigorous 15-step analytical phase-shifting SIM reconstruction method, which processes raw frames spanning 3 spatial orientations and 5 phase shifts.

For the datasets acquired under 2D lattice illumination (Records 2 and 5), ground truth reconstruction was governed by the pattern-illuminated Fourier Ptychography (pi-FP) algorithm19. In contrast to conventional Wiener filtering, which is susceptible to reconstruction artifacts under varying signal-to-noise ratios, pi-FP iteratively retrieves high-frequency components with superior robustness. To ensure reproducibility, the specific algorithmic parameters were set as follows: the iterative optimization was initialized using an up-sampled widefield equivalent image. At each updating step, an experimentally calibrated PSF was utilized for forward projection. To effectively suppress noise amplification during the spectrum update, a Tikhonov regularization parameter of approximately 10−3 was applied. The reconstruction process was set to terminate either when the relative error between consecutive iterations fell below a threshold of 10−4, or after a maximum of 100 iterations, ensuring a perfect balance between artifact suppression and structural fidelity.

Hyperspectral Cell Dataset

For the hyperspectral dataset (Record 5), the intensities are provided in raw 16-bit format. We recommend applying min-max normalization per spectral channel for visualization, as the signal intensity varies with the emission spectrum of the fluorophores. The reconstruction protocol was executed independently across each of the 31 spectral channels (spanning 503–689 nm). The resulting super-resolution images were stacked to form a high-dimensional (x, y, λ) data cube (Fig. 1c). Similar to the MCoSM pipeline, the paired widefield references for each spectral band were generated by summing the raw phase-shifted lattice images, ensuring rigorous pixel-level correspondence for spectral unmixing and super-resolution tasks. Technical Validation

The quality and fidelity of the provided datasets were rigorously verified through both quantitative metrics and biological structural analysis.

As previously demonstrated in our methodology report16, the MCoSM system achieved a lateral resolution of ~96 nm, visually and quantitatively confirmed in Fig. 3e-i, using the bead datasets now publicly released as Record 1. Structural fidelity

To verify that the SR images represent true biological structures rather than computational artifacts, we compared the intensity profiles of filamentous structures (e.g., actin filaments). As shown in the data records, closely spaced filaments that are indistinguishable in widefield images are clearly resolved in the provided SR images (Fig. 3a–d), consistent with the expected distribution of the cytoskeleton.

Spectral accuracy

For the hyperspectral dataset, we extracted the mean intensity of labeled regions across the spectral stack. The resulting spectral curves matched the emission characteristics of the fluorophores (Fig. 2c), confirming the spectral fidelity of the acquisition module. The absolute 6 nm spectral interval is intrinsically guaranteed by the factory calibration of the electronically tunable spectral filter. It is worth noting that the slight deviation in spectral shape compared to idealized reference spectra in solution is an authentic representation of in situ biological measurements, caused by the intracellular microenvironment. Preserving this real-world spectral variation, rather than providing idealized pure-substance calibrations, is a critical feature of this benchmark dataset for training robust unmixing algorithms.

Usage Notes

To strictly preserve the quantitative fidelity and linear radiometric response of the optical systems, all image datasets in this repository are provided as raw 16-bit unsigned integer TIFF (.tif) files, completely free from lossy compression or non-linear tone mapping. Consequently, because the valid fluorescent signal intensities typically occupy only a specific sub-range of the full 16-bit dynamic depth (0–65,535), the images may appear dark or exhibit seemingly low contrast when initially opened in standard image viewers. This is a normal characteristic of raw quantitative microscopy data. For proper visualization and analysis in general-purpose software such as ImageJ/Fiji, users are strongly advised to manually adjust the display window to the actual data range (by navigating to Image → Adjust → Brightness/Contrast, or utilizing the shortcut Ctrl + Shift + C, and applying auto-scaling).

Furthermore, users may observe a consistent low-frequency baseline (background) in the reconstructed super-resolution images. This baseline represents the authentic out-of-focus fluorescence physically captured by the widefield-based diffractive systems when imaging thick biological specimens. To maintain strict radiometric linearity and preserve the true Poisson-Gaussian noise distribution for deep learning applications, we intentionally abstained from applying arbitrary background subtraction or non-linear thresholding. Retaining this unclipped physical baseline is crucial, as it allows algorithm developers to train robust deep learning models capable of handling real-world background variations. Users who require cosmetically enhanced images for pure visualization purposes may apply standard post-processing algorithms (rolling ball background subtraction in ImageJ).

Limitations

While this dataset provides rigorously paired high-fidelity data, users should be aware of its specific limitations to ensure appropriate use. First, the current repository is restricted to 2D spatial imaging; models trained exclusively on this data may not directly generalize to 3D volumetric deconvolution tasks without transfer learning. Second, the datasets were acquired from fixed cellular specimens; consequently, they do not encapsulate the spatiotemporal dynamics, motion blur, or progressive photobleaching characteristics inherent to live-cell imaging. Finally, as discussed above, the physical noise and out-of-focus background distributions are intrinsically tied to the widefield-based diffractive structured illumination architectures, which may differ from the noise profiles generated by confocal or two-photon point-scanning systems.
