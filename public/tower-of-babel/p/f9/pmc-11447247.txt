Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy and RGB-imaging: a comparative study of non-invasive haemoglobin assessment
Authors: Boris Yakimov, Kirill Buiankin, Georgy Denisenko, Yuliya Shitova, Andrey Shkoda, Evgeny Shirshin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-invasive assessment of haemoglobin (Hb) level in blood is a hot spot in the point-of-care biomedical diagnostics. Several optical methods are suggested as a solution, some of them being approved for clinical use. Still, there is no consensus on the accuracy of optical techniques, the quality of Hb assessment on different tissue sites, and on the ability of combined use of several optical techniques to improve the quality of Hb level prediction. In this work we examined the capabilities of two optical techniques—diffuse reflectance spectroscopy and RGB-imaging of the skin and fingernails areas—in detecting low blood Hb level. The test sample consisted of 240 adult volunteers with 70 volunteers exhibiting Hb level lower than 120 g/L. We show that using simple descriptors of the diffuse reflectance spectrum of the forearm skin and fingernails is applicable for predicting low blood Hb concentration (ROC-AUC = 0.84 ± 0.08), while RGB-imaging shows similar performance when applied to the fingernail areas (ROC-AUC = 0.83 ± 0.07), which can be considered perspective for clinical use and screening properties. We also report that while the joint use of predictions from two optical methods slightly improves the accuracy of non-invasive Hb level assessment (ROC-AUC = 0.86 ± 0.07), the effect is not as high as one might expect from combining predictions of truly independent modalities, indicating the limit of the accuracy one can expect with multimodal optical approach. We review this case and propose possible solutions towards more sensitive non-invasive optical determination of hemoglobin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73084-6.Subject terms: Biophotonics, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

The concentration of haemoglobin in human blood (hereinafter the “Hb level”) is a clinically important parameter. Reduced Hb level, or anaemia, affects approximately 29% of non-pregnant women, 38% of pregnant women, and 43% of children worldwide1–3. As a result, the World Health Organization has designated the fight against anaemia as one of its strategic goals4. While the quantitation of the Hb level is of high importance, its determination is currently carried out invasively using venous blood sampling, the procedure which is painful, time-consuming, and requires qualified medical personnel5.

Due to the pronounced absorption of Hb in the visible and near-infrared (NIR) spectral ranges, numerous studies aim to measure Hb level non-invasively using optical techniques3,5,6. Although the basic idea of the suggested approaches is similar, they vary in tissue sites investigated, spectral ranges, and geometry of the imaging/sensing scheme.

Currently, multispectral photoplethysmography (PPG) method for the Hb level assessment is approved by the FDA and is used clinically7–9. Early studies on PPG demonstrated that detecting transmitted or reflected light from a fingertip allows to determine Hb level by analyzing the oscillating component of diffuse reflection associated with blood pulsation10. Though a number of studies have reported results with an error rate of less than 10 g/l7,8, it is worth noting that independent prospective studies show that even the FDA-certified devices exhibit the measurement error rate of more than 15–20 g/l and have a low sensitivity in detecting anaemia3,11,12. Namely, some studies report sensitivity of detecting a low Hb level using PPG to be ~ 0.4–0.53,11; while other report the detection of a low Hb level with a sensitivity of about 0.813. The sensitivity of the prediction of low Hb level in newborns was reported to be in the 0.7–0.9 range14, while the specificity of the PPG (in prediction of normal Hb level) is generally higher (0.8–0.9). However, this fact may be a consequence of the inconsistency in sampling between patients from the groups with low and normal haemoglobin (the latter group is usually larger than the former).

The alternative to PPG is using RGB-images of tissue sites free from melanin to quantitate blood Hb level or predict its value to be below the threshold15–17. Some research groups propose imaging of the conjunctiva of the eye and demonstrate prediction of low Hb level with sensitivity and specificity of ~ 0.7–0.915–17. In a series of studies on a large population (~ 10,000 patients)18–20 the analysis of RGB images of fundus and the external eye has demonstrated the classification F-measure of 0.85 for fundus images19 and receiver operating characteristics area under curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.8320.

Compared to the conjunctiva and fundus, the fingernails area appears to be the most accessible melanin-free tissue site. Hence, recent studies20–27 have utilized RGB-imaging of fingernails for assessing Hb level. The study of Mannino et al. demonstrated the root mean squared error of ~ 12 g/L and the detection of Hb level lower than 125 g/L with the sensitivity and specificity values of 0.92 and 0.76, respectively. Typically, further studies report similar or even higher regression and classification quality scores21–27, which, however, are not always confirmed in independent prospective studies. For example, RGB-imaging method applied for estimation of low Hb level demonstrated the sensitivity and specificity at the level of 0.4–0.63.

One of the drawbacks of RGB-imaging is that the optical signal is detected with a low spectral resolution (about ~ 100 nm per channel), and, potentially, more detailed spectral information could improve the accuracy of the Hb level determination. Surprisingly, the published sources contain a relatively small number of studies where the applicability of diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) in the assessment of blood Hb level is considered. Thus, the studies28–30 demonstrate the possibility of non-invasive determination of haemoglobin by analysis of the DRS data from the conjunctiva of the eye in the range of 400–700 nm with concentration determination accuracy varying from 10 to 20 g/l, and classification quality expressed as ROC-AUC of > 0.930. In the studies31,32 performed on a relatively small dataset (n = 20–40) the performance of DRS applied to the skin of the forearm was tested. In their recent essay, Banerjee et al.33 demonstrate high accuracy (R2 > 0.95) for determining the Hb level based on DRS data of fingernails of infants.

Thus, existing approaches rely on the use of methods of varying complexity and various melanin-containing and melanin-free tissue areas—nail plates, conjunctiva of the eye, skin of the forearm, etc. Differences in the approaches and tissue sites make it difficult to directly compare and select the most promising methods and tissue regions for the further development of the field of non-invasive optical Hb diagnostics. In particular, it is potentially interesting that hemoglobin levels can be determined solely from tissue color without spectral resolution for selective contrasting of the Hb absorption. Here, using diffuse reflectance spectroscopy and RGB imaging data from the same patients, we are attempting to determine whether the spectral resolution is in fact beneficial, and whether there is difference between the two techniques in terms of how accurately anaemia (Hb level below 120 g/L) can be predicted. The next issue we are addressing is whether the simultaneous use of multiple optical techniques would yield synergy in Hb level assessment. We show that, while the joint use of predictions from the two optical methods increases the accuracy of non-invasive determination of the Hb level, is not sufficiently superior, providing a realistic assessment of what can be expected from optical methods in solving problem of non-invasive blood Hb assessment.

Materials and methods

The study and methods used were approved by the Moscow City Ethics Committee on the basis of the Regulations on the City Ethics Committee and in accordance with the standards of operating procedures (Protocol No. 68 dated July 4, 2022). The methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All patients gave their written informed consent to participate in the study.

Cohort used for the training and evaluation of models predicting low hb level

To train and evaluate models predicting the risk of low haemoglobin, we collected diffuse reflectance spectra of the forearm skin and fingernails and RGB-imaging data of fingernails for 240 volunteers undergoing examination in the emergency department of the 67th City Clinical Hospital (Moscow, Russia). The investigated cohort sample was balanced by sex – there were 117 female and 123 male volunteers. The average age was 56 ± 20 y.o. spanning from 18 to 95 y.o.

In parallel with optical measurements, invasive determination of the Hb level was carried out according to standard clinical protocols for all patients34,35. Venous blood collected from the antecubital vein using the Vacutainer was characterized using the CBC analysis in a certified (ISO 15189:2012) clinical diagnostic laboratory. The measurements were performed 10–15 min after collecting optical data and further were used as a ground truth for low Hb prediction.

The invasively determined Hb level for female volunteers was 120 ± 21 g/L, while the average level for male patients was 137 ± 27 g/L. Patients were split into the group with low Hb (< 120 g/l) with 70 volunteers among whom 52 were female, respectively, and the group with high Hb (≥ 120 g/l) with 170 volunteers, 65 of whom were female.

Independent cohort used to evaluate the risk of low hb level

For additional verification of models trained using the acquired optical response, we also collected a database of DRS data from forearm skin among students and employees of the Lomonosov Moscow State University without additional blood sampling – only non-invasive measurements were made using the optical method. In total, measurements were collected on 274 volunteers (161 female, 113 male) with a mean age of 31 ± 10 y.o. spanning from 18 to 70 y.o.

Diffuse reflectance spectra acquisition and preprocessing

Measurements using DRS were made on two areas of the tissue – the inner side of the forearm and the nail plate (Fig. 1A). For DRS measurements the custom-built fiber probe was used. The fiber probe consisted of transmitting and receiving fused silica fibers (core diameter of 600 μm), located at a distance of 2.5 mm at the measuring ends. The transmitting fiber was connected to the broadband illumination source (halogen lamp with continuous spectrum in the 450–2500 nm range) delivering 1 mW/cm2 on the skin surface, while the collecting fiber was connected to the Ocean Optics Maya 2000 spectrometer (Ocean Optics, USA) detecting light response in the 200–1100 nm range with a spectral resolution of ~ 3 nm. Distance between the measuring ends of the dual-fiber probe was chosen so that the intensity of diffusely reflected light in the spectral region corresponding to the Hb absorption (500–600 nm) was sufficiently high, while the signal was collected from a depth which approximately corresponds to the skin dermis, where small and large blood vessels are located.

Monte-Carlo modelling of light transport in tissues

To estimate the light probing depth by the used dual-fiber probe, we simulated the propagation of light in a medium imitating the optical properties of skin at a wavelength of 570 nm, using two fibers with a numerical aperture of 0.22 and a refractive index 1.47, located at a distance of 2.5 mm, as a source and detector.

The skin was modelled as a 3 mm thick three-layer environment consisting of epidermis, dermis and hypodermis with a length and width of 6 mm each. The thickness of the epidermis was 100 μm, the dermis was 2 mm, and the hypodermis modelled the remaining part of the environment. The absorption and scattering coefficients of the media were taken from the Jacques et al.36 to match the optical properties of epidermis and dermis of the I-II skin type according to Fitzpatrick. Namely, it was assumed that the absorption of the epidermis is determined by the presence of melanin in it (volume concentration 2.5%) so that the absorption coefficient in the epidermis was 10.5 cm− 1, the scattering coefficient was 540 cm− 1, the absorption coefficient in the dermis was determined by the presence of blood in the dermis with volume content of 0.2% and saturation of 0.67, so that the absorption coefficient was 0.5 cm− 1 and the scattering coefficient was 375 cm− 1. The absorption and scattering coefficients of the hypodermis were 0.05 cm− 1 and 400 cm− 1, correspondingly. Scattering was modelled with the Henyey-Greenstein scattering function with anisotropy factor of g = 0.9 for all three layers. The simulation was carried out in PyXOpto python package37, the number of photon packages used to estimate probing depth was equal to 5·106.

To generate the heatmap of the sampling volume obtained by DRS, we tracked the trajectories of photon packets emitted from source fiber and entering a voxel with coordinates and subsequently reaching the detector. By averaging over a significant number of photon packets, we derived an estimate of the probability that a photon will pass through a voxel with coordinates and then reach the detector. RGB-imaging of fingernails

Along with the measurements of the diffuse reflectance spectra, we collected RGB-images of fingernails of participated volunteers under standard illumination conditions. The setup consisted of a ~ 40 × 40 × 20 cm box made of aluminium composite material, inside which a Logitech C615 USB-camera and white LED illuminator providing color temperature of ~ 6000 K were mounted. The box had a rectangular slot with the size of ~ 15 × 10 cm, so the patient could put his hand. After that, the image was taken under standard conditions with the exposure time fixed for all patients.

In the obtained images, the regions corresponding to the fingernails and skin of the index, middle and ring fingers were segmented. To account for possible inhomogeneities, we used only the inner region of the segmented area, selecting the sub image of [0.25w,0.75w]× [0.25 h,0.75 h], where w and h are the width and height of the initial segmented region. In these selected regions we calculated 5-, 15-, 25-, 50-, 75-, 85-, and 95th percentiles of the intensity distribution for each color channel of the image. Then, the average percentile values were calculated for all fingernails and skin areas for each patient and used as features to build a predictive model.

Evaluated classification models

We assessed quality of two relatively simple models – the logistic regression and the random forest – in the task of classification of patients with low and high Hb level in blood using optical descriptors obtained via DRS and RGB-imaging.

As the first model, a logistic regression (LR) with L2-regularization was used to assess the possibility of classifying low and high Hb patients with a linear classifier. Before training a LR using certain subset of features, the features were normalized by subtracting the median value of the feature and dividing by the interquartile range estimated using the training set. We additionally tuned the hyperparameter responsible for the contribution of the regularization term to the LR-loss function using classification score on the validation splits. The specified parameter varied in the 10− 4–104 range with a logarithmic step.

As the second model, the random forest was used38. This model, on the one hand, makes it possible to take into account the nonlinearity of the separating surface in the feature space, on the other hand, it usually demonstrates good performance over a wide range of selected hyperparameters. In our experiments we used a random forest with the number of decision trees equal to 150, with additional tuning of the maximum depth tree on the small subset of values – {3,5,10,15}. The input features were not normalized before being fed into the model.

Predictive models evaluation

Hyperparameters tuning and model evaluation was carried out using the random splitting method: the sample was divided into a training part, on which training was carried out, and a validation part, on which the classification quality was calculated. The validation part was chosen to be balanced, so that the split included the same number of objects of the positive class (objects with low Hb) and the negative class (high Hb). The size of the validation sample was 15% of the total sample size. The ROC-AUC was chosen as a metric assessing the quality of classification.

Input features

We evaluated which sets of descriptors best classify people with low and high blood Hb. For the DRS data we explored the use of two sets of descriptors – features manually engineered from the spectra of the effective optical density as described in Results, “Features engineering from the experimental data” section, and features automatically extracted from the absorbance spectra using the principal component analysis. In the latter approach, the selection of principal components was carried out using only the training set, while the calculation of amplitudes for the validation set was based on the decomposition obtained from the training set. The number of components was equal to 10 to approximately coincide with the number of features extracted manually for the DRS data. For the RGB-imaging method, we used the percentiles of image intensities obtained for individual color channels as features, extracted as described in “RGB-imaging of fingernails” section.

The input features were calculated separately for the fingernails’ and skin areas. This allowed us to compare the quality of classification of patients with low and high Hb according to optical indicators obtained for different tissue sites. To do such comparison, we trained independent models using features computed for the skin, for the fingernails, and combined descriptors computed from skin and nail-plate regions. The combination of features was carried out by simply concatenating features of a certain modality into one feature vector.

Construction of a meta-model combining RGB-imaging and diffuse reflection data

To build a meta-model that combines predictions obtained from RGB-imaging data and diffuse reflectance data, we proceeded using following strategy. We selected the best models trained using DRS and RGB-imaging data: in the case of the “DRS” model, this model was a random forest trained using diffuse reflection data from areas of the skin of the forearm, and in the case of “RGB-imaging” model, it was a logistic regression, which simultaneously uses data from the skin and fingernails areas. Using each of the classifiers, we predicted the probability of each object belonging to the low Hb group using leave-one-out cross-validation: i.e. for each object in the sample, we trained a separate model, and then for the object not participating in training, we predicted the corresponding probability. After this, we trained a simple logistic regression that took as features the probabilities predicted from both “DRS” and “RGB-imaging” models, and evaluated its classification score using standard evaluation procedure described in “Predictive models evaluation” section.

Assessing the risk of low hb in an independent cohort

Using diffuse reflectance spectra of the forearm skin collected from volunteers of the independent cohort described in “Independent cohort used to evaluate the risk of low Hb level” section, which did not participate in the training and evaluation of model quality, we predicted the risk of low Hb level as the probability of belonging to the low Hb class according to the trained model.

Statistical analysis

All data preprocessing, visualization of results, training and validation of models were carried out using custom-written Python 3.9 scripts using the Pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, Scikit-learn, Scikit-image, Seaborn libraries.

Results

Diffuse reflectance and RGB-imaging data collection

Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) and RGB-imaging were performed for the forearm and fingernails region (Fig. 1A). For the selected DRS probe configuration the sensing depth was estimated using the Monte-Carlo light transport modelling. The sampling probability (i.e. probability of the photons emitted by the source fiber and detected by the second fiber to visit a certain area) for the skin-modelling media is shown in Fig. 1B, where it can be seen that the probing depth does not exceed ~ 2 mm: 90% of all detected photons reach a depth of less than 1.6 mm. Hence, Hb in the blood vessels located in the dermis is the main contributor to the detected diffuse reflectance signal.

Figure 1C shows the spectra of the effective optical density () from both measurement sites for 240 volunteers. To investigate the predictive capabilities of DRS in assessing the risk of low Hb level, the studied cohort was divided into two groups: >120 g/L (“high Hb group”, 170 patients), and group with low Hb level < 120 g/L (“low Hb group”, 70 patients). As can be seen in Fig. 1C, for the low Hb group, the Hb absorption bands in the 520–600 nm range are less pronounced.

For each volunteer, along with DRS measurements, images of areas of the hand with well-defined nail plates were captured. For each image, regions corresponding to the nail plates and finger skin were manually segmented. An exemplary image and corresponding segmentation masks are shown in Fig. 1D.

Features engineering from the experimental data

Using the obtained data, we constructed numerical features that could be indicative of changes in the Hb level. As both oxy- and deoxyhaemoglobin exhibit high absorption in the 500–600 nm spectral range, several features related to the spectral properties of this band were calculated. As the first step each OD spectrum was preprocessed by subtracting the mean OD value in the 630–650 nm range to remove the contribution of scattering. Next, the mean OD value in the 520–590 nm range was calculated, which should be proportional to the overall contribution of oxy- and deoxyhaemoglobin to the OD spectrum. The corresponding calculation procedure is depicted in Fig. 2A. To account for the possible contribution of melanin and scattering in the 500–600 nm range we additionally subtracted a linear background from the OD spectra using OD values at 510 ± 5 nm and 610 ± 5 nm wavelength ranges and then calculated the TotalHb descriptor with respect to the performed linear correction (“TotalHblin” in Fig. 2A). This correction procedure, when assessing the optical density spectra of people grouped by Fitzpatrick (FP) skin types, showed that the amplitude of hemoglobin peaks in the range of 500–600 nm for the skin of patients with skin types from FP1 (very light skin tonality) to FP5 (brown skin) remains constant, thus eliminating differences associated with contribution of melanin (see additional considerations in Section S1, Figs. It was observed that the shape of the Hb absorption band varies between volunteers for the same tissue site. Although this indicator is not directly related to the Hb concentration in blood, we examined whether there were indeed differences in the “band shape” for the low and high Hb groups. For this, the ratios of the amplitudes of the OD bands at 560 and 575 nm (OD560/OD575) and 535 and 575 nm (OD535/OD575) were calculated. In a similar manner, the same ratios were calculated after the linear correction procedure. Figure 2B demonstrates the OD spectra of the skin forearm of different volunteers after application of the linear correction procedure along with the positions of the bands used to calculate the OD560/OD575 and OD535/OD575 descriptors. Overall, for each tissue area (skin and nail), seven predictors were calculated for each optical density spectrum. Namely, these descriptors were TotalHb, OD535/OD575, and OD560/OD575 with and without linear correction, and the OD630-650 value.

The calculated optical descriptors differed statistically significant between the high Hb and low Hb groups. Figure 2C presents distributions for the TotalHb and (OD560/OD575)lin parameters for the forearm skin and fingernail areas. Larger differences between groups were observed for the descriptors calculated for the OD spectra obtained from the forearm skin compared to the nail plates.

To numerically characterize the RGB images, segmentation of the nail plate and skin regions were used. To eliminate inhomogeneities, we used an internal area of size [0.25w;0.75w]×[0.25 h;0.75 h], where w and h are the width and height of the corresponding segmented area (dashed line in Fig. 2D) and then calculated 5, 15, 25, 50, 75, 85, 95 percentiles for the distribution of intensity in each spectral channel. To correct for possible variation in detected intensity caused by light level, we used the white area of the image (green box in Fig. 1D) and further normalized the calculated feature values to the median value of the white reference for each image. As an example, Fig. 2E shows histograms of the distribution of the 50th -percentile of the intensity in the G channel for the nail and skin segmented regions, where the shifts of distribution can be seen for the low and high Hb groups.

Predictive models training and evaluation

Using the calculated features, we built the predictive models that distinguished groups with low and high Hb level. As the first step, it was important to choose the method for validating the models and the metric used for classification performance assessment. Since the sample was relatively small (240 patients) and moderately unbalanced (70 patients in the group with Hb < 120 g/L vs. 170 patients in the group with ≥ 120 g/L), we decided to validate the model using random splits balanced by the number of observations for each group. In total, 30 different random splits were used for validating the models.

Two classification algorithms were chosen to classify patients into low and high Hb groups using the obtained non-invasive optical descriptors. As the first model, we chose logistic regression (LR) with L2-regularization and tuned only the hyperparameter of the model that corresponded to the amount of regularization introduced to the model. This type of model shows “how good” two classes are separable by a linear surface in the used feature space. As a second model, the random forest (RF) method was used. This model generally shows good classification results without additional adjustments of the model hyperparameters, accounts for the nonlinearity in the separating surface, and allows to estimate how important the interaction between features is without introducing additional predictors into the model.

The classification quality of the described algorithms was evaluated using the data obtained (i) only for the nail plate, (ii) only for the skin of the hand, and (iii) in the case of combining optical indicators from two tissue sites provided either by the DRS or RGB-imaging method. Also, we compared manually engineered features to the features extracted automatically from the OD spectra using the principal component analysis.

It was found that the best classification model based on the DRS data had a ROC-AUC = 0.84 ± 0.08 on the validation sets (Fig. 3A). Similar accuracy was obtained using the RGB-imaging data (ROC-AUC = 0.83 ± 0.08, Fig. 3B). At the same time, the classification quality differed significantly depending on the measurement site (Fig. 3C). It was observed that models trained on the DRS data from the nail plate area had the lowest accuracy of ROC-AUC = 0.68 ± 0.10 both for LR and RF models in the case of using manually engineered features, and 0.77 ± 0.08 and 0.76 ± 0.09 in the case of using PCA-based features. The classifiers trained on RGB-imaging data obtained solely from areas of the nail plate had higher accuracy (ROC-AUC = 0.78 ± 0.09 for RF and ROC-AUC = 0.79 ± 0.10 for LR). In contrast to this, classifiers trained on the DRS features extracted solely from the skin areas had higher classification scores in comparison with classifiers trained using RGB-imaging data from skin sites: both LR and RF models trained using engineered features extracted from DRS data showed ROC-AUCs of 0.80 ± 0.08 and 0.84 ± 0.08, respectively. The same models trained using the first 10 PCA-components of the skin reflection spectra showed ROC-AUCs of 0.83 ± 0.07 and 0.79 ± 0.07, while classification models trained on RGB-imaging data showed ROC-AUCs of 0.75 ± 0.10 and 0.72 ± 0.09 (LR and RF, respectively). We additionally checked for melanin interference by evaluating the accuracy of a classifier trained using DRS features from skin areas (model #6 from Table 1) in case when the model is trained on data from patients of certain skin tonalities and makes predictions for other skin tonalities. We did not observe significant differences in classification quality for different skin tones—ROC-AUC values varied from 0.7 to 0.8 (Section S1, Fig. S3).

The classification performance of models trained using the features extracted from both tissue sites was higher for both optical modalities. The LR model trained using the engineered DRS features showed the ROC-AUC = 0.76 ± 0.09, ROC-AUC for the RF model trained using engineered features was equal to 0.82 ± 0.08, while models trained using the PCA-components for RGB-imaging data showed ROC-AUCs of 0.83 ± 0.07. We also assessed the importance of manually engineered features for DRS data by calculating the Shapley values for the best model trained using the DRS features obtained from the skin forearm region. These values allow one to estimate the response of the model with and without the selected feature, using considerations from cooperative game theory39. Briefly, for each point in the dataset and for each feature one can calculate the Shapley value. If the selected feature is not significant for the model output, then the Shapley value will be zero, otherwise, if the Shapley value is positive, the model gives preference towards the positive class (in our case anaemic class) with the selected feature for that particular datapoint; if the value of the Shapley parameter is negative, it gives preference towards the negative class. As expected, TotalHb and TotalHblin descriptors were among the most significant predictors – high values of TotalHb and TotalHblin pushed model to give negative predictions (i.e. predict level of Hb ≥ 120 g/L), while low values were considered by the model as strong predictors for the anaemic class (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, the feature responsible for the ratio of absorption peaks of haemoglobin (OD535/OD575)lin also demonstrated high importance, while the average OD values in the 630–650 nm range were less significant.

Although the “synergistic effect” of using multiple tissue sites for one method was not significant, we observed that the simultaneous use of two optical modalities could improve the overall classification performance. To verify this hypothesis we used the probabilities obtained by the best classifiers trained using DRS and RGB-imaging data to build a “meta-classifier” that combines the probabilities of the two predictions into a final class (a logistic regression was used as a meta-classifier). Using this approach, we were able to achieve a classification ROC-AUC of 0.86 ± 0.07 (Fig. 3E), i.e. higher as compared to classifiers using the data from only one optical modality.

Additionally, we tested the developed approach in the “prospective study” manner, i.e., on a completely new data that was not used for training and testing of the model. Since sampling of venous blood is disturbing for the volunteers, we verified the performance of the model indirectly. To do this, we collected diffuse reflectance data from areas of the inner forearm for volunteers of different ages and sex (274 patients not involved in model training and validation) and predicted the “risk” of low haemoglobin using a DRS model trained using manually engineered features, where the risk was assessed as the probability of the patient belonging to the low Hb group Diffuse reflectance spectra for the additional sample are presented in Fig. 4A. We expected a higher risk of Hb level decrease for female volunteers, as blood Hb level is generally lower for them. Indeed, we observed that the effective OD in the Hb absorption range (500–600 nm) was generally lower for females (Fig. 4B), while model predicted a higher risk of anaemia (Fig. 4C).

(A, B) Spectra of the effective optical density of 284 volunteers not included into training and testing of the prediction model coloured with estimated low Hb risk for each spectra (A) and by volunteer’s sex (B). (C) Estimated risk of low Hb level for volunteers of different sex.

Combined model quality evaluation

The performance quality observed for the combined model (ROC-AUC = 0.86 ± 0.07) lied within the error limits of the metrics observed for “single-mode” models. This fact motivated us to conduct a more detailed study to evaluate whether combining DRS and RGB-imaging methods really helps and how large the effect of combining predictions is.

First, we compared whether the quality of the “meta-” model actually differs compared to models using only one source of data. Since the model quality metrics were quite noisy—in particular, depended on the procedure of splitting data into training and test samples—we used the following procedure to verify the effect. We trained the best models for DRS (ROC-AUC = 0.84, #6 in Table 1 of the manuscript), RGB-imaging (ROC-AUC = 0.83, #17 in Table 1) and a combined model on 30 train-test-splits with the same splits shared for all models, and then calculated the quality metric value at each split (Fig. S4A). Using identical splitting partitions allowed us to compare pairwise metric values on each split and apply a t-test for related samples to compare metrics for different models. With this approach, we found that the combined model actually predicted better than both the model using diffuse reflectance data (p < 10− 3) and photo data (p < 10− 4) for most of the splits (Fig. S4B). If the combination of two methods did not provide an increase in accuracy, such an effect would not be observed.

As the second part, we aimed to estimate the maximum increase one can expect upon combining predictions of two models with known ROC-AUC ~ 0.83–0.84. To do this, we used a simple Monte-Carlo procedure to simulate a combination of two classifiers that independently predict a positive or negative class label with given values of ROC-AUC = 0.83. To model a classifier with a given ROC-AUC, we simulated a sample of 100 objects of the positive and negative classes, having a normally distributed synthetic feature with a mean of µ = +1 for the positive class, and µ = -1 for the negative class, and standard deviation σ varied in 0.1–100 range. The values of the synthesized feature were used to estimate ROC-AUC of the simulated “classifier”, while the width of the distribution σ was used to control the ROC-AUC values. Indeed, for large σ values, a low ROC-AUC is expected, as distributions for negative and positive classes significantly overlap, while for small σ values, a high ROC-AUC is expected. Figure 5A, B show examples of distributions of simulated samples with different σ parameters and the estimated ROC-AUC values for the given sample. Indeed, as the distribution width σ increased, we observed a monotonic decrease in the ROC-AUC metric for the simulated classifier (Fig. 5C).

(A, B) Examples of distributions of the synthetic feature used to simulate independent classifiers with different values of the distribution widths. At the top are the ROC-AUC values when classifying data using the feature values. (C) Dependence of the ROC-AUC on the feature’s distribution width. (D) Dependence of the ROC-AUC of a combination of two independent models on the ROC-AUC of one model. The red dotted line indicates the ROC-AUC of a single-mode model, the blue dot indicates the values obtained experimentally by combining two methods: DRS and RGB-imaging.

Using the described procedure one can obtain a simulation of the classifier with necessary ROC-AUC values and independent predictions—we used this fact to estimate the accuracy of a combined classifier. To build a combined model we employed exactly the same procedure used to build a meta-model which combined diffuse reflectance and imaging data, but used predictions of the simulated independent classifiers instead. In this way, we could obtain an estimate of what ROC-AUC can be expected in the best case of combination of two completely independent models. Figure 5D shows the dependence of the ROC-AUC values of combined models on the ROC-AUC of single-mode model. In the case of combination of two completely independent classifiers with ROC-AUC = 0.83, the expected ROC-AUC of the combined model should be 0.915, which is significantly higher than the value we obtained experimentally (ROC-AUC = 0.86).

Discussion

In this work, we compared the performance of two optical techniques, DRS and RGB-imaging, in non-invasive assessment of the anaemia risk by comparing performance of models trained to classify human subjects with low (Hb < 120 g/L) and high (Hb ≥ 120 g/L) blood Hb levels based on the optical descriptors. To detect DRS, we used a dual-fiber probe that collected signal from two tissue sites – the inner side of the forearm and the nail plate. In the case of RGB-imaging, we recorded images of areas of the hand with visible nail plates and skin of the fingers (Fig. 1).

The diffuse reflectance spectrum is formed by two physical processes: scattering by tissue inhomogeneities and absorption of light by molecular chromophores. While the spectrum of the scattering coefficient decreases gradually with wavelength in the visible and near-infrared range, the absorption spectra of molecular chromophores (with the exception of melanin) exhibit distinct absorption bands. Haemoglobin, with its high absorption coefficient in the visible and near-infrared range, together with its high concentration in the skin, is the dominant chromophore. Thus, (1) in tissue areas where only Hb is present as a chromophore in the visible range, and melanin, the other main visible-range chromophore, is absent, and (2) assuming that there is little variation in tissue scattering parameters and tissue vascularization between different patients it can be expected that changes of diffuse reflectance of tissues will be associated with changes in the average concentration of Hb in the blood. Such a “melanin-free” area of tissue, in particular, is the nail plate, so one can directly correlate changes in the intensity of diffusely reflected light from this area with the level of Hb. Similarly, following the logic for melanin-free areas of tissue, one can also assume that in areas where the concentration of melanin is not high enough, for example, on the inner side of the forearm, one can also expect that diffuse reflection will carry information about the hemoglobin content in examined tissue region and, therefore, the hemoglobin content in the blood.

For DRS spectra collected from the skin of the inner side of the forearm and nail plates we manually engineered features suitable for the Hb level assessment, by extracting features, which described the shape of the Hb absorption Q-band, observed in DRS, while for the images of the fingernails percentiles of the intensities inside these segmented regions were calculated for each colour channel as numerical descriptors (Fig. 2). Using the manually constructed features, as well as the features, extracted automatically from DRS data using PCA, classification models were developed.

We found that algorithms using DRS and imaging data exhibited, on the average, the same classification performances, yet, the performance metric for data of different optical modalities was significantly dependent on the tissue site (Table 1). The DRS-based models had good classification quality when trained using data measured from the forearm skin (best ROC-AUC = 0.84 ± 0.08), while their classification quality on the nail plate areas dropped significantly (down to ROC-AUC = 0.68 ± 0.10). These differences in classification scores may be due to the quality of the data measured by the fiber probe: the skin surface of the inner side of the forearm is generally homogeneous, while the surface of the nail plate is heterogeneous. We also observed that the colour characteristics of the nail plates depended more sensitively on the pressure applied with the probe.

In contrast, RGB-imaging-based models demonstrated higher classification performances when trained on the data from the nail plates (ROC-AUC = 0.79 ± 0.09) in comparison to models trained on the imaging data from the finger skin regions (ROC-AUC = 0.75 ± 0.09). These can be explained by the fact that the color characteristics of the outer side of the skin can be largely influenced by the melanin in the patient’s skin, which is not present in the tissues of the nail plate.

At the same time, combining classifiers of two optical modalities gives a statistically significant increase in ROC-AUC value up to 0.86 ± 0.07, but does not allow classifying patients with a level of accuracy expected upon combination of two truly independent classifiers (Fig. 5). Most likely, the observed fact suggests that the predictions of the models for two modalities—DRS and RGB imaging—are not completely independent. Indeed, in essence, these models use the diffuse reflectance signal in the visible range for predictions. We assume that this fact could potentially limit accuracy of optical methods in the non-invasive optical assessment of haemoglobin in a patient’s blood. Presumably, this limitation may be caused by the differences in the morphological structure of the examined tissue sites. The application of both DRS and RGB-imaging is implicitly based on the assumption that the changes in the optical response from skin or fingernail tissues are proportional to the haemoglobin concentration in the patient’s blood. In fact, this response is not only associated with the blood Hb concentration, but also with the volume concentration of blood in the tissues. As tissues may contain different number of vessels per volume, the optically estimated Hb concentration may be proportional to the volumetric density of vessels in the probing region. This morphological variability could be significant: for example, natural “biological” variability in the skin capillaries density (number capillaries per unit area) varies by 10–20% in normal skin of an adult person and also may vary depending on various systemic diseases40. Given the presence of such variability, it can be expected that methods that estimate the haemoglobin concentration in a patient’s blood over a large area of tissue, without taking into account the density of capillaries and vessels, will not be able to reduce the error to less than 10%.

This limitation, however, can be overcome either directly using optical methods and models that take into account the structural features of tissues, or by using calibration procedure for the simple methods. The former approach possibly should involve the microscopic investigation of melanin-free tissue sites, which may be infeasible in real-world applications, while the latter case indeed might be easy-to-implement and applicable. For example, in the case of using optical methods to continuously monitor Hb levels on a single patient over time, the optical response data can be calibrated once using invasive blood sampling for a specific patient. Thus, the accuracy of optical methods can potentially be compared with the accuracy of invasive devices.

Conclusion

In this study we have compared the performance of two optical methods – diffuse reflectance spectroscopy and RGB-imaging – in the task of classifying patients into groups with low (< 120 g/L) and high Hb (≥ 120 g/L). We notice that, on the average, classifiers built on the diffuse reflectance spectra of forearm skin and fingernails were comparable in classification performance to classifiers built using RGB-images of fingernails and finger skin area, yet the classification performance of different models proved to be highly dependable on the combination of tissue site and optical method used. The classification ROC-AUC scores varied in the 0.8–0.85 range for the best models, while by combining methods, the ROC-AUC increased up to 0.86. Using the model trained on DRS data we have also assessed the “risk” of having low haemoglobin in an independent sample of patients, where, as expected, we could observe that the risk of low haemoglobin in female patients showed a statistically significant increase. In our opinion, although the classification accuracy may not be sufficient for direct implementation of this technique in the clinic “as is”, in particular due to the fundamental limitations associated with the variability of tissue morphology, optical methods for non-invasive determination of haemoglobin still can find their niche in the point of care diagnostics or in tasks of continuous monitoring of haemoglobin with additional calibration.
