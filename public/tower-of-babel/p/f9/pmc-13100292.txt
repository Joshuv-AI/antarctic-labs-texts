Promoting physical activity engagement in rural pregnant persons in the United States: a qualitative study
Authors: Kristine Zimmermann, Allissa Desloge, Lisa Sophia Haen, Emily Orr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRural-dwelling pregnant persons in the United States experience disproportionate morbidity and mortality relative to urban. While systems-level solutions are essential, behaviors like physical activity can improve outcomes during pregnancy and beyond. Despite recommendations, however, most pregnant individuals are insufficiently active. We aimed to understand factors affecting physical activity behavior during pregnancy among rural individuals and identify intervention recommendations.MethodsBetween February 2022 and March 2023, we conducted semi-structured interviews, guided by health behavior theories, with 16 insufficiently active pregnant and early postpartum women living in the rural midwestern United States. Through inductive coding, we revealed data categories that aligned with the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behavior (COM-B) model. We subsequently adopted a deductive analytic approach, using the COM-B to aid in generating themes and intervention recommendations.ResultsWe identified three themes that describe interconnected factors linked to physical activity motivation and behavior during pregnancy: (1) physical and psychological factors including pregnancy stage and symptoms; (2) opportunities in the physical environmental, including work and home; and (3) social support and gender norms and roles.ConclusionsInterventions to promote physical activity engagement in rural pregnant persons must accommodate stage of pregnancy, provide opportunities for social support, and align with local resources.Keywords: Pregnant people, Rural health, Physical activity, Health behavior, Health promotion, Qualitative research

Introduction

Globally, adverse maternal outcomes are a persistent challenge; across geographic and economic contexts, rural living is a social determinant associated with maternal mortality (Souza et al., 2024; Ward et al., 2024). In the United States, rural perinatal persons are almost twice as likely to experience intensive care unit admission or death compared to urban counterparts (Hansen and Moloney, 2020; Harrington et al., 2023). Rural community and systems-level factors (Hansen and Moloney, 2020; Ward et al., 2024) are driving disparities in maternal morbidity and mortality and are often associated with chronic diseases and their risk factors (Singh and Lee, 2020). Nevertheless, behaviors like physical activity can reduce risks for pregnancy-associated chronic conditions, including gestational diabetes and pre-eclampsia (Dipietro et al., 2019), thus shaping chronic disease risk during pregnancy and beyond (Song et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2020).

Physical activity encompasses intentional, leisure-time activity, as well as lifestyle activity, including physically demanding work, playing with children, and household activities (e.g., cleaning, lawn mowing). Maintaining physical activity levels and preferences during pregnancy is generally recommended, and previously inactive individuals are encouraged to begin activity; at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity is recommended in pregnancy (World Health Organization, 2020). Despite recommendations, globally, most pregnant women do not meet these guidelines (Silva-Jose et al., 2022).

In the United States, rural-dwelling adults across age groups are less likely than urban to meet physical activity recommendations (Whitfield et al., 2019). Barriers for rural pregnant persons include limited access to sidewalks, parks, or gyms, and lack of awareness of the benefits and importance of physical activity in pregnancy (Melton et al., 2013; Tinius et al., 2020). Additionally, rural pregnant women face challenges in engaging in physical activity interventions; previous interventions have had low attendance and may warrant increased emphasis on physical activity behavior (Melton et al., 2016; Thomson et al., 2016).

This study seeks to address these shortcomings and fill knowledge gaps regarding physical activity promotion for rural pregnant individuals, to inform the development of a community-based, prenatal intervention aimed at improving rural women's health across the life course. The goals of this qualitative study are to explore factors affecting physical activity engagement during pregnancy among rural Illinois residents and identify intervention strategies to promote physical activity engagement.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted semi-structured interviews with pregnant and early postpartum persons living in rural Illinois communities to elicit formative data to inform intervention development. We recruited interviewees through a survey on pregnancy health behaviors and perspectives for rural pregnant individuals. Our qualitative study used a phenomenological approach informed by a social constructivist paradigm, which assumes individuals' perspectives develop from lived experiences and their social and physical environments (Mason, 2018). Thus, a physical activity intervention for rural-dwelling pregnant individuals in Illinois must be tailored for the priority population. Our study was approved by the University of Illinois College of Medicine Rockford Institutional Review Board.

The study team comprised urban-dwelling women with training and experience in public health research. The lead researcher's (KZ) training and experience includes qualitative and quantitative methods, health behavior theory, and developing, implementing, and evaluating behavioral interventions in rural communities. Two doctoral students (AD, LSH) with training and experience in maternal health and qualitative methods supported the research. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, study activities, including recruitment and data collection, were primarily implemented using digital technologies (e.g., internet, email, phone, Zoom) to protect participants' and their families' health.

Recruitment and data collection

Between February 2022 and March 2023, we conducted our survey using convenience sampling with rural-identifying pregnant individuals living in Illinois. Recruitment activities occurred throughout Illinois, where 11.5% of Illinois' population and 92.9% of the land area were considered rural (US Census Bureau, 2026). Survey recruitment involved community outreach with clients of clinical and community-based organizations and local health departments, direct engagement with pregnant individuals, and flyers in community locations and on social media. Survey participants were English-speaking, aged 18 or older, currently pregnant, and self-identified as living in a rural Illinois community. Through the survey, we recruited volunteers to participate in a follow-up interview. Eligible participants had less than recommended physical activity levels for pregnant individuals (i.e., 150 minutes per week) based on based on “exercise vital sign” questions (Coleman et al., 2012; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2018); were in their 2nd or 3rd trimester of pregnancy; had no contraindications restricting physical activity engagement; and lived in a rural Illinois county as defined by the Rural Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Economic Research Service, 2025) or the 2010 United States Census (U.S. Census Bureau, 2015). (Eligibility criteria are further described in Table 1).

Beginning in January 2023, to expand our reach and meet our goal of 16 participants, we began recruiting interview participants who were less than 6 months postpartum. We contacted interested individuals by email or phone to assess eligibility and schedule interviews.

We collected demographic information and self-reported physical activity through the initial survey. For postpartum participants, we collected demographic information during the interview.

We developed a semi-structured interview guide using concepts from social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1998) and a socioecological framework (SEM) (McLeroy et al., 1988) to elicit in-depth responses about health behaviors before and during pregnancy (see Supplementary Material). The interview guide focused on cognitive, behavioral, interpersonal, and environmental factors related to engaging in health behaviors, including leisure-time and lifestyle physical activity, and preferences regarding intervention format, content, and delivery.

We conducted interviews (n = 16) between May 2022 and March 2023 via phone and Zoom (Version 5), depending on participant preference. Participants provided verbal informed consent before the interview began. Interviews were audio recorded, professionally transcribed, and reviewed by a member of the study team to correct transcription errors and remove identifying information. Interview participants received a $50 gift card. Mean interview length was 44.6 minutes (range: 23.2–80.3 minutes).

Analysis plan

We (KZ, AD, LSH) initially used an inductive approach in our analysis and subsequently adopted a deductive approach after identifying an organizing framework (Ravitch and Carl, 2019). Specifically, we first independently reviewed transcripts and created memos, which included commonly discussed topics (e.g., physical activity attitudes, barriers, facilitators) and analysts' interpretive notes. Using the memos, we created topical categories for data organization and discovered our categories aligned with the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behavior (COM-B) Model and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) (Cane et al., 2012; Michie et al., 2011). Mitchie et al. (2011) describe “capability” as one's psychological and physical ability to participate in a behavior of interest; “opportunity” refers to external factors that facilitate engagement in the behavior of interest; and “motivation” refers to one's conscious and unconscious cognitive processes surrounding engagement in the behavior of interest. As COM-B/TDF is designed to support intervention development, we chose it as a framework for our codebook along with constructs from SEM and an intersectional perspective to recognize the diversity of those living in rural communities (Cane et al., 2012; Etherington et al., 2020; McLeroy et al., 1988; Michie et al., 2011). Through an iterative process involving transcript reviews by all analysts, we modified codes and definitions until we agreed on our final codebook, which included four categories (i.e., Intersectionality/Personal Characteristics; Perinatal & Pre-perinatal Health; Barriers, Facilitators, Risks & Benefits; Intervention Input), with 14 “parent” codes, and 33 total codes.

Using the final codebook, two analysts coded each transcript; discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the researchers. After coding, we used the COM-B as our lens to identify themes and subthemes by reviewing excerpts from interviews based on codes and code combinations and mapping excerpts to COM-B domains (Michie et al., 2011). Through this process, we identified patterns within and across COM-B domains, which led to the development of themes. Additionally, to elucidate potential intervention strategies, we identified interviewees' feedback regarding a hypothetical program to encourage physical activity in pregnancy, and we extrapolated intervention recommendations using identified themes.

We used multiple strategies to support trustworthiness (Ravitch and Carl, 2019). To facilitate credibility, interviewers documented reflections after each interview, including the interview process; reactions, perspectives, and biases that may have influenced the discussion or interviewer's interpretations; discussion topics; and considerations for future interviews and analysis. To support transferability, we sought to capture the experience of being pregnant while living in rural Illinois by interviewing individuals who met two criteria for rurality—self-identification as rural-dwelling and living in rural communities as defined by objective criteria (RUCA and the United States Census) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2015; U.S. Department of Agriculture, Economic Research Service, 2025). We recognized the heterogeneity of rural Illinois contexts and experiences by comparing and contrasting participant experiences throughout our data collection and analysis, aiming for conceptual sufficiency rather than thematic saturation (Tight, 2024). To support confirmability, we held weekly discussions to share our interpretations and perspectives, which helped us address biases in our understanding of interview content and minimize presuppositions related to findings. Finally, to support dependability, we kept an audit trail of our methodological processes, ideas, and decisions. We used Dedoose software (Version 10) to facilitate analysis.

Theme 1: Physical, mental, and emotional well-being

Subthemes related to participants' physical, mental, and emotional factors that influenced their ability to be active included beliefs about risks and benefits of physical activity, pre-pregnancy physical condition, and the ways in which participants navigated physical and mental wellness.

Several participants expressed motivation to engage in physical activity before and during pregnancy due to their perceptions of its physiological benefits. For example, prior to pregnancy, participants often described trying to be active to lose weight. Participants also discussed engaging in physical activity while trying to get pregnant, sharing that improving health could support fertility and be beneficial for them and their fetus. Perceived benefits that served as motivators during pregnancy included supporting labor and delivery and managing comorbid conditions like gestational diabetes. In contrast, for many interviewees, safety was a primary concern that decreased motivation. Interviewees discussed practicing caution during strenuous activities to ensure their unborn child's well-being. Prior experience with pregnancy loss or bleeding during pregnancy was particularly influential in deterring physical activity.

While participants expressed that physical activity in pregnancy was safe, beneficial, and carried few risks for those who were active prior to pregnancy, some suggested that having a physical activity routine before their current pregnancy helped to facilitate ongoing activity. For others, perceptions about their pre-pregnancy physical condition affected their physical activity self-efficacy and motivation during pregnancy. For example, some described their lack of conditioning, which deterred physical activity engagement due to its difficulty. Some also connected their lack of conditioning with self-image and self-esteem, such that negative self-perceptions appeared to adversely affect capability and motivation.

Many participants discussed how pregnancy symptoms like pain, nausea, and lack of energy influenced their motivation to be active. For some, pregnancy symptoms made physical activity especially difficult, such that low capability appeared to coexist with low motivation. In contrast, others observed that activity helped improve mood and energy levels or distracted them from their symptoms. Thus, the expectation that physical activity could improve physical and emotional symptoms was a motivator. Participants also noted that bodily changes and pregnancy symptoms led to variations in activity levels. Relief from symptoms after the first trimester often contributed to improved capability, leading to increased physical activity motivation and reported engagement. However, later in pregnancy, participants described limitations due to body size. To mitigate challenges, some participants described strategies such as pacing themselves by taking breaks during periods of activity.

Intervention recommendations

To support capability, most participants desired instructions on specific exercises that are beneficial and safe during pregnancy and that accommodate various abilities and pregnancy stages. They also requested different exercise routines to avoid monotony. To support motivation and accountability, many participants endorsed using cell phone applications or wearable technologies to track their activity. Those with experience in wearable technology viewed them favorably and welcomed their inclusion.

Theme 2: Physical environment

Subthemes related to features in the physical environment that influenced physical activity opportunities, specifically natural resources and the built environment, home environments, and work environments. These features were physical activity motivators as well as deterrents.

Participants described multiple environmental factors that shaped their physical activity experiences. In the natural environment, some described living in secluded communities that supported physical activity engagement for those who preferred to exercise alone. Others discussed living near resources like parks and walking trails, which facilitated physical activity engagement. However, seasonal conditions influenced opportunities, as cold winters and hot summers made it difficult for some to maintain a consistent routine. Participants indicated that when available, local infrastructure, including fitness centers and sidewalks, was helpful for supporting physical activity; however, access to resources varied. For those seeking structured options, gyms and exercise classes were often unavailable locally, requiring travel outside one's community to participate. When fitness centers were available, cost was also a barrier. Additionally, some participants felt uncomfortable engaging in physical activity in their neighborhoods due to a lack of sidewalks and safety concerns. To mitigate environmental barriers, some participants described using alternative indoor spaces, such as shopping centers, to stay active.

While opportunities to engage in physical activity at home were an option for some participants, most shared that limited space and lack of equipment inhibited their opportunities and motivation. Additionally, some perceived technology as a way to access online workout videos and virtual classes at home; however, others experienced poor home internet and cell phone connectivity.

Many participants reflected on how their work environments influenced opportunities and motivation to engage in activity. Some described jobs that involved movement, which facilitated physical activity. While rare, a few participants described workplace cultures that supported intentional physical activity opportunities, such as group walks, which contributed to motivation via social support and accountability. In contrast, several participants, including those with sedentary jobs or unpredictable work schedules, shared challenges to being active at work. Finally, some shared that the mental or physical toll of work diminished their motivation outside of work hours.

Intervention recommendations

Participants' intervention recommendations regarding the physical environment primarily focused on concerns about the feasibility of participating in an in-person or digital intervention. Most saw value in gathering in person due to their desire to connect with others, and faced challenges to doing physical activity at home due to space restrictions and distractions. Some, however, saw value in a virtual intervention because of limited time for travel and attending classes. Others opposed a virtual intervention due to limited internet and cellular service. In addition to intervention format, many participants expressed interest in learning about local community resources supporting wellness during pregnancy.

Theme 3: Social environment

Subthemes related to the social environment included social support and perceived gender roles and norms also appeared to be important for shaping physical activity engagement.

An active social circle often promoted participants' motivation to engage in physical activity. Some noted that shared activity involving accountability and encouragement from family, friends, or co-workers increased their motivation. However, many expressed wanting greater support around physical activity engagement—particularly other pregnant individuals—to walk or exercise with. Finally, several participants discussed the importance of professional recommendations, emphasizing that guidance from their obstetrics providers to increase activity would be particularly motivating during pregnancy.

Some participants described how family, work, and housework responsibilities, often shaped by traditional gender roles, affected physical activity opportunity by leaving little time or energy for physical activity. Particularly for those without external social support, the demands of childcare and household chores posed barriers to prioritizing and engaging in physical activity, diminishing motivation and opportunity. However, many participants highlighted chores and childcare as practical ways to incorporate movement into daily routines. Activities such as walking, playing with their other children, cleaning, and laundry were often perceived as sufficient to meet movement goals, reducing the motivation to seek out structured exercise. Additionally, for some, knowledge of the benefits of physical activity for their children's health and the desire to model positive behaviors emerged as strong motivators to be active during and after pregnancy.

Intervention recommendations

Most participants expressed support for a group intervention and identified the opportunity to connect with other pregnant people as a motivation to participate. Some participants suggested involvement of friends and family in an intervention, particularly their other children, so that childcare would not be necessary for participation. Participants also emphasized the importance of feeling safe and comfortable with the group and intervention facilitators.

Discussion

Pregnancy is a dynamic period involving physical, social/emotional, and cognitive changes. We applied the COM-B model (Michie et al., 2011), situated within a rural context, to better understand how individuals navigate physical activity engagement while pregnant, highlighting the interconnectedness of capability, opportunity, motivation, and behavior. Our findings suggest physical activity capability during pregnancy can drive motivation to be active, and being active can reinforce beliefs about capability. Similarly, while physical activity resources are often limited in rural communities, opportunities to engage in physical activity can drive motivation, and being motivated to be active can facilitate seeking out opportunities. These findings are exemplified in participants who observed relief from early-pregnancy symptoms after being active—activity reinforced motivation via beliefs about capability and opportunity.

Our findings around factors influencing physical activity engagement among rural-dwelling pregnant persons, such as social support, pregnancy symptoms, and access to resources, are consistent with prior research (Tinius et al., 2020) and suggest a need for personalized, multi-strategy approaches, tailored to the physical, social, and gender contexts that influence activity opportunities. One strength of our study was the elicitation of intervention recommendations from rural women with lived experience. Although our research focused on one state in the midwestern United States, participants' diverse perspectives emphasize the heterogeneity of rural contexts (Bain and Adeagbo, 2022). Several participants endorsed an in-person intervention while simultaneously acknowledging barriers to participating, including limited time and the need to travel.

Our findings also align with prior research on how rural environments can influence physical activity engagement during pregnancy (Andreae et al., 2024), suggesting interventions must consider community-specific factors like geography, weather, availability of physical activity resources, social support opportunities, and technology infrastructure. While participants desired interventions to connect with other pregnant individuals, strategies incorporating community health workers or doulas may be more feasible for providing individualized education, motivation, and social support (Thomson et al., 2016) in locations convenient to participants. Home-based interventions have been implemented with rural older adults and individuals with chronic diseases (e.g., (Salisbury et al., 2023), but examples involving rural pregnant individuals are sparse (Thomson et al., 2016).

Our findings are fairly consistent with the few studies conducted globally that used the COM-B model to understand physical activity behaviors during pregnancy (Flannery et al., 2018; Marini et al., 2023; McKeough et al., 2022), with one notable difference. McKeough et al. (2022) identified family and friends' as important in encouraging or discouraging physical activity. In our study, most participants reported not receiving or not listening to family and friends' advice, and desired social connections with other pregnant individuals for encouragement. These findings suggest increasing social support opportunities for rural, pregnant individuals can promote healthy behaviors. Participants also noted that healthcare providers play an important role in promoting physical activity during prenatal visits.

Our findings also emphasize the dynamic nature of pregnancy, contributing to variability in physical activity engagement (Huberty et al., 2016). Research is needed—in rural and urban populations—to understand how to facilitate physical activity capability throughout pregnancy, including accommodating different abilities and fluctuations in pregnancy symptoms.

Our study has several limitations. First, interview participants self-reported physical activity, which was not verified using objective measures. Many discussed activity achieved through chores and engagement with other children, and may have underestimated their actual physical activity levels, particularly lifestyle activity. Additionally, our study was conducted during the COVID pandemic, which may have influenced perspectives about in-person physical activity opportunities. Furthermore, due to the heterogeneity of rural communities, participant perspectives may not represent the diverse viewpoints of pregnant individuals in rural Illinois and beyond. For example, our interviews were conducted in English and excluded Illinois' rural Latiné communities. Additionally, we likely interviewed individuals with higher levels of education compared to rural Illinois residents overall. Due to this heterogeneity and the size of our qualitative sample, we were not comfortable making specific intervention recommendations based on individual characteristics and propose that quantitative studies could generate these findings. Despite these limitations, our study makes a unique contribution to the literature on the use of the COM-B model to better understand how to increase physical activity engagement during pregnancy. Globally, multiple studies have utilized the COM-B model to better understand this relationship (Flannery et al., 2018; Marini et al., 2023); this study adds to the evidence base on perspectives from a rural setting in the United States.

Conclusion

In rural individuals, multiple factors influence physical activity engagement during pregnancy, including physical ability, local resources and social support, and gender norms and expectations—all of which can influence motivation to be active. These factors can fluctuate throughout pregnancy and vary across rural individuals and settings. Interventions to increase physical activity for rural individuals during pregnancy should reflect rural context and consider pregnant individuals' capability, opportunity, and motivation to be active.
