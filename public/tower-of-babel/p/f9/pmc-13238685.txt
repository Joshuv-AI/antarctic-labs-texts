Prevalence of diagnostic Mendelian kidney disease variants in type 2 diabetes with and without diabetic kidney disease
Authors: Yosuke Hirakawa, Yuka Sugawara, Masaomi Nangaku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a serious complication of diabetes mellitus. Since genetic factors contribute to the development of DKD, numerous studies have examined the genetic basis of DKD. Among these, the diagnostic variants of Mendelian kidney disease have recently attracted attention. However, previous studies have only examined patients with DKD without appropriate controls. In this study, we examined the frequency of diagnostic variants associated with Mendelian kidney disease in patients with type 2 diabetes and compared their prevalence between those with and without DKD. We found that approximately 8% of patients with type 2 diabetes had diagnostic variants; however, the frequency did not differ significantly between patients with and without DKD. This highlights the need to establish an appropriate control group for examining the effects of genetic variants.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes, diabetic kidney disease, diagnostic variant

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a major complication of diabetes that deteriorates prognosis and quality of life 1 , 2 . It is also widely recognized as a multifactorial condition 3 . Because diabetes itself is multifactorial, it is essential to compare patients with DKD to those with diabetes without DKD to identify the factors that contribute to DKD development. Many genome‐wide association studies have examined the effects of common variants on DKD 4 , 5 . Additionally, rare variants are important contributors to kidney disease. Several studies have examined the frequency of diagnostic variants associated with Mendelian kidney disorders in patients with DKD 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 . However, these studies reported variant frequencies only in patients with DKD, not clarifying the prevalence of such variants in patients with diabetes without DKD. To address this gap, we analyzed whole‐genome sequencing data from patients with type 2 diabetes, both with and without DKD, to determine whether the frequency of diagnostic kidney‐disease variants differed between the two groups.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tokyo Graduate School of Medicine (2024377G) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional guidelines. As this study used only de‐identified data, the need for individual informed consent was waived.

Extraction of diagnostic variants

In this study, we analyzed the genome data stored on the Japan Agency for Medical Research and Development (AMED) Data Utilization Platform. Using this platform, researchers were able to access the genomic data of 28,798 participants from large‐scale Japanese biobanks. To protect individual privacy, the platform prohibited the reporting of data comprising fewer than 10 participants in the analyzed dataset. As for clinical data, only age, sex, and disease name were available. Therefore, the definitions of type 2 diabetes and DKD were based only on the disease name.

To confirm the genetic ancestry of the study participants, individual genotypes were projected onto the principal component space of the 1,000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel. Ancestry‐informative SNPs were selected based on the pairwise fixation index values among superpopulations or between Japanese and non‐Japanese individuals within the East Asian superpopulation. Principal component analysis (PCA) and projections were performed using PLINK v2.0 by applying allele weights, allele frequencies, and eigenvalues derived from the reference dataset.

From the genomic data, we extracted pathogenic variants from 790 genes associated with Mendelian kidney disease, as previously reported 9 (Table S1). Pathogenic variants were defined as those annotated as “Pathogenic” or “Likely pathogenic” in ClinVar (accessed on October 6, 2025). As part of quality control, we excluded variants with a genotype quality (GQ) below 30 or depth (DP) below 20. Each pathogenic variant was annotated by its gene name in the ClinVar database. The mode of inheritance of each gene was obtained from OMIM (accessed November 22, 2025). If multiple diseases were associated with the same autosomal gene, the mode of inheritance was classified as follows. When at least one associated disease showed autosomal dominant inheritance, the gene was classified as autosomal dominant; otherwise, it was classified as autosomal recessive. Diagnostic variants were defined as (i) variants in genes classified as autosomal dominant or X‐linked, or (ii) variants in genes classified as autosomal recessive when the alternative allele was present in a homozygous state. Multi‐allelic variants were decomposed into individual biallelic records to ensure that all candidate alleles were evaluated during filtering.

Statistical analyses

Continuous data are described as medians [interquartile ranges], and categorical data are described as numbers (percentages). The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the binary data were estimated using the Clopper‐Pearson method. Continuous data were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, and differences in proportions between the two groups were evaluated using the chi‐square analysis or Fisher's exact test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All statistical tests were performed using the R software (4.3.3).

RESULTS

We first checked the ancestry of the study participants with type 2 diabetes and found that all were East Asian, with almost all categorized as Japanese (Figure S1). Of the 2,177 participants, 753 had DKD. Only age and sex were available as background factors on the AMED Data Utilization Platform, and there were no clear differences in these characteristics between the two groups (Table 1). The proportion of participants carrying at least one diagnostic variant was similar between the two groups (111 [7.8%] without DKD and 65 [8.6%] with DKD; P = 0.55). Since conventional risk factors for DKD, such as diabetes duration, glycemic control indicators, blood pressure, albuminuria, and eGFR, were not available, adjustments for these key features of DKD were not performed. Among the participants with DKD, age and sex did not differ when stratified by the presence of diagnostic variants (Table S2). Taken together, the diagnostic variants of kidney disease were not more frequent in participants with DKD than in those without DKD, and no clinical characteristics, such as younger age, appeared to predict the development of DKD.

Subsequently, we narrowed our analysis to several well‐known Mendelian kidney diseases. Because we were only allowed to report information on diseases in which causative variants were detected in more than 10 participants, we focused specifically on diseases with relatively frequent variants in the study population. In total, 18, 16, and 18 participants harbored variants associated with autosomal dominant Alport syndrome (COL4A3 and COL4A4), cystinuria (SLC3A1 and SLC7A9), and lipoprotein glomerulopathy (APOE), respectively. Even when the analysis was restricted to these categories, the difference in the proportion of individuals carrying the variants between the DKD and non‐DKD groups was not statistically significant (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we analyzed the frequency of the diagnostic variants in Mendelian kidney diseases. The number of participants with DKD (N = 753) was the largest among studies investigating the diagnostic variants of Mendelian kidney disease. This study is novel because it included participants with type 2 diabetes without DKD as the control group. We found that diagnostic variants were present in approximately 8% of participants with type 2 diabetes, regardless of their DKD status. Even when focusing on several Mendelian kidney diseases, we did not detect a difference in the frequency between participants with and without DKD. Although this study included over 2,000 patients in total and 753 patients with DKD, incorporating even more cases could potentially make the difference—8.6% with DKD and 7.8% without DKD—statistically significant.

In this study, the prevalence of diagnostic variants in patients with DKD was 8.6%. This frequency was similar to that reported by Dahl (8.7% in 206 DKD patients) 8 and Hashiba (12.7% in 79 DKD participants) 9 , lower than that reported by Lazaro‐Guavera (22% in 109 DKD patients) 7 , and higher than that reported by Groopman (1.6% in 370 DKD patients) 6 . The reported proportions varied depending on factors such as the definition of DKD, number of genes included in the gene list, version of ClinVar used, and strictness of variant quality control. In this study, the definitions of DKD and type 2 diabetes mellitus were based solely on registered disease names, and laboratory data or prescription records were not considered. We did not update the gene list from previous studies 9 , although updates to ClinVar are likely to increase the number of diagnostic variants. Because we were unable to conduct validation analyses, such as Sanger sequencing, we applied strict quality thresholds (DP ≥20 and GQ ≥30), which likely reduced the number of variants classified as diagnostic. However, it remains unclear whether differences in the prevalence of diagnostic variants are the main reason for the similar rates of diagnostic variants observed between patients with DKD and patients with type 2 diabetes without DKD, because no previous studies have conducted comparative analyses that included appropriate control groups.

Because many of the diseases included in the gene list had relatively mild effects on kidney function, it is possible that the impact of genes that strongly influenced the kidneys was underestimated. Therefore, we examined three specific Mendelian diseases: autosomal dominant Alport syndrome, cystinuria, and lipoprotein glomerulopathy. Each of these diseases can accelerate the development of DKD and contribute to end‐stage kidney disease; in some cases, these diseases cannot be clearly distinguished from DKD. Alport syndrome is a glomerular disease caused by abnormalities in the glomerular basement membrane and is characterized by hematuria followed by proteinuria 10 . Although most patients with DKD do not present with hematuria, a small but non‐negligible proportion of patients with DKD do 11 . Cystinuria is the most common genetic disorder that causes urinary stones and can lead to end‐stage kidney disease 12 ; however, diabetes itself is also a risk factor for kidney stones 13 . Therefore, kidney stones are usually understood as a complication of DKD; however, in some cases, they may be rooted in cystinuria. Lipoprotein glomerulopathy is a genetic kidney disease characterized by intraglomerular lipoprotein thrombi that lead to nephrotic syndrome 14 . Its clinical presentation resembles that of DKD and cannot be distinguished without a renal biopsy. Although lipoprotein glomerulopathy has been reported worldwide, most reported cases have been of Japanese or Chinese origin. This finding is compatible with the relatively high frequency of lipoprotein glomerulopathy in this study 15 .

The finding that a similar proportion of the participants carried diagnostic variants of Mendelian kidney disease does not necessarily indicate that these variants are unrelated to DKD development. If clinicians consider a patient's clinical course to be atypical for DKD—based on the timing of urinary abnormalities or family history—they may avoid labeling the patient as having DKD. Such practices are likely to affect research that relies on disease names for classification. As this study did not incorporate laboratory and prescription data, future research using laboratory‐ and prescription‐based definitions may help address this limitation. The lack of important clinical information resulted in an inability to adjust for diagnostic variants with DKD risk factors other than age and sex. This issue must be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. Another limitation was the lack of information regarding renal biopsy. We could not determine whether a biopsy had been performed to confirm or rule out DKD, and more precise diagnostic information could potentially alter the results.

In conclusion, we found that approximately 8% of the participants with diabetes carried diagnostic variants of Mendelian kidney disease, and the frequency of these variants did not differ between the groups stratified by DKD status. Future studies investigating the influence of diagnostic variants on DKD development should include appropriate controls.
