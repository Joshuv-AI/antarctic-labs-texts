The Keynote‐177 Randomized Controlled Trial of First‐line PD‐1 Blockade for Microsatellite‐High or Mismatch Repair‐Deficient Advanced Colorectal Cancer: Potential Consequences of the Cross‐Over Trial Design and Related Issues
Authors: S L Gerritse, E van Meerten, A J Duits, J B Schnog
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSome new oncology drugs offer substantial patient health benefit. However, due to high costs, many remain unavailable to patients in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Next to exorbitant drug pricing, uncertainty of the magnitude of benefit of new treatments can contribute to differences in global access to cancer treatments, even in high‐income countries. Health technology assessment (HTA) organizations employ predetermined criteria to assess whether a drug's efficacy suffices for local approval, ideally informed by randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that provide data on the clinically relevant endpoints overall survival (OS) and quality of life (QoL). Contemporary oncology RCTs increasingly suffer from design and interpretation issues that lead to inadequate data for HTA assessments. The Keynote‐177 RCT was the landmark study demonstrating the superiority of pembrolizumab over chemotherapy in terms of progression free survival and subsequently OS, as a first‐line treatment for patients with metastatic microsatellite instable high (MSI) colorectal cancer (CRC). However, in a trial testing the initial efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced MSI CRC, the allowance of crossover delayed the OS signal. Along with other design‐related limitations, this complicates the precise determination of the absolute benefit of first‐line immunotherapy on OS or quality of life in advanced MSI CRC.Keywords: cross‐over, global access, HTA, randomized controlled trials, subsequent anti‐cancer therapy, unbalanced censoring

Introduction

Of many new cancer drugs coming to market, some offer substantial benefit to patients. However, many show little (if any) benefit to patients in clinical trial settings (efficacy) or, upon further analysis, in real life (effectiveness). However, with a low bar set for regulatory drug approvals by agencies like the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA), such drugs often get approved nonetheless, mostly followed by uptake in treatment guidelines [1].

It is well recognized that shortcomings in the design and interpretation of contemporary oncology RCTs, such as the use of unvalidated surrogate endpoints, inappropriate control arms, inappropriate cross‐over, and informative censoring, led to the generation of suboptimal data for cancer policy decision making. This complicates the assessment of the value of cancer drugs or drug indications [1]. Due to the low bar for regulatory agency drug approvals, new cancer treatments without proper proof of efficacy flood the market.

Over the last decade, checkpoint inhibitors have been approved for treatment of many cancer types and have been regarded as a major advance for managing patients with advanced cancer. The collective excitement for checkpoint inhibitors is in contrast with the general modest survival increase for management of most advanced cancers (less than 3 months gain in overall survival (OS)), where these drugs are costly and thus, for most indications, should be regarded as low value treatments (with the value of the drug regarded as the clinical benefit in relation to its costs) [2]. The high costs of such drugs lead to unsustainable cancer care expenditure even for high income countries. From a public health perspective, reimbursement of any medical intervention should be limited to high value treatments. It is therefore critical that data generated by well‐designed randomized controlled trials (RCTs) quantify the magnitude of benefit on robust clinical endpoints such as OS and quality of life (QoL).

Reliable quantification of the magnitude of benefit enables health technology assessment (HTA) organizations to fairly prioritize reimbursement for treatments with significant value over those demonstrating limited or no benefit following regulatory agency approvals. What absolute increase in OS and/or QoL is considered relevant for HTA can differ between countries, cultures and age groups [1, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has established OS benefit as a fundamental criterion for the selection of essential cancer medicines since 2015. For a drug to be considered essential on the WHO‐Essential Medicines List (WHO‐EML), it should at least increase OS by 4–6 months as compared to standard of care [1, 4]. Conceding the challenges of costly drugs becoming available especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries, in general and for cancer drugs, when included on the WHO‐ELM the availability of these drugs is higher as compared to drugs not included on the WHO‐ELM [5, 6, 7]. Here we argue that the lack of proper assessment of efficacy in RCTs may also unduly limit and/or delay access to potentially truly efficacious and effective cancer drugs or drug indications. The Keynote 177 RCT was a seminal global study addressing the efficacy of the checkpoint inhibitor pembrolizumab as a first‐line treatment compared to chemotherapy in newly diagnosed patients with metastatic microsatellite instable high (MSI) colorectal cancer (CRC) [8]. Keynote 177 demonstrated both a PFS and OS benefit of pembrolizumab as compared to chemotherapy [9]. However, trial‐specific design issues have not only delayed the important OS signal but have also made it challenging to quantify.

Microsatellite Instable High‐Metastatic CRC

Approximately 15% of CRCs exhibit mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency, mainly due to sporadic causes like MutL protein homolog 1 gene promoter methylation, with about 3% having a germline mutation in one of the MMR genes. Both germline and somatic aberrations lead to a substantial mutational burden in the tumor with MSI. Evidence from phase I and II trials indicates that pre‐treated patients with MSI‐High/MMR deficient CRC respond to single‐agent programmed cell death protein‐1 (PD‐1) blockade, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 33% in patients treated with ≥ 2 prior lines of chemotherapy and 33% (95% CI, 22%–46%) in patients treated with ≥ 1 prior lines, with a median PFS of 2.3 months (95% CI, 2.1–8.1 months) and 4.1 months (95% CI, 2.1–18.9 months), respectively [10, 11, 12]. Based on this activity, the KEYNOTE‐177 RCT evaluated the first‐line efficacy of pembrolizumab (a PD‐1 receptor inhibitor) versus chemotherapy in advanced MSI‐H/MMR deficient metastatic CRC patients [8]. The co‐primary end points of the trial were PFS and OS. The primary endpoint of PFS in the intention‐to‐treat population demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in the pembrolizumab group compared to the control group (HR: 0.60 [95% CI: 0.45–0.80]; p = 0.0002), with a median PFS of 16.5 months (95% CI: 5.4–32.4) in the pembrolizumab group as compared to 8.2 months (95% CI: 6.1–10.2) in the control group [8]. At the final analysis (median follow‐up of 44.5 months [IQR 39.7–49.8]), median OS was not reached (NR; 95% CI 49.2–NR) in the pembrolizumab arm (NR; 95% CI 49.2–NR) as compared to the chemotherapy arm (36.7 months (27.6–NR) (hazard ratio [HR] 0.74; 95% CI 0.53–1.03; p = 0.036)), with the OS difference not reaching statistical significance [9]. Subsequent reports from the Keynote‐177 also demonstrated an improvement in health‐related quality of life (hrQoL) for pembrolizumab [13]. These data led to both the Food and Drug Administration (FDA; June 2020) and European Medicines Agency (EMA; January 2021) approvals of pembrolizumab for first‐line treatment of patients with MSI‐H or MMR deficient metastatic CRC. Long term results have recently been published demonstrating a 5‐year OS rate of 54.8% median OS 77.5 months versus 36.7 months with chemotherapy (hazard ratio, 0.73; 95% CI 0.53–0.99) [9]. Even though pembrolizumab is clearly a superior treatment to chemotherapy for first‐line treatment of advanced MSI‐CRC patients, the true magnitude of benefit (OS and/or hrQoL) of pembrolizumab for advanced MSI‐CRC remains, in our opinion, unclear.

Design and Interpretation Issues of the KEYNOTE‐177 Trial

Cross‐Over in Absence of Efficacy Data in Later Lines

The Keynote‐177 trial studied pembrolizumab as a first‐line treatment, without proof of efficacy in later treatment lines. With the hype pertaining to immunotherapy in cancer, it is understandably argued that withholding cross‐over would be unethical and may limit patient participation in a RCT. However, in a RCT assessing the fundamental efficacy of a new intervention, cross‐over is considered inappropriate [14]. Early phase studies aim to identify drug activity and provide data for subsequent efficacy testing in follow‐up RCTs. Comparisons of outcomes obtained in phase II studies with subsequent RCTs with identical treatment regimens across all cancer drug classes have almost universally shown an overestimation of efficacy in phase II trials [15, 16, 17, 18]. Without previously demonstrated efficacy, there is no ethical basis for cross‐over at the outset in a RCT [14]. Allowing cross‐over without demonstrated efficacy in later treatment lines, both potential benefit and harm can be masked. Furthermore, a signal of OS improvement can be masked and/or delayed and underestimated. This may complicate both HTA assessment as well as delay formal recognition as an essential drug and subsequently availability for patients globally [3, 4]. In the RCT testing the fundamental efficacy of immunotherapy for the first‐ line treatment of stage III (unresectable) or stage IV melanoma, ipilimumab combined with dacarbazine was compared to placebo with dacarbazine. Despite the dismal prognosis of advanced melanoma and demonstrated activity of ipilimumab in phase 2 clinical trials, cross‐over to ipilimumab after disease progression was strictly prohibited [18, 19, 20, 21]. Furthermore, the use of anti‐CTLA‐4 antagonists and CD137 agonists was also prohibited during the follow‐up phase such that the magnitude of OS benefit and harm of ipilimumab could be reliably determined.

Prior data from phase I–II trials in previously chemotherapy‐treated advanced MSI CRC patients have demonstrated drug activity, not efficacy. Nonetheless, cross‐over from chemotherapy to pembrolizumab upon disease progression was permitted per initial protocol in the Keynote‐177 trial. Fifty‐seven out of 154 patients (37%) received subsequent pembrolizumab within the trial and 39 (25.3%) received an anti‐PD‐(L)1 antibody outside of the trial, resulting in a cross‐over rate of 62% for the intention to treat population [9]. Of the 57 patients who crossed over to receive on‐protocol pembrolizumab, 21 (36.8%) completed 35 cycles of pembrolizumab, and 36 (63.2%) discontinued treatment, most commonly due to disease progression.

Cross‐over was permitted at the discretion of the treating physician. This further complicates the interpretation of the data, as it likely introduced selection bias. It is unlikely that such treatments for this indication would be equally available outside of the trial. Therefore, the cross‐over of physician selected patients where anti‐PD‐(L)1 antibody treatment was available may have undermined the effects of prior randomization. If cross‐over is allowed, it should be incorporated as such within the trial protocol and be made available to all participating patients in the control arm. With physicians choosing whom to expose to pembrolizumab and potential unequal availability or access to anti‐PD‐(L1) treatment after progression, it is possible and probable that patients whom received second line immunotherapy differed from those who did not. Cross‐over has limited both the potential of the Keynote‐177 to both properly quantify the likely potential OS benefit and has delayed the OS signal. Furthermore, granular description of all ultimately received post‐protocol, which is crucial for interpretating the reported OS findings, is lacking [9].

Unbalanced Censoring in the Keynote 177

Recalculation of the PFS Kaplan Meijer estimate curve demonstrated unbalanced censoring occurring in the control arm of the trial (7.8% (n = 12) at 4 months in the control arm and 1.3% (n = 1) in the experimental arm [analyzed with Digitizelt 2.5.10 and R Studio Version 2023.12.1 + 402]), approximating 12 patients in the control arm not to have received any treatment (0 in the experimental arm) [8, 22]. A recognized reason for patients dropping out from control‐arms directly after randomization in open label studies is patient disappointment [23]. Immediate withdrawal of consent is hypothesized to occur more likely in in patients with higher health literacy, especially if they have alternative ways to have access to the desired treatment outside of a trial [23]. A clear example was the VISION trial in prostate cancer, in which at trial initiation 56% of control arm patients withdrew consent due to disappointment as many would receive treatments they had already progressed on [24, 25]. Patient characteristics and precise reasons for withdrawal have not been made available. Due to the early unbalanced censoring, the control arm may have potentially been enriched with a more vulnerable patient population. Unbalanced early censoring is informative and subverts the effect of randomization. Studies have shown that a high rate of unbalanced early censoring, when assuming best‐ and worst‐case scenarios for censored patients, can change trial outcomes for PFS from positive to negative [23, 25]. Unbalanced informative censoring is increasingly recognized as a potential cause of inflated surrogate endpoint (e.g., PFS) improvements in oncology RCT's and may have biased the results in favor of the experimental arm in the Keynote 177.

hrQOL Assessment in the Keynote 177

The reported improvement in hrQoL with pembrolizumab compared to standard chemotherapy may be overestimated for several reasons. First, subjective endpoints as hrQoL are often overestimated in the experimental arm of open‐label studies [26]. Second, hrQoL should ideally be measured across the entire cancer journey to capture potential late detrimental effects on hrQoL after treatment cessation [27]. Third, unbalanced censoring, as has occurred in the Keynote‐177, also impacts the validity of the results of hrQoL analysis. The difference in compliance rates for this specific endpoint between treatment groups was notable (88% for pembrolizumab and 77% for the chemotherapy group). Since the study was open‐label, one possible explanation for the difference in compliance rates could be that patients receiving pembrolizumab were more willing to participate in study‐related investigations due to beforehand expected benefits of the experimental therapy, foregoing the effect of randomization. In this light, as stated, patients that withdraw from the control arm immediately after randomization (12 in the Keynote 177) likely differ from those remaining on study, which also could influence hrQoL findings. Lastly, a potential negative effect on hrQoL due to personal financial expenditure for drug access is not captured in RCTs where drugs are provided by the trial sponsor [28].

Consequences of the Keynote 177 Trial Design

Immunotherapy has generated a significant hype and high expectations among physicians and patients. It has shown efficacy and effectiveness in some cancers, such as melanoma, but offers limited benefits for many others. Indeed, in a recent analysis of all FDA approved immunotherapy indications from 2011 to 2023, slightly more than half of registration trials reported the impact on OS, which was a modest median of less than 3.0 months as compared with trial control arms [2]. There is a strong biological rationale for applying immunotherapy in patients with MSI‐H or MMR deficient mCRC and pembrolizumab is clearly superior to chemotherapy in first‐line treatment [8]. The magnitude of the OS benefit of first‐line single agent anti‐PD‐(L)1 antibody treatment as compared to investigator choice chemotherapy, however, is not quantified and likely underestimated due to the issues of the Keynote 177 trial detailed above. The suggestion that pembrolizumab seems more efficacious in first as compared to second line treatment in MSI‐H or MMR deficient mCRC cannot be substantiated given the issues with cross‐over as per physician discretion as detailed above [9].

The aim of the WHO‐EML is to provide guidance for rational prescribing and equitable access to drugs by prioritizing cost‐effective medicines that should always be available in functioning health care systems. With the longer follow‐up of the Keynote 177 and the improvement in OS, pembrolizumab has now been deemed an essential drug for advanced MSI high CRC on the latest WHO‐EML, more than 5 years after FDA approval [4, 9]. It is interesting to hypothesize that without cross‐over in the Keynote 177, the OS benefit would have been detected earlier, with potential earlier designation as an essential drug indication.

Conclusion

Regulatory approvals of cancer drugs and their indications are often based on trials with limitations, which can contribute to the introduction of lower‐value oncology treatments. With growing awareness of the limitations of contemporary cancer RCTs, we, as healthcare advocates, should aim to both improve the quality of oncology trials and set appropriate higher standards for regulatory approvals [1]. This could help global HTA processes prioritize interventions supported by robust efficacy data from high‐quality RCTs.

Recognizing that many low‐ and middle‐income countries face affordability constraints that limit implementation of EML recommendations, timely inclusion in the WHO‐EML remains important for HTA and may support efforts to align system capacity with fair drug pricing, fostering advocacy for equitable access [29]. Inclusion of drugs on the WHO‐ELM list increases the global availability as compared to drugs not deemed essential. In the case of the Keynote‐177 trial, we hypothesize that trial‐related factors may have delayed the observation of an OS signal for first‐line pembrolizumab in MSI‐H or MMR‐deficient mCRC, and that the true magnitude of benefit could even be underestimated. This may have influenced the timing of WHO‐EML recognition and may have thereby potentially limited or delayed patient access globally.
