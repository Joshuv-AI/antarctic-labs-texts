Residues of the ribose binding site are required for human ribokinase activity
Authors: Juliana C. Ferreira, Lyudmila Nedyalkova, Adrian J. Villanueva, Samar Fadl, Zeynep Nur Cinviz, Kenana Al Adem, Ozge Sensoy, Wael M. Rabeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Sugars are not only fundamental sources of cellular energy but also precursors for nucleotide production and crucial structural biomolecules. Following cellular uptake, the metabolism of sugars is initiated by phosphorylation catalyzed by specific sugar kinases, which effectively traps the sugars intracellularly for subsequent metabolic utilization. Among sugars, D-ribose holds significant biological importance as an essential constituent of nucleotides, RNA, DNA, and energy carriers such as ATP (Stincone et al., 2015). Phosphorylation of ribose by ribokinase (RK) produces ribose-5-phosphate (Park et al., 2007, Sigrell et al., 1999), an essential metabolite for entry into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), which supplies NADPH (Banfalvi, 2020, Banfalvi, 2021) for redox homeostasis and pentose sugars for nucleotide biosynthesis (Rashida and Laxman, 2021, Stincone et al., 2015). Thus, ribose metabolism is crucial for vital cellular processes, including proliferation, DNA repair, and energy metabolism.

Conditions of metabolic stress, such as hypoxia or ischemia, intensify cellular reliance on nucleotide salvage pathways and ATP regeneration, underscoring the importance of RK for maintaining cellular bioenergetics. Ribose supplementation has emerged as a therapeutic strategy for improving ATP recovery in ischemic tissues, particularly in cardiovascular diseases characterized by compromised energy metabolism and myocardial dysfunction (Bayram et al., 2015, Krueger et al., 2021, Molavi et al., 2021, Shecterle et al., 2010). Initial research on ribose supplementation focused on enhancing athletic performance, and current clinical evidence remains inconclusive (Hellsten et al., 2004, Kerksick et al., 2005, Kreider et al., 2003, Peveler et al., 2006, Seifert et al., 2017). Both animal and clinical studies have demonstrated that increased ribose intake can significantly improve energy level recovery in ischemic myocardial cells (Zimmer, 1983, Zimmer et al., 1984). Elucidating the catalytic mechanisms of human RK is essential for understanding the therapeutic potential of ribose in managing myocardial ischemia and related cardiovascular conditions (Pauly and Pepine, 2000, Shecterle et al., 2010).

RK belongs to the evolutionarily conserved phosphofructokinase B (PfkB) family of carbohydrate kinases (Park and Gupta, 2008, Park et al., 2007), which includes enzymes such as adenosine kinase, inosine-guanosine kinase, ketohexokinase, 1-phosphofructokinase, and 6-phosphofructokinase (Bork et al., 1993). Members of the PfkB family exhibit significant sequence homology across diverse organisms, reflecting their fundamental roles in sugar and nucleotide metabolism. Structurally, PfkB enzymes share a conserved dimeric architecture composed of a central Rossmann-like α/β domain, which is responsible for nucleotide binding, and a dynamic lid domain that regulates substrate access. The structure also unveiled the dimeric architecture, stabilized by a unique β-clasp motif that forms a mobile lid domain(Sigrell et al., 1999, Sigrell et al., 1997, Sigrell et al., 1998). This motif couples dimerization to catalysis by contributing essential residues to the partner's active site. Multiple structural studies capturing the apo, ribose-bound, and multiple ternary complexes visualized a pronounced induced-fit cycle (Sigrell et al., 1999). The enzyme transitions from an initial open conformation that binds ribose to a closed state upon sugar binding, where the lid domain traps the substrate to organize the nucleotide-binding site. Final ATP binding induces smaller conformational changes for the active site to initiate the phosphoryl transfer reaction. This dynamic perspective was reinforced by observing residual lid mobility, where conformational flexibility is essential for catalysis. Two characteristic conserved motifs define RK family sequences: a glycine-rich region at the N-terminus and a distinct region at the C-terminus involved in ATP binding and anion hole formation (Bork et al., 1993, Zayats et al., 2022). The widespread presence of RK in prokaryotes as well as eukaryotes, including plants, animals, and fungi, underscores the ancient evolutionary origins and biological importance of this enzyme family (Hope et al., 1986, Roy et al., 2019).

Studies of prokaryotic homologs, such as E. coli RK, have contributed to an understanding of general catalytic mechanisms (Hope et al., 1986, Park et al., 2007, Sigrell et al., 1998, Zayats et al., 2022). A catalytic aspartate is essential for initiating catalysis by deprotonating the ribose C5 hydroxyl, thereby enabling nucleophilic attack on the γ-phosphate of ATP (Park and Gupta, 2008, Sigrell et al., 1999). The phosphoryl transfer reaction is facilitated by divalent metal ions, particularly Mg2+, which serve as essential cofactors by neutralizing negative charges on the nucleotide and optimally positioning the γ-phosphate for catalysis (Andersson and Mowbray, 2002). Bacterial RKs adopt a dimeric structure stabilized by a distinctive β-clasp interface that permits asymmetric dynamics between protomers (Anderson and Cooper, 1969, Andersson and Mowbray, 2002, Park et al., 2007, Sigrell et al., 1997, Sigrell et al., 1998). However, the functional relevance of this structural arrangement in human RK remains inadequately characterized. Consequently, significant gaps persist in our understanding of the specific roles played by ribose-binding residues in human RK, as well as the relationships between the dimerization, catalytic efficiency, and thermal stability of RK.

Here, we present the crystal structure of human RK at 2.1 Å resolution complexed with ADP and Mg2+, revealing a conserved dimeric architecture and distinct asymmetric lid dynamics facilitated by a β-clasp motif at the dimer interface. The results of targeted mutagenesis, enzyme kinetics, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, mass photometry (MP), and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide a comprehensive biochemical and biophysical characterization of human RK. Mutations of key ribose-coordinating residues substantially impair or abolish catalytic activity, disrupt substrate coordination, and accelerate active-site opening, thus destabilizing substrate binding. Additionally, biophysical assessments reveal that perturbations in the ribose-binding network significantly reduce the thermal resilience and catalytic stability of human RK, underscoring the critical relationship between structural integrity and enzymatic activity. This integrated structural, functional, and thermodynamic approach offers a detailed mechanistic framework for understanding human RK’s catalytic specificity and efficiency and establishing its potential as a therapeutic target for metabolic disorders involving disrupted ribose metabolism.

Results

Structural characterization of human RK

The wild-type (WT) human RK enzyme was expressed in E. coli and purified to > 95 % purity, as confirmed by SDS–PAGE analysis (data not shown). To enhance crystallization and crystal quality, the first 10 residues of the N-terminus were removed in the RK construct. Crystals of RK bound to ADP and Mg2+ were obtained using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method at room temperature. These crystals diffracted to a high resolution of 2.10 Å (PDB ID: 2FV7). The final model revealed a biological homodimer, with each monomer composed of 322 residues and a molecular weight of ∼ 35 kDa. The crystallographic R-work and R-free values were 0.198 and 0.248, respectively, with well-defined electron density for residues 15–322 and for the bound ADP ligand (Table S1, Fig. S1). The first four residues of the truncated RK construct (W11–E14) were not modeled due to weak/absent electron density, making V15 the first ordered residue. Each RK monomer exhibits a two-domain architecture consisting of a central α/β domain and a protruding four-stranded β-sheet known as the lid domain (Fig. 1). The central α/β domain adopts a Rossmann-like fold, a hallmark of the PfkB family of carbohydrate/purine kinases (Rossmann et al., 1974, Sigrell et al., 1998). This fold comprises a central β-sheet of nine parallel strands flanked by eight surrounding α-helices, forming a conserved nucleotide-binding core. The Rossmann-like fold serves as the structural framework for catalytic activity and is conserved across other PfkB family members, including ketohexokinase (KHK) and adenosine kinase (AK), which share similar domain organizations and substrate coordination geometries (Ebenhoch et al., 2023, Ferreira et al., 2024, Helsley et al., 2023, Paul et al., 2015, Paul et al., 2014). The lid domain of RK is composed of a four-stranded antiparallel β-sheet that spans residues 25–52 (β2–β3b) and 107–124 (β6–β7) (Fig. 1B and S4). The lid domain overlays the ribose-binding pocket and plays a key role in the conformational gating of the active site. The β2 and β6 strands run parallel, while the β3b and β7 strands complete the antiparallel arrangement, forming a dynamic flap that regulates substrate access. The extended loop regions connecting these strands are also involved in inter-subunit interactions, particularly at the dimer interface. At the dimer interface, the β-sheets from each monomer interlock to form a distinctive β-clasp motif (Fig. 1A). This motif is stabilized by a series of inter-subunit hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions, notably between β3a of one monomer and β7 of the opposite monomer.

The lid domains form the dimeric interface of human RK (Fig. 1A and S4). The two protomers are related by a twofold rotational symmetry axis, forming an isologous dimer interface—meaning that identical sets of interactions are reciprocally established between each monomer (Garratt et al., 2013, Marsh and Teichmann, 2015). The β-sheets of each protomer are oriented orthogonally (Chothia and Janin, 1982), and a notable bend within strand β3b allows it to extend and wrap around strand β7 of the opposing monomer. These structural features assemble into a highly organized and symmetric configuration resembling a “handshake” known as the β-clasp, a hallmark of PfkB family enzymes (Sigrell et al., 1998, Trinh et al., 2009).

Within the β-clasp, inter-subunit stabilization is driven primarily through two symmetric networks of backbone–backbone H-bonds between strand β3a of one monomer (residues 39–44) and β7 of the opposing monomer (residues 119–123) (Fig. 2). The backbone of β3a (G39, E40, I42, G44) engages the main chain of β7 (N119, I121, I123) with H-bond distances of 2.7–3.2 Å (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). Together, these contacts mirror H-bonding patterns seen in parallel β-sheets (Merkel et al., 1999) and provide the structural basis for the β-clasp mechanism, which is characterized by interlocking backbone contacts between the lid domains of each protomer (Sigrell et al., 1998, Trinh et al., 2009). These interactions stabilize the dimer by anchoring the β-sheets in an orthogonal configuration. Beyond its structural role, the β-clasp also serves as a functional conduit for allosteric communication between the two subunits (Ferreira et al., 2024). The clasp permits cooperative conformational changes, enabling one protomer to adopt a closed, ligand-bound state while the other transitions into an open conformation—a dynamic behavior consistently observed in MD simulations. RK harbors two binding sites for ATP and ribose situated at the interface of the α/β core and the lid domain. Analysis of our ADP-bound crystal structure of RK (PDB: 2FV7) revealed an H-bond between N3 of the adenine base of ADP and the hydroxyl of T256 (3.3 Å), while the ribose moiety of ADP interacts with N295 via its C2′ hydroxyl (2.9 Å) (Fig. 3A). To evaluate sugar binding, D-ribose was analyzed from the structure of human RK co-crystallized with both substrates, ribose and non-hydrolyzable ATP analogue, adenosine 5′-(β,γ-methylene)triphosphate (AMP-PCP; PDB: 6WK0). The D-ribose moiety forms multiple H-bonds with the RK active site. The C1 and C2 hydroxyls of ribose interact with E154 (2.6 Å) and D27 (2.6 Å), respectively (Fig. 3B). The C3 hydroxyl forms dual hydrogen bonds with D27 (2.8 Å) and N57 (3.0 Å). The C5 hydroxyl engages D269 (2.5 Å) and K54 (2.8 Å), and the ether oxygen forms a hydrogen bond with K54 (3.4 Å). These contacts define a precise and selective ribose coordination site, stabilized by conserved catalytic and substrate-recognition sites.

In parallel, ligand binding induces a hinge-like closure of the β-clasp with coordinated displacements of ∼ 8.0–12.2 Å (Fig. S3). Superposing the most open clasp state, RK–ADP (PDB: 2FV7), with the closed substrate-bound RK–ribose–AMP-PCP complex (PDB: 6WK0) reveals a substrate-triggered displacement across both halves of the lid: the N-terminal segment β2–β3b (M25–G52) moves 12.2 Å at Cα(G39) (Fig. S3A), while the C-terminal segment β6–β7 (G107–V124) moves 11.8 Å at Cα(E116) with an additional ∼ 8.0 Å translation along the strands at Cα(N119) (Fig. S3B). Among deposited human RK structures, the RK-ADP represents the most open clasp. This concerted motion draws the lid over the sugar pocket, tightens inter-protomer contacts, and pre-organizes the catalytic loops to promote phosphoryl transfer. Together, the conserved α/β core and the mobile lid domain define the structural fold of the PfkB family and contribute to the catalytic mechanism and allosteric regulation of RK and related enzymes.

Catalytic activity of ribokinase active site mutants

The functional contributions of five active-site residues near the ribose-binding pocket —D27, K54, N57, E154, and A181—to human RK catalysis were investigated through alanine or charge-reversal mutations. Structural analysis of RK bound to ribose (PDB: 6WK0) revealed that all residues form direct H-bonds with the ribose sugar. Alanine substitutions were introduced to disrupt side chain-mediated interactions. The enzymatic activity of the mutants was measured under saturating substrate conditions (3.0 mM ATP and 5.0 mM ribose) using a continuous pyruvate kinase (PK)/lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)-coupled assay at 37 °C.

Enzyme titration experiments revealed that alanine substitutions at D27, K54, and N57 resulted in nearly complete loss of activity (Fig. 4, Fig. 4). This was unexpected, as the mutated residues, except K54, do not directly interact with the ribose C5 hydroxyl group, the site of phosphorylation. Interestingly, the E154A mutant retained ∼ 58 % of WT activity, suggesting a non-essential but supportive role of this residue in catalysis. In contrast, the charge reversal mutant E154R showed no detectable activity, highlighting the importance of maintaining appropriate electrostatic interactions within the ribose binding site. To confirm that the loss of activity in these mutants was not due to global misfolding, the secondary structures of WT and mutant RK were assessed by far-UV circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. CD spectra recorded between 200 and 260 nm at 25 °C revealed that all RK variants had conserved α-helical profiles, with characteristic minima at 208 nm and 222 nm (Fig. S5A). This pattern was maintained even in catalytically inactive mutants such as D27A, K54A, N57A, and E154R, confirming that the overall protein fold was preserved despite loss of enzymatic activity. However, small differences in ellipticity were observed. Most mutants exhibited a decrease in the intensity of the Cotton effect, particularly at 208 nm, indicating a mild reduction in overall helicity or altered local packing. The E154A mutant, by contrast, exhibited a more pronounced ellipticity minimum at 208 nm than WT RK, suggesting local changes in secondary structure that may reflect subtle rearrangements near the catalytic site. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the inactivation of several of the mutants was not due to gross misfolding but instead arose from localized perturbations in secondary structure near the ribose-binding region or catalytic core.

To further probe the functional plasticity of key active site residues in RK, mutations were designed based on structural homology with a related member of the PfkB enzyme family, KHK-A (Ferreira et al., 2024). The comparative structural analysis revealed that K54 and A181 of RK align with N42 and K174 of KHK-A, respectively. Notably, K174 of KHK-A—equivalent to A181 of RK—is positioned ∼ 5.3 Å from the C6 hydroxyl of the fructose substrate. Although this interaction is not required for the phosphoryl transfer step, it supports substrate binding and proper positioning within the active site (Fig. S6) (Ferreira et al., 2024). To mimic these substitutions and probe their functional relevance, K54 was mutated to asparagine (K54N) and A181 to lysine (A181K). The introduction of the residues from KHK-A into RK failed to retain enzymatic activity, indicating that these substitutions were not functionally tolerated in the RK scaffold. In a similar vein, replacing E154 with a positively charged arginine (E154R) abolished activity, highlighting the critical importance of electrostatic and geometric compatibility at this position. Collectively, these findings underscore that catalytic competence in RK depends not merely on the presence of conserved residues but on the precise spatial arrangement and chemical properties of the active site side chains, which act cooperatively to stabilize substrate binding and orientation for efficient catalysis.

Initial velocity studies of WT and E154A RK

Enzymatic activity assays revealed that D27A, K54A, and N57A—as well as the charge-reversal variants E154R and A181K—resulted in nearly complete loss of activity (Fig. 4, Fig. 4). Of the five residues evaluated, only the E154A variant retained measurable catalytic activity under saturating substrate concentrations (∼58 % of WT) and thus allowed further kinetic characterization. Kinetic parameters for E154A and WT RK were determined by measuring the initial reaction rate at varying ribose concentrations under multiple fixed ATP concentrations. The resulting velocity data were fit to a random Bi–Bi sequential kinetic model, consistent with a two-substrate reaction mechanism (Fig. 4, Fig. 4). Compared with WT RK, E154A exhibited a 2-fold decrease in the turnover rate (V/Et), a 13.5-fold increase in the Michaelis constant for ribose (KRib), and a 3.5-fold increase in KATP, indicating impaired binding of both substrates (Table S2). These changes translated to a 28-fold reduction in catalytic efficiency for ribose (V/KRibEt) and a 5-fold decrease for ATP (V/KATPEt). Thus, mutation of E154 greatly impaired ribose and ATP binding. Because E154 is not in direct proximity to the ATP-binding site, its mutation likely perturbs local electrostatics or conformation in a way that indirectly compromises nucleotide binding. These results underscore a broader role of E154 in organizing the active site geometry to permit efficient substrate coordination and catalysis.

Oligomeric state analysis of RK variants

To determine whether alanine or charge-substituting mutations affect the oligomeric state of human RK, we employed mass photometry (MP), a label-free, light-scattering-based technique that allows accurate single-molecule mass measurements in solution (Sonn-Segev et al., 2020, Wu and Piszczek, 2021, Young et al., 2018). Interferometric contrast values were calibrated against a standard curve constructed using proteins of known molecular weights. All RK variants were measured at 60-s acquisition yielding ∼ 3000 binding events. The histogram data obtained from the experiment were transformed into a continuous probability density distribution using kernel density estimation (Fig. S5B).

The mass distribution of WT RK showed a major peak centered at ∼ 67 kDa, consistent with its expected dimeric form (theoretical MW ∼ 69.3 kDa). WT RK also exhibited several minor populations with higher masses (131, 203, 269, and 336 kDa), suggesting low-abundance higher-order oligomers or transient associations. To assess substrate-induced effects on RK oligomerization, MP analysis of WT RK in the presence of ribose, ATP, or ADP was performed (Fig. S5B). Ribose and ATP had no measurable impact on the oligomeric state of WT RK, with distributions mirroring the apo enzyme. However, the addition of ADP shifted the probability density of WT RK toward higher molecular weight, suggesting ADP-induced conformational stabilization or transient complex formation.

All mutant forms of RK—D27A, K54A, K54N, N57A, E154A, E154R, and A181K—retained a predominant peak around 67 kDa, indicating that these substitutions did not disrupt the dimeric architecture of RK (Fig. S5C). However, both E154A and E154R RK exhibited an additional shoulder peak around 130 kDa, indicative of a minor subpopulation with potential dimer–dimer association. Despite these variations, no significant shift in oligomeric state was observed across the mutant panel, indicating that the loss of catalytic activity in these mutants was not due to changes in their quaternary structures.

Molecular dynamics reveal the structural basis of RK mutant inactivation

To elucidate the structural mechanisms underlying the catalytic defects observed in the RK active site mutants, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of WT and mutant RK were performed, focusing on conformational dynamics, active site accessibility, and ribose coordination stability. To assess the conformational dynamics of WT RK, protomer opening and closing were monitored by measuring the distance between the Cα atoms of E116 and V262. These residues are located on opposite domains: E116 is located in the lid domain, within the β6–β7 loop, while V262 is in the central α/β domain within the loop connecting β13 to α-helix H8, as determined by state-specific information (SSI) analysis. Thus, this inter-residue distance reflects the movement of the lid domain relative to the catalytic core (Fig. S7A). The MD simulations revealed asymmetric behavior between the protomers of WT RK. Both protomers initially adopted a closed conformation. Around 20 ns into the simulation, one protomer transitioned to an open state and remained open for the rest of the 300-ns trajectory, while the other remained stably closed. This divergence in conformational trajectories suggests the presence of inter-protomer communication and supports a model of functional asymmetry or cooperativity reminiscent of the alternating protomer mechanism proposed for KHK-A (Ferreira et al., 2024). Notably, this asymmetric opening may be stabilized by the β-clasp motif, which forms a β-sheet interface at the dimeric junction. As a result, the β-clasp motif acts as a conformational anchor, keeping one protomer in a closed, catalytically competent state while allowing the opposing protomer to adopt an open conformation. Thus, the β-clasp structure appears to play a pivotal role in modulating protomer opening and coordinating the conformational transitions necessary for catalysis.

Consistent with the lack of activity of D27A, N57A, E154R, and A181K RK, the MD simulations showed that these mutations accelerate opening of the active site. To quantify this, the distance between the backbone Cα atoms of G50 (located at the end of the β3 strand in the lid domain) and G306 (at the start of α-helix H10 in the central α/β domain) was used as a reliable indicator of active site opening. G50 and G306 were selected as optimal markers due to their proximity to the ribose-binding site of RK. Whereas WT RK retained a closed conformation for most of the 150-ns simulation, the D27A, N57A, and A181K mutants transitioned to an open conformation within the first 20 ns (Fig. S7B and S7C). Interestingly, despite their reduced activity, the opening dynamics of E154A and E154R RK were similar to those of WT RK throughout the 95-ns simulation time, suggesting that accelerated opening alone is insufficient to explain the loss of catalytic efficiency (Fig. S7C).

Alongside conformational changes associated with active site opening, the stability of ribose coordination within the active site was analyzed during MD simulations to better understand the structural basis of catalytic inactivation in the mutants. The sugar-binding interactions were characterized by measuring the distances between key coordinating residues—N27, K54, and E154—and corresponding ribose hydroxyl groups. Specifically, we tracked the distances between the side chains of K54 and C4-OH, N27 and C3-OH, and E154 and C1-OH. In WT RK, ribose remained stably bound throughout the 150-ns simulation, with minor fluctuations observed between 80 and 120 ns (Fig. S8A–S8D). By contrast, the E154R mutant exhibited rapid ribose dissociation within 25 ns, while the A181K and E154A mutants showed dissociation after ∼ 80 ns (Fig. S8B and Supplementary Video). The N57A mutant displayed persistently large distances (>4 Å) between the active site residues and ribose, culminating in sugar release at ∼ 120 ns. Only the D27A mutant maintained sugar-binding interactions comparable to those of WT RK throughout the simulation.

Collectively, these simulations demonstrated that both stable ribose coordination and regulated pocket opening are critical for RK catalytic function. Mutations that compromise either structural feature destabilize substrate binding and are likely to impair catalytic turnover. The findings are consistent with the results of the biochemical characterizations and align with mechanistic insights from the structurally related KHK-A enzyme, reinforcing the central role of the ribose-binding pocket in RK activity.

Thermodynamic stability of WT and mutant RK

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed to investigate the thermal stability of WT and mutant RK both in the absence and presence of individual substrates (2.0 mM ribose or 3.3 mM ATP) (Fig. 5). All thermal unfolding profiles displayed a single cooperative transition, as indicated by a single melting temperature (Tm) calculated from the apex of the thermal peak. The associated calorimetric enthalpy (ΔHcal) was determined by integrating the area under each peak, which represents the total heat absorbed during thermal denaturation. The results were grouped into two categories: catalytically active variants (WT and E154A) and inactive variants (D27A, K54A, K54N, N57A, E154R, and A181K) (Fig. 5, Fig. 5). The Tm values of WT RK were nearly identical across all conditions, with minimal variation between the apo (79.5 ± 0.1 °C), ribose-bound (79.7 ± 0.1 °C), and ATP-bound (80.1 ± 0.1 °C) states. These results suggest that substrate binding has a negligible impact on the overall thermal stability of WT RK (Fig. 5A). Despite remaining catalytically active, E154A RK exhibited the second lowest thermodynamic stability among all mutants tested, with a pronounced reduction in Tm of ∼ 9 °C compared with WT in both the apo and substrate-bound states (70.7 ± 0.4 °C). The IT1/2 of WT RK was ∼ 76 °C, reflecting high thermal tolerance (Fig. 6, Fig. 6). To further investigate the long-term effects of thermal exposure on enzyme stability, t1/2 of both WT and E154A RK was determined by incubating each variant at 65 °C and 70 °C for up to 60 min. t1/2, defined as the time required for enzymatic activity to decrease by 50 % of its initial value, was determined by monitoring residual activity at 15-min intervals. Enzymatic activity was continuously monitored via NADH production from the PK/LDH-coupled assay. The resulting activity profiles were plotted as the percentage of initial activity versus time and fitted to a first-order kinetic decay model to extract t1/2 values for each variant. E154A RK exhibited rapid thermal inactivation, with a t1/2 of 33 min at 65 °C and just 12 min at 70 °C (Fig. 6, Fig. 6). By contrast, RK WT demonstrated exceptional thermal stability, maintaining ∼ 90 % of its initial activity after 60 min at 65 °C and displaying a t1/2 of ∼ 64 min at 70 °C. These findings, together with the results of the DSC analysis (Fig. 5), further confirm that the E154A mutation impairs both kinetic and thermodynamic stability, highlighting the essential role of E154 in preserving the structural integrity and catalytic resilience of RK.

Discussion

Ribose metabolism is central to cellular homeostasis, linking sugar utilization to nucleotide biosynthesis, ATP generation, and redox regulation via the PPP pathway. RK is a key enzyme in carbohydrate metabolism that catalyzes the phosphorylation of D-ribose to ribose-5-phosphate—a central precursor for nucleotide biosynthesis, ATP generation, and the PPP, a crucial metabolic route for generating reducing equivalents (NADPH) (Blanco and Blanco, 2022, Cosgrove, 1998). The present structural and functional characterization of human RK not only reveal the architecture of the enzyme but also highlight the critical roles of specific ribose-binding residues in supporting catalysis, substrate selectivity, and thermal stability. These findings have broad implications for understanding the specificity of PfkB family kinases and may inform the development of therapeutics and nutraceuticals based on ribose metabolism.

Our structural analysis confirmed that human RK adopts the conserved dimeric architecture of the PfkB family, which is characterized by a central α/β domain with a Rossmann-like fold and a distinct lid domain formed by a four-stranded β-sheet (Cabrera et al., 2010, Kim et al., 2021, Ohshima et al., 2004, Rossmann et al., 1974). The lid domains from each protomer interlock through a distinctive β-clasp interface—an arrangement supported by extensive backbone H-bonding interactions (Arnfors et al., 2006, Ebenhoch et al., 2023, Ferreira et al., 2024, Morgan et al., 2013, Schumacher et al., 2000, Sigrell et al., 1999, Sigrell et al., 1997, Sigrell et al., 1998). This “handshake” architecture is not only critical for dimerization but also enables conformational asymmetry between protomers, facilitating dynamic substrate access and allosteric communication during catalysis. MD simulations of WT RK confirmed asymmetric protomer behavior. Initially, both protomers adopted a closed conformation; however, after ∼ 20 ns, one protomer transitioned into an open state, while the other remained closed throughout the 300-ns trajectory. This asymmetric switching was stabilized by the β-clasp, which likely acts as a conformational anchor. The clasp restricted one protomer in a catalytically competent closed state, while allowing flexibility in the opposing protomer, mirroring the alternating mechanism proposed for KHK-A and further supporting a model of functional asymmetry and allosteric communication (Ferreira et al., 2024).

Within each active site, ribose binding is secured through a network of H-bonds that engage hydroxyl groups at C1, C2, C3, and C5 and the ether oxygen of ribose. These interactions involve highly conserved residues, including D27, K54, N57, and E154. While the C5 hydroxyl group is the actual site of phosphorylation by ATP, our mutagenesis studies revealed that nearly all residues stabilizing other parts of the ribose ring are equally essential. Alanine scanning identified three ribose-coordinating residues (D27, K54, and N57) as essential for catalysis. N27 anchors the sugar through dual H-bonds to the C2 and C3 hydroxyls; K54 bridges the ether oxygen and the C5 hydroxyl, positioning ribose and electrostatically stabilizing negative‐charge build-up on the γ-phosphate during the transition state; and N57 secures the C3 hydroxyl. Disrupting any of these contacts distorts substrate geometry and blocks efficient phosphoryl transfer to C5 of ribose. Biophysical characterization ruled out global misfolding or altered assembly as the source of catalytic loss. The far-UV CD spectra of the RK mutants overlaid well with the CD profile of WT RK, confirming the integrity of their secondary structure. Additionally, MP showed that all variants remained predominantly dimeric, with only the WT enzyme exhibiting a minor population of higher-order oligomers. Thus, the observed loss of activity was caused not by misfolding or altered oligomerization but by localized disruptions in the ribose-binding pocket. These observations redefine our understanding of substrate recognition in RK by indicating that effective catalysis depends on the global stabilization of the sugar’s orientation within the active site, rather than solely on the positioning of the reactive hydroxyl group.

To evaluate the stability of ribose binding, MD simulations were performed to monitor the distances between key coordinating residues and the ribose hydroxyl groups. In WT RK, ribose remained stably bound throughout the 150 ns simulation. By contrast, ribose dissociated from E154R RK within 25 ns, from E154A and A181K after ∼ 80 ns, and from N57A within 120 ns (Supplementary Video). Notably, the D27A mutant maintained stable coordination of ribose throughout the simulation, despite transitioning to an open conformation earlier than any other RK variant analyzed. Together, these findings demonstrate that RK catalytic function depends on a tightly tuned interplay between conformational gating and sustained substrate coordination. Mutations that impair either domain motion or sugar-binding contacts destabilize the active site architecture, ultimately disrupting catalysis.

Structural alignment of the sequences of RK and KHK-A revealed that in KHK-A, an asparagine replaces K54 of RK. Introducing the K54N substitution in RK inactivated the enzyme, underscoring that lysine’s longer, positively charged side chain is required to span the distance to the C5 hydroxyl and stabilize the transition state—an interaction that the shorter asparagine cannot maintain. Similarly, we introduced the A181K substitution with the aim of creating an extra H-bond to the sugar substrate, as a lysine in KHK-A occupies the position equivalent to Ala181 in RK. Instead, the mutant retained only 14 % of WT activity. The loss of activity was likely due to the introduction of a bulky, positively charged lysine side chain into the tightly packed ribose-binding pocket, which may have disrupted the local environment by causing steric clashes and backbone rearrangements that ultimately distorted the active-site architecture. These mutational results underscore a single principle: RK’s substrate specificity hinges on a rigorously defined 3D arrangement of active-site residues. Even conservative substitutions—those that preserve charge or H-bonding capacity—disrupt activity because they alter the precise geometry required for ribose recognition. Although RK shares the canonical PfkB fold, the precise positioning of side chains, such as N27, K54, and N57, fine-tunes the pocket to accept only ribose. The K54N variant illustrates this point vividly: asparagine’s shorter side chain cannot reach the ribose C5 hydroxyl, breaking a critical contact and abolishing catalysis. Thus, RK’s stringent spatial demands, rather than global structural differences, confer unique substrate specificity.

Kinetic values for human RK determined here (KRib = 0.28 mM and KATP = 0.07 mM) match those reported for E. coli RK (Park et al., 2007). Among the RK mutants, only E154A RK retained partial activity (∼58 % of WT). E154 makes a relatively long H-bond (4.5 Å) to the C1 hydroxyl of ribose, and its replacement with alanine increased KRib by 13.5-fold and KATP by 3.5-fold and reduced catalytic efficiency by 28-fold, indicating that E154 helps bind both substrates. Although E154 does not directly interact with ATP, the increase in KATP suggests that it plays a role in maintaining local conformations critical for ATP binding and maintaining proper local conformations around the RK active site. E154A RK also exhibited significant thermal destabilization, a 9 °C decrease in both Tm and IT1/2, and a five-fold shorter kinetic half-life at 70 °C (12 min vs 64 min for WT RK). This demonstrates that E154 is functionally coupled to both catalytic activity and structural stability. Despite its compromised thermal stability, E154A RK retained enzymatic activity, suggesting that the destabilization is localized to the catalytic pocket and that its intramolecular contacts are important for thermal stability but play a limited role in catalysis.

In DSC analysis, all mutants showed a marked reduction in ΔHcal relative to WT RK (Fig. 5D). These decreases reflect a loss of polar bonding interactions and increased hydrophobic exposure, indicative of weakened intramolecular stabilization and an altered folding energy landscape (Arntfield and Murray, 1981, Ferreira et al., 2022, Nawaz et al., 2018, Privalov and Khechinashvili, 1974). These disruptions are particularly relevant in the ribose-binding network, where precise side chain interactions appear essential not only for catalysis but also for maintaining the structural integrity of the catalytic pocket. Changes at the secondary structure level, though subtle, were evident in the CD spectra. All mutants exhibited reduced ellipticity at 208 nm compared to WT, consistent with slight alterations in helical content. Notably, the local elliptical minimum at 208 nm was more pronounced in E154A than in WT, reinforcing the conclusion that even catalytically competent variants may adopt a perturbed fold that compromises their thermal resilience.

The broader metabolic and therapeutic significance of RK stems from its finely tuned role in ribose metabolism (Addis et al., 2012, Coley et al., 2013, Kreider et al., 2003, Li et al., 2021, Omran et al., 2003). As the enzyme responsible for ribose phosphorylation and the initiation of its metabolism, RK serves as a critical entry point into the PPP, thereby contributing directly to nucleotide biosynthesis, cellular energy production, and redox homeostasis (Addis et al., 2012, Coley et al., 2013, Kreider et al., 2003, Li et al., 2021, Omran et al., 2003). Given its central role in energy metabolism, RK activity holds significant relevance to a range of diseases associated with impaired energy metabolism and ATP production (Hellsten et al., 2004, Kerksick et al., 2005, Peveler et al., 2006, Seifert et al., 2017), including heart disease (Bayram et al., 2015, Pauly and Pepine, 2000), fibromyalgia, and chronic fatigue syndrome (Teitelbaum et al., 2006). D-Ribose supplementation has been proposed as a therapeutic strategy to restore ATP levels, particularly following ischemic stress or intense physical exertion. Several studies suggest that D-ribose may help replenish ATP stores in muscle cells post-exercise, although findings remain inconclusive (Hellsten et al., 2004, Kerksick et al., 2005, Kreider et al., 2003, Peveler et al., 2006, Seifert et al., 2017). However, the efficacy of such interventions depends on the efficient phosphorylation of D-ribose, a reaction catalyzed exclusively by RK. This positions RK as a potential therapeutic gatekeeper. Understanding the structural basis for RK’s activity, specificity, and stability is therefore crucial.

In summary, human RK achieves catalytic efficiency through a coordinated ribose-binding network governed by a highly cooperative and evolutionarily refined mechanism of substrate recognition. Our comprehensive biochemical, mutational, and computational analyses demonstrate that this efficiency depends on precise side-chain geometry, asymmetric protomer dynamics modulated by the β-clasp motif, and spatial-electrostatic complementarity within the active site. Remarkably, even subtle perturbations—whether proximal or distal to the ribose pocket—can result in significant functional losses, highlighting the enzyme’s finely tuned structural integrity. Central to this architecture are the β-clasp dimerization interface, the dynamic lid domain, and an extensive H-bonding network, which collectively stabilize the catalytic pocket and facilitate ribose phosphorylation. These findings redefine our understanding of substrate specificity in human RK and provide an integrative framework linking structural features, binding dynamics, and thermal resilience. This mechanistic insight explains RK’s strict selectivity for D-ribose—a property likely evolved to optimize flux into nucleotide and NADPH-generating pathways—and offers a foundation for future investigations, including probing RK regulation under metabolic stress, mapping disease-associated mutations, engineering substrate-specific variants for synthetic biology, and developing small-molecule inhibitors for therapeutic use. Together, these advances position human RK as a key target for both biomedical and biotechnological applications.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction and protein purification

The genes encoding WT and mutant RK with an N-terminal Hisx6 tag were introduced into the pET-28b (+) bacterial expression vector by GenScript, Inc. (Piscataway, NJ). For crystallographic studies, the first 10 N-terminal residues of RK were truncated to enhance crystal formation and diffraction quality. The vectors were used to transform E. coli BL21-CodonPlus-RIL (Stratagene) for protein expression. To express the target protein, 100 mL of overnight culture grown in Luria-Bertani medium was used to inoculate 1.8 L of Terrific Broth medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin and chloramphenicol at 37 °C. When the OD600 reached ∼ 3.0, protein expression was induced by adding 1.0 mM IPTG, the temperature was reduced to 15 °C, and the cells were allowed to grow overnight before harvesting and flash-freezing. The cell pellets were suspended in binding buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, 3 mM β-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (0.1 mM benzamidine-HCl and 0.1 mM phenylmethyl sulfonyl fluoride). The resuspended cells were lysed by sonication. The lysate was then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was loaded onto a 3-mL Ni-NTA column (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) that had been equilibrated with the same binding buffer at 4 °C. The Ni-NTA column was washed with 150 mL of wash buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 3 mM β-mercaptoethanol), and the protein was eluted with 15 mL of elution buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 3 mM β-mercaptoethanol). The protein was further purified by passage over a gel filtration column [Superdex 200 pg (16/600) (Cytiva, Marlborough, Massachusetts)] pre-equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP. All proteins were concentrated using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts) to a final concentration of 3 mg/mL for biochemical assays and 10 mg/mL for crystallization trials. Protein concentrations were measured using a Bradford assay (Bradford, 1976) (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California), and the purity of the proteins was judged to be > 95 % based on SDS-PAGE analysis.

Determination of oligomeric state by circular dichroism and mass photometry

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was performed to confirm the structural fold and determine the secondary structure of WT and mutant RK. CD scans of the RK variants were collected in 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) in the 200–260 nm wavelength range at a scanning rate of 1 nm/s. The protein concentration was 0.4 mg/mL, and the samples were scanned in 1-mm quartz cuvettes at 25 °C in a Chirascan CD spectrometer (Applied Photophysics, Surrey, England). The spectrometer was calibrated with aqueous camphor-10-sulfonic acid.

Mass photometry (MP) measurements of RK variants were performed using a TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK) at room temperature. Microscope cover slips (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK) were cleaned with Milli-Q water and isopropanol and dried with a clean nitrogen stream. Reusable six-well silicone gaskets (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK) were mounted to the center of the cover slip. An 18-µL drop of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to a well to allow focusing of the lens on the glass surface. To enable MP mass measurements, contrast-mass calibration was performed by measuring isolated solutions of monomeric creatine kinase (47 kDa), bovine serum albumin (66 kDa, 132 kDa, and 198 kDa), and alcohol dehydrogenase (150 kDa) in PBS pH 7.4. A total of five data points were used for mass calibration—achieving a linear relationship with an R2 of 0.9993.

The distribution of light-scattering events, expressed as interferometric contrast, was Gaussian-fitted using DiscoverMP software (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK). Contrast values were converted to masses by establishing a linear relationship between the contrast and mass of the binding object from the standard set of proteins. The accepted R2 values of the standard curves were set to > 0.999. Protein samples were diluted to 100 nM in PBS (Wu and Piszczek, 2021). The acquisition time for each data collection was fixed to 60 s. Data were collected using AcquireMP software (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK) and further processed using DiscoverMP (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK), which yielded a distribution histogram of the number of counts of each contrast value (or mass after calibration). The number of binding events from each run was always greater than 3000. Kernel density estimation (KDE) was applied to the counts to convert the histogram into a curve.

Crystallization and structural determination

Crystals of WT RK with 5 mM ADP and 5 mM MgCl2 were grown by sitting-drop vapor diffusion in 40 % PEG 550 MME at 18 °C. The crystals reached a size of approximately 100 μm within one to two days. Crystals grew in clusters. Diffraction data were collected on a Rigaku FR-E with a Rigaku RAXIS image plate at a wavelength of 1.5418 Å, and data were processed with the HKL2000 suite (Otwinowski and Minor, 1997). The crystal structure was solved by molecular replacement using Phaser in the CCP4 program suite—using E. coli RK (PDB: 1RKD) as the search model (Sigrell et al., 1998). The structure was refined with REFMAC5. The treatment for B-factors was isotropic, and the dimer was in the asymmetric unit. The model was deposited with PDB code 2FV7. Figures were prepared with the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, version 1.8, Schrödinger LLC.

Molecular dynamics simulations of WT and mutant RK

A crystal structure of the doubly occupied RK homodimer in which the two protomers are bound with ATP and ribose is not available. Therefore, we constructed the homology model of the protein using human RK (PDB ID: 6WK0) in complex with ribose and adenosine 5′-(β,γ-methylene)triphosphate (AMP-PCP). In the crystal structure, the two protomers have different occupancies: one protomer is bound to sugar in a closed conformation, while the other protomer is bound only to the ATP analogue AMP-PCP in an open conformation. This prevented the direct transfer of the sugar to the empty protomer, and we instead replaced the apo-protomer with the sugar-occupied one. AMP-PCP in both protomers was converted to ATP by changing the carbon atom between the β − and γ − phosphates to oxygen. To model single mutant forms of RK, namely, D27A, N57A, E154A, E154R, and A181K, we used the Maestro module of Schrödinger (Schrödinger, 2025). The protonation state of the protein was adjusted to pH 7 using the ProteinPrep tool of Schrödinger (Schrödinger, 2025). The systems were solvated and neutralized using the Solution Builder of the CHARMM-GUI server (Jo et al., 2017). Charmm36m (Huang et al., 2017) and TIP3P (Mark and Nilsson, 2001) were used to model protein and water, respectively. The studied systems were neutralized with 0.15 M KCl.

WT and mutant systems were simulated using the GROningen Machine for Chemical Simulations (GROMACS) package (Van Der Spoel et al., 2005). We used the steepest descent minimization algorithm (Wardi, 1988) to remove any possible atomic clashes in the system. We then performed an equilibration run in the NVT ensemble for 125 ps prior to the production run. The temperature and pressure were maintained at target values (310 K and 1 atm, respectively) using the Nose-Hoover thermostat (Kleinerman et al., 2008) and Parrinello-Rahman barostat (Martoňák et al., 2003), respectively. The LINCS algorithm (Hess et al., 1997) was used to constrain H-bonds during minimization, equilibration and the production run. We performed three replicates for each of the systems, each starting with a different velocity distribution. The velocities and coordinates of atoms were saved every 10 ps during simulations.

State-specific information analysis (SSI)

The distance between the Cα atoms of residues was determined by SSI analysis to examine the opening and closing mechanism of the enzyme. The binding site residues were in contact with the ribose if the distance between the sugar and coordinating residues was < 6 Å. The coordinating residues were determined by referencing the crystal structure. In SSI, ISSIxf reports on the degree of conformational state transitions of a given feature xf, providing information about ensembles pertaining to iandj or the transitions between them, as shown in Eq. 1(Vögele et al., 2025).ISSIxf=∑s∈Sxfe∈i,jPs,elogps,epspe(1)Sxf represents the transformation of the combined data points across ensembles iandj into data points referring to a feature state identifier s derived from the discretized probability distribution of xf. The variable e is a generic representation of the ensemble ID with possible values iorj; ps,e is the joint distribution of a feature state of xf and the ensemble state, and psandpe are marginal distributions. In SSI analysis, 1 bit represents the maximal shared information, whereas 0 bit represents no shared information between the ensemble and the feature.

Initial velocity and thermal inactivation studies

The RK reaction rate was measured spectroscopically using a pyruvate kinase and lactate dehydrogenase (PK/LDH) coupled reaction by monitoring NADH consumption from the PK/LDH reaction at 340 nm (ε = 6220 M−1 cm−1) using a Cytation 5 multi-mode microplate reader (BIOTEK Instruments, Winooski, Vermont). Enzyme titration experiments were performed at fixed substrate concentrations of 3 mM ATP and 1 mM ribose. In the kinetic studies, the enzymatic reaction contained 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 2 mM MgCl2, 150 mM KCl, 0.2 mM NADH, 0.3 mM phosphoenolpyruvic acid (PEP), 0.4 U/µl PK, and 1.2 U/µl LDH at different concentrations of ribose and ATP ranging from 0.5 to 10 times Km. The RK reaction was monitored in a 96-well microplate at 37 °C for 10 min at an absorbance wavelength of 340 nm. All data were plotted in double-reciprocal form to determine the quality of the data and then fitted using Eq. (2) for a sequential mechanism:v=VmaxABKaKb+KbA+KaB+AB#2where v and Vmax are the initial and maximum velocities, respectively, A and B are the substrate concentrations, and Ka and Kb are the Michaelis constants for substrates A and B, respectively. Data were fitted using global fitting analysis in the kinetics module of SigmaPlot (Systat Software, Inc. San Jose, California, U.S.A., www.sigmaplot.com).

The half-inactivation temperature (IT1/2) was determined from the remaining enzymatic activity of WT or E154A RK after incubating the enzyme at different temperatures from 40 °C to 80 °C for 5 min. A fixed volume of heat-treated 300 nM WT or E154A RK was assayed at 37 °C in a mixture containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 2 mM MgCl2, 150 mM KCl, 0.2 mM NADH, 0.3 mM PEP, 0.4 U/µl PK, and 1.2 U/µl of LDH. RK activity was monitored for 10 min following the disappearance of NADH at 340 nm. Error bars were calculated from the triplicates of each reaction.

The half-life (t1/2), defined as the time required for enzymatic activity to decrease by half, was determined by monitoring residual enzymatic activity after incubation at fixed temperatures of 65 °C and 70 °C for specific time intervals of up to 1 h. WT and E154A RK were each incubated separately at 65 °C and 70 °C, and fixed volumes of enzyme were removed every 15 min for determination of residual activity using a coupled-enzyme assay as explained above. The reactions were initiated by adding enzyme aliquots to reaction mixtures pre-equilibrated at 37 °C, and activity was continuously monitored by following the production of NADH. The residual enzymatic activities were plotted as the percentage of initial activity (set at 100 %) versus incubation time. The residual activities of WT and E154A RK were analyzed using a first-order activity decay model. The enzymatic activity decay was fit to a first-order reaction (Eq. (3)) or single-phase exponential decay using Prism 6 in GraphPad Software:lnA=-kt+lnA0#3where A0 and A are the initial and remaining enzymatic activity at different time intervals, respectively; k is the rate constant for enzyme inactivation; and t is the incubation time. The half-life (t1/2) was calculated as ln2/k.

Thermodynamic analysis by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Calorimetric thermal unfolding measurements of WT and mutant RK were performed on a Nano-DSC (TA Instruments, New Castle, Delaware) at a protein concentration of 0.6 mg/mL in 20 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP. All samples were heated at a scanning rate of 1 °C/min from 20 °C to 100 °C at 5 atm in the presence or absence of a single substrate (3.3 mM ATP or 2 mM ribose). Background scans were collected in buffer (with or without substrates) under the same temperature and pressure conditions. The DSC thermograms were corrected by subtracting the corresponding buffer baseline and converted to plots of excess heat capacity (Cp) as a function of temperature. The melting temperature (Tm) was determined at the apex of the thermogram peak, and the calorimetric enthalpy (ΔHcal) was estimated from the area under the thermal transition and fitted using the Nano Analyzer™ Software package from TA Instruments.
