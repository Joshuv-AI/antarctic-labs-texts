An associate of suspicious axillary node and sentinel node in breast cancer to minimizing axillary surgery, prospective cohort study
Authors: Prakasit Chirappapha, Panuwat Lertsithichai, Lakkana Adireklarpwong, Supanat Kaeosuwan, Cholatip Wiratkapun, Sarunthorn Lapkittichot, Navalee Taerakul, Muntana Trimankha, Kanitha Nimitkul, Wiriya Pipatsakulroj, Thongchai Sukarayothin, Monchai Leesombatpaiboon, Ronnarat Suvikapakornkul, Yodying Wasuthit
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Arm morbidity and postoperative complications following sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) or axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) are common problems in patients with breast cancer. The de-escalating axillary surgery is increasing; however, there is a lack of patients with suspicious nodes. This study aimed to reduce the need for SLNB in suspicious lymph node cases.Methods:A prospective cohort study of women with invasive breast cancer who underwent surgery between January 2021 and December 2022. The study included two cohorts: Cohort A comprised patients with stage cT1-2N0-1M0 cancer who planned upfront surgery, while Cohort B included patients with stages cT1-4N2M0, cT3-4N1M0, or cT1-2N0-1M0 who received neoadjuvant systemic treatment. During the study, a clip was inserted into the suspicious lymph node on imaging to determine whether it could serve as a sentinel node and potentially replace SLNB or reduce the need for axillary surgery.Result:In cohort A, 22 surgeries were performed, while in cohort B, seven surgeries were performed. The median follow-up time was 15 months. In cohort A, 71% of the patients had cN0 disease, while 28% had cN1 disease. A suspicious node correlated to a sentinel node was noted in 66.67% of cohort A. The false-negative rate (FNR) was 14.28%. However, when the clip node removal procedure was performed instead of the sentinel node procedure, the FNR was 10%.Conclusion:In early breast cancer, suspicious nodes in imaging studies could not currently represent sentinel lymph nodes, and the FNR was still high. Nevertheless, more studies with larger populations will provide a better understanding due to the limited number of patients.Keywords: axillary lymph node dissection, breast cancer, sentinel lymph node biopsy, suspicious lymph node, targeted axillary dissection

The recent treatment for breast cancer on the axillary therapy is in the de-escalating surgery era after studies in the 1990s indicated no significant benefit of axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) and identified unnecessary lymph node removal1–3. The ALND has more morbidity compared to sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) in lymphedema, sensory loss, and shoulder motion4. In the study of node-negative cancer, the outcome between SLNB and ALND does not differ in recurrence or survival1–3.

In those of clinically T1-3N1 breast cancer receiving neoadjuvant systemic treatment (NST), recent years have seen data of dual techniques SLNB with achieving at least three sentinel nodes or targeted axillary dissection (TAD), which involves placing the clip into a biopsy-positive lymph node and removing the clip node, combined with performing sentinel lymph node biopsy after NST without ALND if no positive nodal metastasis is improving the accuracy and reducing the need for ALND5–8. TAD allowed for selective removal and improved the pathologic evaluation of residual nodal disease after chemotherapy. This led to an FNR of 4.2% for the TAD6. These methods promise to decrease postoperative complications without impacting survival rates, although long-term data is still awaited9.

The combination of mammograms, ultrasound, and fine needle aspiration (FNA) for axillary staging has significantly improved diagnostic accuracy10. However, most studies on lymph node surgery have either involved a low number of suspicious nodes or have not included patients with suspicious nodes detected from imaging.

This study aimed to reduce the need for SLNB in cases with suspicious lymph nodes. The study hypothesizes that if a suspicious node could be the sentinel lymph node (SLN), then SLNB may no longer be necessary for clinical N0 patients or those with FNA-proven negative nodes. Furthermore, for clinical N1 patients with FNA-proven positive nodes, preoperative clip node placement can help reduce postoperative complications by changing from ALND to TAD. The correlation between clipped nodes and SLN in patients with locally advanced breast cancer who underwent NST and had a clip inserted at a metastatic node before treatment supports the reliability and precision of clip placement in determining nodal status. Further node dissection may not be necessary if ALND specimens reveal no remaining nodes outside the TAD specimen. SLNB may be used instead of ALND in cases with low lymph node burden.

Methods

Study design and patient characteristics

A prospective cohort study of 28 women, aged more than 18 years, with breast cancer and had suspicious nodes detected by mammogram or ultrasound imaging underwent surgery between January 2021 and December 2022. Patients with prior cancer, ineligibility for FNA or CNB of the axilla, or allergies to nickel or titanium were excluded. A clip was inserted into the suspicious lymph node in all patients. Eligible patients were assigned to one of two cohorts: Cohort A included 21 patients with stage cT1-2N0-1M0 undergoing surgery upfront. Cohort B had eight patients with stages cT1-4N2M0, cT3-4N1M0, or early cT1-2N0-1M0 who planned to receive NST before surgery. All the patients provided written informed consent for participation. The Institutional Review Boards of Mahidol University provided this study’s ethical approval in full compliance with the International Guidelines for Human Research Protection.

Data on demographics, BMI, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, clinical and pathological staging, tumor size, and axillary node details were collected. Patients were categorized based on the type of surgery (mastectomy or breast-conserving) and the sentinel node tracing technique used (technetium-99m sulfur colloid (Tc-99), indocyanine green (ICG) or blue dye, palpable node, Tc-99 and blue dye, ICG and Tc-99, blue dye and ICG, or triple technique). The ALND procedure was performed as anatomical level I and II dissections.

To reduce selection bias, all eligible patients will be advised to attend the study by one surgeon who was not an attending surgeon and did not operate. The radiologist will do the imaging and place the clip before knowing the group. Cohorts A and B patients were assigned after obtaining all the information and planning for treatment. The attending surgeon responsible for operating the case will not know about the group until the surgery date. The work has been reported in line with the strengthening the reporting of cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies in surgery (STROCSS) (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MS9/A625) criteria11.

Definition

A suspicious axillary node is characterized by one of the following: size greater than 1 cm, abnormal round shape, abnormal hypoechoic cortex, cortical thickening (eccentric cortical thickening), increased blood flow (nonhilar vessels), abnormally high resistance flow, and total obliteration of the fat hilum, as seen on ultrasound or mammography by a radiologist.

The SLN that found the clip was a matched sentinel. Before surgery, a suspicious node identified on mammography or ultrasound was confirmed cytologically by FNA and placement of a clip.

The FNR was the number of negative clipped nodes, but the SLN was positive, and there was no information on the nodal from ALND.

Nodal imaging and clipping

Patients underwent mammograms and ultrasounds of the breast and bilaterally axilla in all cases to identify suspicious axillary lymph nodes by a breast radiologist. If found suspicious node, nodes were biopsied by FNA or core needle, and a U-shaped clip made of nickel-titanium, measuring 4.2×4.0 mm, was inserted into the biopsy node under ultrasound-guided and local anesthesia at the same time. In palpable nodes, FNA or core needle biopsy was performed, and a clip was inserted into this node regardless of the positive status of the lymph node before surgery.

Surgical management

During surgery, performed by breast surgical oncologists, the preferred approach was either mastectomy or breast-conserving surgery. The SLNB employed the TAD technique with isosulfan blue dye, Tc-99, and ICG for node mapping. Mapping agents were injected on the day or at the time of surgery, and a gamma probe in the Tc-99 setting was used to identify the hot node. All blue, hot, ICG, and palpable nodes were removed and labeled as SLN. Techniques like intraoperative ultrasonography or fluoroscopy helped locate the clip node.

In the case with negative FNA, the axillary procedures were SLNB. Intraoperatively, the surgeon first searched and identified the clipped node using the standard SLNB technique, then removed the SLN. All nodes containing blue dye, radioactivity, and palpability during surgery were removed and labeled as SLN. For some surgeons, intraoperative ultrasound was utilized to find the clip node in the axillary area before performing SLNB. If the clip cannot be found, a procedure, such as a specimen fluoroscopy or mammogram, will be performed to confirm the clip in the specimen. The surgeon might localize the clip using intraoperative fluoroscopy at the axillary area. If the clip node cannot be found, it was left in vivo.

ALND will be performed if FNA reveals a metastatic node. The surgeon must search for SLN, remove the clip node if found, and perform ALND. If the clip cannot be found, an additional technique such as intraoperative ultrasonography, intraoperative fluoroscopy, or specimen spot mammography may be employed. If the clip node could not be located, it was left in vivo (Fig. 1).

Pathologic evaluation

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned into 5 µm sections and stained with H&E for evaluation by a pathologist. The pathology of the clip node is provided separately in the pathology report.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was determining whether a suspicious node was SLN by correlating the suspected axillary nodes with SLN in both cohorts. Secondary endpoints included the accuracy of FNA of suspicious nodes and loss of clip rate.

Statistical analysis

The χ 2 test was used to compare two or more variables. The student t-test was utilized for continuous data with normal distribution. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous data with non-normal distributions. Wilcoxon signed-rank test and rank-sum test were utilized between two related samples. CIs were computed using a 95% confidence. P-values of 0.05 or less were considered significant. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value, including the area under the ROC curve, were used to identify the accuracy of FNA. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 16.

Treatment results

Sentinel matching and loss clip rate

In all populations, 20 procedures (71.43%) found a correlation between SLN and suspicious nodes (matched SLNs). A loss clip was observed in 17.24%. Match sentinel was noted in 13 (61.9%) surgeries in cohort A, while loss clips were reported in 5 (23.81%) procedures. While there was no clip loss in cohort B, a match sentinel was seen in 6 (85.17%) surgeries (Table 1).

Matched versus nonmatched sentinel

The clinical or pathological stage or subtype was the same in all individuals. The median tumor size was 2.2 cm in the matched group and 2.6 cm in the nonmatch group (SDC Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/MS9/A626). In cohort A, the median tumor size was 1.8 cm in the match group and 2.3 cm in the nonmatch group. The lymph node was palpable in 15.3 and 37.5% of the match and nonmatch groups, although this was not statistically significant. We also observed a loss clip with five cases that did not match. The FNA clip node was the sentinel node in all instances in the match group. More techniques were used in the nonmatch group to identify the clip node (Table 2).

Accuracy of FNA and clip suspicious nodes

In cohort A, the accuracy of FNA and clip suspicious nodes with SLNB results was 14.29%, sensitivity was 71.4% (95% CI: 29–96.3), specificity was 100% (95% CI: 73.5–100), and the FNR was 14.28% (Table 3). There were two cases with FNA clip node negative but final SLN positive. The area under the curve (AUC) of FNA and the clip suspicious node in cohort A was 0.8571(Fig. 2). The power of the study was 0.85.

Recurrence, survival, and complication

In the entire population, there was no metastasis or recurrence. All patients remain alive. There were no recorded cases of complications or surgical site infections.

Discussion

In patients with suspicious nodes, there were limited studies about de-escalating surgery. In our study, sentinel matching occurred in 67.8% of cases in cohort A, with a high FNR of 14% found via the FNA of the clip node. FNR could be lowered to 10% by removing just one specific node, though the sample size was small. Moreover, the impact of targeted removal of clip nodes on arm morbidity remains unclear, and the previous breast cancer studies had generally limited SLN removal to 1–2 nodes12–15. Due to the study’s limitations, including its small size, being single-center, and having a brief follow-up, it concluded that current SLNB practices should continue but noted a trend towards lower FNR with FNA clip nodes and sentinel negative cases. The study promotes further research to minimize axillary surgery and decrease related arm morbidity.

The SOUND trial explored the outcome of omission SLNB in EBC, cT1N0M0, suitable for breast-conserving surgery, and negative US finding or one suspicious axillary node with a negative biopsy. After 5 years, there were no significant differences in distant disease-free survival (DDFS) or overall survival (OS) between the observation group and those who underwent SLNB16. However, this study was consistent with some of the individuals in our study population who had small tumors but were observed to have suspicious lymph nodes. Unfortunately, because it did not meet our inclusion criteria, our study cannot be applied to patients without questionable nodes.

Current trends in axillary surgery lean towards less invasive methods. For cN1 patients downstaged to cN0 post-NST, both the SLNB dual technique (extracting more than two sentinel lymph nodes) and TAD are considered acceptable. The dual technique has a FNR of 5–11%, which TAD can potentially lower to below 5%5–8. Issues with clip detection highlight the need for precise procedural planning6,17–19. In cohort B, initial node positivity was common, with no FNR among suspicious FNA nodes, indicating high accuracy of FNA in that setting. Clip detection was successful in all instances. The proposal is that TAD could be used in patients with FNA positivity and minimal or no remaining LN disease after clip placement. However, challenges include undetectable FNA-positive LNs or clip nodes positioned away from the sentinel area, with 7.14% of patients ending up with pN3 status. The limited patient numbers make it hard to compare these findings to previous studies.

A meta-analysis and the SOUND trial examined the effectiveness of ultrasound-guided FNA (US-FNA) and CNB in evaluating suspicious nodes. The meta-analysis showed US-FNA had a sensitivity of 79%, specificity of 96, which improved with CNB to a sensitivity of 85%, specificity of 93. The SOUND trial highlighted variability in detected LN involvement sensitivity (24–94%) and an FNR of 13.7% in EBC with small axillary nodes16,20. Our study on two cohorts using US- FNA found cohort A had a sensitivity of 71.4%, specificity of 100%, FNR of 14.28, whereas cohort B showed a sensitivity of 100.0%, specificity of 50%, FNR of 60 (SDC Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/MS9/A627). Our study’s limitations include a small sample size and differences in ultrasound criteria for suspicious nodes, leading to lower sensitivity compared to previous findings. The FNRs in our study were comparable to the SOUND trial. However, SOUND included patients with normal-looking lymph nodes, indicating a need for consistent criteria in evaluating suspicious nodes.

The study addressed challenges in clip retrieval during surgery, noting that clips could sometimes not be located. In instances, clips were found distant from the SLN but within axillary level 1, attributed to possible clip migration or initial placement outside the SLNs. Despite a reported loss clip rate of up to 30%, there was no impact on local recurrence (LR), OS, or disease-free survival (DFS)6,17–19. Various methods like fluoroscopy, wire localization, I-125 seeds, tattooing, magnetic seeds, and scout radar have been explored to reduce clip loss, though costs limit their suitability for all patients21–26.

If the clipped node was identified as the SLN, it was confirmed to be accurate. However, if it was not the SLN, accuracy may decrease if the result of the clipped suspicious node differs from the SLNB result. In our small population study, potential factors causing FNR in cohort A might include the clipped node not being the SLN, the positive node being in another node that was not the SLN, loss of the clip, and pT3, which tended to cause nonmatching sentinel nodes.

Currently, while the study provides valuable insights into the challenges of axillary staging in breast cancer, it reaffirms the current standard practice of SLNB in clinically node-negative patients and ALND in selected cases. In patients with clinically node-positive cancer, ALND is the standard procedure for cN2-3 or for cN1 converted to cN0, those ineligible to undergo SLNB using the dual technique and achieve at least three SLN or using the TAD technique. If the patient can undergo SLNB using the dual technique and achieve at least three SLN or use the TAD technique with a positive SLN result, ALND will still be the standard and must be performed next. Until further evidence is available, clinicians should continue following established guidelines for axillary surgery in breast cancer treatment.

There were many limitations of this study. The main limitation was the study’s sample size, which was small, particularly in cohort B, which was relatively small, which may limit the statistical power of the findings and the generalizability of the findings. A larger sample size would provide more robust evidence. The imaging study’s validity was that the axillary lymph node ultrasound was an operator-dependent procedure and may not be performed in all hospitals. Although there is a correlation between suspicious nodes on imaging and sentinel nodes, the FNR remains high, mainly when relying solely on the FNA. The impact of clip node removal in cN0 with a suspicious node that potentially decreases the FNR to 10% must be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample size. The FNR result may be the limit and bias that we do not accurately know all node information as standard FNR that uses ALND nodal status, which may cause the FNR to be low. Our study cannot determine the effects on LR, OS, or DFS due to limited follow-up time, making it impossible to evaluate the impact of clip loss on axillary recurrence at this stage. Additionally, if future longer follow-up reveals over 10% FNR without affecting LR and survival rates, reducing it may not be necessary.

Further research with larger populations is recommended to validate these results and explore the feasibility of clip node removal as an alternative to SLNB. The article needs long-term data, and more than the median follow-up time of 15 months may be required to assess long-term outcomes. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct studies with extended follow-up periods to evaluate the impact of different surgical approaches on long-term outcomes such as nodal recurrence, disease-free survival, and overall survival.

Conclusion

In the suspicious node detected EBC patient, the use of an imaging study to replace SLNB or the use of only clipped nodes retrieval in biopsy positive node patient might not be valid and should not be use at this moment. The sentinel lymph node biopsy still be the standard procedure. More studies with larger populations and longer follow-up will provide a better understanding and solve the limit of study.
