Clinical study on the pathogenic risks of different genotypes of high-risk HPV infection and multiple infections
Authors: Meiyu Song, Minhong Mao, Huirong Zhao, Chen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the oncogenic risks of distinct high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) genotypes and whether multiple infections exacerbate pathogenicity, with analysis of age stratification and viral load impact.MethodsClinical and pathological data from 2,525 patients undergoing colposcopy-directed biopsy for cervical abnormalities (2020–2023) were analyzed. HPV genotyping (18 types) and viral load quantification (Ct-values) were performed using PCR-membrane hybridization. Histopathology (CC/LSIL/HSIL/SCC) was evaluated by blinded experts. Statistical analyses included age stratification (< 35 vs. ≥35 years) and multivariate adjustment for viral load (Ct ≤ 30).ResultHR-HPV single-type infections predominated (1,774 cases, 70.26%), with genotype distribution: 16 (17.98%), 52 (10.50%), 58 (7.88%), 53 (4.63%), and 18 (4.55%). Multiple infections occurred in 474 cases (18.77%). Versus HPV-negative/low-risk controls, the highest pathogenic risks were: type 16 (OR = 7.96, 95% CI:5.41–11.71), type 58 (OR = 5.80, 95% CI:3.75–8.99), multiple infections (OR = 5.02, 95% CI:3.42–7.37), type 18 (OR = 4.86, 95% CI:2.95–8.02), and type 35 (OR = 4.07, 95% CI:1.82–9.10) (all P = 0.001). Age ≥ 35 years independently increased HSIL + risk (OR = 2.16, 95% CI:1.62–2.88, P = 0.001), with HPV16/18/35 showing significantly higher ORs in this group. High viral load (Ct ≤ 30) independently predicted HSIL + progression (OR = 2.98, 95% CI:1.92–4.63, P = 0.001). Multiple infections did not increase risk for genotypes 16/18/33/35/52/56/58/68 versus single infections, except for HPV51 (P = 0.01).ConclusionGenotype 16 demonstrates the strongest oncogenic potential, with pathogenic hierarchy: 16 > 58 > 18 > 35. Multiple infections do not synergistically increase risk for dominant genotypes. Age ≥ 35 years and high viral load (Ct ≤ 30) independently elevate cervical lesion severity, supporting their integration into risk-stratified screening protocols.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: High-risk human papillomavirus, Multiple infections, Cervical lesions, Pathogenic risks

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a critical global health challenge, ranking as the fourth most common malignancy and cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide. According to 2023 GLOBOCAN estimates, approximately 640,000 new cases and 340,000 deaths occur annually, with over 90% of these burdens concentrated in low- and middle-income countries [1]. The disease disproportionately impacts younger women, causing substantial years of life lost and socioeconomic disruption [2].

Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) is the necessary cause of cervical carcinogenesis, implicated in >99.7% of cases [3]. To date, over 200 HPV genotypes have been identified, of which 12–14 are classified as high-risk (e.g., HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 52, 58) based on their oncogenic potential [4]. The carcinogenic mechanism involves viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 disrupting critical tumor suppressor pathways: E6 degrades p53 via ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, while E7 inactivates retinoblastoma protein (pRb), collectively driving uncontrolled cell proliferation and genomic instability [5]. Notably, HR-HPV genotypes exhibit hierarchical carcinogenicity. HPV16 and 18 dominate global cervical cancer etiology, accounting for 70% of cases [6]. However, genotype distribution and associated risks vary geographically. In Asia, genotypes 52 and 58 demonstrate unexpectedly high prevalence and oncogenic activity [7].

The clinical significance of multiple HR-HPV infections remains contentious. While some studies suggest synergistic oncogenic effects due to increased viral load and immune evasion [8], others report competitive viral interference attenuating individual genotype risks [9]. This paradox necessitates population-specific investigations given regional variations in HPV genotype distribution and host genetics.

This study analyzed 2,525 women with abnormal cervical screening (TCT + HPV DNA) undergoing colposcopy-directed biopsy at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital (2020–2023). Through PCR genotyping of 18 h-HPV types, viral load quantification (Ct-values), and blinded histopathological review, we aimed to: (1) Compare pathogenic risks across prevalent Beijing genotypes (HPV16/18/52/58/53), addressing regional variations in oncogenic potential; (2) Determine whether multiple HR-HPV infections (≥ 2 types) synergistically increase progression to HSIL/SCC compared to single-type infections. Analyses incorporated age stratification (< 35 vs. ≥35 years) and adjusted for viral load thresholds (Ct ≤ 30) to evaluate effect modification. The study seeks to clarify genotype-specific carcinogenicity and co-infection impacts in Chinese women, informing risk-adapted screening protocols.

Methods

Study population

A consecutive cohort of 2,525 patients with cervical inflammation or lesions undergoing colposcopy-directed biopsy was enrolled at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University (Shijingshan Campus) between April 2020 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) abnormal cervical screening (ASCUS + cytology and/or HR-HPV positivity); (2) histopathological classification into chronic cervicitis (CC), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL), or squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) per WHO guidelines; (3) complete clinical records. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pregnancy/lactation; (2) prior cervical surgery/conization, hysterectomy, or pelvic radiotherapy. The study protocol was approved by the hospital ethics committee (No. CYLL-2020-038), with written informed consent obtained.

Specimen collection and processing

Cervical exfoliated cells were collected by gynecologists using specialized HPV samplers (Hybribio, China). Patients abstained from sexual activity (24 h) and vaginal agents (72 h) pre-procedure. After speculum insertion and cervical cleansing, cells were harvested from the ectocervix and endocervical canal, stored in preservation solution at 4 °C, and processed within 72 h.

HPV genotyping and viral load quantification

HR-HPV detection utilized PCR-membrane hybridization (HR-HPV Typing Kit, Kaipu Biotech, Chaozhou; Lot: C230403A), identifying 18 genotypes: 16,18,26,31,33,35,39,45,51,52,53,56,58,59,66,68,73,82. Viral load was quantified via cycle threshold (Ct) values from real-time PCR curves, with Ct ≤ 30 defining high viral load based on manufacturer’s linear range (1 × 10³–1 × 10⁸ copies/mL). Based on the manufacturer’s calibration curve (y = -3.32x + 42.07, R²=0.99), a Ct value of 30 corresponds to a viral load of ≥ 1 × 10⁵ copies/mL. All viral load measurements were performed in duplicate; samples with Ct value discrepancies > 1.0 cycle underwent repeat testing, and the mean Ct value was used for analysis.

Histopathological evaluation

Colposcopies were performed by two senior gynecologists blinded to HPV status, using Reid criteria to identify abnormalities (acetowhite epithelium, punctation, mosaicism, atypical vessels). Targeted biopsies were fixed in 4% neutral formaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned (4 μm), and H&E-stained. Pathological diagnoses (CC/LSIL/HSIL/SCC) were independently reviewed by two pathologists, with discrepancies resolved by a third expert. Cases were stratified as low-risk (CC/LSIL) or high-risk (HSIL/SCC) groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp, USA). Continuous variables (e.g., viral load Ct values) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey tests for multi-group comparisons. Categorical variables (e.g., HPV genotypes, pathological grades) were presented as frequencies (%) and analyzed by χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were employed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for evaluating associations between HPV infection status (genotype, multiplicity, viral load) and high-grade cervical lesions (HSIL/SCC), adjusting for age and pathological diagnosis. Age-stratified analyses were conducted by grouping patients into < 35 and ≥ 35 years. Viral load threshold (Ct ≤ 30) was predefined based on manufacturer validation. Statistical significance was set at two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Basic information

A total of 2525 subjects were selected for the study, including 277 cases (10.97%) of HPV-negative or low-risk types and 1774 cases (70.26%) of single virus infection with high-risk HPV types. The top 5 types were 16 (454 cases, 17.98%), 52 (265 cases, 10.50%), 58 (199 cases, 7.88%), 53 (117 cases, 4.63%), and 18 (115 cases, 4.55%). There were 474 cases (18.77%) of multiple infections with high-risk HPV types, including 318 cases (67.09% of mixed infections), and the top 5 types of dual infections were 16/18, 16/52, and 16/58, respectively. There were 156 cases (32.91% of mixed infections) of types 16/39 and 52/58, with three or more infections, as shown in Fig. 1.

Comparison of the pathogenic risk factors for different genotypes of HPV

Compared with HPV-negative or low-risk types overall, multiple or single infections of high-risk HPV significantly increased the risk of HPV disease (OR = 3.91, 95% CI: 2.76–5.55, P = 0.001). When high-risk HPV multiple infections or single infections were compared with HPV-negative or low-risk types, the top 5 pathogenic risk factors were type 16 (OR = 7.96, 95% CI: 5.41–11.71, P = 0.001), type 58 (OR = 5.80, 95% CI: 3.75–8.99, P = 0.001), high-risk multiple infections (OR = 5.02, 95% CI: 3.42–7.37, P = 0.001), type 18 (OR = 4.86, 95% CI: 2.95–8.02, P = 0.001), and type 35 (OR = 4.07, 95% CI: 1.82–9.10, P = 0.001), as shown in Table 1.

Age-stratified analysis of HR-HPV pathogenicity risk across cervical lesion severity

To further evaluate the impact of age on HPV pathogenicity, patients were stratified into two groups: <35 years (n = 1,218, 48.24%) and ≥ 35 years (n = 1,307, 51.76%). In the < 35-year group, the top 3 genotypes associated with HSIL + risk were HPV16 (OR = 5.41, 95% CI: 3.02–9.68, P = 0.001), HPV58 (OR = 4.37, 95% CI: 2.25–8.49, P = 0.001), and multiple infections (OR = 4.12, 95% CI: 2.41–7.04, P = 0.001). For the ≥ 35-year group, HPV16 exhibited significantly higher risk (OR = 10.85, 95% CI: 6.12–19.23, P = 0.001), followed by HPV18 (OR = 6.22, 95% CI: 2.95–13.11, P = 0.001) and HPV35 (OR = 5.94, 95% CI: 2.15–16.41, P = 0.001). Notably, multiple infections showed attenuated risk in older women (OR = 3.58, 95% CI: 1.99–6.45, P = 0.001). Age itself was an independent risk factor for HSIL+ (OR = 2.16, 95% CI: 1.62–2.88, P = 0.001) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Association between HR-HPV viral load and progression of cervical lesions

Viral load was quantified by PCR cycle threshold (Ct) values (lower Ct = higher load). HPV16/18 exhibited significantly higher viral loads in HSIL+ (mean Ct = 24.3 ± 3.1) compared to LSIL (Ct = 28.7 ± 2.8, P = 0.001) and CC (Ct = 31.6 ± 3.4, P = 0.001) (Table 4). For non-16/18 genotypes, high viral load (Ct ≤ 30) was associated with increased HSIL + risk (OR = 3.12, 95% CI:1.86–5.24, P = 0.001), particularly in multiple infections containing these types (OR = 4.05, 95% CI:2.11–7.78). Notably, in multiple infections with HPV16/18 co-present, viral load of 16/18 dominated lesion risk (16/18 Ct vs. co-type Ct: r = 0.87, P = 0.001), while non-16/18 high load alone did not increase risk (P = 0.214). Multivariate analysis confirmed high load (Ct ≤ 30) as an independent predictor for HSIL + after adjusting for age and genotype (OR = 2.98, 95% CI:1.92–4.63, P = 0.001) (Table 5).

Discussions

There are notable variations in HPV infection patterns across different countries and regions. In this study, the predominant high-risk HPV types identified were 16, 52, 58, 53, and 18. This finding aligns with previous reports on HPV infection characteristics in Beijing [10, 11] and broadly represents the HPV infection status in this area.

HPV, a double-stranded circular DNA virus, infects epithelial tissues. The persistent expression of the E6 and E7 oncoproteins from high-risk HPV types is a critical factor in the development of cervical cancer. During persistent infection, HPV in its integrated form transcribes significant amounts of E6 and E7 mRNA, leading to the production of the corresponding proteins. The E6 protein specifically binds to the p53 tumor suppressor protein via E6-associated protein (E6-AP), promoting its rapid degradation. This results in uncontrolled cell cycle progression, an effect equivalent to p53 gene mutation. The E7 protein exhibits high affinity for the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), causing dissociation of the E2F-pRb complex. This disruption in cell cycle control ultimately leads to precancerous lesions and cervical cancer through a cascade of molecular events [12–14]. However, the pathogenic potential varies significantly among different high-risk HPV genotypes [3, 4], with type 16 demonstrating the highest oncogenicity [15]. Importantly, infections with other high-risk HPV types also contribute substantially to the occurrence of precancerous lesions and cervical cancer [16], warranting significant clinical attention. Multivariate logistic regression analysis in this study revealed that, compared to HPV-negative individuals or those with low-risk HPV infections, the top five factors associated with increased disease risk were: type 16 (OR = 7.96), type 58 (OR = 5.80), high-risk multiple infections (OR = 5.02), type 18 (OR = 4.86), and type 35 (OR = 4.07). This risk hierarchy is consistent with earlier studies [3, 4].

A consensus is currently lacking on whether multiple HPV infections increase disease risk compared to single infections. Some studies propose that coinfections involve higher viral loads and can alter host immune status, potentially elevating the rate of persistent infection and consequently increasing the risk of HPV-related disease [17, 18]. Conversely, other reports suggest that interactions between different HPV subtypes might mean multiple infections do not elevate, or could even reduce, the risk or severity of disease [19–21]. This study indicates that multiple infections involving the high-risk types 16, 18, 33, 35, 52, 56, 58, and 68 do not confer a higher pathogenic risk compared to single infections with the respective individual types. Similarly, coinfection with HPV 16 and 18 does not increase risk relative to single infections with either HPV 16 or 18 alone. Notably, however, multiple infections including high-risk HPV type 51 were associated with a greater pathogenic risk than single infections with HPV 51. This may be attributable to the relatively lower intrinsic oncogenic potential of HPV 51 itself, coupled with the presence of other high-risk types with greater pathogenic potential in the coinfection scenario.

Our study confirms significant genotype-specific variations in HR-HPV pathogenicity, with HPV16 demonstrating the strongest oncogenic risk (OR = 7.96), consistent with global reports [10]. The novel age-stratified analysis revealed substantially elevated HSIL + risk for HPV16/18/35 in women ≥ 35 years (OR up to 10.85), aligning with Damgaard et al.‘s findings on persistent infection risks in older cohorts [15]. Crucially, viral load quantification showed HPV16/18 loads increased exponentially with lesion severity, mechanistically supporting E6/E7-mediated p53/pRb degradation pathways described by Mukherjee et al. [13]. While multiple infections showed no synergistic risk escalation for dominant genotypes, we observed that high viral load (Ct ≤ 30) independently predicted HSIL+ (OR = 2.98) after adjusting for confounders—a finding refining Gong et al.‘s model where load interacted with multiplicity [17]. Clinically, this underscores the necessity of age-specific genotyping and load assessment: older women with HPV16/18 require intensified surveillance, whereas non-16/18 carriers with Ct ≤ 30 warrant expedited colposcopy despite ambiguous genotypes. Limitations include single-center recruitment and undersampling of rarer genotypes, potentially masking their risk profiles as noted by Xu et al. [4]. Future multi-center studies should validate load thresholds across diverse populations.

Our findings directly inform risk-stratified management for cervical screening. In women ≥ 35 years, detection of HPV16/18/58 necessitates immediate colposcopy referral due to their sharply elevated oncogenic risk (OR: 4.95–10.85). For non-16/18 genotypes, high viral load (Ct ≤ 30) independently predicts HSIL + progression (OR = 2.98) and should trigger colposcopy even without high-risk genotyping, as viral load reflects biological aggressiveness more accurately than genotype alone in these cases. Conversely, multiple infections without HPV16/18/58 or high viral load may not justify escalated intervention. This triage strategy integrating age, targeted genotyping (16/18/58), and viral load thresholds, optimizes colposcopy referrals by prioritizing highest-risk cases while reducing overtreatment of transient infections. Standardized viral load assays are needed before routine implementation, but our data support piloting this approach in regions with high HPV52/58 prevalence.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center trial conducted in Beijing, geographical constraints may limit generalizability to populations with distinct HPV genotype distributions, such as rural areas or other ethnic groups. Second, the sample size restricted robust analysis of rare HR-HPV types (e.g., HPV31/39/45), potentially masking their true pathogenicity, as observed in larger cohorts. Third, viral load quantification relied on Ct values without standardization against international units (IU/mL), though our Ct ≤ 30 threshold aligns with recent Chinese multicenter validations. Future studies should: (1) establish multi-center cohorts covering diverse regions to validate age-stratified risk models; (2) standardize viral load reporting using WHO reference materials; (3) explore host immune biomarkers (e.g., CD4+/CD8 + ratio) that may modulate HPV pathogenicity in multiple infections.

Conclusion

In summary, HR-HPV genotypes exhibit hierarchical pathogenicity: HPV16 confers the highest oncogenic risk (OR = 7.96), followed by HPV58, multiple infections, HPV18, and HPV35. Critically, multiple infections do not synergistically increase disease risk for dominant genotypes (e.g., HPV16/18) but may elevate risk for lower-pathogenicity types when co-infected with high-risk partners. Age stratification reveals significantly amplified carcinogenicity in women ≥ 35 years, while viral load (Ct ≤ 30) independently predicts HSIL + progression. These findings advocate for risk-adapted cervical screening: prioritizing genotyping for HPV16/18/58 in older women and incorporating viral load assessment to refine management of non−16/18 infections.
