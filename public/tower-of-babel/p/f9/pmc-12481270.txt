Food security, climate change and mental health problems in a Colombian Indigenous paediatric population
Authors: Felipe Agudelo-Hernández, Marcela Guapacha-Montoya, María Camila Pinzón-Segura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the complex inter-relations among mental health, food security and climate anxiety in the Emberá-Dobidá paediatric Indigenous community in Caldas, Colombia. Although the community was displaced due to armed conflict, our focus is on the subsequent environmental changes driven by climate change that have emerged as independent stressors in the new territory. These stressors have compounded the community’s vulnerabilities, with particular impacts on children and youth. By framing territory not merely as geographic space but as a sociocultural and symbolic construct, the study adopts a Latin American critical perspective to analyse health disparities.MethodsA mixed-methods study was conducted involving the paediatric population aged 6–16 years (n=40) from the Emberá-Dobidá Indigenous community, along with 65 caregivers representing 25 families. In-depth interviews explored community perceptions of environmental changes, food security and mental health. A cross-sectional correlational analysis used two culturally adapted instruments, the Pediatric Assessment of Community Emotional and Spiritual Health Scale and the Colombian Household Food Security Scale, to assess paediatric mental health and food security.ResultsQualitative findings revealed two key themes: ‘Increased Heat in Recent Years’ and ‘Disconnection from Water and Cultural Identity’. Quantitative results showed significant correlations between food insecurity, spiritual disharmony and mental health problems, including emotional distress and suicide risk. Notably, 30% of children were at risk of suicide, and 50% exhibited emotional problems. The findings demonstrate that environmental degradation and disrupted territorial ties are not only ecological but also psychological and cultural stressors.ConclusionsMental health in Indigenous communities, understood as spiritual harmony, must be addressed through culturally grounded approaches that integrate traditional ecological knowledge, food security and the recovery of territorial connection. From a Latin American perspective, territory is not a passive backdrop but an active determinant of health shaped by power, memory and resistance. Our findings call for climate-resilient, community-driven interventions that reterritorialise public health responses in Indigenous settings.

Introduction

Climate change is increasingly recognised as a critical factor exacerbating socioeconomic and mental health challenges in Indigenous populations. For these communities, the land and environment are not just habitats but core parts of their identity, spirituality and cultural heritage.1 2 Disruptions caused by climate change, such as rising temperatures, altered rainfall patterns and environmental degradation pose direct threats to the health and well-being of Indigenous communities.35

This is particularly true for Latin American Indigenous communities who are confronting climate-related disruptions that undermine their traditional agricultural systems, causing widespread food insecurity and cultural erosion. Ongoing research highlights severe climate vulnerabilities in Brazil’s protected areas, inhabited predominantly by Indigenous populations, where biodiversity loss also threatens traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) and ways of life.2 3 6 These studies emphasise the interconnection between biodiversity conservation and Indigenous stewardship, which has been demonstrated to be a critical factor in mitigating the impact of climate change.

In this region, traditional ways of life are being transformed, inducing stress, anxiety and depression that are compounded by food insecurity due to climate-driven impacts on agriculture.69 As traditional food sources become unreliable, nutritional deficiencies become more prevalent, directly affecting children’s mental and physical health.8 10 11

This study examines how climate change, by altering the fundamental socioenvironmental conditions of the Emberá-Dobidá community, affects their cultural identity, food security and overall well-being. These environmental changes in the postdisplacement territory—distinct from the direct trauma of forced relocation—have led to increased mental health issues among children and young people (CYP), including heightened rates of suicide.2 7 8 By exploring the complex interplay between these elements, the study seeks to provide an expanded understanding of the cascading effects of climate change on Indigenous peoples.

Methods

This mixed-methods study (2023–2024) explores the relationships between climate change, food security and mental health within the Emberá-Dobidá community, also known as the River or Water People.1113 The research focused on how changes in climate patterns—increased temperatures, altered rainfall and environmental degradation—impact the traditional livelihoods and well-being of this Indigenous community, ultimately affecting mental health outcomes. The study encompassed both qualitative and quantitative phases. The qualitative phase involved in-depth interviews with CYP and caregivers to explore personal experiences with environmental changes, food security challenges and related mental health concerns. The quantitative phase employed a cross-sectional correlational design to assess impacts on the paediatric population.

Participants

In 2019, 119 individuals from the Emberá-Dobidá community were displaced from Alto Baudó-Chocó (Colombia) due to armed conflicts and resettled near a river polluted to the extent that it lacked fish and clean drinking water. By the time of the study, the population had increased to 158 inhabitants. The sudden resettlement provided a unique opportunity to examine the direct and indirect effects of environmental stressors on health outcomes. Our analysis specifically examines the impact of climate change, manifested as altered water quality and increased heat, on the community’s health—as environmental stressors distinct from the effects of relocation.

The study included 105 participants from the Emberá-Dobidá community: 40 children (6–16 years, mean age 9.23 years, 52.5% female) and 65 primary caregivers (mean age 32.5 years, 61.5% female), across 25 families. Among these 25 families, 15 completed both qualitative interviews and quantitative surveys, while 10 participated only in the quantitative phase.

Community members and leaders actively contributed to the design of quantitative instruments and the formulation of qualitative research questions, ensuring that the study was firmly grounded in local perspectives linking mental health with harmony of body, community and territory.14 The research also drew on comparative insights from other Latin American Indigenous groups and wove in TEK, particularly around resilient crop varieties and water-management practices, by adapting methods used in vulnerability assessments of Brazilian protected areas and food security studies among the Shawi of Peru.3 6 9

Instruments

The study employed multiple types of instruments to capture the multifaceted impacts of climate change. Specifically, qualitative interviews, demographic surveys and paediatric examinations were used to capture unique environmental and cultural experiences that standardised instruments might not fully address, as well as the health status of children. Validated quantitative instruments, that is, the Pediatric Assessment of Community Emotional and Spiritual Health (PACES) and Colombian Household Food Security Scale (CHFSS), were also used.

Interviews

In-depth interviews were conducted with CYP and their caregivers to investigate the impacts of climate change on their traditional agricultural practices, access to water and overall territorial connections. These semistructured interviews were facilitated by a psychiatrist and community youth, with discussions held in both Emberá and Spanish. Recordings were initially made in Emberá, a spoken but unwritten language, and later translated and transcribed into Spanish for analysis to ensure the community’s psychological and cultural responses to environmental changes were accurately captured. To focus the conversation, trigger questions, such as ‘How has the experience been moving from Chocó to Caldas? What positive aspects do you highlight? What negative aspects? What environmental changes have you noticed in recent months? And have any of these changes affected your well-being, that of your family, or your community’s relationship with the territory, and if so—how so?’ were incorporated into the interview protocol. These inquiries provided rich insights into the community’s lived experiences and perceptions, significantly enhancing our understanding of how environmental changes affect their cultural and social dynamics.

Sociodemographic and environmental perceptions survey

This section of the data collection instrument was divided into two subsections: (a) sociodemographic data, which collected age, gender, family composition and socioeconomic status to characterise the sample and (b) environmental and psychosocial perceptions of climate change, reasons for migration, current living conditions challenges, psychological impacts and effects on traditional community practices. This structure clarifies the distinction between baseline sociodemographic variables and those capturing symbolic and ecological relationships, providing a more precise contextual framework for analysis.

Paediatric assessment

A comprehensive clinical paediatric physical assessment was performed on all 40 children, examining anthropometric measures to evaluate risks of malnutrition and anaemia, as well as screening for respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms. The assessment also included oral and skin health, neurological development, signs of abuse and other general symptoms. From these data, a dichotomous variable ‘General Health Problems’ was created to indicate the presence or absence of any identified health issue.

Pediatric Assessment of Community Emotional and Spiritual Health Scale

Designed to assess key domains of paediatric mental health in Indigenous peoples, the PACES Scale comprises 42 items distributed across four domains: parenting (PA), which evaluates the quality of caregiver–child interactions; behaviour (C), which measures externalising and internalising behavioural problems; emotions (E), which assesses the spectrum of emotional states and distress and suicide risk (S), which gauges the potential risk of suicidal ideation or behaviours. Developed and validated collaboratively with Indigenous communities in Colombia, this scale employs a Likert-type format and is administered via audio recordings to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness. It demonstrates robust internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.911), with subscale scores calculated by summing item responses according to established thresholds.15

Colombian Household Food Security Scale

The CHFSS is a validated, low-cost and quick-to-administer instrument designed to assess levels of food insecurity in Colombian populations.16 In this study, it was applied to examine how recent environmental changes experienced by the Emberá-Dobidá community may have affected food access and dietary patterns. It comprises 12 items that capture a range of experiences related to food insecurity, distinguishing between adult and child dimensions. Adult-related items assess whether an adult ate less than desired, skipped meals or went to bed hungry due to insufficient funds for food. Child-related items inquire if a child missed meals, ate less than expected or experienced hunger. These items are arranged along a continuum reflecting increasing severity of food insecurity, thus providing a comprehensive evaluation of both the frequency and the intensity of food-related hardships. Additionally, the ethno-educator translated the CHFSS into Emberá to ensure culturally relevant and accurate responses.

Quantitative analysis

Data were analysed with SPSS V.26 for descriptive statistics, including means and SDs. Bivariate correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between mental health, food security, paediatric health problems and environmental changes. Our analysis is grounded in a theory-informed exploratory framework, using bivariate correlations to examine plausible relationships among key variables. This approach, sometimes referred to as a ‘hypothetical relational model’, is appropriate for small-sample, context-sensitive studies where the objective is to identify patterns of association rather than to infer causality.17 18 Given our small sample size and the focus on associations within a population, concerns about multicollinearity are minimised, and advanced dimensionality reduction techniques were not employed.

Qualitative analysis

Data were analysed using thematic analysis, in accordance with the checklist based on the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).19 This approach involved the initial coding of the data and the subsequent identification of themes that accurately captured the participants’ experiences and perspectives. N-VIVO, data analysis software for qualitative and mixed methods research, was used to perform open, axial and selective coding, which facilitated the systematic construction of the main thematic categories. In addition, interpretations were validated through participant verification, with the support of a translator, thereby reinforcing the credibility of the findings. This method allowed for a nuanced identification of recurrent themes related to the research topics, ensuring the analysis process was clearly documented and reproducible.20

The average age of the CYP participants was 9.23 years (53.7% females and 46.3% males), as detailed in table 1. 75% of families included both parents and children; 25% were single-mother households. In 25% of the latter, the fathers had been killed in Alto Baudó, Chocó. Reflecting the impact of community displacement, 62.5% of families reported recent unemployment, with reliance on natural resources, for example, agriculture or fishing, for subsistence. All families reported monthly incomes below 150 USD, corresponding to the lowest socioeconomic stratum in Colombia.

Paediatric health assessment

Significant health issues linked to environmental changes and food insecurity were identified. Among the children, 35% had dental caries, 30% palmar pallor, 30% scabies and 30% fever. Chronic malnutrition affected 50% of the population, and 20% had incomplete vaccination.

Mental health findings

The PACES Scale indicated that 30% of CYP were at risk of suicide, with 50% showing emotional problems. A notable proportion of CYP identified behavioural problems and parenting challenges, highlighting the influence of environmental and social factors on mental health.

Food security

The CHFSS revealed severe food insecurity, with significant proportions of families lacking sufficient food (97.5%) and money to purchase food (72.5%)—affecting CYP’s nutrition and overall family well-being (table 2). While both forced displacement and climate-driven environmental degradation have shaped current living conditions, this study focused on food insecurity as it relates specifically to recent environmental stressors in the new settlement area, such as loss of access to fishable rivers, agricultural land and traditional food sources.

The correlations presented in the table highlight the significant interplay between environmental and territorial stressors and indicators of spiritual disharmony in children. The data suggest that spiritual disharmony in this context emerges not from individual pathology but from the disintegration of collective, ecological and symbolic ties, particularly among children. Qualitative component

The qualitative analysis revealed deep insights into the community’s lived experiences of environmental changes. Two overarching themes emerged from the interviews: (1) increased heat in recent years and (2) disconnection from water and cultural roots. These themes reflect both the emotional and physical discomfort caused by climate change, as well as the cultural disorientation stemming from disrupted relationships with territory and traditional practices. The following sections present these themes, grounded in participants’ own narratives and interpreted through a relational and culturally informed lens.

Increased heat in recent years

Community members consistently described rising temperatures as a source of physical discomfort and diminished capacity for daily tasks, particularly those involving subsistence-based activities such as agricultural work, fishing, gathering and caregiving. These practices are not only sources of food and survival but also expressions of TEK that integrate ecological rhythms, spiritual meaning and community cohesions. For example, J, a 13-year-old boy, shared, "The sun here is very strong for us, and in this new place there is less shade, fewer trees, and no river,” highlighting how these new environmental stressors curtail outdoor activities and limit access to natural cooling and hydration. Similarly, M, a 16-year-old, noted, “The sun mistreats us; it is difficult to work when it is very sunny; it damages our skin.” These personal accounts corroborate quantitative findings (see table 2), where difficulties in accessing clean rivers and cultivating land were significantly associated with higher rates of general health problems, emotional distress and food insecurity. Together, these findings illustrate how climate-induced heat disrupts both the physical and cultural dimensions of well-being in the new settlement area.

Disconnection from water and cultural roots

This theme encapsulates the deep emotional and cultural impacts resulting from the loss of access to clean water and traditional water-based practices. S, a mother, shared her perspective on the environmental injustice affecting her community:

We don't do anything to the land, but the land is charging us… for what others do… the water is dirty and no fish come down the river… it makes us sick… but we are not to blame, the children even less so, and they are the ones who get sick the most.

Her words articulate the profound disorientation and distress experienced by the community due to the pollution of water bodies, which have historically been central to their way of life. She emphasised that environmental degradation not only leads to significant health issues but also highlights the injustice of their situation. Ja, a community leader, deepened this sentiment by lamenting the loss of their identity as ‘People of the River’. He described how displacement and the resultant lack of clean water have severed their spiritual connections and exacerbated mental health challenges:

We are Emberá, meaning we are People of the River… we come from Chocó, the place in the world with the most water… Now we are in a beautiful territory, which has welcomed us, but it lacks clean water. This makes us sick because we are far from our identity.

Discussion

The findings of this study illustrate the complex interplay between climate change, food insecurity and mental health issues within an Indigenous community—the Emberá-Dobidá. The community’s sudden displacement created the conditions for observing accelerated impacts of climate change in a new environment, allowing us to isolate how postdisplacement environmental degradation has shaped current health and food security outcomes. Our analysis captures a polycrisis, where intersecting challenges of climate change, food insecurity and sociocultural disruptions compound the vulnerabilities of an Indigenous community.

The experiences of the Emberá-Dobidá mirror systemic challenges faced by other Indigenous groups across Latin America, including the Shawi in Peru and various tribes in Brazil. Many Indigenous communities confront forced displacement, food insecurity and the erosion of TEK, yet each adapts in unique ways. For instance, the Shawi employ participatory mapping and agroforestry to improve food security, whereas Brazilian Indigenous communities emphasise ecological connectivity and biodiversity conservation to ensure cultural and environmental sustainability.12 810 22 The Emberá-Dobidá, known as the Water People, consider water as a vital communal asset; its loss or degradation intensifies the psychological and social impacts of climate change by undermining cultural identity and mutual care.8 10 11 20 Moreover, although conflict catalysed their displacement, our study capitalises on the environmental changes in the community’s new location—allowing an assessment of accelerated climate change effects that might otherwise require years to manifest.

These subsistence-based activities, such as fishing, small-scale agriculture, gathering and caregiving, are not only means of survival but also expressions of TEK rooted in the community’s relational understanding of land and life. Among the Emberá-Dobidá, these practices are embedded in cultural rhythms and environmental cycles—where work, spiritual and ecosystem stewardship are deeply intertwined. As such, environmental changes that disrupt these practices also destabilise cultural continuity and psychosocial resilience.

Research in Brazilian protected areas has shown that Indigenous communities can effectively leverage TEK to adapt to and mitigate the effects of climate change—approaches that the Emberá-Dobidá could also adopt.6 Available evidence underscores the need for localised, culturally sensitive adaptation strategies that not only mitigate environmental degradation but also address associated ‘climate anxiety’ and food insecurity. Furthermore, land rights are critical for enabling Indigenous communities to manage their resources and preserve cultural practices; however, existing government policies and international agreements often do not fully protect these interests, necessitating stronger advocacy and more inclusive policymaking.

From a Latin American perspective, territory must be understood not merely as a physical or administrative space, but rather as a sociohistorical, symbolic and political construction where the conditions for identity, life, health and well-being are established. As conceptualised by Borde and Torres-Tovar, “[t]erritory is the space where processes of social production and reproduction take place, together with the society-nature metabolism”.26 These processes constitute the foundation of the social determination of health, illness and death.

In the case of the Emberá-Dobidá community, the disconnection from water, a central element of their identity as ‘People of the River’, and the loss of ties to their ancestral land erode the material and spiritual foundations of their existence. Drawing on the work of Machado-Aráoz, Borde and Torres-Tovar, the concept of extractivism is established as producing what they refer to as ‘eco-biopolitical dispossession’ (translated from Spanish):26 an ecological and bodily expropriation in which natural elements, such as water, air, soil and biodiversity, are privatised and detached from the territories and bodies that sustain them. In this sense, for Indigenous people, the integration of territory, culture and the body is necessary to establish well-being.

The phenomenon of ‘climate anxiety’ among Indigenous youth—marked by heightened stress, depression and suicidal ideation—is the result of deep-seated fears about irreversible environmental degradation and cultural erosion.27 For the Emberá-Dobidá, these anxieties are compounded by the loss of ancestral lands and diminished ecosystem productivity, creating a ‘polycrisis’ where forced displacement, food insecurity and environmental degradation intersect, thereby eliminating the attribution of mental health outcomes to any single factor.7 11 22 28 Addressing this complex intersectionality requires interdisciplinary interventions that tackle both immediate stressors and the underlying causes of distress.28

Our mixed-methods design provided a layered understanding of the vulnerabilities and resilience of the Emberá-Dobidá community. Quantitative analysis revealed significant associations between environmental hardships and markers of spiritual disharmony, including behavioural problems (r=0.590, p<0.01) and food insecurity (r=0.460, p<0.01). These findings were enriched by qualitative narratives that added contextual depth to the statistical associations, for example, testimonies describing emotional distress linked to polluted rivers, the absence of traditional food sources and disconnection from sacred water spaces. This convergence of data underscores the relevance of interpreting mental health in Indigenous communities as a relational construct embedded in territory, culture and collective identity.

Given the small sample size, the exploratory nature of the study and the relational worldview of the participants, we adopted a theory-informed analytical model based on bivariate correlations rather than predictive or causal inference. This approach aligns with established mixed-methods principles for working with under-researched or culturally specific populations.17 18 To ensure methodological coherence, we considered several key factors, including cultural specificity, relational worldviews and the need for integration of qualitative and quantitative strands. Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into the interplay between climate change and mental health in an Indigenous community, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although the displacement due to armed conflict provided a distinctive context for the study, disentangling its effects from those of subsequent experience of environmental degradation remains challenging. Second, the trauma associated with the loss of parental figures in 25% of households may independently contribute to adverse mental health outcomes, reflecting a polycrisis where multiple stressors intersect, complicating the attribution of outcomes solely to climate change. Finally, limitations such as language barriers and a relatively small sample size may restrict the generalisability of our findings to other Indigenous communities. Future research should involve deeper community engagement to capture more nuanced perspectives and enable meaningful comparisons across diverse Indigenous groups.14 27

Conclusions

To bolster the resilience of Indigenous communities against the impacts of climate change, it is imperative to integrate TEK and recognise territory not merely as a geographic space, but as a sociocultural and historical foundation for identity, health and collective well-being. While armed conflict resulted in the displacement of the Emberá-Dobidá community, our findings show that climate-related environmental changes now function as independent and compounding stressors, significantly affecting children’s mental health, food security and cultural cohesion.

As discussed earlier, Latin American approaches to health and territory highlight the importance of the society–nature metabolism, an interdependent relationship between ecological and social processes in sustaining life. The loss of ancestral lands and ecosystems, such as rivers and forests, not only disrupts access to food and water but also fractures the symbolic and material foundations that support Indigenous ways of knowing, healing and being. Among the Emberá-Dobidá, this disconnection translates into spiritual disharmony and emotional distress, particularly for children and youth.

Policy makers must therefore prioritise culturally grounded and community-led interventions that defend Indigenous rights to territory, support the intergenerational transmission of TEK and ensure continuity of cultural and spiritual practices. These efforts should be complemented by expanded access to mental health services designed in alignment with Indigenous worldviews and by food security programmes rooted in traditional knowledge and local sovereignty.

Ultimately, reinforcing Indigenous territorialities offers both a strategy for survival and a framework for reimagining public health through principles of dignity, interdependence and reciprocity—a concept grounded in mutual respect and responsibility between people, communities and the ecosystems they inhabit.

Community engagement, cultural safety and Indigenous data sovereignty

Efforts were made to align with the ethical principles put forward by Indigenous organisations, particularly regarding culturally safe research. Key considerations included the historical context of colonisation, extractivism and discriminatory policies, which have contributed to ongoing trauma and high rates of mental health challenges among Indigenous peoples. It is important to note that in Latin American decolonial thought, ‘extractivism’ includes the appropriation of Indigenous knowledges, data and cultural expressions without meaningful consent, reciprocity or benefit-sharing. This includes research practices that reinforce colonial dynamics of knowledge accumulation and disempowerment.29 30 Additionally, the study recognised the importance of Indigenous data sovereignty—the right of communities to autonomously decide what, how and why data are collected, accessed, used and disseminated. The research was conducted in accordance with principles of community-led knowledge production, reciprocity, and Indigenous data sovereignty, recognising the historical context of colonisation and the implications of knowledge extractivism.
