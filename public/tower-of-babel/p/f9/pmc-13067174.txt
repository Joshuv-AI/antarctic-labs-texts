Glutamine metabolism in ovarian carcinoma: disentangling tumor aggressiveness from chemoresistance
Authors: Siegfried Hélage
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Dr. Tong for the careful reading of our article and for the thoughtful and constructive commentary on our work entitled “Glutamine metabolism drives tumor aggressiveness but not chemoresistance in ovarian carcinoma cell lines” (1). We appreciate the opportunity to further clarify the scope, rationale, and limitations of our study, and to address the insightful questions raised in his letter regarding signaling cross-talk, metabolic imaging, chemosensitivity, and the clinical boundaries of glutamine supplementation (2).

At the outset, we would like to restate the central message of our study, as articulated in the Abstract, Discussion, and Conclusion. Our work was not designed to provide an exhaustive mechanistic dissection of ovarian cancer chemoresistance, nor to propose immediate clinical interventions. Rather, it aimed to demonstrate, under controlled experimental conditions, a dissociation between (I) an intense glutaminolytic phenotype associated with tumor aggressiveness and (II) the lack of modulation of chemosensitivity to cisplatin and paclitaxel by variations in extracellular glutamine availability. This distinction underpins the interpretation of all subsequent points, including those discussed by Dr. Tong (2).

Signaling cross-talk and tumor aggressiveness

Dr. Tong appropriately highlights the need to further clarify the interaction between glutamine metabolism and other pro-invasive signaling pathways, including JAK1/STAT3 and glutamatergic signaling via mGluR1 (2). As detailed in the Results and Discussion sections of our article, the aggressive phenotype of TOV-21G cells was characterized by increased glutamine consumption, overexpression of glutaminase, GDH1, and c-Myc, together with marked activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 pathway (1). This phenotype is consistent with activating mutations in PI3K and JAK1 reported for this cell line and absent in ES-2 and OVCAR-3, as documented in public databases (3).

While we did not experimentally dissect STAT3 phosphorylation or mGluR1-driven feedback loops in this study, these pathways were explicitly discussed as biologically plausible contributors to invasiveness based on prior work showing that glutaminolysis fuels PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 signaling and promotes invasion in ovarian cancer models (4-6); direct functional validation was beyond the scope of the present study. As stated in the Discussion, the absence of significant differences in extracellular glutamate levels despite enhanced glutaminolysis in TOV-21G suggests rapid downstream utilization—potentially via GDH1-driven anaplerosis or glutathione synthesis—rather than extracellular accumulation and signaling alone (1,7). We agree with Dr. Tong that future studies integrating phospho-STAT3 analysis, mGluR1 expression, and functional inhibition experiments will be required to delineate the hierarchy and feedback structure of these signaling networks. Our data should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating with respect to signaling cross-talk, rather than as a definitive mechanistic map.

18F-fluoroglutamine positron emission tomography (PET) and metabolic heterogeneity

We fully concur with Dr. Tong that metabolic heterogeneity across ovarian cancer subtypes represents a major challenge for clinical translation (2). In our Introduction and Discussion, we emphasized that the proposed role of 18F-fluoroglutamine PET is prognostic and stratificational rather than universally diagnostic (1). Our suggestion that this imaging modality may be particularly relevant for clear cell carcinomas was grounded in two observations discussed in the manuscript: the relatively low 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) uptake reported in clear cell tumors compared with high-grade serous carcinomas (8), and the pronounced glutaminolytic phenotype observed in TOV-21G cells (1,4,9-12).

Importantly, we explicitly acknowledged the limitations of extrapolating from cell lines to patients, and from a single ascites-derived primary sample to the broader population. The primary ascites cells derived from a platinum-resistant high-grade serous papillary ovarian carcinoma, which were 18F-FDG PET-positive in vivo, exhibited lower consumption of both glucose and glutamine, as well as lower lactate production, compared with the three established cell lines. This finding further illustrates the metabolic divergence between in vitro cell line models and patient-derived clinical samples (1,13). As stated in the Discussion and Conclusion, larger cohorts of patient-derived cells and in vivo studies will be required to validate the sensitivity and prognostic value of 18F-fluoroglutamine PET across histological subtypes, including high-grade serous carcinoma, as emphasized by Dr. Tong (2).

Glucose availability, glutamine metabolism, and chemosensitivity

The interaction between glucose concentration and glutamine metabolism is another key point raised by Dr. Tong (2) that we intentionally controlled for in our experimental design. As described in the Methods and discussed extensively in the Discussion, glucose concentration was maintained at 11 mM across all conditions to isolate the effect of glutamine availability (1). This choice was deliberate, reflecting hyperglycemic conditions commonly encountered during chemotherapy due to corticosteroid administration.

Within this defined metabolic context, varying glutamine concentrations (0.5–4 mM) did not alter chemosensitivity to cisplatin or paclitaxel, nor basal cell density in untreated controls (1). We agree that under hypoglycemic or metabolically stressed conditions, glutamine may play a different role in redox balance and survival, as reported previously (14). These possibilities were explicitly acknowledged in our Discussion as important avenues for future investigation, but they fall outside the scope of our study’s stated aims and experimental framework.

Regarding OVCAR-3, we emphasized that its chemoresistance appears independent of glutamine availability and likely reflects alternative mechanisms, such as drug efflux or DNA repair pathways, which we proposed for future analysis (1,15). Thus, our findings do not negate metabolic contributions to drug response, but rather delineate their limits under the conditions tested.

Clinical boundaries of glutamine supplementation

We also appreciate Dr. Tong’s thoughtful discussion regarding the clinical implications and safety boundaries of glutamine supplementation (2). As stated in the Conclusion, our data do not constitute a recommendation for routine supplementation, but suggest that, pending in vivo confirmation, glutamine supplementation may be considered without exacerbating chemoresistance or tumor growth under certain conditions (1). We deliberately framed this conclusion with caution, emphasizing the need to define patient selection, dosage, timing, and tumor metabolic phenotype before clinical application.

We agree that immune modulation, tumor subtype, and long-term effects on recurrence risk are critical variables that require prospective evaluation (16). Our work provides a preclinical rationale for such studies, rather than definitive clinical guidance.

Conclusions

In summary, we thank Dr. Tong for his insightful and balanced commentary (2), which highlights important mechanistic and translational questions that align closely with the limitations and future directions already discussed in our article. We believe that our findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of glutamine metabolism in ovarian carcinoma by distinguishing tumor aggressiveness from chemoresistance and by underscoring the importance of metabolic context. We hope that this exchange will stimulate further in vivo and clinical investigations aimed at refining metabolic stratification and therapeutic strategies in ovarian cancer.
