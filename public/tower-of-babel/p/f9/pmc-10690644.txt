Epileptic seizures worsen the gait and motor abnormalities in adult patients with Dravet syndrome (with a case report and literature review)
Authors: Xiaoping Du, Shizhong Lian, Meizhen Sun, Ruilong Li, Huifang Wang, Xiaoping Yang, Huifen Wang, Xiaobin Zhang, Fengpeng Wang, Yi Yao, Junhong Guo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractDravet syndrome (DS), previously known as severe myoclonic epilepsy in infancy (SMEI), is considered the most serious “epileptic encephalopathy.” Here, we present a man with a de novo SCN1A mutation who was diagnosed with DS at the age of 29. In addition to pharmaco‐resistant seizures and cognitive delay, he also developed moderate to severe motor and gait problems, such as crouching gait and Pisa syndrome. Moreover, it deteriorated significantly following an epileptic seizure. The patient presented with severe flexion of the head and trunk in the sagittal plane and fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for camptocormia and antecollis. After a week, it spontaneously alleviated partially. We applied levodopa to the patient and had a good response. Functional Gait Assessment (FGA) was assessed at three different times: 4 days after the seizure, 1 week after the seizure, and after taking levodopa for 2 years. The results were 4, 12, and 19 points, respectively. We postulated that: (1) gait and motor deficits are somehow influenced by recurrent epileptic episodes;(2) the nigrostriatal dopamine system is involved. To our knowledge, we were the ones who first reported this phenomenon.Keywords: crouching gait, Dravet syndrome, gait disturbances, prognosis, seizure

INTRODUCTION

Dravet syndrome (DS), previously known as severe myoclonic epilepsy in infancy (SMEI), is considered the most serious “epileptic encephalopathy” 1 and the most representative of hereditary epilepsy. Approximately 80% of affected patients harbor an SCN1A gene mutation, 2 , 3 and 5% of patients PCDH19 gene mutation. 4 Besides pharmaco‐resistant seizures and cognitive delay, some patients later evolve into moderate to severe gait and motor disturbances, such as crouching gait and low tension. 5 , 6

Herein, we report a man diagnosed with DS at 29 years who developed moderate‐to‐severe motor and gait problems. An interesting phenomenon is that it deteriorated significantly following an epileptic seizure and spontaneously alleviated partially 1 week later. The case is extremely important for comprehending the late symptoms of DS and examining potential gait and motor disruption causes.

CASE REPORT

This was a 29‐year‐old male with normal growth and development before onset. At the age of 8 months, febrile convulsions occurred monthly. Since one and a half, he developed afebrile convulsions, including myoclonus, atypical absences, and secondary tonic–clonic seizures. Because the parents did not pay attention to the first two types of seizures, it is unknown how frequently they occurred. Secondary tonic–clonic seizures occurred in three to six clusters per month. He has been diagnosed with epilepsy and treated with many kinds of antiseizure medications, such as “carbamazepine, phenytoin, valproate, phenobarbital, topiramate, and so on.” However, no apparent improvement was observed, and the convulsions still occurred in clusters. Seizures were frequently triggered by heat or hot baths. Since the onset of the disease, the patient's psychomotor development has been significantly lagged, and he could not go to school and was unable to take care of himself. No special founding in internal medicine examination and no obvious founding in the cranial nerves, sensory, muscle tension, and muscle strength were found. The pathological reflexes were negative. Four days after the seizure, the patient presented with severe flexion of the head and trunk in the sagittal plane fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for camptocormia and antecollis 7 (Figure 1A and Video S1). “Crouching gait” was seen during walking, mainly characterized by knee and ankle flexion (Figure 1A and Video S1). The Functional Gait Assessment (FGA) score was 4 points. An interesting phenomenon, the patient's camptocormia, and antecollis improved to baseline spontaneously 1 week after the attack (Figure 1B and Video S2), and the trunk leaning to the right side could be seen, fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for Pisa syndrome (Figure 1C and Video S2). The Functional Gait Assessment (FGA) score was 12 points. The MMSE score was 6/30 points, suggesting severe cognitive impairment. No definite lesion in the brain MRI except for insignificant brain atrophy at 29 years was found (Figure 2). The video‐EEG showed diffuse background slowing and diffused and multifocal spikes were both presented. Photic stimulation paroxysmal response and photic convulsive reaction were recorded. A heterozygous mutation (c. 1501C > G, p.) was found in the SCN1A gene. This single base substitution of C to G resulted in mutation at the 1501st amino acid, from threonine to arginine. Sanger sequencing of DNA extracted from the parents confirmed that the mutation occurred de novo. Electroclinical features were consistent with DS syndrome and were finally confirmed by genes.

We discontinued the irrational antiepileptic drugs (carbamazepine) and adjusted them to valproic acid, clonazepam, and topiramate. After 2 years of regular antiepileptic therapy, seizure frequency was reduced to one seizure every 2 months. We also tried to apply levodopa 125 mg three times a day, and the patient's slowness of walking and facial rigidity significantly improved with no side effects (Video S3, after taking levodopa for 2 years). The Functional Gait Assessment (FGA) score was 19 points.

DISCUSSION

Dravet syndrome is well‐studied in the pediatric population; however, there is limited information regarding DS in adults. Their motor symptoms and gait become progressively worse as their age. In this paper, we report a DS patient who was 29 years old when they received their diagnosis. This is crucial to our comprehension of the late‐stage features of DS and is exceedingly uncommon in clinical practice. For this patient, the seizure made the gait and motor abnormalities worse, and many days later, partial spontaneous remission could be observed. We hypothesized that recurrent epileptic seizures contribute in some way to the development of gait and motor impairments. To our knowledge, this phenomenon was first reported by us.

Clinically, the patient is consistent with the SCN1A gene mutation‐induced form of DS. Except for recurrent seizures, cognitive delay, gait disorder, and parkinsonian symptoms were observed. The patient continued to experience regular tonic–clonic and myoclonic seizures throughout adulthood, along with frequent status epilepticus episodes. The EEG revealed a slow background with multifocal and widespread discharge. Photosensitivity was still persistent. According to reports 8 , 9 that status recurrent status epilepticus become less frequent with time, and may not be seen in adolescence and young adulthood. Hyperthermia as a seizure trigger may become less problematic. Even now, our patient frequently experiences status epilepticus or cluster seizures, which might be the result of irrational combinations. Seizures usually have focal features and originate in the frontal region. 7 For this patient, a clear head‐eye version early in the seizure suggested focal onset.

In addition to cognitive deterioration, some individuals may develop neurological abnormalities in the late stages, such as gait ataxia and postural abnormalities. Fasano et al. 10 observed 12 adult Dravet patients, 11 of whom presented with severe cognitive impairment, while 12% exhibited mild muscular rigidity. All patients had mild to severe head and neck flexion in the sagittal plane, and eight patients (66%) met the diagnostic criteria for antecollis, with severity significantly correlated with age. Three of the oldest patients developed camptocormia, and one had pizza syndrome. Five of the 12 patients were found to have a “crouch gait”, which is commonly used to describe the gait of patients with cerebral palsy, refers to the gait with increased hip, knee flexion, and ankle dorsiflexion in the sagittal plane, accompanied by cross‐sectional medial thigh twist, lateral tibial twist, and foot abduction evagination. 11 Different from cerebral palsy, the crouching gait of DS patients is not accompanied by or with very slight rigidity and contraction, 11 which disappears after lying down. Another study 11 also showed that camptocormia only manifested in individuals older than 13 years old and was age‐related. Severity significantly correlated with age. Quantitative analysis of gait in this population is only recently reported. 12 , 13

Dravet syndrome is caused by mutations in the sodium channel gene, but how it causes these late symptoms is still unknown. The etiology of these postural abnormalities is difficult to determine and is likely caused by a multifactorial process. It has been demonstrated via studies on animals that the sodium channel Na V 1.1 is essential for the excitability of Purkinje neurons in the cerebellum. It is crucial for the peak shape, persistence, regeneration, and continuous action potential discharge of sodium current. In Na V 1.1‐deficient mice, Purkinje fiber excitability is decreased, which causes tremors, ataxia, and other functional abnormalities. Ataxia in DS patients has reportedly been linked to sodium channel deficit. 14 The findings of 10 individuals with the SCN1A gene mutation DS were reviewed by Jennifer et al. 15 along with the associations between tonic–clonic seizure status, aberrant gait, and gene mutation locations. The genetic abnormalities were divided into four types: A, B, C, and D. Among them, patients with A (missense mutations in the pore‐forming region) and B (frameshift mutations in the final exon, leading to early protein truncation) type mutations developed gait abnormalities in the late stage. Patients with type C (splice site mutation) and D (missense mutation outside the pore‐forming region and voltage sensor regions) had no gait abnormalities. Only patients with type A, pore‐forming missense mutations needed a wheelchair. Therefore, sodium channel mutations and mutation sites were the keys to abnormal gait in DS patients. However, the missense mutation of our patient was outside the pore‐forming region and voltage sensor regions, which belonged to type D. It was located on the Domain3–Domain4 linker, the function of which was unknown, only one nearby locus in the same region resulting in a predominantly loss‐of‐function. 16 As we know, incorrect drug usage and a missed diagnosis might affect adult outcomes. Our patient had a delay in diagnosis and has long‐term exposure to several drugs that are not recommended. In individuals with DS, prolonged use of prohibited medications during the first 5 years of life has been linked to poorer long‐term cognitive outcomes. 17 Our patient showed obvious trunk bending and crouching gait after the cluster attack. When the attack was controlled for a few days, the posture and gait improved partially. This phenomenon suggests that repeated seizures play a role in the formation of abnormal postures. In addition, our patient responded well to levodopa administration and Parkinsonian symptoms improved. Fasano et al. 10 also applied levodopa to two of the most severely ill patients and had a good response to levodopa, suggesting that the nigrostriatal dopamine system is involved in the formation of late posture and gait abnormalities, although we could not obtain other support such as imaging of the nigrostriatal dopamine system.

In conclusion, DS is drug‐resistant epilepsy, and our patient reveals the late pattern of postural and gait impairment in adult. Except for the SCN1A mutation and mutation sites, the frequency of convulsive seizures probably constitutes a risk factor for motor retardation. Inappropriate medication treatments maybe correlate with cognitive and motor impairment in our patient. Therefore, early and correct intervention is very important for prognosis. However, additional studies are necessary to analyze the mechanism.
