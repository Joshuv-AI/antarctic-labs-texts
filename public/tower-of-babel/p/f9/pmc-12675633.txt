The predictive value of the orientation and offset of angle α and angle κ for visual outcomes after trifocal intraocular lens implantation in an Asian cohort
Authors: Xia Zhang, Shuang Song, Wenda Sui, Lu Sun, Zaowen Wang, Lulu Chen, Ailing Bian, Yong Zhong, Shunhua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the predictive value of the preoperative orientation and offset of angle alpha(chord alpha) and angle kappa(chord mu) for visual outcomes in patients who underwent trifocal intraocular lens (IOL) implantation.MethodsPatient records of eyes that underwent AT LISA tri 839MP implantation were retrospectively collected and grouped according to the preoperative offset and orientations of chord alpha and chord mu. The two-dimensional location of each angle was described by the interaction of the orientation and offset. Multiple regression models and likelihood ratio tests were established to determine their predictive value for postoperative visual outcomes, National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ) and Quality of Vision Questionnaire (QoV) scores.ResultsOne hundred and two patients (184 eyes) were included. The orientation of chord alpha had a significant overall prediction to postoperative visual outcomes (p = 0.014). A superior chord alpha was an independent predictor of better postoperative uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA, p < 0.001) and a larger modulation transfer function (MTF) curve average height (p = 0.041). However, the offsets of chords alpha and chord mu and the orientation of chord mu did not significantly predict UDVA, uncorrected intermediate visual acuity (UIVA), uncorrected near visual acuity (UNVA), MTF, total ocular aberration (TOA), or high-order aberration (HOA).Better patient reported general vision was correlated with a nasal chord alpha (p = 0.012), whereas better near vision was correlated with a smaller kappa offset (p = 0.022) and nasal chord alpha (p = 0.019). The absence of postoperative halos was independently associated with the location of nasal chord alpha(p = 0.039), while starbursts were independently correlated with a larger chord mu offset (p = 0.007).ConclusionA superior chord alpha was an independent predictor of better postoperative visual outcomes after trifocal IOL implantation. Introduction

Multifocal intraocular lenses (MIOLs), particularly trifocal intraocular lenses, have gained popularity worldwide, as they have been demonstrated to lead to good visual acuity after cataract or refractive lens exchange surgery [1]. The indications for trifocal intraocular lenses (TIOLs), on the other hand, have rapidly expanded to rather extended refractive statuses [2, 3]. The selection of appropriate candidates for TIOL implantation remains crucial in clinical practice since some patients may experience postoperative visual disturbances, such as glare, halo or starbursts, which can seriously affect their quality of life and satisfaction with the procedure [4]. Previous studies have revealed that the misalignment of the visual axial, pupil center, and optical centers of the MIOL can result in an increase in intraocular higher-order aberrations and visual disturbances [5]. Angle alpha (chord alpha) and angle kappa (chord mu), therefore, are important factors for predicting the postoperative position of MIOLs. The angle kappa is determined as the angle spanning between the visual axis and the pupillary axis, whereas the angle alpha is the angle between the optical axis (or axis of rotational symmetry, if the eye were a rotationally symmetric optical system) and the visual axis [5]. Since neither the angle alpha nor the angle kappa can be measured directly, there is a convention to express these angles in terms of the distance of their piercing point on the cornea, known as the chord alpha and chord mu. In clinical ophthalmology in China, the predominant devices employed for measuring angle kappa and angle alpha are the iTrace (Tracey Technologies, Houston, Texas, USA) and the OPD-Scan III (Nidek Co., Ltd., Aichi, Japan). To date, a substantial portion of the research and clinical consensus regarding these angles has been found on data acquired from these two instruments [6].

Although the predictive values of chord mu and chord alpha for visual quality after MIOL implantation have been investigated in a considerable number of studies, the results have been quite mixed [7–10]. As a result, there is currently no clear conclusion on whether chord alpha or chord mu, or both, should be considered when selecting patients before surgery or when predicting outcomes.

One potential explanation for these conflicting findings is that visual axis or pupillary axis deviation were influenced by light conditions, pupil sizes and ocular axial length, which share wide range of variance among the population [11–13].

In addition, the orientation of these angles was relatively neglected in previous studies. In 2020, Wang et al. [14] confirmed that the locations of chord mu derived from iTrace were mostly scattered on the temporal side of the corneal light reflection, whereas the locations of chord alpha were well organized in the horizontal position on the temporal side of the corneal light reflection. However, no study has investigated the direction of the α-angle and κ-angle in relation to visual prognosis for patients undergoing MIOL implantation. Therefore, in this retrospective study, we analyzed the predictive value the orientation of chord alpha and chord mu, as well as the interaction of orientation and offset of those angles on postoperative visual acuity, intraocular higher-order aberrations, and patient reported visual disturbances in patients who underwent trifocal IOL implantation.

Methods

Subject

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Union Medical College Hospital. A review of charts was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Peking Union Medical College Hospital. An informed consent form was signed by each patient prior to the surgical procedure to authorize the use of their medical data.

We retrospectively investigated 204 patients (356 eyes) aged between 30 and 80 years who underwent cataract or refractive Lens exchange surgery with implantation of AT LISA tri 839MP trifocal IOLs by one cataract surgeon (S. Z.) at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between January 2021 and August 2024. We enrolled 184 eyes (102 patients) in the present study in accordance with all of the following inclusion criteria: (1) cataract/refractive lens exchange surgeries completed uneventfully; (2) observation period between June 2021 and October 2024 and followed ≥ 6 months postoperatively; (3) refractive astigmatism at the first follow-up post operation not exceeding ± 0.75 D; (4) measurement of angle kappa, angle alpha, and aberration analysis performed before and 6 months after surgery with at least one reliable result each time (see Angle Alpha and Kappa Measurements and Postoperative Evaluation section of Methods) during the observation period; (5) no intraocular surgeries or corneal laser refractive surgeries (laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis, laser-assisted subepithelial keratectomy, small incision lenticule extraction, or photorefractive keratectomy) performed before cataract surgery or during the follow-up period; (6) no eyes with coexisting corneal or retinal disease, uveitis, any type of glaucoma, or nonglaucomatous optic neuropathy before or during the observation period; and (7) no systemic diseases with existing evidence of a correlation with a dry eye disease, such as Sjogren’s syndrome, thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy and systemic lupus erythematosus. Diabetic patients with no evidence of retinal involvement were included. All patients underwent routine follow-up postoperatively. Patients who lacked any reliable follow-up data (6 ± 1 months postsurgery) within this designated interval were excluded from the analysis. All assessments were performed at this single time point.

Intraocular lens

The trifocal IOL AT LISA tri 839MP from Carl Zeiss Meditec is a preloaded, ultraviolet-filtering, four-haptic Lens with an overall diameter of 11.0 mm and an optical zone of 6.0 mm. The intraocular Lens is made of hydrophilic acrylic material and has hydrophobic surface properties. It incorporates a single-piece diffractive multifocal design, and the edge of the posterior optical zone is frosted to reduce potential edge glare effects. The near add is 3.33 diopters (D), and the intermediate add is + 1.66 D, both of which are calculated in the IOL plane.

Surgical procedure

All operations were performed under topical anesthesia (0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride ophthalmic drops, 5.0 ml; Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) by one surgeon (S.Z.) following a standardized Peking Union Medical College Hospital refractive cataract surgery protocol to achieve consistent outcomes. Corneal incisions, capsulorhexis and nuclear cleavages of all the eyes were performed manually. The main corneal incision (2.4 mm) was located at the steepest corneal meridian shown in the preoperative corneal topography (TMS-4; Tomey Corp., Japan) to correct corneal astigmatism. A lateral incision (1.0 mm) was placed at an angle of 90° clockwise relative to the primary incision. A standard continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis of 5.5 mm was executed for each patient. Phacoemulsification was performed with a standard ultrasonic instrument (Centrion, Alcon Laboratories, Inc., USA). The IOL was implanted in the capsule bag with the anterior capsule covering 360° of the IOL rim. Anti-inflammatory glucocorticoids and antibiotics were used for 2 weeks after the operation.

Preoperative and postoperative assessment

All patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination preoperatively and postoperatively performed by the same experienced ophthalmologist (S.Z.). Preoperative examinations included measurements of uncorrected (UDVA) and corrected (CDVA) distance (5 m) visual acuities, manifest refraction, slit lamp anterior segment evaluation, keratometry, and dilated retina wide-field photography. Refraction status, UDVA and CDVA were evaluated within one week before surgery. Postoperative examinations included measurements of the UDVA, CDVA, uncorrected intermediate (UIVA, 80 cm) and near (UNVA, 40 cm) visual acuities. Visual acuities were measured by an experienced optometrist (W.S.) under the same photopic conditions using Snellen visual charts and then converted into the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) notation.

Preoperatively, the patient’s ocular axis length (AL), corneal refractive power (AK), anterior chamber depth (ACD) and white-to-white distance (WTW) were measured with partial coherence laser interferometry (IOLMaster 700, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG). Specifications of the preoperative IOL of all patients were calculated by the Barrett Universal II formula. The selected target refractive outcome aimed as closest to 0D to achieve emmetropia.

The iTrace aberrometer (Tracy Technologies, Houston, Texas, USA) was adopted to measure the aberrations within one week before surgery and at the one-week and 6-month follow-ups according to the multifocal implantation clinical triage of the hospital. This device is used to calculate wavefront aberration data on the basis of ray-tracing aberrometry and corneal topography, allowing analysis of visual quality. During each visit, an experienced technician (L.S.) measured the patient three consecutive times under the same mesopic/scotopic lighting conditions. During the retrospective data screening period, two ophthalmologists (X.Z. and L.C.) evaluated the examination results and excluded: (1) the eyelid blocked the cornea; (2) rejection points ≥ 10; (3) the transmission of the retinal tracking image was not located in the macula fovea; and (4) no energy spike was detected in the energy map. If both researchers believed that the image was unreliable, the follow-up results were excluded. When there was a disagreement between the two researchers, an experienced cataract specialist (Z.W.) made the final judgment. The modulation transfer function (MTF) curve was developed from built-in software after correction with a standard 3 mm pupil in no refraction correction mode. Ocular aberrations, internal aberrations, and corneal aberrations were obtained with 3 mm and 5 mm pupil diameters in a dark room. The average height (AH) of the MTF curve was used to quantify the MTF curve.

Angle alpha and angle kappa

Preoperative and postoperative angle alpha and angle kappa was measured by iTrace aberrometer (Tracy Technologies, Houston, Texas, USA). The aberrometer captures an iris image through an infrared camera to display the center of the pupil, the center of the visual axis, and the center of the limbus. Angle kappa (chord mu) was defined by the radial distance between the center of the pupil and the visual axis, estimated by the center of the first Purkinje reflex. Angle alpha (chord alpha) was defined as the radial distance between the center of the limbus and the visual axis. One picture with the best quality was selected for analysis.

After the pupil position was manually corrected by one researcher (X.Z.), the orientations and offset of chord mu and chord alpha were automatically read by the built-in algorithm on the basis of the previous result of the mirror symmetry distribution of chord alpha and chord mu in both eyes [13]. The orientations of angle alpha and angle kappa were determined based on the relative positions of the pupil’s center (angle kappa) or limbus center (angle alpha) and the fixation point of visual axis. For instance, if the pupil’s center was located nasally relative to the fixation point in one eye, the orientation of angle kappa for that eye was classified as nasal (Fig. 1).

Distribution and categorization of angle alpha and angle kappa: In both eyes, angles were read counterclockwise, with the right direction set to 0 degrees. Eyes were classified into superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal groups on the basis of the orientation of angle alpha and angle kappa, defined by the 45° and 135° axes. The left eye is shown in this figure. The offset for chord mu, indicated by the distance between the pupil center (green cross) and the fixation point (red cross), was 0.211, with an orientation of 183° in the nasal quadrant. The offset for chord alpha was the distance between the limbus center (blue cross) and the fixation point (red cross), which measured 0.158, with an orientation of 43° in the temporal quadrant

To evaluate the impact of the offset of chord alpha and chord mu on visual outcomes, we categorized the patients into four groups: <0.3 mm (included), 0.3–0.4 mm (included), 0.4–0.5 mm (included) and > 0.5 mm.

Patient survey

The Chinese versions of the National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (NEI VFQ-25) [15] and the Quality of Vision (QoV) [16] were administered to all enrolled patients but were only analyzed for patients with binocular trifocal IOL implantation.

Statistical analysis

Data that followed a normal distribution are expressed as the means ± standard deviations, whereas data that did not follow a normal distribution are expressed as the medians with interquartile ranges (Q1, Q3). First, patients were grouped according to the preoperative angle alpha and angle kappa orientations and offsets (see the Angle Alpha and Angle Kappa section of the Methods section). The chi-square (χ2) test was used to compare demographic categorical data among the groups. For multiple comparisons, a Kruskal‒Wallis test, followed by a post hoc Mann‒Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction, was used to compare continuous demographic data and visual outcome differences. In these comparisons, each group was tested against a combination of the remaining three groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for the χ2 and Kruskal‒Wallis tests. For the post hoc Mann-Whiteney U test, a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of p < 0.016 (0.05/3) was applied. Second, the patients were grouped based on visual outcomes. Preoperative parameters were compared with Student’s t tests and the independent sample Mann‒Whitney U test. For categorical variables, the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test were adopted. Finally, multiple linear regression models were constructed to determine the predictive value of the preoperative orientations and offsets of angle kappa and angle alpha for the MTF, HOA, TOA and VFQ-25 scores as the continuous dependent variables adjusted for age, sex, axial length (AL), anterior chamber depth (ACD), white-to-white (WTW) distance, spherical equivalent (SE), and pupil diameter (PD). Logistic regression models were constructed to determine the predictive value of the two angles for postoperative UDVA, UMVA and UNVA following adjustment for age, sex, AL, ACD, WTW, postoperative refraction errors (RE), SE, and PD. Hierarchical regression was then established to test the interactions of the offsets and orientations of angle alpha and angle kappa. A likelihood ratio test was conducted to test the overall predictive value of the orientations of angle alpha and angle kappa for the postoperative visual outcomes. Specifically, for the orientation of angle alpha and kappa, a secondary model was developed that included postoperative residual refractive errors to validate the robustness of the findings.

The results of the VFQ-25 and QoV were applied to both eyes for analysis. Values exceeding a threshold of 3 standard deviations in either direction were subjected to a thorough examination by one statistician (S.S.) and two ophthalmologists (X.Z. and S.Z.) to determine if they were outliers and were considered missing values. All the coverts and categorizations were made by an ophthalmologist (X.Z.) and then examined by a statistician (S.S.) using R 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software V.26.0. 0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 356 eyes (204 patients) were screened from January 2019 to December 2024 and underwent cataract surgery and trifocal IOL (AT LISA tri 839MP) implantation at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. A total of 184 eyes of 103 patients were included (flowchart in Supplemental Fig. 1). The preoperative spherical equivalent refraction was − 3.64 ± 5.67 D. The median preoperative best-corrected visual acuity (LogMAR) was 0 (0, 0.2). The best preoperative BCVA was 0.0 LogMAR, accounting for 39.7% (73 eyes) of the whole cohort, whereas the worst preoperative BCVA was 0.9 LogMAR (Table 1). The average postoperative refractive error of the entire cohort was − 0.24 ± 0.41 D, with no significant differences observed among groups with different chord mu or chord alpha offsets or orientations (Tables 1 and 2, Supplemental Table 1, and Supplemental Table 2).

Visual outcomes in patients with different offsets and orientations of preoperative chord alpha

As shown in Table 1; Fig. 2, chord alpha was found to be predominantly located in the temporal quadrant of the cornea, accounting for 84.2% (155/184) of the eyes, followed by the superior (7.1%, 13/184), nasal (5.4%, 10/184), and inferior (3.3%, 6/184) quadrants. Patients with a superior chord alpha had significantly better UDVA than patients with an chord alpha in the other three quadrants did (post hoc Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction, p < 0.001), whereas patients with a temporal chord alpha had significantly worse UDVA (p = 0.001, Table 1). However, no visual outcomes were significantly different among patients with different offsets of chord alpha (p for all > 0.05, Supplemental Table 1).

Distribution and predictive value of chord alpha and chord mu for UDVA, UIVA and UNVA: chord alpha was predominantly located in the temporal quadrant of the cornea (84%), followed by the superior (7.1%), nasal (5.4%), and inferior (3.3%) quadrants. The chord mu was more evenly distributed in the four quadrants, with 46.2% in the temporal quadrant, followed by the inferior (23.3%), superior (21.2%), and nasal quadrants (9.2%). The red dots represent the eyes considered to have unsatisfactory postoperative outcomes (uncorrected visual acuity worse than logMAR 0.2, included). No significant differences were detected in the distributions of chord alpha and chord mu between these eyes. Even though all patients with an unsatisfactory postoperative outcome of UDVA were patients with a temporal chord mu, logistic regression revealed no predictive value of the offset or orientation of chord alpha or chord mu

Visual outcomes in patients with different offsets and orientations of preoperative chord mu

Chord mu, as shown in Table 2; Fig. 2, was more evenly distributed in the four quadrants, with 46.2% (85/184) in the temporal quadrant, followed by the inferior (23.3%, 43/184), superior (21.2%, 39/184), and nasal quadrants (9.2%, 17/184). No visual outcomes, including all distance visual acuities, MTF, TOA, HOA, and SA, showed significant differences when patients were grouped by the orientation of chord mu (Table 2). Among all the other parameters, total aberration was significantly smaller in patients whose chord mu was greater than 0.5 mm (p = 0.003, 0.008, and 0.007, respectively; Supplemental Table 2).

The predictive value of preoperative orientation and offset of chord mu and chord alpha for postoperative visual outcomes

Patients were categorized into two groups on the basis of their postoperative visual outcomes: those with satisfactory visual outcomes and those with unsatisfactory visual outcomes. Unsatisfactory visual outcome was defined as an uncorrected postoperative visual acuity equal to or worse than logMAR 0.2. Among the total cohort of 184 patients, 4 exhibited unsatisfactory UDVA, 19 had unsatisfactory UIVA, and 8 demonstrated unsatisfactory UNVA. The orientations and offsets of chord mu and chord alpha, as well as other biometrics, were compared between the two groups. Despite the observation that all cases of unsatisfactory postoperative UDVA were associated with a temporal chord mu (Supplemental Tables 3 and Fig. 2), no significant differences were identified in either the offset or orientation of chord alpha or chord mu when the satisfactory and unsatisfactory outcome groups were compared (p > 0.05 for all comparisons, as detailed in Supplemental Table 3). Furthermore, logistic regression analysis revealed that neither the offset nor the orientation of chord alpha or chord mu had predictive value for unsatisfactory visual outcomes (p > 0.05 for all, Supplemental Table 4). A smaller WTW was detected in patients in the unsatisfactory UDVA (p = 0.005) and UNVA (p = 0.006) groups, whereas a smaller ACD (p = 0.033) was detected in patients in the unsatisfactory UNVA group (Supplemental Table 3).

Linear regression models controlling for age, postoperative residual refractive errors (postoperative RE), AL, ACD, WTW, PD, and preoperative equivalent spherical showed that the offset of either chord alpha or chord mu was not independently correlated with postoperative visual acuity (all p > 0.05, Table 3.). However, a superior chord alpha was significantly independently correlated with a better UDVA (β=−0.257, p < 0.001; Table 3.) and a larger MTF average height (β = 0.158, p = 0.041; Table 3.; Fig. 2). No significant predictive value of the offset or the orientation of chord alpha or chord mu was detected for UIVA, UNVA, TOA or HOA (p > 0.05, Table 3.; Fig. 3). The interaction of the orientation and the offset of chord alpha, on the other hand, was not significant for the prediction of UDVA (p = 0.698) or the MTF average height (p = 0.197).

The predictive value of preoperative orientation and offset of chord mu and chord alpha for patient-reported visual quality

Sixty-one patients who underwent binocular trifocal implantation were included in this part of the study, and reports of driving visual quality were obtained from 45 of them. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are shown in Supplemental Table 6. The overall score for general vision was 74.18 ± 32.16, near vision 86.88 ± 17.37, computer use 96.72 ± 8.47, and dim light walking 91.94 ± 14.92. The overall average score for day driving was 99.44 ± 3.70, whereas that for night driving was 72.16 ± 26.91 (Supplemental Table 6).

Discussion

According to a previous study, the distribution of chord mu and chord alpha, as well as the pre- and postoperative lens tilt, has important mirror symmetry [17]. Hence, we grouped the orientations of chord mu and chord alpha into four quadrants in both eyes. The results of our study indicated that chord mu and chord alpha were distributed predominantly in the temporal quadrant of both eyes, which was consistent with the findings of Mahr et al. [18] and Wang et al. [14].

Chord alpha, defined as the intersection of the visual axis and the optic axis, is a partial reflection of the center of the lens capsule and the visual axis and has been reported to be a predictor of postoperative IOL tilt. Our results suggest that the orientation rather than the size of chord alpha influences the postoperative visual outcome. A superior orientation of chord alpha was independently correlated with better UDVA. Although patients with better UDVA had a smaller chord alpha, the offset of chord alpha alone did not impact the postoperative visual outcome. This was probably because patients with better visual acuity also showed a more myopic trend (negative preoperative SE, larger ACD and AL), which was correlated with a smaller chord alpha and outweighed the impact of the latter in the regression model.

In our results, a superior chord alpha was also independently correlated with a larger MTF average height. This result was consistent with the fact that a superior chord alpha was positively correlated with the UDVA. The MTF was previously described as an excellent predictor of visual acuity as well as contact sensitivity after multifocal IOL implantation [19]. The total aberration was significantly smaller in patients whose chord mu was larger than 0.5 mm, which was not consistent with the findings of previous studies [7]. This was probably due to the significantly smaller pupil size in this group of patients, which was correlated with smaller aberrations [20].

The offset of chord mu, on the other hand, had no predictive value for visual acuity at any distance, which was consistent with some recent studies [9, 21]. An earlier study by Prakash et al. [22] identified a larger chord mu as a potent predictor of diminished UDVA; however, the regression model did not include AL, ACD, WTW or PD. Notably, UDVA can be influenced by residual refractive error, which has been demonstrated to be significantly correlated with AL in various formulas for both monofocal and trifocal lens implantation [23].

Few studies have focused on the predictive value of the orientation of chord alpha and chord mu for postoperative aberrations. The results of our study, however, revealed that neither a temporal nor nasal orientation of chord mu independently impacted postoperative aberrations. The natural position of the crystalline lens was temporally aligned [24], and temporal decentration of the IOL was suggested to be associated with the greatest risk of poor outcomes after multifocal IOL implantation in an earlier study [25]. This suggestion was recently confronted by Fernández et al. [26], who argued that temporal centration of MIOLs ensures better alignment with the center of the cornea, therefore reducing aberrations caused by optical surface misalignment.

We are aware that HOAs are influenced by pupil size. However, in our study, all measurements were conducted under standardized mesopic conditions, and aberrations were calculated using fixed pupil diameters (3 mm and 5 mm) as provided by the iTrace system. Pupil size was also included as a covariate in our regression models. Moreover, no significant differences in pupil size were found between groups with different chord mu or chord alpha orientations, further minimizing the potential for confounding.

Patient-reported visual quality is always considered one of the most important dimensions in the evaluation of multifocal IOL implantation. According to our study, patients achieved excellent general vision, daytime driving vision and mid-distance vision, which was consistent with the findings of a previous study [27]. The nasal chord alpha was independently correlated with better performance in patient reported general vision and near vision. A smaller kappa offset was independently correlated with better performance in near vision. Chord alpha was significantly larger in halo group than in no halo group, however, AL and ACD, which were considered associated with chord alpha, were also significantly larger in Halo group. When AL and ACD were adjusted in regression models, the offset of chord alpha failed to show independent predictive power of the present of Halo. Nevertheless, presence of starbursts was found independently correlated with a larger offset of chord mu. We considered that patient-reported visual quality was influenced by other factors, such as cognitive status and personality [28], which were difficult to include in the regression models and limited their explanatory power.

Importantly, in our study, both chord mu and chord alpha were derived from iTrace, a wavefront aberrometer. For chord alpha specifically, the limbal center was used to estimate the optical axis rather than the lens center, potentially introducing bias due to the omission of zonular positioning. For chord mu, even though we controlled for pupil diameter in our regression models, the fact that iTrace only measures the apparent pupil center as seen through the cornea means our result can only be considered an ‘apparent chord mu’ [6]. At present, no commercial device, including iTrace, can provide measured angle alpha or angle kappa values [29] that exactly align with their mathematical definitions, and discrepancies have been reported between measurements derived from different devices [30]. However, since iTrace is now widely used in the preoperative assessment of multifocal lens implantation both in China and worldwide and the prospect of interchangeable measures cannot take place in the short term, detecting whether chord and chord mu derived from iTrace has predictive value for surgery is highly important. The predictive capacity of the actual angle kappa and angle alpha for visual outcomes following multifocal lens implantation is worth further exploration.

Our study has several limitations. First, the results were derived from one 6-month postoperative follow-up. Data with multiple follow-ups and longer intervals should be investigated in the future. Second, this was a retrospective study, and prospective studies should be conducted in the future for higher evidence Levels. Third, the patient OSDI survey and dry eye status were not evaluated in this study, which may have introduced some bias despite the exclusion of any systemic Sjogren syndrome patients. Thirdly, NEI VFQ 25 and QoV questionnaires were sent out through emails for patients’ convenience which may introduce bias to the conclusion. However, an experienced ophthalmologist (X.Z.) then telephoned the patients to help them through the questionnaire. In addition, bias induced by individual differences was inevitable considering the relatively limited sample size, and it was limited to a southeastern Asian cohort. Studies with larger cohorts are needed in the future. Finally, recent studies have found that the magnitude of the chord mu decreases significantly after surgery, which may explain the limited predictive effect of pre-operative chord mu to visual outcomes. Thus, further studies should be conducted focusing on correlation/difference between pre- and post- operative measures.

In conclusion, the results of this retrospective study revealed that a superior chord alpha orientation was an independent predictor of better postoperative visual outcomes. The predictive value of both the orientation and offset of chord alpha and chord mu for the postoperative TOA and HOA was insignificant. For patient reported visual outcomes, The nasal chord alpha was independently correlated with better patient reported general vision, near vision and absence of halo. A smaller kappa offset was independently correlated with better performance in patient reported near vision and fewer starbursts.
