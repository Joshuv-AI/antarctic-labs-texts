Orthogonal Enzyme–Substrate Design Strategy for Discovery of Human Protein Palmitoyltransferase Substrates
Authors: Robbins Puthenveetil, Shelby A. Auger, Natalia Gómez-Navarro, Mitra Shumsher Rana, Riki Das, Liam Brendan Healy, Kiall F. Suazo, Zhen-Dan Shi, Rolf E. Swenson, Mark D. Distefano, Anirban Banerjee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Protein palmitoylation, with more than 5000 substrates, is the most prevalent form of protein lipidation. Palmitoylated proteins participate in almost all areas of cellular physiology and have been linked to several human diseases. Twenty-three zDHHC enzymes catalyze protein palmitoylation with extensive overlap among the substrates of each zDHHC member. Currently, there is no global strategy to delineate the physiological substrates of individual zDHHC enzymes without perturbing the natural cellular pool. Here, we outline a general approach to accomplish this on the basis of synthetic orthogonal substrates that are only compatible with engineered zDHHC enzymes. We demonstrate the utility of this strategy by validating known substrates and use it to identify novel substrates of two human zDHHC enzymes. Finally, we employ this method to discover and explore conserved palmitoylation in a family of host restriction factors against pathogenic viruses, including SARS-CoV-2.

Protein lipidation is a key form of protein modification that intersects with all areas of cellular physiology.1 Protein palmitoylation results in the attachment of long-chain fatty acids, typically the 16-carbon palmitic acid, to cytosol-facing cysteines through a thioester linkage. Protein palmitoylation was discovered more than four decades ago,2 but the lability of the thioester and the reversibility of protein palmitoylation by thioesterases have made palmitoylation challenging to study. Over 10% of the proteome is targeted by this modification,3 which is catalyzed by members of the zDHHC family of integral membrane enzymes.4,5 The chemistry and biology of many of those enzymes remain poorly understood, and for several members, only a few substrates have been characterized. Intriguingly, there are no primary sequence determinants that dictate sites of protein palmitoylation: only a posteriori analyses revealed that cysteines proximal to the membrane have a high propensity for palmitoylation.

In the first step of catalysis, zDHHCs undergo autoacylation by palmitoyl-CoA, thereby generating an acylated enzyme, which, in a subsequent transpalmitoylation step, transfers the palmitoyl group to the substrate.6 Considering the 23 zDHHC enzymes and the approximately 5000 substrates identified in proteomic studies,3 each zDHHC must act on multiple protein substrates. Yet, it has been shown that many substrates can be palmitoylated by more than one zDHHC enzyme.7 Thus, the cellular zDHHC–substrate network is complex and interconnected, and much of it has not been elucidated. The pairing of a substrate with its cognate zDHHC enzyme has thus far been accomplished after confirming the palmitoylation status of a substrate and subsequently surveying the entire zDHHC family, one by one, through overexpression or knockdown strategies8,9 while simultaneously monitoring changes in the levels of palmitoylation of the substrate. Here, we describe a general zDHHC-targeted approach, which we had proposed earlier,10 that enables the identification of novel palmitoylated proteins without perturbation of the cellular pool of zDHHC enzymes. To our knowledge, this has not been applied to any transmembrane enzymes (although another palmitoyl transferase chemical genetic system was recently posted on a preprint server11).

To resolve the challenge of a shared substrate, palmitoyl CoA, and a catalytic mechanism common to all zDHHC enzymes, we developed a strategy (Scheme 1) on the basis of engineering orthogonal enzyme–substrate pairs for the exploration of a specific enzyme’s selectivity12 through the pairing of a synthetic fatty acyl CoA that can be utilized selectively by a mutant zDHHC enzyme but not the wild-type.10 This has been successfully applied to other enzyme families, including protein kinases,13 methyltransferases,14 acetyltransferases,15 glycosyltransferases,16 and ADP-ribosyltransferases,17 where one substrate is common to the family, but each member has distinct protein substrates. Here, we demonstrate that this strategy led to the identification of both known substrates and new substrates of zDHHC3 and zDHHC20, including a protein family thus far not reported to be palmitoylated in the literature.

Principle of Design and Use of Orthogonal Acyl CoA-zDHHC Pairs

All wild-type zDHHC enzymes can utilize palmitoyl CoA (left), but only the mutant of a specific zDHHC member can utilize the synthetic acyl CoA (right). In presence of the synthetic analogue, only the substrates of this specific zDHHC get labelled with the analogue, which are then identified with the use of the alkyne as a click chemistry handle.

Our high-resolution structure of human zDHHC20 (hDHHC20) with 2-bromopalmitate10 revealed that the fatty acyl-binding cavity is formed by the transmembrane helices (Figure 1A). On the basis of biochemical experiments,10 we thus hypothesized that synthetic fatty acyl CoA analogues could be designed, which should be selectively used by engineered variants of hDHHC20, by modulating cavity-lining residues10 but not by the wild-type. Interrogation of the substrates labeled in cellulo with the synthetic analogue in the presence of the engineered hDHHC20 would then enable identification of substrates of hDHHC20. Four new synthetic analogues of palmitoyl-CoA containing bulky aromatic substituents at the termini of the acyl chain (Figure 1B and S1) were tested with two hDHHC20 mutants, Y181A (DHHC20-YA) and C182F (DHHC20-CF), as well as the wild-type, using autoacylation assays.18,19 Analogue 3 showed remarkable selectivity for the mutants over the wild-type (Figure 1C), thereby leading us to synthesize two related alkyne-containing probes, 5 and 6 (Figure 1D and S2). Click chemistry assays showed that 6 is highly selective for hDHHC20-CF over wild-type hDHHC20 (Figure 1E) and shows better overall incorporation (Figure S3). Consistent with our experimental strategy, in these experiments, the expression level of the mutant was much higher than that of the native hDHHC20 (Figure S4).

Encouraged by these results, we next evaluated analogue 6 for another zDHHC enzyme using sequence homology to guide our choice of mutation sites.20 Human zDHHC3 (hDHHC3) was chosen because mutation of the residue homologous to hDHHC20 Cys182 (Figure S5) was known to have a significant effect on fatty acyl chain selectivity.21 Analogue 6 showed remarkable selectivity for the Ser183Phe mutant of hDHHC3 (DHHC3-SF) over the wild-type (Figure 1F and S5). Armed with these results, analogue 6 was employed with the hDHHC20-CF and the hDHHC3-SF mutants for the identification of the protein substrates of hDHHC20 and hDHHC3 using a bottom-up proteomic approach.

Next, the sample preparation workflow (Figure 2A) was optimized. Initial attempts resulted in a lack of reproducibility and robustness (Figure S6). This was caused by several problems, including reduced solubility of palmitoylated proteins, the deacylation of the labeled proteins, and excessive sample complexity resulting in a large amount of nonspecific enrichment. To improve solubility, dodecylphosphocholine (DPC), a detergent enabling efficient extraction of membrane proteins, was used. Additionally, to protect the thioester linkage, samples were processed in 2 days with minimal refreezing. This rapid processing helped prevent loss of modified proteins due to deacylation. Decreasing sample complexity was accomplished through a 2-fold increase in washes during the enrichment process. Finally, to increase certainty in the proteins identified as substrates of the respective zDHHCs in the analysis, all proteins with a sequence coverage <5% were removed. This filtering method has been previously used to remove weakly identified proteins and simplify complex data, thereby increasing statistical confidence in the proteins identified.22−24

The above optimization yielded proteomic data for hDHHC3-SF and hDHHC20-CF, which identified 167 and 16 enriched proteins, respectively (Figure 2B,E; Table S1). Encouragingly, in both, hDHHC3 and hDHHC20 were found to be enriched in their respective data set. This is consistent with the formation of autoacylated thioester intermediates involved in the catalytic mechanisms of these enzymes. Of the proteins identified, 56% of those screened with hDHHC3-SF and 88% of those using hDHHC20-CF were predicted to be palmitoylated using SwissPalm (Figure 2D,G). Promisingly, more proteins were enriched in the hDHHC3-SF data set, thereby suggesting a broader substrate scope compared with hDHHC20, which is consistent with the literature.1 Among the top identified substrates for hDHHC3-SF were the cysteine string protein α (CSPα) and tetraspanin-28 (CD81). Those proteins span the enrichment range of the data set (see ranking by π-value,25Figure 2C), so they capture the depth of the data. For hDHHC20-CF, heme oxygenase 2 (HMOX2) and serine incorporator 1 (SERINC1) were chosen for additional analysis. SERINC1 was not predicted to be palmitoylated using SwissPalm but was chosen for further investigation as a potentially novel palmitoylated protein.

The presence of established substrates of hDHHC3 in our data set validates our probe design and strategy. One of the previously known substrates is CSPα,26 a member of the DNAJ/Hps40 family of cochaperones. Coexpression of hDHHC3 but not catalytically inactive hDHHS3 increased the palmitoylation of CSPα, thereby suggesting that CSPα is, indeed, palmitoylated by hDHHC3 (Figure 3A). We next sought to validate CD81, a known palmitoylated protein27 but one that has not been shown to be a substrate of hDHHC3. Wild-type hDHHC3 but not catalytically inactive hDHHS3, when coexpressed with CD81, increased palmitoylation, thus confirming CD81 as a substrate of hDHHC3 (Figure 3B).

hDHHC20 plays important roles in a wide range of cellular processes ranging from regulation of the SARS-CoV-2 lifecycle28,29 to controlling receptor localization;30 however, the number of known hDHHC20 substrates remains low. Thus, we focused on two novel substrates of hDHHC20 identified in our data set, HMOX2 and SERINC1. HMOX2 is a heme oxygenase critical for heme metabolism31 that has never been reported to be palmitoylated. Our experiments indicated robust palmitoylation of HMOX2 and that HMOX2 is a substrate of hDHHC20 (Figure 4A).

SERINC1 belongs to a family of transmembrane proteins conserved from yeast to humans32 that plays an important role in the restriction of several pathogenic viruses, including HIV-1 and SARS-CoV-2.33−35 However, there has been no report of any SERINC protein being palmitoylated. Click-chemistry-based assays revealed SERINC1 to be a palmitoylated protein (Figure 4B) and a substrate of hDHHC20 (Figure 4C). Despite its importance, the biochemical characterization of the SERINC family of proteins is limited. Accordingly, we investigated whether palmitoylation is a conserved feature within the family. All SERINC family members, except SERINC4, which could not be tested because of poor expression, were shown to be palmitoylated and substrates of hDHHC20 (Figures 4B,C). This provides an exciting impetus for further investigation of the role and biological importance of palmitoylation in SERINC function.

The complexity and expanse of protein palmitoylation have grown explosively in the last two decades since the discovery of zDHHC enzymes. Almost certainly, the actual substrates of each zDHHC enzyme vary across cell types and in different disease contexts. To appreciate and investigate the biology of this modification, it is essential to identify the physiological substrates of each zDHHC enzyme in the different settings. The strategy described here provides a much-needed robust platform for enabling this discovery process. Beyond simply determining the enzymes responsible for the palmitoylation of known proteins, we demonstrate that this approach can also lead to the identification of new and previously undiscovered palmitoylated proteins, thereby further expanding our understanding of the chemistry and biology of protein palmitoylation.
