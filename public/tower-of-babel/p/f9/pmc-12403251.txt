A roadmap for navigating child health research data sharing across Canada and beyond – building on UCAN CAN-DU
Authors: Brittany Gerber, Gillian R. Currie, Alexander Mosoiu, Alexander Bernier, Francois P. Bernier, Kym M. Boycott, Guillermo Fiebelkorn, Kristien Hens, Bartha M. Knoppers, Claire LeBlanc, Stephen W. Scherer, David Shaw, Chris Viney, Carl Virtanen, Susanne M. Benseler, Rae S. M. Yeung, Deborah A. Marshall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSharing health data within and across jurisdictions is important for research and improving healthcare quality; however, researchers, governments and funders must balance the benefits of data sharing with data privacy. Though frameworks exist to guide data sharing it can be difficult to translate these into practice. Therefore, our aim was to create a practical example of data sharing for researchers in pediatric rheumatology.MethodsWe utilized expert consultation with leaders in child health, genomics, rheumatology, bioethics, privacy and records, bioinformatics, and legal counsel to better understand barriers and enablers for sharing of health data. We used these barriers to frame the learnings of UCAN CAN-DU (Understanding Childhood Arthritis Network Canada-Netherlands Personalized Medicine Network in Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatic Diseases) which is a collaboration that collects and shares phenotypic, genomic, health economic and patient reported data across centers in Canada and the Netherlands in order to provide a real-life, practical example of data sharing across borders in pediatric rheumatology.ResultsBarriers to data sharing include lack of standardized consent, ethics review processes for multi-site projects, developing data governance frameworks aligned with institutional and regulatory requirements, differing data standards and a lack of interoperability, and managing data access. UCAN CAN-DU provides lessons for navigating these barriers through standardized consent forms, centralized ethics review and reciprocity agreements; building a network to support data interoperability and harmonization of procedures; documentation to support data sharing, including legal agreements, utilization of a secure healthcare data storage compute facility, and a data access advisory committee with clear policies for secondary data use.ConclusionWe have shared how UCAN CAN-DU navigated barriers to data sharing, providing an example of data sharing for researchers in pediatric rheumatology. This work highlights the importance of research networks in establishing interoperability including minimal data sets, standard operating procedures, and institutional legal/contracts partnerships that ultimately support data sharing. The barriers and enablers presented are broadly applicable across countries and provide direction on areas for future research and initiatives to foster data sharing.

Background

Sharing of health data plays an important role in improving health care quality, surveillance, system management, and research [1]. The data sharing landscape is complex and requires balancing the benefits of sharing health data within and across jurisdictions and the need to maintain and ensure patient privacy, as indicated by legal, ethical and regulatory boards. In Canada, the Tri-Agency research funding agencies support data management and data sharing practices as part of the Tri-Agency Research Data Management Policy which guides health researchers and institutions [2], with a joint commitment to increasing the availability of research data within the scientific community in a way that is equitable, ethical and efficient [3, 4].

Guidance and frameworks for data sharing have been developed for some data types, including genomics and related health data by the Global Alliance for Genomics and Health (GA4GH) [5–8], biobanking for children with rheumatic diseases [9], neuroscience data [10] and clinical trial data [11]; however, many researchers are still ‘bogged down’ by the application of these frameworks into actual practice. There are gaps in the existing guidance, as these are largely focused on genomics data and biobanking and provide little guidance for other types of data. As a result, while national policies, frameworks and guidance exist to promote research data management and stewardship practices, data sharing still only happens to a limited extent [12, 13]. Practical guidance regarding the uptake and application of principles and guidelines in the development of international research collaborations is needed.

To provide researchers with a practical example of a successful data sharing initiative in pediatric rheumatology, our aim was to identify barriers to data sharing, and to share our learnings from a successful data sharing initiative in pediatric rheumatology.

Methods

In order to create a practical example of data sharing, we combined two approaches. We conducted a structured expert consultation focused on identifying barriers and enablers for data sharing. We then used the learnings from the UCAN CAN-DU project to demonstrate how these barriers, identified through expert consultation, can be overcome.

Expert consultation

We used a structured approach to consult with experts in a variety of fields; we purposively sampled international leaders in child health research, genomic medicine, rheumatology and rare diseases, bioethics, privacy and records, bioinformatics, as well as legal counsel. In total, three expert meetings were held over a timeframe of two months. These meetings were led by our research team (DM, GC, BG), and included two to five experts per 90-minute session. Prior to these sessions, we sent background information on the current data sharing landscape and the UCAN CAN-DU project, along with a list of three topics to be discussed during our meeting: (1) critical barriers to data sharing (of multiple types of data) across international borders, (2) key enablers of data sharing (e.g., how to address, remove or mitigate barriers), and (3) which barriers should be addressed first (e.g., are there barriers acting as a bottle neck that should be prioritized). Following these sessions, a summary document was prepared and shared with the experts as a member checking exercise, providing an opportunity for feedback and clarification [14, 15].

UCAN CAN-DU

Understanding Childhood Arthritis Network (UCAN) is an international research network, with a primary goal of harmonizing existing practices and research infrastructure, including the creation of universally accepted standards for the collection, processing, transfer and storage of biological samples from patients with childhood arthritis [16].

Building on UCAN’s standardized research infrastructure, including globally standardized data and sample collection, phenotype reporting, and archiving systems catalyzed by the UCAN initiative, UCAN CAN-DU is a multinational project focused on establishing a personalized medicine network for childhood arthritis and rheumatic diseases. The network is comprised of two primary research hubs, SickKids Research Institute in Toronto, Canada and the University Medical Centre Utrecht, Utrecht, Netherlands (hereinafter referred to as SickKids and Utrecht, respectively). The UCAN CAN-DU project aims to enroll children across all pediatric rheumatology centers in Canada and the Netherlands and capture the clinical phenotype, medication effectiveness and toxicity, standardized monitoring instruments, genomic data including genes, transcripts, and protein level data in addition to functional pathways, patient experiences and preferences and detailed health economic data. The UCAN CAN-DU eHealth platform comprises a set of electronic, web-based health tools for clinical and patient reported data capture, developed with the Centre for Digital Therapeutics at the University Health Network in Toronto. The web-based tools include platforms for both clinicians and patients, and clinical and electronic patient reported outcome (ePRO) data feeds directly into the eHealth Platform’s database. The UCAN CAN-DU collaboration aligns with the jointly developed international recommendations for collaborative data and specimen sharing for pediatric research from the Single Hub and Access point for paediatric Rheumatology in Europe (SHARE) initiative [9].

The project team worked with the UCAN CAN-DU program manager (AM), and key documents including the UCAN CAN-DU website, consent forms, legal and governance framework including the data access policy were reviewed to contextualize several areas of the project pertaining to data sharing, including data governance, consent, data collection, and data sharing processes, and to inform lessons that could be learned from the project and applied by other researchers in future data sharing projects.

Results

Through the expert consultation we identified barriers to data sharing, including consent and ethics oversight, interoperability, data governance, infrastructure and technology, data access, and types of data collected and shared, detailed below. Below we will describe how UCAN CAN-DU approached and overcame these barriers.

Consent

Standardized consent (or lack thereof) was noted as a key barrier to data sharing: our experts highlighted the need for the development of a universal, standardized consent form, including language regarding data sharing and future use, as an enabling cornerstone.

Our experts also discussed data sharing and use beyond the scope of the study a participant is consenting to, including the use of dynamic consenting models to periodically re-assess consent (e.g., in instances where we are asking for consent for future data sharing, how long does consent really last after the initial consent, without a formal withdrawal). In the case of pediatric participants, alternate guidance may recommend the development of a strategy at the outset of the research to address the ongoing consent and re-contact of participants which considers the potential for cohort attrition. This can include notifying participants of their right to opt-out from the continued use of their data at the time of majority (for the purposes of research consent), the use of anonymization or a REB waiver of consent to determine the appropriate ongoing use of data after the conclusion of the study, or other approaches accounting for both jurisdictional requirements and the nature of the ongoing relationship between the research participants and the researchers [17, 18].

Ethics oversight

Our expert group identified that centralization of ethics review may foster an environment for data sharing, as current processes for review of multi-site projects can be a lengthy process for the lead site as well as subsequent sites. For example, Clinical Trials Ontario Streamlined Ethics Review is an initiative overcoming the need for local review at each study site that offers centralized review, utilizing the same ethics application forms and standardized consent form templates for all study sites [19].

In the UCAN CAN-DU project:

Multilingual ethics application, consent and assent forms and legal contracts were required for each center in Canada; some centers are embedded in frameworks such as Clinical Trials Ontario [19] or have reciprocity agreements with other centers. Contracts that specify the number of patients to be enrolled, the costs associated with enrollment including personnel, processing and administrative costs had to be established between the national coordinating centers and each participating center.

Another barrier highlighted through expert consultation is the problematic relationship between the legal frameworks for data sharing and ethical committees or institutional review boards who are not necessarily well-equipped to provide a comprehensive review of what a project entails regarding how data will flow and what legal barriers may be encountered.

Interoperability

As identified through our expert consultation, differing data standards and a lack of interoperability, within Canada and internationally, limit data sharing. For example, within Canada there is variation within and across provinces and territories, as for clinical health data, several different electronic medical records are used.

Our expert consultation highlighted that there is a need for a national set of data standards or reference datasets to create interoperability, such as for example, the Pan Canadian Health Data Strategy [21].

Data governance

A major hurdle to data sharing identified through expert consultation is the lack of consistent approaches to data sharing and the need for robust data governance frameworks. While most jurisdictions share data protection or privacy laws that they have agreed to, the rules for how regulatory principles are achieved have not been agreed to. That is, things such as record keeping obligations, downstream use of data/re-use, are done in a bespoke way across jurisdictions, regulators, and even institutions. These differences can be challenging for researchers and institutions to navigate.

Agreements including a Collaboration/Consortium Agreement between SickKids and Utrecht and a General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)-compliant Data Processing Agreement, which supports the transfer of personal data from the Utrecht to SickKids, were executed to enable data sharing across borders.In addition to the primary research hubs, the project involves several study sites. All sites completed an Accession Form to agree to the terms of the master collaboration agreement between SickKids and Utrecht. While the UCAN-CAN DU project illustrates how data governance can be approached at the project level (e.g., through GDPR-compliant agreements), our experts highlighted approaches to creating harmonized approaches to data governance broadly, including ongoing work by the GA4GH in trying to create harmonized ways of approaching technical and security features involved in data sharing, as well as regulatory considerations [5–8, 22]. There is a need to insulate best efforts compliance from liability or serious enforcement actions, as researchers and institutions are disincentivized to share data given the penalties for non-compliance and the lack of guidance as to which technical approaches are acceptable.

Attention to Indigenous autonomy and data sovereignty, including adherence to the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance, is paramount where data on Indigenous peoples is involved [23, 24].

Infrastructure and technology

Our experts agreed that a key to encouraging data sharing is finding approaches on the technical and legal side of things that communities agree are low risk in terms of data privacy but high value in terms of downstream data use.

In the UCAN CAN-DU project:

UCAN CAN-DU operates as a central data repository, with data from all Canadian and Dutch sites stored at HPC4Health, a compute facility for healthcare data, where the eHealth tools and platform are hosted. During our expert consultation, we discussed federated data analysis or secure processing environments, where the data remains locally stored [25]. However, these approaches require technical infrastructure that must be widely adopted. If the tools and platforms are in place, then researchers can use them. Within Canada, we first need to focus on developing a single national platform for data, with analytical tools and capacity to support data sharing in Canada and with institutions working together to make sure their platforms are compatible.

Data access

During the expert consultations, we discussed how data access is granted. Often, secondary use of research data is managed by the principal investigator or by a project data access committee.

Based on each user’s role and access scope, they have varying access to the main dashboard, biological data, clinical data, exploratory data analysis, and/or ability to download biological or clinical data. For example, site investigators are granted site-level access, including an overview of biological data, clinical data, exploratory data analysis for their site, while study analysts have access to all data modules and the ability to download data (clinical, biological, and health economics) for the study.Outside of the UCAN CAN-DU network, investigators may apply to access clinical data and/or biologic data/samples for use in secondary research. Another approach to managing data access, highlighted during our expert consultations, is the adoption of a broader data access committee (e.g., at the level of the consortium, the repository, or the research institution). Institutions must also consider concerns of dual use of research data (e.g., when the data could be misused to do harm) and how to manage data requests (e.g., from private companies through freedom of information legislation (which varies from province to province)). A recent development in some countries is a focus on “knowledge security” which concerns not only dual use but also the possibility that data might be accessed by a country that shouldn’t have access to it – in terms of both intellectual property (IP) and sensitive data [28].

Types of data collected

During our consultation, experts reflected on the implications of sharing for various types of data and noted that data sharing may also be complicated by both the type and extent of data being linked; for example, the risk of re-identification for genomics data on its own versus genomics data linked to clinical and demographic data, as is often collected for longitudinal research.

During the expert consultation, it was noted that the increased role of multi-omics and phenotypic data in precision medicine may warrant greater sensitivity given the increasing amount of sequenced information available and being shared. For example, sharing proteomic data raises additional questions about how much phenotypic information is being shared, which adds a layer of risk beyond re-identification, in that it may provide information on health status and disease risk [29]. While in the genomics space many projects are using the same sequencing technology, much of the technology is not unified for other multi-omics data and there is significant inter-platform and institutional variation.

Within Canada there is variation in what data is collected in electronic health records (and how), as well as what data is included in administrative databases and how that data is accessed across provinces. Likewise, for health economics data, there are various tools used, as well as multiple generic and condition-specific patient reported outcome measures available.

Enablers for data sharing

To enable data sharing, our expert consultation highlighted the need for: dedicated, sustainable funding streams to support data sharing activities (including human resources and widespread infrastructure, practical costs including storage and computing costs, and publishing in open access journals); the development of standardized consent forms and centralized ethics review for efficiency, and national data standards to enable interoperability; national infrastructure that is responsive and flexible, to house data and support data sharing efforts; and the establishment of approved and agreed methods of data sharing between accredited organizations and jurisdictions that researchers can use without concerns about compliance issues.

Our expert consultation also emphasized building on existing knowledge and incrementally building additional capacity for data sharing. At the project level, our experts encouraged an incremental approach, building on existing data sharing projects and standardization work, including creating cross-institutional networks, nationally and internationally, to continue to work towards standardized data standards and interoperability. It is also important to leverage work underway by international groups like the GA4GH, to perfect data sharing practices for genomics [5–8, 22], which can be extended and then be applied to other types of data.

Discussion

In this paper we identified barriers for data sharing through a structured expert consultation process and described how UCAN CAN-DU, which collects and shares multiple types of data from children with JIA from all pediatric centres in Canada and the Netherlands, navigated these barriers, providing a practical example for other researchers in pediatric rheumatology. Key learnings from UCAN CAN-DU included: implementation of standardized consent forms across sites, centralized ethics review and reciprocity agreements; drawing on the UCAN network to support data interoperability and standard operating procedures; documentation to support data sharing, including legal agreements, utilization of a secure healthcare data storage compute facility, and a data access advisory committee with clear policies for secondary data use. This work adds to the literature regarding barriers to data sharing, for example, barriers to sharing routinely collected public health information [30], secondary use of clinically acquired data [5], or challenges experienced by collaborative research projects in Europe [31], by illustrating practically how some barriers to data sharing were overcome by the UCAN CAN-DU project.

For future data sharing initiatives, our experts encouraged building on existing data sharing projects and standardization work, including the building of cross-institutional networks, nationally and internationally, to continue to work towards standardized data standards and interoperability. The UCAN story is an example how this can be a successful approach: As shown early in Fig. 1, the development of UCAN occurred in the context of, and in collaboration with, other existing research networks and organizations in pediatric rheumatology, including the Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA), the Pediatric Rheumatology Collaborative Study Group (PRCSG), the Pediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organisation (PRINTO), the Pediatric Rheumatology European Society (PReS), and the Canadian Alliance of Pediatric Rheumatology Investigators (CAPRI). Partnerships are imperative to support work towards harmonization and standardization. UCAN was able to create a network of pediatric rheumatologists, define a minimal clinical dataset for research, and standardize the research infrastructure required to enhance translational research for childhood arthritis, which was then leveraged to execute multi-national research projects, including UCAN CAN-DU [32] and UCAN CURE [33]. Translational research collaborations that will use data from this research program include European Joint Programme (EJP) funded project, Producing an Arthritis Value-Framework with Economic Evidence (PAVE) [34, 35], and Personalized Medicine to Tackle Immune Dysfunction in Refractory Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (PerMIDRIAR) [36]. Other researchers in pediatric rheumatology can similarly build from existing projects and standardization work to incrementally progress data sharing initiatives.

In Canada, there have been several initiatives to support interoperability, infrastructure and data governance for sharing of genomics data including: the Pan-Canadian Genome Library, which will provide a centralized federated national database for capturing, storing and accessing genomics data within Canada [37, 38] and the Canadian Genomic Data Commons initiative, to support a Canadian data sharing ecosystem for genomic data [39]. Earlier work in this space includes the development of Genomics4RD, the first pan-Canadian rare disease data platform, developed by the Care4Rare Canada Consortium [40–42]. The pan-Canadian All for One precision health partnership will address barriers for sharing genome-wide sequencing data for rare diseases in Canada through the Clinical Genomics Network [43, 44] and builds on the work of six implementation projects across Canada [45]. Most recently, in 2025, Genome Canada announced the launch of the Canadian Precision Health Initiative, which includes the building of a Canadian alliance for genomics in health, advancing the utility of genomic data, and implementing data governance and policies for genomic data [46].

More broadly, our expert consultation highlighted the importance of leveraging established and ongoing work to support data sharing, including guidance, frameworks, and technical solutions, such as those produced by the GA4GH, which has established several frameworks and policy guidance documents addressing specific components of responsible data sharing [5–8, 22]. In pediatric rheumatology, researchers can draw on the recommendations for data sharing from the Single Hub and Access point for paediatric Rheumatology in Europe (SHARE) initiative [9] and the GA4GH Key Implications of Data Sharing in Pediatric Genomics framework [7, 8].

A notable strength of this project is that we shared practical learnings based on the experiences to date of the UCAN CAN-DU project; because much of the learnings surrounding data sharing occur in the initiation and set up phase, there is a lot of rich information to draw from. Another strength is the inclusion of experts from a variety of backgrounds, from Canada and Europe, and beyond the UCAN project, in our consultation process. Thus, we believe that these barriers and learnings are relevant and applicable to other areas of child health and other countries. Of note, we have shared lessons and examples from the UCAN CAN-DU project to date. The project is ongoing – and as such, lessons learned will evolve with further experiences.

Conclusion

We conducted expert consultation to identify barriers to data sharing and focused on the UCAN CAN-DU study as an example of how these barriers can be overcome. UCAN CAN-DU paved the way to share clinical, genomic and health economic data between Canada and the Netherlands, providing several pertinent lessons for future research studies navigating these barriers. Our intention is that in focusing on barriers to data sharing that are commonly faced by researchers identified through expert consultation with researchers in a variety of fields related to data sharing, these learnings will be applicable more broadly to other areas of child health and data sharing.
