Natural food-derived antimicrobials against beer spoilage microorganisms
Authors: Chang Liu, Jiale Tang, Peng Lu, Satoka Takabayashi, Dai Yagaki, Kenta Tsutsumi, Suguru Okuda, Hideaki Itoh, Hidenori Matsukura, Saki Toyota, Masaki Shimokawa, Koji Suzuki, Koji Nagata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe spoilage of beer significantly threatens the quality and safety of products in the beverage industry. Certain natural foods contain beneficial bioactive components that are considered to be safer than chemical additives. This study aimed to identify extracts of natural foods as potential alternatives to chemical preservatives for controlling beer spoilage microorganisms. Among the two extraction methods applied to 176 natural foods, the extracts of clove alone effectively inhibited the growth of representative beer spoilage microorganisms, specifically Levilactobacillus brevis, Sporolactobacillus vineae, Pectinatus frisingensis, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. diastaticus. High-performance liquid chromatography and half-maximal inhibitory concentration analysis revealed that gallic acid and eugenol in cloves were active compounds with antimicrobial properties in vitro that were of a similar order of magnitude to those of commercial preservatives (potassium sorbate and sodium benzoate) under the tested conditions. Scanning electron microscopy observations and a fluorescence leakage assay using 3,3′-dipropylthiadicarbocyanine iodide indicated morphological alterations and membrane perturbation in treated microorganisms, except for the limited effect of gallic acid on S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus. Introduction

Beer, one of the most widely consumed fermented beverages, has occupied an undeniable place in human food culture since ancient times (McGovern et al. 2004). Health benefits, unique flavors, and textures have rendered it popular worldwide (Cuamatzin-García et al. 2022; Tamang et al. 2020). The microbial fermentation process typically occurs under anaerobic conditions, wherein the base ingredients, such as carbohydrates, are converted into alcohol, organic acids, and carbon dioxide (Chilton et al. 2015). These specific conditions are usually not conducive to other microorganisms’ growth; therefore, spoilage of beer is considered relatively difficult (Hutkins 2008). However, some microorganisms can still grow and multiply under such unfavorable conditions, leading to spoilage and deterioration in beer and related brewing systems (Rodríguez-Saavedra et al. 2020). These problems alter the organoleptic properties of beer (i.e., taste, texture, and odor) and affect product quality and marketability, leading to significant disturbances in the brewing industry.

Various microorganisms, particularly Levilactobacillus brevis (a Gram-positive bacterium), Pectinatus frisingensis (a Gram-negative bacterium), and the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. diastaticus, are well-known spoilage agents in the beer industry (Sakamoto and Konings 2003). Over the past three decades, L. brevis has been known to be a common cause of beer spoilage, having been associated with about 20%–50% of beer spoilage events in Europe and causing spoilage of other fermented foods, such as sausages (Suzuki 2020; Pellegrini et al. 2023). Recently, advances in brewing and packaging technology have significantly decreased the amount of dissolved oxygen in beer, which provides adequate conditions for the growth of the strictly anaerobic P. frisingensis (Suzuki 2011, 2020). This bacterium is known to cause turbidity and off-flavors in beer, seriously affecting its quality. Moreover, while Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a well-known fermenting microorganism, it can also spoil wine and bread under certain conditions (Pham et al. 2011; Minervini et al. 2015). S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus, a variant of this brewing yeast, can lead to undesirable sensory characteristics and excessive attenuation in beverages, causing significant quality control issues (Meier-Dörnberg et al. 2017, 2018). In addition, a sporulating bacterium called Sporolactobacillus vineae, which belongs to a genus of heat-resistant microorganisms, is difficult to eliminate during food processing, thus further increasing the risk of food spoilage. Although originally isolated from vineyard soil, members of this genus have been detected in brewing-related environments, and their spore-forming ability enables survival during thermal processing and subsequent growth under favorable conditions. Therefore, S. vineae was included in this study as a representative stress-tolerant spoilage model microorganism relevant to beer production systems, particularly for evaluating the resistance of spore-forming contaminants that may survive beverage processing conditions.

Although various measures exist, such as strict cleaning and hygiene management of factory equipment and the use of chemical preservatives in traditional food processing to prevent deterioration and spoilage, their effective implementation poses increasing challenges (Byakika et al. 2019; Tungkijanansin et al. 2020). In particular, there has been a surge in public concern regarding the use of chemical preservatives and their potential adverse health effects on the human body (Rydlo et al. 2006). However, chemical preservatives remain widely used in many sectors of the food supply chain, from production to consumption, especially at the transportation and distribution stages, where the risk of food spoilage often increases due to the uncertainties in refrigeration conditions. Therefore, there is a growing need for new strategies and technologies, especially those exploring natural substances as potential preservatives.

Because of their inherent bioactive profile, natural food extracts exhibit remarkable potential in food preservation (Giacometti et al. 2018). This is mainly attributed to their abundance of bioactive compounds, such as phenols, flavonoids, tannins, and alkaloids, which are highly valued for their unique antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties (Ávila et al. 2023; Jantapaso et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2022). Recent reviews further highlight the growing interest in plant-derived and probiotic-derived bioactive compounds as next-generation food biopreservatives (Ullah et al. 2022; Teneva and Denev 2023). These compounds are mainly found in various plant tissues, including the leaves, stems, flowers, bark, fruits, fruit rinds, seeds, and roots (Kuropka et al. 1991; Olawore et al. 2005). Thus, the targeted extraction of specific plant parts may help discover additional active compounds. Given the specific needs of the brewing industry, hot-water extraction methods are often preferred in food applications because of their safety and efficacy in extracting bioactive compounds. Although many studies have reported on inhibiting foodborne bacteria by natural product extracts, data on food-grade extracts effective against beer spoilage microorganisms such as P. frisingensis and S. vineae under beverage-relevant conditions remains limited (Pisoschi et al. 2018). In this study, “natural food-derived extracts” refer to extracts prepared from edible plant materials, culinary spices, or food-related botanical resources traditionally associated with the food supply chain, including materials consumed directly or indirectly as ingredients or seasonings.

Therefore, this study aimed to address this gap by screening hot-water and 50% ethanol extracts derived from different natural food materials for antimicrobial activity against representative beer spoilage microorganisms. The active compounds in the most effective extracts were identified, and their antimicrobial effects were compared with those of commonly used chemical preservatives, such as potassium sorbate and sodium benzoate. Furthermore, the microbial inhibitory mechanisms were also explored. The results suggest that clove-derived compounds may represent promising natural antimicrobial candidates, although further validation in real food matrices is required.

Materials and methods

Bacterial and yeast strains and growth conditions

The L. brevis strain used in this study was obtained from Asahi Breweries, Tokyo, Japan. This strain was initially isolated from the brewery environment and subsequently identified as a potent beer-spoilage bacterium because of its superior spoilage ability in a variety of beer types. S. vineae (JCM 14637) was purchased from the Japan Collection of Microorganisms. P. frisingensis (DSMZ 6306) was purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures. S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus (ATCC 13007) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Table 1). To simulate the conditions that are present in commercially available fermented products, all strains were anaerobically precultured using specific media formulations: L. brevis was cultured in beer-based modified De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (BMRS) broth, prepared with draft beer instead of water and vigorously stirred to remove air bubbles; P. frisingensis was cultured in thioglycolate II (TGC) broth medium; S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus were cultured in yeast and mold (YM) broth medium at 25 °C; and S. vineae was cultured in standard methods broth (SMB) medium at 30 °C. The pH of each medium was adjusted to 4.6 with hydrochloric acid to simulate the acidic conditions of commercially available beer, and the media were sterilized by autoclaving prior to inoculation. All anaerobic incubations were performed using an AnaeroPack system (Mitsubishi Gas Chemical, Japan).

Hot-water extraction and 50% ethanol extraction of natural food samples

In this study, we purchased 176 natural products (Fig. S1), including spices and herbs, from S&B Foods, Ohtsuya, and Tokiwa Phytochemical, while oranges, mushrooms, and seaweeds were obtained from local supermarkets in Japan. The plant materials selected for extraction were edible plant materials, culinary spices, or food-related botanical resources traditionally associated with the food supply chain, including materials consumed directly or indirectly as ingredients, seasonings, or food-related botanical materials. To maximize the extraction of active components from these natural products, we employed two methods. First, hot-water extraction was performed, in which the samples were dried, ground into powder, and extracted at a ratio of 1 g of powder to 80 mL of water at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by filtration and lyophilization under vacuum. To prepare antimicrobial experiments, these dried extracts were redissolved in sterile water and diluted to a final concentration of 7500 µg/mL. Second, 50% ethanol extraction was performed by shaking 1 g of powder with 5 mL of 50% ethanol at room temperature (25 °C) for 10 min, followed by filtration and dilution so that the final ethanol concentration in each assay well was 2%, in order to avoid affecting microbial growth. Hot-water and aqueous ethanol extraction approaches were selected because both water and aqueous ethanol are widely used food-grade solvents compatible with industrial food processing, enabling efficient recovery of hydrophilic and moderately hydrophobic bioactive compounds while avoiding the use of toxic organic solvents. Although alternative extraction techniques using other solvent systems may recover additional hydrophobic compounds, the present study focused on food-compatible extraction methods that are readily applicable to fermented beverage production systems. All extracts were stored at –30 °C until use. An ethanol-only control (2% final concentration) was included in all antimicrobial assays to account for any residual solvent effects.

Screening of natural food extracts

A 96-well plate was used for antimicrobial experiments with natural product extracts. The controls included a sterile control (300 µL of medium and sterilized water), a positive control (25 µL of cells plus 275 µL of medium and antimicrobial agents: chloramphenicol at 100 µg/mL for L. brevis, S. vineae, and P. frisingensis; or hygromycin B at 200 µg/mL for S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus), and negative control (25 µL of cells plus 300 µL of medium and sterilized water). The test group had 25 µL of cells, 275 µL of medium, and the extract prepared in the previous section. The initial cell counts were kept at 103–104 cfu/mL. OD600 measurements were performed before and after incubation at 25 °C (30 °C for S. vineae) under anaerobic conditions. Samples with an initial OD600 below 0.5 were evaluated for their inhibition rate by comparing the OD differences of the initial phase vs. stationary phase, with an inhibition rate of ≥ 80% being considered effective. The 80% inhibition threshold was selected as a practical criterion for primary high-throughput screening to identify extracts with sufficiently strong antimicrobial activity for subsequent dose–response analyses while minimizing the inclusion of marginally active samples. Samples with an initial OD600 ≥ 0.5, indicating high medium turbidity that could interfere with turbidity-based growth evaluation, were diluted and incubated for an additional week, and effectiveness was determined by colony counting, with no or few colonies indicating effectiveness. The OD600 threshold of 0.5 was established to avoid the effect of turbidity of sample used. All screening experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates (n = 3), each measured in technical triplicates.

Dose-dependent effects of the extracts

Based on the initial screening results, which indicated that the hot-water extracts and the 50% ethanol extracts yielded similar effects in terms of antimicrobial activity, besides considering the cost-effectiveness and plant safety of the compounds, we decided to focus our subsequent experiments on the antimicrobial properties of the hot-water extracts. We investigated the dose-dependent effects of the hot-water extracts with significant potential for application, including clove, cinnamon, bilberry, ginkgo, maca, and milk thistle. The experimental manipulations and methods were carried out according to the procedures described in the previous section. High-performance liquid chromatography analysis

Methanol and acetic acid for HPLC analysis were obtained from Fujifilm-Wako, Japan. Gallic acid and eugenol standards (Nacalai Tesque, Japan) were used to verify the ingredients of the natural products. Freeze-dried clove was dissolved in Milli-Q water at a concentration of 7500 µg/mL, and the mixture and each standard were injected into an HPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) using an injection volume of 100 μL. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a PEGASIL ODS SP100AQ column (250 mm × 4.6 mm i.d., 5 µm; Senshu Scientific, Japan). Solvent A was 98.5% Milli-Q water, 1% methanol, and 0.5% acetic acid; solvent B was 98.5% methanol, 1% Milli-Q water, and 0.5% acetic acid. The linear gradient of 0–5 min, 0% solvent B; 5–35 min, 0%–80% B; and 35–40 min, 80%–80% B was applied at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The chromatograms were monitored at 280 nm.

Determination of half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of antimicrobial compounds and commercial preservatives

Twofold serial dilutions were prepared from stock solutions of gallic acid and eugenol, with a highest tested concentration of 7500 µg/mL. The IC50 value of propyl gallate was also determined for comparison, as it is derived from gallic acid and widely used as a food antioxidant. Propyl gallate was included only as a reference compound derived from gallic acid for comparative evaluation and was not considered part of the screened natural extracts. This study aimed to compare the antimicrobial activities of gallic acid, eugenol, and propyl gallate with those of the commercial preservatives, potassium sorbate and sodium benzoate, by determining IC50 values. Potassium sorbate and sodium benzoate are two important preservatives widely used in the food industry. CAS Registry Numbers of all tested compounds are provided in Table 2. For each assay, diluted antimicrobial solutions were mixed with microbial suspensions (103–104 CFU/mL) in 96-well plates and incubated under the corresponding culture conditions described above. The inhibition rate was calculated as described based on OD600 changes before and after incubation, and the concentration–survival curve was fitted using non-linear regression to obtain the IC50 value. All IC50 determinations were performed using three independent biological replicates (n = 3), each measured in technical triplicates.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of gallic acid and eugenol were determined using a broth microdilution method in 96-well microplates under the same culture conditions described above for each microorganism. Serial dilutions of each compound were prepared in the corresponding culture media to obtain the desired final concentrations. Each well contained 137.5 μL of medium, 137.5 μL of diluted antimicrobial solution, and 25 μL of microbial suspension adjusted to 103–104 CFU/mL. Sterility controls (medium only), growth controls (medium plus inoculum), and solvent controls were included in all assays. After incubation, MIC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no visible microbial growth was observed. To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), 100 μL aliquots was taken from wells showing no visible growth in the MIC assay and spread onto the corresponding agar plates. After incubation under the appropriate culture conditions, plates were examined for colony formation. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no colony growth was detected on agar plates. MIC assays were performed in three independent biological replicates (n = 3), each measured in technical triplicates. MBC evaluation was conducted using samples obtained from these MIC assays.

Antimicrobial mechanism

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Precultured microorganisms were treated with the potential antimicrobial agents at the 4 × IC50 concentration for 2 h. The negative control consisted of precultured microorganisms without treatment, whereas the positive control consisted of precultured microorganisms treated with 0.125% Triton X-100 for 2 h. The sample was fixed with 1 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde. The fixed sample was washed twice with phosphate buffer for 10 min each and treated with different concentrations of ethanol (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%) for 30 min before dehydration. The sample was secured with carbon tape on both sides of the specimen holder, coated with platinum-palladium in an Ion Sputter (E-1030, HITACHI, Japan), and examined using a scanning electron microscope (S-4100, HITACHI, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 5.0 kV. Experiments were conducted using three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

Fluorescence leakage assay

Precultured microorganisms were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 15 min and washed with an acetate buffer (5 mM sodium acetate, 20 mM glucose, pH 4.6). Subsequently, the addition of 0.2 mM EDTA and centrifugation at 4000 × g for 15 min were used to increase the permeability of the outer membrane, thus enabling the uptake of dyes. Cells treated with EDTA were suspended in acetate buffer containing 0.1 mM EDTA and the membrane-potential-sensitive fluorescent dye 3,3-dipropylthiadicarbocyanine iodide (DiSC3(5)) at a concentration of 4 μM (AAT Bioquest, Japan), transferred to an anaerobic environment and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C in the dark (Winkel et al. 2016). The cells were then washed with acetate buffer and diluted again. Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates (n = 3), each measured in technical triplicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was applied. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Screening of antimicrobial natural food extracts

In the initial screening, the results obtained from the hot-water extracts demonstrated that 14 natural food extracts, including clove and cardamom, were effective against L. brevis at 7500 µg/mL. Similarly, 69 extracts, including mace and thyme, were effective against S. vineae at the same concentration, whereas 70 extracts, including mace and garlic, were effective against P. frisingensis, and nine extracts, including garlic and chives, were effective against S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus at 7500 µg/mL. Conversely, 50% ethanol extracts of clove, hop, and galangal showed an inhibitory effect on L. brevis, whereas 121 extracts were effective against S. vineae, 37 were effective against P. frisingensis, and 21 were effective against S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus.

Moreover, several extracts could inhibit the growth of two or three different microorganisms. Notably, among the two extraction methods employed here, only the extract derived from clove effectively inhibited the growth of all four tested beer spoilage microorganisms (Table S1).

Dose-dependent effects of the extracts

Among the six extracts selected for further evaluation, only the hot-water extract of clove was effective in inhibiting the growth of all four tested microorganisms (L. brevis, S. vineae, P. frisingensis, and S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus) in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1). The inhibitory effect increased with increasing extract concentration. Notably, the crude extract of clove at concentrations as low as 750 μg/mL effectively inhibited the growth of the Gram-positive S. vineae and Gram-negative P. frisingensis (Fig. 1B, C), with a significant difference (P < 0.05) observed vs. the control. However, maca and milk thistle extracts did not exhibit antimicrobial activity under the tested conditions (Fig. 1).

HPLC analysis of the potential antimicrobial agents of clove

Two peaks at 15.8 min and 35.6 min with the highest peak intensities were observed in the HPLC analysis of clove (Fig. 2A). Co-injection experiments with standard compounds confirmed that both peaks stemmed from clove constituents. Notably, peak 1 at 15.8 min corresponded to gallic acid, which exhibited hydrophilic properties consistent with the methanol concentration in the mobile phase (Fig. 2B). Peak 2 at 35.6 min was identified as eugenol, a hydrophobic compound, as the methanol concentration had reached its maximum (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that gallic acid and eugenol are major detectable constituents of the clove extract under the present analytical conditions (Fig. 2). Furthermore, HPLC fractionation indicated that fractions corresponding to gallic acid and eugenol exhibited antimicrobial activity, whereas the remaining detectable fractions showed no measurable inhibitory activity under the tested conditions (Fig. S2).

Comparison of antimicrobial effects between potential antimicrobial agents extracted from clove and commercially available preservatives

Eugenol exhibited remarkable antimicrobial activity against L. brevis, S. vineae, P. frisingensis, and S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus, with half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 110, 180, 130, and 250 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 3, Table 2). Gallic acid also demonstrated antimicrobial activity against L. brevis (IC50 = 3,000 µg/mL), S. vineae (IC50 = 400 µg/mL), and P. frisingensis (IC50 = 91 µg/mL), but was not effective against S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus. The gallic acid-related antioxidant propyl gallate also demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect against L. brevis (IC50 = 130 µg/mL), S. vineae (IC50 = 100 µg/mL), P. frisingensis (IC50 = 29 µg/mL), and S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus (IC50 = 1200 µg/mL). Compared with commercially available preservatives, potassium sorbate showed IC50 values of 1300, 1400, 160, and 110 µg/mL against L. brevis, S. vineae, P. frisingensis, and S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus, respectively. In contrast, sodium benzoate showed IC50 values of 360, 240, 76, and 220 µg/mL against the same microorganisms, respectively (Fig. 3, Table 2). The results showed that gallic acid, eugenol, and propyl gallate exhibited antimicrobial activity in acidic environments with IC50 values comparable to those of commercial preservatives, under the tested in vitro conditions, indicating their potential applicability.

Mechanistic analyses

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) evaluation of the morphology of treated microorganisms

The morphological changes of the target microorganisms were observed using SEM to characterize the mechanism underlying the effect of the active compounds on the cells. As shown in Fig. 4A–D, the untreated cells exhibited a typical bacillus fungal morphology with a smooth and intact surface. In turn, the cells of the positive control were visibly disrupted (arrows) (Fig. 4E–H).

SEM images of L. brevis, S. vineae, P. frisingensis, and S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus treated with 4 × IC50 of gallic acid, eugenol, or propyl gallate for 2 h. Scale bar, 1 µm. A–D untreated control, E–H treated with Triton X-100, I–L treated with gallic acid, M–P treated with eugenol, and Q–T treated with propyl gallate

L. brevis treated with gallic acid, eugenol, and propyl gallate exhibited numerous small perforations and wrinkles on their surface (arrows), resulting in cellular damage (Fig. 4I, M, Q). S. vineae treated with gallic acid displayed severe wrinkling on their surface, whereas treatment with eugenol and propyl gallate led to wrinkling and lysis of the outer cell membrane (arrows) (Fig. 4J, N, R). Gallic acid, eugenol, and propyl gallate altered the cell morphology of P. frisingensis, as many malformed cells were observed, and there were wrinkles on the cell surface (arrows) (Fig. 4K, O, S). Notably, S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus cells treated with gallic acid exhibited no changes in morphology compared with naturally grown cells (arrows) (Fig. 4L), which is consistent with the results of the IC50, indicating that gallic acid has no inhibitory effect on S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus (Fig. 3, Table 2). In contrast, eugenol caused significant damage to S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus cells, and cells treated with propyl gallate exhibited melting features on the surface (arrows) (Fig. 4P, T).

Taken together, these SEM observations indicate the presence of morphological changes consistent with cell envelope perturbation, although they do not alone establish a definitive membrane-disruptive mechanism.

Fluorescence leakage assay

By measuring changes in fluorescence intensity, it was possible to assess the effects of different compounds on the cell membrane (Fig. 5A). The comparison of the fluorescence leakage induced by the compounds with that of the positive control allowed the quantification of the effect of the compounds on the cell membranes of the target microorganisms (Fig. S3). After treating L. brevis with 4 × IC50, 2 × IC50, and IC50 concentrations of gallic acid, emission rates of 30%, 14%, and 11% were observed, respectively (Fig. 5B). In comparison, the emission rates of S. vineae under the same treatment conditions were 16%, 15%, and 6%, respectively (Fig. 5C). In particular, when P. frisingensis and S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus were treated with a 4 × IC50 concentration of gallic acid, the emission rate only decreased to 3.0%. However, the treatment of P. frisingensis with different concentrations of gallic acid yielded significant differences, implying that gallic acid disrupted the cell membrane of P. frisingensis, although the effect was marginally significant. In turn, treatment of S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus did not yield significant differences between concentrations (Fig. 5D, E).

Under similar experimental conditions, treatment of L. brevis, S. vineae, and P. frisingensis with 4 × IC50 concentrations of eugenol resulted in emission rates of 36%, 25%, and 24%, respectively, indicating a tendency toward a decrease with decreasing concentrations (Fig. 5). For S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus, the emission rates were 24%, 21%, and 23% after treatment with 4 × IC50, 2 × IC50, and IC50 concentrations of eugenol, respectively (Fig. 5D).

In addition, the emission rates were 56%, 42%, 35%, and 49% after treatment of L. brevis, S. vineae, P. frisingensis, and S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus with 4 × IC50 concentrations of propyl gallate, respectively, also indicating a decreasing trend with decreasing concentration (Fig. 5).

Overall, these fluorescence leakage results demonstrate measurable but moderate membrane perturbation, suggesting that membrane effects contribute to the antimicrobial activity of the tested compounds but are unlikely to represent the sole mechanism of action.

The fluorescence intensity of the sample treated with the addition of 0.125% Triton X-100 to the microbial solution was used as the positive control.

MIC and MBC values

MIC measurements (Table 3) indicated that eugenol exhibited MIC values of approximately 1000 µg/mL against L. brevis, 500 µg/mL against P. frisingensis, and 500 µg/mL against S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus. In contrast, gallic acid showed weaker antimicrobial activity, with an MIC of approximately 6000 µg/mL against L. brevis and 800 µg/mL against P. frisingensis. The MIC of gallic acid against S. cerevisiae var. diastaticus could not be determined within the tested concentration range (IC50 > 7500 µg/mL). The MIC for S. vineae could not be determined because reproducible growth suitable for MIC assessment was not achieved under the experimental conditions used in the present study.

Discussion

The use of natural products as food preservatives is rising as public health awareness increases. The present study found that among the two extraction methods used for 176 natural foods, the clove extracts exhibited in vitro antimicrobial activity against all four types of beer spoilage microorganisms. Aqueous extracts of clove are more cost-effective than organic solvent extracts, as they are more water soluble, healthier for humans, and able to reduce the risk of flammability during industrial production (Singh et al. 2022). These findings suggest that clove extracts may represent promising candidate antimicrobials under controlled in vitro conditions, although validation in model and real food systems is required. The strength of the present study lies in the systematic screening of 176 food-compatible extracts under beer-mimicking acidic and anaerobic conditions against multiple representative beer spoilage microorganisms.

Recent advancements in technology, especially the enhancement of sealing techniques for canned beverages, have created a more stringent anaerobic environment, which has led to an increase in incidents of beverage spoilage caused by the anaerobic bacterium P. frisingensis (Haikara and Helander 2006; Juvonen 2015). This study demonstrated that clove extracts inhibited the growth of P. frisingensis and S. vineae at a lower concentration of 750 µg/mL. Further studies determined that eugenol and gallic acid, the main active constituents of cloves, and propyl gallate, a derivative of gallic acid, showed significant antimicrobial activity. Notably, these natural bioactive compounds exhibited in vitro IC50 values comparable, under the tested broth culture conditions, to those of potassium sorbate and sodium benzoate, which are chemical preservatives commonly used in foods and beverages. However, these comparisons are limited to in vitro conditions, and antimicrobial performance may differ in complex food matrices, including those with higher ethanol concentrations, hop-derived compounds, and heterogeneous colloidal structures.

MIC and MBC determinations supported the IC50 findings, with eugenol generally exhibiting stronger activity than gallic acid. It should be noted that IC50 values and MIC values are not directly comparable, as they are derived from fundamentally different methodological endpoints: IC50 represents the compound concentration required to reduce OD600-based microbial growth by 50% relative to an untreated control, whereas MIC represents the lowest concentration at which no visible turbidity is observed. Accordingly, MIC values are expected to be higher than IC50 values for the same compound and organism, as observed in the present study. For L. brevis, identical MIC and MBC values were observed, suggesting a bactericidal effect against this strain under the present assay conditions rather than a purely bacteriostatic action; however, confirmation by time–kill kinetics would be required for definitive classification. These findings are consistent with SEM observations showing membrane-associated morphological damage. MBC values were not determined for the other tested microorganisms, and therefore, the extent to which such bactericidal activity applies to those strains remains to be clarified in future studies. The MIC for S. vineae could not be determined due to insufficient reproducible growth under the present conditions.

Eugenol is recognized as the dominant bioactive compound in clove and has been widely reported to possess antimicrobial properties (Pathak et al. 2004; Shan et al. 2005). Consistent with previous studies (Marchese et al. 2017), eugenol demonstrated activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and yeast species. Our mechanistic analyses suggest that membrane perturbation contributes to its antimicrobial activity, likely due to its amphipathic structure enabling partial insertion into lipid bilayers (Laekeman et al. 1990; Marchese et al. 2017; Nazzaro et al. 2013).

Gallic acid has been reported to exhibit both antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Manach et al. 2005; Yen et al. 2002; Borges et al. 2013; Kang et al. 2018). Although previous studies described growth-promoting effects under aerobic conditions (Reguant et al. 2000; Stead 1994; Alberto et al. 2001), the present study demonstrated inhibitory effects under anaerobic conditions. Differences in redox state and metabolic adaptation may contribute to these divergent observations, although direct comparative experiments were not performed.

Propyl gallate, a synthetic compound widely used as a food additive and antioxidant (Huu Nguyen et al. 2022; Javaheri-Ghezeldizaj et al. 2023; Strippoli et al. 2000), was included as a comparative reference compound and showed antimicrobial activity comparable to commercial preservatives and weaker activity against the tested yeast species. Increased hydrophobicity associated with alkyl chain length may enhance membrane interactions (Choińska et al. 2021). The membrane-disruptive effect of gallic acid appeared less pronounced in the tested Gram-negative species, possibly due to outer membrane protection.

The methodological design emphasized food-compatible extraction methods and beer-mimicking anaerobic conditions in order to approximate aspects of the physicochemical environment of fermented beverages. Nevertheless, the simplified broth-based model does not fully reproduce the complexity of commercial beer, including ethanol content, hop-derived antimicrobial compounds, dissolved CO2, colloidal interactions, and dynamic microbial community effects. Therefore, caution is required when extrapolating the present findings to real beverage systems or other fermented food matrices, and the antimicrobial efficacy observed here may not directly translate to finished products.

In addition, practical implementation would require careful evaluation of matrix effects, sensory impact (particularly the strong aroma associated with clove and eugenol), regulatory limitations, toxicological safety at realistic concentrations, and compatibility with desirable fermentation microbiota. These technological and regulatory considerations may represent substantial constraints for industrial application. The screening concentration used in this study (7500 μg/mL) was selected as a conservative threshold for identifying candidates and may exceed practical use levels in finished products, thus serving primarily as an initial selection benchmark rather than a directly applicable usage level.

Overall, the present findings should be regarded as proof-of-concept evidence demonstrating the antimicrobial potential of clove-derived constituents under controlled in vitro beer-mimicking conditions, rather than direct validation of their suitability as commercial food preservatives. Importantly, because these results were obtained in a simplified broth model, they should not be directly extrapolated to commercial beer or other fermented beverages. Further validation in real beverage matrices and under industrially relevant conditions will be required to assess feasibility, safety, and sensory acceptability, with particular attention to sensory impact, regulatory compliance, and compatibility with desirable starter cultures/microbiota.
