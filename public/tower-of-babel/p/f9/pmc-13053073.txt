My father’s deaths
Authors: Stephen Workman
Published: 2026

“If he were my father, I would come see him now.”

I already knew — but could not readily admit — what was being told to me by another general internist, who was also on call, but from a different emergency department more than 1000 km away. I have led similar conversations many times in my 30-year career. Yet, I clung, perhaps to hope, perhaps to avoidance. I was being told, gently but unmistakably, that my father was dying.

Two days earlier he had been diagnosed with noncardiac chest pain and sent home. The pain worsened. A CT scan revealed pneumonia and a loculated empyema. The fluid had been partially drained, but a large volume remained, and he grew more frail.

The moment I heard the diagnosis, I thought, “He is going to die.” When I had visited a few months earlier, he was already so diminished. The family reunion planned for August needed to be cancelled. I finished my shift that night by establishing palliative goals for a patient with a malignant pleural effusion. It was too close to home. My chest ached; my eyes brimmed as I struggled to manage another family’s grief as well as my own.

More than a decade of Parkinson disease had ravaged my father’s body, leaving his mind to struggle against infirmity. He maintained a cane-free, walker-free existence through force of will, circling his vintage Ford tractor around the back acreage, cutting the fields that kept him as much as he kept them. A fall in the barn months earlier had left him on the cold floor for more than an hour, unable to regain his feet. “I have strength but no power,” he told me, a physics lesson he had previously imparted. His clinical acumen remained sharp; he still offered keen second opinions. We talked but did not speak of grief or his impending death. We did not cry together.

When I arrived, I learned he had been designated “no code.” I agreed; he was as good a candidate for CPR as for space-flight. Six months earlier, he had told my sister he was considering medical assistance in dying — his disease, he said, had taken all he enjoyed. Yet, after he was admitted, no one, myself included, asked whether he wanted palliative care.

Much had been done with more to do. Thoracic surgery was consulted. The surgeon recommended medical management alone. I was relieved.

For the first several days, I fully expected him to die. He was tachycardic, febrile, with distended neck veins — on the brink of heart failure. My grief and tears arrived abruptly and at inopportune times. In speaking to my father’s attending, I struggled to convey the impact his death would have on me and my family. “You obviously love him very much,” he said.

After a week in a parlous state, my father rallied. The physiotherapists praised his unsteady steps. There was talk of rehab. Other family members returned home. Then, sudden delirium, after which he became nonresponsive.

Two weeks after readmission, late at night and alone with him, I watched as his apneic spells lengthened. The flat black surface of the lake outside his window mirrored the darkness and grief within me.

Earlier that day, I had read about palliative care in Parkinson disease. In the terminal phase, stopping Parkinson medications may ease delirium without diminishing quality of life. I feared my father might awaken trapped in the body that had increasingly refused his commands to walk, speak, swallow.

I met the resident on call in a windowless room lit by computer screens, my face wet with tears. I did not wipe them away. I spoke of my father’s life, my fear of his death. Grief rose in waves — pressure in my chest, tightness of my throat, an inability to speak. The resident regarded me throughout our discussion with quiet and soft-spoken compassion.

Together, we agreed to focus on comfort and to stop his Parkinson medications. A burden shared is halved.

At 2 am, his apneic spells lengthened. I gathered his belongings: razor, hairbrush, his leather shaving kit I’d known since my childhood. His Velcro closure shoes were a testament to what had been lost. The room was very quiet. I was so sad.

A cot was brought in. I slept beside him.

In the morning, he awoke lucid, hungry, without tremor. He looked for his hairbrush. I said nothing. Only later did I understand the moment I missed to say, “Dad, I thought we’d lost you.” His recovery felt miraculous. It was not to last.

I returned home. A week later, he developed esophageal impaction. He had already endured a six-hour procedure to disimpact his esophagus three months earlier and had no desire to repeat the experience. I flew back.

The MAiD assessor confirmed his capacity to make his own decision and his sustained wish to die, and noted the risk of losing capacity to consent at any time. “How about tomorrow?” they asked. He bowed his head, palms together — a gesture of acceptance and supplication.

The next day, my father was surrounded by wife, children, and grandchildren. We sang, and at times my father sang with us. Through the sunlit window, clouds and sailboats cast shadows on the whitecapped lake. The physician was delayed. I stepped out to the lakeshore, wind in my face and what remains of my hair. Before leaving, I told my adult nephew, “Don’t let them kill Dad while I’m gone.”

When the physician arrived, consent was reconfirmed, and medications were administered. At my father’s request, as he died, my sister sang Leonard Cohen’s “Hallelujah.”

His last words were, “I am so lucky.” I returned home, to grieve.

Two months later, still weighed down with grief, I had a vivid dream. In it I had looked deeply into my father’s cobaltblue eyes — perhaps for the last time.

“Why did you leave me?” I asked.

He paused.

“I never did.”

I awoke abruptly. I had said goodbye to my father for a third time. As I lay in the darkness, I searched my mind as the tongue searches for a roughened tooth. My grief was gone.

In the years since, I have come to understand that my grief carried not only my father’s absence, but all those years I had struggled to keep my sorrows to myself.
