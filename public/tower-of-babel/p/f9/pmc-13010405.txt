Giant hydronephrosis mimicking subacute intestinal obstruction: A 4-case series
Authors: Sayantani Ghosh, Monidipa Ghosh, Aritra Mukherjee, Kamalesh Mazumder, Dipra Dattasarma, Akriti Singh, Mainak Mandal, Abhishek Praharaj, Sudipta Sardar, Tapan Dhibar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiant hydronephrosis (GHDN) is an uncommon sequela of chronic urinary obstruction, defined by accumulation of more than 1 L of urine within the renal collecting system. Although pelvi-ureteric junction (PUJ) obstruction is the most common cause, obstructive urolithiasis and congenital or acquired anomalies may also contribute. Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and may mimic gastrointestinal pathologies, including subacute intestinal obstruction (SAIO), due to progressive abdominal distention and mass effect on bowel loops. Ultrasonography frequently demonstrates a large intra-abdominal anechoic lesion but may not reliably identify its renal origin. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) provides definitive diagnosis by characterizing the extent of renal dilation, cortical thickness, and obstructing lesions. We present 4 patients with progressive abdominal distention initially interpreted as subacute intestinal obstruction. Ultrasonography was inconclusive in each case, whereas CT imaging confirmed GHDN. Three patients had unilateral GHDN secondary to obstructive ureteric calculi, and one had bilateral hydronephrosis due to pelvi-ureteric junction obstruction. All patients underwent appropriate urological intervention, including percutaneous drainage, ureteroscopic stone extraction, or nephrectomy, and recovered well. This case series highlights the diagnostic pitfalls associated with ultrasound evaluation of large abdominal fluid collections and underscores the essential role of CT in distinguishing GHDN from abdominal mimickers. Early recognition facilitates timely intervention and prevents complications such as infection, rupture, and irreversible renal damage.Keywords: Giant hydronephrosis, Subacute intestinal obstruction, Pelvi-ureteric junction obstruction, Ureteric calculus, Computed tomography, Cortical thinning

Introduction

Giant hydronephrosis (GHDN) is classically defined as the accumulation of ≥1 liter of urine within the renal collecting system, or radiologically as a kidney occupying a hemi-abdomen, crossing the midline, or spanning at least 5 vertebral bodies [1]. It is an uncommon but clinically important manifestation of chronic urinary tract obstruction.

Historically GHDN was more frequently encountered in the pre-imaging era. However, despite widespread availability of modern imaging, GHDN continues to present late, even today, due to insidious symptom progression, nonspecific abdominal complaints, delayed healthcare access, and frequent misinterpretation on initial ultrasonography.

The most frequent underlying cause is pelvi-ureteric junction (PUJ) obstruction, followed by obstructive urolithiasis, trauma, congenital anomalies, ureterovesical junction obstruction, and extrinsic compressive pathology [2]. Chronic obstruction results in progressive pelvicalyceal distention, marked cortical thinning, parenchymal atrophy, and displacement of abdominal structures. These alterations may produce symptoms mimicking gastrointestinal disorders, most notably subacute intestinal obstruction (SAIO).

Ultrasonography is commonly the initial imaging modality used in the evaluation of abdominal distention. However, severe hydronephrosis may appear as a large anechoic lesion, often mistaken for ascites, mesenteric cysts, or dilated bowel loops [3].

In contrast, CT imaging provides comprehensive anatomical assessment, delineating the degree of dilatation, cortical thickness, location and cause of obstruction, and mass effect on adjacent organs. CT findings also guide management decisions, including drainage, reconstructive surgery, or nephrectomy, depending on residual renal function.

This series describes 4 patients with abdominal symptoms initially suggestive of SAIO but ultimately diagnosed with GHDN via CT. It emphasizes the limitations of ultrasound, the critical diagnostic advantage of CT, and the favorable outcomes achieved with timely intervention.

Case series

Overview

Four patients presented with progressive abdominal distention and abdominal discomfort. Initial ultrasonography demonstrated large intra-abdominal anechoic lesions in all cases but did not definitively identify the renal origin. CT imaging subsequently confirmed GHDN. Three patients had unilateral hydronephrosis due to obstructive ureteric calculi, and 1 patient had bilateral hydronephrosis due to PUJ obstruction. All patients underwent successful urological intervention and recovered well.

Case 1

A 45-year-old female presented with a 2-week history of progressive abdominal distention, vague abdominal pain, and bloating. Ultrasound revealed a large cystic lesion occupying the right abdomen, initially suggestive of SAIO. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) demonstrated giant right hydronephrosis with more than 1 L of urine, severe pelvicalyceal dilation, and cortical thinning. A large obstructing ureteric calculus was identified as the etiology. The left kidney appeared normal. Percutaneous drainage yielded ∼1 L of urine, followed by ureteroscopic stone removal. The patient improved clinically and recovered without complications (Fig. 1).

Case 2

A 34-year-old female presented with diffuse abdominal pain and distention, preceded by intermittent right flank discomfort for 3 months. Ultrasound was inconclusive, demonstrating a large cystic structure without definitive renal origin. CECT confirmed giant right hydronephrosis due to a large ureteric calculus, with a thin cortical rim and displacement of bowel loops to the left. Approximately 1.19 L of urine was drained via percutaneous nephrostomy, followed by successful retrograde ureteroscopic stone extraction. The patient recovered well and remained symptom-free on follow-up (Fig. 2).

Case 3

A 65-year-old male with a history of bilateral pyelonephritis and prior double-J stenting presented with 1 month of progressive abdominal distention. Ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral hydronephrosis. CECT confirmed bilateral PUJ obstruction with massive pelvicalyceal dilation and marked cortical thinning. Due to negligible functional parenchyma, the patient underwent open bilateral nephrectomy. Intraoperatively, 8 L and 9 L of fluid were drained from the right and left kidneys, respectively. The patient recovered without postoperative complications and continued under nephrology care (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

Case 4

A 58year old male presented with gradual abdominal distention and nonspecific abdominal discomfort. Ultrasonography showed right sided large well defined anechoic lesion crossing the midline. CECT demonstrated giant right hydronephrosis with cortical thinning and displacement of adjacent bowel loops. The precise level of obstruction could not be identified likely due to long-standing high-grade obstruction with marked distortion of normal collecting system anatomy, a known limitation in chronic GHDN. The patient underwent renal decompression and experienced immediate symptomatic improvement with satisfactory recovery (Table 1).

Discussion

GHDN is a rare but important differential diagnosis in patients presenting with abdominal distention or symptoms suggestive of SAIO. Its infrequent occurrence and nonspecific clinical presentation often lead to diagnostic delays. The varied causes of GHDN—including PUJ obstruction, stones, trauma, and congenital anomalies—have been well described in previous literature [1,2,4].

Diagnostic challenges

Despite advances in diagnostic imaging, GHDN is still frequently identified at an advanced stage, largely because of its gradual clinical evolution, vague abdominal symptoms, delayed presentation to healthcare facilities, and frequent diagnostic ambiguity on initial ultrasonographic evaluation. Ultrasound is frequently the first modality used in acute abdominal presentations.

However, in GHDN, massive pelvicalyceal dilation produces an anechoic mass that may be misinterpreted as ascites, mesenteric cyst, pancreatic pseudocyst, or dilated bowel loops [3]. The absence of readily visible renal parenchyma and lack of identifiable ureteric continuity further complicate ultrasound-based diagnosis.

These features are essential for determining whether renal salvage is feasible or whether nephrectomy is required.

Although contrast-enhanced CT is the diagnostic modality of choice for GHDN, its use may be limited in patients with advanced renal insufficiency or iodinated contrast allergy. In such scenarios, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serves as an effective alternative, allowing accurate delineation of pelvicalyceal dilatation, renal parenchymal thickness, and mass effect on adjacent abdominal structures without ionizing radiation or nephrotoxic contrast agents.

Management outcomes

Management strategies depend on renal function and etiology. Options include:

•Percutaneous nephrostomy for immediate decompression.

•Ureteroscopic removal of stones.

•Pyeloplasty for correctable PUJ obstruction.

•Nephrectomy for non-functional kidneys.

In alignment with existing reports [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]], all patients in this series benefited from decompression and definitive intervention, demonstrating favorable outcomes.

Renal scintigraphy (e.g., DTPA or MAG3 renography) plays a crucial role in the functional assessment of kidneys affected by GHDN. It aids in evaluating split renal function and renal salvageability, thereby guiding management decisions between reconstructive procedures and nephrectomy, particularly in cases demonstrating severe cortical thinning on cross-sectional imaging. However, due to unavailability in our hospital setup and weak economical status of the patients, it was not used.

Conclusion

GHDN is a rare but clinically significant differential diagnosis for abdominal distention and conditions mimicking SAIO. Ultrasonography may fail to identify the renal origin of massive abdominal cystic lesions, whereas CT provides definitive characterization and guides appropriate management. Early recognition and intervention prevent irreversible renal damage and improve clinical outcomes.
