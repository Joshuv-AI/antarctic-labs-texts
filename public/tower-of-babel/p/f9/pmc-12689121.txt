Post-operative seroma formation following laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sunil Basukala, Sunischit Chandra Neupane, Sulav Neupane, Sujan Tamang, Niranjan Thapa, Oshan Shrestha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Seroma formation is the most frequent complication after laparoscopic hernia repair, and there are reports of seroma being mistaken for recurrences, with the correct diagnosis being made only after groin exploration. Laparoscopic hernia repair, first introduced by LeBlanc and Booth in 1993, has gained increasing popularity over time. The two most common techniques – total extraperitoneal (TEP) and transabdominal preperitoneal – offer advantages such as shorter hospital stays, less postoperative pain, and lower rates of surgical site infection. Hence, this study aims to study the prevalence and associated seroma among patients who underwent laparoscopic hernia repair.Methods:This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Surgery at a tertiary level hospital over 4 years (January 2020–December 2024), with ethical approval from the Institutional Review Committee. Data were collected using a predesigned proforma and hospital records, including follow-ups at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-surgery. Information on hospital stay, antibiotic use, intraoperative complications, and surgical site infections was recorded. A nonprobability convenient sampling method was used. Based on a calculated sample size of 218 and accounting for incomplete data, 250 patients were included. Data were coded in Excel and analyzed using SPSS v24.0 with descriptive statistics.Results:Among the 250 patients who underwent laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair, seroma formation was observed in 17.6% of cases. Patients who developed seromas had significantly larger hernial orifice and sac sizes compared to those without seromas (P = 0.000 and P = 0.021, respectively). The TEP approach was more frequently used in the seroma group (50%) than in the non-seroma group (25.7%; P = 0.001), suggesting an association between the TEP technique and increased risk of seroma. Operative time did not differ significantly between the two groups, and no intraoperative bowel or bladder injuries were reported. Postoperative outcomes, including chronic pain and recurrence, showed no significant differences between the seroma and non-seroma groups. Surgical site infections were slightly more common in the non-seroma group (9.2 vs. 2.3%, P = 0.952), while port site hematoma was more frequently seen in the seroma group (22.7 vs. 2.4%, P = 0.221), though neither difference reached statistical significance. Rates of scrotal edema were similar between groups, and average hospital stay was comparable, indicating that seroma formation did not significantly affect short-term recovery. Limitations of the study include its single-center design, limited representation of female and obese patients, reliance on clinical diagnosis for seroma detection, and potential bias in surgical approach selection due to surgeon preference. Long-term complications may also be underrepresented due to limited follow-upConclusion:Seroma formation was observed in 17.6% of patients undergoing laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair. It was significantly associated with higher BMI, larger hernial orifice and sac size, and the use of the TEP approach. Although seromas did not significantly affect long-term outcomes such as chronic pain, recurrence, or hospital stay, their presence highlights the need for meticulous surgical technique and close postoperative monitoring. Further multicenter studies are recommended to confirm these findings and develop strategies to reduce seroma incidence. In our cohort, seromas were clinically detected, self-limiting, and did not require additional intervention beyond reassurance and follow-up.

However, seroma formation is the most frequent complication after laparoscopic hernia repair, and there are reports of seroma being mistaken for recurrences, with the correct diagnosis being made only after groin exploration[6]. Many reasons have been attributed to the development of seroma formation after laparoscopic repair, including the dissection of the preperitoneal space for mesh placement, the existence of dead space after hernia sac reduction, and the irrigation of prosthetic materials implanted in the preperitoneal space[7].

Hence, this study aims to study the prevalence and associated seroma among patients undergoing laparoscopic hernia repair.

Methods

This is a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted in the Department of Surgery in a tertiary level hospital for a period of 4 years from January 2020 to December 2024. The ethical approval was taken from the Institutional Review Committee before the conduct of the study. The data were collected using a predesigned proforma. Records of the patient were collected from the data sheet obtained from the record section. Regular follow-up details were collected from the data sheet at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after surgery. This was followed by the collection of details regarding length of stay in the hospital, the use of antibiotics, intraoperative complications, and post-operative SSI if any. Nonprobability convenient sampling method was adopted. The manuscript work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria[8]. The sample size was calculated using the following formula: n=Z2xpx(1−p)/e2

where n = required sample size, z = 1.96 at 98% confidence interval, p = prevalence of seroma undergoing laparoscopic repair to be 11.3%[9], and e = margin of error, 7%. With this formula, n = 218.

However, assuming 10% of the incomplete data are taken into consideration, a sample size of 250 participants who underwent laparoscopic repair was used for the study.

All the collected data were tabulated and coded in Microsoft Excel. Descriptive data were presented for the different items and the prevalence of Non Seroma Group. Tabular presentation and descriptive statistics were used for the quantitative data analysis. Seroma was diagnosed clinically during routine follow-ups (1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively). Imaging was not performed routinely but reserved for suspected recurrences. All identified seromas were clinically relevant, as they were palpable, though none were complicated. The data were extracted to SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) version 24.0.

Results

A total of 250 individuals met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled for analysis who underwent laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair during the study period. All seromas were managed conservatively with reassurance. None required aspiration, drainage, or re-operation. Among them, 206 (82.4%) patients did not develop seroma postoperatively, while 44 (17.6%) developed seroma. The mean age of the participants was 51.88 ± 13.89 years (mean ± SD), ranging from 18 to 76 years. Of the total participants, 235 (94%) were males, and 15 (6%) were females. The seroma group had a significantly higher proportion of female patients (13.6%) compared to the non-seroma group (4.4%; P = 0.030). BMI was also significantly higher in the seroma group (26.21 ± 2.22 kg/m2) compared to the non-seroma group (23.40 ± 2.32 kg/m2; P = 0.001). Discussion

Groin hernia repair is one of the most common surgeries performed in Nepal, with more than repairs performed every year, with the majority being repaired by open techniques. However, laproscopic techniques have been gaining popularity as they offer the advantage of rapid postoperative recovery and reduced hospital stay. TAPP and TEP approaches are the two most accepted techniques for laparoscopic repair of groin hernias. Laparoscopic hernia repair in the elective setting has been shown to have a comparable recurrence rate with less persistent pain and numbness as well as quicker return to usual activities when compared with open mesh repair[10].

Postoperative complications of the hernia repair include hematomas, seromas, urinary retention, vascular injury, and spermatic vessel injury[11]. Seroma formation is a common postoperative complication following laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair. Seroma is defined as a collection of fluid detected in the subcutaneous space through clinical or radiographical investigation and is most common following surgery that involves extensive tissue dissection, resulting in a “dead space”[12]. Seroma formation results in patient dissatisfaction, poor aesthetic outcome, and serious SSIs[13]. While clinically significant seroma is reported in up to 17% of hernia repairs, studies have reported that the rate of seroma detection approaches 100% when diagnosed based on radiological criteria alone, and there are reports of seroma being mistaken for recurrences, with the correct diagnosis being made only after groin exploration[14]. In our study, most seromas were benign and self-limiting, resolving spontaneously without intervention. Their clinical importance lies mainly in causing patient anxiety and sometimes mimicking recurrence, rather than causing long-term morbidity.

This study evaluated the prevalence and associated factors of postoperative seroma formation in patients undergoing laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair over 4 years. A total of 250 patients were analyzed, out of which 44 (17.6%) developed seromas. This prevalence falls within the previously reported range of 3–20% in the literature, reaffirming that seroma is a relatively common complication following laparoscopic hernia repair[15–17].

Demographic factors

The mean age of patients in the seroma and non-seroma groups was comparable (53.59 ± 13.63 vs. 51.49 ± 13.94, P= 0.64), suggesting that age alone may not be a significant determinant for seroma development. However, a statistically significant difference was observed in gender distribution. Female patients constituted a higher proportion of the seroma group (13.6%) compared to the non-seroma group (4.4%; P= 0.030). This could indicate anatomical or physiological differences, or relatively silent clinical occurrence of inguinal hernias among the female population, which do not garner clinical attention until it becomes surgically complicated, or may reflect a smaller sample size of female patients, which warrants further investigation[18]. In female patients, the round ligament was generally preserved, and systematic ligation was not performed. Due to the small number of female patients, we were unable to analyze the association between round ligament handling and seroma formation. This remains an area for future study.

Additionally, patients in the seroma group had a significantly higher BMI (26.21 ± 2.22 vs. 23.40 ± 2.32, P= 0.001), supporting previous findings that elevated BMI may predispose individuals to fluid accumulation due to greater dead space, increased intrabdominal pressure, and altered lymphatic drainage[19]. While obesity is seen as a protective factor in the development of inguinal hernias, the increased incidence of seromas in these populations seems counterintuitive and warrants further research into its association[20,21].

Preoperative assessment

There was no statistically significant difference in hernia location between the two groups, though right-sided hernias were slightly more prevalent overall. Interestingly, in our study, indirect hernias were more commonly associated with seroma formation (70.45% in the seroma group vs. 66.01% in the non-seroma group), with the difference reaching statistical significance (P= 0.005). This challenges the conventional notion that indirect hernias that are in line with the anatomy of the groin have a curtain-like closure after excision of the hernia sac from the inguinal canal, while direct defect will persist as evagination of the transversalis fascia once repaired and are more prone to postoperative seroma[2]. Our findings might suggest that the mechanism may not be solely anatomical but also procedural or patient related.

Intraoperative findings

Hernial orifice size and hernial sac size were both significantly larger in patients who developed seromas (P= 0.000 and P= 0.021, respectively), reinforcing the theory that larger defects result in severe tissue adhesion needing more dissection and forming more dead space, thus increasing the risk of seroma[17].

Additionally, the TEP approach was more frequently used in the seroma group (50%) compared to the non-seroma group (25.7%; P= 0.001). These findings are well in line with similar studies where the reported range of seroma formation after TEP repair is higher (0.5–12.2%) than for TAPP (3.0–8.0%)[2]. However, as this was a non-randomized study without adjustment for confounders, this association should be interpreted with caution and regarded as correlation rather than causation. This may be due to the more extensive dissection involved in the TEP technique, potentially leading to increased fluid collection, consistent with Chen et al’s study[22].

Operative time did not significantly differ between the two groups, and there were no intraoperative bowel or bladder injuries in either group, indicating the overall safety of the laparoscopic techniques used. These findings are in alignment with the study by Krishna et. al, where intraoperative and post-operative complications, as well as costs, were comparable between both of the laparoscopic techniques used, proving both repair methods to be equally effective in their outcomes[23].

Postoperative outcomes

There were no significant differences in the occurrence of chronic postoperative pain or recurrence between the two groups. Similar study by Eklund et al compared chronic pain outcomes between laparoscopic and open hernia repairs over a 5-year follow-up, where the results showed no significant difference in chronic pain incidence between the two groups, suggesting that the occurrence of seromas does not substantially impact long-term pain outcomes[24]. SSIs were actually slightly more frequent in the non-seroma group (9.2 vs. 2.3%), though this difference was not statistically significant (P= 0.952). Interestingly, port site hematoma was more commonly observed in the seroma group (22.7 vs. 2.4%), although this also did not reach statistical significance (P= 0.221). The higher frequency may suggest an overall trend of increased postoperative fluid-related complications in patients with seroma. Scrotal edema rates were similar across both groups. The average length of hospital stay was nearly identical, indicating that seroma formation did not significantly prolong immediate postoperative recovery[25,26].

This study has several limitations. First, being a single-center study restricts the generalizability of the findings. The patient population was not fully representative, with a limited number of female and obese patients, which reduced the strength of subgroup analyses. Second, the use of nonprobability convenience sampling introduces the possibility of selection bias. Third, seroma diagnosis was based primarily on clinical examination during routine follow-up, and imaging was not performed systematically; therefore, asymptomatic or subclinical seromas may have been underestimated. Fourth, no multivariate analysis was performed, and potential confounders were not adjusted for. As a result, the observed associations – such as the link between higher BMI, larger hernia dimensions, the TEP approach, and increased seroma formation – should be interpreted as correlations rather than causal relationships. Additionally, the choice between TEP and TAPP was influenced by surgeon preference, which may have further introduced bias. Finally, the relatively short duration of follow-up limits our ability to capture late complications. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse cohorts, prospective designs, and multivariate modeling are needed to validate and expand on these findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights that seroma formation remains a common postoperative complication following laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair, with a prevalence of 17.6%. Significant associations were observed between seroma formation and factors such as higher BMI, larger hernial orifice and sac size, and the use of the TEP approach. While seromas did not significantly impact long-term outcomes like chronic pain, recurrence, or hospital stay, their occurrence underscores the importance of careful intraoperative technique and postoperative monitoring. Further multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations are recommended to validate these findings and guide strategies to minimize seroma formation. In our setting, postoperative seromas were clinically relevant but self-limiting and did not require additional intervention, highlighting the importance of patient reassurance and routine follow-up.
