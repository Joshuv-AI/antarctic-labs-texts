Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a malignant tumor that poses a significant threat to human health. Global cancer statistics from 2020 report that CRC ranks fifth among the most common cancers worldwide, with an estimated 11.48 million new cases and 578,000 deaths reported annually. These numbers account for 6.0 % of the total new cases and deaths from malignant tumors [1]. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 data, colorectal cancer had 1926,118 new cases and 903,859 deaths worldwide, making it the third most common cancer. Its high incidence and mortality rates not only pose a serious challenge to public health, but also highlight the urgent need to further understand its pathogenesis and develop more effective early diagnosis and treatment strategies [2]. Among the various subtypes of CRC, colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) stands out as the most prevalent pathological type. The incidence of COAD has been increasing in recent years, particularly among individuals with chronic inflammatory bowel disease, colon polyps, and obesity. Changes in lifestyle, such as a high-fat diet, have been found to alter the microbial composition in the human gut, contributing to the rising incidence of COAD. Due to its insidious symptoms and low diagnostic rate, most COAD patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage. COAD is highly invasive, with high mortality and recurrence rates [3]. Over the years, the five-year survival rate of COAD patients has improved due to advancements in surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and targeted therapy. However, even with systemic treatment, the five-year survival rate for COAD remains below 10 % due to the low early diagnosis rate and the aggressive nature of the disease [4]. Currently, screening tests for early detection of COAD mainly include fecal and blood tests, radiological tests, and colonoscopy [5]. However, the accuracy of these methods is relatively low. Surgical intervention is effective for early-stage COAD patients, but for advanced-stage patients, targeted therapy combined with chemotherapy is the main treatment strategy. However, these chemotherapy regimens often lead to severe side effects, including gastrointestinal reactions, immune system damage, and bone marrow suppression [6]. Therefore, the search for prognostic markers for COAD and the development of new methods for early detection and treatment are of great significance for effective disease management and improving the quality of life and survival rates of COAD patients.

Research has shown that the proliferation of fibrous tissue, often leads to an increase in alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). Additionally, α-SMA is also essential for cancer metastasis [7]. Therefore, high expression levels of alpha-smooth muscle actin (ACTA2) in fibrous tissue, especially in SMA-positive smooth muscle fibrous tissue and invasive cancer cells, often correlate with a poor prognosis. Furthermore, mutations in ACTA2 can lead to smooth muscle cell disorders, such as intestinal malrotation, decreased peristalsis, aortic and cerebral vascular diseases, fixed dilated pupils, hypotonic bladder, and pulmonary arterial hypertension [8]. In our study, we found that ACTA2 is downregulated in COAD, but once ACTA2 highly expressed in COAD tissues, it is associated with poor prognosis, indicating that elevated expression of ACTA2 may be potentially important in the development of COAD. Most existing studies have primarily focused on the direct relationship between ACTA2 expression and diseases. However, further research is necessary to investigate the dysregulated genes that are associated with the high expression of ACTA2.

Therefore, this study used bioinformatics methods to analyze the expression levels and functions of ACTA2 and its related genes and differentially expressed genes in COAD. We also investigated the expression levels of ACTA2 at the mRNA and protein levels through Real Time Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and Western blot experiments. Additionally, we predicted the immune cell infiltration and upstream regulators of the clinical scoring model based on ACTA2. Finally, we constructed a multi-omics landscape for the model based on multi-omics data.

Materials and methods data

Data resources and preprocessing

The expression profiles and relevant clinical information data of COAD were obtained from the Cancer Genome Atlas database (TCGA, https://www.cancer.gov/) [9]. The expression profile includes 512 samples, among which there are 471 COAD tissues and 41 adjacent non-tumor tissues (Control). The clinical information included gender, age, tumor size (T), lymph node involvement (N), distant metastasis (M), staging, overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and survival duration. The data were normalized using the limma package in R software [10]. Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA) [11] was conducted to observe the differential expression of COAD tissue samples and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples. We collected 8 cases of COAD tissue samples and 8 cases of their corresponding normal tissue samples from at Zhujiang Hospital. All patients involved in this study have signed written informed consent. The patients all underwent colon cancer radical surgery in the general surgery department of Zhujiang Hospital, and postoperative pathology confirmed colon cancer. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Zhujiang Hospital and conducted under its supervision (2023-KY-259-01).

RT-qPCR

We used the (FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2) (Vazyme) to extract total RNA from the tissue, and the NanoDrop™ One/OneC micro UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to measure the RNA concentration by OD value. The reverse transcription was performed using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme) under the conditions of 37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s. α-Tublin was chosen as the reference gene. ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) was used for qPCR, and the samples were mixed and detected using the QuantStudio 6 Flex real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the following thermal cycling conditions: 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s; and 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, and 95 °C for 15 s. The primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai) were designed as follows: α-Tublin 5′-TGTTGGTCAGGCTGGTGTCC-3′ and 5′-TGAAGAAGGTGTTGAACGAGTCATC-3′; ACTA2 5′-AGAAGAGTTACGAGTTGCCTGATGG-3′ and 5′-GCTGTTGTAGGTGGTTTCATGGATG-3′. The relative gene expression level was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

Differential protein expression analysis of ACTA2

We obtained the immunohistochemistry atlas of ACTA2 from the Human Protein Atlas (https://v15.proteinatlas.org/) [12]. In this resource, we identified the protein level and localization of ACTA2.

Western blot

The cells isolated from tissue samples were washed with PBS and lysed on ice with a mixture of PIPA+PMSF (Beyotime). The lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was combined with Protein Sample Loading Buffer (epizyme) and heated at 95 °C in a metal bath for 10 min. The protein concentration of each sample was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Biosharp). The protein samples were separated in a 10 % SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a 0.2 µm PVDF membrane (Merck). After blocking with skimmed milk powder (Biosharp) at room temperature for 1 hour, the PVDF membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with Mouse anti-α-SMA (YM3364, Immunway) and Mouse anti-α-Tublin (RM2007, Beijing Ray Antibody Biotech) antibodies. The membrane was washed 6 times with TBST (Boster Bio), then incubated at room temperature for 1 hour with the secondary antibody Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L)-HRP (RM3001, Beijing Ray Antibody Biotech). Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) was used to visualize the bands.

Analysis of the diagnostic and prognostic potential of ACTA2 in COAD

To explore the diagnostic potential of ACTA2 as a biomarker for COAD, we conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using the pROC package [13] in R language. The area under the curve (AUC) is a measure of diagnostic accuracy, with AUC values closer to 1 for AUC > 0.5 indicating better diagnostic performance. Next, we determined the cutoff point for ACTA2 expression using the median expression level in the TCGA dataset. The patients with ACTA2 expression equal to or higher than the median were classified as the ACTA2 high expression group, while those with ACTA2 expression lower than the median were classified as the low expression group. We performed Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis [14] to evaluate the prognostic potential of ACTA2 in terms of RFS and OS. We employed the log-rank test to determine the statistical significance of the results, considering a p-value of < 0.05 as statistically significant.

Differentially expressed gene analysis

We conducted differential analysis using the limma package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) between the COAD and control groups, as well as between the high-expression and low-expression groups of ACTA2 [10]. The DEGs and DEmiRNAs with adjusted p value (p.adj) < 0.05 were considered significant. Among these, we defined the consistently upregulated or downregulated DEGs and DEmiRNAs as dysregulated genes and dysregulated miRNAs associated with the high expression of ACTA2 in COAD.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

We conducted a co-expression network analysis using the WGCNA package [15] in R language to explore the co-expression patterns of dysregulated genes associated with the high expression of ACTA2 in COAD. We cluster genes with correlated expression patterns into co-expression modules. The parameters of WGCNA are all set with default parameters. Candidate powers (1 to 20) are used to test the average connectivity of different modules and their independence. If the degree of independence is > 0.85, select the power value. Subsequently, we investigated the correlation between modules and phenotypes. We visualized the module-phenotype relationships using heatmaps, highlighting the modules that exhibited the highest correlation with ACTA2 and relevant clinical variables. We selected genes from the module with the strongest association for further analysis.

Functional enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis

We performed gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package [16] in R language on the module genes. Additionally, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [17] using the molecular signature database (MsigDB) [18] c5.bp.v7.0.entrez.gmt and c2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt as reference gene sets. We input the phenotype and the complete gene expression profile for GSEA to investigate the potential biological functions of dysregulated genes associated with the high expression of ACTA2 in COAD. Through GSEA, we inferred whether the gene sets showed an upregulation or downregulation trend based on the enrichment results. Enriched functions or pathways with p.adj < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. This analysis enabled us to gain insights into the potential biological implications of the dysregulated genes related to ACTA2 high expression in COAD.

Construction and validation of a clinical scoring model based on ACTA2

We utilized the most significantly positively correlated module genes of ACTA2 for survival analysis. Subsequently, we selected the top 50 genes that were significantly associated with COAD prognosis and ACTA2 as scoring gene. Subsequently, scoring gene analysis was performed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression. After that, we obtained a ACTA2-based scoring model. Furthermore, we further explored the relationship between the ACTA2 prognostic scoring genes and clinical indicators such as OS, RFS, stage, grade, gender, age, and constructed a clinical indicator model based on ACTA2. In order to examine the semantic similarity of genes in the ACTA2 scoring model, we utilized the GOSemSim software package [19]. Additionally, we evaluated the predictive performance of the ACTA2-based clinical model for both RFS and OS by plotting calibration curves and column line diagrams through the use of the rms software package.

Immune cell infiltration analysis of acta2-based clinical scoring genes

The CIBERSORT tool [20] was employed to analyze the abundance of immune cell infiltration in the control group, as well as in the high and low expression groups of ACTA2. Based on a correlation analysis, the relationships between ACTA2, the ACTA2-based scoring model, the abundance of immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoint genes, and tertiary lymphoid structure marker genes were visualized.

Identification of upstream regulators of the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene set

The hypergeometric test method was employed to identify the upstream regulators that govern the ACTA2-based scoring model gene set, which includes long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), transcription factors (TFs), and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). Additionally, the regulatory impacts of the identified upstream regulators on the expression of the scoring genes of interest were investigated.

For the selected transcription factors, HADDOCK software was used to calculate their binding affinity to downstream gene DNA. Protein three-dimensional structures were obtained from the PDB database (https://www.uniprot.org/), while DNA structures were constructed using an online prediction platform (https://scfbio-iitd.res.in/software/drugdesign/bdna.jsp#). In molecular docking analysis, a binding energy < 0 indicates potential binding between the transcription factor and DNA. Lower binding energy values indicate stronger binding affinity, suggesting a potentially more important biological role in transcriptional regulation.

Construction of multi-omics landscapes based on the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene regulatory network

The maftools software package in R language was used to visualize the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of the genes in the ACTA2-based scoring model in COAD. Subsequently, copy number variation (CNV) plots of these genes were generated. Finally, we explored the relationship between methylation modification levels and transcript levels of acta2-based clinical scoring genes by correlation analysis.

Data analysis and statistics

All bioinformatics analyses in this study were performed through the Bioinforcloud platform (http://www.bioinforcloud.org.cn).

Results

Prognostic significance of dysregulated expression of ACTA2 in COAD

The flowchart of the study is depicted in Fig. 1. PCA analysis of ACTA2 expression values using 471 samples of COAD tissue and 41 samples of control group showed significant distinction in the expression of ACTA2 between COAD tissue and the control group (Fig. 2A). Next, we visualized the expression patterns of ACTA2 in these two groups, where ACTA2 was found to be downregulated in COAD tissue and upregulated in the control group (Fig. 2B). Simultaneously, RT-qPCR analysis verified this finding, and the mRNA expression level of ACTA2 in COAD was lower compared to the control tissue (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, we observed a relative decrease in the intensity of the immune reaction bands formed by ACTA2 protein in COAD tissues in WB analysis, indicating lower expression of ACTA2 in COAD compared to the control group (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, ROC analysis showed that the AUC of this gene in COAD was 0.766, the sensitivity was 68.3 % (Fig. 2E), and the specificity was 70.9 %, suggesting that it had a moderate level of discrimination ability. Moreover, survival curves displayed the prognostic potential of ACTA2 for COAD in terms of RFS and OS (Figs. 2F and 2G), indicating that high ACTA2 expression might be linked to lower patient survival rates. Consequently, we surmise that elevated ACTA2 expression in COAD tissue acts as an adverse prognostic factor and has diagnostic significance.

Gene co-expression modules characterizing the global regulatory network of ACTA2 in COAD

Through differential analysis (Fig. 3A), we obtained 20,137 DEGs (10,150 upregulated DEGs and 9987 downregulated DEGs), 439 DEmiRNAs (147 upregulated DEmiRNAs and 292 downregulated DEmiRNAs) from the comparison of COAD and control groups. Similarly, we obtained 12,927 DEGs (7284 upregulated DEGs and 5643 downregulated DEGs), 260 DEmiRNAs (129 upregulated DEmiRNAs and 131 downregulated DEmiRNAs) from the comparison of ACTA2 high and low expression group. We further identified 8249 consistently upregulated or downregulated DEGs and 140 consistently upregulated or downregulated DEmiRNAs, defined as dysregulated genes and miRNAs associated with high expression of ACTA2 in COAD. The potential effects of ACTA2 on dysregulated genes and miRNAs in COAD were filtrated through scatter plot (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, we further visualized the ACTA2 overexpression-associated COAD dysregulated gene set miRNAs as heatmaps (Fig. 3C and 3D).

Through WGCNA, specifically choosing 6 as the soft threshold (Fig. 1), we constructed a co-expression network and identified modules related to the differential expression genes of ACTA2 in COAD. Genes exhibiting similar expression patterns are grouped together as 8 co-expression modules (Fig. 3E). We utilized different branches and colors to differentiate the gene members of the ACTA2 co-expression modules involved in COAD regulation (Fig. 3F). Utilizing the module-trait heatmap (Fig. 3G), the correlation between the gene co-expression modules and ACTA2, OS, stage, T, N, M, gender, and age is further revealed. The data suggest that the turquoise module demonstrates the most significant correlation with ACTA2 (R = 0.57, p = 3e-45), in addition, the turquoise module is also significantly positively correlated with T (R = 0.48, p = 2e-30) and Stage (R = 0.36, p = 1e-16), suggesting that the co-expression in the turquoise module may be a key module mediating prognostic progression in COAD patients via ACTA2 and has potential research value.

Significant biological functions and signaling pathways regulated by ACTA2

A series of correlation scatterplots was generated using Pearson correlation coefficient analysis (Fig. 4A), revealing a significant positive correlation between ACTA2 and its co-expression modules. Through multiple group enrichment analysis, enrichment black, blue, brown, green, pink, turquoise and yellow gene modules’ biological processes (BP) and KEGG pathways (Fig. 4B and 4C) and the research results indicate that the turquoise module gene plays a significant role in biological processes (BP) such as epithelial cell proliferation, regulation of angiogenesis, as well as KEGG pathways including ECM-receptor interaction. Furthermore, GSEA was conducted to investigate the potential biological functions of dysregulated genes associated with high expression of ACTA2 in COAD. It was found that pathways such as Wnt signaling pathway, Hedgehog signaling pathway, and p53 signaling pathway were significantly activated in COAD samples (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, pathway maps were used to illustrate the gene expression differences in the Wnt signaling pathway and p53 signaling pathway (Fig. 4E). In conclusion the findings of this study indicate that high expression of ACTA2 impacts aberrantly regulated genes in COAD, significantly influencing its biological functions including epithelial cell proliferation, regulation of angiogenesis, and signalling pathways such as ECM-receptor interaction.

The ACTA2-based prognostic clinical model demonstrates significant prognostic efficacy

Subsequently, we selected ACTA2 and the top 50 genes significantly correlated with COAD prognosis as scoring genes for which Cox regression analyses were performed to obtain Cox multifactorial prognostic scores (Fig. 5A). And, we performed RFS and OS prognosis analysis on the scoring model obtained from COX regression analysis (Fig. 5B and 5C), revealing a poorer prognosis in the high ACTA2-based scoring (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the ACTA2-based score was significantly positively correlated with the T, N, M, and staging of COAD patients (Fig. 5D). Through GO semantic similarity analysis, we found that genes such as TNFSF11, NOTCH3, C5orf46, and MUCL3 had significant biological associations with ACTA2 (Fig. 5E). The forest plot (Fig. 5F) displays the multifactorial prognostic efficacy of ACTA2-based scoring and clinical indicators such as T, N, M stage. Furthermore, based on factors including T, N, M, gender, and ACTA2 scoring, we predict the 1,3,5year survival rates of COAD patients (Fig. 5G). Kaplan-Meier survival curves confirm the significant clinical utility of the column-line plot in predicting COAD prognosis (Fig. 5H and 5I). Subsequently, we further validated the accuracy of RFS and OS predictions using the ACTA2-based scoring model (Fig. 5J and 5K). And we found that high scores of ACTA2-based scoring model were associated with a worse prognosis for COAD.

The dysregulation of ACTA2-based clinical scoring genes reprograms the immune microenvironment of COAD

We utilised heat maps to display the abundance of immune cell infiltration across the control, high and low expression cohorts of ACTA2 (Fig. 6A). In addition, we explored the correlation between immune cell infiltration abundance and ACTA2 expression. We identified a positive correlation between the abundance of infiltrating immune cells such as naive B cells, Macrophages M0, and Macrophages M2, and the expression of ACTA2. On the other hand, we observed a negative correlation between the infiltration abundance of immune cells such as T cells CD4 memory activated, T cells CD4 memory resting, and T cells follicular helper, and the expression of ACTA2 (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, further correlation analysis demonstrated that a majority of ACTA2-based scoring genes were positively correlated with immune cell infiltration abundance (Fig. 6C). Additionally, the scoring genes were also positively correlated with immune checkpoint-related genes and markers of tertiary lymphoid structures (Fig. 6D). Therefore, we propose that high scores from the ACTA2-based scoring model are associated with an unfavorable prognosis for patients. Furthermore, we suggest that elevated expression of ACTA2 may indirectly affect the immune microenvironment of COAD by modulating the expression of immune-related genes.

The upstream regulators of the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene set

We using hypergeometric test method to identify upstream regulators that regulate the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene set. The results showed that CENPA, KDM1B, POLR2A, TBL1X, and UBN1, five upstream lncRNAs, significantly regulated the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene set (Fig. 7A). DDX59, CDC40, RBM47, and other upstream RBPs exhibited a stronger regulatory effect on certain subsets of the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene set (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, the expression of RBPs was remarkable higher in the control group in comparison with the ACTA2 high and low expression groups (Fig. 7C). Hypergeometric testing was performed to identify TFs that regulate the ACTA2-based clinical model gene set, including AR, TP53, SP1, and E2F1. Subsequently, we demonstrated the regulatory effects of the TFs on the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene set and we observed significant differential expression of E2F1 at the transcriptional level across different groups (Fig. 7D and 7E). Finally, the molecular docking results (Fig. 7F) demonstrated the regulatory effect of the upstream transcription factor SP1 on the expression of the scoring gene IGFBP3.

We found that LTBP2 (25 %), COL18A1 (23 %) and NOTCH3 (23 %) had relatively high mutation frequencies, and ACTA2 mutations occurred at a frequency of 4 %, with missense mutations being the most common type of mutation. Next, a CNV bar plot was generated to illustrate the CNV landscape of the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene (Fig. 8C). Moreover, the relationship between the ACTA2-based clinical scoring gene and methylation regulatory sites was explored (Fig. 8E), revealing significant negative correlations with genes such as SERPINE1, TDRD7, SIX4, and corresponding methylation regulatory sites. Discussion

COAD is a challenging malignant tumour to treat, presenting with subtle early symptoms, high recurrence and metastasis rates post-surgery, and considerable mortality rates in advanced stages. In recent times, clinicians have focused on differential gene expression in tumour tissue and their potential applications in clinical settings [21]. Therefore, additional research into COAD oncogenes aids in discovering novel biomarkers and insights into the treatment approaches for COAD.

Our results showed that the overall expression level of ACTA2 in COAD tissues was lower than that in paracancerous tissues. However, survival analysis showed that in the COAD patient population, high ACTA2 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis. This apparent contradiction may be due to the differences in the analysis objects and comparison methods: the expression analysis was based on the comparison of paracancerous tissues with the whole COAD samples, while the survival analysis was performed within the COAD samples according to the high and low ACTA2 groups. Studies have shown that α-SMA-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) can promote the invasion of cancer cells, and in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, the expression level of α-SMA is closely related to advanced tumor stage, invasive growth and tumor budding [22]. Therefore, it can be speculated that although ACTA2 is relatively downregulated in the whole COAD samples, its expression level may be increased in tumor subpopulations rich in invasive phenotypes, resulting in a poor prognosis for patients in the high-expression group.

Smooth muscle cell contract to perform many physiological functions, such as regulation of blood flow and pressure in arteries, pupil constriction, intestinal motility, and bladder voiding [8]. α-SMA is encoded by the ACTA2 gene, is a contractile protein specific to smooth muscle cells found in the vasculature. It plays a crucial role in cellular vasomotion and constriction. A decrease in aortic function was observed in experiments with ACTA2 knockout mice [23]. This is in line with the biological functions and pathways found in the enrichment analysis carried out in this study, such as epithelial cell proliferation and vascular regulation. Additionally, GSEA allowed us to identify pathways significantly activated in COAD. Among them, aberrant Wnt signalling pathway confers drug resistance to tumours and promotes tumour establishment and recurrence [24]. Aberrant activation of the Hedgehog signalling pathway is associated with tissue invasion and increased metastatic potential [25]. Activation of the p53 signalling pathway is associated with suppressing multiple tumours and mediating a favourable prognosis in COAD [26]. Therefore, these findings offer fresh perspectives in researching the pathogenesis of COAD and constructing efficient targeted treatments.

In this study, we assessed the turquoise module that displayed the most significant positive correlation with ACTA2. We then constructed a clinical model that relied on ACTA2 for scoring. We examined clinical and pathological characteristics of samples from various groups and predicted the correlation between levels of immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint analysis. Combined with existing research, it is hypothesised that mature stromal fibrosis is associated with reduced ACTA2 expression and smaller spindle-shaped myofibroblast-like cells, while the ACTA2 high expression in the immature stromal fibrosis group is accompanied by large and plump myofibroblast-like cells that resemble scar tissue. These cells may promote cancer infiltration and lead to a poor prognosis [22]. Therefore, we examined the association between ACTA2 and various immune checkpoints and discovered that the majority of genes with high scores had a positive correlation with genes associated with immune checkpoints and markers of tertiary lymphoid structures. The modulation of immune checkpoints by certain tumors obstructs the host immune system, resulting in immune evasion. Studies have indicated that immune checkpoint expression, such as the elevated expression of IL1R1 in α-SMA, is positively associated with the expression of genes related to Th17 cells and fulfills an immunoregulatory function in the tumour microenvironment [27]. Nonetheless, excessive infiltration of Th17 cells frequently culminates in increased tumour progress and immune evasion, contributing to decreased patient survival and unfavourable prognosis. It is clear that forthcoming studies ought to investigate the feasibility of using both IL1R1 blockade and checkpoint inhibitors jointly. The elevated expression of ACTA2 leads to the proliferation of α-SMA and consequently high expression of IL1R1 and a negative prognosis. This bolsters the hypothesized pessimistic prognosis for the ACTA2 high expression cohort.

We found that ACTA2 was significantly negatively correlated with Tfh cells. Tfh cells primarily exert their anti-tumor effects by assisting B cells in producing anti-tumor antibodies and enhancing humoral immunity [28]. Furthermore, ACTA2 was positively correlated with M2 macrophages, which are generally considered to have pro-tumor functions, supporting tumor progression by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines and promoting tumor angiogenesis, invasion, and immune evasion [29]. These results suggest that ACTA2 may promote COAD progression by regulating the tumor immune microenvironment, suppressing anti-tumor immunity while enhancing the activity of pro-tumor immune cells. This may explain one of the mechanisms by which elevated ACTA2 expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in COAD patients.

Subsequently, we investigated the upstream regulatory factors of the clinical indicator gene set, which is based on ACTA2, including lncRNAs, RBPs and TFs. It has been demonstrated that the expression levels of lncRNA, such as POLR2A, have a close association with their gene copy number in COAD. These findings suggest that inhibiting POLR2A could be a novel therapeutic strategy for human cancers with this common genomic alteration [30]. The function of certain RBPs in COAD has been extensively reported. For instance, the decreased expression of RBM47 mRNA in COAD and its downregulation at specific stages independently associate with reduced overall survival, demonstrating RBM47 as an inherent regulator of intestinal growth, inflammation and tumorigenesis pathways [31]. Additionally, numerous TFs have been validated to perform vital functions in COAD. For example, SP1 is associated with various fundamental biological processes and has been demonstrated to regulate and participate in the proliferation and metastasis of different tumors in tissues, as well as the expression of key genes [32].

Finally, we analysed the somatic mutation status of the ACTA2-based clinical scoring genes and presented the landscape of SNPs mutations. Our study revealed that LTBP2, COL18A1, and NOTCH3 had higher mutation frequencies in COAD within the ACTA2-based clinical scoring genes. Moreover, LTBP2 has been validated as a potential oncogene in the progression of COAD, which has significant implications for predicting prognosis [33]. It is theorised that elevated expression of ACTA2 raises the mutation frequency in downstream genes, thereby affecting the incidence and progression of COAD. Moreover, ACTA2 can undergo missense mutations at various sites. Recently, it was shown that ACTA2 mutations induce multi-systemic smooth muscle dysfunction syndrome [34], which could potentially contribute to COAD progression by impacting CAFs. Furthermore, modifications in gene methylation have widespread effects, and abnormal methylation has been observed in colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, cervical cancer, and other cancers. This suggests that these abnormal methylation events may be one of the significant causes of tumorigenesis [35]. Although additional clinical validation analyses were not performed in this study, the multi-omics signature based on ACTA2 provides a multi-faceted reference for clinical application. Mutations and methylation abnormalities in ACTA2 and its related scoring genes can be combined with existing COAD clinical indicators (such as TNM stage, CEA levels, and microsatellite instability) to provide more accurate risk assessment and prognostic prediction for patients.

In brief, we created and verified a novel clinical scoring model for forecasting COAD patients' prognosis, offering fresh avenues for COAD therapy. The ACTA2-based scoring clinical model may serve as a useful tool for guiding clinical treatment of COAD and promoting personalized therapy. However, this study has limitations, and additional in vitro and in vivo experiments are necessary to validate the accuracy of the clinical model based on ACTA2 scoring.
