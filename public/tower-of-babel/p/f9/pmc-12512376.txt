Efficacy and safety of urethral sparing versus other robot-assisted simple prostatectomy for large volume benign prostatic hyperplasia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Junhui Ying, Changchun Zhou, Yili Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aim to evaluate the efficacy and safety of urethra-sparing robot-assisted simple prostatectomy (US-RASP) compared to conventional robot-assisted simple prostatectomy (RASP) for treating large-volume benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), with particular attention to preserving ejaculatory function.MethodsA thorough search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases for studies published until September 26, 2024. Five cohort studies involving 771 patients met the inclusion criteria. Data on perioperative outcomes, International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF), Male Sexual Health Questionnaire Ejaculatory Dysfunction (MSHQ-EjD), Maximum Flow Rate (Qmax), Post-Void Residual Volume (PVR), Quality of Life (QoL) and complications were extracted and analyzed using RevMan 5.3 and Stata 15.ResultsThe meta-analysis revealed that US-RASP was markedly more effective than RASP in preserving ejaculatory function (MSHQ-EjD, WMD 8.09, 95% CI 5.92–10.26, p < 0.0001). No notable differences were identified between the two approaches regarding IPSS, IIEF, Qmax, PVR, QoL, operative time, or intraoperative blood loss and complications. Nonetheless, US-RASP showed benefits in shortening hospital stay and catheterization time.ConclusionsUS-RASP offers significant benefits in preserving ejaculatory function and is comparable to RASP in terms of safety and efficacy. It is a preferable option for younger patients or those desiring ejaculatory preservation, pending further validation through large-scale, prospective randomized trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01937-x.Keywords: Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Prostatectomy, Urethra-sparing, Robot-assisted

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a prevalent urological condition that predominantly affects older men [1]. Around 20% of males over the age of 50 encounter urinary difficulties, and its occurrence rises considerably with age, affecting over 50% of men by 70 years old [2]. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease study reported a 105.70% increase in the number of BPH cases between 1990 and 2019 [3]. Recent clinical guidelines recommend surgical intervention for patients with severe lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) unresponsive to medical therapy or those experiencing BPH-related complications, including refractory urinary retention, recurrent urinary tract infections, renal insufficiency, gross hematuria, or bladder stones [4, 5]. According to the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, open or minimally invasive simple prostatectomy, holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP), and bipolar enucleation of the prostate are recommended as primary treatment options for patients with prostate volumes exceeding 80 ml [6]. A meta-analysis revealed comparable efficacy and long-term outcomes between these two surgical approaches, with endoscopic enucleation of the prostate (EEP) demonstrating superior perioperative results but a higher incidence of short-term postoperative incontinence [7], and both conventional techniques exhibit significant limitations in preserving ejaculatory function.

RASP was first introduced into urology in 2008 [8]. In RASP, traditional open simple prostatectomy (OSP) techniques are commonly employed, with transvesical [9] and transcapsular [10] approaches being the mainstream clinical methods. However, these methods are not suitable for patients seeking to preserve ejaculation. Despite various surgical options available for treating BPH, ejaculatory dysfunction (EjD) continues to be an unresolved issue in functional outcomes, greatly affecting the quality of life for sexually active men [11, 12].

Wang et al. [13] and Simone et al. [14] proposed a novel urethra-sparing robot-assisted technique, demonstrating that US-RASP is a safe and effective procedure for addressing lower urinary tract obstruction symptoms in patients with large-volume BPH. This technique not only shows comparable outcomes to conventional surgical methods but also offers significant advantages in preserving ejaculatory function. A retrospective multicentric study comparing US-RASP to waterjet ablation therapy (WAT) for prostates > 80 ml found comparable symptom relief and ejaculatory function preservation, with US-RASP achieving a 13.6% ejaculatory dysfunction rate at one year [15]. Another study highlighted that US-RASP achieved a 54.7% BPH-6 composite endpoint success rate, with prostate volumes between 110 and 180 ml and higher preoperative Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) scores as predictors of favorable outcomes [16]. Retrograde ejaculation, anejaculation, or both have long been considered unavoidable consequences of BPH-related surgeries [17], leading to reduced sexual quality of life and concerns about fertility [18]. With the decreasing onset age of BPH and increasing life expectancy [11], this issue has become increasingly significant.

Currently, there is a significant gap in the literature no systematic review or meta-analysis has specifically compared US-RASP with conventional RASP in patients with large-volume BPH, particularly in terms of efficacy, safety, and preservation of ejaculatory function. This study aims to fill that gap by systematically evaluating the perioperative outcomes, safety profiles, and ejaculatory function preservation of the two kind surgical approaches. The findings will provide evidence-based guidance for clinical practice, assisting clinicians in selecting the most appropriate surgical technique based on individual patient needs, while ensuring effective treatment and optimizing postoperative quality of life.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19]. Furthermore, the study has been registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration number: CRD42024599847).

Search strategy

A systematic search was performed in the PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases, utilizing a combination of MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms and free-text keywords. The search terms included “Prostatic Hyperplasia,” “Prostatectomy,” “Robotic Surgical Procedures,” and “Urethra.” This strategy covered all relevant studies published from the inception of the databases to September 26, 2024. The complete search strategy, including detailed MeSH terms and their combinations, is provided in (Additional file 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Population (P): Patients with large-volume benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Intervention (I): US-RASP.

Comparison (C): RASP (e.g. Millin or Freyer approach).

Outcomes (O): MSHQ, IPSS, IIEF, Qmax, and PVR. Operative time, hospital stay, duration of catheterization, intraoperative blood loss, and complications. Data must be complete and extractable.

Study Design (S): Published cohort studies or randomized controlled trials.

Exclusion criteria

Case series, surveys, letters, editorials, reviews, and animal studies.

Studies without original data or articles published in languages other than English.

Literature screening

The retrieved studies were imported into Endnote 21 for processing. Duplicate records were identified and removed through both automated detection and manual review. Subsequently, the titles and abstracts were initially screened to identify potentially eligible original studies, followed by downloading the full texts of these studies for detailed evaluation. A comprehensive full-text review was then conducted to finalize the studies that met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review. The study screening process was conducted independently by two researchers (Yili Jin and Changchun Zhou), with cross-checking performed upon completion. Any disagreements were resolved with the assistance of a third researcher (Junhui Ying).

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (Yili Jin and Changchun Zhou). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (Junhui Ying). A specifically designed electronic data extraction form was used to capture the following information from the included studies: general details such as the first author, publication year, and country; population characteristics such as age and follow-up duration; and other key data points including operative time, hospital stay, catheterization duration, prostate size, complications, estimated intraoperative blood loss, MHSQ, IIEF, IPSS, Qmax, PVR, and QoL. For studies with incomplete data, we attempted to contact the corresponding authors for clarification.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included cohort studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [20], which assesses three domains: selection of study groups, comparability between groups, and ascertainment of outcomes. The NOS score ranges from 0 to 9, with a score of 7 or above indicating high-quality studies. Quality assessment was independently performed by two reviewers (Yili Jin and Changchun Zhou), and any disagreements were resolved through consultation with the corresponding author (Junhui Ying).

Statistical analysis

This study was performed using RevMan 5.3 and Stata 15. Heterogeneity across studies was evaluated with the Cochrane Q test and the I² statistic. For studies with I² < 50%, indicating low heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model was employed. Conversely, when I² ≥ 50%, signifying substantial heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results. Continuous data were analyzed by calculating the mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). For studies reporting medians, quartiles, or ranges, data were converted into means and standard deviations (SD) using the conversion tables provided by McGrath et al. [21]. For categorical data, risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Sensitivity analysis was performed by systematically excluding one study at a time to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall findings. In our analysis, for the questionnaire score, we used the change value (postoperative score minus preoperative score) rather than the absolute preoperative or postoperative scores. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Literature screening process

A total of 771 records were identified from the four databases. After removing 247 duplicates through automated and manual processes, 524 records were available for initial screening according to the titles and abstracts. After that, 508 were excluded for reasons specified in Fig. 1. Subsequently, 16 articles underwent full-text review, and 5 studies met the inclusion criteria [22–26]. Basic characteristics of included studies

The five included studies were published between 2020 and 2024. Among them, two studies were conducted in Korea [24, 25], and three were conducted in Italy [22, 23, 26]. Results of Meta-Analysis

Results of international prostate symptom score (IPSS)

Five studies [22–26] reported on the IPSS. Heterogeneity analysis yielded an I² value of 52% and a Q-test p-value of 0.08, indicating moderate heterogeneity. Consequently, a random-effects model was utilized for the meta-analysis. The pooled results from this model demonstrated a significant difference between conventional RASP and US-RASP (WMD: 2.30; 95% CI: 0.68–3.91; p = 0.005). (shown in Fig. 2)

Forest plot illustrating the international prostate symptom score

International index of erectile function (IIEF)

Three studies [22, 23, 26] reported data on the IIEF. Heterogeneity analysis revealed an I² value of 0% and a Q-test p-value of 0.97, indicating no significant heterogeneity. As a result, a fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The pooled results from the fixed-effects model showed no significant difference between RASP and US-RASP (WMD: 0.93; 95% CI: −0.59–2.45; p = 0.23). (shown in Fig. 3)

Forest plot illustrating the international index of erectile function

Male sexual health questionnaire ejaculatory dysfunction (MSHQ-EjD)

Four studies [22–24, 26] reported on the Male Sexual Health Questionnaire-Ejaculatory Dysfunction (MSHQ-EjD). Heterogeneity testing yielded an I² value of 68% and a Q-test p-value of 0.02, indicating substantial heterogeneity. Accordingly, a random-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. The pooled results from this model revealed a significant difference between RASP and US-RASP (WMD: 8.09; 95% CI: 5.92–10.26; p < 0.0001). (shown in Fig. 4)

Forest plot illustrating the male sexual health questionnaire ejaculatory dysfunction

Qmax (Maximum flow Rate)

Four studies [23–26] reported on maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax). Heterogeneity analysis indicated an I² value of 52% and a Q-test p-value of 0.10, suggesting moderate heterogeneity. Consequently, a random-effects model was utilized for the meta-analysis. The pooled results from this model showed no significant difference between RASP and US-RASP (WMD: 0.49; 95% CI: −1.37–2.34; p = 0.61). (shown in Fig. 5)

Forest plot illustrating the maximum flow rate

Post-Void residual volume (PVR)

Three studies [24–26] reported on PVR. Heterogeneity analysis yielded an I² value of 0% and a Q-test p-value of 0.57, suggesting no significant heterogeneity. Consequently, a fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The combined results from this model showed no significant difference between RASP and US-RASP (WMD: 5.44; 95% CI: −13.33 to 24.21; p = 0.57). (shown in Fig. 6)

Forest plot illustrating the post-void residual volume

Quality of life (QoL)

Three studies [24–26] assessed QoL. Heterogeneity analysis showed an I² value of 0% and a Q-test p-value of 0.50, indicating no significant heterogeneity. As a result, a fixed-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. The pooled results from this model indicated no significant difference between RASP and US-RASP (WMD: 0.12; 95% CI: −0.20 to 0.44; p = 0.45). (shown in Fig. 7)

Forest plot illustrating the quality of life

Operation time

Five studies [22–26] reported on operation time. Heterogeneity analysis yielded an I² value of 56% and a Q-test p-value of 0.06, indicating moderate heterogeneity. As a result, a random-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. The pooled results from this model demonstrated no significant difference in operation time between RASP and US-RASP (WMD: 0.35; 95% CI: −6.89 to 7.59; p = 0.92). (shown in Fig. 8)

Forest plot illustrating the operation time

Hospital stay

Four studies [22, 24–26] analyzed hospital stay. Heterogeneity testing yielded a high I² value of 94% and a Q-test p-value of < 0.00001, reflecting substantial heterogeneity. Thus, a random-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. The combined results demonstrated a significant reduction in hospital stay for US-RASP compared to RASP (WMD: −1.63; 95% CI: −3.25 to −0.01; p = 0.05). (shown in Fig. 9)

Complications

We summarized the complications reported in the included studies. Y. S. Shin et al. [24] reported no intraoperative complications, whereas F. Porpiglia et al. [26] noted two intraoperative complications with the RASP procedure. Apart from Y. S. Shin et al. [24], which did not provide data on postoperative complications, all other 4 studies [22, 23, 25, 26] reported relevant data (details presented in Table 3). Consequently, we conducted a meta-analysis of these four studies. Heterogeneity analysis revealed an I² value of 0% and a Q-test p-value of 0.75, indicating no significant heterogeneity. Therefore, a fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The pooled results showed no significant difference in postoperative complications between US-RASP and RASP (RR: 0.93; 95% CI: 0.82–1.06; p = 0.30), as illustrated in Fig. 12.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of individual studies by using the leave-one-out method. The results showed no significant changes in the outcomes when individual studies were excluded, indicating that the meta-analysis has good reliability. Further details are provided in the Additional file 2.

Publication bias

The ability to effectively detect publication bias is limited when fewer than 10 studies are included, reducing the statistical power of such analyses. Commonly used bias detection methods, such as funnel plots or Egger’s test, may yield false-positive or false-negative results in small sample sizes. As a result, further analyses for publication bias were not conducted [27, 28].

Discussion

This study is the first meta-analysis to evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of US-RASP versus RASP in managing large-volume BPH. The results indicate that US-RASP significantly outperforms RASP in preserving ejaculatory function. Nonetheless, the two techniques showed no significant differences in perioperative outcomes or improvements in voiding function. These findings suggest that US-RASP may be the preferred treatment option for relatively younger patients with BPH, particularly those who prioritize ejaculatory function preservation.

A deeper analysis of the data highlights the superiority of US-RASP in preserving ejaculatory function. For example, F. Porpiglia et al. [26] found that one year postoperatively, US-RASP patients showed no change in their MHSQ scores, with 81% maintaining antegrade ejaculation. In contrast, RASP patients exhibited a mean MHSQ decrease of 6 points, with only 8.8% retaining antegrade ejaculation. Similarly, Shin et al. reported that six months postoperatively, US-RASP patients had a mean MHSQ decrease of 0.5, with 75% maintaining ejaculatory function, compared to a mean decrease of 5.5 and only 20% retention in RASP patients. These findings strongly support the advantage of US-RASP in maintaining ejaculatory function.

The underlying mechanism for this advantage may lie in the precise preservation of anatomical structures achieved through robotic technology. Gil-Vernet et al. hypothesized that ejaculation could still occur if tissues surrounding the verumontanum remain intact, even when the bladder neck is open [29]. Modern robotic techniques, with their advanced visualization and precise dissection capabilities, facilitate the preservation of essential structures, including the prostatic urethra, bladder neck, and ejaculatory ducts [22, 26, 29]. This technical precision likely explains the improved outcomes for ejaculatory function among US-RASP patients.

Beyond ejaculatory function, both US-RASP and RASP demonstrated significant and comparable improvements in other voiding function outcomes, such as IIEF, Qmax, PVR, and QoL. These results align with the findings of F. Porpiglia et al. and others. However, for IPSS improvement, US-RASP showed slightly lower gains compared to RASP. Interestingly, some studies, such as those by A.M. Bove et al. and Shin et al., reported that US-RASP achieved comparable or even superior short-term improvements in IPSS. Shin et al. suggested that the urethra-preserving characteristics of US-RASP reduce postoperative trauma and inflammation, accelerating symptom relief. These discrepancies likely stem from differences in study design and patient populations. Our meta-analysis incorporated multicenter data with broader representation, while single-center studies typically involved smaller samples and focused on short-term follow-up.

Perioperative outcomes, such as operative time and intraoperative blood loss, showed no significant differences between US-RASP and RASP, consistent with findings by F. Porpiglia et al. Moreover, Johnson et al. reported that RASP operative times significantly improved with surgical expertise, decreasing from 160 min in early cohorts to 134 min in later cohorts [30]. Technological advancements in robotic systems are likely to reduce operative times. Additionally, Choi et al. highlighted that EUS-RASP, compared to RASP, preserves the urethra and bladder neck, avoids bladder injury, and results in minimal postoperative hematuria, further supporting the safety and effectiveness of these approaches.

Regarding perioperative complications, both techniques demonstrated low complication rates (0–16.7%), with most complications classified as Clavien-Dindo grade < 3. Choi et al. reported complication rates were 12.5% in the EUS-RASP group and 16.7% in the RASP group, including two cases of grade 3a complications (right inguinal hernia). Despite these minor differences, both procedures are considered safe when performed by experienced surgeons. US-RASP significantly reduced hospital stay by eliminating the need for postoperative bladder irrigation and minimizing catheterization time as reported by Y.S. Shin et al. This benefit enhances patient comfort and reduces healthcare resource utilization, making US-RASP particularly appealing for patients prioritizing faster recovery.

However, this review has several limitations. A major limitation of this meta-analysis is the inclusion of only retrospective studies, which may introduce selection bias as the patient cohorts assigned non-randomly to either US-RASP or control groups may not be representative. Furthermore, the retrospective design is susceptible to confounding factors such as variations in surgeons’ technical proficiency or patient characteristics, as well as inconsistencies in data collection, potentially compromising the reliability and generalizability of the results. Moreover, due to the limited number of included studies, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses comparing US-RASP specifically with the Freyer or Millin approaches. Consequently, potential differences in clinical outcomes between these two surgical techniques could not be separately evaluated. This limitation may have contributed to heterogeneity and reduced the precision of our conclusions regarding the comparative effectiveness of US-RASP. To address these issues, future research should prioritize high-quality RCTs to minimize bias, standardize outcome measures, and provide robust evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of US-RASP.

The future development of RASP and emerging techniques such as US-RASP faces real-world challenges, including the high cost of advanced robotic systems and the infrastructure requirements, which may limit their accessibility in resource-constrained healthcare settings. In addition, the technical complexity of these procedures demands a high level of surgical expertise, and the associated learning curve may affect perioperative outcomes, particularly in centers with limited experience in robotic surgery. Notably, the introduction of new robotic platforms, such as the HUGO™ RAS system [31], offers significant advantages, including a modular design and enhanced range of motion, which can optimize surgical precision and efficiency. Large-scale, multicenter studies are needed to validate these advantages, address cost-effectiveness concerns, improve accessibility, and develop strategies to mitigate the impact of the learning curve, thereby facilitating broader adoption in non-oncologic urologic surgery.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that US-RASP offers significant advantages for patients with a desire to preserve ejaculatory function, while maintaining safety and efficacy comparable to RASP. However, these results require further validation through large-sample, prospective, multicenter, and long-term follow-up RCTs. Based on these findings, US-RASP should be considered the preferred option for patients with a strong desire for ejaculatory preservation, provided robotic surgical systems and technical expertise are available.
