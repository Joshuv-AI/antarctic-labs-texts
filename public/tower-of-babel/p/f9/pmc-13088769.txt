Clinical prognostic value of cartilage intermediate layer protein 1 (CILP-1) in elderly patients with acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Li Xiang, Li Xiong, Yi Li, JiaPeng Chu, Chunlai Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate the levels of human cartilage intermediate layer protein 1 (CILP-1) in elderly patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), its correlation with myocardial ischemia and ventricular remodeling, and its prognostic value for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).MethodsA total of 1869 elderly patients admitted to the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University for chest pain within 24 h of onset were enrolled. Participants were categorized into the AMI group (n = 849) and the non-AMI group (n = 1020). The plasma CILP-1 levels were measured upon admission. In the AMI group, the wall motion score (WMS) was recorded within 24 h of pain onset, and the SYNTAX score was evaluated based on coronary angiography. Statistical analysis of MACE occurrence during 12-month follow-up was performed in the AMI patients.ResultsCILP-1 levels were significantly higher in the AMI group compared to the non-AMI group (1058.33 [793.73, 1397.05] ng/L vs. 975.60 [700.24, 1279.41] ng/L, P < 0.05), and notably higher in the MACE group than in the non-MACE group (1650.3 [1074.61, 1718.04] ng/L vs. 976.40 [738.00, 1287.78] ng/L, P < 0.05). CILP-1 expression correlated positively with WMS, SYNTAX score, myocardial injury markers, C-reactive protein (CRP), and left-ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd), and negatively with left-ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (P < 0.05). Univariate Cox analysis indicated that CILP-1, age, cardiac troponin T (cTn-T), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), myoglobin, CRP, LVEF, WMS, and SYNTAX were significant predictors of MACE in AMI patients (P < 0.05). After adjusting for confounding factors, CK-MB, LVEF, WMS, and SYNTAX remained independent predictors of MACE (P < 0.05). Furthermore, incorporating CILP-1 into a baseline model (comprising LVEF, male sex, SYNTAX, WMS, CRP, and CK-MB) significantly enhanced risk estimation for MACE (AUC 0.78, 95% CI:0.75–0.80 vs. AUC 0.59, 95% CI:0.55–0.62; P < 0.05).ConclusionsElderly AMI patients present significantly elevated CILP-1 levels, which positively correlate with the degree of ischemia and ventricular remodeling. CILP-1 may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker for risk stratification in elderly AMI patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05648-5.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is one of the most critical cardiovascular diseases, particularly prevalent among the elderly population. It is characterized by ischemia-induced necrosis that triggers segmental wall motion dysfunction and ventricular aneurysm formation. This process often culminates in adverse myocardial remodeling, fibrosis and cardiac enlargement, ultimately leading to ischemic cardiomyopathy and a significant decline in long-term survival and quality of life [1].Excessive protein deposition in the extracellular matrix of myocardial cells after myocardial ischemia is one of the main causes of myocardial remodeling in AMI patients through the pathway of cardiac fibrosis. Cartilage intermediate layer protein 1 (CILP-1), primarily derived from cardiac fibroblasts [2], has been shown playing a role in post-infarction fibrosis, where its expression significantly increases, particularly in the late stages of heart failure [3, 4]. However, there is limited data on the expression of CILP-1 in the elderly patients with AMI. Furthermore, the correlation of CILP-1 with severity of ischemia, and impact of CILP-1 on long-term prognosis remains poorly defined. In this study, we quantified serum CILP-1 levels in elderly AMI patients to evaluate their association with clinical parameters such as wall motion score (WMS), SYNTAX score, and myocardial necrosis markers. We further examined the relationship between CILP-1 and indicators of ventricular remodeling, such as left-ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd) and ejection fraction (LVEF). Additionally, we conducted a follow-up to assess the prognostic value of CILP-1 in predicting major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), with the goal of improving risk stratification and facilitating early intervention in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Selection and exclusion criteria for participants

This study employed a retrospective design, consecutively screening 2,249 elderly patients (aged ≥ 60 years) who presented with chest pain at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2018 and June 2022. A total of 1901 patients finally remained, and 32 patients were lost to follow-up in the end, resulting in a final sample size of 1869 patients, including 849 patients with AMI and 1020 patients without AMI.

Diagnostic criteria for AMI were based on the universally accepted definition [5]: Cardiac troponin T(cTnT) increases and/or decreases, exceeding the 99th percentile of the upper limit of normal value at least once, and meeting at least one of the following criteria: (1) symptoms of myocardial ischemia; (2) new ischemic electrocardiogram changes; (3) emerging pathological Q-waves; (4) imaging findings suggesting that there are new occurrences of viable myocardial loss or segmental wall motion abnormalities consistent with ischemia; (5) coronary angiography or autopsy confirming the presence of blood clots in the coronary artery.

Based on electrocardiogram (ECG) manifestations, AMI patients were stratified into ST-segment-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI, n = 465) and non–ST-segment-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI, n = 384).

The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: age ≥ 85 years; loss to follow-up; severe valvular heart disease; acute heart failure unrelated to myocardial infarction; a history of previous myocardial infarction or heart failure, previous coronary intervention surgery and coronary artery bypass graft, myocarditis, pericarditis, congenital heart disease, pulmonary heart disease, severe infection, major trauma, autoimmune diseases, rheumatic diseases, hematological diseases, and severe hepatic or renal dysfunction.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. JD-LK-2021-029-02, review). All of the enrolled patients signed informed consent forms.

Clinical characteristics

Baseline clinical characteristics were obtained for all participants, including age, gender, smoking and drinking habits, history of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and heart failure. A family history of coronary artery disease (CAD) was also documented.

We recorded the initial ECG and vital signs such as blood pressure and heart rate. Cardiac function was assessed according to the Killip grade.

Fasting blood samples were obtained upon admission including blood routine, C-reactive protein (CRP), liver and kidney function tests, lipid profile, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), myoglobin, and cardiac troponin T (cTn-T).Simultaneously peripheral blood samples were collected prior to coronary artery reperfusion for CILP-1 quantification and stored at − 80 °C before analysis. The concentration of CILP-1 was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Shanghai Xinle, catalogue number: XL-EH0786). The assay had an analytical sensitivity of 20 ng/L, with intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV) of < 10% and < 12%, respectively.

Echocardiography

All of the participants underwent echocardiographic examination within 24 h of admission using a Vivid IQ ultrasonic diagnostic system (GE, USA), following the guideline standards [6]. Echocardiography was performed by experienced physicians, and standard cardiac windows, including the left-ventricular long axis, short axis, and apical four-chamber, five-chamber views were systematically visualized. The degree of abnormal wall motion in the AMI patients was quatified using the wall motion score (WMS) [7]. Segmental function was categorized as follows: 1 (normal), 2 (hypokinetic), 3 (akinetic), 4 (dyskinetic), and 5 (aneurysmal).

Standard treatment

The AMI patients received standardized pharmacotherapy treatment based on established clinical guidelines individually. Coronary angiography and interventional treatment were performed as clinically indicated [8]. Two experienced cardiologists assessed the SYNTAX scores based on the coronary angiography results [9].

Follow-up

The participants with AMI were followed up for at least 12 months (average 12.6 ± 0.2 months) via outpatient visits, hospital records, and telephone interviews. All of the AMI patients were screened for MACE during the follow-up, which was defined as a composite of recurrent angina or AMI requiring target vessel revascularization, malignant ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or coronary artery disease–related death. The observation period started the day after discharge, with the time-to-event calculated from this baseline until the first occurrence of MACE or the final follow-up date. The follow-up time was calculated in months.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0, GraphPad Prism 9, MedCalc, and R (version 4.4.1). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD), while those with skewed distribution were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequency and percentage. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted, and survival analysis along with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves was performed to assess predictors of MACE in the AMI patients. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis were generated using the rms and rmda R packages to assess model calibration and clinical benefit, respectively.

Comparison of CILP-1 expression levels between different groups

The levels of CILP-1 in the AMI group were higher than those in the non-AMI group (1058.33 [793.73, 1397.05] ng/L vs. 975.60 [700.24, 1279.41] ng/L, P < 0.05; Fig. 1A). Among the AMI patients, the MACE group had a higher CILP-1 expression than the non-MACE group (1650.39 [1074.61, 1718.04] ng/L vs. 976.40 [738.00, 1287.78] ng/L, P < 0.05; Fig. 1B). However, there was no significant difference between the STEMI group (n = 465) and the non-STEMI group (n = 384) (1058.61 [804.25, 1397.13] ng/L vs. 1047.76 [783.50, 1397.29] ng/L, P > 0.05; Fig. 1C).

Logistic regression analysis of factors associated with MACE in AMI patients

Over an average follow up of 12.6 ± 0.2 months, 146 AMI patients experienced MACE, including 117 cases of heart failure, 5 cases of malignant ventricular arrhythmia, 15 cases of recurrent angina requiring revascularization, 6 cases of cardiogenic shock, and 3 cases of coronary heart disease–related deaths.

The univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis identified several variables significantly associated with MACE, including CILP-1, age, cTn-T, CK-MB, Myoglobin, CRP, LVEF, WMS, and SYNTAX (P < 0.01). After adjusting for potential confounders in the multivariate model, CK-MB, LVEF, WMS, and SYNTAX remained independent predictors of MACE in AMI patients. Notably, WMS proved to be the strongest independent risk factor, yielding an HR of 1.49 (P < 0.05, Table 3).

This study performed follow-up assessments on patients in the AMI group, with a mean duration of 12.6 ± 0.2 months. When stratified by sex, the Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed clear divergence between the two groups (log-rank test, P < 0.001). As illustrated in the figure (females indicated in red; males in green), males experienced a higher number of MACE events, a more rapid decline in cumulative survival probability, and poorer survival prognosis compared to females.(Please refer to Supplementary Material Fig. 1.KM curve for details).

Clinical value of CILP-1 for MACE in AMI patients

As shown in Fig. 3, the Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve indicated that basic risk factors (including LVEF, male sex, SYNTAX score, WMS, CRP, and CK-MB) were the predictors of MACE (AUC 0.59, 95% CI 55.4–62.2). The addition of CILP1 as a covariate resulted in a substantial incremental prognostic value in risk estimation over traditional risk factors for MACE (AUC 0.77, 95% CI 74.6–80.3, P < 0.05).

Discussion

This study discovered that serum CILP-1 levels are significantly elevated in AMI patients, and demonstrated that CILP-1 was a potential risk factor for predicting MACE. These fundings may suggest the clinical utility of CILP-1 in the early detection of high-risk individuals, potentially facilitating timely intervention to mitigate adverse cardiovascular outcomes and improve long-term prognosis.

AMI commonly arises from coronary artery lesions and leads to a sudden reduction or interruption of coronary flow, where the rupture or erosion of unstable plaques trigger subsequent formation of red or white thrombi [5]. Our study further confirmed that AMI patients exhibit a higher prevalence of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and male gender, alongside elevated LDL-C levels, consistent with the current consensus [10], reaffirming that all of these are critical risk factors for the occurrence of acute myocardial infarction.

Aerobic metabolism is interrupted due to suspended coronary blood supply, leading to myocardial necrosis in the ischemic area. Ischemic myocardium undergoes fibrosis and remodeling, which ends up with adverse cardiac events such as heart failure, malignant arrhythmias, and cardiogenic shock, ultimately severely impacting prognosis [11]. Crucially, the risk of adverse cardiac remodeling persists even after percutaneous coronary intervention, as necrotic tissue remains a substrate for structural deterioration, which can affect the prognosis of post-infarction patients [12]. Consequently, early identification of high-risk individuals is urgently needed both before and after effective reperfusion intervention.

Myocardial necrosis markers are released into the circulation system after AMI, with their peak concentrations reflecting the infarct size. Tanriverdi O’s study demonstrated that they are closely associated with the left ventricular dysfunction [13]. WMS increases due to weakened or absent wall motion at the site of ischemic injury [14]. SYNTAX score provides comprehensive evaluation of coronary morphology and ischemic burden. A higher SYNTAX score signifies more extensive coronary involvement and severe ischemia [15]. Conventionally, myocardial injury markers, WMS, and SYNTAX scores are employed to assess the extension of myocardial ischemia in AMI patients, with higher values indicating a poor prognosis [16, 17]. Our findings showed that AMI patients with MACE exhibited higher levels of myocardial necrotic markers including WMS, total number of diseased vessels, and SYNTAX scores. These factors increased the risk of MACE in AMI patients. Moreover, the study also revealed positive correlations of CILP-1 with WMS, SYNTAX scores, and cTnT, suggesting that elevated CILP-1 levels are associated with a higher degree of myocardial ischemia and a larger ischemic area and contribute to the occurrence of MACE.

Severely damaged myocardium repairs mainly through the formation of collagen and scars with a process mediated by complex inflammatory/reparative cascades, which typically involves the deposition of extracellular matrix proteins and leads to myocardial interstitial expansion—a condition known as myocardial fibrosis (MF) [18], a critical pathophysiological process in ventricular remodeling after severe myocardial damage and a crucial step in the pathogenesis of ischemic cardiomyopathy. Additionally, MF is a major substrate of MACE, such as heart failure and malignant arrhythmias in AMI patients [19].

There may be multiple pathways contribute to the development of myocardial fibrosis. Specifically, the TGF-β signaling pathway has been extensively studied and is considered to play a role in mediating the transformation of inflammation into scar after myocardial injury [18, 20, 21]. Previous studies have shown that expression levels of TGF-β and Smad3 are upregulated in an infarcted myocardial area, and the TGF-α/Smad and MAPK signaling pathways are regulated by membrane receptors, intracellular signaling molecules, and gene levels, which induce cardiac fibroblast proliferation, promote type I and III collagen synthesis and deposition, and regulate the development of fibrosis [22, 23]. CILP-1 is primarily secreted by cardiac fibroblasts and exerts an anti-MF effect by inhibiting the TGF-β signaling activity [24]. Higher expression of CILP-1 suggests the presence of MF and ventricular remodeling.

The inflammatory cascade can also drives ventricular remodeling through mechanisms involving angiotensinogen, oxidative stress, myocardial cell apoptosis, and fibrosis. Elevated CRP levels often indicate inflammatory responses. Studies by Ahmad et al. demonstrated that early measurement of CRP has predictive value for 1-year mortality in AMI patients [25]. Our study showed that CRP and CILP-1 were upregulated in AMI patients, and were positively correlated, which is consistent with the research findings of PARK S’s [26]. Given that both CRP and CILP-1 were identified as risk factors for predicting the occurrence of MACE, we posit that high expression of CILP-1 in AMI patients may exert its anti-MF and ventricular remodeling effects through the inhibition of TGF-β–mediated inflammation.

CILP-1 is a matrix cell protein with a molecular weight of 132.5 kDa. It is commonly found in the extracellular matrix of articular cartilage and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various cartilage diseases [2]. CILP-1 expression is induced by TGF-β in chondrocytes, while in myeloid cells, CILP-1 inhibits the TGF-β–induced expression of extracellular-matrix genes [24]. Apart from its presence in the extracellular matrix of articular cartilage, CILP-1 is also available in human bone and myocardial tissue [4]. This study identified a significant positive correlation between CILP-1 levels and both CK and CK-MB. Given that CK-MB is one of the established predictors for MACE, this finding suggests that elevated expression of CILP-1 may be associated with skeletal muscle and myocardial injury.

As myocardial fibrosis progresses after myocardial infarction, CILP-1 expression significantly increases, indicating its integral role in ischemia-induced ventricular remodeling [4]. Previous studies have shown that overexpression of both N-terminal and C-terminal fragments of CILP-1 attenuates Smad3 phosphorylation, interferes with SMAD signaling, inhibits the activity of TGF-β, and slows down the process of myocardial fibrosis [2], which may explain why CILP-1 levels increase when myocardial fibrosis occurs. Recent studies have also shown that the TGF-βinhibitor CILP serves as a novel biomarker for cardiac fibrosis [27]. Left-ventricular dilatation and LVEF decline are commonly used as indicators of ventricular remodeling in clinical practice. This study also revealed a positive correlation between serum CILP-1 expression and LVDd, as well as a negative correlation with LVEF, further validating CILP-1’s involvement in structural deterioration of AMI patients. Consequently, CILP-1 could be a potential target for post-AMI remodeling, offering a novel strategic approach to managing post-AMI heart failure.

Conclusions

To conclude, CILP-1 can serve as a valuable biomarker for the prognostic evaluation of elderly AMI patient Early and accurate risk stratification can facilitate timely and proper interventions to reduce the occurrence of MACE and improve the long-term prognosis of AMI patients. However, the study has limitations as it was a single-center retrospective study. Future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are still warranted to further validate the predictive utility of CILP-1 for AMI prognosis and the occurrence of adverse cardiovascular events.
