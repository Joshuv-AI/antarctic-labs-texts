Utilization of expired sulphadimidine in the fabrication of a metal free graphitic carbon nitride to photodegrade doxycycline in water: an experimental and statistical study
Authors: Lalita Yadav, Md Zainul Abedeen, Madhu Agarwal, Ragini Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDoxycycline (DC) consumption increased greatly during the COVID-19 period, while sulphadimidine is being prescribed to treat fungal infections in both humans and livestock. When not required, these drugs are often improperly disposed off and dumped into water bodies, resulting in antimicrobial resistance and significant water contamination worldwide. Hence, to nullify two pollutants in a single strategy a nitrogen and sulphur co-incorporated graphitic carbon nitride (NSCN), metal free photocatalyst has been synthesized using expired sulphadimidine drug and melamine, which effectively degraded 98.3% of DC. However, pristine graphitic carbon nitride exhibited a degradation efficiency of only 41.1% towards DC under identical optimal conditions. The catalyst has been analyzed thoroughly using various characterization techniques. The incorporation of nitrogen and sulphur causes a shift in the energy band gap (Eg) from 2.7 eV to 1.8 eV, enhancing its potential as an efficient photocatalyst. Also BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) exhibited an elevation in surface area of catalyst from 28 m2/g in pristine CN to 155.5 m2/g in NSCN after incorporation of N, and S. Batch studies exhibited that NSCN efficiently mitigates DC within 30 min from an aqueous solution at optimum parameters of catalyst dosage 10 mg, DC concentration of 20 mg/L, and obeys pseudo first-order kinetics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22468-3.Keywords: g-C3N4 photocatalyst, Expired sulphadimidine, Doxycycline, Recyclability, Response surface methodologySubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Antibiotics are extensively prescribed for both humans and animals to treat or prevent bacterial infections. The growing use of antibiotics has exacerbated issues such as bacterial resistance, ecological disruption, and water pollution1–3. Among various antibiotics, tetracycline are produced on a huge scale and are among the most commonly utilized4,5. The persistent and non-biodegradable characteristics of antibiotics represent a significant risk to the integrity of natural water sources, thereby endangering human health. Tetracyclines, in particular, have been detected in natural water systems at concentrations ranging from 0.11 to 0.42 µg L-16. Moreover, due to the limited metabolic breakdown of tetracyclines, their residual concentrations often accumulate and become prevalent in wastewater treatment plants7,8. Within the tetracycline family, doxycycline (DC) stands out as a widely utilized antibiotic in animal husbandry9. Doxycycline exhibits significant bioresistance, allowing it to persist and accumulate in wastewater treatment systems. This persistence poses serious health risks to humans and disrupts the balance of the entire ecosystem. Hence, its removal becomes a prime necessity for well being of human and aquatic systems. Recently, there has been a hike in interest of policymakers, environmentalists, and researchers in developing advanced strategies for addressing water contamination10. Several techniques, such as photocatalysis11, coagulation10, chemical oxidation12, flocculation13, and aerobic biological treatments14 are commonly employed to remove antibiotics from wastewater. Photocatalytic process has demonstrated significant potential among these methods, primarily due to its efficient utilization of renewable solar energy and no production of secondary contaminants15,16.

The improper disposal of expired and unused medications has emerged as a major contributor to global pollution17. Despite increasing awareness, there remains a strong emphasis on developing sustainable strategies for effective waste management and mitigation18. Expired drugs, which contribute significantly to solid chemical waste, have largely been overlooked. To date, only a few reports have studied their potential applications in chemical transformations and battery development19,20.

Sulphadimidine, a drug that belongs to the sulphonamides class of antibiotics, which are widely utilized in treating infections in humans and animals21. It is a commonly employed antimicrobial agent incorporated into the feed of meat-producing animals to combat infections22. Sulphadimidine has become a major pollutant found in water sources worldwide and in wastewater treatment plant effluents, largely due to improper disposal, dumping, and insufficient wastewater treatment processes23,24.

Consequently, to tackle two pollutants with a single strategy and move toward a sustainable solution, a metal-free photocatalyst has been synthesized from melamine and expired sulphadimidine through a straightforward single pot pyrolysis method, resulting in N, S co-incorporated graphitic carbon nitride (NSCN). Graphitic carbon nitride (CN) possesses a graphite-like structure composed of layered arrangements with an approximate band gap of 2.7 eV25. The potency of pure CN to act as a photocatalyst is less due to its weak light absorption and the rapid recombination of photoexcited charge pairs26. To overcome these challenges and improve its efficiency, various strategies have been considered, like structural modifications27, heterojunction formation28, integration with a co-catalyst29, heteroatom doping30, plasmon sensitization31, and combinations of these approaches.

From these approaches, heteroatom or non-metal doping proves to be a highly effective method for maintaining the characteristics of non-metal catalysts. It enhances light absorption, reduces the recombination of photoinduced charges, and improves the morphology, electronic structure, and surface properties of CN32,33. By adjusting the bandgap, doping enhances the performance of photocatalysts by generating additional defects and surface vacancies, increasing reactivity, and modifying the material’s morphology. The performance of CN has been improved through the incorporation of various heteroatoms, including S, N, O, P, B, and C34. Xing et al. reported N-doped porous thin-walled CN nanotubes, which degraded 93% tetracycline within 60 min32. Similarly, another study by Zhu et al. reported N-doped CN, which eliminates 70.1% phenol in a time duration of 3 h35. While Fan et al. proposed the synthesis of sulfur-doped CN porous rods, which revealed almost complete photodegradation of RhB dye in 120 min36. Although these newly developed heteroatom-doped CN outperform pure CN, their enhancement in adsorption and catalytic efficiency remains limited due to the low content of single-atom doped elements. To address this issue, researchers are increasingly exploring the co-doping of photocatalysts with two or three different electronegative elements. This approach not only enhances the incorporation of heteroatom dopants but also creates a synergistic effect through a unique electron distribution, thereby further improving the adsorption and catalytic performance of photocatalyst. By modifying the bandgap, co-doping enhances the efficiency of photocatalysts by creating additional defects and surface vacancies, which increase activity and alter the surface morphology. Therefore, the main motive of this research is to synthesize a nitrogen, sulphur co-incorporated photocatalyst from expired medicine sulphadimidine which was utilized to treat noxious emerging antibiotics pollutant.

Experimental

Reagents

Pure reagents were used for all studies. All the studies were performed in deionized water (DI). Chemicals like p-benzoquinone (BNQ), doxycycline, melamine, di-sodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate dihydrate (Na2EDTA.2H2O), tertiary butyl alcohol (t-BuOH), and sodium azide (NaN3) were taken from Sigma Aldrich. Details of instrumentation have been given in text S1.

Synthetic procedure of NSCN

In summary, 0.2 g of expired drug was combined with melamine (3 g) and ground using a pestle and mortar for 15 min at room temperature without any solvents. The finely grounded powder was placed in a covered silica crucible and subjected to pyrolysis at 550 °C for 4 h at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in an inert atmosphere of argon. The resulting solid mass was then grounded again to obtain a greyish-black product, referred to as NSCN. For optimization experiments, the photocatalyst was also synthesized by varying the mass of drug (0.05 g, 0.2 g, and 0.4 g) and was designated as 0.05NSCN, 0.2NSCN, and 0.4NSCN, respectively. In contrast, pristine CN was also prepared by following the same procedure with pure melamine (3 g) (Fig. 1).

Pictorial representation of the synthetic procedure of NSCN.

Evaluation of photocatalytic activity

To evaluate the photocatalytic performance of the NSCN catalyst towards the degradation of DC, systematic batch experiments were conducted under natural sunlight exposure during mid-day hours in the months of April and May at Malaviya National Institute of Technology, Jaipur, Rajasthan, India. During this period, typical solar irradiance on clear days around noon (from 12:30 to 2:00 PM) ranges from approximately 7.7 to 6.8 kWh/m², with average daytime temperatures between 30 °C and 38 °C and a UV index reaching up to 11. All the experiments were carried out at neutral pH (pH 7). For experiments, conical flasks (100 mL) having 20 mL of DC solutions at varying initial DC concentrations (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mg/L) were employed, with the catalyst dosage precisely adjusted to 5, 10, 15, and 20 mg to identify the influence of both pollutant concentration and photocatalyst loading on degradation efficiency. Prior to light irradiation, the prepared solutions were sonicated for 5 min on ultrasonicator and then agitated in the dark for 1 h using a mechanical shaker to ensure adsorption–desorption equilibrium between the catalyst and DC molecules. Subsequently, the conical flasks containing samples were taken to sunlight to facilitate photocatalytic degradation. On exposure to sunlight no external stirring was applied. As prior to each experiment, the suspension was ultrasonically dispersed, and due to the fine particle size of the catalyst and the relatively low dosage, the suspension remained stable without visible settling during the entire reaction period. At predetermined intervals of time, 1 mL of the DC sample was withdrawn, centrifuged, and checked by a UV-vis spectrophotometer to assess the degradation. The absorbance of DC was noted at a λmax value of 275 nm. Electrochemical studies

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) was taken as the working electrode, whose dimension was 2 cm x 2 cm, and the catalyst was coated to cover the surface area of the electrode using a micropipette. We employed the Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) for the reference electrode, and the platinum wire was taken as a source of the counter electrode. All these electrodes were kept in the 0.1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte solution for recording the Mott-Schottky and EIS experiments. For both of these experiments, different parameters were optimized. The slurry was prepared by taking 20 mg of 0.2NSCN or CN in a separate vial, then 400 µL of isopropanol and 50 µL of Nafion solution were added. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min to obtain uniform dispersion, and then 20 µL was drop-casted on the working electrode. A single layer was coated on the FTO electrode such that it covers an area of 4 cm2 (2 cm × 2 cm). A uniformly single-layer coating was done on the electrode in such a way that it covers the entire electrode, and was kept for drying for 2 hours at room temperature.

Results and discussion

Characterization of 0.2NSCN

XPS was utilized to analyze the elemental composition of the CN and 0.2NSCN samples. Carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen were detected in CN through the full-spectrum survey scan, as presented in Figure S1. Meanwhile the survey of synthesized photocatalyst 0.2NSCN revealed the existence of elemental sulphur (Fig. 2a). The high-resolution deconvoluted N 1 s spectrum of 0.2NSCN revealed distinct peaks at binding energies of 397.6 eV, 398.9 eV, and 400.1 eV (Fig. 2b), corresponding to sp²-hybridized nitrogen in C–N = C groups of triazine rings, tertiary nitrogen atoms (N–(C)₃), and terminal amino functionalities (C–N–H), respectively32. High resolution C 1 s spectra for 0.2NSCN revealed the presence of peaks at 283.9 eV, 285.3 eV, and 287.1 eV, implying C-C, C-O or C-S and N-C = N (sp2 hybridized carbon) (Fig. 2c)41–43. While the deconvoluted spectra of S 2p (Fig. 2d) exhibited a peak at 163.6 eV, which shows S–C bonds, implying the successful incorporation of sulphur into CN44–47, and the O 1 s deconvoluted spectrum displayed a peak at 531.5 eV corresponding to C–O or O–H groups, respectively (Fig. 2e)42,48,49.

(a) Full scan XPS of 0.2NSCN; (b, c, d, e) deconvoluted spectra of N 1 s, C 1 s, S 2p, and O 1s.

FTIR spectroscopy was utilized to elucidate the functional groups present within the samples. Figure 3(a) illustrates the FTIR spectrum of 0.2NSCN closely resembles that of pristine CN, indicating comparable structural features. The bands observed from 1223 to 1612 cm-1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of aromatic C–N heterocycles35. Notably, the pronounced peak at 804 cm-1 corresponds to the characteristic in-plane bending vibration of triazine moieties43,50. Additionally, the broad absorption band at 2961–3351 cm-1 region is indicative of uncondensed terminal amino functionalities50. While in FTIR of sulphadimidine, the peak at 3439, 3343, and 3240 cm-1 corresponds to -NH stretching of primary amine and amine group of sulphonamide group. The peaks at 1634, 1595, and 1142 cm-1 depict the presence of C = C, C = N, and S = O vibrations. The absence of characteristic peaks associated with sulphadimidine in the 0.2NSCN spectrum suggests that the structural framework of CN remains intact following the synthesis of 0.2NSCN.

(a) FTIR of sulphadimidine, CN, and 0.2NSCN, (b) XRD diffraction pattern of CN, 0.2NSCN, and (c) BET of 0.2NSCN photocatalyst.

Further, the synthesized samples showed XRD patterns, with two characteristic peaks at 13.00° (100), and 27.65° (002) representing the in-plane periodic arrangement of heptazine units and structural stacking of tri-s-triazine heterocycles, respectively35. The catalyst 0.2NSCN exhibited low intensity peaks to that of pristine CN with a slight shift in peak position from 27.6˚ to 27.2˚, likely attributed due to the introduction of nitrogen and sulphur atoms (Fig. 3b)51. The BET method offers a comprehensive assessment of the textural properties of the catalyst. As illustrated in Fig. 3c, catalyst 0.2NSCN demonstrates a behavior of Type-IV adsorption with a distinct H3-type hysteresis loop, as classified by IUPAC pore categorization52. The synthesized catalyst exhibits an elevated surface area of 155.5 m²/g from 28 m2/g to that of CN18, representing a significant accessible surface for interactions. Furthermore, its pore volume is determined to be 0.404 cm³/g, with a pore radius of 1.698 nm, indicating its mesoporous nature53. While CN exhibits pore volume 0.231 cm³/g with pore radius 1.59 nm16.

Morphological variations were seen by taking FESEM of CN and 0.2NSCN. As depicted in Fig. 4a, CN displays a bulk, smooth-surfaced structure, in agreement with previously reported observations [29]. In contrast, the 0.2NSCN sample (Fig. 4b) exhibited pronounced surface roughness and structural heterogeneity, likely arising from the incorporation of nitrogen and sulphur. Additionally, Energy-Dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis was conducted to reveal the elemental distribution within the catalyst 0.2NSCN (Fig. 4c). Elemental mapping confirmed the presence of carbon (46.3%), nitrogen (51.2%), oxygen (2.29%), and sulphur (0.21%), validating the successful incorporation of these elements and the effective synthesis of photocatalyst (Fig. 4d).

Electrochemical studies

The photodegradation mechanism by energy band structure was explored using the Mott-Schottky (MS) experiment illustrated in Fig. 5(b). The energy level was calculated by the MS plot of the catalyst (0.2NSCN and CN), which was recorded at a frequency of 1 kHz. As shown in the plot, the plot’s positive slope confirms the n-type semiconducting nature of both catalysts. Extrapolating the MS plots to the intercept where 1/C² = 0 can determine the flat band potentials. This method calculated the flat band potential as −1.46 V and − 1.19 V (Vs Ag/AgCl) for the CN and 0.2NSCN, respectively. To convert these values in reference to normal hydrogen electrode scale (vs. NHE), the relation was employed, which yields the flat band potentials of −1.26 V for CN and − 0.993 V for 0.2NSCN (vs. NHE)54. According to the previous literature, the flat band potential can be approximated to the conduction band edge35,55. Using the above equation, the valence band energy (EVB) can be calculated as 1.48 eV and 0.81 eV for the CN and 0.2NSCN, respectively.

These results indicate that nitrogen, sulphur co-doping in the catalyst leads to a downshift in the CB edge position and an upshift in the VB edge of 0.2NSCN with respect to CN on the potential scale, collectively contributing to a markedly reduced band gap (Fig. 6b)35. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to gain deeper insight into the charge transfer characteristics of the catalyst at the frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz, and voltage amplitude of 5 mV. Figure 5c displays the EIS spectra for both CN and 0.2NSCN, characterized by a semicircular region at high frequencies followed by a linear segment at lower frequencies56. The diameter of the semicircle corresponds to the charge transfer resistance (Rct), which was quantitatively evaluated through equivalent circuit fitting, as illustrated in the inset. In general, a smaller semicircular arc in the Nyquist plot signifies a lower Rct, indicating more efficient electron transport and higher electrical conductivity57. The charge transfer resistance for the CN and 0.2NSCN is calculated as 1075 Ω and 830 Ω, respectively. Also, it is evident from the plot that 0.2NSCN exhibits a noticeably smaller arc compared to CN, reflecting its superior charge transfer capability58. This enhancement suggests that doping markedly improves the electronic conductivity of the catalyst, thereby facilitating a more efficient charge transfer process.

Photodegradation studies

The activity of the synthesized samples was systematically assessed via the photodegradation of DC under sunlight irradiation. Control experiments (blank) were conducted in the absence of photocatalyst using the same parameters as the experimental set up which reveals that DC (20 mg/L) exhibits negligible degradation in aqueous solution after exposure to sunlight for 35 min, exhibiting negligible photolytic degradation (Fig. 6c). The duration of sunlight exposure was 35 min but there was no significant change in degradation of DC after 30 min therefore 30 min time was considered as the optimized time for further studies. After 30 min. of continuous light exposure, 0.2NSCN achieved a DC removal efficiency of 98.3%, significantly exhibiting higher degradation to that of 0.05NSCN (77.3%), and pristine CN (41.1%) (Fig. 6a). Notably, 0.4NSCN exhibited a reduced removal efficiency of 91.9%, likely attributed to structural aggregation effects (Figure S3) which was also evident by zeta potential. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis gives the average particle size (hydrodynamic diameter), and electrophoretic mobility measurements give the zeta potential value of the synthesized sample, whose spectra are shown in Figure S3 (c-f). Zeta potential serves as a key parameter for evaluating the electrostatic interactions of catalyst particles when dispersed in a solvent (water). The values of zeta for 0.2NSCN and 0.4NSCN were found to be −25.2 and − 21.8 mV, respectively (Figure S3(a). A Lower value of zeta corresponds to a less stable suspension and aggregation of particles. As in the case of 0.4NSCN, the value of zeta potential is less, so the particles have the tendency to get aggregate in comparison to 0.2NSCN and exhibited less degradation efficiency59. Figure S3(b) depicts the diameter distribution diagram of the 0.4NSCN and 0.2NSCN, whose mean diameter was observed to be 1043 nm and 417 nm, respectively. This increase in the diameter of the 0.4NSCN may be due to the adhesive nature of solvent molecules of water or agglomeration of the particle60. Also, the comparative table S1 depicts the effectiveness of this catalyst over other reported literature.

Further, the influence of the mass of the catalyst was analyzed by varying the mass. Figure 6b indicates that the DC mitigation rate is elevated with a higher quantity of catalyst. Beyond 10 mg, additional catalyst does not enhance degradation, likely due to light scattering/shielding effects32. Notably, even at a low catalyst amount of 5 mg, a DC mitigation rate of 65.3% is achieved, underscoring the exceptional photocatalytic activity of the catalyst.

The photocatalytic behaviour is strongly affected by the concentration of the target pollutant. To evaluate the influence of varying initial concentrations on photocatalytic efficiency, a systematic study was conducted using a series of concentrations: 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mg/L of DC. Figure 6c shows the degradation efficiency of 0.2NSCN toward DC declines from 98.3% to 51.3% as the initial DC concentration increases from 20 to 50 mg/L. This reduction in photocatalytic efficiency can be partially ascribed to diminished light penetration at higher DC concentrations. Elevated DC levels attenuate the transmission of light through the solution, thereby restricting photon absorption by the 0.2NSCN and affecting its photocatalytic activity32. Additionally, the accumulation of intermediate species formed during degradation at higher concentrations exhibits competitive behaviour with molecules of DC for the active sites of catalyst 0.2NSCN, further hindering the overall degradation process. The rate constant (k) for mitigation of DC decreased from 0.123 min-1 to 0.020 min-1 as the concentration of DC increased from 10 to 50 mg/L (Fig. 6d). Figure 6e depicts the UV graph of degradation of DC by 0.2NSCN at regular time intervals (initial to 35 min).

Radical trapping experiments and mechanism of degradation

For identifying the key radical species responsible for DC mitigation by the catalyst 0.2NSCN, selective trapping experiments were conducted using radical quenchers. Isopropanol (IPA) was employed to trap hydroxyl radicals (OH•), Na₂EDTA was used to capture photogenerated holes (h⁺), BNQ was utilized to neutralize superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻), and NaN3 was used to trap singlet oxygen. Figure 8a illustrates that the degradation of DC decreases by 58.2% and 48.8% upon the addition of BNQ and Na₂EDTA, respectively. In contrast, IPA exhibits less effect on the degradation of DC. While NaN3 showed a negligible effect on degradation. These results confirm that photogenerated holes (h⁺) and O₂•⁻ play a crucial role in the photocatalytic degradation of DC. The electrochemical data further substantiates the findings from the scavenging experiments, demonstrating that the conduction band potential of 0.2NSCN (ECB = − 0.99 eV vs. NHE) surpasses the threshold necessary for O2/O₂•⁻ generation (− 0.046 eV vs. NHE). As a result, the electrons present in the conduction band can effectively facilitate the formation of O₂•⁻ radicals, which subsequently interact with DC, promoting their transformation into degradation products. Based on these findings from the experiments, a plausible mechanism of photodegradation is illustrated in Fig. 8b. Moreover, the generation of O₂•⁻ by 0.2NSCN under sunlight irradiation was further validated through the nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) assay (Fig. 8c). A marked decline in absorbance at 259 nm was observed within 30 min of sunlight exposure in the presence of 0.2NSCN, indicating the effective formation of superoxide radicals.

Analysis of the mitigation of DC by NMR

Furthermore, NMR analysis of the DC was performed in D₂O to examine its degradation. The control DC exhibited peaks representative of protons within the aromatic region (δ 6.6–7.5 ppm) as well as in the aliphatic (δ 2.0 − 4.5 ppm) region (orange line). Following degradation, the characteristic peaks of DC disappeared (red line), with only minor peaks observed in the aliphatic region (Fig. 9), indicating effective breakdown of the DC. These spectral modifications signify the disruption of the DC molecular structure. Hence, the results confirm that the DC undergoes substantial degradation in the presence of 0.2NSCN under solar irradiation.

Also, from literature, a plausible degradation pathway may be described in Fig. 10, which shows that DC may be degraded by following any of the pathways61,62. In pathway 1, DC (m/z = 445) undergoes ring opening to form ‘a’ (m/z = 196), which then undergoes demethylation, yielding ‘b’ (m/z = 169). Subsequent decarboxylation and hydroxylation steps lead to the formation of ‘c’ (m/z = 124) and d (m/z = 108). In pathway 2, initial demethylation yields ‘e’ (m/z = 417). Subsequent deamidation and hydroxylation of ‘e’, accompanied by ring opening, generate ‘f’ (m/z = 333). Further C–C bond cleavage of ‘f’ results in the formation of ‘g’ (m/z = 278). In pathway 3, DC first undergoes demethylation and ring opening to yield ‘h’ (m/z = 246). Subsequent deamination of ‘h’ produces ‘i’ (m/z = 233), which, upon further ring opening, generates ‘j’ (m/z = 190). In pathway 4, DC is subjected to hydroxylation and demethylation to form ‘k’ (m/z = 432), followed by the generation of ‘l’ (m/z = 276). ‘l’ then undergoes carbon–carbon bond cleavage and hydroxylation to yield ‘m’ (m/z = 180), with an additional ring-opening step producing ‘n’ (m/z = 156).

Recyclability study

Recycling experiments were conducted to evaluate the stability of 0.2NSCN (Fig. 11a). The methodology closely followed that of the photocatalytic activity tests, with a slight modification in the final step. Specifically, after the complete degradation of the pollutant, the remaining catalyst was reused to degrade a fresh DC sample, identical to the one used in the initial step. After completion of each cycle, the catalyst was recovered by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 15 min, washed thoroughly with deionized water (10 ml ×3) and then with ethanol (10 ml ×3) to remove any residual intermediates, and then dried at 75 °C for 8 h. The dried sample was directly reused in the next run without any additional treatment. The recovered mass was monitored, and only a negligible loss was observed, which may be attributed to handling during recovery and washing. This process was repeated for a minimum of seven cycles. The catalyst retained up to 87% of its activity towards the degradation of DC even after seven consecutive cycles. Moreover, the XRD and FTIR patterns of the used photocatalyst (Fig. 11 (b, c)) closely resemble those of the fresh photocatalyst. No additional peaks corresponding to bending or stretching vibrations were observed in the FTIR spectrum, nor were any new phases detected in the XRD pattern. The FTIR shows slight broadness with a decrease in intensity of peaks at 1612 cm−1, 1250 cm−1 corresponding to aromatic C–N heterocycles and triazine units at 804 cm−1 after degradation of DC. These results confirm that the 0.2NSCN photocatalyst remains stable under the given reaction conditions.

Conclusion

This study focuses on the reuse of pharmaceutical waste to synthesize 0.2NSCN, a cost-effective and sustainable photocatalyst effective under natural sunlight, which effectively targets and degrades the emerging antibiotic pollutant DC. The synthesized photocatalyst demonstrated high efficiency, achieving 98.3% degradation of DC under optimized conditions, including a DC concentration of 20 mg/L, a catalyst dosage of 10 mg, and a time of 30 min. The catalyst’s activity was further assessed using RSM, which confirmed a strong correlation between experimental and predicted parameters. XPS and FESEM analysis revealed the presence of sulphur content with the incorporation of expired drug compared to bulk CN, resulting in 0.2NSCN exhibiting a narrower band gap, thereby enhancing its photocatalytic degradation efficiency for DC. Additionally, the catalyst remains highly effective even after seven reuse cycles, showing no significant decline in activity. The degradation pathway was validated through NMR and scavenging studies. In summary, the outstanding reusability, strong photocatalytic performance, stability, cost-effectiveness, and ability to recycle pharmaceutical waste highlight 0.2NSCN as a promising catalyst for treating DC contaminated water.
