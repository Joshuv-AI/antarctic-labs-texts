Clinical value of tumor mutational burden for neoadjuvant and adjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitor in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Julia C F Quintanilha, Gerald Li, Ryon P Graf, Amaya Gasco, Jerry Mitchell, Richard SP Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor mutational burden (TMB) and PD-L1 are established biomarkers for guiding immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). As ICI use expands into early-stage disease, we explored the feasibility of using TMB, which can be determined via a comprehensive genomic profiling assay along with EGFR and ALK genomic alterations, as a biomarker for outcomes in both neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings. TMB-high status (≥10 mut/Mb) showed a numerically higher, but not statistically significant, rate of pathological complete response among patients receiving neoadjuvant ICI and significantly associated with more favorable time to recurrence in patients receiving adjuvant ICI, particularly among patients with PD-L1 expression <50%. TMB should be considered in future early-stage NSCLC ICI clinical trials to further validate these results.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Biomarker, Lung Cancer, Tumor mutation burden - TMB, Adjuvant

Introduction

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and PD-L1 immunohistochemistry are companion diagnostics used to guide immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy use in the advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) setting.1 As ICI use is expanding into the early-stage setting, biomarkers for stratifying responders and non-responders to ICI are needed. As EGFR and ALK genomic alterations (GA) are now standard biomarkers for early-stage NSCLC, the ability to multiplex ICI biomarkers in this setting is becoming necessary for tissue stewardship.2 3

Since TMB can be determined via a comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) assay along with EGFR and ALK GA, we sought to explore the feasibility of using TMB as an ICI biomarker in early-stage NSCLC, specifically for identification of patients with higher chances of having a pathologic complete response (pCR) in the neoadjuvant setting and for identification of durable responders to ICI in the adjuvant setting.

Methods

Study population

This study included patients with early-stage (stage I-III) NSCLC, who underwent tissue genomic testing using Foundation Medicine CGP assays and were included in the US-based deidentified Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine NSCLC Clinico-Genomic Database (FH-FMI CGDB). Clinical data from the Flatiron Health Research Database are linked to genomic data, derived from FMI’s CGP tests (FoundationOne/FoundationOneCDx), in the FH-FMI CGDB by deterministic matching, providing a deidentified dataset.4,7

To ensure that the FMI CGP test was performed during the early-stage disease course, the following criteria were applied: (1) the specimen was collected more than 30 days prior to the diagnosis of advanced disease (if applicable) or, for patients not known to be advanced, no more than 60 days after the last recorded visit (to exclude specimens that may have been collected from patients who experienced an unobserved progression after the end of the electronic health record); (2) the specimen was obtained within 90 days of the initial diagnosis; (3) the test report was issued more than 30 days prior to the diagnosis of advanced disease (if applicable) or, for patients not known to be advanced, no more than 60 days after the last recorded visit; and (4) the report was generated within 180 days of the initial diagnosis.

The study population was divided into two different cohorts for further analysis. The first cohort included patients receiving ICI as neoadjuvant therapy, and the second cohort included patients receiving ICI as adjuvant therapy following adjuvant chemotherapy.

Comprehensive genomic profiling

Hybrid capture-based NGS assays (FoundationOne or FoundationOneCDx) were performed on patient tumor specimens in Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified, College of American Pathologists-accredited laboratory (Foundation Medicine). Samples were evaluated for GAs as previously described.6 TMB was determined on up to 1.1 Mb of sequenced DNA and the minimum tumor purity required to call TMB was 20%, with the clinical and analytic validation previously described.6

Statistical analysis

χ2 tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to assess differences between groups of categorical and continuous variables, respectively. pCR rates were compared between TMB-high (TMB-H, ≥10 mut/Mb) and TMB-low (TMB-L, <10 mut/Mb) among patients receiving neoadjuvant ICI using the Fisher’s exact test. In patients receiving adjuvant ICI following chemotherapy, time to recurrence (TTR) was calculated from the start of the ICI therapy to first local or distant recurrence. For patients without a record of becoming advanced, deaths were censored at last structured activity. TTR was landmarked at 12 months after ICI treatment initiation and compared TMB-H and TMB-L groups among patients receiving adjuvant ICI using Cox models. 12 months was chosen based on the typical time for which patients receive adjuvant ICI in clinical practice.8 Model fit was compared using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) to evaluate whether inclusion of TMB10 provided additional explanatory power beyond PD-L1 for TTR. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (c-statistic), calculated for models including TMB10 alone, PD-L1 alone, and the combined TMB10+PD-L1 model. R V.4.4.2 software was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

A total of 250 patients were included in the study: 38 received neoadjuvant and 212 adjuvant ICI (figure 1). Across the full study cohort, four patients (1.6%) had an EGFR mutation while three patients (1.2%) had an ALK rearrangement. Among patients who received neoadjuvant ICI, 28.9% had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) 1–2, 86.8% had history of smoking, and 2.6% (n=1), 36.8% (n=14), and 60.5% (n=23) were stages I, II, and III at diagnosis, respectively. Among patients who received adjuvant ICI, 51% had ECOG PS 1–3, 94% had history of smoking, and 2.8% (n=6), 24.1% (n=51), and 73.1% (n=155) were stages I, II, and III at diagnosis, respectively (table 1).

TTR among patients receiving adjuvant ICI by TMB10 status

Among patients with early-stage NSCLC who received adjuvant ICI following adjuvant chemotherapy, those with TMB-H had more favorable TTR (median not reached for both groups, HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.82, p=0.008, figure 2B). Because the median was not reached, we also report the 75th percentile: 30.9 vs 10.2 months. A sensitivity analysis excluding the 12-month landmark was performed (online supplemental figure S1), and no difference in TTR between TMB-H and TMB-L was observed in the first 12 months, but a difference between the cohorts became apparent after 12 months. What this exemplifies is that while TMB was not able to determine initial response to ICI, it was able to discern who are long-term responders. This may also imply the need for longer ICI treatment in TMB-L patients. As shown in online supplemental table S1, the distribution of TMB10 was similar between patients included in the study and those excluded based on the 12-month landmark.

To demonstrate the added value of TMB beyond PD-L1, we evaluated differences in TTR by TMB status among patients with PD-L1 <50%. When restricting to only patients with PD-L1 <50% (excluding those with unknown PD-L1 status), the difference was more pronounced, with patients with TMB-H (n=43) vs TMB-L (n=61) having even more favorable TTR (median not reached vs 35.7 months, 75% percentile not reached vs 6.9 months, HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.82, p=0.012, figure 2C).

Among patients with both TMB10 and PD-L1 data available (n=167), addition of TMB10 to a model including PD-L1 significantly improved model fit for TTR (LRT p=0.003), whereas addition of PD-L1 to a model including TMB10 did not result in a significant improvement in model fit (LRT p=0.303). Model discrimination was higher for the TMB10-only model (c-statistic 0.63, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.67) than for the PD-L1-only model (c-statistic 0.56, 95% CI 0.51 to 0.60), and was further improved in the combined TMB10+PD-L1 model (c-statistic 0.66; 95% CI 0.62 to 0.71).

We also evaluated TMB20 (defining TMB-H as TMB≥20 mut/Mb and TMB-L as TMB<20 mut/Mb) as an exploratory analysis, given prior evidence that TMB20 may identify patients with durable benefit from ICI in advanced disease.9 A similar trend was observed in our cohort, although it did not reach statistical significance. (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.35 to 1.94, p=0.64, online supplemental figure S2).

Discussion

In this study, we explored the feasibility of using TMB for neoadjuvant and adjuvant ICI decision making. First, we discovered that TMB could stratify patients with durable responses from adjuvant ICI use at a TMB cut-off of 10 muts/Mb in both the PD-L1 agnostic and also the PD-L1<50 cohort, similar to what we observed in advanced NSCLC.9 Although no TMB cut-off has been clinically validated for early-stage NSCLC, we selected 10 muts/Mb because it aligns with the threshold used in the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved assay. To the best of our knowledge, the current literature is limited in clinical studies examining TMB in relation to ICI in early-stage NSCLC using the 10 muts/Mb cut-off of the FDA-approved assay. The IMpower010 study conducted an exploratory analysis of disease-free survival by TMB and PD-L1 expression in resected stage II–IIIA NSCLC (n=549), defining TMB as above or below the median based on whole exome sequencing. One conclusion from the analysis was that TMB-H experienced better outcomes than those with TMB-L when receiving atezolizumab.10 This finding is consistent with our study demonstrating a significant association between TMB ≥10 mut/Mb and improved TTR in patients receiving adjuvant ICI, suggesting that TMB may serve as a biomarker in this setting. Despite the differences between the Impower010 and this real-world cohort (TMB definition; and baseline characteristics such as higher ECOG status, greater smoking prevalence, and more stage III disease in our real-world population, (online supplemental table S2), the consistency of the results reinforces the validity of this real-world finding. Currently, about 25% of NSCLC patients receive ICI in the adjuvant setting, and the adoption of ICI in this setting is increasing.11 Understanding the likelihood of durable response with TMB could be a datapoint for treating oncologists to determine whether they should continue their patients on ICI, especially in patients experiencing toxicity to the ICI.

In addition, an exploratory signal was observed for TMB as a potential biomarker for neoadjuvant ICI, though this was not statistically significant. Due to the small number of patients in the CGDB fitting our study criteria, this analysis is underpowered and no definitive conclusion could be made. To the best of our knowledge, similar to TMB in the adjuvant setting, the literature is sparse on TMB as a biomarker for neoadjuvant therapy in early-stage NSCLC, though the results in those few studies are largely consistent with our observations in this study.12

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and the small sample size of the neoadjuvant cohort. Follow-up in this cohort was also relatively limited for the TTR endpoint, as evidenced by neither TMB arm in the primary analysis reaching median TTR, limiting our ability to detect longer-term trends. Additionally, the requirement for patients to have undergone CGP to be included in the CGDB may limit the generalizability of the dataset to the broader early-stage NSCLC population, especially because CGP is not currently standard of care in early-stage NSCLC. When compared descriptively with a published early-stage NSCLC Flatiron Health cohort (online supplemental table S3), our study population demonstrated a higher prevalence of stage III disease and high PD-L1 expression, supporting the notion that early-stage CGP is selectively applied in real-world practice to patients perceived to have higher risk or greater likelihood of benefiting from ICI.13

In sum, strong consideration should be given to incorporating CGP into routine testing for early-stage NSCLC patients given that EGFR and ALK are already companion diagnostics utilizing CGP, multiple other biomarkers such as KRAS and RET are being investigated in early-stage NSCLC clinical trials,14,16 and now we have evidence on the utility of a genome-wide biomarker such as TMB in this patient population. Further early-stage NSCLC ICI clinical trials should include TMB to further validate these results.
