Exploring the impact of melatonin on cancer-related fatigue: a dose-response meta-analysis with GRADE evidence evaluation
Authors: Sajjad Etesamnia, Mahdiyar Nasiraie-Moghadam, Pariya Mostafazadeh, Mohammad-Reza Jowshan, Kamran Roudini, Seyed Rohollah Miri, Alireza Jahan-Mihan, Alireza Haghighi, Soraiya Ebrahimpour-Koujan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer-related fatigue (CRF) is a prevalent and debilitating symptom among patients undergoing cancer treatment, yet its management remains a clinical challenge. Previous studies evaluating the role of melatonin in CRF have yielded inconsistent findings. This meta-analysis aimed to assess the overall effect of melatonin supplementation on fatigue and to explore potential dose–response relationships.MethodsA systematic literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to 24 August 2025. Randomized controlled trials comparing melatonin with a placebo in adult cancer patients were included. SMDs with 95% CIs were calculated using a random-effects model. Nonlinear and linear dose–response analyses were conducted, and certainty of evidence was evaluated with the GRADE approach.ResultsTwelve trials (18 effect sizes, n = 1,977) met the inclusion criteria. Pooled analysis showed a significant reduction in fatigue scores with melatonin supplementation (SMD: −0.45; 95% CI: −0.69, − 0.20; P < 0.001). Dose–response analysis revealed a significant inverse association for melatonin doses between 5 and 15 mg/day (P-nonlinearity = 0.03). Short-term interventions (≤ 4 weeks) demonstrated greater benefit, whereas longer durations showed no consistent effects. However, substantial heterogeneity (I² = 78%) and very low certainty of evidence limit the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsMelatonin supplementation may reduce fatigue in cancer patients, particularly at doses of 5–15 mg/day and with short-term use. Nonetheless, the certainty of evidence remains very low, highlighting the need for high-quality, large-scale RCTs with standardized methodologies to validate these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16236-3.

Background

Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is one of the most prevalent and debilitating symptoms experienced by patients undergoing cancer treatment, affecting up to 80% of those receiving chemotherapy [1, 2]. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), CRF is defined as a distressing, persistent, subjective sense of physical, emotional, and/or cognitive tiredness or exhaustion related to cancer or cancer treatment that is not proportional to recent activity and interferes with usual functioning [1]. Unlike ordinary fatigue, CRF is more severe, more distressing, and less likely to be relieved by rest, and may persist long after treatment completion.

CRF symptoms include physical fatigue, stress, attention deficit, mental fatigue, nausea, short-term memory loss, physical pain, and lack of concentration [3]. Risk factors for increased fatigue include elevated levels of interleukin-6 soluble receptor and heightened fatigue before therapy [4]. The pathophysiology of CRF is multifactorial, with proposed mechanisms including dysregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction, circadian rhythm disruption, and serotonin dysregulation [5].

Among available interventions, exercise has demonstrated the most consistent evidence for alleviating CRF, with combined aerobic and resistance training showing the highest efficacy in network meta-analyses [6]. Nevertheless, pharmacological approaches remain relevant, particularly for patients with advanced disease or limited functional capacity in whom exercise is not feasible, and the search for effective adjunct therapies continues.

Melatonin (MLN) is a neuroendocrine hormone primarily secreted by the pineal gland in a circadian rhythm. Beyond its classical role as a circadian regulator, melatonin exerts antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects, and is synthesized in various peripheral tissues, including bone marrow, lymphocytes, skin, retina, and the gastrointestinal tract [7–9]. Given that circadian rhythm disruption is a key proposed mechanism of CRF, and that cancer patients generally exhibit lower melatonin levels compared to healthy individuals, melatonin represents a biologically plausible candidate for CRF management. At physiological concentrations [10–13], melatonin supports homeostatic metabolic rhythms and may confer protection against various illnesses [14, 15].

Cancer patients generally have lower levels of melatonin compared to the healthy population, and it has been shown that melatonin supplementation helps patients to overcome sleep disorders and improves their quality of life, besides its potential anti-tumoral effects [10–13]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of melatonin therapy have reported improvement in tumor remission, 1-year survival, and decreased radiotherapy (RT) and chemotherapy-related side effects [16–18]. Only two recent meta-analyses are available. The first, pooled data from 5 trials of patients receiving melatonin during concurrent chemo-radiation, did not observe a significant improvement in fatigue scores among cancer patients following the use of melatonin supplements [19]. That meta-analysis was limited by missing data from primary studies and the absence of dose–response analyses [20–22]. The second meta-analysis reported similar limitations to those of the previous study [23].

Given these limitations and the considerable inconsistency across primary studies evaluating the effects of melatonin on cancer-related fatigue, we conducted a dose–response meta-analysis, applying the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, to provide a more comprehensive and reliable synthesis of the available evidence.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [24]. The PROSPERO database has the study’s protocol registered under the number (CRD42024570716).

Search strategy

We performed a comprehensive literature search in the online databases of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (Core Collection ISI), and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), up to 10 January 2026. The search strategy was developed using MeSH terms and free-text keywords across four domains: intervention (melatonin and its synonyms), outcome (fatigue and related terms), population (cancer and related terms), and study design (RCT-related terms), combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR). The strategy was adapted for each database according to its specific syntax. The following keywords were used in the search strategy: (melatonin OR “Melatonin supplementation” OR “n acetyl 5 methoxytryptamine” OR “melatonin agonist” OR “melatonin receptor agonist”) AND (Fatigue OR tiredness OR exhaustion OR lethargy OR “Cancer Fatigue” OR “chronic Fatigue Syndrome”) AND (neoplasm OR cancer OR malignancy OR carcinoma OR tumor OR Neoplasia) (supplementary Table 1).

Moreover, searches were performed on Google Scholar, where the top 30-page results were retrieved, as well as in clinical trials registries, to ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant studies. Relevant articles were also hand-searched through the reference lists of identified studies to prevent missing any publications. No limitation was made on the time of publication or language.

Inclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria were defined based on the PICO framework as follows: We included eligible studies that met the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled clinical trials; (2) studies on adult subjects (≥ 18 years) with cancer diagnosed according to National Cancer Institute codes, regardless of cancer type, cancer stage (early, middle, or advanced), and current treatment (such as radiation therapy, chemotherapy, surgery, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, a combination of any of the above treatments, or without any treatment; (Population) (3 RCTs with at least one week’s duration of intervention (to ensure sufficient exposure to melatonin, as its effects on circadian rhythm and fatigue outcomes typically require repeated administration [25]; (Intervention: melatonin supplementation vs. placebo) and (4) controlled trials that reported mean changes and their standard deviations (SDs) of fatigue score throughout the trial for both intervention and control groups or presented required information for calculation of those effect sizes (Outcome).

Exclusion criteria

In the current meta-analysis, we excluded trials with incomplete missing outcome data and those with a cohort, cross-sectional, and case-control design, review articles, and ecological studies. We also excluded trials without a placebo or control group and those that were not randomized and/or were performed on children or adolescents.

Study selection

Every citation and abstract study that was searched was imported into the EndNote software for screening. Duplicate citations were eliminated afterward, and the remaining studies were then imported into EndNote software for additional assessment. Unpublished studies were excluded. Two researchers independently evaluated the studies against the eligibility criteria. Any conflicts during the assessment were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers (S E and M N-M). The principal investigator was responsible for resolving any disagreements between reviewers (S E-K).

Data extraction

Data extraction from each study was performed by one of two investigators (S.E.), with subsequent review for mistakes and inconsistencies by the other investigator (P M). Quantitative data regarding effect-size measures such as mean and standard deviations (SDs) or mean and standard errors (SEs) or median and interquartile range (IQR) of fatigue scores before and after intervention in each intervention and control group, and the mean ± SD fatigue scores at both baseline and the end of the trial were extracted for each group. In addition, information on the first author’s name, year of publication, country, study design, sex, duration of intervention, participant information (sample size, mean age), time of melatonin intake, dose (mg/day), study population (type of cancer), mean BMI, adjustment (yes/no), and outcome measurement was acquired. When a single trial included more than one usable comparator arm, the meta-analysis regarded each arm as an independent effect size [26–29]. The sample size was extracted based on the actual number of subjects used in the final analysis. Clinical trials that included a second intervention group were regarded as two distinct investigations [30]. In studies by Sedighi Pashaki A [22], where the values were presented as 2-dimensional bar plot figures, we obtained fatigue scores using an online Web Plot Digitizer version 4.5 (Ankit Rohatgi). Web Plot Digitizer is a web-based tool designed to extract information from a range of plots, such as XY coordinates from time-series data. The validity and reliability of this tool for extracting graphed data have already been established [31].

Risk of bias assessment

Two investigators (S E and P M) independently evaluated the quality of each study using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (ROB), version 2.0, for interventional studies [32]. Conflicts were settled by agreement between the two investigators and, if required, by consulting a third investigator (S E-K). The RoB 2 tool assesses risk of bias in five domains: randomization process (D1), deviations from intended interventions (D2), missing outcome data (D3), outcome measurement (D4), and selective reporting of results (D5) [32]. Each domain was evaluated and categorized as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias, according to responses to the signaling questions provided by the RoB 2 tool. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined by the highest level of risk identified in any domain, with studies considered high risk if any domain was rated high, and classified as having some concerns if any domain indicated uncertainty.

Grading quality of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [33]. The certainty of evidence was downgraded based on RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other relevant factors. Potential publication bias was assessed through funnel plots and Egger’s regression test [34]. The overall quality of evidence (QoE) was rated as very low, low, moderate, or high, and summary of findings tables were created using GRADEpro GDT software (2021 version) [35].

Statistical analysis

We obtained the overall effect sizes using the mean changes and their standard deviations of fatigue scores in the melatonin and control groups. Additionally, we transformed standard errors (SEs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and interquartile ranges (IQRs) to SDs using the method of Hozo et al. [36]. If a study reported medians and IQRs, we used a method for non-normal data to convert them to the mean and SD using a suitable formula (SD = IQR/1.35) [37]. The findings were displayed as a bias-corrected standard mean difference (SMD) and 95% confidence interval (CI) to correctly indicate the impact of melatonin supplementation on fatigue score in cancer patients. We calculated the mean difference between the post-intervention assessments and the pre-intervention values for each group to compare the fatigue scores of the intervention and control groups. The formula “[(SD1) 2 + (SD2) 2] − [2 × r × SD1 × SD2]” was used to compute the changes in standard deviation (SD) of fatigue score in both intervention and control groups, assuming a correlation coefficient (r) of 0.7 [38]. To determine the possibility of differences between studies in the analysis, we used a random effects model based on the Der Simonian and Laird approach to calculate the overall effect size [39]. We employed I2 and Cochran’s Q-test to evaluate the degree of heterogeneity in a study. Specifically, if the I2 is greater than 50% or the p-value of Cochran’s Q test is less than 0.05, then we consider the study to have a high degree of heterogeneity [40].

Subgroup analysis was performed to provide a more precise description of the data and to identify potential sources of heterogeneity, if significant. To obtain more comprehensive insights into the data, subgroup analysis was carried out by considering the country (developed vs. developing), dosage of melatonin (< 18 vs. ≥ 18 mg/day), duration of the intervention (> 4 vs. ≤ 4 weeks), outcome measurement (FACIT-F vs. other questionnaires), general risk of bias (high, some concerns, low), and adjustment for baseline levels of the outcome variable (adjusted vs. non-adjusted). To assess the impact of each study on the overall study outcomes, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. To identify publication bias, we used a funnel plot and employed statistical analyses with the Egger regression asymmetry and Begg’s rank correlation test. To determine the non-linear effects of melatonin dosage (mg/d) and intervention duration (weeks) on fatigue scores in cancer patients, fractional polynomial modeling was applied. In this investigation, we considered statistical significance present when P-values were less than 0.05. For data extraction, we used Microsoft Excel and employed Stata/MP version 17.0 software for analysis.

Results

Literature search

The initial search yielded 7,055 publications through PubMed (n = 101), Scopus (n = 6,741), and Web of Science (n = 213). Additionally, Google Scholar was searched (top 30 pages of results), through which three additional records were identified and included in the screening process. After excluding 619 duplicates, a total of 6,439 articles were obtained for title and abstract screening. After screening the titles and abstracts, 6424 articles were excluded, and 18 primary studies were recovered for full-text evaluation. Two articles were excluded as they are not RCTs [41, 42], while three did not report fatigue scores [11, 43, 44]. Additionally, one article was excluded as the participants were not cancer patients [45]. Therefore, 12 papers were included in the qualitative synthesis. At last, the 12 included studies provided 18 independent effect sizes for quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), as several studies reported outcomes from multiple subgroups, intervention durations, or co-interventions. Study characteristics

The characteristics of 12 clinical trials with 18 effect sizes (EF) in our current systematic review are outlined in Table 1. All articles were published between 2013 and 2024 and were carried out in developed countries (7 articles) [20, 26, 28–30, 46, 47] and developing countries (5 articles) [21, 22, 27, 46, 48]. All papers were conducted using a parallel design. These studies were predominantly performed on female subjects with breast cancer [20–22, 26, 27, 29, 30, 48, 49], and others were executed on males with lung cancer [28, 47] and both genders with advanced cancers [46]. In total, 1977 participants (999 intervention and 978 placebo) were included, with an age of 50–67 years. Intervention durations ranged from 1 to 144 weeks. In the included studies, researchers administered a daily dose of melatonin that ranged from 1 to 20 mg. All studies administered melatonin for the intervention group and a placebo for the control group, except [30], where the intervention group was different, and we considered it as an independent study. Almost all studies gave the melatonin at night, except one that compared the dosage given both in the morning and at night [28]. Outcome measurement of the investigations was conducted on the FACIT-F questionnaire [20, 26, 30, 49], VAS and KSS questionnaire [29], MFI-20 questionnaire [46, 47], BFI questionnaire [21, 22, 48], FA subscale questionnaire [27], and the QLQ-C30 questionnaire [28]. The majority of these studies had no adjustment, whereas some performed adjustments.

GRADE assessment

The certainty of evidence was rated very low for fatigue scores in patients with cancers, downgraded mainly due to risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision. Overall, melatonin supplementation shows some potential for alleviating fatigue, but the evidence remains inconsistent, and the effects on fatigue scores are still unclear (Table 2, Supplemental Table 2).

Findings from meta-analysis

Following an analysis of the results from 12 trials with 18 effect sizes, the pooled SMD from the random effect model showed that there was a significant change in fatigue score that was identified after melatonin supplementation in cancer patients (SMD: −0.45; 95% CI: −0.69, − 0.2, P < 0.001; tau2 = 0.18, I2 = 78%) (Fig. 3). To determine the source of heterogeneity, we performed a subgroup analysis. In order to perform subgroup analyses, the data were stratified based on the country, the daily dosage administered, the length of the intervention, outcome measurement, general risk of bias, and adjustment for baseline levels of the outcome variable (Supplemental Table 3). In developing countries, with a dosage of < 20 mg/day, an intervention period of ≤ 4 weeks, assessment with the FACIT-F questionnaire, high risk of bias, and non-adjusted analyses, we observed a significant reduction in fatigue scores. Additionally, the subgroup for risk of bias could be a factor in the observed heterogeneity. The results did not alter significantly when studies were eliminated one at a time, according to the sensitivity analysis. The funnel plot was first examined to look for publication bias, and it reveals an asymmetry. Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s regression asymmetry were employed to look at this further. Begg’s rank correlation test (p-value = 0.76), as well as Egger’s regression asymmetry (p-value = 0.2) (Supplementary Figs. 1,2), demonstrated that publication bias was not statistically significant. According to our dose-response analysis, a significant reversed association was observed between 5 and 15 mg/day of melatonin supplementation on fatigue scores (P-nonlinearity = 0.03) (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the nonlinear dose–response analysis indicated a statistically significant inverse association between melatonin administration and fatigue score changes during short-term interventions (≤ 4 weeks) (P-nonlinearity = 0.43) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first dose–response meta-analysis to evaluate the effect of melatonin supplementation on CRF with GRADE evidence assessment. Despite substantial heterogeneity among included studies, melatonin supplementation was associated with a statistically significant reduction in fatigue scores among cancer patients. Subgroup analyses demonstrated significant reductions in fatigue in studies conducted in developing countries, with doses of less than 20 mg/day, intervention durations of ≤ 4 weeks, studies using the FACIT-F questionnaire, and non-adjusted analyses. Dose–response analysis revealed a significant inverse association at doses of 5–15 mg/day. However, no significant effect was observed in studies with low risk of bias or adjusted analyses, and the certainty of evidence was rated as very low according to the GRADE assessment, indicating that the observed effect does not reach the threshold for clinical significance.

Melatonin is safe and commonly used to reset the circadian timing system in subjects with circadian disturbance [50]. Melatonin is secreted by the pineal gland in a circadian rhythm, with high levels at night peaking early in the morning and low levels during the day. Exposure to light inhibits pineal activity and melatonin synthesis via neural input from the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). The normal daily cycle of melatonin secretion is altered in cancer patients, typically with a muted difference between daytime and nighttime values [51, 52]. Cancer patients often experience sleep disturbances due to chemotherapy, significantly impacting their fatigue levels. Factors such as pain, anxiety, and the side effects of treatment contribute to insomnia and other sleep-related issues, affecting approximately 30% to 50% of these individuals [53]. There is still incomplete mechanistic evidence supporting melatonin’s ability to lessen cancer patients’ fatigue. Also, the dosage and duration of use for CRF are not well established. Ongoing research suggests that melatonin supplementation can help patients fall asleep faster and achieve deeper sleep, thereby reducing fatigue associated with cancer treatments [26].

According to our updated meta-analysis, melatonin administration significantly lowers cancer patients’ fatigue scores overall. However, a previous meta-analysis failed to find significantly improved cancer patients’ fatigue scores after taking melatonin supplements [19]. In the prior meta-analysis, one of the studies was excluded from the analysis, leading to missing data, while several additional articles published between 2022 and 2024 were incorporated into our review. Although a new meta-analysis was recently published [23], the study lacked both dose-response analysis and the application of the GRADE framework for quality assessment.

Results from the Innominato et al. study showed that taking 5 mg of melatonin before bed for two months improved fatigue intensity, sleep quality, global quality of life, and cognitive and social function scales in patients with metastatic breast cancer. However, there was no significant impact of constipation, lack of appetite, shortness of breath, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, or role acting on emotional performance [11]. According to a study by Palmer et al., taking 20 mg of melatonin for 10 days before and during adjuvant chemotherapy resulted in neuroprotective effects, decreased adjuvant chemotherapy side effects, and enhanced mental function, depression, and sleep in breast cancer patients [12]. In contrast to earlier findings, Hansen et al. found that women with breast cancer aged 30 to 75 years, receiving either 6 mg of melatonin or a placebo for three months, had no therapeutic benefit on breast cancer patients’ cognitive function [29].

According to subgroup analyses, melatonin significantly reduced fatigue scores in studies conducted in developing countries, with dosages < 20 mg/d and intervention durations of ≤ 4 weeks. Due to significant heterogeneity, we identified articles that could be a source of heterogeneity. Additionally, we identified a non-significant U-shaped dose-response relationship between melatonin dosage and fatigue scores, with doses ranging from 5 to 15 mg/day showing the greatest reduction in fatigue scores, but this is not clinically significant. As cancer patients undergo treatment, the dosage of chemotherapy often increases over time, which can lead to diminished efficacy of the drugs. Initially, melatonin may play a significant role in enhancing the therapeutic effects of chemotherapy, particularly in the early stages when the body is more responsive to treatment. Melatonin has been shown to improve sleep quality and reduce fatigue associated with chemotherapy, thereby helping to maintain a better overall quality of life for patients. However, as treatment progresses and dosages escalate, the effectiveness of melatonin may gradually decline due to changes in the pharmacodynamics of both melatonin and the chemotherapeutic agents. Additionally, higher chemotherapy doses can result in increased toxicity and adverse effects, complicating patient management. Therefore, while melatonin may initially enhance chemotherapy effectiveness, its impact may diminish over time, highlighting the need for continuous evaluation of treatment regimens and further research into optimal dosing strategies for adjunct therapies. Furthermore, melatonin administration was significantly and inversely associated with changes in fatigue scores during short-term interventions lasting ≤ 4 weeks. One of the notable side effects of melatonin is daytime drowsiness, which can become particularly pronounced at higher doses, specifically those exceeding 20 mg per day. This increased drowsiness may exacerbate fatigue conditions in cancer patients, who often experience elevated levels of fatigue due to their illness and treatment regimens. Therefore, careful consideration of dosing and duration of supplementation is essential in managing cancer patients, as higher doses may not only fail to provide the intended benefits but could also worsen fatigue symptoms, affecting overall quality of life over time.

This systematic review and meta-analysis have several important limitations and strengths to consider. To our knowledge, this is the first dose-response meta-analysis evaluating the effects of melatonin supplementation on fatigue scores in cancer patients. Second, to provide the most comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis, we included all articles that met the inclusion criteria, regardless of language or publication date restrictions, which improves the generalizability of the results. Also, we had great internal validity and no publication bias, as all studies included were randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials. Furthermore, we conducted sensitivity analyses and non-linear dose and duration response analyses, which increased the power of our analysis. However, the current article has several limitations that must be recognized. First, there was considerable heterogeneity among the included studies. In the subgroup analysis, factors such as country, dosage, duration, outcome measurement, and general risk of bias could explain the variation between studies. Second, the included studies exhibited considerable heterogeneity in cancer type, treatment modality, timing of assessment, and disease stage, all of which are known to influence CRF severity and responsiveness to intervention. Subgroup analyses stratified by these variables were not feasible due to the limited number of trials within each potential subgroup. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted with caution, and future large-scale RCTs with more homogeneous populations are warranted. Genetic features may potentially affect the effectiveness of melatonin supplements, but we were unable to evaluate this possibility due to a lack of data from the study population. Third, in this present meta-analysis, various questionnaires were utilized to assess fatigue levels in cancer patients, reflecting the multidimensional nature of cancer-related fatigue. Given all of these restrictions, more primary clinical trials, including larger sample sizes and more advanced designs, are required to confirm these results. Additionally, several included studies had small sample sizes or pilot designs, which may have contributed to imprecision in the pooled estimates. Future studies should prespecify a minimum sample size threshold for inclusion.

Conclusion

In conclusion, melatonin supplementation was associated with a significant reduction in fatigue scores among cancer patients, particularly at doses of 5–15 mg/day and with short-term use of ≤ 4 weeks; however, the certainty of evidence remains very low according to the GRADE assessment, and the observed effect does not reach the threshold for clinical significance. From a clinical perspective, melatonin may be considered as a complementary approach for managing CRF, particularly in patients with advanced disease or limited functional capacity in whom exercise-based interventions are not feasible, though clinicians should interpret these findings with caution. Future large-scale RCTs with homogeneous cancer populations, standardized fatigue assessment tools, longer intervention durations, and a wider range of melatonin doses are warranted to establish optimal dosing strategies and confirm the clinical relevance of melatonin supplementation for CRF management.
