A neonatal extra-anatomical bypass for a “corkscrew” descending aorta associated with interrupted right aortic arch
Authors: Yasuyuki Kobayashi, Jill J Savla, Ankita Chauhan, Ammie M White, Jonathan M Chen
Published: 2026

Extra-anatomical bypass with an 8-mm PECA graft for the right interrupted aortic arch.

Central Message

A neonatal extra-anatomical bypass with a PECA graft is a valid option for an interrupted right aortic arch and corkscrew aorta with a long distance between the ascending and descending aorta.

A neonate with a prenatal diagnosis of rare congenital heart disease was delivered at our institution (birth weight 3.4 kg). Postnatal imaging confirmed the cardiac diagnosis of right aortic arch branching pattern, type B interrupted aortic arch, no dominant ductal arch, multiple ventricular septal defects, and mild right ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Prenatal genetic testing demonstrated a paternally inherited variant of uncertain significance in MED13L on whole-genome sequencing.

Computed tomography imaging demonstrated that the left common carotid artery was normal in caliber as the first branch of the right aortic arch, but the origin of the right brachiocephalic trunk was stenotic (Figure 1). A left patent ductus arteriosus had a tortuous course with complex cervical artery connections in the left lower neck/superior mediastinum, which gave rise to the isolated left subclavian artery and “corkscrew” descending aorta. At 5 days old, the patient was cooled to 18 °C intraoperatively using dual-arterial cannulation to the left common carotid artery and patent ductus arteriosus (Figure 2). For left common carotid artery cannulation, a narrow and shallow purse string suture was placed on it, and an 8-Fr Biomedicus arterial cannula (Medtronic) was inserted antegrade. Bilateral cerebral near-infrared spectroscopy was monitored to be above 70 during the cardiopulmonary bypass. Under circulatory arrest, the right descending thoracic aorta was opened inferior to the interrupted segment. Given the considerable distance between the ascending and descending aorta, the patient underwent an extra-anatomical bypass with an 8-mm PECA graft (PECA Labs), passing behind the superior vena cava (SVC) and through a newly created tunnel in the pericardium (below the phrenic nerve). Given the ventricular septal defects, internal pulmonary artery banding was performed using a 0.4-mm Gore-Tex patch (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc) with a 4-mm fenestration. Both postbypass epicardial imaging and predischarge transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated an unobstructed pathway from the ascending aorta to the descending aorta. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy demonstrated an unobstructed right bronchus, and she was discharged home on postoperative day 22. Institutional review board approval was not required, and written patient consent for inclusion in the study was waived.

Discussion

This study describes a case of type B interrupted right aortic arch with corkscrew descending aorta undergoing extra-anatomical aortic bypass in the neonatal period. A corkscrew descending aorta is a rare anatomic variant, characterized by an extremely tortuous aortic arch that can be associated with aortopathy.1 Its association with an interrupted right aortic arch is even more rare, with only one surgical case reported, to our knowledge, with a posterior direct connection.2 Given that the family history is notable for paternal asymptomatic status without symptoms of MED13L syndrome, and the patient did not have any physical examination findings consistent with this diagnosis, this corkscrew descending aorta was considered to occur in isolation.

Surgical options for arch repair in the present case include a conventional interrupted aortic arch repair, a swing-back technique, interposition with a pulmonary autograft tube, and extra-anatomical bypass.3 Because of the long distance between the ascending and descending aorta, the conventional repair was not feasible, and the ascending aorta was not long enough to perform the swing-back technique. Patch augmentation of the hypoplastic tortuous aorta and its connection to the ascending aorta has been described but relies upon the most pathogenic segment of the aorta to establish continuity and carries the risk of right main bronchus compression.2 Pulmonary autograft interposition could be an alternative in relatively short aortic bypass cases.

Extra-anatomical bypass for aortic arch obstruction in children has not been described for cases involving a right aortic arch.4 Unlike the left aortic arch, a right descending aorta is easier to visualize and facilitates performing a right-sided intrathoracic anastomosis to a right aortic arch. Passing the conduit above the SVC and down to the descending aorta risks conduit kinking, which can be alleviated by placing the graft posterior to the SVC and running directly to the descending aorta. The conduit, however, occupies considerable space and may compress surrounding structures (such as the SVC, the right pulmonary artery, the right bronchus, and the trachea). This technique allows for the possibility of balloon expansion of either internal pulmonary artery band aperture or interposition graft size to adjust for somatic growth if necessary, whereas an 8-mm conduit is adequate for a neonate; the PECA graft has been shown to dilate to up to 300% of its original diameter.5 Serial follow-up with intermittent catheter-based dilation will hopefully minimize the need for future surgical conduit revisions.

Conclusions

An extra-anatomical aortic bypass with an 8-mm PECA graft was performed for an interrupted right aortic arch with corkscrew descending aorta in a neonate. Serial follow-up with intermittent catheter-based dilation will hopefully minimize future surgical conduit revisions.
