Altitudinal influence on chemical composition, antioxidant potential and methyl ester profile of kapok seed oil: a sustainable biodiesel option
Authors: C Selvam, K Muralidharan, P Manoj Kumar, V Naresh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKapok seed oil extracted from seeds of Ceiba pentandra naturally grows in tropical regions. It has been gaining attention for being used as a sustainable, non-food feedstock for the production of biodiesel. However, the quality of kapok seed oil is strongly influenced by the altitudinal variations. To address this, the present experimental investigation explores the impact of three different altitudes on the chemical composition, antioxidant characteristics, and fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) and also assesses its potential as an environmentally friendly biodiesel source. Seeds from three different altitudes were collected, and two-step transesterification was employed to produce kapok oil methyl ester (KOME). Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) was performed to quantify the profile of FAME and aliphatics. The GC-MS analysis revealed the presence of FAMEs. In which, methyl oleate increased from 21.84 ± 0.23% to 36.94 ± 0.25, and methyl stearate increased from 2.80 ± 0.05% to 5.97 ± 0.13%, showing substantial positive relationships with increasing altitude. The oil extraction efficiency was increased from 92.26% ±0.49% to 98.32 ± 0.51% at high elevation. Additionally, oxygenated sesquiterpene showed a minor increase with altitude because it increased from 1.12% to 1.46%, which resulted in improved radical scavenging abilities. Further, the antioxidant capacity improved with increasing altitude according to the DPPH and ABTS IC₅₀ values, which decreased from 212.33 ± 2.52 µg/mL to 188.67 ± 1.53 µg/mL and 228.33 ± 2.52 µg/mL to 199.00 ± 2.00 µg/mL, thus demonstrating an 11% − 12% boost in radical scavenging capabilities. The one-way ANOVA method identified major FAMEs and antioxidant indices that differed significantly among altitudes (p < 0.05). The higher monounsaturated fatty acid content and improved oxidative stability at higher altitude indicated superior biodiesel characteristics. Hence, altitude is an important environmental factor in influencing the quality and biodiesel potential of kapok oil.Keywords: Kapok seed oil, KOME Transesterification, GC-MS Analysis, Antioxidant Activity, Alternative fuel, Bio-dieselSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

The global demand for energy is growing day by day. Petroleum-based fossil fuels, which are the primary source of energy, are intensifying the greenhouse gas emissions and exacerbating environmental problems. Further, their supply is continuously decreasing every day. Owing to the environmental advantages, lower emissions, and compatibility with diesel engines, biodiesel is being recognized as a viable alternative fuel. It mainly consists of FAMEs, which are derived from renewable lipid feedstocks. Non-edible oil seeds are acknowledged as a important feedstocks and also they can mitigate the conflict between food and fuel associated with edible oils. In particular, kapok seed oil is an inexpensive, sustainable non-food feedstock12. Further, the seed oil extracted from kapok has received notable interest due to its oil content, which ranges from around 18 to 25%, and the presence of desirable fatty acid composition3. Their methyl ester produced through transesterification shows potential for use in diesel engines4 and for reducing emissions5. Though kapok seed oil exhibited potential source for biodiesel production, a number of inherent obstacles like high FAMEs and variations in physicochemical properties exist. However, this could be overcome by implementing acid esterification as a pretreatment that facilitates efficient biodiesel production.

The composition of bioactive compounds and fatty acids directly influences the properties of biodiesel. It is essential to understand the factors affecting oil quality in the feedstocks. Many researchers investigated the non-edible oil feedstock characterization and the production of biodiesel. Kurniawan et al.6 employed the biorefinery integration technique to extract the biodiesel, bio-oil, and char from the kapok seeds. The effects of altitude differences on methyl ester content and biodiesel quality were not investigated. The biodiesel produced from kapok seed oil was found to be competitive with respect to methyl ester content and fuel specifications when compared to Jatrophacurcas and Sterculiafoetida feedstock7. However, it did not provide an understanding of feedstock variations under different growing conditions. Kusumaningtyas et al.8 examined the esterification process of kapok seed oil with methanol in the presence of a sulfuric acid catalyst and achieved an optimal conversion of 95.14% at the reaction temperature of 60°. Pooja et al.9 optimized the production of biodiesel from kapok seed oil using an immobilized lipase catalyst. The fuel properties were in compliance with ASTM requirements, and the emission behavior was found to be within the allowable limits. They did not explored the impact of altitude on methyl ester composition, and biodiesel quality. Marhaini et al.3 examined the transesterification process of kapok seed oil using activated rice husk and evaluated the biodiesel properties like density, viscosity, calorific value, etc. Their investigation ignored the analysis of antioxidant activity and biodiesel-relevant properties at various altitudinal locations. The characterization studies confirmed that the kapok methyl esters with appropriate catalysts can meet biodiesel standards and revealed compatible fuel properties10. Changes in growing conditions were considerably influenced by the altitudinal gradient, which could affect FAME composition and antioxidant properties, thereby limiting its application.

Research papers reported methods to produce biodiesel from sustainable feedstocks, which included Gossypium arboreum, waste lemon peel and cashew nut shell waste for engine applications11–13. Many researchers studied extensively the optimization of the biodiesel process and examination of fuel properties. However, very few research papers addressed the content of FAMEs and antioxidant activity, particularly in the context of altitude variation. Gulsoy et al.14 observed the influence of altitude on the variation of phenolics, antioxidant activity, and FAME composition in turkish hazelnuts. The temperature, UV radiation, and level of soil nutrients are the external factors that affect the phytochemical synthesis, which in turn influences the fatty acid dynamics. These factors will vary depending upon the altitudes at which seed oils are produced. The effect of altitude on the composition of seed oil extracted from camellia chekiangoleosa was studied15. Rezvanjo et al.16 reported the variation in FAME composition, antioxidant capacity, flavonoid and phenolic content of iranian hazelnut cultivars with respect to altitudinal gradients. The results indicated the importance of altitude in improving the nutritional quality.

A very few research papers focused on the effect of eco-geographical factors on biodiesel quality in kapok feedstocks. In particular, the altitudinal variation significantly affects the biodiesel-relevant properties of kapok seed oil. However, a detailed understanding of the influence of altitudinal variation in chemical composition, especially on methyl ester composition and antioxidant capacity for kapok oil, remains limited. Further, the existing literature primarily concentrated only on the production of biodiesel and fuel characterization and neglected the impact of altitude. In this study, the effect of altitudinal gradient on biodiesel-relevant characteristics in kapok seed oil was investigated by combining detailed, robust antioxidant assays, methyl ester characterization, and GC-MS profiling across three different altitude sites. The main objective was aimed to produce kapok oil methyl ester through the transesterification process and to systematically characterize its chemical composition and antioxidant characteristic for three different altitudes of 201 m (Chinna Anangur), 220 m (Town Center), and 418 m (Mallur) respectively, within same agro-climatic zone. Prime FAMEs and their approved retention indices were found using GC-MS profiling, while the antioxidant potential was assessed using DPPH and ABTS to determine its free-radical inhibition capability.

Experimental work

The experimental workflow included kapok seed oil extraction and characterization, two-step transesterification to produce KOME, GC-MS profiling of methyl esters, and evaluation of antioxidant activity using DPPH and ABTS assays. The experimental research, field studies on plants, and all procedures involving plant materials were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations.

Materials

In May and June, the fresh seed samples were harvested from kapok trees in the same agro-climatic zone, ensuring that the seeds were completely matured for oil processing. This collection took place at three distinct locations: Chinna Anangur, Town Center, and Mallur, near Komarapalayam in the Namakkal District of Tamil Nadu, India, at altitudes of 201 m, 220 m, and 418 m, respectively. The kapok seeds (Ceiba pentandra) used in this study were obtained from cultivated plants on private land with the full consent of the landowners and were not collected from wild populations. Therefore, no specific permissions and/or licences were required.

Production of KOME through transesterification

A two-step transesterification process (refer to Fig. 1.) that included acid esterification and alkaline transesterification was employed to convert kapok seed oil. The acid esterification was applied to decrease the high content of FAME, which led to prevention of soap formation during alkaline transesterification. At a temperature of around 60°, methanol and kapok seed oil in the molar ratio of 6:1 were allowed to react in the presence of sulfuric acid (1.0 vol% H₂SO₄) as a catalyst, under stirring conditions. As the free fatty acid level was reduced below 1 wt%, the reaction was considered to be completed. Then, the pretreated mixture was permitted to get settled and the remaining methanol and aqueous impurities were then removed. During alkaline transesterification, the sodium hydroxide (1.0 wt% NaOH) dissolved in methanol was reacted with pretreated oil in the molar ratio of 6:1. The reaction mixture was kept at a temperature of around 60° with continuous agitation for the effective conversion of FAMEs. After completion, glycerol and biodiesel layers were allowed to be separated. Then, glycerol was removed and the biodiesel phase was cleaned with warm distilled water to remove remaining catalyst and soaps. It was then dried to produce pure KOME.

GC-MS of KOME

GC-MS was conducted by an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph system fitted with an Agilent 5977 A mass selective detector. An HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness) was used to attain separation, and it was appropriate for resolving FAMEs. The gas of helium was utilized at a flow rate of 1.0 mLmin[-1. A temperature of 250 °C was kept at the injector. Samples were injected using split mode injection, with an injection volume of 1 µL and a split ratio of 20. The GC oven temperature program employed for KOME analysis was shown in Table 1.

The mass spectrometer worked with an ion source temperature of 230 °C and a quadrupole temperature of 150 °C. Antioxidant activity

The DPPH and ABTS assays were employed to quantify the antioxidant capacity of the kapok seed oil collected across three different altitudinal locations.

DPPH method

DPPH is a nitrogen-centered free radical scavenging assay displayed by a dark violet color in methanol solution. The collected kapok seed oil sample was mixed with methanol to produce a 0.1 mM concentration DPPH solution, which was then incubated in the dark room to protect it from light for a period of 30 min. UV-visible spectrophotometer enabled to measure the reduction in absorbance at 517 nm, and the reduction resulted in a change of color as pale yellow. Under the same conditions, ascorbic acid was employed as reference standard and methonal was used as blank. The results were presented as % DPPH radical scavenging activity as mentioned in Eq. ABTS Assay

The ABTS radical cation scavenging assay was employed further to assess the antioxidant capacity of kapok seed oil samples. An aqueous ABTS (7 mM) was reacted with potassium persulfate (2.45 mM) to prepare ABTS⁺• radical cation, and it was kept in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h. Prior to testing, the ABTS⁺• solution was mixed with ethanol until it had an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. 0.2 mL of each oil sample (50 to 250 µg/mL) was blended with 2.0 mL of the diluted ABTS⁺• solution and kept in the dark at room temperature for 6 min. The reduction of absorbance was reported at 734 nm. Under the same experimental conditions, ethanol served as the blank, while ascorbic acid was utilized as the reference antioxidant. One-way ANOVA was employed to investigate the impact of altitude on the composition of FAMEs in kapok seed oil. The mean and SD values for the amount of methyl stearate present in the samples were 2.80 ± 0.05% for low altitude, 0.61 ± 0.04% for medium altitude, and 5.97 ± 0.13% for high altitude. Its F-value of 3351.8 and p < 0.001 confirmed a high significant altitudinal effect. A similar effect was also observed in methyl oleate, which displayed 21.84 ± 0.23% at low, 21.54 ± 0.23% at medium, and 36.94 ± 0.25% at high altitude. Its statistical significance was demonstrated by a substantially high F-value of 4217.7 and p < 0.001. In kapok seed oil, the content of methyl stearate and methyl oleate was strongly influenced by the altitudinal difference, and it was confirmed by the strong positive correlation values of r = 0.843 and r = 0.932 (refer to Table 3) respectively. By contrast, the F-value of 6065.8, p < 0.001, and r = −0.709 (refer to Table 3) indicated that a decrease in the content of methyl linoleate with an increase in altitude. Thus, a metabolic change from polyunsaturated to more oxidation-stable fatty acids was observed at higher altitudes. The altitudinal difference was also exhibited as significant for methyl palmitate, and it showed a negative correlation value of r = −0.596 (refer to Table 3). The calculated F-value was 5280.1 and p < 0.001. The calculated F-values of FAMEs were found to be much higher than the tabulated F-values (F₀.₀₅,₂,₆ = 5.14). Hence, a significant difference among the altitudinal locations in the composition of FAMEs in kapok seed oil was statistically confirmed.

Altitudinal influence on oil yield

The content of oil yield was increasing from 97.02%±0.45% (201 m) to 98.32 ± 0.51% (418 m) as the altitude increased. The F-value of 130.36, p < 0.001, and r = 0.720 (refer to Table 3) for oil yield exhibited that there was a strong influence of altitude on lipid content in kapok seed oil. Further, the positive correlation value of methyl oleate r = 0.671 (refer to Table 3) was obtained in the correlation matrix. This was due to the increased oxidation-stable fatty acids synthesis at higher altitudes, and it is beneficial for biodiesel production. Hence, altitude is a key factor for influencing the quantity and quality of kapok seed oil.

Antioxidant activity assessed by DPPH and ABTS assays

The DPPH and ABTS proved that antioxidant activity had a significant relationship with altitude. The strong radical scavenging activity was indicated by the lower IC₅₀ values, which were detected at the higher altitudes. Further, it was confirmed by the higher radical scavenging percentage of 52.9 ± 0.3% in DPPH and 50.26 ± 0.25% in ABTS. The F-values of 127.03 for DPPH and 158.74 for ABTS demonstrated the highest statistical significance. The Pearson correlation coefficient values of r = −0.863 of DPPH and r = −0.881 of ABTS validated the consistency in obtaining IC₅₀ values. Also, DPPH showed the strong negative correlations with methyl oleate (r = −0.821), as in Table 3. The findings indicated that samples with larger amounts of saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids had more antioxidant potential. The interpretation revealed that the co-occurrence of minor bioactive constituents with FAME contributed to improve the antioxidant tendency.

Integrated interpretation of ANOVA and correlation analysis

The complementary results were obtained from the ANOVA and correlation table pertaining to the altitudinal influence on kapok seed oil. According to the ANOVA results, it was confirmed that the altitudinal variation substantially affected the FAME composition, oil yield, and antioxidant activity. The correlation matrix proved that these variables exhibited consistent relationships among themselves.

Particularly, the increases in methyl oleate and methyl stearate at higher altitude were led to a higher oil yield and enhanced antioxidant activity, as indicated by lower IC₅₀ values. These observations implied that altitude indirectly affected the antioxidant potential by the combined effect of changes in lipid metabolism and related bioactive components. The statistical results provided the support for the reliability of these findings.

At higher altitudes, the increase in monounsaturated fatty acids like methyl oleate can cause a substantial improvement in the cetane number, oxidative stability, and cold flow properties. The simultaneous increase in oil yield and antioxidant capabilities at high altitudes makes kapok seed oil a viable and sustainable feedstock for biodiesel synthesis.

GC-MS analysis of kapok seed oil obtained from three locations in Komarapalayam was conducted. A notable difference in chemical composition had been identified, and a total of 32 compounds were classified into monoterpene hydrocarbons, oxygenated monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, phenylpropanoids, and FAMEs (refer to Table 4; Fig. 2). The detected components accounted for 92.26 ± 0.49% – 98.32 ± 0.51% of the total oil, which indicated the efficient extraction.

Classes of compounds in KOME oil.

FAMEs and aliphatic esters account for 84.78 ± 0.45–90.27 ± 0.47% of the composition of oil, implying that it primarily consists of lipids that are suitable for the production of biodiesel. Also, methyl oleate, linoleate, and palmitate were the key compounds of the oil, contributing to more than 20% of the total oil composition across all tested regions. Chinna Anangur reported a greater methyl linoleate content of 38.82 ± 0.28% (refer to Fig. 3), whereas Mallur recorded a higher methyl oleate of 36.94 ± 0.25% (refer to Fig. 5). The increased content of methyl oleate is desirable because monounsaturated fatty acids improve the oxidative stability, cetane number, and cold flow performance of biodiesel fuel. The Town Center samples contained a comparatively higher quantity of methyl palmitate (41.26 ± 0.28%) (refer to Fig. 4) indicating a higher degree of saturation. Though this feature improves storage stability, it also slightly increases the viscosity of fuel. The variations in the statistical significance p < 0.05 were represented by different superscripts, and they indicated that altitudinal or microclimatic conditions lead to the production of different lipid biosynthesis rates between these regions. The altitudinal variation within these regions had a significant effect on fuel quality standards. Though monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes were present in a very small amount of less than 3%, they would contribute to biologically related properties. In the sample from Chinna Anangur, oxygenated monoterpenes such as linalool (2.14 ± 0.12%), terpinen-4-ol, and α-terpineol were detected and recognized for antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. Further, the detected oxygenated sesquiterpenes, such as caryophyllene oxide and spathulenol possessed the ability of radical scavenging. These minor phytochemicals proved the antioxidant capacity observed in the results of DPPH and ABTS tests.

Particularly in Mallur, sesquiterpene hydrocarbons like germacrene D (refer to Fig. 5) exhibited a comparatively higher percentage of 2.49 ± 0.11%, the presence phenyl propanoids of eugenol which was caused by the secondary metabolites that accumulated as a result of environmental stress conditions. These metabolites synergistically act with unsaturated fatty acids in improving the oxidative resistance of the oil.

Due to the larger fraction of unsaturated FAMEs together with its oxygenated terpenoids’ presence, kapok oil has both antioxidant and biodiesel properties. The compositional variations at different altitudes demonstrated that environment can significantly affect fuel quality and bioactive properties. Hence, it is essential to choose specific regions for creating environmentally friendly biodiesel feedstock solutions.

Gas chromatography is a useful tool for detecting target chemicals in bioactivity studies, and standardization. The comparison of gas chromatograms shown in Figs. 3, 4 and 5 demonstrated that consistent peaks and desirable chemical profile was noticed in the samples collected at Mallur.

In experimental results among three places, KOME showed antioxidant activity and excellent properties at higher altitudes compared to standards (Table 5). B50 refers to a fuel blend containing 50% KOME and 50% diesel by volume. Such blends are generally utilized to balance the renewable and oxygenated benefits of biodiesel, such as enhanced combustion and reduced emissions, while maintaining fuel characteristics (viscosity, CV, and engine compatibility) closer to those of conventional diesel.

The fuel properties of pure KOME and its B50 blend are summarized in Table 5 and show good agreement with international biodiesel standards. The CI of pure KOME (51) meets the minimum requirement of both ASTM (≥ 41) and EN (≥ 51), while the B50 blend exhibits a higher CI (55), indicating enhanced ignition quality compared to neat biodiesel. The kinematic viscosity at 40 °C for both pure KOME (4.79 mm²/s) and B50 (4.08 mm²/s) lies well within the specified limits of ASTM (1.9 to 6.0 mm²/s) and EN (3.5 to 5.0 mm²/s), confirming acceptable flow and atomization characteristics. Overall, the outcomes indicate that both pure KOME and its B50 possess fuel properties comparable to standard biodiesel requirements, helping their suitability as alternative diesel fuels.

Conclusions

The present experimental investigation revealed that the yield, composition, and antioxidant properties of kapok seed oil are significantly influenced by altitudinal gradient, and the following points are concluded.

The altitudinal changes significantly affected the seed oil production, FAME profile, and antioxidant capacity of kapok plants, as confirmed by one-way ANOVA. The oil yield showed an increase with altitude, increasing from 92.26% ±0.49% (at 220 m) to 98.32 ± 0.51% (at 418 m), which resulted an improvement of 6.6% at high altitudes.

The altitudinal changes significantly affected the seed oil production, FAME profile, and antioxidant capacity of kapok plants, as confirmed by one-way ANOVA. The oil yield showed an increase with altitude, increasing from 92.26% ±0.49% (at 220 m) to 98.32 ± 0.51% (at 418 m), which resulted an improvement of 6.6% at high altitudes.

The major FAME components exhibited strong positive relationships with increasing altitude. In particular, methyl oleate showed a percentage increase of approximately 69%, while a percentage increase of approximately 113% was observed in methyl stearate with altitudinal increase. Further, these compounds showed positive relationships with oil yield (r ≈ 0.671, −0.903) and antioxidant activity (r ≈ −0.541, −0.869), which demonstrated that higher monounsaturated and saturated fractions lead to better oxidative stability and extractability.
