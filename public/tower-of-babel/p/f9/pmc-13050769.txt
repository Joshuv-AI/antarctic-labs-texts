Research progress on nucleic acid amplification–based detection technologies for phytopathogenic fungi
Authors: Baoyu Wang, Mei Liu, Hui Wang, Chenfang Wang, Wei Zhang, Jiye Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractPhytopathogenic fungi are highly diverse and globally distributed, posing a major threat to agricultural production worldwide. The annual losses caused by plant diseases can reach up to 30% of global crop yields, with over 80% of infections caused by fungal pathogens. The accurate identification of pathogenic fungal species is crucial for effective disease prevention and control. Thus, establishing accurate and rapid detection technologies for phytopathogenic fungi is crucial for implementing targeted control strategies and reducing agricultural losses. Molecular detection technologies based on nucleic acid amplification have recently become indispensable tools for pathogen detection. This review examines the principles and advancements of nucleic acid-based detection techniques, including thermal cycling-based methods (e.g., conventional PCR, real-time quantitative PCR, and droplet digital PCR) and isothermal amplification platforms (e.g., loop-mediated isothermal amplification and recombinase polymerase amplification), as well as CRISPR/Cas-assisted assays coupled with isothermal amplification (e.g., RPA–CRISPR and LAMP–CRISPR), with the aim of evaluating their strengths, limitations, and practical applicability in the rapid diagnosis and precision management of phytopathogenic fungal diseases.Key points Nucleic acid amplification technologies enable rapid and sensitive detection of phytopathogenic fungi.Isothermal amplification and CRISPR/Cas-assisted platforms facilitate field-deployable and low-instrumentation diagnostics.Integrated workflows support early diagnosis and precision management of phytopathogenic fungal diseases.Keywords: Phytopathogenic fungi, Nucleic acid extraction, Nucleic acid amplification, Rapid detection

Introduction

Fungi are among the most diverse biological groups, with global diversity estimated to range from 2 to 11 million species (Hawksworth and Lücking 2017; Phukhamsakda et al. 2022). To date, approximately 205,305 fungal species have been formally described (http://www.speciesfungorum.org/; 2 March 2026), of which approximately 10,000 have been identified as phytopathogens (Kainat et al. 2025). Phytopathogenic fungi account for 70–80% of all plant pathogens, suggesting that fungi are the primary etiological agents of plant diseases (Manoharachary and Deshaboina 2021). Warmer temperatures have been predicted to increase the abundance of latent phytopathogenic fungi in agroecosystems (Delgado-Baquerizo et al. 2020; Li P et al. 2023; Casu et al. 2024). Phytopathogenic fungi can cause severe crop yield and quality losses. Under favorable environmental conditions, these pathogens can infect plants at all growth stages and during post-harvest storage. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) has reported that fungal pathogens reduce annual yields by 10–23% in 168 crops that are critical to global nutrition, with post-harvest losses further diminishing outputs by 10–20% (Steinberg and Gurr 2020). These losses cumulatively equate to the annual food requirement of 600 million to 4 billion people. The current reliance on conventional strategies, such as crop management practices, single-gene resistance breeding, and single-target fungicides, has failed to adequately mitigate the threat posed by phytopathogenic fungi. Innovative technologies that enable effective early detection and sustainable disease management are urgently needed (Stukenbrock and Gurr 2023).

The rapid and accurate detection of phytopathogenic fungi is critical for early pathogen identification, proactive disease management, and agricultural loss reduction. Traditional morphological diagnostics, which are primarily based on symptom observation, colony morphology, sporulation structures, and spore characteristics, are foundational for fungal disease diagnosis due to their ability to provide intuitive visual results (Gurjar and Kanade 2020; Senanayake et al. 2020). However, these methods have the following limitations: (1) time-consuming processes; (2) microbial competition biases that favor fast-growing strains during culturing and the inability to culture obligate parasites, resulting in incomplete pathogen identification; (3) frequent inability to achieve species-level resolution based solely on morphological characteristics, as many closely related taxa can exhibit highly similar macro- and micromorphological traits (Cho et al. 2025; Han et al. 2025); (4) dependence on researchers’ professional skills and taxonomic knowledge for accurate identification; and (5) morphological detection depends on visible symptom manifestation, limiting opportunities for early disease intervention (Raja et al. 2017).

The rapid detection of phytopathogenic fungi has increasingly shifted to the molecular level due to the advent of nucleic acid amplification–based molecular techniques, which offer speed, accuracy, and high sensitivity, thus addressing the limitations of traditional approaches. Molecular detection enables pathogen identification during early infection or latent stages, making it indispensable in modern plant disease diagnostics (Hariharan and Prasannath 2021; Meena et al. 2025). Current molecular techniques used for phytopathogenic fungi include polymerase chain reaction (PCR), multiplex PCR (mPCR), nested PCR (nPCR), real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR), digital PCR (dPCR), loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA). Among these, dPCR is increasingly adopted for absolute quantification and improved tolerance to inhibitors in complex plant matrices (Morcia et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025c). Recent studies and reviews highlight the expanding application of isothermal amplification, particularly LAMP and RPA. These approaches are widely adopted for rapid, equipment-light detection of plant pathogens, including phytopathogenic fungi (Zhang et al. 2024b; Manjunatha et al. 2025; Németh and Kovács 2025; Yang et al. 2025). In recent years, CRISPR/Cas-assisted assays integrated with isothermal pre-amplification (e.g., LAMP–CRISPR and RPA–CRISPR) have further enhanced analytical specificity and enabled portable or visual readouts in field-oriented settings (Fang et al. 2024; Gong et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Shaizadinova et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2024). At the same time, the translation of molecular diagnostics toward field use has been accelerated by portable and integrated workflows (e.g., rapid extraction coupled with chip-based amplification and smartphone-assisted readout) (Shymanovich et al. 2024; Yadav and Yadav 2025).

Molecular diagnostics enable the precise detection of single and multiple infections, culturability-independent identification of pathogens, and diagnosis of presymptomatic diseases. Therefore, the establishment of early, precise, and rapid detection technologies is vital for monitoring pathogen spread and guiding targeted disease management. This review systematically evaluates molecular diagnostic platforms for plant fungal pathogens, summarizes their current applications, and analyzes existing challenges and potential solutions. We aim to provide a reference for advancing research and practically implementing the molecular detection of phytopathogenic fungi.

Nucleic acid amplification–based molecular detection technologies

Nucleic acid amplification–based molecular detection uses enzyme-mediated reactions under optimized conditions to achieve rapid amplification of target nucleic acids in vitro, thus enabling detection and analysis. In conventional PCR, thermocycling through denaturation, primer annealing, and extension enables repeated template copying, resulting in an exponential increase in target DNA copies under ideal conditions. Since its introduction, PCR has evolved from endpoint gel-based detection to qPCR, which quantifies target abundance by real-time fluorescence monitoring, and dPCR, which achieves absolute quantification through reaction partitioning coupled with Poisson statistics. In parallel, isothermal amplification platforms (e.g., LAMP and RPA) have further reduced instrumentation requirements for rapid on-site testing.

Collectively, these platforms offer high analytical specificity; however, their analytical sensitivity can vary considerably across amplification platforms, as illustrated by representative studies summarized in Table 1. Conventional PCR generally has a higher limit of detection than qPCR, dPCR, and many optimized isothermal amplification assays, and is therefore less suitable for the detection of very low-abundance targets. Analytical sensitivity can also vary substantially across assay chemistries, instruments, and sample matrices (Bustin et al. 2025; Wilkinson et al. 2024; Nwe et al. 2024). When assays are appropriately designed, optimized, and validated for the intended matrix, nucleic acid amplification can enable reliable detection of low-abundance targets, which is critical for the early identification of phytopathogenic fungi and for timely disease management and outbreak mitigation. The standardized workflow for nucleic acid amplification–based molecular detection comprises four key steps: pathogen-specific primer design, nucleic acid extraction, amplification reaction, and amplification product detection. Primer design underpins specificity and multiplex compatibility, nucleic acid extraction determines yield and inhibitor removal, amplification chemistry dictates speed and sensitivity, and product detection provides qualitative or quantitative readout suitable for laboratory or field settings.

Primer design

The successful use of molecular detection technologies for phytopathogenic fungi depends on the design of specific primers capable of unambiguously recognizing target nucleic acid sequences. Appropriate target gene selection is critical and typically involves the following: (1) conserved, multicopy ribosomal DNA sequences (e.g., 18S, 5.8S, 28S, and ITS regions) (Zhao et al. 2024; Vojvodić et al. 2024; Lee and Choi 2025); (2) constitutively expressed housekeeping genes with high intraspecies conservation and inter-species variability (e.g., actin, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and translation elongation factor 1-alpha (EF-1α)) (Paul et al. 2024; Shaizadinova et al. 2025); and (3) pathogen-specific genes that are absent in host plants or other microorganisms (Fang et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2025). Primers should be designed using bioinformatics tools (e.g., National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Primer-BLAST, Oligo 7, and Primer Premier) to comply with detection platform specifications. Notably, recent years have witnessed the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI)-powered tools for automated primer design, such as PrimeGen, Genome-wide Primer Scan (GPS), and ThermoPlex, which significantly enhance the efficiency and specificity of primer development for targeted pathogens (Agmata et al. 2025; He et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2026).

Primer parameters, including primer length, GC content, melting temperature (Tm), and 3′ end stability, should be checked, and secondary structures (e.g., hairpins, self-dimers, and cross-dimers) should be avoided. To reduce primer–dimer formation, primer design should avoid 3′-end complementarity, long homopolymer runs, and excessive complementarity among primers, and should be followed by empirical optimization of primer concentrations and cycling/isothermal conditions to suppress nonspecific amplification (Xie et al. 2022; Agmata et al. 2025).

For multiplex detection of fungal pathogens, primer/probe sets should typically be co-designed to (i) share similar Tm values and amplification efficiencies, (ii) minimize cross-reactivity and primer–primer complementarity (especially at 3′ ends), and (iii) employ distinguishable signal outputs or amplicon characteristics (e.g., distinct hydrolysis probes/fluorophores in multiplex qPCR) to reduce signal cross-talk and improve assay throughput (Romero-Cuadrado et al. 2024; Agmata et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025a). In addition, for assays targeting SNP-defined variants of phytopathogenic fungi (e.g., fungicide-resistance alleles), allele-specific primer/probe designs typically position the discriminatory nucleotide at or near the 3′ terminus and may introduce an additional deliberate mismatch to enhance allelic discrimination; because primer-template mismatches can alter amplification efficiency, allele-specific assays should be validated for both specificity and efficiency (Knight et al. 2024; Sánchez-Zelaia et al. 2024; Zulak et al. 2025). Primer specificity is initially confirmed through in silico validation using Primer-BLAST to perform local alignments against the NCBI nucleotide database prior to experimental validation. Primers that only align with the target species sequence are classified as validated specific primers (Kumar and Chordia 2015).

Nucleic acid extraction

Nucleic acids are the primary templates for molecular diagnostics. Traditional nucleic acid extraction methods (Fig. 1A) include cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) (Murray and Thompson 1980), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Xia et al. 2019), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) (Kim et al. 1997), alkali extraction (e.g., NaOH and polyethylene glycol extraction) (Wang et al. 1993; Chomczynski and Rymaszewski 2006), boiling lysis (Lim et al. 2022), high salt precipitation (Al-Samarrai and Schmid 2000), column purification (Lin et al. 2021; Chang et al. 2024), magnetic bead method (Li Y et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2024; Vutukuru et al. 2025), and commercial kits (Balduque-Gil et al. 2024; Dong et al. 2025). CTAB-based or optimized CTAB protocols have been widely adopted for nucleic acid extraction from tissues rich in polysaccharides and polyphenols (Balduque-Gil et al. 2024; Dar et al. 2025).

A Conventional nucleic acid extraction methods, encompassing organic solvent extraction (e.g., phenol–chloroform), column purification, and magnetic bead-based methods. B Rapid nucleic acid extraction techniques, including plastic sample preparation bags, Flinders Technology Associates (FTA) cards, cellulose-based filter paper extraction, microneedle patches, and in situ fixation (ISF)

A conventional DNA extraction workflow typically involves three sequential phases: (1) cell lysis through mechanical disruption (liquid nitrogen grinding, micro-homogenizer, sonication, or magnetic beads) (Yang et al. 2022a, b; Haveman et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024a), chemical agents (CTAB, SDS, Triton X-100, or NaOH), or enzymatic treatments (proteinase K); (2) nucleic acid purification and separation via phenol–chloroform, spin column, salt precipitation, or magnetic beads; and (3) nucleic acid precipitation with isopropanol, ethanol, or PEG/NaCl solutions and resuspension in nuclease-free water or Tris–EDTA (TE) buffer (pH 8.0). Resuspending nucleic acids in nuclease-free water should be done cautiously because atmospheric CO2 absorption can acidify the solution, compromising its long-term stability. Protocol optimization is dependent on the sample; for example, woody plant tissues are challenging due to high polysaccharide, polyphenol, and secondary metabolite concentrations, which inhibit downstream nucleic acid amplification (Demeke and Jenkins 2010). To address these challenges, Spadoni et al. (2019) developed an optimized CTAB protocol using a high concentration of NaCl (1.42 M) and CTAB (3%) to prevent polysaccharide co-precipitation, 1% PVP for polyphenol sequestration, and lyophilized tissue pretreatment to accelerate processing.

The recent advancements in molecular detection technologies, such as LAMP and RPA, have increased the demand for efficient nucleic acid extraction in the field. Conventional extraction approaches rely on laboratory-based equipment, limiting their on-site applicability. Consequently, the development of rapid, cost-effective plant DNA extraction protocols or portable DNA extraction devices is urgently needed. This shift has stimulated growing interest in expedited nucleic acid isolation techniques, including plastic sample preparation bags (Valasevich and Schneider 2017; McCoy et al. 2020; Ivanov et al. 2021; Diksha et al. 2024), Flinders Technology Associates (FTA) cards (Borman et al. 2006; Grund et al. 2010; Lu et al. 2016), cellulose-based filter paper extraction (Zou et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021b, a; Liu et al. 2024a), microneedle patch technology (Paul et al. 2019; Li J et al. 2023; Shymanovich et al. 2024), magnetic silica beads (Vutukuru et al. 2025), magnetic ionic liquids (MILs) (Marengo et al. 2019), and rapid DNA extraction kits (Sun et al. 2024). In these field-deployable extraction formats, the underlying principle is generally simplified into rapid tissue disruption, short-time adsorption or capture of nucleic acids onto a solid support (e.g., cellulose, silica, or magnetic particles), brief washing to remove amplification inhibitors, and direct elution or transfer of the captured DNA into downstream amplification systems. This simplified workflow reduces dependence on centrifugation and other bench-top instruments and is therefore particularly suitable for integration with isothermal amplification platforms.

These streamlined extraction methods significantly enhance diagnostic efficiency when integrated with isothermal amplification platforms, such as LAMP and RPA, enabling direct field implementation for plant pathogen detection and serving as a critical advancement for effective plant disease management and prevention strategies (Liu et al. 2025b; Zou et al. 2024). For example, Wang et al. (2021b, 2021a) developed an EZ-D (EASY DNA extraction) stick in which a cellulose filter paper strip (Whatman No.1) is placed on a polyvinyl chloride (PVC) sheet. This innovative approach achieves plant tissue DNA extraction in 30–60 s at a low cost of approximately USD 0.03 per sample. Building on filter paper–based extraction, Zhang et al. (2020) implemented an integrated RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a–lateral flow assay (LFA) detection platform for field applications and demonstrated its efficacy in the identification of both Magnaporthe oryzae and transgenic rice. Shymanovich et al. (2024) achieved rapid detection of Alternaria solani in 30 min in the field using microneedle-mediated DNA extraction coupled with LAMP. Similarly, Sun et al. (2024) developed a magnetic bead–based rapid extraction method combined with RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a and incorporated either LFA or endpoint fluorescence visualization to detect Diaporthe phaseolorum in soybean within 60 min. These advancements highlight the critical role of streamlined nucleic acid workflows in enabling real-time phytosanitary decision-making.

Nucleic acid amplification

Nucleic acid amplification technologies enable the exponential amplification of target nucleic acid sequences via enzymatic reactions under optimized conditions. Thermal cycling platforms (e.g., PCR, qPCR, and dPCR) depend on Taq DNA polymerase, a thermostable enzyme derived from Thermus aquaticus, to withstand repeated denaturation cycles. Isothermal amplification systems demonstrate distinct enzymatic requirements. LAMP utilizes Bst DNA polymerase with intrinsic strand displacement activity (Notomi 2000), and RPA depends on three core proteins, namely recombinase, single-strand DNA-binding protein (SSB), and strand-displacing DNA polymerase, to achieve target DNA fragment amplification at constant temperatures (Piepenburg et al. 2006). PCR-based methods (PCR, qPCR, and dPCR) require precise temperature modulation for denaturation, annealing, and extension phases through specialized instrumentation, whereas isothermal platforms (LAMP and RPA) operate at fixed temperatures without thermal cycling or complex equipment. Maintaining an optimal reaction temperature is critical for ensuring amplification efficiency and specificity across all platforms. Suboptimal temperature control can lead to non-specific amplification (e.g., primer dimer formation) or reaction failure, primarily due to inefficient denaturation or annealing. Therefore, the precise regulation of amplification temperature during experimental design and operation remains critical for ensuring reliable and specific nucleic acid amplification outcomes.

Nucleic acid amplification product detection

Nucleic acid amplification technology requires specific product detection methodologies to ensure analytical accuracy. An overview of these nucleic acid amplification product detection strategies is presented in Fig. 2. Agarose gel electrophoresis is the standard confirmatory method for PCR, LAMP, and RPA amplicons. In addition to this foundational approach, LAMP technology has evolved multiple detection modalities to accommodate diverse operational requirements. (1) Real-time turbidimetric detection relies on the formation of magnesium pyrophosphate precipitate as a byproduct, which increases the solution’s turbidity proportionally to amplicon yield (Mori et al. 2004). (2) Fluorescence-based detection employs either SYBR Green I intercalation or sequence-specific probes coupled with LAMP primers to track fluorescence changes during amplification (Cui et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2024). (3) Colorimetric assays utilize visual indicators, such as Hydroxy naphthol Blue (HNB) (Goto et al. 2009), calcein (Tomita et al. 2008), pH indicators, and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), which undergo target-dependent aggregation, forming a red pellet visible to the naked eye (Novi et al. 2024). (4) Lateral flow detection integrates fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled probes with lateral flow dipstick (LFD) systems, which facilitates rapid endpoint detection, significantly enhancing field-deployable diagnostics (Zhou et al. 2016). Closed-tube detection systems are recommended to minimize aerosol contamination associated with post-amplification manipulation (Wu 2020). For example, HNB can be pre-mixed in the reaction buffer prior to LAMP initiation, and other dyes may be pre-loaded in tube caps or walls for centrifugal mixing post-reaction, eliminating the need for post-amplification tube opening.

Analysis methods for amplification products of various molecular detection technologies. RPA technology similarly enables real-time fluorescence monitoring for product detection using two principal probe systems: exonuclease III (exo) with exo fluorescence probes and formamide pyrimidine DNA glycosylase (fpg) with fpg fluorescence probes. This approach achieves field-deployable quantitative analysis when coupled with portable isothermal amplification devices (Powell et al. 2018; Jiao et al. 2024). RPA can also be adapted for lateral flow detection by incorporating endonuclease IV (nfo) and nfo fluorescence probes (Yang et al. 2023). qPCR and dPCR enable nucleic acid quantification. qPCR enables the relative or absolute quantification via standard curves, whereas dPCR achieves absolute quantification without calibration by partitioning samples into thousands of microreactors. These technological advancements have collectively enhanced nucleic acid detection sensitivity and precision, establishing robust frameworks for molecular diagnostics.

PCR-based detection of phytopathogenic fungi

Polymerase chain reaction

Since its initial development by Kary Mullis in 1985 (Mullis and Faloona 1987), PCR has emerged as a cornerstone for identifying and detecting phytopathogenic fungi (Moreno-Velázquez et al. 2019; Kashyap et al. 2020; Hudson et al. 2021). PCR enables the rapid in vitro amplification of target DNA sequences via thermal cycling with sequence-specific primers, a DNA template, deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), and thermostable Taq DNA polymerase. As early as (1991), Nazar et al. analyzed the ribosomal DNA ITS sequences of two closely related Verticillium species (V. albo-atrum and V. dahliae). By designing species-specific primers based on merely five key nucleotide differences, they achieved rapid detection and precise differentiation of these pathogens in infected alfalfa plants. It remains a mature and reliable detection method in this field to this day. In 2024, Paul et al. developed a PCR assay targeting the EF-1α gene, enabling the specific and sensitive detection of the okra wilt pathogen Fusarium buharicum directly from infected plants and infested soils.

PCR has distinct advantages over culture-based methods; it circumvents the need for pure fungal cultures, enables target DNA detection in complex biological matrices, and provides high specificity, sensitivity, and rapid processing. However, conventional PCR has inherent limitations, including its reliance on agarose gel electrophoresis for endpoint analysis, its inability to quantify target DNA, and susceptibility to false-negative results due to low template DNA concentrations or inhibitors in plant tissues. Over the years, the growing diversity and complexity of plant diseases have posed increasing challenges that conventional PCR alone cannot address. In response, advanced PCR-derived methodologies, such as multiplex PCR, nested PCR, qPCR, and dPCR, have been developed for enhanced phytopathogen diagnostics. These approaches have advanced phytopathogen diagnostics, with multiplex PCR increasing throughput through simultaneous multitarget detection, nested PCR improving sensitivity and specificity for low-abundance targets, qPCR enabling real-time monitoring and quantitative analysis, and dPCR providing highly precise absolute quantification with improved tolerance to inhibitors in complex samples.

Multiplex polymerase chain reaction

As an advancement of conventional PCR, mPCR enables the simultaneous amplification of multiple target DNA sequences in a single reaction by employing two or more sequence-specific primer pairs. Fungal species differentiation is achieved via distinct amplicon sizes resolved by electrophoresis. For example, Dai et al. (2021) established an mPCR system to differentiate between four Fusarium species—F. acuminatum (487 bp), F. oxysporum (344 bp), F. solani (245 bp), and F. graminearum (162 bp)—through fragment size analysis, achieving a limit of detection (LOD) of 1 × 10−4 ng/μL. This system has high sensitivity and specificity in identifying co-infections of soybean root rot pathogens. Similarly, Liu et al. (2024b) established a duplex PCR assay for detecting tomato wilt caused by co-infection with F. brachygibbosum and F. oxysporum. Zhang et al. (2017) similarly developed a duplex PCR method to simultaneously detect Botryosphaeria dothidea and Neofusicoccum parvum, facilitating the rapid screening of fungal contamination in grapevine nurseries. Although mPCR reduces reagent consumption and the processing time, persistent technical challenges include primer competition for template DNA and nonspecific amplification caused by cross-reactivity among multiple primer pairs. To mitigate primer competition in multiplex PCR, Deng et al. (2024) employed a strategy using a single universal forward primer in combination with multiple specific reverse primers, thereby substantially reducing the total number of primers in the reaction.

Nested PCR

nPCR uses a two-stage amplification approach with two primer pairs: universal outer primers for initial target amplification and highly specific inner primers for secondary amplification of the primary PCR product. This sequential approach enhances specificity and sensitivity by reducing non-specific binding and amplifying low-abundance targets. For example, Negahban et al. (2024) developed an nPCR assay for detecting Stilbocrea banihashemiana, the causal agent of fruit tree cankers, which employed the universal primers EF1-728F/EF1-2218R in the first round, followed by the species-specific primer set TEF-Sb3 (TEF-SbF1/TEF-SbR2) in the second round. This method achieved ultra-sensitive detection of the pathogen in both symptomatic and asymptomatic (latently infected) plant samples. Similarly, Ma et al. (2023) established an nPCR assay to detect Puccinia polysora in maize leaves, and the method demonstrated a 500-fold higher sensitivity than conventional PCR, enabling the reliable detection of latent infections. When analyzing 1 g of maize leaf tissue containing urediospores, uredinia, or infection sites, nPCR had significantly higher positive detection rates than conventional PCR. Paul et al. (2024) achieved a LOD of 0.1 fg/μL for F. buharicum, the causal agent of okra wilt, using nPCR, representing a 107-fold improvement in sensitivity compared to standard PCR (1 ng/μL). These findings highlight the enhanced accuracy of nPCR for detecting low-abundance fungal pathogens in plant tissues, particularly during the early infection stages, and suggest that its improved analytical sensitivity may increase detection success when target DNA is present at low levels in sampled infection sites.

Quantitative real-time PCR

As a second-generation PCR technology, qPCR represents a transition from qualitative to quantitative nucleic acid analysis, integrating fluorescent reporters into the reaction system to monitor real-time fluorescence changes during amplification. qPCR platforms primarily employ two fluorescent chemistries: SYBR Green I dye and sequence-specific TaqMan probes. The SYBR Green I method involves nonspecific binding to double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), providing cost-effective and simple operation for the broad-spectrum detection of pathogenic fungi. However, its tendency to fluoresce with any double-stranded product can generate false-positive results due to nonspecific amplification or primer dimer formation, limiting its application in multiplex qPCR analyses. In contrast, TaqMan probes consist of oligonucleotides labeled with a 5′ fluorophore (e.g., FAM, VIC, and CY5) and 3′ quencher (e.g., BHQ and TAMRA), achieving enhanced specificity through sequence-specific hybridization, which is particularly suitable for multiplex qPCR detection systems. However, this method requires custom-designed probes for each target gene, substantially increasing the synthesis and validation cost.

qPCR employs either absolute or relative quantification. Absolute quantification uses a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of a known DNA concentration for determining the initial template quantity in unknown samples. This method correlates the logarithmic values of template copy numbers with their corresponding threshold cycle (Ct) values. Absolute quantification enables the precise evaluation of phytopathogen infection severity, and continuous pathogen load monitoring provides data-driven insights for optimizing field pesticide application strategies. Relative quantification compares Ct values between target and reference genes to evaluate differential gene expression. Although this method is suitable for determining expression differences among samples, it requires comparable amplification efficiencies between target and reference genes to ensure validity. Compared to conventional PCR, qPCR enables direct target quantification without post-amplification analysis, significantly increasing the detection speed while minimizing contamination risks through closed-tube detection. It also offers a broad dynamic range, high reproducibility, and compatibility with multiplex and high-throughput detection. However, its quantitative accuracy depends on amplification efficiency, standard curves or reference genes, and DNA quality, and may be affected by inhibitors in complex plant or soil samples. These advantages have driven the widespread adoption of qPCR in phytopathology research, particularly for quantifying fungal pathogens, including Rhizoctonia solani (potato black scurf) (Salamone and Okubara 2020), Diaporthe aspalathi (soybean stem canker) (Xu et al. 2022), Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (wheat stripe rust) (Liu et al. 2022b, a), and Didymella segeticola (tea leaf spot and leaf blight) (Zhang et al. 2024c).

Multiplex qPCR (mqPCR) enables the simultaneous quantification of multiple targets using spectrally distinct fluorescent probes. For instance, Ahmed et al. (2020) developed an mqPCR assay to detect four major pathogenic fungi on citrus and demonstrated its high specificity and reproducibility, with a LOD of approximately 200 copies/μL. Comparative analysis of 103 citrus samples using both mPCR and mqPCR showed complete agreement (100%) in the detection outcomes. This approach facilitates pathogen diagnosis in samples with ambiguous symptoms or latent infections and reduces the detection time and costs. Similarly, Zhu et al. (2023) established a triplex qPCR system to detect Lonicera japonica pathogens causing brown spot, powdery mildew, and anthracnose. The use of FAM-, JOE-, and ROX-labeled probes demonstrated 100-fold higher sensitivity than conventional PCR (LOD: 2.94 × 102 copies/μL). The system demonstrated rapid and accurate pathogen differentiation when applied to 15 infected honeysuckle leaf samples. Romero-Cuadrado et al. (2024) developed a triplex real-time qPCR system for the simultaneous detection of Botryosphaeria dothidea, Neofusicoccum parvum, and fungi within the Botryosphaeriaceae family. This technique enables highly sensitive quantification of these pathogens in woody crops, nursery propagation materials, and environmental samples (such as soil and rainwater). However, multiplex qPCR requires stringent primer–probe design to prevent cross-reactivity and primer dimer formation. Additionally, template competition can suppress the amplification efficiency, affecting accuracy.

Digital PCR

As a third-generation PCR technology, dPCR is used for absolute quantification of nucleic acid based on reaction partitioning and Poisson statistics, without reliance on external standard curves. Commercial dPCR platforms are categorized as follows: (1) droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), which uses water-in-oil droplet generation; (2) microchamber digital PCR (mdPCR), based on microwell chip arrays; and (3) microfluidic chip digital PCR (mcdPCR), which is controlled by precision fluidics. dPCR enables single-molecule amplification by distributing templates across thousands of discrete reactions. Post-amplification endpoint fluorescence detection combined with Poisson distribution analysis enables the precise quantification of target copy numbers. More rigorously, absolute concentration estimates in dPCR still depend on accurate partition volume definition and appropriate statistical analysis. Compared to conventional molecular detection techniques, dPCR offers superior sensitivity, provides absolute quantification without standard curves, exhibits greater tolerance to PCR inhibitors, and has robust performance across diverse samples. It also reduces dependence on amplification efficiency and standard curve construction, although its application is constrained by high instrument and consumable costs, specialized data analysis requirements, and limited field portability. Dreo et al. (2014) first applied ddPCR to detect and quantify two quarantine phytopathogens, Erwinia amylovora and Ralstonia solanacearum, infecting Rosaceae and Solanaceae species. Subsequent studies expanded dPCR applications in plant disease diagnostics. For example, Xu et al. (2020) developed a rapid dPCR assay for Tilletia laevis quantification in wheat and achieved a LOD of 30 fg/μL, which is significantly lower than that observed using ScAR marker and qPCR methods. Lovera et al. (2024) first applied ddPCR to detect Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4 (Foc TR4), a devastating pathogen in banana. The assay demonstrated high sensitivity, with a LOD one order of magnitude lower than that of qPCR and conventional PCR, and enabled direct pathogen detection from macerated plant tissues and environmental samples such as soil and water without prior DNA extraction.

Multiplex dPCR overcomes amplification competition between template molecules by physically partitioning reaction compartments, enabling precise quantification of individual targets and partially mitigating the cost limitations of dPCR platforms. For example, Larrabee et al. (2021) concurrently quantified Alternaria spp. and Botrytis cinerea in sweet cherry at different growth stages using duplex ddPCR, achieving a LOD of 0.1 pg. Their findings revealed that pathogen quantities were lowest at petal fall and highest during the late season. Abdelrazig et al. (2022) designed species-specific primers targeting the actin gene to detect Colletotrichum truncatum and C. scovillei, the causal agents of chili anthracnose. The duplex ddPCR assay achieved a LOD 10,000-fold lower than that of conventional PCR. Morcia et al. (2024) developed a duplex cdPCR assay for the precise quantification and identification of Botrytis cinerea in tomato. By simultaneously amplifying both the pathogen and tomato host-specific genes, this method enables highly sensitive absolute quantification of fungal load within plant tissues. While dPCR offers significant advantages in sensitivity and quantification, its reliance on specialized instrumentation limits its field application.

Isothermal amplification techniques

In addition to LAMP and RPA, isothermal amplification also includes other formats, such as helicase-dependent amplification (HDA) and smart amplification process (SMART-AMP). However, compared with LAMP and RPA, these methods have been less frequently reported in phytopathogen detection, particularly for field-deployable fungal diagnostics. Current applications and methodological optimization in this area remain dominated by LAMP and RPA, which therefore are the main focus of this review.

Loop-mediated isothermal amplification

Developed by Notomi (2000), LAMP is an isothermal nucleic acid amplification technique that targets six specific regions of the target gene. This method amplifies nucleic acids at 60–65 °C using two inner primers (FIP/BIP), two outer primers (F3/B3), and Bst DNA polymerase with strand displacement activity. The reaction generates characteristic nucleic acid structures, such as stem loops or cauliflower-like configurations. The technique offers several key advantages for phytopathogen detection: (1) high specificity due to 4–6 primer binding sites per target; (2) visualization of detection results; (3) requirement for only basic heating equipment (no thermal cycler needed); and (4) suitability for field-deployable phytopathogenic fungus detection. LAMP has been successfully adapted to detect diverse phytopathogenic fungi, including Botrytis cinerea in grape (Si Ammour et al. 2020), Coniella granati causing pomegranate shoot blight (Yang et al. 2020), toxigenic Fusarium temperatum in maize residues (Shan et al. 2019), and Botryosphaeria dothidea causing apple ring rot (Wang et al. 2020a, b).

A significant advantage of LAMP is its superior sensitivity, often achieving detection at the fg/μL level, which surpasses conventional PCR. Wang et al. (2021b, a) developed LAMP primers to detect Botryosphaeria dothidea, which causes soft rot, in kiwifruit at 10 fg/μL, with field test results fully consistent with traditional isolation methods. Marimuthu et al. (2020) established a Ces gene-based LAMP assay to detect Plasmopara viticola in grapevines, achieving a LOD of 20 fg/μL. Wu et al. (2024) developed a LAMP assay targeting the ITS region of Puccinia melanocephala, with amplification results visualized via SYBR Green I-based colorimetric assay and gel electrophoresis. The established method exhibited a detection sensitivity of up to 1 pg/μL, which was comparable to nested PCR and approximately 100-fold higher than that of conventional PCR.

The robustness of LAMP is further evidenced by its high tolerance to common inhibitors present in plant tissues, making it particularly suitable for integration with rapid DNA extraction methods to enable on-site detection of plant pathogens. Zou et al. (2024) developed a filter-disc extraction-based LAMP (FDE-LAMP) system for Colletotrichum siamense, which incorporates a 30-s DNA extraction step and requires no sophisticated instruments. This method achieved a detection sensitivity of 1 pg/μL, representing a tenfold improvement over conventional PCR. Furthermore, Megariti et al. (2024) established a LAMP assay capable of directly detecting Verticillium dahliae in crude woody samples from naturally infected olive trees, even at very low pathogen levels, whereas nested PCR and SYBR qPCR failed to detect it in unpurified samples.

To enhance amplification efficiency, the addition of loop primers can accelerate LAMP reactions (Nagamine et al. 2002). For instance, Wang et al. (2020a, b) optimized their LAMP assay for F. proliferatum (maize ear rot pathogen) by introducing two loop primers, enabling the detection of 10 pg/μL target DNA within 30 min at 62 °C. Similarly, Li et al. (2022) improved the detection protocol for citrus Huanglongbing by incorporating both loop and stem primers, achieving visible results within 30 min. Despite these advantages, LAMP has limitations, including stringent primer design requirements and high sensitivity, which increases the risk of false positives due to aerosol contamination. These technical challenges warrant careful consideration when implementing LAMP-based diagnostic systems.

Recombinase polymerase amplification

First described by Piepenburg et al. (2006), RPA is a nucleic acid amplification technique performed under isothermal conditions. Recombinase proteins bind to RPA primers during the reaction, forming complexes that scan dsDNA for homologous sequences. Upon target recognition, the complex is inserted into the template chain to form a D-ring structure and start the chain replacement reaction. The recombinase unwinds double-stranded DNA and mediates primer hybridization. Single-stranded DNA-binding proteins (SSBs) stabilize the displaced strand, preventing reannealing of the complementary DNA. Subsequently, the recombinase dissociates from the primer, enabling the Bsu DNA polymerase to bind to the 3′ end of the primer and initiate the synthesis of a new complementary strand. This cycle repeats, achieving exponential target sequence amplification. Agarose gel electrophoresis requires the purification of amplification products prior to detection. Real-time fluorescence methods employ fluorescently labeled probes and portable detection devices for continuous monitoring. RPA combined with detection methods such as lateral flow assays (LFA) or fluorescent reporters (using a portable UV lamp) enables the easy visualization of results without additional processing (Lu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). As demonstrated by Zhao et al. (2024) for detecting Setophoma terrestris, the results can be easily interpreted either by observing bright green fluorescence under a portable blue light or by reading red bands on a lateral flow strip within 40 min.

RPA has emerged as a promising nucleic acid amplification technology due to its operational simplicity, rapid reaction time (typically 10–30 min), constant low operating temperature (37–42 °C), high detection efficiency in crude extracts, and compatibility with portable devices without requiring thermocycling equipment. Considering these advantages, RPA has been successfully used for the early stage detection of phytopathogenic fungi, including Phytophthora nicotianae (tobacco black shank pathogen) (Yang et al. 2022a, b) and Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. fragariae (strawberry Fusarium wilt pathogen) (Burkhardt et al. 2019). RPA exhibits high specificity and sensitivity for phytopathogenic fungus detection. For instance, a comparative study utilizing both TaqMan qPCR and RPA assays to detect the spinach downy mildew pathogen (Peronospora effusa) achieved detection thresholds of 100 and 900 fg/μL, respectively. Both methods successfully detected the pathogen in presymptomatic leaves, with RPA detecting infections as early as 5 days before the appearance of symptoms, whereas qPCR detected the pathogen after 24 h of plant exposure to airborne spores (Clark et al. 2022). Tang et al. (2023) optimized an RPA-LFD assay for potato late blight pathogen (Phytophthora infestans) detection, achieving a LOD of 5 × 10−4 fg DNA in 25 min at 37 °C, which was better than that reported for molecular detection assays. Symptomatic leaves were detected in 10 min, but extending amplification to 20 min enabled the detection of most latent infections.

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 12a

Technology based on CRISPR and CRISPR-associated proteins (e.g., Cas12 and Cas13) have recently been shown to be effective for specific nucleic acid fragments detection. Chen et al. (2018) demonstrated that the crRNA-Cas12a complex binds to target nucleic acid and results in robust indiscriminate single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) cleavage activity. Leveraging this mechanism, they combined isothermal amplification with target-dependent Cas12a ssDNase activation, developing the DNA Endonuclease-Targeted CRISPR Trans Reporter (DETECTR) platform, which provides a simple platform for nucleic acid-based, point-of-care diagnostics. This platform integrates isothermal amplification with Cas12a activation for the detection of phytopathogens in the field (Shin et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2023; Changtor et al. 2023). For instance, Sun et al. (2024) established an RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a system to identify Diaporthe aspalathi and D. caulivora in soybean, achieving a total time from DNA extraction to detection of ≤ 60 min. The results are visualized using lateral flow strips or portable flashlights with a blue light (450 nm). Fang et al. (2024) combined LAMP with CRISPR/Cas12a to develop a detection method for Verticillium dahlia (cotton verticillium wilt) in the soil, completing fluorescence-based detection in less than 90 min and lateral flow strip readouts in less than 120 min using crude DNA extracts, achieving a LOD of 10 or 1 fg/μL genomic DNA per reaction.

Transferring amplification products in RPA/LAMP-CRISPR assays increases the risks of aerosol contamination and false positives and reduces the specificity. A one-pot detection method that integrates nucleic acid amplification and CRISPR detection into a single reaction can obviate these problems. However, it suffers from poor detection efficiency due to the loss of amplification templates elicited by CRISPR/Cas12 cleavage. To resolve this, Lin et al. (2022) demonstrated that one-pot RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a reactions with glycerol promoted amplification efficiency, achieving a sensitivity comparable to the canonical two-step method. Lu et al. (2022) optimized the sensitivity of the one-pot assay using crRNAs that target substrates with suboptimal protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs), which reduced the Cas12a binding affinity and attenuated cleavage activity and thus promoted the balance shift to isothermal amplification. Additionally, photo-controlled CRISPR detection technology (Hu et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2022) and spatial compartmentalization (physical separation of reaction components) (Tian et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2022b, a; Li et al. 2024) have also proven effective in resolving the incompatibility between amplification and CRISPR detection, enabling one-pot systems.

Discussion

Due to their high specificity and sensitivity, molecular detection technologies have become essential tools for diagnosing phytopathogenic fungi. Conventional PCR-based methodologies, including nPCR and mPCR, remain widely utilized in laboratory settings for the identification of phytopathogenic fungi. Advanced iterations, such as qPCR and dPCR, offer enhanced analytical precision, particularly for early stage pathogen detection. However, their reliance on specialized laboratory infrastructure limits both their field deployment and large-scale screening in resource-limited environments. In contrast, isothermal amplification techniques, such as LAMP and RPA, enable rapid nucleic acid amplification and detection without thermocycling equipment, making them ideal for on-site rapid detection. The integration of CRISPR/Cas12a systems has expanded molecular detection capabilities. Coupling isothermal amplification techniques, such as RPA or LAMP, with CRISPR/Cas12a systems has enhanced specificity via crRNA-guided target recognition. This integrated approach has significant potential for the detection of phytopathogenic fungi, particularly in scenarios requiring rapid and precise identification. When selecting molecular detection platforms, a comprehensive evaluation of the technical advantages, limitations, and application-specific requirements (summarized in Table 2) is essential for determining optimal methodologies.

In addition to amplification-based assays, sequencing-based nucleic acid detection technologies are becoming important complementary tools for phytopathogenic fungi. Next-generation sequencing (NGS), including amplicon sequencing, metabarcoding, and metagenomic sequencing, enables culture-independent detection and can improve the identification of mixed infections or unculturable microorganisms directly from plant or environmental samples (Venbrux et al. 2023; Cubero et al. 2024; Ogbuji and Agogbua 2025). DNA barcoding, most commonly based on the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and supplemented when necessary with additional loci, remains useful for rapid species-level identification, although its discriminatory power may be limited for closely related taxa (Choudhary et al. 2021; Feau et al. 2023). Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) provides higher resolution for species delimitation, population structure analysis, virulence-associated gene mining, and fungicide-resistance surveillance, and is expected to increasingly complement nucleic acid amplification-based diagnostics in future phytopathogen detection and epidemiological monitoring (Cubero et al. 2024; Ogbuji and Agogbua 2025).

Looking ahead, the development of molecular diagnostics for plant pathogenic fungi will increasingly focus on the integration and systematization of technologies. A notable trend is the creation of integrated “sample-in-answer-out” detection systems. For instance, combining rapid nucleic acid extraction (e.g., using filter paper methods), isothermal amplification, CRISPR/Cas12a recognition, and readout methods such as lateral flow strips or smartphone-based fluorescence detection can lead to the development of portable, user-friendly platforms for field use. Furthermore, integrating these processes into microfluidic chips can automate and miniaturize the detection process, significantly increasing throughput and efficiency.

The core value of these technological advancements lies in their deep integration into modern Integrated Pest Management (IPM) systems. They serve not only as diagnostic tools but also as crucial sources of data for decision-making systems. For example, using qPCR or dPCR to accurately quantify pathogen loads during the latent infection period provides a scientific basis for precisely timing fungicide applications, thereby reducing chemical usage and delaying the development of resistance. Simultaneously, combining field and environmental pathogen monitoring data (e.g., from air or soil samples) with meteorological information facilitates the construction of disease prediction models, promoting a shift from reactive responses to proactive prevention. Additionally, these technologies play significant roles in assessing the effectiveness of IPM strategies, such as screening for latent infections in resistant plant varieties or tracking pathogen population dynamics following the application of biological control agents. Ultimately, they contribute to forming a sustainable crop health management loop encompassing detection, early warning, and precise intervention.

Despite these advancements, challenges persist, such as primer design complexity and aerosol contamination risks. To address sensitivity limitations in one-pot RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assays, Lin et al. (2022) demonstrated that adding glycerol mitigated CRISPR interference and maintained amplification efficiency. Concurrently, technological innovation continues to prioritize enhanced sensitivity, speed, and simplicity. For instance, dPCR achieves ultra-low nucleic acid detection limits, enabling earlier pathogen identification Lovera et al. (2024). Interdisciplinary advances in fields, such as nanotechnology and bioinformatics, are expected to address these limitations, enabling the development of next-generation diagnostics for phytopathogenic fungi. These advancements will improve the detection accuracy and transform plant disease management through predictive monitoring and precision interventions.
