Risk of Non‐Fatal Self‐Injury Among Adolescent and Young Adult Survivors of Cancer: A Population‐Based Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Jingxuan He, Paul C Nathan, Sumit Gupta, Rinku Sutradhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA diagnosis of cancer in an adolescent or young adult (AYA) can result in prolonged psychological distress, increasing the risk of depression and self‐harm. While non‐fatal self‐injury (NFSI) rates shortly after diagnosis have been studied, little is known about these risks in long‐term survivors of AYA cancer. This study examined the risk of NFSI among survivors of AYA cancer compared with matched cancer‐free controls in Ontario, Canada.MethodsThis retrospective population‐wide matched cohort study included all 5‐year survivors of a first diagnosis of cancer between January 1, 1997, and December 31, 2016, who were aged 15–39 years at the time of primary diagnosis. They were 1:5 matched to cancer‐free controls based on birth year, sex, and forward sortation area. To examine the association between survivorship and the risk of NFSI, adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models were implemented, accounting for the matched design.ResultsThe cohort consisted of 48,374 survivors and 241,870 matched cancer‐free controls, with a median age at diagnosis of 33 years. After multivariable adjustment, the risk of NFSI was significantly higher among survivors than their corresponding matched controls within the first 5 years after reaching survivorship (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.01–1.31; p = 0.029); no significant risk difference was found beyond 5 years.ConclusionAYA cancer survivors experienced increased risks of NFSI over the initial 5 years of follow‐up. These findings highlight the importance of initiating long‐term psychosocial surveillance and mental health support as early as possible in survivorship or even during cancer treatment.Keywords: adolescent or young adult cancer, cancer survivorship, cox proportional hazards regression models, non‐fatal self‐injury, population‐based matched cohort study

Introduction

A diagnosis of cancer can result in enormous emotional and psychological distress [1]. Fear of death, disability, and disfigurement can lead to chronic stress and increase the risk of depression and self‐harming behaviors [2, 3]. These mental health issues may negatively impact quality of life, potentially leading to higher utilization of healthcare services [3, 4].

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs), defined as individuals aged 15–39 years, represent a distinct group within the cancer population. AYAs often face unique medical, psychological, and social challenges given their unique stage in the life course [5]. In 2023, approximately 9200 AYAs were diagnosed with cancer in Canada, highlighting the need for research specific to this population [6]. Over the past few decades, the number of AYA cancer survivors has increased, with over 80% surviving more than 5 years post‐diagnosis [7]. However, survivors may face persistent physical and emotional late effects related to cancer and its treatment, which can contribute to long‐term psychological distress [8].

Previous studies that examined non‐fatal self‐injury (NFSI) among adults aged 18 years or older showed that NFSI rates were higher during the first year following cancer diagnosis compared to cancer‐free controls [9]. While some research has explored the incidence of NFSI among cancer patients in the early years following diagnosis, little is known about the long‐term risk, particularly among survivors of AYA cancer. This study aimed to fill that gap by evaluating the risk of NFSI in a population‐based cohort of AYA cancer survivors who survived at least 5 years after diagnosis, compared to matched cancer‐free controls in Ontario, Canada.

Materials and Methods

Databases

This study utilized multiple population‐based administrative and clinical databases to identify exposures, outcomes, and covariates. The Ontario Cancer Registry (OCR) is a provincial database that captures the information about all Ontario residents diagnosed with cancer [10]. The Pediatric Oncology Group of Ontario Networked Information System (POGONIS) database contains detailed clinical information on patients diagnosed with cancer before 18 years old at any of five specialized childhood cancer programs across Ontario [11]. The Cancer Activity Level Reporting (ALR) data includes patient‐level details about radiation and systemic therapy services, as well as outpatient oncology clinic visits. ALR was developed by Cancer Care Ontario to collect data from various cancer centers across Ontario [12]. The Registered Persons Database (RPDB) contains demographic information for all individuals covered by the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) and who are eligible for the Ontario Drug Program [13]. The Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) Permanent Resident database provides immigration and citizenship‐related information of permanent and temporary residents, allowing identification of immigrant status [14]. The Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS) contains data on day surgeries, outpatient and community‐based clinic visits, and emergency department visits for hospital‐based and community‐based ambulatory care [15]. The CIHI Discharge Abstract Database (DAD) captures hospital admission, clinical, and discharge information [16]. Lastly, the CIHI Ontario Mental Health Reporting System (OMHRS) includes information about mental and physical health service use and social supports [17]. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at the Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre and the Hospital for Sick Children, which waived the requirement for informed consent. Datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES (previously known as the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences).

Study Population

This was a retrospective matched cohort study that included all AYA cancer survivors with first diagnosis of cancer between January 1, 1997, and December 31, 2016, who were aged 15–39 years at the time of primary diagnosis. All patients were first identified via the OCR or POGONIS based on their date of cancer diagnosis. An eligible survivor was defined as an individual who had survived at least 5 years following their primary cancer diagnosis date. This 5‐year mark served as the index date (time 0).

Each survivor was hard‐matched to five individuals without any history of cancer (controls) using the RPDB based on birth year, sex, and forward sortation area. Controls had to be alive at the corresponding survivor's index date, which was assigned as the control's dummy index date.

Outcome Definition

Survivors and controls were deterministically linked to Ontario health administrative databases housed at ICES. The primary outcome was the time to the emergency department (ED) presentation for a NFSI event defined as a self‐harm emergency due to medications, alcohol, toxic chemicals, or physical injury. These events were identified using ICD‐10‐CA codes for intentional self‐harm (X60–X84) or self‐harm of undetermined intent (Y10–Y19, Y28) in NACRS [18, 19, 20, 21].

Primary Exposure and Covariates

The primary exposure was cancer survivorship, defined as a binary baseline variable indicating whether an individual was a cancer survivor or a cancer‐free control. Demographic variables included sex, age at index date (continuous), socioeconomic status, and immigration status. Socioeconomic status was measured using the Nearest Census Based Neighborhood Income Quintile from RPBD, which had 6 categories: rural, urban Q1 (lowest), urban Q2, urban Q3, urban Q4, and urban Q5 (highest). Immigration status from IRCC was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no) indicating whether the individual was an immigrant to Canada. Pre‐diagnosis mental health visits (either to the ED or hospitalizations) were captured as covariates, defined as a binary variable (yes/no) of any such visits between 6 months and 5 years prior to the primary cancer diagnosis. The mental health and addictions (MHA)‐related ED visits were captured in the NACRS using ICD‐10‐CA, and MHA‐related hospitalization records were from the DAD based on ICD‐10‐CA and the OMHRS using Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th and 5th Edition (DSM‐IV and DSM 5) standard [22, 23, 24].

Survivor‐Specific Covariates

To further explore factors associated with NFSI within the survivor cohort only, additional disease‐ and treatment‐related variables were extracted from OCR, ALR, and POGONIS. The pre‐diagnosis mental health visits (ED visits, hospitalizations, and outpatient services) here were defined as the total number of such visits between 6 months and 5 years before cancer diagnosis. The MHA‐related outpatient services provided by psychiatrists, family physicians/general practitioners, and pediatricians in outpatient settings (office, home, long‐term care) were captured using OHIP variables of physician specialty, diagnosis code, and fee code [25]. The treatment era was categorized into early (1997–2003), middle (2004–2010), and late (2011–2016) periods. Cancer type was classified into three groups: leukemias/lymphomas, brain tumors, and solid tumors. The locus of cancer care was categorized as Regional Cancer Center (RCC), community hospital, or pediatric center. Cancer stage at diagnosis was treated as a categorical variable (stages 1–4). Additional binary variables included receipt of chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery.

Statistical Analyses

Distributions of baseline characteristics among survivors and matched controls were compared using standardized mean differences (SMD), where a value < 0.1 indicated distributional balance. Time to non‐fatal self‐injury was measured from the index date (5‐year mark) onward, with individuals censored at the earliest occurrence of death, any cancer occurrence (cancer recurrence, second malignancy, new cancer diagnosis), loss of Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) eligibility, or reaching the maximum follow‐up date (December 31, 2023).

A cumulative incidence function to non‐parametrically estimate and compare the risk of non‐fatal self‐injury over time among survivors and controls was plotted, along with 95% confidence intervals. As there was evidence of convergence in NFSI risk after 5 years of follow‐up, we elected to focus on outcomes within the first 5 years following index. To analyze the association between cancer survivorship and the hazard of NFSI, Cox proportional hazards regression models were implemented. In addition to univariate models, a multivariable regression model was employed to adjust for baseline covariate imbalances. A robust sandwich variance estimation was applied to account for the matched study design. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.1.2). All statistical tests were two‐sided, with significance defined as p < 0.05.

Age‐Stratified Analysis

As secondary analyses, we also wanted to examine whether the association between cancer survivorship and NFSI varied by age at diagnosis. We therefore re‐ran all analyses stratified by age at diagnosis, using the median age of 33 years as the cut‐off. In the absence of a pre‐specified, clinically established threshold, the median was selected as a data‐driven cut point to provide balanced strata and more stable statistical estimates without relying on outcome data for cut point optimization. As survivors and controls were originally hard‐matched on birth year, we were able to retain all individuals within a matched set during this stratification.

Survivors‐Only Analysis

To further examine factors associated with NFSI risk among survivors only, we implemented multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models using the additional survivor‐specific measures captured from the OCR, ALR, and POGONIS databases. We assessed for multicollinearity using variance inflation factors, and no concerns were found.

Age‐Stratified Analyses

The analyses were further stratified by age at diagnosis, dividing individuals into one of two strata: 33 years or younger, and above 33 years. Figure 2 illustrates the stratified estimated cumulative incidence functions of NFSI. Among matched individuals 33 years or younger, the risk of NFSI was consistently higher among survivors compared to controls; however, no significant differences were seen in the older matched cohort. Furthermore, after running the multivariable Cox regression model among those 33 years or younger at diagnosis, the risk of NFSI was significantly higher among survivors compared with cancer‐free controls (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.08 to 1.49; p = 0.003). In contrast, among those older than 33 years at diagnosis, there was no significant difference between survivors and controls (p = 0.86).

Survivors‐Only Analysis

In the multivariable analysis among survivors only (Table 3), older age at cancer (HR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.94 to 0.98; p = 0.001), and being an immigrant (HR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.38 to 0.82; p = 0.003) were significantly associated with a decreased risk of NFSI. Residing in rural or lower‐income (Q1 and Q2) urban areas contributed to a significantly increased risk of NFSI compared to the highest income (Q5) urban areas. Previous mental health visits (outpatients, hospitalizations, and ED visits) occurring prior to cancer diagnosis were associated with higher NFSI risk, with prior hospitalizations demonstrating the strongest association (HR, 1.23; 95% CI, 1.18 to 1.29; p < 0.001).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates a significantly higher risk of NFSI among AYA survivors during the first 5 years after reaching survivorship (defined as 5 years from primary diagnosis), compared to matched cancer‐free controls. This difference in NFSI risk did not persist after 5 years. Nguyen et al. found that individuals with cancer had a 17% increased risk of NFSI within the first year after diagnosis compared to controls, underscoring the psychological distress associated with a cancer experience [9]. Our study extends these findings by focusing on NFSI among survivors of cancer, who are free of cancer recurrence, second malignancy, or new cancer diagnosis. These findings are consistent with prior literature emphasizing that psychiatric disorders remain a critical issue in long‐term AYA survivors [26, 27, 28, 29]. It highlights the ongoing mental health challenges that persist beyond the initial cancer period.

Notably, although relative hazards for NFSI were elevated for survivors compared to the matched cancer‐free population, the absolute risk remained low. NFSI behavior may only represent the tip of the iceberg of psychological distress. Small increases in NFSI likely correlate with larger clinical increases in depression, anxiety, and mental‐health service needs among people with cancer and survivors. A recent study observed that Dutch‐speaking survivors with a history of non‐suicidal self‐injury experienced more depressive symptoms than those without NFSI [30]. Another systematic review of mental health outcomes among AYA cancer survivors revealed that 58% of the studies reported poorer mental health outcomes among AYA cancer survivors compared to peers without cancer [31]. Our findings support the recommendations in the guidelines for survivors of childhood and AYA cancers, which call for awareness of potential late effects and emphasize the importance of routine long‐term follow‐up care to monitor the psychological health of AYA survivors [32].

The differences in NFSI risk between survivors and controls within the first 5 years of survivorship are driven by the younger population, as shown in the age‐stratified analyses. This age‐related disparity echoes findings from previous research that demonstrated that younger AYA survivors are more likely to experience depression and suicide, which may shape their key developmental life milestones such as education, employment, and relationship formation [33]. These results illustrate why there is an urgent need for age‐specific mental health interventions that help survivors recover from these disruptions early in their lives. Overall, our research findings confirm that mental health services need to become integral components of survivorship care. Clinical interventions such as routine psychosocial screening for early detection of high‐risk patients, along with mental health support that matches the survivors' age group and cultural background, should be incorporated into survivorship planning. Such approaches may help mitigate the risk of self‐injury and promote improved quality of life for AYA cancer survivors.

In the survivor‐only analyses, we identified that age, immigration status, socioeconomic status, and prior mental health visits were the most significant factors associated with NFSI. This is in line with a recent population‐based study in Ontario, Canada, which highlighted the importance of age, prior self‐injury, and the history of severe psychiatric illness related to NFSI within 5 years of a cancer diagnosis [34]. Interestingly, socioeconomic and immigrant‐related disparities were found in our analysis. Immigrant survivors demonstrated a significantly lower risk of NFSI, which aligns with the findings described in the broader suicide and self‐harm literature for recent immigrants in Ontario [35]. To our knowledge, research specifically addressing non‐fatal self‐harm rates among immigrant AYA cancer survivors is limited. Rural and lower‐income urban survivors were at an increased risk of NFSI compared to the highest income urban population. Similar associations have been observed among 5‐year AYA cancer survivors for severe psychiatric outcomes [26]. These patterns underscore the need for survivorship supports tailored to local socioeconomic status, even when average risk among immigrants appears lower.

This study had several strengths. It used a population‐based cohort study using individual‐level health administrative data, allowing for generalizability across the Ontario AYA population. The 1:5 matched exposure‐control design ensured comparability between survivors and controls on key sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. The individuals with any indication of cancer occurrence were censored to ensure that NFSI events were only captured during cancer‐free periods. Despite these strengths, the results of the study should be interpreted with caution due to some limitations. First, the outcome only involves ED visits for non‐fatal self‐injury. Excluding other types of mental health care utilization may underestimate the true incidence of NFSI. As emergency services require higher costs, individuals with low income may experience different barriers to accessing mental health care. Second, as our analysis is based on the AYAs with OHIP eligibility, findings may not apply to populations without universal health insurance. Lastly, although the regression models were adjusted for confounding variables, unmeasured confounders such as family history of mental illness or mental health care received during cancer treatment periods were not captured.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this Ontario‐wide cohort study emphasizes that AYA cancer survivors are at significantly elevated risk of non‐fatal self‐injury compared with similar individuals without a cancer diagnosis within the first 5 years following survivorship. This study reinforces the need for long‐term psychosocial surveillance and mental health support in cancer survivorship care. Survivorship care needs to be strengthened through targeted mental health interventions that focus on early survivorship care for young survivors to minimize long‐term psychological harm and boost quality of life.
