Prevalence, determinants, and sociocultural practices of traditional uvulectomy among neonates in Gondar City, Ethiopia: a community-based mixed-methods study
Authors: Addisu Ginbu, Mehretie Kokeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional uvulectomy (TU) is a culturally rooted practice performed on neonates in parts of Ethiopia despite its associated health risks. Evidence on its prevalence and determinants in Gondar City remains limited. This study assessed the prevalence, practices, and determinants of TU among neonates aged < 28 days in selected communities of Gondar City, Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study using quantitative and qualitative approaches was conducted in 12 kebeles of Gondar City (5 rural and 7 urban). A total of 633 neonates aged < 28 days and their mothers or caregivers were included. Quantitative data were collected using structured interviewer-administered questionnaires, and qualitative data through focus group discussions with mothers and traditional healers. TU prevalence was summarized using descriptive statistics. Multiparity was associated with lower odds of TU (AOR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.26–0.61). Mothers who did not receive antenatal counselling had higher odds of practicing TU, while ANC counselling was protective (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.09–0.24). Home delivery increased the likelihood of TU (AOR = 5.86; 95% CI: 2.51–13.68). Qualitative findings showed that TU was viewed as a safe cultural practice in the community.ConclusionTraditional uvulectomy in neonates remains highly prevalent in Gondar City, driven by cultural norms, family influence, and access to traditional healers. Background

Traditional uvulectomy (TU) is a long-standing cultural practice performed in several African countries, including Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Tanzania. It is commonly carried out on neonates and young children with the intention of preventing uvular swelling, throat infections, and other locally perceived illnesses [1, 2]. Despite these perceived benefits, TU is associated with serious health risks, including bleeding, infection, aspiration, and voice changes, some of which may be life-threatening [6].

In the present community-based study conducted in Gondar City, 17.8% of neonates who underwent TU experienced at least one complication, with bleeding (38.9%), infection (34.8%), voice changes (18.9%), and aspiration (7.4%) being the most commonly reported. These findings underscore the potential harm associated with the practice, particularly among neonates, who are highly vulnerable to adverse outcomes.

In Ethiopia, the prevalence of TU varies widely across regions, ranging from approximately 45% to over 80% [4, 5]. The practice is deeply rooted in cultural beliefs, family traditions, and community norms [8, 9], and is typically performed at home by traditional healers who are widely trusted for their indigenous knowledge. Families often prefer TU over formal healthcare services due to its lower cost, accessibility, and perceived effectiveness [11].

Previous studies have identified several factors associated with TU practice, including maternal parity, home delivery, limited antenatal care (ANC) counselling, and strong socio-cultural influences [12, 16–17]. Although some caregivers are aware of the potential risks, decision-making is often shaped by cultural expectations and economic considerations, which may outweigh biomedical knowledge [3, 7].

Despite its high prevalence, evidence on TU among neonates in Gondar City remains limited, as most studies have focused on under-five children or broader geographic areas [12, 20]. Given the increased vulnerability of neonates to complications, there is a critical need to better understand the magnitude, determinants, and socio-cultural context of TU in this population. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of traditional uvulectomy among neonates (aged < 28 days), identify its determinants, and explore cultural and community perceptions of the practice among mothers and traditional healers in Gondar City, Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area and setting

The study was conducted in Gondar City, located in the Central Gondar Zone of the Amhara Regional State in north-western Ethiopia, approximately 727 km from Addis Ababa. Gondar is a historically and culturally significant city, renowned for its heritage and UNESCO-recognized sites, including Fasil Ghebbi (Fasilides’ Castle).

Administratively, the city is divided into six sub-cities and 36 kebeles (25 urban and 11 rural). Each kebele is served by health extension workers, who maintain updated household and maternal–child health records, providing an important foundation for community-based health research and service delivery. The total population is estimated at 457,938, including approximately 105,000 women of reproductive age (15–49 years) and 41,623 children under five, of whom around 4,160 are infants aged 6–12 months.

Gondar City is served by one referral teaching hospital (University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital), one general hospital, eight health centres, and 14 health posts, offering comprehensive maternal, neonatal, and child health services. These characteristics make the city a suitable setting for research on maternal and child health, including neonatal practices such as traditional uvulectomy.

Study design and period

A community-based, multistage cross-sectional study was conducted among 633 mothers with neonates aged below 28 days in Gondar City over a three-month period, from July 1 to September 30, 2025.

Source population, study population, and study participants

Source population

The source population comprised all mothers residing in Gondar City who had neonates aged below 28 days during the study period.

Study population

The study population included mothers with neonates aged below 28 days residing in the 12 kebeles selected for the study (7 urban and 5 rural). Lists of eligible mothers in each selected kebele were obtained from health extension workers and served as the sampling frame.

Study participants

Study participants were mother–neonate pairs systematically selected from the sampling frame after proportional allocation of the total sample size. Only one eligible mother–neonate pair per household was included.

Inclusion criteria

Mothers residing in the selected kebeles of Gondar City with neonates aged < 28 days who were available and willing to participate.

Exclusion criteria

Mothers who were critically ill, whose neonates had congenital anomalies or severe illnesses, or who had lived in the kebeles for less than six months.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Quantitative component

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming a prevalence of 52.2% for traditional uvulectomy based on a previous study in the South Gondar Zone [23]. As no neonatal-specific prevalence data were available, this estimate from caregivers of children under five was used as a proxy, assuming cultural practices regarding uvulectomy are similar in early childhood. A 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error were applied. To account for multistage cluster sampling, a design effect of 1.5 was included, and a 10% non-response rate was added. Assuming an average cluster size of 12 participants, 53 clusters were included, resulting in a final sample size of 633 mother–neonate pairs.

Data collection tools and procedures

Quantitative data collection

Quantitative data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire developed specifically for this study. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and back-translated to English to ensure consistency and accuracy. It included sections on sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric and neonatal factors, maternal knowledge and attitudes toward traditional uvulectomy (TU), and health service–related factors. Data were collected face-to-face at participants’ homes by 24 trained data collectors under the supervision of two supervisors.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

Qualitative data were collected through focus group discussions (FGDs) to explore perceptions, experiences, and cultural beliefs related to TU. Three FGDs were conducted: two with mothers of neonates and one with traditional practitioners performing uvulectomy, each consisting of seven participants.

Participants were recruited using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling:

Mothers were purposively selected from study kebeles to ensure diversity in residence (urban vs. rural) and neonatal care experiences.

FGDs were facilitated by two trained moderators using a semi-structured discussion guide. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved, i.e., when no new themes emerged. Due to cultural sensitivity, participants declined audio recording; instead, detailed contemporaneous notes were taken independently by both moderators. Notes were immediately reviewed, compared, and expanded after each session to ensure accuracy, completeness, and consensus.

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis. Transcripts and field notes were independently coded by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Regular team meetings were held to refine codes and themes, enhancing the credibility and consistency of the findings.

Data quality assurance

Quantitative data

To ensure data quality, the questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the study population in a kebele not included in the main study. Necessary modifications were made to improve clarity, consistency, and relevance. Prior to data collection, data collectors and supervisors were trained on study objectives, interview techniques, ethical considerations, and standardized questionnaire administration.

During data collection, daily supervision was conducted, and completed questionnaires were reviewed for completeness and accuracy by supervisors. Continuous feedback and timely corrective measures were provided to data collectors. After data collection, data were checked for completeness, coded, entered and cleaned using standardized procedures, including consistency and range checks, to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Data processing and analysis

Quantitative data

Data were entered into EpiData version 3.5.4 and analysed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic, maternal, and neonatal characteristics, as well as the prevalence of traditional uvulectomy (TU).

To account for the multistage cluster sampling and the design effect of 1.5, complex samples logistic regression was performed, specifying kebeles as clusters and urban/rural residence as strata. Variables with p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariable model to identify independent determinants of TU, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Data quality checks included assessment of multicollinearity using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF < 10) and evaluation of model fit using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the Omnibus likelihood ratio test.

Qualitative data

Focus group discussion (FGD) notes and transcripts were analysed using thematic analysis. Data coding was conducted independently by two researchers, and key themes were identified through iterative review and discussion. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus, and regular team meetings were held to refine codes and ensure consistency and credibility.

Qualitative findings were triangulated with quantitative results to enhance the validity and depth of interpretation, providing a comprehensive understanding of the cultural beliefs, practices, and determinants associated with TU.

Prevalence and practices of traditional uvulectomy

Among the 633 neonates included in the study, 535 underwent traditional uvulectomy (TU), yielding a prevalence of 84.5%. The procedure was most commonly performed at home by traditional healers. The mean age at which TU was performed was 5.78 ± 5.03 days.

Neonates born to mothers aged 21–29 years had significantly higher odds of undergoing TU compared to those born to mothers aged < 20 years (AOR = 2.27; 95% CI: 1.03–5.28; p = 0.041). However, the association for mothers aged 30–39 years was not statistically significant.

Parity was significantly associated with TU. Neonates born to multiparous mothers had lower odds of undergoing TU compared to those born to primiparous mothers (AOR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.25–0.74; p = 0.002), indicating a protective effect.

Antenatal care (ANC) counselling showed a strong protective effect. Neonates whose mothers did not receive ANC counselling were significantly more likely to undergo TU, whereas those who received counselling had 85% lower odds of TU (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.09–0.24; p < 0.001).

A history of TU among older children was also a strong determinant. Neonates without such history had significantly lower odds of undergoing TU (AOR = 0.18; 95% CI: 0.09–0.37; p < 0.001), suggesting the influence of prior maternal experience.

Place of delivery was another key factor. Neonates delivered at home were nearly six times more likely to undergo TU compared to those delivered at health facilities (AOR = 5.86; 95% CI: 2.51–13.68; p = 0.001).

Interestingly, mothers who perceived healthcare as better than TU were still more likely to practice TU (AOR = 2.05; 95% CI: 1.28–3.28; p = 0.036), indicating that cultural norms and social influences may outweigh individual preferences for modern healthcare.

In contrast, knowledge that TU is harmful was not significantly associated with its practice (AOR = 1.48; 95% CI: 0.96–2.29; p = 0.079).

Overall, these findings indicate that TU practice is shaped by a combination of maternal characteristics, prior experience, healthcare utilization, and cultural perceptions, emphasizing the need for culturally sensitive and community-based interventions.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence, determinants, and sociocultural context of traditional uvulectomy (TU) among neonates in Gondar City using a mixed-methods approach. The findings indicate that TU remains highly prevalent, with more than four out of five neonates undergoing the procedure. The practice was commonly performed at home by traditional healers and influenced by maternal, healthcare-related, and sociocultural factors.

The prevalence observed in this study is consistent with previous findings from Ethiopia, including studies conducted in Debre Birhan and South Gondar, where TU prevalence among children ranged from approximately 70% to over 80% [4, 5, 20]. Similar patterns have been reported in other sub-Saharan African countries such as Nigeria and Tanzania, highlighting the widespread and persistent nature of this practice across the region [1, 6, 10]. The continued practice of TU reflects deeply embedded cultural beliefs and longstanding traditions surrounding neonatal health and illness [8, 13].

Maternal age was significantly associated with TU, with mothers aged 21–29 years more likely to practice TU compared to younger mothers. Similar associations have been reported in other Ethiopian studies, where maternal demographic characteristics influenced engagement in traditional practices [12, 17]. This may be related to differences in social roles, exposure to cultural norms, or decision-making autonomy.

Parity was inversely associated with TU, as multiparous mothers were less likely to perform the procedure. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that maternal experience and repeated exposure to maternal and child health services may reduce reliance on harmful traditional practices [12, 16]. Experienced mothers may be more likely to adopt alternative health-seeking behaviors over time.

Antenatal care (ANC) counselling was strongly associated with TU, with mothers who received counselling being less likely to practice the procedure. This is consistent with evidence indicating that ANC provides a critical opportunity to influence maternal knowledge and behaviors related to neonatal care [7, 17]. Health education delivered during ANC visits has been shown to reduce harmful traditional practices by improving awareness and promoting safer alternatives.

Place of delivery was another important determinant, with home delivery significantly associated with higher TU practice. Similar findings have been reported in other studies, where mothers delivering outside health facilities were more likely to rely on traditional practices due to limited interaction with formal healthcare systems [11, 18]. Institutional delivery, on the other hand, facilitates early counselling and access to skilled care, which may discourage harmful practices.

The history of TU among older children was also significantly associated with neonatal TU, suggesting intergenerational continuity of the practice. This finding is consistent with studies showing that cultural practices are often transmitted within families and reinforced through repeated experience [3, 8]. Such patterns highlight the influence of family norms and prior behavior on neonatal care decisions.

Interestingly, maternal knowledge that TU is harmful was not significantly associated with reduced practice. Similarly, mothers who perceived healthcare as better than TU were still more likely to practice TU. These findings are supported by previous studies indicating that knowledge alone is insufficient to change behavior in the presence of strong cultural norms and social pressures [3, 7, 19]. Decision-making is often influenced by collective beliefs, family expectations, and perceived social acceptability rather than individual knowledge.

The qualitative findings provide important context for these quantitative results. Participants described TU as an urgent and necessary intervention to prevent severe illness, reflecting local illness beliefs similar to those reported in ethnomedical studies [10, 14]. Traditional healers were widely trusted and viewed as legitimate providers due to their perceived expertise and long-standing role in the community [11, 13]. In addition, TU was described as affordable and accessible, which aligns with findings from other low-resource settings where economic factors influence healthcare choices [1, 18].

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings highlights the interplay between measurable determinants and underlying sociocultural factors. While quantitative analysis identified key predictors such as ANC counselling, place of delivery, and maternal experience, qualitative findings revealed that these are closely linked to beliefs about illness, trust in traditional healers, and cost considerations. Similar patterns have been reported in systematic reviews and ethnographic studies, which emphasize the role of cultural norms and health system accessibility in shaping neonatal care practices [8, 15, 16].

The findings of this study have important implications for intervention strategies. Efforts to reduce TU should extend beyond awareness creation and incorporate culturally sensitive approaches that engage families, community leaders, and traditional healers. Strengthening ANC counselling and promoting institutional delivery may provide effective entry points for behavioral change. These are also strongly recommended strategies worldwide [21, 22]. Furthermore, improving access to affordable and culturally acceptable healthcare services may help address the economic and trust-related barriers that sustain the practice.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

The use of a mixed-methods design integrating quantitative data on prevalence and determinants with qualitative insights from mothers and traditional healers provided a comprehensive understanding of traditional uvulectomy (TU) practices.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Self-reported data may be affected by recall or social desirability bias, despite efforts to minimize these. The high TU prevalence observed in rural areas may reflect both true practice and sampling variability. Sample size calculations relied on under-five prevalence data due to limited neonatal-specific evidence, which may slightly affect precision. Findings are specific to Gondar City and may not generalize to other settings with different cultural or healthcare contexts.

Conclusion

Traditional uvulectomy (TU) remains highly prevalent among neonates in Gondar City, particularly in rural kebeles, and is strongly influenced by maternal age, parity, antenatal care engagement, place of delivery, and cultural perceptions. Despite awareness of potential risks, caregivers often continue the practice due to perceived benefits, trust in traditional practitioners, and accessibility.

The findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive interventions that integrate maternal education, community engagement, and enhanced antenatal counselling, while respecting local traditions. Efforts to strengthen the use of formal healthcare services and address misconceptions about TU may contribute to improved neonatal health outcomes.

Although the study provides important insights into the prevalence, determinants, and community perceptions of TU, these results are specific to Gondar City and should be interpreted in the context of local cultural and healthcare practices.
