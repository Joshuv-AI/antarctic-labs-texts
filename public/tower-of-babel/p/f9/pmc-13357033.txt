Novel Recruitment, Data Collection, and Safety Reporting Methods in the Era of COVID‐19: Design of a Post‐Emergency Use Authorization COVID‐19 Vaccination Safety‐Surveillance Study
Authors: Cherise Wong, Katherine Kendrick, Laura Webb, Lauren Cohen, M Patricia McAdams, Heather Rubino, Patrick Caubel, Emily O'Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeWithin one week of the Food and Drug Administration granting the COVID‐19 vaccine Emergency Use Authorization, the HERO‐Together study launched. We describe how the study utilized novel methods for streamlined enrollment and longitudinal data collection to efficiently monitor early and long‐term safety of the COVID‐19 vaccine.MethodsHERO‐Together is a fully remote, prospective observational cohort study, conducted through an online portal. Participants were recruited from an existing online community of healthcare workers that expanded to family and community members as vaccine eligibility expanded, as well as through marketing and point‐of‐vaccination efforts. Individuals received notifications to submit data at scheduled intervals for two years, and automated reminders prompted participants with missed check‐ins, with centralized call center outreach for non‐responders.ResultsMultiple recruitment approaches were adopted, including direct outreach, national and local media campaigns, downloadable enrollment toolkits (e.g., social media), social media influencer partnerships, and pharmacy referrals. A study population of n = 19, 858 was enrolled within nine months of emergency use authorization, most self‐reporting as white, female, working in healthcare, and all for whom variable survey completion was observed.ConclusionCollectively, diverse recruitment strategies, data collection through the online portal, and electronic consent enabled rapid, decentralized enrollment of study participants in order to remotely monitor COVID‐19 vaccine safety during the early days of the pandemic. Trial Registration: EUPAS38671.Keywords: COVID‐19 vaccine, observational, recruitment, remote, safety

Introduction

In December 2019, a viral pneumonia outbreak of unknown origin was identified in Wuhan, China [1]. By January 2020, the outbreak was confirmed to be caused by a novel coronavirus named severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) [2]. The outbreak quickly reached pandemic levels, spreading to 213 countries and territories worldwide. In February 2020, the World Health Organization formally named the disease caused by SARS‐CoV‐2 the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19), subsequently declaring a global pandemic due to SARS‐CoV‐2 in March 2020 [3]. In the United States (US), by April 2020 early estimates indicated that 395 926 COVID‐19 cases across all 50 states had been identified [4], and this rose to 13.8 million cases with 272 525 deaths by the Fall of 2020, necessitating a multipronged approach to mitigating SARS‐CoV‐2 transmission [5].

There was an urgent need to stem the unprecedented tide of COVID‐19 cases and deaths. As one component of the response strategy in the US, the BNT162b2 vaccine candidate by Pfizer‐BioNTech (hereafter referred to as the Pfizer‐BioNTech COVID‐19 vaccine) received emergency use authorization (EUA) in December 2020 [6]. Although clinical trial data provided early insight into Pfizer‐BioNTech COVID‐19 vaccine safety [7], evidence generation on real‐world and longer‐term safety data was needed in broader populations, also impacted by the scale in disruption of the shelter‐in‐place policy on a national level [8]. Consistent with the goal of Operation Warp Speed, a vaccine would serve as a countermeasure to significantly shape the pandemic arc [9], but it engendered a need to rapidly advance the state of knowledge in vaccine safety in the general population.

In stride with Pfizer's Project Lightspeed to develop a COVID‐19 vaccine [10], the HERO Together study, a fully remote, prospective, observational cohort study was launched 6 days after EUA from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). During its planning, it factored in the landscape of vaccine administration outside of traditional settings, the prioritization of healthcare workers for early receipt of vaccines to prevent SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, and was complementary to the FDA‐CBER surveillance strategy in its endeavor to have a robust pharmacovigilance plan [11]. The HERO Together study adopted novel remote methods for streamlined enrolment and longitudinal data collection needed to efficiently monitor early and long‐term safety, translating its study materials to reach populations not typically included in clinical trials [12]. Leveraging existing infrastructure, operationalization of this voluntary postauthorization safety study was rapid, and safety data were read out and submitted to the FDA within 7 months of receiving EUA.

A review of study recruitment, data collection, and their procedures is described herein, for which the overarching study objective was to determine incidence rates of adverse events of special interest and other clinically significant events following receipt of the Pfizer‐BioNTech COVID‐19 vaccine, and evaluate for any excessive risk of events post‐vaccination.

Methods

Study Population

The Healthcare Worker Exposure Response and Outcomes Together (HERO Together) study was a US‐based, prospective cohort study that ran from December 2020 to June 2023. In terms of inclusion criteria, HERO‐Together initially focused on healthcare workers (including but not limited to firefighters, first responders, nurses, physicians, emergency medical services) as the sole population from whom many vaccinated persons could be recruited, largely a function of national recommendations for vaccine eligibility and the need to prioritize distribution of limited quantities of the vaccine under the EUA at the time. As vaccine supply grew sufficient to make the vaccine more widely available outside of limited occupational and age groups, the HERO‐Together study inclusion criteria were expanded to provide a comprehensive assessment of post‐vaccine outcomes in a diverse population, and included members of HCW families and members of the community. Additional eligibility criteria were that participants be a minimum of 18 years of age, could speak and read English or Spanish, had received their first dose of a COVID‐19 vaccine (no manufacturer restriction) within the past 60 days, and self‐consented to participate in the study [13].

Ultimately, three source populations contributed to the study cohort. The first source population was an existing registry study, the HERO Registry, established to characterize COVID‐19 risk factors and outcomes among US healthcare workers [14]. Briefly, HERO Registry members enrolled during April 2020 to July 2020 comprised an online community of healthcare workers followed for exposure to and diagnosis of COVID‐19, viral and antibody tests, and physical and emotional distress experiences [15, 16]. The second source population was from the Project Baseline Community Study launched in April 2019 as an observational cohort intended to monitor molecular and phenotypic variability and comprised an existing community of volunteers enrolled in an existing registry [17]. Lastly, a national retail pharmacy that distributed the vaccine per local jurisdictional vaccine rollout plans facilitated targeted diversity enrollment across the US among individuals in the community voluntarily receiving the vaccine [18].

Recruitment

The HERO Together study had a multifaceted approach to study recruitment and retention during December 17, 2020 to September 23, 2021 and for up to 24 months, respectively. In addition to the direct outreach to individuals from the HERO registry, Project Baseline Community Study, and health system at points of vaccination, additional layers of recruitment strategies generated study awareness and spanned: digital promotion through online advertising, partnerships with local health organizations and professional societies, educational presentations to institutional audiences, social media influencer partnerships, and participant‐driven outreach (e.g., tag‐a‐friend and Valentine's campaigns, “Why I joined” videos on study website). These were supported by national and local media marketing campaign efforts (e.g., radio, trade journals), social media and other advertising (e.g., Facebook Live, pod‐casts), downloadable enrollment toolkits (including a social media kit), and printed enrollment materials (English and Spanish versions of study flyers and information cards) with information about the study at vaccination sites. For national retail pharmacy efforts, individuals who recently received a first COVID‐19 vaccine dose were contacted via email containing a link that directed them to the study website to learn more and enroll.

Participants self‐enrolled into the study and could do so by one of three mechanisms. For existing members of the HERO Registry or the Project Baseline Community Study, participants could log into their existing online profile or be emailed a link to remotely complete the electronic informed consent document (eICD), advantageous as obtaining written consent during the pandemic was a challenge. Secondly, participants could enroll at the vaccination site after signing the eICD. In this scenario, study navigators were available to assist with enrollment after an individual received their COVID‐19 vaccine in the recovery area (e.g., locate and assist with data entry of vaccine date, manufacturer, lot number). Lastly, participants could opt to self‐enroll remotely from anywhere after signing the eICD. As there were no treating healthcare providers overseeing recruitment and enrollment, recruitment was not bound to any one physical site or state.

Data Collection and Retention

The exposure of interest was receipt of the COVID‐19 vaccine, regardless of manufacturer. The decision to be vaccinated was made at the discretion of the recipient.

Outcomes included all‐cause hospitalization and 41 safety events of interest based on the Priority List of Adverse Events of Special Interest from the Brighton Collaboration's Safety Platform for Emergency vACcines (SPEAC) Project [19] and Centers for Disease Control (CDC) enhanced safety monitoring recommendations [20]. Briefly, outcome categories spanned neurologic, immunologic, COVID‐19, cardiac, hematologic systems, and other (i.e., pregnancy outcomes, death, non‐anaphylactic allergic reactions, narcolepsy and cataplexy).

Efforts were made to validate all self‐reported events among study participants who provided signed medical record release forms. Initially, medical record release forms were obtained from participants at the time of study enrollment. However, post‐launch, in order to streamline the enrollment process and alleviate participant burden these forms were requested downstream, only when and if participants self‐reported an event on the study portal. When a signed medical record release was available, supporting medical records were chased for all reported safety events of interest, by the Call Center. Requests made of the facilities could either be accepted (i.e., the study‐specific IRB approved medical release forms were accepted) or declined (in which case the participant could be sent a local medical record release form for additional follow‐up). After medical records were obtained (i.e., emergency room notes, discharge/death summary, admission history, physical exam, progress/clinic/urgent care notes, diagnostic tests, lab reports, medication records), self‐reported outcomes proceeded through adjudication conducted by a Clinical Events Ascertainment team (clinicians with specialties as cardiologists, immunologists, neurologists, and others, were represented), based on Brighton Collaboration criteria.

During follow up all participants, regardless of their source cohort, received automated notifications to enter data into the secure, remote, participant‐facing web portal according to a fixed schedule of assessments (1‐, 2‐, 4‐, 8‐, 12‐weeks, and 6‐, 9‐, 12–18‐, and 24‐months; Figure 1). Participants were queried regarding their general health and outcomes requiring medical attention; data elements included demographics, medical history, medications, COVID‐19 infection, and safety events of interest, as well as quality‐of‐life assessments, well‐being, and overall health. Participants also received weekly newsletters, enrollment updates, interim report result summaries, and had access to a HERO community newsfeed within the portal providing COVID news and information (https://dcri.org/hero‐research).

Survey completion was monitored over time. For those who did not complete surveys within a notable period of time, automated reminders (email notifications or SMS texts) or Call Center outreach took place after the predetermined waiting period of 7 days, in efforts to optimize retention for non‐responders and mitigate missing data for all participants regardless of their source cohort. Surveys were anchored to the first vaccine dose date, and surveys were available on the portal for participant completion within windows of 5–14 days up to 21–90 days for earlier and later surveys, respectively. If surveys were not completed within these intervals, reach‐out efforts were implemented. For instance, after enrollment, participants could fill out a baseline form on the portal, but if this task was not completed, an email was sent between 2 and 7 days after its expected completion date. However, ever present (non‐expiring) surveys were available on the portal for participants to access and complete at any time, for ad hoc hospitalizations or ad hoc visits with a medical provider.

Data Flow and Infrastructure

In terms of electronic data capture, study data inflow was drawn from the single web‐based portal that supported all data collection in addition to interactions between participants and the Call Center. Related operational systems supporting the Call Center activities were embedded within each of those organizations and interfaced via a routine set of data transfers or application interfaces. Data flow diagram. EPRO = electronic patient reported outcome; eCRFs = electronic case report forms.

The study portal, developed by the study's technology partner, a Google company, leveraged existing HIPAA‐compliant infrastructure, including hosting, security (e.g., authentication, access management, and password management), and the study‐specific data system, in which participants completed the eICD prior to enrollment in addition to their medical release form and self‐reported data. Despite leveraging Google infrastructure, it was a stand‐alone portal and no data were shared from other sources with the study, and no study data were shared with any other Google systems. When Call Center staff contacted study participants directly, to obtain follow‐up information should a participant not respond directly within the portal, the participant contact information was transferred from the portal into the communications system used by the Call Center. This system managed call queues, scheduling, and call processing information. The study data obtained by the Call Center were also entered directly into the study portal. The study data were managed using a set of tools including Oracle (relational database management system), Informatica (data exchange/extract‐transform‐load procedures), Cognos (operational reporting), MS SQL, SAS, and SAS Visual Analytics (statistics).

Results

Cohort Description and Enrollment

Recruitment efforts were implemented during December 17, 2020 to September 23, 2021. As shown in Figure 3, from a total of n = 21 125 participants consented and screened for inclusion criteria, n = 19 858 study participants were eligible for inclusion in the study population (referred to henceforth as the All Consented Population; ACP). Approximately n = 9043 (46%) of participants were HERO Registrants, followed by n = 6456 (32%) participants from the national retail pharmacy, and n = 4359 (22%) participants were Project Baseline Community members. The n = 1267 deemed ineligible were largely due to individuals not completing the enrollment process on the platform for > 60 days since vaccination (55%) [13]. Of the full study population, n = 9518 received the Pfizer‐BioNTech COVID‐19 vaccine and enrolled in this study within 10 days of the first dose of vaccination (i.e., the Primary Analysis Safety Population; PASP). The remaining n = 10 340 of 19 858 included three subsets of participants (collectively referred to as the Consented but Not in the Primary Analysis Population; CNPASP): (1) participants who received the Pfizer‐BioNTech COVID‐10 vaccine but enrolled more than 10 days after the first dose of vaccination, (2) participants who received a non‐Pfizer vaccine and enrolled within 10 days following their first dose (or only) dose of vaccination, and (3) participants who received a non‐Pfizer vaccine and enrolled more than 10 days following their first (or only) dose of vaccination.

Cumulative number of HERO together study participants consented and screened during the recruitment period, December 17, 2020 to September 21, 2021.

In terms of baseline characteristics (Table 1), distributions in the PASP and CNPASP were qualitatively similar. The majority of the ACP was female (68.1%), White (67.5%), and not Hispanic or Latino (75.6%), as was the PASP and the CNPASP. Median age ranged from 36.6 years in the PASP to 39.1 years in the CNPASP, with an overall range of 18 to 90 years. Registered or Licensed Practical Nurse was the professional role in the greatest proportion of healthcare workers (12.9% overall, 11.5% in the PASP, and 14.1% in the CNPASP).

Percentages based on the number of participants expected to complete a given visit (derived from date of enrollment and Call Center rescue windows) represent a minimum of 4979 respondents at Week 1 to a maximum of 12 603 participants at Month 6 (42% to 64% of the ACP, respectively). Over the 24 months of follow‐up for each individual participant, visit completion compliance in the PASP varied from the lowest rates of 39.5% (Month 18) and 45.5% (Week 1) to the highest rates ranging from 51.5% to 66.1% at all remaining timepoints. Discussion

Amidst the pandemic, decentralizing recruitment, integrating remote consent, leveraging existing infrastructure to use digital health tools, and direct‐to‐participant data collection enabled study scalability, an engaged user experience in the HERO Together study, and expedient operationalization of a voluntary post‐authorization safety study to the FDA. Distinct from pharmacovigilance systems in which spontaneous reports of adverse events are reported after product exposures [21], HERO Together actively surveilled acute and chronic safety events of interest among individuals in real‐world settings, launched in less than 7 days post‐EUA, and provided preliminary safety results to the regulatory agency within 7 months of its start.

Learnings post‐launch were applied to enhance recruitment, reduce participant burden, and encourage continued participation. Aggregate participant characteristics were regularly reviewed for representativeness and engagement strategies were tailored to address areas of underrepresentation, including expanding recruitment strategies, placement of study navigators in geographically diverse vaccination sites, and broadening inclusion to the enrollment of healthcare workers' family members as well as community members. Inclusion criteria were broadened to reflect the expanding authorization of who could receive a COVID‐19 vaccine and the study enrolled participants beyond those in healthcare worker settings. Call Center ‘rescue’ phone calls for non‐responder follow up became limited to those participants who received either Pfizer vaccine only for both initial doses, or Pfizer vaccine and other vaccine. Additionally, intervals at which Call Center reached out to participants to follow up on a missed survey were reduced, which may account for the difference in completion rates at various timepoints.

Key features stand as strengths in this study. Preeminently, cognizant of the need for participant centricity during the exceptional circumstances of the pandemic, this study brought itself to vaccine recipients in their natural environment, without risk of additional exposure to SARS‐CoV‐2 [22]. The use of technology and deployment of remote electronic consent was also crucial to the ethical collection of safety data in a time in which vaccines were administered outside of the traditional settings of doctor's offices and pharmacies, therefore not fully captured in secondary data sources such as claims [23]. Additionally, approaching an existing cohort of healthcare workers, the HERO Registry, and participants from the Project Baseline Community Study were individuals who were already engaged in COVID‐related research, recruited through diverse methods, with a familiarity and facility with utilizing the study web portal.

Yet, a few limitations should be acknowledged. Participants voluntarily enrolled in the study were convenience and snowball‐sampled, and therefore not necessarily generalizable to the broader US population. Participants were largely female, white, and more than half were healthcare workers. While the inclusion criteria for HERO‐Together were intentionally broad, the enrolled study population may have a greater tendency toward health‐seeking behavior, which may reduce overall event risk [24]. However, this composition correlates with the population for whom the vaccine was authorized for and prioritized in receiving post‐EUA. Notably, in leveraging the existing registry infrastructure, enrollment numbers stratified by recruitment method and source cohort were not tracked, precluding assessment of which strategies were most effective. Additionally, and consistent with inclusion criteria, enrollment several days or weeks following vaccination was permitted and may have introduced the potential for preferential self‐enrollment of individuals experiencing a safety event (or early symptoms of a safety event). To help mitigate this recall bias, individuals with more than a 2‐day interval between vaccination and enrollment were administered an assessment to capture self‐reported safety information occurring within this interval, and the primary analysis was restricted to individuals enrolling within 10 days following the first vaccination dose. To minimize information bias due to misclassification of self‐reported events, all outcomes proceeded through to medical record review and validation by a centralized adjudication committee. Medical records could not be obtained for fewer than 10% of these events [13]. For these cases, linkage to administrative claims or electronic medical records data could have helped confirm event occurrence and mitigate gaps due to unavailable records. By the end of the study, approximately half of the study population did not complete the study, an observation consistent with prior digital studies with varying attrition for long‐term app users [25]. Of those providing a withdrawal reason, more than half cited a loss of interest in continued participation. Recognizing early on the challenges that come with survey completion over an extended follow‐up and with no face‐to‐face interactions as in a traditional clinical study, vigorous strategies to follow up with and engage with participants were incorporated.

Conclusions

Through novel, digitally‐driven methods for enrollment and data collection, HERO‐Together was able to quickly begin enrollment, making it the first safety study to launch and provide post‐EUA safety information to regulators. HERO‐Together enrolled 19 858 individuals within 9 months and informed the early and longer‐term safety of the COVID‐19 vaccine to the FDA.
