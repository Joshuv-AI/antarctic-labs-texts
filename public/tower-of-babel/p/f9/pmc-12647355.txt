Endothelial IL-36 receptor activation promotes vascular stability to limit pathological microvessel permeability in the CNS
Authors: Erin Fahey, Lucia Celkova, Valentina Frezza, Graeme P Sullivan, Kieran Byrne, Avril Watson, Yasmina E Hernandez-Santana, Kieva Byrne, Rachel Dalton, Hande Efe-Cicek, Jeffrey O’Callaghan, Conor Delaney, Ema Ozaki, Patrick T Walsh, Seamus J Martin, Matthew Campbell, Sarah L Doyle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPathological increases in vascular permeability cause edema and swelling, driving retinal and neurological disorders. Few factors are known to specifically enhance barrier integrity and prevent fluid leakage. Here, we examine the effects of IL-36 receptor (IL-36R) activation on vascular permeability in vivo in mice, ex vivo in tissue explants, and in vitro in primary mouse and human microvascular endothelial cells. Using a soluble, biologically active DEVD-modified recombinant IL-36β cytokine, we find that the processed DEVDIL-36β strengthens endothelial barrier function, reduces vascular leakage, and limits pathology. Cell-specific knockdown of IL-36R confirms that endothelial IL-36R signaling mediates these barrier-promoting effects. IL-36R localizes to the plasma membrane in response to the loss of endothelial cell-cell contact, consistent with its role in tissue stress responses. Mechanistically, IL-36R signaling enhances adherens and tight junctions, induces vasculoprotective processes, and drives vessel remodeling and stabilization. RNA sequencing supports these findings, establishing IL-36R as a regulator of vascular integrity.Keywords: IL-36 cytokine, IL-36 receptor, IL-36 receptor antagonist, retinal neovascular disease, vascular stabilization, vascular permeability, tight junctions, VE-cadherin, blood-retinal barrier, choroidal neovascularization

Introduction

Increased vascular and microvascular permeability is a key driver of organ injury in many diseases, and strategies that regulate vascular integrity are promising therapeutic targets. Blood vessels in the central nervous system (CNS) possess unique barrier properties that prevent infiltration of unwanted substances and tightly control delivery into neural tissues. Barrier breakdown is implicated in neurological and retinal conditions associated with neovascularization, because nascent vessels that contribute to disease often have a barrier incapable of maintaining the integrity of the vasculature, leading to pathological leakage.1 Although many agents and disease processes exacerbate vascular permeability, only a handful of factors are known to enhance barrier function and make vessels resistant to leakage. There is substantial evidence implicating members of the interleukin (IL)-1 family in the regulation of vascular function in the endothelium.2,3 Both IL-1α and IL-1β drive barrier permeability as a result of downregulating components of the endothelial cell barrier, including components of the adherens and tight junctions, promoting vascular edema associated with inflammation.2,3 IL-33 has similarly been shown to increase vessel permeability by reducing VE-cadherin and tight junction proteins.4,5 IL-18 appears to either promote or limit permeability in a context-dependent manner. In the lungs, IL-18 is reported to increase lung vascular permeability.6 However, in the CNS, brain, and retina, IL-18 administration reduces vascular permeability in models of spontaneous bilateral neovascularization and status epilepticus.7,8

The IL-36 family has three different, but highly homologous signaling molecules, IL-36α, IL-36β, and IL-36γ, which compete for IL-36 receptor (IL-36R) availability with a naturally occurring IL-36R antagonist (IL-36Ra). These are expressed as leaderless cytokines with low biological activity requiring proteolytic processing and N-terminal truncation to release their full activity.9 To date, the IL-36 signaling cascade has been studied most extensively in psoriasis, where IL-36R activation drives dermal inflammation,10 acting as an indirect mediator of angiogenesis via macrophage activation.11,12,13,14,15 More recently, IL-36 cytokines have been shown to directly regulate trophoblast migration, promote their interaction with endothelial cells, and induce expression of pro-angiogenic factors.16 In addition, IL-36R activation contributes to inflammatory angiogenesis in the cornea by upregulating the expression of vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGFs) and their receptors in vascular endothelial cells and directly promoting their proliferation and tube formation.17 However, neither its role in the regulation of vascular permeability in general nor its specific function at the blood-brain barrier (BBB) or blood-retinal barrier (BRB) have been reported to date.

In this study, we use the mouse retinal vasculature as an accessible in vivo model, amenable to manipulation, for studying the direct effects of IL-36R activation on blood vessel permeability in the CNS of adult mice. This model has the advantage of enabling the intravital acquisition of fluorescein angiography images to accurately gauge vascular leakage in real time at sites of injury and in non-injured adjacent microvasculature. Previously, Martin and colleagues18,19 generated highly soluble and biologically active modified human IL-36 cytokines by inserting a caspase-3 cleavage motif (DEVD) proximal to residues identified as cleavage sites in Towne et al. (2011).9 Here, we similarly generated a DEVD-modified murine IL-36β (DEVDmIL-36β) cytokine, processed and activated by caspase-3, and used it to demonstrate that IL-36R signaling has potential therapeutic application for reducing microvascular leakage in vivo. Furthermore, the introduction of DEVDmIL-36β directly into the eye by intravitreal injection is well tolerated and not toxic to surrounding neural tissue. Mechanistically, we find that IL-36R is mobilized to the plasma membrane of endothelial cells, and its activation acts as a potent inhibitor of vascular permeability, upregulating the translation of central components of the adherens junctions (AJs) and tight junctions (TJs). In this way, our data expand the functions of IL-36R signaling to include the uncommon actions of enhancing vessel stabilization and endothelial barrier integrity, suggesting that this signaling pathway may inform future therapies for BRB and BBB disorders.

Results

Loss of IL-36 receptor antagonist increases vascular complexity in the CNS

To examine the effects of unchecked IL-36 signaling on microvascular integrity of the CNS in vivo, the retinal vascular network was examined for structural changes in 12-month-old mice lacking IL-36 receptor antagonist (Il36rn−/−). Without the key negative regulator, which competes with IL-36 ligands for receptor availability, these aged mice are exposed to sustained long-term IL-36R activation over time. The retina is part of the CNS, and retinal blood vessels are functionally analogous to the cerebral blood vessels.20 Using isolectin (Ib4) staining to highlight the vasculature in retinal flatmounts, it was evident that there were alterations to the retinal vascular structure in Il36rn−/− mice compared to age-matched wild-type controls, with a greater density of vessels observed in Il36rn−/− mice (Figure 1A). There was also evidence of neovascular growths beyond the vascular plexus in these mice (Figure 1A bottom panel, z axis) as is often associated with increased retinal vasculature. Quantifying these observed differences in representative flattened Z-stack images from wild-type and Il36rn−/− mice, we found that mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was significantly increased in Il36rn−/− mice compared to their wild-type counterparts (Figure 1B). As Ib4 binds to α-d-galactose residues of glycosaminoglycans expressed on the endothelial cell surface, this increase in overall fluorescence could either be caused by an increased number of Ib4-binding sites or reflect a true increase in vascular density. To address this, we carried out a more detailed structural analysis, employing the Rapid Editable Analysis of Vessel Elements Routine (REAVER) tool21 to measure the total length of vessels, the number of branch points, and vascular tortuosity, i.e., the distance required to go from point to point (Figure 1C). It was evident that Il36rn−/− mice had significantly increased retinal vascular length and increased incidence of vascular branching points compared to wild-type animals (Figures 1D and 1E), but there was no evidence for an increase in tortuosity in Il36rn−/− mice (Figure 1F). These data suggest that excess IL-36 signaling can lead to structural remodeling of the retinal microvasculature.

Microvascular endothelial cells of the CNS are directly activated by IL-36 cytokines

The vascular network analysis of the Il36rn−/− mice demonstrated that IL-36 signaling affected microvessel geometry. However, IL-36 could be acting in a direct or indirect manner on the endothelial cells. Recent literature has indicated a pro-angiogenic role for IL-36 via both direct and indirect mechanisms in non-CNS-related disease.11,12,13,16,17 Using the publicly available endothelial translatome database, a resource providing information on genes undergoing active translation in vivo (http://www.rehmanlab.org/ribo),22 we found that brain endothelial cells actively express IL-36R (Il1rl2), IL-18R (Il18r1), IL-18R accessory protein (Il18rap), IL-1R accessory protein (Il1rap), and IL-1R (Il1r1) under baseline conditions with increased expression of IL-36R, IL-18Rap, and IL-1R post inflammatory (lipopolysaccharide [LPS]) challenge in vivo (Figure S1A). To ascertain whether these data on IL-36R signaling in endothelial cells of the mouse neurovasculature were likely to translate across species, we examined expression levels of IL-36R in primary human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMECs). Indeed, we found that both IL-36R and the accessory protein required for IL-36 signaling (IL-1Rap) are endogenously expressed at prominent levels basally in confluent HRMECs (Figure 1G). Localization of IL-36R was then assayed in HRMEC monolayer cultures by microscopy. IL-36R was observed at the cell membrane, where it co-localized with the endothelial-specific AJ component VE-cadherin (Figure 1H, closed white arrows). However, IL-36R was commonly found in the nucleus and/or peri-nuclear region (Figure 1H, open arrows), indicating that, like some other IL-1 family receptors, IL-36R contains a nuclear localization signal (NLS). Indeed, analysis of the IL-36R sequence using cNLS Mapper23 revealed two putative bipartite NLSs from residues 501 and 506, with high NLS scores of 6.6 and 5.9, respectively. We next examined whether IL-36R is capable of signaling in HRMECs. IL-36, like other IL-1 family members, is known to activate the transcription factor nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) in a number of cell types.24 Here, treatment with recombinant human IL-36α and IL-36β resulted in phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 (Ser536) within 5 min of stimulation with cytokine (Figure S1B), confirming that IL-36 signaling pathways are functional in HRMECs. In addition, this also led to the upregulation of VCAM-1, a well-established target of IL-1 family signaling in endothelial cells25 (Figure S1C), providing further evidence that microvascular endothelial cells can respond to IL-36.

IL-36R activation directly facilitates microvascular remodeling in vitro

The ability to form vessels or tubes is essential for the process of vascular modeling and reorganization; newly formed vessels can then mature or undergo regression. The increased branch points in the Il36rn−/− mice implied a role for IL-36R signaling in microvascular remodeling. We utilized a Matrigel-dependent tube formation assay to determine the effect of IL-36R activation on this process in a monoculture of HRMECs.26 Figure 2A depicts representations of network complexity for IL-36 cytokine treatments. Using recombinant human IL-36α and IL-36β, we found that IL-36β significantly increased the length of tubes compared to the unstimulated control (Figure 2B, left panel) and enhanced network complexity, as indicated by a significant increase in the number of tubes (Figure 2B, middle panel) and branching points (Figure 2B, right panel). VEGF was used as a positive control in all cases. Restoration of a stable vascular bed or monolayer requires endothelial cell proliferation and migration.27 Both cytokines resulted in an increase in the number of cells after 24 h (Figure 2C), with IL-36β again eliciting a stronger response than IL-36α. This was further confirmed by an MTS/PMS proliferation assay, which showed a significant increase in the metabolic activity of cells in response to both IL-36α and IL-36β (Figure 2D). Wound-healing assays were next used to examine the action of IL-36R activation on endothelial cell migration (Figure 2E). It was evident that both IL-36α and IL-36β treatment applied at the time of the scratch significantly increased the rate of wound closure (Figure 2F), indicating that IL-36 cytokines are capable of driving endothelial cell migration to repair the monolayer in vitro. It is noteworthy, however, that the increased rate of closure appears most obvious after the 8-h time point. This may be due to the maximum rate of closure occurring in the early hours post wounding, or it may indicate that IL-36R activation is driving migration in an indirect manner through regulation of gene expression.

IL-36R activation directly enhances endothelial monolayer integrity and reduces permeability in vitro

One of the most important functions of endothelial cells is the ability to form and maintain an organized and stable vascular network. Loss of barrier properties in established vascular networks can lead to disease, and similarly, pathology associated with neovascularization is often due to disorganized growth and nascent vessel incapacity to maintain appropriate barrier function. In the retina, the inner BRB (iBRB) maintains stringent control over the influx and efflux of material into the retina, analogous to the BBB.20,28 Dysregulation of neovascular processes is usually accompanied by a loss of barrier function, so we next examined whether IL-36 cytokines could directly affect endothelial monolayer permeability. To this end, we performed transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran flux assays29 in HRMECs grown on permeable transwell inserts and treated with IL-36α or IL-36β. TEER readings were taken prior to initiation of flux assay and demonstrated that, when normalized to baseline values, treatment with both IL-36α and IL-36β could significantly increase TEER compared to untreated control (Figure 3A). In line with increasing the electrical resistance across the endothelial barrier, FITC-dextran flux assays confirmed that pre-treatment of HRMECs with IL-36α/β for 6 or 24 h decreased the rate of FITC-dextran migration through the HRMEC monolayer (Figure 3B). Accordingly, IL-36α/β induced a significant decrease in apparent permeability (Papp) in a time-dependent manner, indicating a direct IL-36-dependent increase in endothelial monolayer tightness (Figure S2). We further validated these findings in a perfusable microvascular network using a 3D multiculture microfluidic organ-on-a-chip model (Figure 3C). We found a striking reduction in microvascular leakage of FITC-dextran following IL-36β treatment (Figures 3D and 3E and Video “IL-36β on retinal vessel-on-a-chip,” Videos S1 and S2). 3D-projected confocal images of the microvascular network stained with VE-cadherin and DAPI indicate a more organized network of microvessels in the IL-36-treated chip, with larger areas of extravascular space, highlighted in white boxes in Figure 3D (bottom panels). This suggests that the observed decrease in FITC-dextran permeability in response to IL-36 could be attributed to enhanced stability and maturity of the microvascular network. Taken together, these data demonstrate that IL-36R activation can enhance endothelial barrier function in vitro.

DEVDmIL-36β regulates the organization of ex vivo choroidal sprouting

Endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation are typically associated with barrier breakdown and increased permeability. As this was contrary to what we observed, we hypothesized that IL-36R signaling might promote carefully orchestrated reorganization and remodeling of the microvasculature to enhance overall barrier integrity and vascular stability as part of tissue repair processes following an injury, rather than promoting uncontrolled pathological growth of leaky blood vessels. To examine whether IL-36R activation regulated vascular remodeling in tissue explants, as it had in tubule formation assays, we performed a choroidal sprouting assay. Choroidal explants seeded in Matrigel and treated with DEVDmIL-36β consistently demonstrated a reduced area of sprouting compared to vehicle control (Figures 3F and 3G). In addition to reducing the area of neovascularization, treatment with DEVDmIL-36β appeared to produce explants with greater structural organization, indicative of enhanced vascular remodeling/pruning (Figures 3F and 3G).

DEVDmIL-36β enhances barrier function in the CNS microvasculature under inflammatory conditions in vivo

The barrier-enhancing properties following endothelial IL-36R activation in vitro were striking, so we wished to examine whether this function translated to the in vivo setting. Overall, IL-36β appeared to be a more potent activator of IL-36R on endothelial cells in vitro compared with IL-36α and was selected as the preferred ligand for in vivo studies. Full-length IL-36 cytokines possess low biological activity and require proteolytic removal of their N-terminal domains at specific cleavage sites to boost their function9 (Figure 4A). However, the endogenous proteases that mediate this processing are not known; therefore, we generated a modified recombinant murine IL-36β protein by introducing a caspase-3 cleavage motif DEVD N terminis to the previously identified Ser31 cleavage site9 using a method analogous to that described by Martin and colleagues for human cytokines18,19 (Figure 4B). This DEVD-modified IL-36β was subsequently processed by purified recombinant caspase-3 (Figures 4C and S3A), significantly increasing its biological activity, as demonstrated by its ability to induce production of high levels of IL-6 and KC (CXCL1) in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (Figure S3B).

The retina is supplied with nutrients from two vascular beds: the inner retinal vasculature that contributes to the iBRB, and the outer retinal, fenestrated, choroidal vasculature. To examine the effects of IL-36R activation on vascular integrity in the CNS in vivo, DEVDmIL-36β was administered at two doses (0.3 or 3 ng/eye) by injecting it directly into the vitreous of wild-type C57BL/6J mice immediately after they received a laser-induced injury in both eyes (Figure 4D). Laser-induced injury encourages pathological neovessel growth from the choroidal vessels in the outer retina, termed choroidal neovascularization (CNV). Notably, administration of laser-induced CNVs had no effect on the expression of IL-36 signaling components in the retina or the underlying RPE/choroid (Figure S4). Importantly, these nascent CNVs have poor barrier properties and are permeable to small-molecular-weight tracer molecules, such as sodium fluorescein, allowing the acquisition of dynamic fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) imaging profiles. FFA was performed on day 5 post laser-induced CNV and DEVDmIL-36β intravitreal injection (Figure 4D). To account for individual variability in intrinsic factors such as blood flow, for each animal, the signal intensity was normalized to the imaging starting point (i.e., 2 min post fluorescein injection) and monitored over time. We observed a striking reduction in fluorescence intensity, reflecting an enhancement of the overall barrier function with 3 ng of DEVDmIL-36β. This effect was particularly evident at the site of CNV. However, in addition, DEVDmIL-36β-dependent enhanced barrier function was also detected in the inner retinal vasculature, encompassing both the superficial and deep vascular plexus, outside the laser injury-affected area, as well as in microvessels of the deeper vascular plexus (Figures 4E and 4F). Thus, these data identify DEVDmIL-36β as a potential vascular-stabilizing factor improving barrier integrity at sites of direct injury as well as at adjacent sites of microvessel inflammation.

DEVDmIL-36β has the therapeutic potential for stabilizing pathological vascular leakage

Our data supported a function for IL-36 signaling in enhancing barrier properties and pointed to the potential for DEVDIL-36β as a therapeutic regulator of microvascular leakage. However, given the literature indicating a pro-angiogenic role for IL-36 in non-CNS-related disease11,12,13 and our evidence for baseline vascular remodeling in vivo (Figure 1) and in vitro (Figure 2), we wished to examine the impact of DEVDmIL-36β on neovascularization in vivo in a pathological setting. To do this, CNV lesion volume was assessed by Ib4 staining. Volume analysis indicated that DEVDmIL-36β does not increase neovascular lesion size, with no significant change in lesion volume observed with either dose (0.3 or 3 ng/eye) of DEVDmIL-36β used (Figures S5A and S5B). These data demonstrate that DEVDmIL-36β does not worsen pathological neovascularization nor does it alter the viability of retinal photoreceptors in vitro (Figure S5C) or in vivo (Figure S5D). Thus, in contrast to IL-1,30,31,32 DEVDmIL-36β does not induce cell death or cause structural abnormalities of the neural retinal tissue at concentrations 10 times the dose effective at reducing microvessel permeability. Furthermore, acute treatment with intravitreal DEVDmIL-36β had no effect on structural organization in healthy retinal microvasculature (Figure S6).

DEVDmIL-36β-mediated protection against leakage from neovascular lesions is dependent on IL-36R signaling in endothelial cells

Vascular permeability is regulated by the neurovascular unit comprising endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes/Müller glia, all working together to ensure vessel stability. To assess the extent to which IL-36R signaling can alter growth factors known to play roles in vascular remodeling and permeability in vivo and determine whether this could present a mechanism of action, retinal lysates were isolated from C57BL/6J mice treated with intravitreal DEVDmIL-36β administered at two doses (0.3 or 3 ng/eye) in conjunction with laser-induced injury (Figure 4D). However, we found that DEVDmIL-36β did not affect the concentrations of VEGF, FGF1, or FGF2 (Figure 5A). Our in vitro data indicated that IL-36 cytokines could act in a direct manner on endothelial cells to enhance vascular integrity. Thus, to determine if DEVDmIL-36β-induced alleviation of neovascular leakage was dependent on endothelial cell expression of IL-36R in vivo, we generated Tie2Cre+Il36rfl/fl mice by crossing Il36rfl/fl mice with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the Tie2 promoter. The resulting progeny give rise to endothelial cell-specific IL-36R knockouts (Il36rΔEC; Figure 5B). The vascular phenotype of these mice appeared normal and comparable to their Il36rfl/fl counterparts, with no structural or leakage abnormalities detected in either strain (Figure S7). However, when Il36rΔEC mice were treated with DEVDmIL-36β (3 ng/eye) or vehicle control, FFA profiling of the laser-induced CNVs demonstrated no significant differences between treatment groups (Figure 5C, right panel; 5D), while in their Il36rfl/fl littermate controls, DEVDmIL-36β retained its capacity to reduce vascular permeability, as previously observed in wild-type mice (Figure 5C left panel; D). These data imply that the barrier-enhancing properties of DEVDmIL-36β are predominantly due to IL-36R signaling in endothelial cells.

Transcriptomics reveals IL-36R activation regulates cell-cell adhesion and junction assembly pathways in brain-derived endothelial cells

To identify IL-36R-regulated functional effects on endothelial cells in an unbiased manner, we performed RNA sequencing on primary mouse brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMVECs) isolated from C57BL/6J wild-type mice and treated with DEVDmIL-36β for 24 h. Our lab uses a well-established protocol for routine BMVEC isolation, and given their functional homology to endothelial cells of retinal origin, these were selected as an accessible alternative to primary microvascular endothelial cells isolated from mouse retina. A heatmap and volcano plot show that DEVDmIL-36β signaling regulates gene expression in BMVECs, with genes both up- and downregulated (Figure 6A). Interestingly, the top two differentially expressed genes upregulated in response to DEVDmIL-36β, Csf3 and Lcn2, have both been reported to have vasculoprotective roles in the retinal and cerebral endothelium, respectively (Figure S8A).33,34 Examining the 20 most significantly enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms, terms related to immunological response, including “regulation of innate immune response” and “response to IL-1” appeared as expected (Figures 6B and S8B). In addition to the enrichment for genes in immune-related biological processes, there was evidence of enrichment for upregulation of genes involved in pathways related to “cell-cell adhesion” among others (Figure 6C). Downregulated pathways included pathways involved in negative regulation of cell division (Figure 6D). Further gene sequence enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed a particularly strong enrichment of genes involved in the regulation of cell-cell adhesion in response to DEVDmIL-36β treatment of BMVECs and also indicated the enrichment of gene sets involved in the regulation of tight junction organization and assembly (Figure 6E). Notable genes induced by DEVDmIL-36β treatment include Cldn5, a tight junction protein that prevents solutes and water from passing through the paracellular space between endothelial cells; F11r, another tight junction protein; and Micall2, which regulates the endocytic recycling of tight junction proteins occludins, claudins, and cadherins to the plasma membrane.

IL-36R activation increases the expression of TJ and AJ components

Much of the control of retinal vascular integrity and low rates of fluid-phase transcytosis are determined by the endothelial cell-cell barrier complex, comprising TJs, gap junctions, and AJs, controlling the influx and efflux of molecules from the circulation to tissue and vice versa. Our RNA sequencing data in BMVECs imply that cell-cell adhesion is a biological function significantly regulated at the level of gene expression by DEVDmIL-36β (Figure 6). AJs mediate cell-cell adhesion through the actions of catenins and cadherins. VE-cadherin is a critical regulator of vascular permeability and an AJ component that mediates the maturation and maintenance of cell-cell contact.35,36 In support of the pathway analysis, we found that IL-36R activation increased the gene expression of VE-cadherin in HRMECs at 24 h (Figure 7A). Furthermore, RNA sequencing specifically indicated that claudin-5 was one of the top TJ genes induced by DEVDmIL-36β. Indeed, in addition to increasing expression of AJ VE-cadherin, IL-36 treatment increased gene expression of claudin-5, one of the critical TJs for barrier integrity in the CNS; tricellulin, a TJ protein found at the specialized structures occurring where three cells meet together; and, to a lesser extent, ZO-1 (Figure 7A). We compared this IL-36-induced upregulation of the TJ proteins claudin-5, tricellulin, and ZO-1 against RepSox, a TGFβR inhibitor recently shown to increase barrier integrity through upregulation of the TJ protein claudin-5,37 and found that IL-36R activation induced claudin-5 and tricellulin to an equivalent level as RepSox (Figure S9A). Enhanced gene expression of VE-cadherin and tricellulin translated into IL-36-enhanced protein expression in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7B). We also observed a positive trend toward upregulation of retinal protein levels of claudin-5 in the laser-induced CNV model following DEVDmIL-36β injection (Figure S10A). There was clear evidence for a strong enhancement of claudin-5 in some animals, although the overall significance might be masked by the high degree of inter-individual variability in baseline claudin-5 levels, depending on factors such as vascular density, as well as the response to laser injury itself. Interestingly, DEVDmIL-36β administration had no effect on signal intensity in healthy Cldn5 promoter-driven green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter mice (Figure S10B). Thus, these data suggest that IL-36R activation could contribute to the overall AJ and TJ stability, specifically in scenarios where it is disrupted by pathological processes.

Inhibition of protein translation results in loss of IL-36-mediated barrier enhancement

Our data suggest that IL-36-induced improvement in barrier integrity may be, in part, due to the upregulation of multiple junctional and junctional assembly components following IL-36R activation. As knockdown of specific AJs and TJs would inevitably reduce monolayer integrity, we instead investigated whether inhibiting new protein translation resulted in a loss of IL-36-dependent barrier enhancement (Figure 7C). Therefore, HRMECs grown on permeable transwell inserts were stimulated with IL-36α/β in the presence of a translation inhibitor, cycloheximide (CHX), prior to carrying out an FITC-dextran flux assay as previously described (Figure 3). Inhibition of protein translation resulted in complete loss of IL-36-dependent barrier enhancement, reverting barrier integrity back to untreated levels (Figures 7D and S9B). This strongly suggests that IL-36-dependent gene expression and translation of junctional proteins is the primary mechanism by which IL-36R activation can improve endothelial cell monolayer integrity.

Loss of cell-cell contact mobilizes IL-36R to the plasma membrane

In endothelial monolayer cultures, we had observed that areas where cells had lost cell-cell contact coincided with the highest plasma membrane expression of IL-36R (Figure 1H, closed white arrows). We were interested to assess whether endothelial cell stress in the form of loss of cell-cell contact was a danger signal resulting in enhanced IL-36R expression at the plasma membrane. Therefore, we cultured HRMECs under confluent (Figure 7E, left panel) or sub-confluent (Figure 7E, right panel) conditions and stained for IL-36R and VE-cadherin. The difference in the pattern of IL-36R localization was striking. Under confluent conditions, IL-36R was observed mainly in the nucleus and in the cytosol in a perinuclear pattern (Figure 7E, left panel). In stark contrast, under sub-confluent conditions, in areas where lack of cell-cell contact is evident, IL-36R is clearly detected at the plasma membrane (Figure 7E, right panel). This, together with our data showing that IL-36R signaling increases expression of adhesion proteins, indicates that IL-36R is potentially mobilized to the plasma membrane as part of a cell stress response aimed at resolving endothelial cell monolayer integrity through upregulation of junctional components.

Discussion

It is well established that vascular barrier breakdown and excessive plasma leakage contribute to a wide variety of disease processes and are especially critical in impacting neurological and retinal disorders. Regulation of vascular permeability can occur via direct and indirect mechanisms, and members of the IL-1 family of ILs are known to affect vessel function,7,38,39 with IL-1 demonstrating a potent ability to reduce endothelial barrier properties.40 However, despite a significant overlap in immune functionality mediated by IL-1 and IL-36, there remains a lack of information on how IL-36 cytokines impact vascular permeability. Therefore, our aim was to assess whether IL-36R signaling impacts vessel integrity in microvasculature associated with the CNS.

In common with other members of the IL-1 cytokine family, IL-36 cytokines require N-terminal truncation for full activation. While cleavage sites and a variety of proteases responsible for this processing have been identified for the human cytokines,9,18,19 the details of this activation process are less clear for the mouse cytokines. Here, to facilitate investigations into the biological role of IL-36R activation in the CNS vasculature, we employed a method previously described by Martin and colleagues18,19 and inserted a caspase-3 cleavage motif proximal to the known processing site (Ser31)9 to generate a highly precise N-terminal truncation. Once cleaved by purified caspase-3, this modified protein yields a highly soluble cytokine with dramatically enhanced biological activity, while at the same time producing sufficient quantities to be used for functional in vivo assays.

A major finding of our study is that IL-36R activation specifically on endothelial cells appears to be vasculoprotective, providing striking protection of endothelial barrier function under pathological conditions. Importantly, this protection from vessel leakage in vivo was observed not only in areas of immediate injury but also in the iBRB microvasculature distal to laser injury. Immune dysregulation and inflammatory processes have been linked with laser-induced injury both clinically and experimentally, and the vascular permeability observed at distal vessels is likely a result of laser-induced local inflammation.41 In addition, the neovascularization and nascent vessel permeability observed in response to laser injury are highly dependent on the upregulation of VEGF.42 Therefore, these data indicate that IL-36R signaling has the potential to enhance barrier integrity and protect against both VEGF- and inflammation-induced vessel leakage. This function is uncommon and in direct contrast to IL-1’s established role in driving vascular permeability.2,3

Vascular barrier breakdown is associated with a host of retinal and neurological disorders; however, extremely few barrier-enhancing agents have been discovered to specifically increase barrier integrity and make vessels resistant to leakage. Angiopoietin 1 (Ang1) and a TGFβR inhibitor, RepSox, are two such factors, both of which enhance barrier integrity through induced expression of AJ and TJ proteins, and both are undergoing intense study for their therapeutic potential.37,43,44 In our study, we observed striking parallels with what is known about Ang1 and what we observe for IL-36. For example, despite long-term expression of Ang1 in vivo resulting in a greater number of large blood vessels,45 acute administration of Ang1 in vivo does not increase neovascular lesion size in multiple disease models44,46,47; instead, it induces highly ordered, hierarchical, stable, and leakage-resistant vascular structures. We similarly found that long-term unregulated IL-36 signaling, modeled using Il36rn−/− mice, demonstrated a role for IL-36R activation in vascular remodeling, with increases in vessel length and branch points. However, we also found that short-term IL-36R signaling induced higher-ordered, leakage-resistant structures in vitro in cell monolayers, 3D microfluidic cell culture, and ex vivo tissue explants. Moreover, while significantly inhibiting microvessel permeability, the introduction of recombinant DEVDmIL-36β into adult mice did not increase neovascular lesion volume in vivo in a pathological setting. While these observations may appear counterintuitive, it is important to note that contrasting roles in pathologies are common for IL-1 family cytokines, with several members of the IL-1 superfamily displaying similarly contrasting functional variations. IL-36 itself has been reported to both promote and prevent pathology in cancer, skin disease, and gut-related pathologies. It is of particular relevance to note that IL-36 can either exacerbate chronic inflammation and epithelial barrier breakdown in inflammatory bowel disease or promote a protective role in accelerating intestinal barrier repair and mucosal wound healing. In fact, several studies report a role for IL-36 in the maintenance of epithelial barrier homeostasis in skin and gut through the upregulation of wound-healing processes.48,49,50,51,52 Our data now implicate a similar role for IL-36 in maintaining endothelial barrier homeostasis in response to injury.

The biggest difference between normal vessels and new pathological vessels is that new vessels lack TJ proteins, allowing plasma and serous fluid in a neovascular lesion to leak into the surrounding tissue. Our data indicate that IL-36 cytokines enhance barrier function, at least in part due to IL-36-dependent transcriptional regulation of TJ and AJ proteins, in particular claudin-5 and VE-cadherin, which are key components in the neurovascular unit directly responsible for maintaining vascular integrity. Indeed, when de novo protein translation was inhibited by CHX, IL-36 treatment lost all ability to enhance barrier function in vitro. IL-36R activation appears to promote pathways that result in vessel remodeling and maturation, in addition to facilitating the tightening of endothelial cell junctions, resulting in a stabilized vascular network that does not incite edema. These functional data are supported by network analysis on RNA-sequencing data. In this context, there is scope to consider IL-36 signaling as a regulator of vascular healing, an interpretation that is in keeping with the IL-1 family role in responding to tissue stress. Indeed, analysis of IL-36R expression indicates that the receptor is mobilized to the plasma membrane in response to the loss of endothelial cell-cell contact, which represents an endothelial stressor. IL-36R activation, in turn, enhances cell migration and expression of cell-cell adhesion genes, including VE-cadherin, a major determinant of endothelial cell contact integrity, and claudin-5, a key regulator of paracellular transport in endothelial cells, to ultimately resolve cell stress and stabilize the vessels.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. We employed an engineered IL-36β to achieve robust activation for in vivo models, and while supportive in vitro experiments were performed, the engineered protein may not fully reflect native signaling. In addition, we employed an acute laser-induced neovascularization model; while this is the gold standard approach, it may not fully recapitulate human neurovascular disease. Finally, while we found that IL-36R activation improves barrier properties, the downstream signaling pathways and molecular interactions remain incompletely defined and require further analysis.

In conclusion, our data present modified DEVDIL-36β as a potent IL-36R activator that drives vascular remodeling to stabilize blood vessels and enhance endothelial barrier properties in the CNS. Strategies to potentiate endothelial IL-36 signaling may represent promising new therapeutic avenues for reducing microvascular leakage in diseases where barrier integrity is compromised by inflammation or elevated VEGF levels.

Experimental model and study participant details

All mice used in this study were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility, where animal welfare staff carried out daily monitoring. Animals had unrestricted access to food and water and were kept on a 12-h light/dark cycle, with lights on from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the internal ethics committee at TCD and conducted under project licenses granted by the Health Products Regulatory Authority (HPRA). The research was performed in full compliance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) guidelines for the use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research. There was no influence of sex on the study, animals used were both male and female. Unless otherwise stated (as for Figure 1), all animals were between 8 and 13 weeks when used and had not been used for any other previous procedures. Il36rn−/− mice were a gift from Prof. Manfred Kopf details in Tortola et al.57 l1rl2floxed (Il36rfl) transgenic mice were generated by Prof. Patrick Walsh details in Hernandez-Santana et al.53 Tie2Cre mice was sourced from Jackson labs strain #008863. C57BL/6J were sourced from Jackson labs and bred in house. For Figure 1, 12-month-old mice were used, comprising 3 wildtype (C57BL/6J) and 4 Il36rn−/− mice. For Figure 4, adult C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to three treatment groups (untreated control, 0.3 ng DEVDmIL-36β per eye, or 3 ng DEVDmIL-36β per eye), with 9–13 mice per group. For Figure 5, adult C57BL/6J mice were again assigned similarly (untreated control, 0.3 ng or 3 ng DEVDmIL-36β per eye), with 14–18 mice per group. Tie2Cre-negative (Il36rfl/fl) or Tie2Cre-positive (Il36rΔEC) were also used, with 5–10 mice per group. Interestingly we noted early cataract formation in these Il36rΔEC mice.

Primary mouse brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMVECs) were isolated using a protocol adapted from Assman et al. (2017).58 In brief, C57BL/6J mice were sacrificed and the brain was removed. Under sterile conditions the olfactory bulb, cerebellum and meninges were discarded. The remaining brain sections were homogenized and centrifuged at 600 g for 5 min at 4°C in working buffer (10 mM HEPES, 0.5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS)). The resulting pellet was resuspended in 22% (w/v) BSA and centrifuged at 1,000 g for 20 min. The myelin plug was recovered, resuspended in fresh 22% (w/v) BSA and centrifuged again to increase vessel yield. The supernatant was discarded, pellet resuspended in complete digestion media (1 mg/mL collagenase/dispase, 20 μg/mL DNase I, 0.147 μg/mL TLCK, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin in HBSS) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h and 15 min, with frequent shaking. Following digestion, the cell suspension was centrifuged at 600 g for 5 min, the supernatant removed, and pellet washed once by resuspending in PBS and centrifuging as before. The vessel fragments were resuspended in EGM-MV2 media (PromoCell) with all supplements along with puromycin (5 μg/mL) and seeded on to plates that had been coated with collagen IV (100 μg/mL) and fibronectin (50 μg/mL) (both Sigma-Aldrich). After 24 h of incubation, the media was replaced for a further 24 h. On day 3 post seeding, the puromycin-containing media was removed and replaced with EGM-MV2 growth media without puromycin for 5 days until the cells became confluent.

Method details

Retinal flatmount analysis

Eyes were enucleated from 12-month-old wild-type C57BL/6J and Il36rn−/− mice (received from M. Kopf, Zürich)57 and fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min before anterior parts of the eye (cornea, iris, lens) were removed and posterior eye cups were given 4 radial cuts. Eye cups were incubated with AlexaFluor488-conjugated Griffonia-simplicifolia-IB4 (GS-IB4) isolectin (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at 4°C. Mounted retinal flatmounts were imaged using a Leica SP8 scanning confocal microscope to capture z stack images. Minimum of two fields of view were taken from each retina. Maximum Z projection was used to process Z-stacks and these flattened images were subsequently analyzed using the Rapid Editable Analysis of Vessel Elements Routine (REAVER) tool.21 Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was determined using the publicly available ImageJ software.

Expression and purification of recombinant mouse DEVD-S31-IL-36β and caspase-3

A modified form of murine IL-36β containing a caspase-3-processing motif (DEVD) inserted N-terminal to known processing sites in IL-36β at Ser3118,19 was generated by cloning the DEVD-modified mouse IL-36β coding sequence in frame with the polyhistidine tag sequence in the bacterial expression vector pET45b. DEVD-S31-IL-36β (DEVDmIL-36β) was expressed by addition of isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, 600 μM) to exponentially growing cultures of endotoxin-free E. coli (ClearColi) bacteria followed by incubation for 3 h at 37°C. Bacteria were lysed by sonication and poly-histidine-tagged proteins were captured using nickel-NTA agarose (Amintra, Expedeon Ltd) followed by elution into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2), in the presence of imidazole (100 mM). Recombinant poly-histidine-tagged caspase-3 was expressed and purified similarly. Bacterial cell lysates and purified proteins were visualized on a 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue.

Caspase-3 cleavage of DEVD-S31-IL-36β

Full-length DEVDmIL-36β was processed into its cleaved active form in a protease reaction carried out in reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 75 mM NaCl, 0.1% (w/v) CHAPS, 2 mM DTT) for 2 h at 37°C. The same reaction was performed in the absence of DEVDmIL-36β and used as a vehicle control to account for the presence of active caspase-3.

Laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) and intravitreal injections

All relevant national and institutional approvals were obtained prior to commencing experiments. CNVs were induced in 12-week-old wild-type C57BL/6J mice using a green 532-nm Iridex Iris laser (532 nm, 140 mW, 100 ms, 50 mm spot size, 3 spots per eye) as previously described.39 Following laser injury, 3 μL of vehicle or freshly-processed DEVDmIL-36β were immediately injected intravitreally at concentrations of 100 ng/mL or 1,000 ng/mL. Mice were sacrificed 6 days post laser burn. Tie2Cre+Il36rfl/fl mice were generated by crossing Tie2Cre-expressing animals with Il36rfl/fl mice previously described by Hernández-Santana et al. (2020)53 to allow the endothelial cell-specific knockout of IL-36R. Cre-negative littermates were used as controls for these experiments.

Acquisition of dynamic fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) images

Fundus fluorescein angiography was carried out using the Heidelberg Spectralis HRA+OCT (Heidelberg Engineering) or the MICRON (Phoenix-Micron) imaging platform. Following pupil dilation with 1% (w/v) tropicamide and 2.5% (w/v) phenylephrine, mice were anesthetized using a ketamine/medetomidine mixture. For both methods mice were imaged in the FA mode with sodium fluorescein (100 mg/kg, i.p.) used as a contrast agent to visualize retinal blood vessels. For the Heidelberg system: A-scan images (30° field of view, 1536 × 1536 pixels, average of 100 ART frames) were captured at regular intervals (every 30 s) between 2 and 8.5 min post fluorescein injection, using the Heidelberg Eye Explorer software (version 1.7.1.0). Regions of interest (CNV lesion site(s), inner vasculature outside the CNV(s), microvessels) were selected and quantified in aligned images using ImageJ. To account for inter-individual variability, the signal intensity at each time point was normalized to the starting point and expressed as % value at 2 min. For the MICRON system: images were similarly captured at regular intervals (every 30 s) between 1 and 5 min post fluorescein injection. Again, these images were normalized to the starting point and CNV signal intensity analyzed using ImageJ.

RPE flatmount staining and CNV volume analysis

Eyes were enucleated, neural retina removed and the eye cups were given 4 radial cuts, avoiding the laser burns. The eye cups were fixed in 4% (w/v) PFA for 15 min, blocked/permeabilized for 1 h in 5% (v/v) normal goat serum (NGS) in PBS containing 0.05% (v/v) Triton X-100 and then incubated with rat anti-mouse CD-31 (Abcam) and rabbit anti-mouse vimentin (Cell Signaling Technology) antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with AlexaFluor594-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG, AlexaFluor405-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (both Abcam) and AlexaFluor488-conjugated GS-IB4 isolectin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (all 1:400) for 2 h at room temperature before mounting. CNV Z-stacks were acquired using a Leica SP8 scanning confocal microscope. Ib4 staining-based 3D renders of z stack images were created with Imaris software (Oxford Instruments) and used to calculate CNV volume. Volumetric analysis was performed blinded. Each CNV lesion was assessed prior to inclusion in volumetric analysis, with hemorrhages excluded. Each neural retina was lysed in 200 μL of radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (1% (v/v) IGEPAL CA-630, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 (w/v) sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% (w/v) SDS in PBS) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) and centrifuged at 15,600 g for 20 min. Concentration of VEGF, FGF1 and FGF2 in cleared supernatants was measured using a corresponding DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems) as per manufacturer’s instructions and normalized to mg of total retinal protein as determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

DEVDmIL-36β in vivo viability assay

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining was used to determine if intravitreal injection of DEVDmIL-36β resulted in photoreceptor apoptosis. Adult wild-type C57BL/6J mice were injected intravitreally with 3 μL of vehicle or freshly-processed DEVDmIL-36β (up to 10 μg/mL) and sacrificed 3 days post injection. Enucleated eyes were embedded in paraffin and sectioned. After rehydration, TUNEL staining was performed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, TMR red (Roche Molecular Systems), as per manufacturer’s instructions, sections were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Sigma-Aldrich) and imaged using a Leica SP8 scanning confocal microscope.

Choroidal sprout assay

Eyes were enucleated from 3-week-old C57BL/6J mice, anterior parts of the eye (cornea, iris, lens) removed along the pars plana and neural retina gently peeled away. The RPE/choroid/sclera complex was cut into thin strips of approximately 1 mm × 1 mm in size which were carefully positioned in 60 μL of Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel (Corning) in a 96-well plate, with the RPE side facing upwards. Culture dishes were incubated at 37°C for 20 min to allow Matrigel to solidify before the addition of 200 μL of endothelial growth medium EGM-MV2 (PromoCell). Explants were cultured for 7 days with media replenished every 48 h. Beginning on day 3 post seeding, treatments were added to the explants as indicated and replenished at media changes. On day 7, phase contrast images of explant wells were taken using an Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius). The area of choroidal sprouting in the well was measured using ImageJ. The area of organized sprouting was determined as the area where sprouting vessels were clearly defined and visible and expressed as a percentage of the total sprouting area.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

BMVECs were treated with processed DEVDmIL-36β (100 ng/mL) for 24 h and RNA was extracted post treatment as described above. RNA sequencing was outsourced to Novogene who performed the following procedure. Messenger RNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. After fragmentation, the first strand DNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and second strand DNA was synthesized using dTTP. The library was then subjected to end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, amplification and purification. The library was checked with Qubit and RT-PCR for quantification and Bioanalyzer for size distribution detection. The libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina platform. Raw data was processed through fastp software to generate clean data. Paired end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.05. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled via StringTie v1.3.3b56 in a reference-based approach. To count the reads numbers mapped to each gene featureCounts v1.50-p3 was used, which then allowed the FPKM to be calculated.

Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 v1.38.3 in R.59 Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was carried out using version 4.3.3 of the GSEA software (Broad Institute, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp).60,61 Input gene expression data were provided in FPKM format, as processed through Novogene’s standard RNA-seq pipeline. These data were reformatted into GCT files and class labels for group comparisons were specified in CLS format. The analysis was executed in “no collapse” mode, preserving original gene identifiers without conversion to gene symbols. Genes were ranked using the Signal-to-Noise (S2N) metric. A total of 5,000 gene set permutations were applied, which is appropriate for datasets with limited sample sizes. The enrichment score was computed using a weighted scoring approach, and results were normalized using mean-dividing (norm = meandiv). Gene sets containing fewer than 2 or more than 5,000 genes were excluded from the analysis. All gene sets were obtained in GMT format from the MSigDB database. Only genes with valid and recognized symbols were included. Final enrichment results were sorted by normalized enrichment score (NES), with statistical significance defined as a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 and nominal p-value < 0.05. GO enrichment analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes identified from RNA-seq data. Adjusted p-values (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) for enriched biological process terms were obtained from enrichment results. For visualization, two types of bar plots were generated. To highlight specific biological processes of interest that were related to cell adhesion and tight junction processes, GO terms related to cellular junctions and adhesion were filtered based on the presence of predefined keywords in the GO term descriptions. These included terms such as adhesion, tight junction, cell-cell adhesion, cadherin, integrin, and others relevant to junctional structure and function. Enriched GO terms matching these keywords were extracted and –log10(adjusted p-value) was calculated for each. Terms with adjusted p < 0.05 were highlighted in red. Separately, the top five most significantly enriched GO terms among downregulated genes were selected based solely on adjusted p-value, without keyword filtering. These were visualized in a similar bar plot format, showing the log10(adjusted p-value) and highlighting statistical significance by bar color (green). GO descriptions were wrapped or resized to improve readability. All plots were generated using Python with matplotlib, pandas, and numpy.

Immunocytochemistry

HRMECs were seeded on coverslips at 0.8 × 105 cells/mL and grown until confluent or sub-confluent. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min and blocked/permeabilized for 1 h in 5% (v/v) NGS in PBS containing 0.05% (v/v) Triton X-100, followed by an overnight incubation at 4°C with rabbit anti-human IL-1Rrp2/IL-1R6 (1:100, Novus Biologicals) and mouse anti-human VE-cadherin (1:100, Santa Cruz) primary antibodies. Cells were then washed with PBS and incubated with AlexaFluor488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam) and AlexaFluor594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen) secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature (both 1:500). Following this incubation, the coverslips were washed with PBS and counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (1:10,000) for 10 min before being washed again as before. The coverslips were mounted on slides and then imaged using an Echo Revolution fluorescence microscope or a Leica SP8 scanning confocal microscope.

Tube formation assay

Cells were seeded 2 days prior to the experiment in a T75 flask in complete growth media and allowed to become ∼80% confluent. They were placed in low-serum media overnight prior to use in the experiment. 96-well plates were coated with 60 μL of Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning) and placed in a 37°C incubator for 30 min to set as per manufacturer’s instructions. HRMECs were then seeded onto the plates at a density of 8 × 104 cells/mL and treated with cytokine as indicated. Phase contrast images of the wells at 10× magnification were taken at 30-min intervals using an Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius) and analyzed using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plug-in for ImageJ.26

Proliferation assays

HRMECs were seeded on coverslips in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL and allowed to grow overnight in growth media. They were then washed with PBS, placed in reduced serum media and incubated with treatment for 24 h. The coverslip was then washed with PBS, fixed in ice-cold methanol and stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:10,000). The coverslips were then affixed to slides and imaged via fluorescence. 10 images per slip were taken and averaged to give a cell count per coverslip. For the MTS/PMS assay (Promega) cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL in a 96-well plate. They were allowed to grow overnight in complete growth media before being washed and placed in low-serum media with the treatment for 24 h. The MTS/PMS assay was carried out as per manufacturer’s protocol.

Wound healing assay

Cells were cultured in 12-well plates until confluent and then placed in low-serum media for 12 h. Each well was ‘scratched’ through the center using a P2 tip in a straight line to create a wound in the cell monolayer. Cells were then incubated in low-serum media containing various concentrations of IL-36 cytokines. Images of the cells were taken with a light microscope at regular intervals for a 24-h period. The size of the wound was measured using ImageJ software and converted into a percentage of the original wound size to determine the rate of wound healing.

Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement

HRMECs were grown in transwells (Corning) for 5-7 days to polarize. They were treated as indicated with IL-36 cytokines in both apical and basal chambers. An EVOM2 epithelial voltmeter with STX2 chopstick electrodes (World Precision Instruments) was used to measure the stabilized TEER value of each transwell, with measurements taken in Ω and accounting for the nascent resistance of the transwell membrane by taking a measure of a well with culture media that had no cells. The resistance value of this control well with no cells was subtracted from each reading to obtain true blank-corrected resistance of the cell monolayer. The unit area resistance was calculated by multiplying the blank-corrected resistance by the effective surface area of the membrane insert and expressed in Ω cm2. To account for well-to-well variability, all readings were normalized to the baseline value taken prior to treatment.

Permeability (flux) assay

HRMECs were grown in transwells (Corning) as described above. The cells were placed in low-serum media and treated with IL-36 cytokines for 6 or 24 h, before fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated dextran (Sigma-Aldrich) diluted to 1 mg/mL in low-serum media was added to the apical chamber. Sampling aliquots were periodically removed from the basal chamber and placed in a 96-well plate. The basolateral chamber was then replenished with fresh media. This was repeated at 20-min intervals for 2 h. The fluorescence of FITC-dextran was measured using a fluorescent plate-reader (BioTek) at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm. Relative fluorescence units (RFUs) were converted to ng/mL using a FITC-dextran standard curve and corrected for background fluorescence and serial dilutions over the course of the experiment. The apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) was calculated as Papp (cm/s) = (dQ/dT)/(A x C0), where dQ/dT is the apparent rate of FITC-dextran appearance in the basolateral chamber, A (cm2) is the surface area of the membrane insert, C0 (mg/mL) is the initial FITC-dextran concentration in the apical chamber. dQ/dT is determined from the slope (y = mx + c) calculated by plotting the cumulative amount (Q) against time (s).

Inhibition of translation

HRMECs were grown in transwells as above. Once polarized, they were treated with the translation inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX, 10 μM; Sigma-Aldrich) and/or IL-36 cytokines, followed by a permeability assay which was carried out as described above.

Vessel-on-a-chip model

Outer retinal vasculature was modeled using HRMECs (Cell Systems) and primary human retinal pericytes (HRPs; Cell Systems), co-cultured in microfluidic culture chambers (AIM Biotech) over a period of 7 days. Prior to co-culture, HRMECs were maintained in Endothelial Cell Growth Media 2 (PromoCell) and HRPs in Complete Classic Medium with Serum and CultureBoost (Cell Systems). All cells and microfluidic chips were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2. For chip setup, all cells were trypsinized on day 0 using trypsin-EDTA 0.25% (Gibco), centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min and resuspended at a density of 36 × 106 cells/mL in endothelial cell media with thrombin from bovine plasma (4 U/mL; Sigma-Aldrich). A 6 μL suspension of HRMECs and HRPs at a ratio of 6:3 respectively was prepared per chip. The cell suspension was combined with equal volumes of fibrinogen (6 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), quickly mixed with gentle pipetting and 10 μL loaded into the inlet of the central channel in each microfluidic chip. The plate was humidified using sterile dH2O during a 15-min incubation to facilitate the thrombin-fibrinogen reaction. Following this, the media channels were coated with fibronectin from human plasma (60 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) in Medium A (endothelial cell media with recombinant human VEGF (50 ng/mL; Bio-Techne)) for 1 h at 37°C to support the addition of supplementary HRMECs on day 2. The chips were fed using Medium A thereafter with 70 μL added to the right inlet and 50 μL to the left inlet on both top and bottom media channels. This volume differential generates an interstitial flow of media throughout the culture chamber. The microfluidic chips were fed daily, VEGF was removed from the media from day 4 to day 7. On day 2, HRMECs were trypsinized and centrifuged as previously described. Cells were resuspended in respective culture medium at 1.5 × 106 cells/mL. 10 μL of the HRMEC cell suspension was added to each port on the top media channel (equivalent to a total of 30,000 cells) and repeated in the same manner on the bottom channel thereafter. dH2O was removed from the humidity chambers and the plates incubated upside down at 37°C for 1 h to facilitate cell attachment. After 1 h, dH2O was replaced and microfluidic chips cultured as normal. Weekend-free feeding was achieved by placing the microfluidic chips on the OrganoFlow (Mimetas) with continuous rocking to 11° every 60 min. On day 7, the formed outer retinal vasculature was treated with rhIL-36β (100 ng/mL) and after 24 h, the vessels were perfused with 70 kDa FITC-dextran (0.1 mg/mL) in endothelial cell medium. The perfusion was imaged over a 15-min timelapse with 20-s intervals using a Leica SP8 scanning confocal microscope, with MFI quantified in ImageJ.

Western blotting

Protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Samples were denatured in sample buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 2% (w/v) SDS, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.01% (w/v) bromophenol blue, 50 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)) at 95°C for 10 min, resolved by SDS-PAGE and proteins transferred to methanol-activated polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% (v/v) Triton X-100 (TBS-T) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody against phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology), total NF-κB p65 (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology), tricellulin (1:1,000; Thermo Fisher Scientific), VE-cadherin (1:1,000; Santa Cruz), claudin-5 (1:1,000; Invitrogen) or β-actin (1:2,000; Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in blocking buffer. The membranes were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich) as appropriate and developed using the WesternBright ECL HRP substrate kit (Advansta) to visualize the proteins of interest.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism software. All data were checked for normal (Gaussian) distribution using the Prism’s normality tests (D’Agostino-Pearson, Anderson-Darling, Shapiro-Wilk, Kolmogorov-Smirnov). Where data did not follow normal distribution, a non-parametric test was used to assess data. A correction for unequal variances was applied where appropriate. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and relevant statistical tests are indicated in figure legends.
