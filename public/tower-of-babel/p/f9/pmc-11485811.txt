Knowledge and perception of a clinical trial among the participants: An experience from oral cholera vaccine study in Koshi Province, Nepal
Authors: Shipra Chaudhary, Surendra Uranw, Basant Rai, Nisha Keshary Bhatta, Gauri Shankar Shah, Prakash Poudel, Basudha Khanal, Rosan Prasad Shah Kalawar, Nikita Rai, Jenisha Shrestha, Merina Shrestha, Shalu Parajuli, Birendra Prasad Gupta, Sridhar Vemula, Katerina Rok Song, Julia Lynch, Tarun Saluja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClinical trials (CT) have been the cornerstones in developing medicines, vaccines, and new interventions. Despite tremendous efforts, a CT cannot succeed without participants’ involvement. Various factors can affect people’s attitude and participation in a CT. Hence, this study was conducted to assess the knowledge and perception of a clinical trial among the participants of a CT at B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences, Dharan, Nepal. This was a population-based descriptive cross-sectional study conducted during 2021–2022. Participants involved in a phase III clinical trial were enrolled, and an in-depth interview was conducted using a semi-structured questionnaire. Demographic information, knowledge and perception about CT were assessed, and a descriptive analysis was done. Among 622 participants, the majority were males (321; 51.6%), literate (587; 94.37%), of indigenous ethnic groups (Janajatis 61.4%), and without previous experience of CT (504; 81.1%). The majority had adequate knowledge (566/622; 91%) and positive perception (595/622; 95.7%) of CT. Most participants (467; 75.1%) had participated in CT to get protection from the particular disease from the provided vaccine and (603; 96.9%) believed CT to benefit mankind. Further studies comparing pre- and post-participation knowledge and perception might be helpful for planning and better management of CTs in such settings.KEYWORDS: Clinical trial, knowledge, perception, participants, oral cholera vaccine

Introduction

With the paradigm shift created by Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM), clinical trials (CTs) have been the cornerstones in developing medicines, vaccines, and new interventions.1,2 National Institute of Health (NIH) defines a clinical trial (CT) as “a research study in which one or more human subjects are prospectively assigned to one or more interventions (which may include placebo or other control) to evaluate the effects of those interventions on health-related biomedical or behavioral outcomes.”3 A well-conducted Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) is given a high position in the hierarchy of evidence in EBM pyramid.4

While CTs are important, the whole process from concept to completion is very arduous, time-consuming, and costly.5,6 Apart from the various hurdles related to funding, planning and management of resources (human/non-human), the recruitment of participants plays a decisive role in the success of a CT.5–7 A good accrual can improve the statistical power, decrease the rate of Type II errors and make the results of a CT more generalizable.7

Incidents of dehumanization and exploitation of people in the name of science have led to suspicion and mistrust toward CTs.8 With the advent of Good Clinical Practice (GCP) for the conduction of CTs, voluntary participation on the part of participants needs to be ensured during any CT.9 Being a specialized field, CTs may not be well understood by even healthcare professionals who are not specifically involved in it.10 A negative perception of CT may also be the result of lack of knowledge and proper understanding of its importance. Moreover, in the developing world, the high prevalence of the concept of CT participants being taken as “guinea pigs” still remains a great challenge to be faced during the conduct of a CT.

With the COVID-19 global pandemic, people are realizing the importance of vaccine trials.11,12 In line with this changing trend, the factors affecting CTs can vary depending on the resources, place or population where it is conducted. The perception of the participants can even change after actual involvement in a CT. Thus, exploring the knowledge and perception of CT among the participants is a crucial aspect in the smooth conduct of a CT. Despite the high burden of infectious diseases in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), most of the CTs originate predominantly from high-income countries, thus limiting the generalizability of the influencing factors.7 Nepal, being one of the resource-limited developing countries, has limited CTs (297 CTs and 11 vaccine trials registered in the NIH clinical trial registry till May 2024).13 This study aims to assess the knowledge and perception of a clinical trial among the participants of a multi-centric phase III CT at B.P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences, Dharan, Nepal. The results of the CT have been published in the Lancet Global Health.14

Materials & methods

This was a population-based descriptive cross-sectional study conducted among the participants involved in a multicentric phase III clinical trial “Oral Cholera Vaccine Simplified (OCV-S)” (www.clinicaltrials.gov NCT04760236) at B P Koirala Institute of Health Sciences (BPKIHS), Dharan, Nepal. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC-BPKIHS). Written informed consent and/or assent was obtained prior to the enrollment. Enumerative convenience sampling technique was used to enroll the participants of OCV-S study who voluntarily consented to participate in the interview at the end of 6 months follow-up visits. The clinical trial –Oral cholera vaccine enrolled participants from very young children to adults – was included in the study.

A semi-structured, pre-tested questionnaire was used to collect the information under four sections: (i) socio-demographic information, (ii) knowledge, (iii) perception, and (iv) experiences. Trained research assistants conducted the interview in the local language (Nepali). We used eight questions each for the assessment of knowledge and perception of the participants. The questions were related to CT in general and their involvement in the particular trial. For knowledge, each correct answer received a score of 1, while the incorrect responses or “don’t know” received a score of 0. Similarly, for perception, positive answers were scored as 1 and negative answers as 0. Answers related to knowledge and perception were then graded on a scale of 0 to 8, with the score of 5–8 classified as “adequate knowledge” or “positive perception” and the score of 0–4 as “inadequate knowledge” or “negative perception.”

Descriptive analysis was performed. The categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and the numeric data as mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) or median (Interquartile range (IQR)) according to the type of data distribution. Bivariate analysis was done to assess the association between socio-demographic characteristics and participants’ knowledge and perception. The associations between knowledge and perception score range with demographic variables were measured using chi-square test with a 95% confidence interval and the p-value <.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 16.0.

Discussion

To our knowledge, there is a scarcity of data pertaining to knowledge and perception toward participation in CTs among the public from this part of the country. In this study, we assessed the knowledge and perception regarding CT among the 622 healthy volunteer participants in a vaccine trial at the end of their follow-up visits. Most of the trial participants had adequate knowledge and positive perception with a willingness to participate in CTs in the future. In our study, female gender and non-indigenous ethnic groups were associated with positive perception and adequate knowledge, respectively.

Our study had participation of 622 out of 633 CT participants, with a retention rate of 98% during the CT. This is comparable to 93% of subjects from influenza vaccine trials in South Africa returning for such observational study.15 The accrual rates varied from 11% in a systematic review from China16 to 67.7% in an individual study from Nepal.17 These differences are supported by the finding of another systematic review that participants are most likely to enroll in studies of observational nature, such as questionnaires as against CTs that include invasive procedures.18 The majority of our participants were naïve to CT experience, with only 11.9% having previous involvement in any type of CT. This is also comparable to 18.1% of previous participation in biomedical research in Jordan19 and 6.5% in Oman.20 This could be due to limited CTs being conducted in LMICs including Nepal. As BPKIHS, Dharan is outside the capital city, we have fewer studies in this part due to challenges related to logistics and administration.

In our study, the majority had adequate knowledge (566/622; 91%) and positive perception (595/622; 95.7%) regarding CT at the end of their participation. In a similar study in South Africa, there was a good general recall of the informed consent process but lack in understanding of important aspects like the concept of placebo and randomization.15 Results of studies across the world vary from a lack of general understanding of CTs and poor attitude in Oman,20 Saudi Arabia,21,22 Jordan,23 and USA24 to moderately favorable feelings toward CTs in Korea.25 In a multinational study during COVID-19, three-quarters (74.9%) of participants had heard about clinical trials before, and 57.6% of them had a positive attitude toward participation in CTs.26 This discrepancy can be explained by the fact that our study assessed the knowledge among the participants already involved in a CT rather than the general public and that too at the end of their involvement in CT in the post-COVID era. This does point toward the positive impact of the involvement of a CT on their knowledge and perception.

In this study, sociodemographic factors like gender and ethnicity were associated with differences in the perception and knowledge of the participants, respectively. Females had better perception, and non-indigenous ethnic groups (non-Janajatis) had better knowledge about CT. This finding is comparable with a few other studies from South Africa,15 Oman20 and Saudi Arabia27 where females had better perception of CTs. The knowledge of indigenous ethnic groups could be less due to their lesser opportunities for education and employment as the racial and ethnic discrimination still exist to some extent in our country. Additional factors like education, income, residence and medical background have been found to be affecting the knowledge and perception in other studies.22,27 Hence, these factors need to be considered while planning CTs.

The primary reasons for participation in our study were some form of personal gain like protection against the disease from the provided vaccine, healthcare privileges and incentives though a few participated for altruism (16.7%). This is similar to a study from Ireland7 where personal factors like obtaining personal gain through participation were the predominant reason but in contrast to predominantly altruistic motives and trust in treating physicians in Austria.28 In our study, the decision to participate in the CT was predominantly made by self (67.5%) as compared to physician influence for decision in Ireland.7 Very interestingly, we found an almost complete willingness (98.1%) of participants to be involved in CT in the future, a response much higher than the 31.1% willingness seen in Jordan19 and 25% in Korea.25 In another study related to COVID-19 in France, about 75% and 48% of the survey respondents were likely to accept vaccination and participation in a CT, respectively.29 Thus, lack of awareness and vaccine hesitancy could be other barriers affecting vaccine CT and also implementing vaccination programs.

Overall, there were several strengths and limitations of the current study that need to be considered during the interpretation of results. First, this study involves the participants of a single CT and hence there are chances of selection and information bias. Our CT participants were well informed about the CT at the time of their enrollment and surveyed at the end of the trial, therefore the results may not be generalizable to the whole population or even to CT participants in other trials in Nepal. Second, there is a lack of comparison of data on pre- and post-involvement in CT which might give better information on the associated factors. Third, there is a lack of response of the participants who had dropped out (Loss to follow-up and consent withdrawal) which might have provided more comprehensive information. Nevertheless, this study has addressed and provided some insights into the participants’ knowledge and perception of CT at some point of time in our part of the world.

In conclusion, our study shows that the majority of the participants of the CT have good knowledge and favorable perception of CT with significant association with female gender and non-indigenous ethnicity. Further, studies involving multiple types of CTs and comparing pre- and post-participation knowledge and perception are recommended for planning and better management of CTs in such settings.
